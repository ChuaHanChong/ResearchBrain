# A Bayesian Reasoning Framework for Robotic Systems in Autonomous Casualty Triage

> [!summary] Summary
> A Bayesian reasoning framework for robotic casualty triage was developed, integrating multimodal sensor data to improve assessment accuracy in mass casualty incidents. In realistic field tests during the DARPA Triage Challenge, the system increased total triage scores by over 100% and overall performance by nearly four-fold.

## Problem

- Mass casualty incidents (MCIs) overwhelm traditional emergency response services, necessitating rapid and reliable triage.
- Autonomous robots deployed in MCIs face challenges from noisy, incomplete, and fragmented sensory data due to cluttered terrains, poor visibility, and sensor occlusions.
- Existing robotic perception algorithms for vital signs often suffer from algorithmic isolation, context blindness, and brittleness under real-world noisy conditions.

## Method

- A knowledge-based cognitive architecture, centered on an expert-elicited ==Bayesian Network (BN)==, integrates multimodal perception inputs from various non-contact sensors (RGB, thermal, radar, lidar, microphone).
- The BN processes ==uncertain evidence== from independent physiological estimators, fusing heterogeneous data sources with variable reliability into a unified ==probabilistic assessment== of a casualty's condition.
- The modular system operates on heterogeneous aerial and ground robotic platforms within a ==ROS 2== framework, designed for real-time inference and efficient resource utilization.

## Results

- The integrated "Robot + BN" system significantly increased total triage scores in field tests, more than doubling the score in one scenario (**25** → **61**) and nearly tripling it in another (**16** → **45**) compared to a baseline robotic system.
- Diagnostic coverage (reliability) improved from **0.31** to **0.95**, and overall performance increased nearly four-fold from **14%** to **53%** with BN integration.
- The solution achieved real-time performance with inference times under **1 ms** and a memory footprint below **100MB**, enabling deployment on resource-constrained, field-deployable robotic hardware.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Bayesian Networks can effectively model uncertainty and interdependencies between vital signs and injuries, enabling robust reasoning and medically plausible casualty assessments even with noisy and incomplete data.
> - Integrating expert medical knowledge into the BN's Conditional Probability Tables (CPTs) addresses the lack of standardized MCI datasets and ensures the model's inferential behavior aligns with clinical reasoning.
> - A modular, sensor/model-agnostic architecture enhances fault tolerance by reducing the impact of errors from single perception modules and allows for flexible integration of diverse sensing capabilities.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Mass casualty incidents (MCIs), such as terrorist attacks or natural disasters, can rapidly overwhelm traditional emergency response services due to the high volume of casualties and complex injury profiles. This context underscores the critical need for efficient and reliable triage methods. Autonomous robotic systems are emerging as a potential solution to address these challenges by surveying disaster zones, identifying victims, and remotely assessing injuries. However, the deployment of such robots in real-world MCI environments introduces significant operational hurdles. These include navigating cluttered terrain, operating under poor visibility, and contending with severe sensor occlusions, which result in noisy, incomplete, and fragmented sensory data.

Existing robotic perception algorithms often exhibit limitations such as algorithmic isolation, where each vital sign is treated independently; context blindness, where clinical interdependencies are ignored; and brittleness, where performance degrades under noisy conditions. This research addresses these limitations by proposing a cognitive architecture that moves beyond isolated sensor outputs to provide a coherent, context-aware triage assessment.

The paper situates its work within the broader advancements in Bayesian Networks (BNs) for medical applications, acknowledging their historical use in clinical triage for conditions like non-traumatic abdominal pain and pediatric asthma. Concurrently, it references recent progress in computer vision and machine learning for remote, non-contact vital sign prediction (e.g., heart rate, SpO2, blood pressure). While these individual models show promise, the challenge of integrating their diverse, often "black-box" predictions into a robust, comprehensive system for overall casualty assessment remains.

In the field of robotics for search and rescue, existing solutions typically focus on either Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) or Unmanned Ground Vehicles (UGVs). UAVs offer rapid coverage but face issues with flight time, payload limits, and stable data collection. UGVs allow for closer interaction and more sophisticated sensors but are limited by terrain accessibility and navigation in debris-filled areas. The present work contributes to the advancement of multimodal data fusion in this domain, aiming to aggregate heterogeneous information streams from various sensors into a reliable triage decision framework. The authors state that their proposed system represents one of the first neuro-symbolic frameworks for this application validated at scale, distinguishing it from purely data-driven or rule-based approaches. Its development and evaluation within the DARPA Triage Challenge (DTC) further underscore its relevance to ongoing efforts to advance autonomous medical triage capabilities.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The fundamental motivation for this research stems from the inherent difficulties faced by emergency responders during mass casualty incidents (MCIs). Such events can rapidly generate hundreds of victims, overwhelming conventional manual triage methods, which often lead to life-or-death decisions made under extreme pressure with potentially suboptimal outcomes. Autonomous robotic systems offer a compelling alternative by enabling rapid surveying of disaster zones and remote injury assessment. However, deploying these robots in chaotic and unpredictable MCI environments presents a unique set of severe operational challenges. Robots must contend with cluttered terrains, poor visibility conditions (e.g., smoke, dust), and severe sensor occlusions, resulting in sensory data that is frequently noisy, incomplete, and fragmented. This unreliability of sensory data poses a significant challenge to robust perception and decision-making.

The research identifies three critical limitations in current robotic perception algorithms in this context:
1. **Algorithmic isolation:** Each vital sign or injury indicator is often treated as an independent observation, disregarding potential interdependencies.
2. **Context blindness:** The system fails to integrate clinical context or inter-dependencies between different indicators. For instance, an amputation and detected blood might not be automatically inferred as active hemorrhage without contextual reasoning.
3. **Brittleness:** Perception algorithms often underperform when faced with noisy or incomplete data, which is characteristic of disaster environments.

To address these limitations and enable reliable robotic decision-making in critical scenarios, the primary objective of this work is to develop a cognitive architecture for autonomous robotic triage. This architecture aims to move beyond a collection of independent detectors by facilitating reasoning under uncertainty and fusing partial evidence into a coherent and medically plausible assessment of a casualty's condition.

The key contributions and specific objectives are:
- To introduce a novel knowledge-based cognitive architecture for autonomous robotic triage. This architecture is centered on an expert-elicited Bayesian Network (BN) designed to integrate multimodal perception inputs from various sensors.
- To establish a robust reasoning framework within this architecture that allows the robot to effectively handle noisy, uncertain, and incomplete sensory data, which are characteristic features of real-world disaster environments.
- To validate the developed system through realistic field scenarios, demonstrating a significant improvement in triage accuracy compared to a baseline system that relies solely on direct, independent outputs from perception algorithms.
- To ensure the system's real-time operational capability and memory efficiency, making it suitable for deployment on resource-constrained, field-deployable hardware.

Ultimately, the motivation is to transform autonomous robots from mere data collectors into effective decision-making agents capable of providing reliable, consistent, and interpretable triage decisions in real time, even when faced with significant environmental and data challenges.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the proposed system is a cognitive architecture centered on a Bayesian Network (BN), designed to integrate outputs from multiple perception algorithms into a unified probabilistic estimate of a patient's condition.

**System Architecture and Integration:**
The BN model was constructed and processed using GeNIe Modeler and integrated into the Robot Operating System 2 (ROS 2) stack via a custom interface developed with SMILE. This integration facilitates real-time inference and data exchange within a modern robotic middleware ecosystem. The perception system operates on a robotic platform equipped with a suite of non-contact, stand-off sensors including a high-resolution RGB camera, microphone, radar, lidar, and thermal camera. Individual physiological estimators, implemented as independent AI models specialized in assessing specific physiological signs (e.g., severe hemorrhage, ocular alertness), function as independent ROS 2 nodes. Each estimator publishes time-stamped predictions to a central BN service. Upon signal of scan completion or at a fixed cadence, the BN service triggers inference to impute unobserved variables and generate a triage posterior, which is then returned to a scoring module. This modular, sensor/model-agnostic architecture allows for the flexible swapping or addition of components without modifying the core reasoning layer. The BN treats incoming predictions as uncertain evidence, fusing heterogeneous sources with potentially variable reliability, thereby reducing the impact of errors from any single module and supporting on-the-fly updates as new data become available.

**Expert Knowledge Elicitation and BN Parameterization:**
Due to the absence of standardized datasets for MCI scenarios, the parameterization of the BN, specifically the Conditional Probability Tables (CPTs), was accomplished through a knowledge-driven engineering process. Three domain experts, each with over a decade of experience in emergency medicine, contributed to this process. The methodology involved an iterative cycle:
1. **Initial Interviews:** The engineering team conducted individual interviews with experts to identify critical observable indicators and their high-level relationships relevant to triage.
2. **Drafting Model:** An initial BN structure and preliminary CPTs were drafted, translating qualitative expert statements (e.g., "a lower extremity amputation almost always causes severe hemorrhage") into quantitative probability values.
 - Strong causal relationships were assigned probabilities in the range of 0.8–0.95.
 - Moderate associations were assigned values in the 0.4–0.6 range.
 - Weak dependencies were represented by low probabilities, near baseline prior probabilities. For example, the probability for "Closed" for "Ocular Alertness" when the prior of "Head Trauma" was "Wound" was set to 0.7.
3. **Feedback and Refinement:** The drafted model was presented back to the experts for validation, identification of gaps, and challenging of assumptions. This iterative feedback loop allowed for manual refinement of the CPTs to ensure the model's inferential behavior was consistent with established clinical reasoning.

**Robotic Platform:**
The experimental infrastructure comprised a heterogeneous team of aerial and ground robots operating within a common ROS 2 framework.
- **Aerial Robots (UAVs):** One custom-made platform and one Indago 4 Scout, both equipped with GPS, standard RGB cameras, and thermal cameras. Their primary function was rapid detection, localization, tracking of casualties, and bleeding detection over large or difficult-to-reach areas.
- **Ground Robots (UGVs):** Three Spot-type quadrupedal robots, each equipped with a LiDAR, GPS, multispectral camera, radar, microphone, depth camera, and thermal camera. Their role involved autonomously locating victims on the ground and performing detailed vital sign estimations.
Predictions from different platforms were processed through matching algorithms to aggregate observations corresponding to the same casualty. These fused data streams were then forwarded to the BN for inference and triage profile generation. NVIDIA 64 GB Jetson AGX Orin units were used for computations on the robots.

**Evaluation Environment and Metrics:**
The study was conducted in field tests simulating MCIs with 20 casualties (human volunteers and high-fidelity manikins) within the DARPA Triage Challenge (DTC) environment. This environment featured unstructured terrain, constrained passages, occlusions, and sensory degradation. Casualties were distributed with varied injuries and postures, sometimes partially hidden. System performance was evaluated using a structured scoring framework (Table I), which awarded points for accurately identifying physiological signs and conditions within a "golden time window." The primary objective was to maximize the cumulative score across all casualties. The system's performance was compared between the BN-based cognitive architecture ("Robot + BN") and a baseline system ("Robot") relying on direct outputs from its perception pipeline. Key evaluation metrics included:
- **Reliability:** The ability of the system to provide an assessment even with partial data (diagnostic coverage).
- **Accuracy:** The rate of correct assignments among those attempted.
- **Overall Performance:** The rate of correct assignments across all possible vitals.
- **Real-time performance:** Inference time and memory footprint.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of the system, conducted during the DARPA Triage Challenge (DTC) Round 1 Systems Competition across two distinct scenarios (Run A and Run B) involving 20 casualty cases, demonstrated substantial improvements in autonomous triage capabilities when utilizing the Bayesian reasoning engine.

**Overall System Performance:**
- The integrated "Robot + BN" system significantly increased total scores compared to the "Robot" baseline, which relied solely on direct perception outputs. In Run A, the score more than doubled from 25 to 61. In Run B, the score nearly tripled from 16 to 45. These results indicate the architecture's capacity to overcome the limitations imposed by sensor failures, environmental occlusions, and ambiguous visual cues prevalent in real-world environments.

**Key Performance Metrics (Table III):**
- **Reliability:** The system's diagnostic coverage, or its ability to provide an assessment even with partial data, increased from 0.31 for the baseline to 0.95 for the "Robot + BN" configuration. This indicates that the baseline system failed to make an assessment in 69% of cases, whereas the integrated system provided an output in 95% of them. The authors note that 100% reliability was not achieved due to the inability of the system to locate one casualty in Run B.
- **Accuracy:** The quality of assessments, measured as correct assignments among those attempted, improved from 46% for the baseline to 56% with the BN integration.
- **Overall Performance:** This metric, representing the rate of correct assignments across all possible vitals, showed a nearly four-fold increase, rising from 14% to 53%. This improvement is attributed to the BN's capacity to infer missing information and resolve conflicting perceptual cues, resulting in a 3.84-fold increase in the total number of correctly assigned vitals (from 25 to 96).

**Specific Vital Sign Improvements (Table III):**
The BN integration led to improvements across nearly all assessed vital signs. Notable increases in correct assignments include:
- Severe Hemorrhage: from 6 to 12.
- Respiratory Distress: from 5 to 16.
- Head Trauma: from 0 to 15 (a significant gain).
- Torso Trauma: from 0 to 11 (a significant gain).
- Lower Extremity Trauma: from 8 to 11.
- Upper Extremity Trauma: from 3 to 8.
- Ocular Alertness: from 0 to 8 (a significant gain).
- Motor Alertness: from 0 to 8 (a significant gain).
- Verbal Alertness: from 3 to 7.

**Comparison to Random Classifier (Figure 3):**
The integrated "Robot + BN" system demonstrated enhanced performance across all evaluated vital signs, surpassing a random classifier that uniformly selects from valid labels. In contrast, the baseline "Robot" system either performed at or below the random classifier for several vital signs.

**Real-time Performance:**
The implemented solution achieved inference times of less than 1 millisecond per update and maintained a memory footprint below 100 megabytes. This performance enables deployment on resource-constrained platforms, such as the NVIDIA Jetson series, without compromising the robot's responsiveness.

The team's performance in the DTC, securing 4th place out of 11 teams overall, further supports the effectiveness of the proposed architecture in operationally realistic scenarios.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presents a significant advancement in the field of autonomous robotics for mass casualty incidents by demonstrating that integrating a Bayesian reasoning engine with advanced vision-based sensing substantially improves casualty assessment capabilities in field environments. This approach addresses critical limitations of standard robotic perception pipelines, which often struggle with noisy, incomplete, and conflicting sensory data by explicitly modeling uncertainty and intermodal dependencies.

**Enhanced Reliability and Decision-Making:** The observed nearly three-fold improvement in physiological assessment accuracy and a nearly four-fold increase in overall triage accuracy underscore the BN's ability to fuse fragmented data into coherent and medically plausible assessments. This capability allows the system to emulate human responders' iterative reasoning, but at machine speeds, thereby transforming the robot from a passive data collector into an active decision-making agent.

**Overcoming Perception Limitations:** The cognitive architecture effectively mitigates challenges such as sensor failures, environmental occlusions, and ambiguous visual cues, which are common in disaster scenarios. By establishing clinical constraints, the Bayesian Network resolves contradictions that might arise from isolated perception modules (e.g., correctly identifying an amputation while another module incorrectly reports normal motor alertness), leading to more consistent and medically sound predictions. This systemic robustness is crucial for robots deployed in safety-critical roles.

**Fault Tolerance and Extensibility:** A key impact of this architecture is its inherent fault tolerance. The system exhibits graceful degradation when perceptual data is incomplete, marginalizing over missing variables rather than suffering catastrophic failure. This operational reliability is essential for autonomous systems operating outside controlled laboratory environments. Furthermore, the probabilistic framework is inherently extensible, allowing for the integration of new sensors or improved perception algorithms with minimal modifications to the core reasoning engine. This modularity and future-proofing capability ensures adaptability to evolving technologies in emergency response.

**Real-World Applicability:** The validation of the system during the DARPA Triage Challenge in realistic MCI scenarios confirms its potential for practical deployment. The achievement of sub-millisecond inference times and low memory footprint further ensures its suitability for resource-constrained, field-deployable hardware.

**Future Directions:** The authors identify several avenues for future work, highlighting areas for continued impact:
- **Human-Robot Teaming and Ethical Deployment:** Emphasizing the need for intuitive interfaces to communicate assessments and uncertainties to human operators, ensuring shared situational awareness, collaborative decision-making, and robust safeguards with human-in-the-loop oversight.
- **Robustness Testing:** Acknowledging the critical importance of comprehensive testing to assess and mitigate cascading error propagation in multimodal architectures, which is essential for safety-critical applications.
- **Data Quality and Availability:** Calling for concerted efforts from the research community to collect relevant, high-quality empirical data for machine learning models in MCI scenarios, which currently suffer from a lack of standardized datasets and the stringent data quality requirements of medical triage.

This research represents a pivotal step towards deploying autonomous systems that are not only capable of data collection but also equipped with robust reasoning capabilities for complex, high-stakes real-world scenarios, ultimately aiming to enhance emergency response and improve patient outcomes in mass casualty incidents.
