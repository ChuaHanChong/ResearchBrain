# Memory for Attention: Language-Conditioned Re-Perception with a Vision--Language--Motion Map

> [!summary] Summary
> A framework for robotic re-perception utilizes a Vision-Language-Motion Map (VLMM) to intelligently allocate a robot's attention budget by leveraging the map's memory of object change history. This memory-driven approach reduces value-weighted staleness by up to 26% and improves performance in downstream tasks, proving more effective for dynamic object tracking and language-conditioned re-perception than for basic spatial navigation.

## Problem

- Static maps rapidly become outdated in dynamic real-world environments, hindering persistent robotic operation.
- Robots face limited perception budgets, making continuous re-observation of all map elements infeasible and requiring intelligent attention allocation.
- Existing dynamic mapping and active perception strategies often lack history-driven, targeted re-observation mechanisms that leverage per-instance object dynamics or language-specified task relevance.

## Method

- Develops a theoretical framework for ==attention scheduling== that minimizes value-weighted staleness, deriving a ==√·-law== to optimally set re-observation frequencies based on item importance and change rates.
- Utilizes a **Vision–Language–Motion Map (VLMM)** to estimate per-instance object change rates by accumulating observed change history, contrasting this with a memoryless, category-based prior.
- Integrates ==language conditioning== into the re-perception framework, allowing natural language instructions to assign importance weights and prioritize tracking of task-relevant dynamic objects.

## Results

- History-based re-perception reduced value-weighted staleness by **1.7%** to **6.5%** vs. on-demand VLM priors (up to **+7.7%** on high-importance objects) in synthetic environments, and by **21%** to **26%** vs. a real CLIP zero-shot movability prior, correcting its noisy appearance-based estimates.
- For language-conditioned tasks, VLMM, combining open-vocabulary relevance and observed dynamics, achieved a **2.5%** lower relevance-weighted staleness than a strong relevance-weighted oldest-first baseline.
- In a downstream fetch task, memory scheduling resulted in **4.9%** fewer wasted trips and **4.4%** less excess travel at high heterogeneity, but its advantage was dependent on task type and observation reliability.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A map's accumulated per-instance object change history guides perception resource allocation better than static category priors, especially for heterogeneous object dynamics; this staleness-reduction value is quantitatively proportional to the scene's root-volatility variance (Var(√λ)).
> - Combining language grounding with per-instance dynamics from a VLMM significantly enhances a robot's ability to maintain awareness of task-relevant dynamic objects, outperforming simpler recency or memoryless VLM approaches.
> - Behavior-annotated maps are primarily valuable for informing intelligent attention and perception resource management, showing limited practical benefit for improving traditional spatial navigation tasks under closed-loop execution.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robotic agents operating in real-world environments frequently encounter dynamic scenes where objects are moved, doors are opened, and items are rearranged. Traditional static maps, which record only object locations and identities, become outdated rapidly in such contexts. This limitation has motivated the development of more sophisticated mapping paradigms.

One line of research focuses on **open-vocabulary maps**, which enable robots to ground language-specified landmarks and concepts to spatial locations. Examples include VLMaps for localizing language-specified landmarks, and ConceptFusion, ConceptGraphs, and HOV-SG which attach features to voxels or object graphs. However, these approaches often assume static environments.

A separate body of work incorporates **behavioral or dynamic information** into maps. Khronos constructs spatio-temporal maps, while Dewan et al. classify LiDAR points based on their movability. Vision–Language–Motion Maps (VLMM) combine both aspects, annotating map elements with open-vocabulary features, a movability prior, an observed cross-frame motion score, and per-element confidence. This current work builds directly on VLMM.

The problem of a robot's limited perception budget necessitates **active perception** strategies, which involve deciding where and when to look. Related areas include next-best-view planning, and persistent monitoring, which aim to maintain observation freshness based on geometric coverage or known dynamics. Dynamic and lifelong mapping approaches perform re-perception and re-mapping, but typically rely on fixed schedules or coverage sweeps rather than history-driven attention. The decision of *when* to re-observe is also conceptually related to problems in web crawling, restless multi-armed bandits, age-of-information scheduling, and change-rate estimation. This paper positions its contribution within this landscape by focusing on how a map's accumulated history of change can inform targeted re-perception, differentiating it from approaches that rely solely on geometric coverage, fixed schedules, or static categorical priors.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The research is motivated by two distinct planning questions a robot faces when equipped with a persistent, behavior-annotated map:

1. **Spatial-navigation question**: "How to walk around a room?" This involves using behavioral fields (e.g., movability information) to shape path planning, for instance, by avoiding regions where objects frequently move. The initial objective of the paper was to investigate if a behavior-aware planner could significantly reduce planning-time objectives and provide tangible benefits under closed-loop execution.

2. **Resource-allocation question**: "What should the robot attend to right now to keep its own map fresh, given a limited perception budget?" This question frames re-perception as an attention decision—specifically, which map elements to re-observe and with what frequency. The motivation here is that a robot cannot continuously re-observe every object due to sensing, travel, or computational constraints. Therefore, an intelligent scheduling mechanism is required to prioritize re-observations. The paper hypothesizes that a persistent map's memory, particularly its recorded change history or recency of last sighting, would be more effective for this task than a memoryless on-demand vision-language model (VLM) which only provides category-level movability.

The central hypothesis guiding this work is that while a behavior-annotated map might offer marginal benefits for spatial navigation under closed-loop execution, its distinctive value lies in informing resource-allocation decisions for perception. The paper aims to demonstrate that the map "earns its keep not by telling the robot how to walk around a room, but by telling it what to pay attention to." A further objective is to explore how language conditioning can be integrated into this re-perception framework, allowing a robot to prioritize keeping track of task-relevant objects specified by natural language instructions.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research adopts a theoretical and empirical approach to evaluate the utility of a behavior-annotated map for perception resource allocation.

**Theoretical Formulation of Attention Decision:**
The core problem is formulated as minimizing value-weighted staleness under a perception budget.
- **Map Elements:** A map contains N elements, with each element *i* changing according to a Poisson process with rate λ_i.
- **Staleness:** If element *i* is re-observed every τ_i steps, its time-average staleness (probability of being outdated) is approximated as g(λ_i τ_i) ≈ ½ λ_i τ_i for small λ_i τ_i (Eq. 1).
- **Importance Weights:** Each element *i* has an importance weight *w_i* (e.g., fragility, task relevance).
- **Perception Budget:** The robot has a budget of K re-observations per step. The policy chooses re-check frequencies f_i = 1/τ_i such that Σ_i f_i = K.
- **Objective:** Minimize total value-weighted staleness S = Σ_i w_i g(λ_i τ_i).

**The √·-law Attention Schedule:**
- **Proposition 1:** Minimizing S yields an optimal frequency f*_i ∝ √(w_i λ_i), resulting in an optimal staleness S* = (Σ_i √(w_i λ_i))^2 / (2K).
- **Proposition 2:** The realized cost S(λ̂) with an estimated rate λ̂_i is always greater than or equal to S*, with equality only if λ̂_i ∝ λ_i. The excess is a Cauchy–Schwarz defect that increases as λ̂ diverges from λ.
- **Corollary 1:** For uniform importance, the difference between a memoryless uniform scanning policy and the optimal √·-law policy is proportional to Var(√λ), the variance of the root-volatility. This implies that memory's value is directly tied to the heterogeneity of the scene's dynamics.

**Rate Estimation:**
Two main sources for estimating λ_i are compared:
1. **On-demand VLM (Memoryless Prior):** This baseline uses a category movability prior ρ_i, which provides a single rate value per object type, lacking per-instance history.
2. **Map's Memory (Observed History):** This method accumulates observed changes (m_i) over observed time (t_i) for each element and forms a Bayesian rate estimate λ̂_i = (m_i + κρ_i) / (t_i + κ), where κ is a pseudo-count starting at the prior.

**Heterogeneity Model:**
To systematically evaluate memory's benefit, per-instance heterogeneity *h* ∈ is introduced: λ_i = λ_max [(1-h)ρ_i + h u_i], where u_i ~ U(0, 1).
- h=0: All instances equal their category prior (memory adds no information).
- h=1: Instance rates are independent of category (only history can inform them).

**Competitive Schedulers:**
Beyond the VLM prior and history-based √·-law, other schedulers are evaluated:
- **Oldest-First:** Observes K highest w_i · age_i (memory-light, no rate model).
- **Weighted Round-Robin:** Distributes budget among weighted items.
- **Thompson Sampling:** A model-free learner with a Gamma posterior on λ_i.
- **Whittle Index Policy:** A more theoretically optimal scheduler for the restless multi-armed bandit formulation of the problem, used as a reference to remove the "schedule floor" observed with the √·-law heuristic.

**Experimental Setup and Evaluation:**
- **Simulation Environment:** AI2-THOR is used for scene generation and object interaction.
- **Object Instances:** 951 movable objects from 40 scenes for general attention studies; 322 objects from 8 scenes for real CLIP prior; 159 objects from 8 queries for language conditioning; 25 live AI2-THOR scenes for the downstream fetch task.
- **Evaluation Metric:** Value-weighted staleness.
- **Held-out Evaluation:** To isolate memory's value, change history is accumulated on one realization of the process, frozen, and applied as a static schedule to an independent realization. This removes benefits from online adaptation or overfitting.
- **Real CLIP Prior:** CLIP is used to compute zero-shot movability priors from rendered object images, replacing the abstract category movability table.
- **Observation Noise & Reliability:** Experiments include simulating observation errors (bit-flips) and integrating real AI2-THOR object detectability rates.
- **Downstream Task:** A fetch task is used to measure the practical impact of fresher memory (wasted trips, excess travel).
- **Spatial Navigation Evaluation:** VLMM's utility for path-shaping is assessed by encoding its fields as a planner cost and evaluating against replans in closed-loop execution.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

**A. Memory Directs Attention Better Than an On-Demand Prior (Held-out Evaluation):**
- Across 951 objects, the history-based policy reduced value-weighted staleness by 1.7% at h=0, increasing to 6.5% at h=1 compared to the on-demand VLM prior (Table I).
- The memory-attributable effect (net of the h=0 schedule floor) ranged from 0% at h=0 to approximately 4.9% at h=1 (Table I, Fig. 2).
- Memory's benefit was concentrated on important objects: for the top-10%-importance objects, the memory-attributable reduction reached +7.7% at h=1, compared to +4.9% on the mean.

**B. Performance Against Competitive Schedulers:**
- The on-demand VLM prior was consistently outperformed; even a memory-light oldest-first scheduler beat it at every heterogeneity level (Fig. 3).
- History-based policies (Thompson sampling and the √·-law memory policy) performed comparably to an oracle, demonstrating their effectiveness.
- Memory's advantage over the best memory-light scheduler (oldest-first) was conditional, ranging from +0.3% to +3.8% under skewed importance, but it lost under uniform importance until h=1.

**C. Whittle-Index Scheduler and Robustness:**
- The Whittle index policy, a more theoretically optimal scheduler, achieved 4.1% lower staleness with true rates than the √·-law (Table II), confirming the √·-law as a heuristic.
- Using the Whittle index, the memory policy's advantage over the prior became clearer: it was negative at h=0 (-1.2%) and rose to +4.5% at h=1, with no schedule floor to subtract, as memory only benefited when heterogeneity was present (Table II).
- The memory-attributable reduction remained positive and significant (+0.9% to +2.2%) across different change processes (bursty, correlated, diurnal) (Fig. 4).
- Sensitivity analysis showed the advantage increased with observation horizon (+0.9% to +2.0%), importance skew (+1.2% to +2.9%), and budget (+0.1% to +5.4%) (Table III).
- The memory advantage was robust to moderate observation noise (up to ϵ=0.2) but degraded significantly with high noise, indicating a corrupted history can be worse than a category average.

**D. Freshness Gap Tracks Root-Volatility Variance:**
- The memory reduction was linearly correlated (R^2=0.99) with Var(√λ), the variance of the scene's root-volatility, as predicted by Corollary 1 (Fig. 5). This quantitatively confirms that memory's value stems from the heterogeneity of object dynamics beyond what a category prior can explain.

**E. Real Vision-Language Prior (AI2-THOR + CLIP):**
- Against a real CLIP zero-shot movability prior (which correlated only r=0.40 with ground truth), the map's observed-change memory yielded a substantial staleness reduction: +21% at h=0, rising to +26% at h=1 (Table IV).
- The 21% base advantage at h=0 was attributed to memory correcting CLIP's noisy appearance-based estimates. The additional +4.8% rise from h=0 to h=1 represented the appearance-independent per-instance heterogeneity effect.
- A controlled sweep of prior quality (Fig. 6) showed that while a more accurate category VLM would reduce the base advantage, the heterogeneity slope (∼5%) remained constant, indicating the unique value of tracking instance usage.

**F. Language-Conditioned Re-Perception:**
- For language-conditioned tasks (setting importance w_i by CLIP relevance), a VLMM using both open-vocabulary relevance and observed dynamics (lang×rate) achieved the lowest relevance-weighted staleness (Fig. 7).
- VLMM beat a strong relevance-weighted oldest-first baseline (lang×recency) by +2.5% (t=7.4), indicating the motion channel added value beyond a last-seen timestamp.
- Dropping language grounding (motion-oldest, no query) resulted in +38% worse performance, and an on-demand VLM (relevance × category prior) was +8.9% worse. This demonstrated the necessity of both language grounding and per-instance dynamics for language-conditioned re-perception.

**G. Downstream Fetch Task:**
- Memory-scheduling resulted in +4.9% fewer wasted trips and +4.4% less excess travel at h=1 compared to the on-demand prior.
- However, at moderate heterogeneity (h=0.5), memory tied the prior, and the memory-light oldest-first heuristic sometimes matched or beat memory on this generic task. This suggests that for general fetch tasks without language conditioning, sophisticated rate modeling might not always be necessary over simpler recency.

**H. Real Perception Reliability:**
- The memory advantage was conditional on observation reliability. With real AI2-THOR object detectability, memory outperformed the prior by +2% to +2.4% when mean detectability was 0.3 or higher.
- At a pessimistic random-viewpoint detectability of 0.15, the advantage reversed to -2.8% (Table V), implying that unreliable observations corrupt memory's rate estimates, making a fixed category prior more robust.

**I. Spatial-Navigation Negative:**
- Encoding VLMM's fields for path-shaping reduced a planning-time objective by ∼35%.
- However, under closed-loop execution, the reduction in real replans was only ∼4% (+14% travel), and observed history performed no better than an on-demand prior. This result indicates that for spatial navigation, reactive replanning and regional aggregation of costs minimize the impact of per-instance dynamic information, limiting the value of map memory in this context.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of robotics by re-evaluating the utility of behavior-annotated maps, shifting the focus from spatial navigation to perception resource allocation. The findings suggest that a map's memory, specifically its accumulated change history, provides a significant advantage in deciding what a robot should attend to, especially under budget constraints.

The quantitative demonstration that memory's benefit is proportional to the variance of the scene's root-volatility (Var(√λ)) offers a theoretically grounded explanation for when and why dynamic map memory is valuable. This "heterogeneity slice" of the advantage is independent of the quality of an initial category prior. This understanding can guide the design of future robotic systems, suggesting that investing in per-instance change tracking is most impactful in environments with diverse and unpredictable object dynamics.

The work's most distinctive contribution lies in validating the role of behavior-annotated maps for **language-conditioned re-perception**. By enabling open-vocabulary relevance grounding combined with per-instance dynamics, the VLMM system demonstrated superior performance over both static open-vocabulary maps and memoryless VLM approaches. This capability is crucial for robots that need to maintain situational awareness of specific, task-relevant objects identified through natural language. The finding that the motion channel adds value beyond a simple "last-seen" timestamp is significant, indicating that explicit modeling of change rates (volatility) is beneficial.

The insights gained are applicable to various robotic applications, including:
- **Long-term autonomy:** Robots operating in human environments could use memory-driven re-perception to maintain fresh information about critical objects.
- **Human-robot interaction:** Language-conditioned attention allows for more intuitive tasking and dynamic prioritization of map elements.
- **Efficient sensing:** By guiding attention, robots can optimize their use of limited sensing and computational resources, extending operational time or reducing energy consumption.

While the magnitude of the improvement is sometimes modest (e.g., 1-5% in mean staleness, or 2.5% in language-conditioned tasks), its statistical significance and concentration on important objects highlight a robust capability. The research also provides important caveats, such as the dependency on observation reliability, suggesting areas for future work in robust perception. Ultimately, the paper reframes the primary value of sophisticated dynamic maps as tools for intelligent attention and resource management, rather than solely for enhancing traditional path planning.
