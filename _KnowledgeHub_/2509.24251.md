# Latent Visual Reasoning

> [!summary] Summary
> Latent Visual Reasoning (LVR) enables Multimodal Large Language Models to perform autoregressive reasoning directly within a shared visual-semantic embedding space, alternating with text generation. This approach substantially improves performance on tasks requiring fine-grained visual understanding and perception, outperforming methods that rely solely on textual reasoning or external visual tools.

## Problem

- Most MLLMs primarily conduct reasoning in the language space, leading to a 'semantic gap' where crucial visual nuances relevant to a query may be missed.
- Visual information, once encoded, often remains static during the reasoning process, and even tool-augmented methods treat visual input as something external to be pre-processed or re-encoded.
- Reliance on external visual tools for 'thinking with images' introduces rigidity, overhead, and bypasses the potential for MLLMs to inherently manipulate or explore visual semantics.

## Method

- LVR introduces a =='latent reasoning mode'== triggered by special tokens, allowing the MLLM to propagate its last hidden states as input embeddings to reconstruct visual semantics relevant to the query.
- A two-stage training pipeline is employed: Supervised Fine-tuning (SFT) uses ==visual reconstruction loss== and next-token prediction loss with annotated regions of interest to teach latent visual reasoning.
- Reinforcement Learning (RL) with an adapted ==GRPO_latent algorithm== refines the LVR process, using text-based accuracy rewards and a format reward to encourage and optimize latent visual reasoning without explicit intermediate visual supervision.

## Results

- LVR achieved state-of-the-art performance, demonstrating significant gains on perception-intensive benchmarks, including a **2.7%** improvement on V*D.A. and a **5.3%** improvement on V*R.P. subsets.
- The method consistently outperformed both 'Thinking with Images' (e.g., PixelReasoner) and 'Thinking about Images' (e.g., PAPO) paradigms across various visual tasks like Counting, IQ-Test, JigSaw, and Spatial Relation.
- Reinforcement Learning with GRPO_latent further boosted performance over the SFT stage, and the 'Fixed Token' decoding strategy for LVR mode was found to be the most stable and effective.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Enabling MLLMs to actively reason over visual information by reconstructing latent visual thoughts in a shared embedding space is more effective than indirectly referencing it via textual Chain-of-Thought or external tool calls.
> - The core LLM can natively align visual and textual semantics in the joint latent space, as adding extra architectural heads for latent reasoning did not yield superior performance.
> - Reinforcement learning, guided by explicit format rewards for activating latent reasoning, successfully encourages and refines the model's ability to perform internal visual computations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper addresses a fundamental limitation in current Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) and proposes a significant paradigm shift in how they process and reason with visual information. The existing landscape of MLLM reasoning can be broadly categorized into two main approaches, which the authors term "Thinking about Images" and "Thinking with Images."

**"Thinking about Images"** refers to approaches that primarily perform Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning within the language space. While these models can process visual inputs via encoders, the actual reasoning steps—decomposition of queries, intermediate thought generation—occur as structured text. Visual information is treated as a static precondition, influencing the text generation but not directly participating in the *reasoning process itself*. Examples include various MLLMs that leverage textual CoT for tasks like scientific VQA or mathematics. The limitation here is that excessive text generation can overshadow crucial visual inputs, and reasoning in text space is an indirect representation of visual understanding.

**"Thinking with Images"** extends the above by incorporating external visual tools during textual reasoning. This allows MLLMs to actively edit or manipulate input images (e.g., cropping, zooming, highlighting, OCR) and re-encode the enhanced visual output back into the reasoning trajectory. While this enriches the visual signal, these methods are constrained by the availability and predefined operations of external tools. They often bypass newly injected sub-images or face difficulties in extending tool APIs, and many fundamental operations could potentially be solved internally by MLLMs. This approach still involves an "external loop" of tool invocation and re-encoding, rather than inherent visual reasoning.

A parallel and emerging area is **Omni-modality Foundation Models**, which aim to generate outputs in both text and visual modalities simultaneously. While promising for certain tasks like navigation, their effectiveness in faithful preservation of original visual information during decode-re-encode cycles remains unclear for general reasoning.

The "LATENT VISUAL REASONING (LVR)" paradigm proposed by Li et al. directly challenges these existing paradigms by asking: "If visual and textual tokens are embedded in a joint semantics space within an MLLM, why not reasoning over both jointly as well?" This question identifies a critical gap: despite sophisticated visual encoders, MLLMs often struggle to capture fine-grained visual details directly relevant to a query, leading to issues like modality projection bias or cross-modality attention bias.

LVR's contribution is to enable **autoregressive reasoning directly in the visual embedding space**, thereby integrating visual and textual signals more deeply throughout the model's reasoning process. Instead of solely relying on text for CoT or external tools for visual manipulation, LVR allows the Language Model (LLM) to generate *latent states* that reconstruct key visual tokens necessary for answering a query. This positions LVR as a significant step towards more integrated and potentially human-like multimodal reasoning, moving beyond indirect textual or tool-dependent approaches to a truly unified reasoning process within the MLLM's latent space. It addresses the core limitation of previous methods by allowing visual information to be *actively reasoned over* rather than just *referenced* or *modified externally*.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation behind Latent Visual Reasoning (LVR) stems from the inherent limitations of current MLLMs in truly understanding and reasoning with visual details. While MLLMs have advanced considerably in multimodal comprehension, their reasoning mechanisms are often constrained:

1. **Language-Centric Reasoning:** Most MLLMs perform reasoning primarily in the language space, even when addressing visual queries. This means visual information is encoded and projected into a linguistic context, but the iterative "thinking" process (Chain-of-Thought) happens through text token generation. This indirect approach can lead to a "semantic gap," where the LLM might fail to capture crucial visual nuances or details most relevant to the query.
2. **Static Visual Input:** In many current setups, visual information, once encoded, remains largely static throughout the reasoning process. Even approaches that use external tools to "edit" images still treat the visual input as something to be pre-processed or re-encoded, rather than a dynamic space for internal reasoning. This bypasses the potential for the LLM itself to directly manipulate or explore visual semantics internally.
3. **Tool Dependency and Constraints:** "Thinking with Images" approaches, while effective, rely on predefined external tools (e.g., for cropping, zooming). These tools are often rigid, difficult to extend, and introduce an overhead of tool invocation and re-encoding. Many fundamental visual operations could ideally be handled intrinsically by the MLLM if it could reason directly in the visual space.

**The core objective of LVR is to overcome these limitations by enabling MLLMs to perform autoregressive reasoning directly within a shared visual-semantic embedding space.** Specifically, the paper aims to:

- **Unify Reasoning:** Create a novel paradigm where reasoning seamlessly alternates between generating text tokens and reconstructing visual semantics in a joint latent space.
- **Enhance Fine-Grained Perception:** Improve MLLMs' ability to understand subtle visual details and perform perception-intensive tasks by allowing them to "think visually" and reconstruct query-relevant visual information internally.
- **Reduce Reliance on External Tools:** Demonstrate that direct reasoning in the visual representation space can be more effective and scalable than depending on external visual-editing tools.
- **Develop a Stable Training Framework:** Introduce an architectural and training pipeline that allows for stable and scalable training of MLLMs capable of LVR, combining supervised fine-tuning with reinforcement learning.

By achieving these objectives, LVR seeks to bridge the fundamental gap between visual inputs and text generation, fostering a deeper, more integrated cross-modal understanding that mimics human visual reasoning more closely.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Latent Visual Reasoning (LVR) paradigm introduces a novel approach to multimodal reasoning by enabling an MLLM to perform hybrid reasoning that alternates between generating text and reasoning directly in the visual embedding space.

#### 3.1. Architecture Overview

LVR is built upon a standard MLLM architecture, specifically the Qwen-2.5-VL series, comprising three key components:
- **Vision Encoder (`vision(·)`):** Transforms an input image (`Xv`) into a sequence of visual features (`V`).
- **Multimodal Projector (`proj(·)`):** Maps these visual features (`V`) into a shared semantic space aligned with the language model, producing `VT = proj(V)`.
- **LLM Backbone (`θ(·)`):** The core language model that processes both textual and projected visual features.

The key innovation is the introduction of a "latent reasoning mode." When the LLM generates a special `<|lvr_start|>` token, it enters this mode. Instead of producing discrete text tokens, the model's last hidden states are directly propagated as input embeddings to subsequent positions. These hidden states are trained to reconstruct visual semantics relevant to the query. Upon generating the `<|lvr_end|>` token, the model exits LVR mode and resumes standard text generation.

#### 3.2. Two-Stage Training Pipeline

##### 3.2.1. Stage 1: Supervised Fine-tuning (SFT)

The initial stage aims to teach the MLLM the fundamental pattern of latent visual reasoning using a teacher-forcing approach.
- **Data:** The `VISUAL COT` dataset is used, which provides image-question pairs along with pre-annotated bounding boxes of regions of interest (ROIs) critical for answering the query.
- **Process:**
 1. For an input image, the image processor crops it into a grid of visual patches.
 2. Based on the ROI bounding box, LVR efficiently selects the corresponding patches and retrieves their indices.
 3. During the forward pass, the image is encoded, followed by text tokens. A subset of visual embeddings corresponding to the ROI is gathered.
 4. These selected visual embeddings are then "enclosed" by the special `<|lvr_start|>` and `<|lvr_end|>` tokens, defining the LVR process.
 5. The remaining textual response is appended for generation.
- **Joint Learning Objectives:**
 - **Visual Reconstruction Loss (`LLVR`):** An MSE loss is applied to the final hidden states generated during the LVR phase, forcing them to approximate the ground-truth visual embeddings of the ROI. This explicitly supervises the model to "reason about" the correct visual semantics.
 - **Next-Token Prediction (NTP) Loss (`LNTP`):** A standard cross-entropy loss is used for the subsequent language modeling phase, maximizing the likelihood of the ground-truth textual response.
 - **Joint Objective (`L`):** The overall loss is a weighted sum: `L = LNTP + λLVR · LLVR`, where `λLVR` balances the two objectives.
- **Training Details:** The visual encoder and multimodal projector are kept frozen, updating only the LLM parameters. An adaptive multimodal data-packing strategy is used to handle variable instance lengths.

##### 3.2.2. Stage 2: Reinforcement Learning (RL)

This stage refines the LVR process, allowing the model to self-evolve its latent reasoning without explicit supervision of intermediate visual outputs.
- **Algorithm:** The authors adapt the GRPO algorithm, proposing `GRPO_latent`, which is designed for latent-reasoning models.
- **Challenge and Solution:** The main challenge is that policy-gradient loss is defined over token distributions, while latent reasoning lacks an explicit token distribution. `GRPO_latent` addresses this by replaying the recorded last hidden states of LVR processes (`e_h_latent`) during the computation of token-wise importance ratios. This ensures consistent conditional log-probabilities for the output sequence, even when the intermediate reasoning is latent.
- **Rewards:** Rewards are computed solely from the text output, with two types:
 - **Format Reward:** +1 if the response includes both `<|lvr_start|>` and `<|lvr_end|>` tokens, 0 otherwise. This encourages the activation of the LVR process.
 - **Accuracy Reward:** +1 if the final answer is correct, 0 otherwise. This indirectly supervises the quality of latent reasoning through its impact on the final textual answer.
- **Data:** `ViRL` is used as the training data for this stage.

#### 3.3. Decoding Strategies

Decoding in LVR presents a challenge: determining when to exit the LVR mode. Three strategies were explored:
- **Fixed Token:** A constant budget of reasoning steps is assigned. Once reached, the model exits LVR. (Found to be the best performer).
- **Latent End Token:** A trainable tensor in the hidden state space. When the last hidden state approaches this tensor, the model exits LVR. (Found to be unstable).
- **Mode Switching Loss:** An auxiliary BCE loss during SFT encourages the final latent reasoning token distribution towards `<|lvr_end|>`. (Failed to work as intended).

This comprehensive methodology, combining targeted SFT with an adapted RL framework, aims to make LVR a stable and effective paradigm for multimodal reasoning.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted by Li et al. demonstrate that Latent Visual Reasoning (LVR) substantially improves MLLM performance, particularly on tasks requiring fine-grained visual understanding and perception.

#### 4.1. Superior Performance on Perception-Intensive Tasks

- LVR achieves state-of-the-art results across most evaluated benchmarks, especially showing significant gains on `V*` and `MMVP`.
- On the `V*D.A.` (Visual Detail Search) subset, LVR improved the base model by **2.7%**.
- On the `V*R.P.` (Relative Spatial Reasoning) subset, it improved performance by **5.3%**.
- For `MMVP`, which tests perception robustness under subtle image perturbations, LVR demonstrates strong performance, surpassing the base `Qwen2.5-VL` model (71.67% vs. 66.67%). This highlights LVR's ability to reconstruct target visual semantics and accurately identify differences.

#### 4.2. Outperformance Against Existing Paradigms

- **"Thinking with Images" Comparison:** LVR outperforms `PixelReasoner` (which relies on external tools like image cropping) in both `V*` and `MMVP` categories. This critical finding underscores that *internally reconstructing visual semantics* (LVR) can be more effective than *depending on external visual-editing tools* for fine-grained visual understanding.
- **"Thinking about Images" Comparison:** Baselines like `PAPO` and `Vision-R1`, which employ text-space Chain-of-Thought (CoT), showed degraded performance on `V*`. This supports the authors' hypothesis that textual-space CoT can introduce cross-modal interference and weaken visual perception, an issue LVR circumvents by reasoning jointly across modalities.

#### 4.3. Effectiveness Across Diverse Visual Tasks

- LVR achieved leading results on `Counting` (object enumeration), `IQ-Test` (geometry puzzles), `JigSaw` (image reconstruction), and `Spatial Relation` (object-relation understanding). These results indicate that LVR effectively unifies capabilities like object detection, visual-dependent logical reasoning, visual reconstruction, and spatial relation understanding within a single framework.

#### 4.4. Reinforcement Learning Further Boosts Performance

- The `GRP O_latent` framework, applied in the RL stage, further enhances LVR performance beyond the SFT stage across multiple benchmarks (Table 2).
- The inclusion of a "format reward" based on the `<|lvr_start|>` and `<|lvr_end|>` tokens was crucial, effectively encouraging the model to perform latent visual reasoning. Removing these trigger tokens destabilized training and led to degraded performance with purely textual responses.

#### 4.5. Ablation Studies and Architectural Insights

- **LVR with Heads:** Experiments with adding MLP or GLU heads on top of the latent reasoning positions showed that the *standard LVR approach (without an extra head)* consistently achieved the best performance. This suggests that the LLM natively aligns visual and textual semantics in the joint space, making an additional head unnecessary.
- **Decoding Strategies:** The "Fixed Token" decoding strategy (assigning a constant budget of reasoning steps) proved to be the most effective. Alternative strategies like "Latent End Token" and "Mode Switching Loss" exhibited significant instability and failed to encode stopping conditions reliably. This points to a need for further research into stable variable-length latent reasoning.

#### 4.6. Identified Limitation

- LVR did not achieve top performance on `Relative Reflect` tasks. The authors attribute this to a distribution shift, as this task requires reasoning over multiple images, while LVR was trained exclusively on single-image data.

In summary, LVR demonstrates compelling evidence that enabling MLLMs to reason directly in the visual embedding space addresses key limitations of prior approaches, leading to superior performance in tasks demanding fine-grained visual perception and holistic multimodal understanding.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Latent Visual Reasoning (LVR) paradigm introduced by Li et al. represents a significant advancement in Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs), with profound implications for the future of AI perception and reasoning.

#### 5.1. Paradigm Shift in Multimodal Reasoning

The most significant contribution of LVR is its proposal of a novel paradigm that moves beyond the established "Thinking about Images" (text-only CoT) and "Thinking with Images" (external tool-augmented) approaches. By enabling MLLMs to perform autoregressive reasoning directly in a shared latent visual-semantic embedding space, LVR fosters a deeper, more integrated fusion of modalities. This represents a fundamental shift from *referencing* or *externally manipulating* visual information to *internally reasoning* over it, paving the way for more intrinsically multimodal AI systems.

#### 5.2. Enhanced Fine-Grained Visual Understanding

LVR demonstrates substantial gains in perception-intensive tasks, particularly those requiring fine-grained visual detail search, relative spatial reasoning, and robustness to subtle image perturbations (e.g., `V*` and `MMVP` benchmarks). This capability is crucial for real-world applications where precise visual interpretation is paramount, such as medical image analysis, autonomous driving, quality control in manufacturing, and detailed visual question answering. The ability to reconstruct relevant visual semantics directly within the model offers a more robust and accurate approach than previous methods.

#### 5.3. Increased Autonomy and Efficiency for MLLMs

By approximating question-relevant visual tokens directly in the visual representation space, LVR reduces the reliance on external tools. This makes MLLMs more self-contained, versatile, and potentially more efficient, as it bypasses the overheads associated with tool invocation, re-encoding, and the constraints of predefined operations. The model learns to perform complex visual-centric tasks internally, fostering a more autonomous reasoning capability.

#### 5.4. Foundations for More Human-like Visual Cognition

The concept of "latent visual thoughts," where query-relevant visual semantics are mentally reconstructed, draws an analogy to human cognitive processes. Humans often "think visually" before articulating a response. LVR's approach takes a step towards mimicking this internal visual reasoning, potentially leading to AI systems that understand and interact with the visual world in a more intuitive and less literal manner.

#### 5.5. Robust Training Framework for Hybrid Reasoning

The two-stage training pipeline, combining supervised fine-tuning with an adapted reinforcement learning (GRPO_latent) framework, provides a stable and scalable method for integrating LVR. The use of a format reward to encourage latent reasoning steps is a clever mechanism for guiding the model towards this new reasoning style. While challenges remain (e.g., stable variable-length decoding), the proposed framework offers a solid foundation for future research.

#### 5.6. Future Research Directions

LVR opens up several exciting avenues for future research, including:
- Improving decoding strategies for stable variable-length latent reasoning.
- Extending LVR to multi-image reasoning tasks and other multimodal inputs beyond static images (e.g., video).
- Exploring the interpretability of these latent visual thoughts and how they correlate with human visual cognition.
- Applying LVR to generative tasks, where the model could potentially "visualize" and generate images based on its internal latent reasoning.

In conclusion, "LATENT VISUAL REASONING" represents a significant conceptual and empirical leap forward in MLLM development. By unifying latent visual reasoning with text generation, it addresses critical limitations of current systems, enhances fine-grained perception, and paves the way for more autonomous, efficient, and sophisticated multimodal AI.
