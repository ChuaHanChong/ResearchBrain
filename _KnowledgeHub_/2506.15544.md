# Stable Gradients for Stable Learning at Scale in Deep Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Mila - Québec AI Institute and Google DeepMind investigated how gradient flow degradation in deep reinforcement learning (RL) hinders scaling neural network capacity in non-stationary environments. They demonstrated that targeted interventions, such as multi-skip residual connections and Kronecker-factored optimization, stabilize gradients, enabling larger RL models to achieve superior performance across various tasks and algorithms.

## Problem

- Deep reinforcement learning (deep RL) struggles to effectively scale neural networks, often leading to performance degradation, unlike supervised and generative learning.
- The inherent non-stationarity of RL training environments (policy-dependent data, changing targets) and the 'deadly triad' intensify gradient pathologies like vanishing gradients, dormant neurons, and ill-conditioned Jacobians.
- Existing solutions for deep RL instability are often complex and do not fully elucidate or address the fundamental causes of scaling difficulties at the gradient propagation level.

## Method

- Empirically diagnosed ==gradient pathologies== (e.g., vanishing gradients, dormant neurons, reduced representational expressivity, flat loss landscapes) in scaled CNNs and MLPs within both non-stationary supervised learning and deep RL settings.
- Proposed and evaluated Multi-Skip Residuals, an architectural modification that broadcasts flattened convolutional features directly to all subsequent MLP layers, ensuring robust gradient flow.
- Introduced Kronecker-factored optimization (Kron) as a second-order optimizer to provide curvature-aware preconditioning for more stable and directionally informed updates in non-stationary environments.

## Results

- Multi-skip residuals and the Kron optimizer prevented performance collapse in PQN on Atari-10 and achieved a median relative improvement of **83.27%** across the full ALE suite.
- The combined gradient interventions improved PPO performance by a median of **31.40%** on ALE and enabled stable, superior learning for larger models in continuous control tasks (e.g., Isaac Gym) where baselines collapsed.
- The proposed interventions enhanced scalability and performance for actor-critic frameworks (Simba with SAC/DDPG on DeepMind Control Suite) and various offline Q-learning algorithms, notably improving performance in sparse-reward environments and increasing robustness to task difficulty.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Gradient flow progressively degrades across network depth in non-stationary deep RL environments, serving as a primary cause for performance collapse when scaling network capacity.
> - Training pathologies such as dormant neurons, reduced representational expressivity (lower SRank), and poorly conditioned loss landscapes (near-zero Hessian trace) emerge and intensify with increased network depth.
> - Simple, targeted interventions that actively encourage robust gradient propagation can stabilize learning and unlock the performance benefits of larger models in deep RL, across diverse algorithms and tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Recent advancements in deep reinforcement learning (deep RL) have enabled deep neural networks to address complex decision-making tasks across various domains, including robotics, game playing, and resource optimization. Inspired by the successes of scaling architectures in supervised and generative learning, where larger models have demonstrated improved representation quality and generalization, researchers have explored similar scaling strategies in deep RL.

However, the current literature indicates that scaling neural networks in deep RL often presents fundamental challenges, frequently leading to performance degradation. Unlike supervised learning, which typically operates on fixed data distributions, deep RL involves policy-dependent data that continuously changes throughout training, introducing significant non-stationarity. This dynamic environment, coupled with the recurrent nature of value-based methods (bootstrapping), can compound estimation errors, particularly in scenarios with sparse or delayed rewards, leading to unstable updates, policy collapse, or value divergence.

Deep networks generally face issues such as vanishing or exploding gradients, ill-conditioned Jacobians, and activation saturation. In deep RL, these pathologies are intensified by the "deadly triad" (the combination of bootstrapping, off-policy learning, and function approximation), off-policy corrections, and continually shifting targets. As networks increase in size, the risk of signal distortion and misalignment grows, potentially resulting in underutilized capacity and brittle learning.

Prior work has addressed aspects of this instability through various mechanisms, but these approaches are often complex and do not always elucidate the underlying causes of the scaling difficulties. One area that has received less attention in deep RL, compared to supervised learning, is the precise mechanism of gradient propagation through the network layers. While gradient signal preservation is recognized in supervised learning, its role in deep RL, where both inputs and targets are in constant flux, remains less understood. This paper positions itself within this landscape by empirically analyzing the interaction between gradient decomposition, non-stationarity, and network scaling in deep RL, aiming to diagnose and intervene upon the identified gradient pathologies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to investigate the mechanisms through which gradient flow is compromised in deep reinforcement learning when network capacity is scaled. The authors hypothesize that the difficulty in scaling deep RL networks and the associated performance degradation stem from a combination of the inherent non-stationarity of RL training environments and architectural vulnerabilities that lead to gradient pathologies.

Specifically, the motivations and objectives include:
- **Diagnosing the root causes:** To empirically analyze and demonstrate that in non-stationary settings characteristic of deep RL—where targets are bootstrapped, policies evolve, and data distributions shift—gradient signals progressively degrade across network depth. This degradation is posited to be a primary reason for the challenges in scaling deep RL architectures. The diagnosis is conducted by examining how gradient pathologies (such as vanishing gradients, dormant neurons, reduced representational expressivity, and flat loss landscapes) emerge and intensify with increased network depth and width in both non-stationary supervised learning and deep RL.
- **Proposing targeted interventions:** To develop and evaluate simple, direct interventions that specifically address the identified gradient flow instabilities. The motivation is to demonstrate that by actively encouraging robust gradient propagation, the stability and performance of deep RL agents can be significantly improved, even with larger networks. The focus is on architectural modifications (like improved skip connections) and optimization strategies (like second-order methods) that are compatible with existing, well-established deep RL algorithms.
- **Enabling robust performance at scale:** To show that these interventions can lead to strong, robust performance across a range of network depths and widths, thereby unlocking the potential benefits of increased model capacity in deep RL. The aim is not merely to mitigate performance collapse but to facilitate performance gains with larger models, similar to what is observed in other domains of deep learning.
- **Generalizing the findings:** To validate that the proposed gradient stabilization techniques are effective across a variety of agents (value-based and policy-based), environments (Atari, continuous control, offline RL), and architectural components (standard CNNs, richer CNNs), demonstrating the broad applicability of the insights.

In essence, the work is motivated by the observation that deep RL struggles to leverage large-scale networks effectively, and it aims to provide a fundamental understanding of why this occurs at the gradient level, followed by practical solutions to address these challenges.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research adopts a largely empirical methodology, structured in two main phases: diagnosis of gradient pathologies under scale and non-stationarity, and the proposal and evaluation of interventions.

#### 3.1. Diagnostic Phase (Section 3)

The initial phase involved a series of controlled experiments to identify and characterize gradient pathologies.
- **Settings:**
 - **Supervised Learning (SL):** CIFAR-10 image classification was used. Both stationary (standard training) and non-stationary (periodically shuffling training labels) settings were explored to mimic RL's dynamic data distribution.
 - **Reinforcement Learning (RL):** PQN (a value-based algorithm without a target network or replay buffer, known for stability with Layer Normalization) was used on the Atari-10 suite (a curated subset of 10 Atari games). The generality was further investigated using DQN and Rainbow.
- **Network Architectures:** Models consisted of standard 6-layer convolutional neural networks (CNNs) followed by multi-layer perceptrons (MLPs). The depth and width of the MLP components were systematically varied (categorized as small, medium, and large in terms of parameter count, as detailed in Table 1) to study the effects of scaling.
- **Metrics:**
 - **Performance:** Training accuracy (SL) and episode returns (RL).
 - **Gradient Flow:** Mean gradient norms across network layers.
 - **Network Activity:** Fraction of dormant neurons (units with near-zero activations).
 - **Representational Expressivity:** SRank (effective rank of penultimate-layer activations).
 - **Loss Curvature:** Hessian trace of the temporal-difference loss, serving as a proxy for sharpness or smoothness of the optimization landscape.

#### 3.2. Intervention Phase (Section 4)

Based on the diagnostic findings, two primary types of interventions were proposed and evaluated, initially with PQN on the Atari-10 suite.
- **Intervention 1: Multi-Skip Residuals for Gradient Stability:** This architectural modification extends standard residual connections. Instead of spanning one or two layers, flattened convolutional features from the encoder are broadcast directly to *all* subsequent MLP layers, ensuring unobstructed gradient propagation from any depth back to the shared encoder. This was compared against a standard fully connected MLP baseline.
- **Intervention 2: Second-Order Optimizers for Non-Stationarity:** The default first-order RAdam optimizer was replaced with Kron (Kronecker-factored optimizer). Kron approximates the Fisher Information Matrix to provide curvature-aware preconditioning, aiming for more stable and directionally informed updates, especially in non-stationary environments.
- **Combined Interventions:** Both multi-skip architecture and Kron optimizer were applied to PQN and evaluated on the full ALE suite (57 games) to assess synergistic effects. The combined approach was also re-evaluated in the non-stationary SL setting.
- **Alternative Gradient-Stabilization Methods:** An ablation study was conducted to compare the proposed interventions against other strategies from prior work. These included various optimizers (AdaBelief, Shampoo, Apollo), regularization techniques (L2 norm, weight clipping, weight decay), activation functions (GELU, CReLU), and learning rate schedules/scaling.

#### 3.3. Generality Validation Phase (Section 5)

To establish the broad applicability of the findings, the interventions were tested in diverse settings:
- **PPO with Gradient Interventions:** The combined interventions (Layer Normalization, multi-skip residuals, Kron) were applied to the PPO algorithm and evaluated on the full ALE suite and continuous control tasks in Isaac Gym (Cartpole, Anymal).
- **Scaled Encoder Variants:** The standard CNN backbone was replaced with the Impala CNN architecture to evaluate how richer visual encoders interact with the gradient flow interventions in PQN and PPO.
- **Simba with Kron Optimizer:** The Simba actor-critic framework (known for its scalability design, including normalization and residual connections) was augmented by replacing its default AdamW optimizer with Kron. This was tested with SAC and DDPG algorithms on the DeepMind Control Suite (DMC) tasks (e.g., Humanoid Walk/Run, Dog Trot/Run).
- **Offline Q-Learning:** The interventions were applied to a suite of offline Q-learning algorithms from Park et al. to assess their impact on scalability in offline RL benchmarks, where input distributions are stationary but credit assignment challenges persist.

All RL experiments were conducted with multiple random seeds (typically 3 or 5) and results aggregated using human-normalized scores, mean, or median, with confidence intervals reported. Computational resources included a single NVIDIA RTX 8000 GPU, 12 CPU workers, and 50GB RAM.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Diagnosis of Gradient Pathologies (Section 3)

- **Non-Stationarity Exacerbates Degradation:** In non-stationary supervised learning (CIFAR-10 with shuffled labels), deep networks failed to recover accuracy, correlating with a marked degradation in gradient magnitudes. In contrast, stationary settings maintained stable gradient norms across scales.
- **Deep RL Performance Collapse:** For PQN (and similarly observed for DQN and Rainbow in Appendix C.1) on Atari games, deeper networks exhibited a collapse in both episode returns and corresponding gradient norms, indicating fragility under non-stationarity.
- **Emergent Training Pathologies:** Increased MLP depth correlated with:
 - A higher fraction of dormant neurons, suggesting underutilized network capacity.
 - Reduced SRank (effective rank of penultimate-layer activations), indicating state representations collapsing into lower-dimensional, less expressive subspaces.
 - Near-zero Hessian trace, signifying poorly conditioned loss landscapes that hinder effective gradient-based updates and correlate with poor learning.

#### 4.2. Effectiveness of Interventions (Section 4)

- **Multi-Skip Residuals:** This architectural design prevented performance collapse in PQN on Atari-10 as network depth increased. It maintained stable learning and improved performance across various widths, consistently supported by higher gradient magnitudes (Figure 5, left). Ablation on skip connection quantity showed performance generally improved with more connections (Appendix C.5).
- **Kronecker-Factored Optimizer (Kron):** Replacing the default RAdam optimizer with Kron prevented performance degradation at greater depths for PQN on Atari-10, even with standard MLP architectures. This was attributed to Kron's ability to preserve gradient signal through curvature-aware updates (Figure 5, right).
- **Combined Interventions:** The combination of multi-skip residuals and Kron optimizer on PQN resulted in superior scalability. On Atari-10, the augmented agent achieved high Human Normalized Scores (HNS) even at large depths, demonstrating increased gradient flow (Figure 6, left). Across the full ALE suite (57 games), this combined approach outperformed the baseline PQN in 90% of environments, achieving a median relative improvement of 83.27% (Figure 6, right). In non-stationary supervised learning, these interventions enabled rapid accuracy recovery and sustained adaptability across depths and widths, supported by stable gradient flow (Figure 7).
- **Comparison with Alternatives:** An extensive ablation of alternative gradient stabilization methods (e.g., other optimizers like Shampoo, Apollo, AdaBelief; various regularization and activation functions) indicated that none consistently matched the performance or stability benefits of the proposed multi-skip and Kron combination (Table 3, Figure 14).

#### 4.3. Generality of Findings (Section 5)

- **PPO Algorithm:** Applying the combined gradient interventions to PPO yielded significant performance boosts. On the full ALE, augmented PPO outperformed the baseline in 83.64% of environments, with a median relative improvement of 31.40% (Figure 8, left). In Isaac Gym's continuous control tasks (Cartpole, Anymal), baseline PPO collapsed with increasing model size, while the augmented variant remained stable and achieved superior performance across all depths and widths (Figure 8, right).
- **Scaled Encoder Variants (Impala CNN):** Both PQN and PPO agents equipped with the Impala CNN encoder (a richer visual representation architecture) benefited significantly from the gradient interventions. The interventions enabled successful scaling of the MLP component, contrasting with performance collapse observed without them, suggesting that deeper networks can better leverage expressive visual features when gradient flow is preserved (Figure 9).
- **Simba with Kron Optimizer:** When the AdamW optimizer in the Simba actor-critic framework (using SAC and DDPG) was replaced with Kron, the agents demonstrated stable and improved learning with larger networks on DeepMind Control Suite tasks. The default Simba, despite its design for scalability, exhibited performance degradation as networks grew (Figure 10, and Appendix E.2).
- **Offline Q-Learning:** Augmenting various offline Q-learning baselines with the gradient interventions generally improved performance across tasks (Figure 11). Performance gains were particularly pronounced in sparse-reward environments (e.g., humanoidmaze-giant-navigate), enabling near-optimal performance where baselines often failed. The interventions also enhanced robustness to increasing task difficulty, maintaining consistent and high performance for several algorithms.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the understanding and advancement of deep reinforcement learning by providing empirical evidence for the challenges of scaling neural networks in non-stationary environments.

**Fundamental Understanding:** The paper identifies that the interaction between deep RL's inherent non-stationarity and gradient pathologies (such as vanishing gradients, underutilized capacity, and flat loss landscapes) is a primary impediment to effective parameter scaling. This shifts the focus beyond solely algorithmic instability or exploration strategies, highlighting the critical role of gradient dynamics. The findings suggest that stable gradient flow is a necessary precondition for unlocking the benefits of increased model capacity in deep RL.

**Practical Solutions for Scalability:** The proposed interventions—multi-skip residual connections and Kronecker-factored optimization—offer relatively simple and compatible mechanisms to stabilize gradient flow. Their effectiveness across diverse RL algorithms (PQN, PPO, SAC, DDPG), environment suites (ALE, Isaac Gym, DeepMind Control Suite), and even offline RL settings demonstrates their broad applicability. These interventions provide practical tools for researchers and practitioners aiming to deploy larger, more expressive neural networks in deep RL.

**Enabling Advanced Architectures:** By facilitating the stable training of deeper and wider networks, this work enables deep RL agents to better leverage increased model capacity. This is further highlighted by the successful integration with richer visual encoders like the Impala CNN and the Simba framework, suggesting a pathway for more complex architectures to extract and process more sophisticated representations from high-dimensional inputs, potentially leading to improved generalization and performance in increasingly complex tasks.

**Future Research Directions:** The research establishes a baseline for future work on gradient stabilization strategies in deep RL. It opens avenues for exploring more computationally efficient gradient stabilization techniques, novel architectural designs that inherently promote robust gradient flow, and advanced optimization methods tailored for the unique challenges of non-stationary learning. The observation that interventions also benefit offline RL, even with stationary inputs, suggests a deeper commonality in gradient pathologies that might be exploited across different RL paradigms.

**Computational Considerations:** While acknowledging that second-order optimizers like Kron introduce additional computational overhead, the paper notes that this cost can be mitigated through vectorized environments and efficient deep RL algorithms. This highlights a trade-off between computational cost and learning stability/performance, and suggests the need for future research into optimizing these interventions for greater efficiency.
