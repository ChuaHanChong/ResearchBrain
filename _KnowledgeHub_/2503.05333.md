# PhysicsGen: Can Generative Models Learn from Images to Predict Complex Physical Relations?

> [!summary] Summary
> PhysicsGen is a benchmark testing whether image-to-image generative models can learn physical simulation. It supplies 300k input-output image pairs across three tasks - urban sound propagation, Brown-Conrady lens distortion, and bouncing/rolling ball dynamics - plus physics-targeted metrics, and evaluates convAE, VAE, U-Net, Pix2Pix, DDPM, Stable Diffusion and DDBM. Models reach roughly 20,000x speedups but degrade sharply on higher-order physical relations.

## Problem

- Prior learned-physics work predicts only coarse properties such as block-tower stability, leaving open whether generative models infer detailed, pixel-accurate physical fields from image pairs.
- Generative modelling research is dominated by appearance tasks like inpainting and style transfer, so no systematic evidence exists on physical correctness of learned image-to-image mappings.
- Physics-grounded datasets are plentiful for reinforcement-learning environments but scarce for generative models, so speedup-versus-accuracy tradeoffs against differential-equation solvers were never measured.

## Method

- **Urban Sound Propagation** task: 25k geo-locations from Overpass API simulated with ==NoiseModelling v4== under ==CNOSSOS-EU== into Baseline/Diffraction/Reflection/Combined decibel maps, scored by line-of-sight versus non-line-of-sight MAE and wMAPE.
- **Lens Distortion** task: 50k CelebA faces warped by the ==Brown-Conrady tangential model== with coefficients p1 and p2 isolated, evaluated by Facial Alignment Network landmark displacement split into X and Y error.
- **Bouncing and Rolling Ball** task built in ==Pymunk== from free-fall, spring-damper impact and oblique-throw phases, with metrics for ball position, rotation, out-of-roundness and ground-inclination angle across variable time intervals.

## Results

- **Pix2Pix** led sound propagation with baseline MAE **1.73** line-of-sight and **1.19** non-line-of-sight versus convAE 3.67/2.74, and **0.138 ms** inference against 204.7 s simulation, roughly **20,000x** faster.
- Non-line-of-sight error rose for every architecture once reflection or diffraction was added; U-Net reflection wMAPE reached **80.46** outside line of sight versus 12.75 inside, despite visually convincing outputs.
- On bouncing balls Pix2Pix was most stable with **11%** non-evaluable frames while convAE and VAE were unusable at **99% and 95%**; Y-position error roughly doubled X-position error.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Prediction error scales with the order of the governing differential equation: second-order terms like rotation and impact-driven vertical motion break models that handle first-order fields well.
> - Visual plausibility is not physical correctness; reflection patterns looked right while acoustic accuracy failed, so appearance metrics must give way to physics-targeted, region-specific evaluation.
> - Failure modes are architecture-specific: GANs render consistent but misplaced scenes, autoencoders blur uncertainty into deformed output, diffusion models hedge by emitting multiple candidate objects.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in "PhysicsGen" is situated within the evolving domain of physical simulation, specifically at the intersection of traditional differential equation-based methods and contemporary data-driven machine learning approaches. Historically, physical simulations have relied on the numerical solution of complex differential equations. However, recent advancements in machine learning have led to a paradigm shift, with increasing research focus on learnable models for these tasks.

The paper builds upon earlier concepts such as Tenenbaum’s intuitive physics engine, which trained machine learning models to infer coarse physical properties from images. "PhysicsGen" extends this idea by investigating the capacity of modern generative models to learn more detailed, complex, and physically accurate mappings directly from image pairs. This approach aims to leverage the image-to-image translation capabilities of generative models, which have demonstrated significant progress in estimating intricate mappings between image distributions in tasks like image in-painting or style transfer.

The integration of learnable methods into physical modeling is a growing field. Prior work has explored physics-guided generative models like PUGAN and FEM-GAN for enhancing performance in complex physical environments, particularly in fluid dynamics and structural system identification, by incorporating physics-based loss functions. Research has also shown that machine learning models can learn to comprehend physical dynamics, moving beyond simple memorization to understanding interactions, as seen in predicting block tower dynamics or generating physically plausible human animations. Physics-guided AI, including grey-box models and geometric Bayesian flow networks, has been developed to embed physical constraints within AI models for enhanced reliability, particularly in handling complex geometrical structures despite challenges like multi-modality and noise sensitivity in generative models.

While deep reinforcement learning has benefited from abundant physics-based environments and datasets (e.g., OpenAI Gym, Robosuite, PyBullet) for training models to understand and predict physical phenomena, the authors identify a notable scarcity of physics-informed datasets and environments specifically tailored for generative AI. This scarcity limits the ability to train generative models on complex physical systems. The PhysicsGen benchmark addresses this gap by providing a standardized dataset and evaluation framework to systematically investigate the potential of generative models in learning complex physical relations from image data.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to systematically investigate the potential of modern generative models, such as Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) and Diffusion models, in the context of physical simulations. Specifically, the authors aim to determine if these models can learn detailed, complex, and physically correct mappings solely from input-output image pairs.

The motivation for this investigation is multifaceted:
- **Learning Complex Relations**: Generative models possess sophisticated image-to-image translation abilities. The authors hypothesize that these capabilities could be harnessed to infer complex, potentially hard-to-formalize but observable, physical relations from visual data. If successful, this could lead to new avenues for modeling physical phenomena where explicit differential equations are challenging to derive or computationally intensive to solve.
- **Simulation Acceleration**: Traditional physical simulations, often based on solving differential equations, can be computationally expensive and time-consuming, especially for iterative processes. Generative models, with their fast inference times once trained, could offer significant speedups, thereby accelerating research and engineering workflows.
- **Addressing Data Scarcity**: Recognizing the limited availability of physics-informed datasets suitable for training generative AI models, the authors were motivated to create a new benchmark. This benchmark would facilitate a thorough and systematic analysis of current generative approaches in learning physical phenomena from images.

To achieve these objectives, the authors introduced the PhysicsGen benchmark, designed to address two specific research questions:
1. **Learning Capability**: Are generative models capable of learning intricate physical relations exclusively from input-output image pairs? This question probes the models' ability to generalize and encode underlying physical laws rather than merely memorizing visual patterns.
2. **Computational Efficiency**: What magnitude of speedup can be achieved by replacing traditional differential equation-based simulations with generative model predictions? This question focuses on the practical utility and performance gains offered by such an approach.

The design of the PhysicsGen benchmark and its constituent tasks was guided by several criteria to ensure comprehensive evaluation:
- **Diversity of Physical Problems**: The selection of tasks aimed to cover a range of physical phenomena, ensuring models are tested across various domains and types of physical interactions.
- **Variety of Numerical Solution Strategies**: Tasks were chosen to include both iterative and closed-form (non-iterative) solvers in their ground-truth simulations, allowing assessment of models' adaptability to different computational complexities.
- **Simplicity of Evaluation Metrics**: The benchmark sought to use straightforward and effective metrics to evaluate model performance, ensuring clear and comparable results.
- **Accessibility to the ML Community**: The chosen tasks were intended to be intuitively understandable, not requiring specialized physics knowledge, thereby making the benchmark broadly applicable and accessible to the wider machine learning community.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core methodology of this work is the introduction and evaluation of the **PhysicsGen Benchmark**, designed to assess the capability of generative image models in learning and predicting complex physical relations from images.

#### 3.1. PhysicsGen Benchmark Design

The benchmark comprises three distinct physical simulation tasks, each contributing 100,000 input-output image pairs, totaling 300,000 samples. The ground truth for these tasks is generated using established differential equation-based simulations.

#### 3.2. Benchmark Tasks

1. **Urban Sound Propagation (Section 3)**:
 - **Description**: This task models the behavior of sound waves in urban environments, accounting for phenomena like diffraction and reflections. It represents physical problems typically solved via expensive, iterative methods involving higher-order differential equations.
 - **Dataset Generation**: 100,000 samples were generated from 25,000 geo-locations across five cities (Hamburg, Hannover, Augsburg, Bonn, Munich), using open-source geo-data from the Overpass API. Urban environments (500m² area) were represented as black (buildings) and white (open spaces) pixels. The NoiseModelling v4 framework, adhering to CNOSSOS-EU standards, simulated sound propagation maps (512x512 or 256x256 pixels, 0-255 decibel scale). Four sub-tasks were defined: Baseline, Diffraction, Reflection, and Combined (incorporating variable sound levels and environmental factors).
 - **Physics**: Sound propagation is described by partial differential wave equations. The discrete, iterative implementation involves calculating receiver amplitude based on source level, geometrical spreading, atmospheric absorption, and diffraction (Eq. 1). Reflections are accounted for by adjusting power levels based on an absorption coefficient (Eq. 2), with complexity growing as O(N²) for N reflective surfaces.
 - **Evaluation**: Metrics include Mean Absolute Error (MAE) and Weighted Mean Absolute Percentage Error (wMAPE). wMAPE specifically penalizes models for predicting high sound amplitudes in physically improbable low-amplitude regions (e.g., behind buildings). Performance is assessed for both Line of Sight (LoS) and Non-Line of Sight (NLoS) regions using ray tracing to identify obstructions.

2. **Lens Distortion (Section 4)**:
 - **Description**: This task investigates physical problems with closed-form solutions, focusing on optical aberrations modeled by the Brown-Conrady distortion model. Generative models learn to simulate facial image appearance distorted by lens effects.
 - **Dataset Generation**: 100,000 samples were derived from a subset of the CelebA dataset (50,000 facial images, cropped to 256x256 pixels). Images were split into groups focusing on horizontal or vertical distortion to isolate the effects of tangential distortion coefficients (p1, p2). OpenCV was used to compute ground truth distortions.
 - **Physics**: The Brown-Conrady model mathematically describes radial and tangential lens distortions. The study focuses on tangential distortion components, expressed by closed-form equations for distorted coordinates (x_dist, y_dist) based on radial distance and coefficients p1, p2 (Eq. 3, 4). Conditional equations illustrate the isolated impact and complexity of each coefficient.
 - **Evaluation**: The evaluation bypasses traditional pixel-based metrics and focuses on accurately reproducing designated facial landmarks. A 2D facial landmark detection method based on the Facial Alignment Network (FAN) is applied to original and distorted CelebA faces. The Euclidean distance between actual and predicted landmark positions is computed, along with separate X Error and Y Error to assess positional accuracy along each axis.

3. **Dynamics of Rolling and Bouncing Movements (Section 5)**:
 - **Description**: This task evaluates generative models' ability to map kinematic movements, specifically predicting the position and rotation of a ball (rolling or bouncing) along an inclined surface for a defined time interval. The bouncing case is more complex, involving higher-order physical relations.
 - **Dataset Generation**: 100,000 samples (50,000 for bouncing, 50,000 for rolling) were generated using the Pymunk Python physics simulation toolbox. Variable parameters include ground inclination, ball start height, ball position, and the time interval between input and target images. A multi-colored line structure was superimposed on the background for increased data diversity and consistency analysis.
 - **Physics**: For bouncing, motion is divided into free fall (Newton's law, Eq. 5), ground impact and rebound (simplified spring-damper model, Eq. 6), and oblique throw (Newton's law, Eq. 7). The bouncing case incorporates second-order derivatives for X and Y movements and ball rotation, making the Y-axis motion particularly complex. The rolling case involves simpler kinematics.
 - **Evaluation**: Four specific error criteria are used: ball position (X, Y in pixels, difference between theoretical and predicted center), ball rotation (angle in degrees), out-of-roundness (standard deviation of radius errors in pixels), and ground inclination angular error (degrees). "General" error criteria are detailed in the Appendix.

#### 3.3. Baseline Model Evaluation

The study evaluates a range of generative image-to-image models:
- **GANs**: Pix2Pix.
- **Autoencoders**: U-Net, Convolutional Autoencoders (convAE), Variational Autoencoders (VAEs).
- **Diffusion Models**: Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPMs), Stable Diffusion (SD), Stable Diffusion with Cross Attention (SD w.CA), Denoising Diffusion Bridge Models (DDBMs).

All models were trained and evaluated independently for each task, using consistent architectures and training procedures (detailed in Appendix A). Training was performed primarily on a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU, with DDBMs requiring four NVIDIA A100 GPUs and SD on a single A100 GPU due to computational demands.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of generative models across the three PhysicsGen benchmark tasks yielded several consistent findings regarding their capability to learn complex physical relations from images and their computational efficiency.

#### 4.1. Computational Speedups

A significant finding is the substantial speedup achieved by generative models compared to traditional differential equation-based simulations. Generative models demonstrated processing speed improvements up to a factor of 20,000, particularly when replacing highly iterative solvers, such as those in the urban sound propagation task, with models like Pix2Pix or UNets. It is noted that this comparison is based on practically available implementations, and the simulation codes were not optimized for GPU execution.

#### 4.2. Physical Correctness and Consistency

While promising in speed, the generative models exhibited limitations in achieving high physical correctness, especially for complex relations.
- **General Trend**: Good results were observed for simpler 0th and 1st order physical problems. However, performance deteriorated for tasks requiring the learning of higher-order physical relations. This suggests a fundamental challenge for current generative models in encoding complex physical laws.
- **Urban Sound Propagation (Table 1)**:
 - Models showed an increase in Mean Absolute Error (MAE) for Non-Line of Sight (NLoS) regions when reflections or diffractions were present, indicating difficulty in accurately predicting sound behavior behind obstacles.
 - Pix2Pix demonstrated a moderate increase in NLoS error, while UNet and Diffusion models showed more pronounced errors in these complex scenarios.
 - Models without skip connections (VAE, ConvAE) tended to blur images and struggled to capture reflection patterns accurately.
 - Qualitatively, models could replicate sound reflection patterns visually, but a discrepancy existed between visual output and actual acoustic accuracy, indicating a lack of full comprehension of the underlying sound physics.
- **Lens Distortion (Table 2)**:
 - Generative models showed varying accuracies in landmark prediction under different tangential distortion parameters (p1, p2).
 - The increased complexity of `x_dist` and `y_dist` equations when `p1` or `p2` were active individually correlated with heightened error rates in the respective dimensions. For instance, a higher Y Error was observed when only `p1` was active, linked to the higher-order dependency on `y`. Conversely, a slight increase in X error and decrease in Y error occurred when only `p2` was active.
- **Dynamics of Bouncing Movements (Table 3)**:
 - The errors from generative networks generally increased with the presence of higher-order terms in the physical motion equations.
 - The Y-direction error for the bouncing ball (involving a second-order derivative for ground impact) significantly exceeded the X-direction error, reflecting the higher complexity of the Y-axis motion.
 - **Pix2Pix (GAN)**: This model provided the most stable representation, achieving competitive results across most evaluation criteria (ball position, rotation, roundness) with fewer failed samples. It also preserved background details well.
 - **UNet**: While providing good predictions for ball position and angle when successful, it produced a higher percentage of non-evaluable error images (e.g., shredded ball shapes) and struggled with ball roundness. Its output backgrounds were often blurred.
 - **Autoencoders (VAE, ConvAE)**: These models generated very blurred images, with the ball often distorted and hardly recognizable. Over 90% of their predictions were non-analyzable.
 - **Diffusion Models (DDPM, SD, DDBM)**: DDPM showed poorer absolute results, particularly for ball rotation (error approximately twice as high as Pix2Pix/UNet). Stable Diffusion (SD) performed comparably to Pix2Pix for position and best for ball roundness, with few error images, but had a poorer angle mapping. Diffusion models generally showed very low rates of non-evaluable images. However, a common issue was the generation of multiple candidate solutions (e.g., multiple balls in one scene) or artifacts, and they exhibited challenges in estimating complex relations like ball rotation. Visually, GANs and Stable Diffusion networks depicted small details and background structures more accurately than UNets.

#### 4.3. Distinct Error Patterns for Model Types

The analysis revealed specific error patterns unique to different generative model architectures:
- **GANs (Pix2Pix)**: Often produced physically plausible outputs, but could generate incorrect outputs for given inputs (e.g., a complete ball at the wrong position).
- **U-Nets, VAEs, and ConvAEs**: Frequently failed to produce consistent scenes, tending to blur uncertain predictions, resulting in deformed balls and destructed backgrounds.
- **Diffusion Models**: Characteristically produced multiple candidate solutions (e.g., several balls in one scene) and encountered difficulties in estimating more complex physical relations like ball rotation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The PhysicsGen benchmark represents a contribution to the emerging field of neural-enhanced physical simulations by providing a standardized framework for evaluating generative image models. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Benchmark for Generative Models in Physics**: PhysicsGen introduces a comprehensive benchmark with diverse physical simulation scenarios, offering a systematic way to evaluate how learning-based image generation models perform in estimating complex physical relations. This is particularly valuable given the identified scarcity of physics-informed datasets for generative AI.
- **Identification of Capabilities and Limitations**: The baseline evaluations clearly demonstrate the potential of generative models to achieve significant computational speedups (up to 20,000x for highly iterative solvers) over traditional simulation methods. This practical acceleration could revolutionize workflows in scientific and engineering tasks that rely heavily on simulations.
- **Highlighting Challenges in Physical Correctness**: Crucially, the research also uncovers considerable limitations in the physical correctness and consistency of current generative models, especially when dealing with higher-order physical relations. This finding indicates that while generative models can visually reproduce patterns, they often do not fully capture the underlying physical laws, leading to discrepancies in accuracy.
- **Guidance for Future Research**: The identified gap between speed and physical accuracy, along with the distinct error patterns observed across different model types, points to critical areas for future research. This includes investigating whether the limitations stem from principal theoretical constraints of current models or insufficient training data, and developing novel methods to enforce physical correctness within generative frameworks. The observation of issues like multiple candidate solutions from diffusion models also opens specific avenues for model improvement.
- **Broader Impact on Scientific and Engineering Domains**: Given that physical system simulations are fundamental to a wide array of scientific and engineering disciplines, PhysicsGen serves as a vital tool for comparing and validating novel AI-enhanced simulation approaches. By focusing on scenarios where physical simulations map between 2D input and output structures, the benchmark addresses a widely applicable case, thereby contributing to the advancement and reliability of neural-enhanced simulations across various domains. The work underscores that while the promise of speed is significant, the challenge of ensuring robust physical correctness remains a primary hurdle for widespread adoption.
