# Mean Flow Policy with Instantaneous Velocity Constraint for One-step Action Generation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Tsinghua University and UC Berkeley developed the Mean Velocity Policy (MVP), a generative policy for reinforcement learning that enables efficient one-step action generation by directly learning a mean velocity field. They also introduced an Instantaneous Velocity Constraint (IVC) to ensure the uniqueness and accuracy of the learned policy, leading to state-of-the-art success rates on robotic manipulation tasks with reduced training and inference times.

## Problem

- Generative policies in reinforcement learning, such as diffusion models, suffer from high computational costs due to their multi-step iterative action generation process.
- The iterative nature of these policies leads to slow training, particularly for online RL, and high inference latency, which is problematic for real-time control applications.
- The underlying first-order ordinary differential equation (ODE) for mean flow identities lacks explicit boundary conditions, potentially leading to learning inaccuracies and limiting the expressiveness of the learned policy.

## Method

- The **Mean Velocity Policy** (MVP) was introduced, which directly learns a *mean* velocity field to transform a base Gaussian noise into a final action in a single step, bypassing iterative sampling.
- An Instantaneous Velocity Constraint (IVC) was proposed as an auxiliary loss to provide a crucial boundary condition for the mean flow ODE, ensuring a unique and accurate solution for the learned ==mean velocity field==.
- A "generate-and-select" mechanism, guided by a learned Q-function, is employed to approximate optimal target actions for policy improvement in the absence of ground-truth optimal actions.

## Results

- MVP achieved an average success rate of **0.88 	 0.05** across 9 challenging robotic manipulation tasks, outperforming FQL (**0.44 	 0.05**), BFN (**0.52 	 0.06**), and matching or exceeding QC (**0.86 	 0.05**) on most benchmarks.
- The policy demonstrated the fastest average online training speed at 153.6 	 11.5 iterations/second and low inference latency of 10.93 	 **0.95 ms**, significantly more efficient than multi-step baselines like BFN and QC.
- Ablation studies confirmed that the Instantaneous Velocity Constraint (IVC) improved learning accuracy, increasing success rates on tasks like Cube-triple-task4 from **0.30 	 0.21** (without IVC) to **0.52 	 0.11** (with full IVC).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Learning a mean velocity field directly enables one-step action generation, providing a computationally efficient alternative to multi-step generative policies without sacrificing expressiveness.
> - Explicitly enforcing a boundary condition via the Instantaneous Velocity Constraint is mathematically essential for unique and accurate learning of the mean velocity field, enhancing policy robustness.
> - The combination of a one-step mean velocity policy with a Q-function guided "generate-and-select" process guarantees policy improvement and is effective for learning in offline-to-online RL settings.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a challenge within reinforcement learning (RL) concerning the design of policy functions that are both expressive and computationally efficient, particularly in complex control environments where optimal action distributions can be multi-modal.

Recent advancements in generative models, such as diffusion models and flow matching, have demonstrated promise as expressive policy representations in RL. These models can transform simple base distributions into complex target action distributions through learnable transformations. However, a significant limitation of existing generative policies in RL is their reliance on iterative, multi-step refinement processes to generate actions from noise. This iterative nature introduces considerable computational overhead, affecting both training speed (especially critical for online RL where action sampling is a per-step requirement) and inference latency (a major impediment to achieving high closed-loop performance in real-time control systems).

Current approaches to mitigate this efficiency issue often involve distilling a complex multi-step generative model into a simpler, faster one-step policy. This paper, however, proposes a direct approach by leveraging advancements in generative modeling that aim for single-step generation, specifically drawing inspiration from "mean flow" concepts developed for tasks like image generation. By applying and adapting these ideas to the RL context, the authors aim to intrinsically enable one-step action generation while retaining the high expressiveness characteristic of generative policies, thus contributing to the field of efficient and expressive policy learning in offline-to-online RL settings.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a novel generative policy function for reinforcement learning that combines high expressiveness with the efficiency of one-step action generation. This objective is motivated by several identified limitations in the current landscape of RL policies:

1. **Addressing the Computational Burden of Generative Policies:** Existing generative policies, such as those based on diffusion models or flow matching, are effective at modeling complex, multi-modal action distributions. However, their reliance on multi-step iterative sampling from noise to action incurs substantial computational overhead. This overhead impacts training speed, particularly for online RL where actions are sampled frequently, and translates to significant inference latency, which is problematic for real-time control applications demanding high closed-loop performance. The motivation is to overcome this efficiency impediment without sacrificing the expressive power that makes these policies valuable.

2. **Bridging the Expressiveness-Efficiency Gap:** The paper notes a trade-off in existing flow-based policies: increasing expressiveness typically necessitates more flow steps, which in turn increases computational cost. The authors seek a method that can unify the expressiveness required for complex action distributions with the efficiency of a single-step action generation process, thereby resolving this trade-off.

3. **Improving Learning Accuracy and Expressiveness of Mean Flow Models in RL:** While the concept of modeling a mean velocity field holds promise for one-step generation, the authors identify a theoretical challenge: the underlying first-order ordinary differential equation (ODE) that defines the mean flow identity lacks explicit boundary conditions. In a mathematical context, this absence can lead to multiple possible solutions for the ODE, potentially resulting in learning inaccuracies and limiting the expressiveness of the learned policy. A specific motivation is to introduce a mechanism that can theoretically and empirically address this issue by providing a necessary boundary condition.

In summary, the research aims to introduce a new policy architecture that inherently supports rapid one-step action generation while maintaining the ability to model intricate action distributions. It further seeks to stabilize and enhance the learning process of this new architecture through a theoretically grounded constraint.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology introduces the **Mean Velocity Policy (MVP)**, a generative policy designed for one-step action generation, and enhances its learning stability and accuracy through an **Instantaneous Velocity Constraint (IVC)** within a standard offline-to-online RL framework.

#### 3.1. Mean Velocity Policy (MVP)

- **Core Principle:** Unlike conventional flow policies that learn an *instantaneous* velocity field `v(a(t), t, s)` requiring integration over time to obtain the final action (Eq. 5), MVP directly learns a *mean* velocity field `u(a(t), t, r, s)`. This mean velocity represents the average velocity over a given time interval `[t, r]` (Eq. 6).
- **One-Step Action Generation:** If the mean velocity model `u*` is perfectly learned, the policy can generate an action `a(1)` in a single step from a base Gaussian noise `a(0)` using the formula `a(1) = a(0) + u*(a(0), 0, 1, s)` (Eq. 7). This design eliminates the iterative sampling process of multi-step flow models, contributing to efficiency.
- **Training the Mean Velocity Model (`L_MF`):** The mean velocity model `u_theta` (parameterized by `theta`) is trained to satisfy the "mean flow identity," which is a first-order ODE relating the mean velocity to the instantaneous velocity (Eq. 8). The training objective, `L_MF` (Eq. 9), minimizes the squared difference between the learned mean velocity and a target derived from the mean flow identity. This objective requires the computation of a total time derivative `(d/dt)u`, which is performed using the chain rule and Jacobian-vector products (Eq. 10). The instantaneous velocity `v` is defined as `a* - a(0)`, where `a*` is a target optimal action.
- **"Generate-and-Select" for Optimal Action Imitation:** In RL, ground-truth optimal actions (`a*`) are not available. MVP employs a "generate-and-select" (or "best-of-N") mechanism to approximate `a*` and guide policy improvement.
 1. **Generation:** For a given state `s`, `N` diverse candidate actions (`a_i`) are generated by sampling from a base Gaussian noise `epsilon_i` and applying the current mean velocity model: `a_i = epsilon_i + u_theta(epsilon_i, 0, 1, s)` (Eq. 11).
 2. **Selection:** A learned Q-function (`Q_phi`) evaluates these `N` candidates, and the action yielding the highest Q-value is selected as the target action `a*` (Eq. 12).
 3. **Imitation:** This `a*` is then used as the target for the policy training loss (`L_MF`).
- **Policy Improvement Guarantee (Theorem 1):** The paper provides a theoretical proof that this generate-and-select mechanism, coupled with the mean velocity policy, guarantees policy improvement. The theorem decomposes the value function difference into a non-negative "BFN improvement term" (benefiting from diverse candidate selection) and a "fitting error term" (dependent on Q-function inaccuracies `epsilon_Q` and mean flow matching error `epsilon_A`). This decomposition highlights the importance of minimizing `epsilon_A`.

#### 3.2. Instantaneous Velocity Constraint (IVC)

- **Motivation for IVC:** The mean flow identity (Eq. 8), which governs the training of `u_theta`, is a first-order ODE. Mathematically, such an ODE requires a boundary condition to yield a unique solution. Without it, the learning process could converge to one of multiple possible solutions, leading to inaccuracies.
- **Mechanism:** The IVC introduces an auxiliary loss, `L_IVC` (Eq. 15), that explicitly enforces a boundary condition. It states that when the time interval `[t, r]` shrinks to a single point (`r = t`), the mean velocity `u_theta(a(t), t, t)` must equal the instantaneous velocity `v = a* - a(0)`.
- **Theoretical Justification (Theorem 2 & 3):**
 - **Theorem 2 (Multiplicity of Solutions):** This theorem mathematically demonstrates that without an explicit boundary condition, the cumulative error (`Delta_u`) between the learned and true mean velocity fields can persist as `C(a, r) / (r - t)`, where `C(a, r)` is an arbitrary, non-zero integration constant. The `L_MF` loss alone cannot force `C(a, r)` to zero.
 - **Theorem 3 (Uniqueness via IVC):** This theorem proves that minimizing `L_IVC` forces the integration constant `C(a, r)` to zero. By ensuring that the boundary error `Delta_v` (the limit of `Delta_u` as `r -> t+`) vanishes, the IVC eliminates the ambiguity, thereby guaranteeing that the learned `u_theta` converges to the unique correct mean velocity field `u*`. This enhances learning accuracy and policy expressiveness.

#### 3.3. Mean Flow Reinforcement Learning Algorithm

- **Overall Policy Loss:** The total policy training loss (`L_policy`, Eq. 19) is a weighted sum of the mean flow matching loss (`L_MF`) and the instantaneous velocity constraint loss (`L_IVC`), with `lambda` as the IVC coefficient (default 1.0).
- **Critic Training:** A standard Q-function (`Q_phi`) is trained using a temporal difference (TD) error loss (`L_Q`, Eq. 20) on transitions from a replay buffer. The target Q-value for `Q(s_{k+1}, a*_{k+1})` uses the `a*` selected by the "generate-and-select" process, ensuring an unbiased policy evaluation.
- **Training Scheme (Algorithm 1):** The algorithm follows a two-phase offline-to-online RL approach:
 1. **Offline Pre-training:** The policy and critic are initialized and trained on a static, pre-collected dataset to provide a warm start.
 2. **Online Interaction and Fine-tuning:** The agent interacts with the environment using the current policy, collects new data, and then fine-tunes the policy and critic using both new and buffered data.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents empirical evaluations across various robotic manipulation tasks and benchmarks, focusing on performance, training efficiency, and inference speed.

#### 4.1. Main Experiment Results

- **Benchmarks:** The evaluation used 9 sparse-reward robotic manipulation tasks: 3 from Robomimic (Lift, Can, Square) and 6 from OGBench (cube-double-task 2/3/4 and cube-triple-task 2/3/4).
- **Baselines:** The Mean Velocity Policy (MVP) was compared against three prominent offline-to-online RL baselines: FQL (Flow Q Learning), BFN (Best-of-N), and QC (Q-Chunking). BFN and QC utilize multi-step flow policies, while FQL trains a multi-step flow and distills it into a one-step policy.
- **Success Rates (Table 1, Figure 3):**
 - MVP achieved an average success rate of 0.88 ± 0.05 across all tasks, surpassing FQL (0.44 ± 0.05), BFN (0.52 ± 0.06), and QC (0.86 ± 0.05).
 - MVP matched or exceeded the state-of-the-art baselines on 8 out of 9 tasks. On the Robomimic-can task, MVP achieved the second-highest success rate (0.92 ± 0.07), just below QC (0.94 ± 0.06).
 - MVP demonstrated notable superiority on more challenging tasks, including Robomimic-square, Cube-double-task4, and all Cube-triple tasks, consistently achieving the highest success rates. For instance, on Cube-triple-task4, MVP recorded a success rate of 0.52 ± 0.11, outperforming QC (0.46 ± 0.13).

#### 4.2. Ablation Study and Supplementary Experiments

- **Impact of Instantaneous Velocity Constraint (IVC) (Figure 4, Table 4):**
 - An ablation study was conducted on the IVC coefficient `λ`. Performance was observed to correlate positively with the IVC weight.
 - On Cube-triple-task4, success rates improved significantly: 0.30 ± 0.21 (without IVC, `λ=0.0`), to 0.44 ± 0.08 (with partial IVC, `λ=0.5`), and further to 0.52 ± 0.11 (with full IVC, `λ=1.0`).
 - These results empirically support the theoretical claims regarding IVC's role in improving learning accuracy.
- **Comparison with One-Step Variants of Baselines (Figure 5, Table 5):**
 - One-step configurations of FQL, BFN, and QC (FQL-Onestep, BFN-Onestep, QC-Onestep) were evaluated.
 - These naive one-step variants showed minimal success (near zero) on complex tasks like Cube-triple-task3 and Cube-triple-task4.
 - In contrast, MVP achieved 0.71 ± 0.06 and 0.52 ± 0.11 on these tasks, respectively. This suggests that simply reducing flow steps is insufficient, and MVP's specific design, including the mean velocity modeling and IVC, is crucial for maintaining expressiveness and stable learning.
- **Training and Inference Time Analysis (Figure 1, Tables 2 & 3):**
 - **Online Training Speed (Table 2, Figure 1):** MVP achieved the fastest average online training speed at 153.6 ± 11.5 iterations/second (iter/s), substantially outperforming FQL (108.5 iter/s), QC (92.6 iter/s), and BFN (68.0 iter/s). This efficiency is attributed to its one-step action generation.
 - **Inference Time (Table 3):** MVP recorded an average inference time of 10.93 ± 0.95 milliseconds (ms) on a CPU-only environment without JIT compilation. This was comparable to FQL (10.76 ± 1.02 ms) and significantly faster than BFN (117.3 ± 13.23 ms) and QC (113.22 ± 11.92 ms).
 - **Efficiency Rationale:** The speed advantage of MVP and FQL over BFN and QC stems from their ability to perform one-step action generation for inference, while BFN and QC rely on a 10-step iterative flow policy. Although FQL achieves fast inference through distillation, its overall training speed and success rates are lower than MVP's due to its multi-policy approach.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Mean Velocity Policy (MVP) and its Instantaneous Velocity Constraint (IVC) represent a contribution to the field of reinforcement learning, particularly in the domain of expressive and efficient policy functions for complex control tasks.

#### 5.1. Addressing Fundamental RL Challenges

The work directly addresses the long-standing challenge of simultaneously achieving high expressiveness and computational efficiency in RL policies. By enabling one-step action generation, MVP bypasses the computational bottlenecks inherent in multi-step generative policies, which historically hinder their application in real-time and online RL scenarios. The demonstrated ability of MVP to model multi-modal action distributions, alongside its efficiency, suggests a step towards resolving this trade-off.

#### 5.2. Theoretical and Methodological Advancements

The introduction and theoretical justification of the Instantaneous Velocity Constraint (IVC) is a notable methodological contribution. By proving that IVC serves as a crucial boundary condition, the authors provide a principled mechanism to improve the learning accuracy and ensure the uniqueness of the solution for the mean velocity field. This enhances the robustness and expressive power of the generative model, which can lead to more stable and effective policy learning.

#### 5.3. Empirical Performance and Practical Implications

The empirical results, demonstrating state-of-the-art success rates on challenging robotic manipulation tasks from Robomimic and OGBench, validate the effectiveness of MVP in complex, sparse-reward environments. The substantial improvements in both training and inference speed are critical for the practical deployment of RL agents:
- **Real-time Control:** Reduced inference latency makes MVP highly suitable for real-time control systems, such as robotics, where rapid decision-making is essential.
- **Efficient Development and Online Learning:** Faster training speeds can accelerate the development and fine-tuning cycles of RL agents, making online RL more feasible and less resource-intensive.
- **Enhanced Generalization:** The improved learning accuracy and expressiveness, supported by IVC, may enable policies to better capture intricate behavioral distributions and adapt more effectively during online fine-tuning.

#### 5.4. Future Directions

The authors identify the additional GPU memory consumption during training, stemming from the Jacobian-Vector Product (JVP) operation, as a limitation. Future work aims to validate the method on a broader range of tasks and real robotic platforms.

In summary, this research offers a practical and theoretically grounded approach to designing efficient yet expressive generative policies for RL, holding implications for the development of advanced robotic control systems and real-time decision-making agents.
