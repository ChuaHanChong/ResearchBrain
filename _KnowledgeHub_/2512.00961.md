# Goal-Driven Reward by Video Diffusion Models for Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> GenReward leverages pre-trained video diffusion models to automatically generate multi-granular, goal-driven reward signals for reinforcement learning agents, addressing the challenge of manual reward engineering. The framework leads to enhanced performance in complex robotic manipulation tasks, demonstrating, for instance, an increase in average episode return for Meta-World Bin Picking from 398 to 822 compared to relying on raw dense environment rewards.

## Problem

- Manually designing effective and generalizable reward functions for Reinforcement Learning (RL) is a complex, labor-intensive process requiring extensive domain expertise, hindering scalability.
- Existing methods using expert demonstrations or pre-trained vision models do not fully exploit the 'generative world knowledge' of video diffusion models for explicit goal-driven reward signals.
- Many current approaches lack the integration of action information into fine-grained reward mechanisms, which can limit the coherence and efficiency of learned trajectories.

## Method

- A pre-trained video diffusion model (CogVideoX) is finetuned on domain-specific manipulation video datasets to generate task-specific goal videos based on high-level prompts.
- Video-level rewards are computed as the cosine similarity between the latent representation of the agent's observed trajectory and the generated goal video, both encoded by a 3D Causal VAE.
- Frame-level, action-aware rewards are derived by selecting a key goal frame (using OpenCLIP) and learning Forward-Backward (FB) representations to quantify the likelihood of reaching the goal state from a given state-action pair.

## Results

- GenReward consistently achieved competitive and often superior episodic returns across challenging Meta-World manipulation tasks, outperforming DreamerV3 with raw dense environment rewards and other diffusion/VLM-based baselines (e.g., Bin Picking average return increased from 398 to 822).
- The framework demonstrated robustness in sparse reward settings and across visually distracting environments (Distracting Control Suite) and dexterous manipulation tasks (Adroit), consistently surpassing baselines.
- Ablation studies confirmed that both video-level and frame-level action-aware reward components are crucial for optimal performance, and the framework showed resilience to generated video hallucinations and non-optimal goal frame selections.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Rich world knowledge implicitly learned by large-scale pre-trained video diffusion models can be effectively extracted and transferred into actionable, goal-driven reward signals for RL.
> - A multi-granular reward structure combining both trajectory-level (video-level alignment) and fine-grained state-action-level (frame-level progression) guidance significantly enhances learning robustness and efficiency.
> - Incorporating action information into fine-grained reward calculation, via a forward-backward representation, provides superior guidance for coherent goal achievement compared to state-only similarity metrics.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has demonstrated capabilities in various domains, but its practical application often encounters a significant challenge: the design of effective reward functions. These programmatic reward functions are typically crafted manually, requiring extensive domain expertise and human effort. This manual process can be time-consuming, prone to errors, and often results in reward functions that do not generalize well across different tasks or environments, thereby limiting the scalability and applicability of RL in complex real-world scenarios.

Existing research has explored several avenues to mitigate the need for hand-engineered rewards. One common approach involves leveraging expert demonstrations or videos to infer reward signals. For instance, RoboCLIP utilizes pretrained Vision-Language Models (VLMs) to compute similarity between task descriptions or demonstration videos and an agent's historical frames as a reward. Diffusion Reward employs conditional video diffusion models, pre-trained on expert videos, to derive rewards from the entropy of predicted distributions. TADPoLe uses a frozen text-conditioned diffusion model to generate zero-shot rewards based on alignment between visual observations and text descriptions. Other works, such as VLM-RMs and LIV, also explore VLMs as reward models, while some, like VIPER and UniPi, use video prediction or text-to-video generation for reward or policy learning. Furthermore, there is a growing body of work on incorporating videos into decision-making processes, aiming to learn representations or dynamics from diverse video sources.

Despite these advancements, a gap exists in fully exploiting the rich world knowledge contained within *generated* videos from large-scale pre-trained generative models as *goal-driven* reward signals. Many existing methods rely on expert demonstrations or directly use pre-trained vision models for similarity metrics, which might not explicitly transfer the generative model's understanding of sequential goal achievement. Moreover, some approaches do not integrate action information into the fine-grained reward mechanism, potentially limiting the coherence of learned trajectories.

This paper positions itself within this landscape by proposing a novel framework, GenReward, that directly leverages off-the-shelf video diffusion models to provide goal-driven rewards. It differentiates itself by:
1. **Using generated videos:** Rather than relying solely on expert demonstrations or static text/image prompts, it actively generates goal-conditioned videos.
2. **Multi-level goal integration:** It formulates rewards at both a video-level (trajectory alignment) and a frame-level (fine-grained state achievement).
3. **Action-awareness:** It specifically incorporates action information into the frame-level reward via a forward-backward representation, which is not a common feature in VLM- or diffusion-based reward functions.

By doing so, the work aims to transfer the implicit world knowledge learned by large-scale generative models into actionable reward signals, addressing the limitations of manual reward design and enhancing RL agents' ability to learn complex behaviors without explicit expert demonstrations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a novel reward framework, GenReward, that enables Reinforcement Learning (RL) agents to acquire effective policies without relying on handcrafted or programmatic reward functions. This framework aims to automatically generate informative reward signals by leveraging the rich world knowledge encapsulated within pre-trained video diffusion models.

The specific motivations and objectives driving this work are detailed as follows:

1. **Overcoming Reward Design Challenges:** A central motivation stems from the inherent difficulties associated with designing effective reward functions in RL. Handcrafted rewards often require significant domain expertise, are labor-intensive, and may not generalize across variations of a task or to new environments. This limitation significantly hinders the scalability and broader applicability of RL in complex, real-world scenarios, particularly those involving robotic manipulation or visually rich environments. The research seeks to alleviate this burden by proposing a method for automated reward generation.

2. **Exploiting Pre-trained Generative World Knowledge:** Large-scale video diffusion models, pre-trained on vast datasets of real-world videos, possess a deep, implicit understanding of temporal dynamics, object interactions, and common sequences of events. This "world knowledge" is a valuable resource that, if effectively transferred, could guide an RL agent's learning process. The objective is to devise a mechanism to extract and translate this inherent knowledge from generated videos into actionable reward signals.

3. **Providing Goal-Driven Guidance:** The framework aims to provide rewards that are directly tied to task-specific goals, rather than generic metrics. This involves generating explicit "goal videos" that visually depict the desired behavior for a given task. The agent's progress is then evaluated against these generated goals.

4. **Multi-Granular Reward Signals:** To ensure comprehensive guidance, the research aims to provide reward signals at two distinct levels of granularity:
 - **Video-level goals:** These rewards evaluate the overall alignment of the agent's trajectory with the generated goal video, encouraging the agent to mimic the desired behavior over a sequence of actions.
 - **Frame-level goals:** To facilitate more precise and fine-grained achievement, these rewards focus on specific key states within the desired trajectory, guiding the agent towards critical intermediate steps or the final goal state.

5. **Incorporating Action Information for Coherent Trajectories:** A specific objective for fine-grained goal achievement is to integrate action information into the reward calculation. By considering the agent's actions in relation to potential future states, the framework seeks to promote more coherent and efficient goal-directed behaviors, moving beyond state-only similarity measures. This action-aware reward aims to guide the agent towards actions that are most likely to lead to the desired goal state.

6. **Improving Online Performance:** Ultimately, the motivation is to demonstrate that by using these generative, goal-driven rewards, RL agents can achieve superior online performance on downstream tasks compared to methods relying on dense programmatic rewards or other VLM/diffusion-based reward approaches.

By addressing these objectives, the research aims to make RL more accessible and powerful for complex tasks, drawing inspiration from how humans can learn desired behaviors from high-level instructions or demonstrations without explicit reward engineering.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The GenReward framework operates in three main stages to provide goal-driven reward signals for Reinforcement Learning agents: Video Diffusion Model adaptation, Video-Level Goal as Reward, and Frame-Level Goal as Reward. The overarching idea is to leverage a pre-trained video diffusion model (VDM) to generate goal-conditioned videos and then derive rewards by measuring the agent's alignment with these generated goals.

**a) Video Diffusion Model Adaptation:**
The initial step involves adapting a pre-trained video diffusion model to the specific domain of robotic manipulation tasks.
- **Base Model:** The framework utilizes CogVideoX, an image-to-video generation model, as its foundation. CogVideoX is built upon Video Diffusion Transformers (DiTs) and has been pre-trained on large-scale video datasets, embedding a rich prior of world knowledge.
- **3D VAE:** CogVideoX employs a 3D Variational Autoencoder (VAE) to compress raw video data (V ∈ R^(F×3×H×W)) into a patchified video latent space (x). This latent space is where the diffusion process is trained, enabling efficient manipulation of video content.
- **Finetuning:** The pre-trained DiT within CogVideoX is finetuned using domain-specific manipulation video datasets (e.g., RT-1, Bridge dataset, RLBench). This finetuning process conditions the model on task specifications, typically in the form of text and image embeddings (C = {c_text, c_image}), extracted from prompts.
- **Diffusion Objective:** The model learns to reverse a forward Markov chain that gradually adds Gaussian noise to the latent variable x₀. The denoising model (ˆϵ_θ) is optimized to predict this noise, conditioned on the noisy latent variable, time step, and task conditions, using a standard diffusion objective (Eq. 2). This adaptation allows the model to generate task-specific videos that visually represent desired goal behaviors based on high-level prompts.

**b) Video-Level Goal as Reward:**
This stage focuses on providing rewards that encourage the agent to mimic the overall desired behavior at a trajectory level, by aligning its sequence of observations with a generated goal video.
- **Goal Video Generation:** Given a task specification (e.g., "pick up the blue fork" and a relevant image), the finetuned video diffusion model generates a goal video (V_goal) that illustrates an expert-like execution of the task.
- **Latent Representation Extraction:** The 3D Causal VAE encoder, which is part of the finetuned CogVideoX, is employed to extract latent representations from videos. This encoder is crucial because it compresses pixel-level video inputs into meaningful latent vectors, preserving both spatial and temporal information, and has been regularized (e.g., with Kullback-Leibler divergence) to learn a structured latent space.
- **Observation Encoding:** A sequence of the agent's recent historical visual observations (o₀:T) is encoded into a latent vector (z_v) using this 3D Causal VAE (Eq. 3). To handle varying lengths, 16 frames are uniformly sampled from each sequence.
- **Goal Video Encoding:** Similarly, the generated goal video (V_goal) is encoded into its own latent vector (z_goal) using the same 3D Causal VAE (Eq. 4).
- **Reward Calculation:** The video-level reward (r_video) is then computed as the cosine similarity between the agent's observation latent vector (z_v) and the goal video's latent vector (z_goal) (Eq. 5). A higher cosine similarity indicates better alignment between the agent's observed trajectory and the desired goal video, incentivizing the agent to follow the generated visual plan.

**c) Frame-Level Goal as Reward:**
While video-level goals provide overall trajectory guidance, fine-grained control requires rewards that focus on specific states and actions. This stage addresses that by providing frame-level goal rewards that are also action-aware.
- **Key Frame Selection:**
 - **Purpose:** To identify a specific "goal state" image from the generated video that represents a critical point of achievement.
 - **Method:** OpenCLIP is used to select the most relevant frame (I*) from the generated goal video. It calculates the cosine similarity between the text description of the task (G) and the image embedding of each frame (I_i) from the generated video (Eq. 6). The frame with the highest similarity score is chosen as the key frame, representing the frame-level goal.
- **Forward-Backward (FB) Representation Learning:**
 - **Purpose:** To learn a representation that effectively estimates the long-term probability of reaching the goal state (I*) from any given state-action pair (s, a), thus incorporating action information.
 - **Components:** Two representation networks are learned: a backward representation B: S → Z and a forward representation F: S × A × Z → Z. Here, Z is a d-dimensional representation space.
 - **Goal and State Embeddings:** The selected goal image (I*) is encoded into a semantic representation (ψ(I*)) using a frozen DINOv3 model. The agent's current observation (o) is encoded into a model state (s) using a DreamerV3 encoder (ϕ(o)).
 - **Optimization:** The FB representation is optimized by minimizing a Bellman residual (Eq. 8). This objective encourages the product Fᵀ(s, a, z)B(s') to approximate the long-term probability of transitioning from (s, a) to s' under a policy (π_z) that maximizes this similarity. Target networks (¯F, ¯B) are used for training stability, and an orthonormalization loss (L_norm) regularizes backward representations to prevent collapse. The variable `z` in this context serves as an embedding of the goal state, often set to ψ(I*).
- **Frame-Level Reward Calculation:** Once the FB representations are learned, the frame-level forward-backward reward (r_FB) is defined as F(s, a, ψ(I*))ᵀB(ψ(I*)) (Eq. 9). This reward quantifies how likely the agent is to reach the goal state I* from its current state-action pair (s,a), providing action-aware guidance towards the fine-grained goal.
- **Combined Generative Reward:** Finally, the overall generative reward (r_gen) used by the RL agent is a weighted sum of the video-level reward (r_video), the frame-level forward-backward reward (r_FB), and any raw environment reward (r_env) provided by the simulator (Eq. 10). The weighting coefficients (α and β) allow for balancing the influence of each reward component.

This comprehensive approach leverages the generative capabilities of VDMs to create visual goals, then employs specialized encoders and learned representations to align the agent's behavior at both macro (trajectory) and micro (state-action) levels, thereby guiding learning without manual reward engineering.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of the GenReward framework was evaluated through extensive experiments on widely recognized benchmarks.

**Experimental Setups:**
- **Benchmarks:**
 - **Meta-World:** Used for complex robotic manipulation tasks. Five medium-to-hard tasks were selected: Pick Place, Pick Out of Hole, Bin Picking, Shelf Place, and Disassemble. Episodes were limited to 256 steps.
 - **Distracting Control Suite (DCS):** A modified DeepMind Control Suite with visual distractors (e.g., varying backgrounds) to assess robustness to visual variability.
 - **Adroit:** Complex dexterous manipulation tasks with sparse rewards.
- **Goal Video Sources:** Videos collected from robotic manipulation tasks in RT-1, Bridge dataset, and Robot Learning Benchmark (RLBench) were used as source domains for finetuning the VDM and generating goal videos for Meta-World. For DCS, text-to-video generation was used without finetuning.
- **Base RL Algorithm:** The performance of reward models was evaluated by integrating them with a strong model-based RL algorithm, DreamerV3. For DCS and Adroit, DrQ-v2 was also used as a model-free backbone.
- **Reward Settings:** Both dense and sparse reward settings provided by the environment were considered.
- **Baselines:** GenReward was compared against:
 - **Dense Reward:** The original dense reward from the environment.
 - **RoboCLIP:** Leverages pre-trained VLMs for reward via similarity to task descriptions or demonstration videos.
 - **Diffusion Reward:** Uses conditional video diffusion models trained on expert videos to estimate rewards based on negative conditional entropy.
 - **TADPoLe:** Employs a text-conditioned image diffusion model for zero-shot dense rewards by aligning observations with text via denoising gradients.

**Main Comparison (Meta-World with Dense Reward):**
- GenReward consistently demonstrated competitive and often superior performance in episodic returns across all five Meta-World manipulation tasks (Figure 5).
- It significantly outperformed DreamerV3 trained with the raw dense environment reward in several challenging tasks: Pick Out of Hole (increasing average episode return from 193 to 582), Bin Picking (398 to 822), and Shelf Place (154 to 814).
- Compared to other video- or diffusion-based reward baselines (RoboCLIP, Diffusion Reward), GenReward showed a notable advantage, attributed to its effective exploitation of generated videos and transfer of underlying world knowledge.
- TADPoLe generally underperformed other baselines, indicating that it did not provide sufficiently effective rewards for these tasks.
- Qualitative analysis on the Bin Picking task (Figure 6) illustrated that GenReward enabled the policy to complete the grasp in fewer steps and achieve the goal more effectively than baselines, including Dense Reward and RoboCLIP, while TADPoLe and Diffusion Reward exhibited notable failures.

**Results with Sparse Rewards and Other Benchmarks:**
- GenReward maintained consistent improvements even under challenging sparse reward settings (Figure A for Meta-World Bin Picking), demonstrating its robustness in environments where intrinsic rewards are crucial.
- On DCS and Adroit tasks (Table A), GenReward consistently outperformed baselines across various environments and reward settings. For instance, in Walker Walk, it achieved 782 ± 110 return, surpassing Dense Reward (640 ± 74) and RoboCLIP (695 ± 94).

**Model Analyses:**
- **Ablation Studies:**
 - Removing the video-level reward component from GenReward resulted in a performance degradation (Figure 8, Left), highlighting the necessity of trajectory-level guidance.
 - Removing the frame-level forward-backward (FB) reward also led to reduced performance, emphasizing its role in achieving fine-grained goal completion.
- **Sensitivity Analyses:**
 - The weighting coefficients (α for video-level reward and β for FB reward) were found to be crucial. Both excessively small or large values for α or β negatively impacted agent performance (Figure 8, Middle and Right), indicating the importance of balancing these reward components.
- **Effects of Generated Video Domain:**
 - GenReward consistently benefited from goal-driven reward, irrespective of the source domain of the generated videos (Bridge, RT-1, or RLBench datasets) (Figure 9). This suggests the approach is robust in transferring world knowledge from various video diffusion models.
- **Computational Complexity:**
 - Training GenReward for 1 million steps on Meta-World Pick Place took approximately 106 hours, which was competitive with or faster than RoboCLIP (119 hours) and Diffusion Reward (109 hours), though slightly slower than using raw dense reward (102 hours) (Table E). This indicates an acceptable computational overhead for the benefits gained.
- **Robustness to Hallucinations and CLIP Selection:**
 - The method showed resilience to hallucinations in generated videos (Table B), with the agent still benefiting from the proposed reward mechanism.
 - While CLIP was used for goal frame selection, it occasionally did not select the *absolute most* relevant frame (Figure B). Nevertheless, the framework still achieved improved performance, suggesting that the frame-level goal, even if not perfectly optimal, contributes positively to fine-grained achievement.
 - Ablation studies (Table C) confirmed that using CLIP for goal image selection and DINOv3 for encoding yielded better performance than random selection or alternative encoders (SigLIP 2).
- **Importance of Action Information:**
 - Removing action information when computing the FB reward led to decreased performance (Table D), reinforcing the significance of incorporating action-awareness for effective fine-grained goal achievement.

Overall, the findings demonstrate that GenReward effectively addresses the challenge of reward engineering by leveraging video diffusion models to provide multi-granular, goal-driven, and action-aware intrinsic rewards, leading to significant performance improvements across various complex RL tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The GenReward framework presents several significant contributions to the field of Reinforcement Learning and offers substantial potential impact:

1. **Reduced Reliance on Handcrafted Rewards:** The most direct significance is its ability to bypass the complex, labor-intensive, and expertise-dependent process of designing programmatic reward functions. By automatically generating goal-driven rewards from video diffusion models, GenReward significantly lowers the barrier to entry for applying RL to new and complex tasks. This can accelerate research and development in areas where reward engineering is a major bottleneck.

2. **Effective Transfer of World Knowledge:** The framework demonstrates a robust mechanism for transferring the rich, implicit world knowledge embedded in large-scale video diffusion models to guide RL agents. Generative models, especially those trained on vast video datasets, learn sophisticated representations of dynamics, object interactions, and temporal sequences. GenReward successfully translates this latent knowledge into actionable reward signals, enabling agents to learn desired behaviors without explicit human demonstrations in every scenario.

3. **Multi-Granular and Action-Aware Guidance:** Providing rewards at both video-level (trajectory-level coherence) and frame-level (fine-grained state achievement) ensures comprehensive guidance for the agent. The innovative integration of action information within the forward-backward representation for frame-level rewards is particularly impactful. This action-awareness promotes more efficient and coherent goal-directed behaviors, making the agent learn not just *what* the goal state looks like, but *how* to actively reach it from its current situation.

4. **Enhanced Performance and Generalization:** The experimental results show consistent and often substantial improvements in episode returns across challenging robotic manipulation tasks (Meta-World) and robust performance in visually distracting environments (DCS). This indicates that the generative, goal-driven rewards are not only effective but can also lead to more capable and potentially more generalizable policies compared to traditional methods or other VLM/diffusion-based reward approaches. The framework's effectiveness in sparse reward settings further highlights its ability to provide crucial intrinsic motivation where external signals are scarce.

5. **Robustness to Imperfections:** The findings regarding the framework's resilience to video hallucinations and non-optimal CLIP frame selections suggest practical applicability. In real-world scenarios, generated content or automated selections might not always be perfect, and the ability of GenReward to still derive beneficial signals under such conditions enhances its utility.

6. **Paving the Way for More Autonomous RL:** This work represents a step towards creating more autonomous RL agents that can learn from high-level, human-like instructions (e.g., text prompts) without requiring detailed behavioral programming or extensive demonstrations. By leveraging generative priors, RL systems can move closer to how humans learn, by understanding a desired outcome and devising a plan to achieve it. This could accelerate progress in areas such as general-purpose robotics, assistive AI, and complex task automation.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
The paper acknowledges a limitation regarding the computational overhead introduced by calculating both video-level and forward-backward rewards during training. While shown to be competitive with other baselines, this aspect could be a focus for future optimization to enhance scalability further, especially for real-time applications or extremely long training horizons. Future work could also explore methods to make the goal frame selection even more robust and adaptable, or investigate how to integrate more sophisticated representations of human intent directly into the generative reward process. The potential for GenReward to be applied to a broader range of tasks, beyond manipulation, is also an area for further exploration.
