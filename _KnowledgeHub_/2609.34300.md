# When World Models Lie: Adaptive Safety Analysis Under Wrong Imaginations

> [!summary] Summary
> An adaptive latent safety filter that grounds Hamilton-Jacobi safety reasoning in directly observed world-model error. It measures the gap between predicted and observation-inferred latent states, uses Adaptive Conformal Inference to build online uncertainty sets, and evaluates safety pessimistically by minimizing the learned value over those sets. With a finite-time coverage guarantee, it cuts failures versus prior latent safety filters in simulation and hardware while preserving task success.

## Problem

- Latent safety filters learn safety value functions inside world-model imagination, so biased or hallucinating dynamics propagate into overconfident safety estimates that delay interventions exactly when needed.
- UNISafe's ensemble disagreement (Jensen-Renyi divergence) can stay low when predictions are systematically biased, leaving the filter confidently wrong under dynamics mismatch such as a slippery region.
- ACoFi's value-target consistency residual measures self-consistency within an incorrect model rather than disagreement with the real system, so it misses accumulated world-model prediction error.

## Method

- Pessimistic imaginations: replace the nominal value at the predicted next latent with its minimum over an ==uncertainty ball==, solved via ==projected gradient descent==, then filter nominal actions with the threshold rule.
- ==Adaptive Conformal Inference== on latent prediction error: the ==nonconformity score== is the L2 gap between predicted and encoder-inferred latents, and online miscoverage updates expand or shrink the uncertainty radius.
- Trajectory-level conformal ==trust region==: a calibration set fixes a maximum radius capping pessimism, starting fully cautious, with a theorem bounding long-run miscoverage by target level plus calibration tolerance plus a finite-time term.

## Results

- Franka ManiSkill globe-to-shelf with natural model errors: **11%** failures versus **44%** LatentSafe, **36%** ACoFi, **48%** unfiltered at **51%** success; UNISafe gets **12%** failures but **67%** incompletion.
- Under deployment-time world-model shifts (time-step mismatch, observation staleness), it keeps the highest success (**40%**) with **21.5%** failures versus **43.25%** LatentSafe and **40.75%** ACoFi; UNISafe stalls at **77.25%** incompletion.
- Hardware Franka tomato-throw (**480** runs): rescues **16**/20 failures under observation staleness versus at most **8** for baselines, **16**/20 on forceful throws, at **39.59** ms per step on an RTX **5090**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Auxiliary uncertainty signals like ensemble disagreement or critic residuals inherit the world model's blind spots; observation-grounded prediction error is a more trustworthy indicator of model mismatch.
> - Scaling pessimism to measured error, rather than treating out-of-distribution states as unsafe, avoids stalling the robot and preserves recovery and task completion in contact-rich manipulation.
> - Conformal guarantees assume calibration-deployment exchangeability, which safety interventions themselves can break when recovery actions are absent from calibration data; such filters stay reactive, not formally safe.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A Franka Panda robot uses a dustpan to throw a tomato into a trash can. Its safety filter reasons through a learned world model, and that model can confidently predict the wrong thing. The authors tested two mismatch conditions: a forceful throw, and observation staleness, imposed by conditioning the model on delayed observations with $L=10$.

The filter does not trust the model's prediction outright. It checks how the prediction disagrees with what is then observed, and it considers less favorable possibilities before allowing the proposed action. Each condition used 20 failed and 20 successful nominal trajectory replays, with failure defined as the tomato landing outside the trash can. Under observation staleness, the method rescued 16 of 20 failed replays, compared with 8 of 20 for LatentSafe, the strongest other method there. This is evidence from open-loop replays, and it is not a general guarantee for live deployment.

Columns are In-distribution, Forceful Throw, and Observation Staleness. Ours has the most rescues in each condition. UNISafe is close in Forceful Throw (13 versus 16), and Ours preserves 19/20 successes under stale observations.

### 2. Turning mismatch into caution

A world model is a learned, compact predictor of the next latent state from the current latent state and action. A latent state is the model's compressed representation of an observation. A latent safety filter uses a learned safety value $V$ to decide between two options: let a task policy act, or switch to a learned safety fallback.

The method works in a sequence of steps:

1. It compares the predicted latent state with the latent state inferred from the new observation.
1. It treats that discrepancy as an online error score.
1. It applies Adaptive Conformal Inference (ACI) to set an uncertainty radius. ACI is an online method that adjusts a score quantile in response to observed misses.
1. It forms a neighborhood of that radius around the next predicted latent state.
1. It minimizes the nominal safety value over the neighborhood, using projected gradient descent.
1. It feeds that pessimistic value into the filter's existing switching decision.

A larger observed error expands the set of plausible states and can lower the safety value, prompting earlier fallback. With little online data, the radius starts at its calibrated maximum, so the method begins cautiously. It can then adapt as observations accumulate. The maximum radius involves a trade-off: covering more model error versus considering latent states that may not be physically meaningful.

### 3. A different signal from prior methods

[[2505.00779|UNISafe]] uses ensemble disagreement (Jensen–Rényi Divergence, or JRD) to identify out-of-distribution latent states and treats them as unsafe. [ACoFi](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2604.18482) adapts a safety threshold using the discrepancy between a learned value estimate and its bootstrapped target. The paper argues that neither signal necessarily exposes a world model that is consistently wrong. Ensemble members can agree on a biased prediction, and a value estimate can be self-consistent with a faulty model. The proposed signal instead compares a prediction directly with the observation-inferred latent state.

The paper tests this in a Dubins-car example. There, a "slippery" region raises speed by a factor of 1.5 over the training dynamics, and the world model was trained without the faster zone. At entry, around $t=10$, the method's observed prediction error rises and its filter intervenes earlier. Meanwhile, ACoFi's score stays at zero for several steps, and UNISafe's JRD score remains below its calibrated threshold until collision. This is reported behavior in this test; it does not establish that those baselines always fail.

The hardware stale-observation result tests the same contrast in a different setting. That experiment is open-loop, so UNISafe does not experience the persistent covariate shift or robot stalling reported for the closed-loop simulation.

### 4. Boundaries

The ACI result is a finite-time bound on the fraction of observed latent prediction errors that exceed the adaptive radius. It holds with confidence conditional on exchangeability between the deployment trajectory and the calibration set. It does not guarantee collision-free behavior, nor that every latent state considered is physically realizable.

The method is reactive and does not provide formal safety guarantees. Its pessimistic optimization also costs more computation than existing methods. On the hardware platform, the reported average was 39.59 ms per step on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5090, which the authors say fits the 15 Hz control budget.

When imagined and observed dynamics diverge, the filter uses that discrepancy to test safety less optimistically before intervening.
