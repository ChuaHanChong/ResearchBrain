# Learning Visual N-Grams from Web Data

> [!summary] Summary
> A model trained on web image-comment pairs predicts arbitrary visual n-grams (multi-word phrases) using a differentiable Jelinek-Mercer smoothing loss, demonstrating improved performance in phrase-level image tagging, retrieval, and zero-shot transfer compared to baseline methods. The approach learns nuanced visual concepts directly from noisy, large-scale web data, addressing the scalability limitations of traditional supervised computer vision.

## Problem

- Traditional supervised image recognition systems, relying on manually annotated datasets, are unscalable for 'open-world' scenarios with an enormous and dynamic number of visual concepts.
- Vast quantities of image data and noisy user-generated metadata available on the web remain largely underutilized for comprehensive visual understanding.
- Existing vision-language models often focus on single words or full sentences, sometimes failing to capture nuanced multi-word concepts, and recurrent models can be slow for inference.

## Method

- A **visual n-gram model**, comprising a ==ResNet-34== backbone and an ==n-gram embedding matrix==, is trained on 30 million image-comment pairs from the YFCC100M dataset.
- The paper introduces a novel, ==differentiable Jelinek-Mercer (J-M) loss== function that recursively composes likelihoods for all n-grams, including infrequent and out-of-dictionary phrases, from their smaller sub-grams.
- ==Stochastic gradient descent over outputs== is employed to efficiently handle the large output vocabulary of 142,806 n-grams during training, enabling a computationally efficient ==feed-forward prediction== model.

## Results

- The Jelinek-Mercer loss model consistently outperformed baseline approaches in phrase-level image tagging (e.g., **20% relative improvement** in Recall@k) and phrase-based image retrieval on the YFCC100M dataset.
- The model demonstrated an ability to distinguish visually similar but semantically distinct n-grams (e.g., 'Market Street' vs. 'street market'), showcasing its capacity for nuanced visual understanding.
- Without finetuning, the model achieved performance comparable to or better than state-of-the-art language models on standard captioning benchmarks (COCO, Flickr) and exhibited non-trivial zero-shot transfer capabilities to out-of-domain classification datasets.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Differentiable Jelinek-Mercer smoothing effectively enables learning visual representations for rare and out-of-dictionary phrases by recursively leveraging their constituent parts, making the model robust to noisy and vast vocabularies.
> - Large-scale, noisy web data, specifically image-comment pairs from YFCC100M, serves as a rich training source for learning nuanced visual concepts (multi-word phrases) without requiring manual annotation.
> - A feed-forward convolutional network, combined with a specialized loss function, can achieve strong performance in image-conditioned phrase prediction tasks while offering efficiency benefits over recurrent networks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Traditional approaches to visual recognition have relied heavily on manually annotated datasets, such as ImageNet or COCO, which define a fixed, relatively small set of discrete classes. While successful, this paradigm faces significant limitations when applied to "open-world" scenarios where the number of visual concepts is vast, dynamic, and includes generic objects, landmarks, scenes, and actions. Manually annotating images for tens of thousands or even millions of such classes becomes economically and practically infeasible.

This paper positions itself within the growing research trend of **webly supervised learning** (also known as weakly supervised or self-supervised learning) for computer vision. This field seeks to leverage the immense quantities of image data available on the web (e.g., Flickr, Instagram) along with their associated, often noisy, metadata (tags, comments, captions, geolocations) as a substitute for expensive manual annotations. Prior work in this area has explored using search engine results, tags, or simple image-text pairs for learning, but often focused on single-word concepts or introduced unquantifiable biases.

Furthermore, this research contributes to the broader goal of **bridging the semantic gap between vision and language**. Many existing approaches in this domain, such as image captioning, typically rely on recurrent neural networks (RNNs) trained on meticulously curated datasets with "ground-truth" captions. While effective for generation, RNNs can be slow at inference time and often struggle with the vast and noisy vocabulary of real-world web data. The paper implicitly challenges the sole reliance on recurrent models for image-language understanding by proposing a more efficient feed-forward alternative for "phrase prediction."

The concept of "visual n-grams" itself is an innovation. While n-grams are fundamental in language modeling, their direct application to visual recognition in a feed-forward, image-conditioned manner, especially with differentiable smoothing, is a novel contribution. Previous work often treated images and text in separate pipelines or relied on joint embeddings primarily at the word or sentence level. By focusing on multi-word phrases (n-grams), the authors aim to capture more nuanced and specific visual concepts than single words, addressing a critical shortcoming of earlier weakly supervised methods that might confuse "city park" with "Park City" or "Washington DC" with "Washington State."

In essence, this work pushes the boundaries of scalable visual recognition by:
- Moving beyond discrete, manually annotated classes to continuous, phrase-level concepts.
- Demonstrating the power of truly unsupervised web data for training.
- Introducing a novel, efficient deep learning architecture for image-conditioned phrase prediction.
- Addressing the inherent noise and vast vocabulary of web data through language modeling inspired techniques.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop **visual n-gram models that can predict arbitrary phrases relevant to the content of an image**, trained solely on large numbers of images and their associated user comments from the web, *without* requiring any manually labeled images.

The key motivations driving this objective are:

1. **Scalability for Open-World Recognition:** Traditional supervised image recognition systems, which require thousands of manually annotated images per class, are inherently unscalable to the "open-world setting." In this setting, the number of visual concepts to recognize is potentially enormous and highly varied (e.g., specific landmarks, actions, and nuanced object descriptions). Manual annotation simply cannot keep pace with this demand.
2. **Leveraging Abundant Web Data:** Photo-sharing websites like Flickr contain an effectively infinite supply of images paired with user-generated metadata (tags, comments, captions, geolocations). While this metadata is often noisy and "weakly" supervised, it represents a vast, untapped resource for learning. The motivation is to devise methods that can effectively extract meaningful visual-semantic relationships from this readily available, large-scale, uncurated data.
3. **Bridging the Semantic Gap with Nuance:** Previous efforts to relate images and language often focused on single words or full sentences, sometimes failing to capture the subtle distinctions between closely related concepts (e.g., "city park" vs. "Park City"). By focusing on "n-grams" (multi-word phrases), the authors aim to predict more precise and descriptive visual concepts, thereby taking a significant step in bridging the semantic gap between vision and language.
4. **Addressing the Out-of-Vocabulary (OOV) Problem:** Traditional image recognition models have a fixed vocabulary of classes. Real-world applications often encounter new or unseen concepts. The motivation is to develop a model that can handle such "out-of-vocabulary" phrases naturally, rather than being limited to a predefined set of labels.
5. **Efficiency and Practical Deployment:** While recurrent networks are powerful for language generation, their inference can be slow. A key motivation is to develop a feed-forward model that is computationally more efficient at test time, making it more suitable for deployment in real-world, high-throughput applications.

In summary, the paper aims to build a robust, scalable, and nuanced image understanding system by creatively adapting language modeling principles to vision, all while relying entirely on the abundant, albeit noisy, information found on the web.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core methodology revolves around training a convolutional neural network (CNN) to predict visual n-grams from images, utilizing a novel loss function inspired by language modeling smoothing techniques.

#### 3.1. Dataset

The primary dataset used for training is **YFCC100M**, a massive collection of 99.2 million images with associated multi-lingual user comments. For this research, the authors filtered the dataset to include only images with English user comments, resulting in approximately 30 million image-comment pairs for training and testing.
- **Text Preprocessing:** Punctuation was removed, and `[BEGIN]` and `[END]` tokens were added to each sentence.
- **Image Preprocessing:** Images were resized to 256x256 pixels, central 224x224 crops were taken, and pixel values were normalized (mean subtraction, std deviation division).
- **N-gram Dictionary:** A dictionary of English n-grams (from unigrams to five-grams) was created, including all phrases with more than 1,000 occurrences in the 30 million English comments. This resulted in a dictionary of 142,806 n-grams (22,869 unigrams, 56,830 bigrams, 32,560 trigrams, 17,351 four-grams, and 13,196 five-grams). The crucial aspect is that the smoothed n-gram models are trained and evaluated on *all* n-grams in the dataset, not just those in the dictionary.

#### 3.2. Model Architecture

The visual n-gram model consists of two main components:
- **Convolutional Network (I;✓):** A **ResNet-34** is used as the backbone to extract high-level image features. This network is initialized with weights pre-trained on ImageNet.
- **N-gram Embedding Matrix (E):** This matrix (D x |D|, where D is feature dimension and |D| is dictionary size) maps image features to likelihoods for each n-gram in the dictionary. The likelihood for an n-gram is computed by the inner product between the image features and its corresponding embedding vector in `E`, followed by a softmax function.

#### 3.3. Loss Functions

The paper introduces two loss functions:

- **Naive N-gram Loss:** This is a standard multi-class logistic loss. It calculates the negative log-likelihood for in-dictionary n-grams present in a comment. Out-of-dictionary n-grams are entirely ignored during training. While simple, it has limitations in handling infrequent or unseen phrases. At prediction time, an ad-hoc "stupid" back-off model is used to infer probabilities for out-of-dictionary n-grams from smaller sub-grams.

- **Jelinek-Mercer (J-M) Loss:** This is the core technical contribution. Inspired by Jelinek-Mercer smoothing in traditional language modeling, this novel differentiable loss function addresses the shortcomings of the naive loss by:
 - Considering *all* n-grams (in-dictionary and out-of-dictionary) during training.
 - For infrequent or out-of-dictionary n-grams, their likelihood is recursively composed from the likelihoods of smaller sub-grams. This is achieved by combining the direct "observational likelihood" (from the softmax over dictionary n-grams) with a smoothed likelihood from shorter (n-1)-grams, using a smoothing constant `λ`.
 - Crucially, this recursive smoothing process is designed to be **differentiable**, allowing gradients to be backpropagated through the convolutional network and the n-gram embedding matrix. This enables the model to learn meaningful representations even for rare or unseen phrases by leveraging their constituent parts.

#### 3.4. Training Procedure

- **Initialization:** The ResNet-34 is initialized with ImageNet-trained weights.
- **Optimization:** Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) with Nesterov momentum (0.9), weight decay (0.0001), and an initial learning rate of 0.1 (decaying by 10 when loss stabilizes, to a minimum of 0.001) is used.
- **Batch Size:** 128.
- **Epochs:** 10.
- **Computational Efficiency:** A major challenge is the large number of outputs (142,806 n-grams). To circumvent the computational intensity of a full softmax over all outputs, the authors employ **stochastic gradient descent over outputs**, where only a random subset of negative examples (columns of E) are considered during each forward-backward pass, along with all positive n-grams in the batch. This approximation significantly reduces training time.

This carefully designed methodology allows the model to learn robust visual representations for a vast vocabulary of phrases directly from noisy web data, effectively addressing the scalability and expressiveness limitations of traditional supervised approaches.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper evaluates the proposed visual n-gram models across four key tasks, consistently demonstrating the superiority of the Jelinek-Mercer (J-M) loss over naive approaches and baselines.

#### 4.1. Phrase-level Image Tagging

- **Perplexity:** Measured on a held-out YFCC100M test set (10,000 images, in-dictionary unigrams only).
 - J-M loss (276) significantly outperforms Naive n-gram (297) and ImageNet + linear baseline (349).
 - Applying J-M smoothing at test time further reduces perplexity for both naive and J-M trained models, highlighting the benefit of propagating probability mass.
- **Recall@k for Phrase Prediction:** Measures how often a comment's n-grams are among the top-k predicted n-grams.
 - J-M loss (R@1=6.2, R@5=13.0, R@10=18.1) consistently outperforms Naive n-gram (5.5, 11.6, 15.1) and ImageNet + linear (5.0, 10.7, 14.5). This represents approximately a 20% relative improvement in Recall@k for the best model over the ImageNet baseline.
- **Human Evaluation Accuracy:** Human raters assessed the relevance of the highest-scoring predicted n-gram to the image.
 - J-M loss model achieved 42.0% accuracy, indicating that a substantial portion of its top predictions are visually relevant according to human judgment, despite noisy training data.
- **Effect of N-gram Length:** Performance generally increases with longer n-grams included in the dictionary (up to five-grams), but with diminishing returns beyond trigrams. This validates the "n-gram" approach for capturing more complex concepts.

#### 4.2. Phrase-based Image Retrieval

- **Qualitative Results:** The model effectively distinguishes visually similar but semantically distinct n-grams like "Market Street" vs. "street market," "city park" vs. "Park City," and various "Washington" related concepts (state, city, baseball team, hockey team). This vividly demonstrates the model's ability to learn nuanced visual representations for multi-word phrases.
- **Quantitative Precision-Recall (P-R) Curve:** Shows the J-M model significantly outperforms the Naive n-gram and ImageNet baselines in retrieving relevant images for n-gram queries.
- **Noisy Ground Truth Acknowledgment:** The authors candidly admit that overall precision values are fairly low, attributing this to the "false-negative noise" in the YFCC100M "ground truth," where many relevant images might not be explicitly associated with a given n-gram in the comments. This is a crucial point for understanding the challenges of webly supervised learning.

#### 4.3. Relating Images and Captions

- **Performance on YFCC100M Test Set:**
 - **Caption Retrieval (Image to Caption):** J-M model (R@1=7.1, R@5=16.7, R@10=21.5) vastly outperforms baselines (e.g., ImageNet + linear: R@1=1.1) and even a unigram-only model. Human rating showed 53.1% relevant caption retrieval for the J-M model.
- **Cross-Dataset Evaluation (COCO-5K, Flickr-30K, COCO-1K):** Models trained on YFCC100M were evaluated directly on these standard captioning benchmarks.
 - **Image Retrieval (Caption to Image) and Caption Retrieval (Image to Caption):** The J-M model, even without finetuning on these datasets, performed roughly **on par with state-of-the-art *language models*** (e.g., BRNN, M-RNN) on both COCO and Flickr datasets.
 - It performed worse than models *dedicated specifically to retrieval tasks* (e.g., Deep CCA, Wang et al.) on Flickr-30K, but surprisingly *on par* with a state-of-the-art retrieval model on COCO-5K.
 - **Finetuning Benefit:** When finetuned on the target datasets, the J-M model's performance significantly improved, reaching or exceeding many state-of-the-art baselines, demonstrating its strong representational power and adaptability.
 - **Large Vocabulary Advantage:** The authors emphasize that their model operates with a much larger vocabulary (142K n-grams) compared to most baselines, implying its strong performance generalizes to a wider range of visual concepts.

#### 4.4. Zero-Shot Transfer

- The Flickr-trained J-M models were applied directly (without finetuning) to out-of-domain datasets (aYahoo, SUN, ImageNet) for classification.
- **Results:** Non-trivial accuracies were achieved (e.g., 88.9% on aYahoo in-dictionary classes, 35.2% on ImageNet in-dictionary classes). Performance was particularly strong on common classes (aYahoo) and less so on fine-grained classifications (ImageNet, due to lack of specific breeds in YFCC100M).
- **Significance:** This demonstrates the **generalizability and transferability** of the learned visual representations to new tasks and datasets without explicit training, a crucial capability for open-world AI.

Overall, the results strongly support the efficacy of learning visual n-grams from web data, particularly with the novel Jelinek-Mercer smoothing loss. The model's ability to handle multi-word concepts, its strong performance in various retrieval tasks, and its impressive zero-shot transfer capabilities highlight its potential for scalable and robust visual understanding.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research offers several significant contributions and has a substantial potential impact on the field of computer vision and its applications:

1. **Scalable Visual Understanding in Open-World Settings:** The most profound impact lies in demonstrating a viable pathway towards building visual recognition systems that are not constrained by the limited, fixed vocabularies of manually annotated datasets. By leveraging vast, readily available web data and learning "visual n-grams," the model can recognize tens of thousands of diverse visual concepts, including highly specific phrases and landmarks. This is crucial for real-world applications where the "classes" are virtually infinite and constantly evolving.

2. **Paradigm Shift Towards Webly Supervised Learning:** The paper reinforces the growing importance of weakly or webly supervised learning. It provides a strong case that meaningful visual-semantic representations can be learned purely from noisy, unstructured web data, thereby reducing the immense cost and effort associated with manual data annotation. This approach is essential for achieving the scale and versatility required for general-purpose visual AI.

3. **Novel Differentiable Language Model Smoothing for Vision:** The introduction of the differentiable Jelinek-Mercer loss is a key technical innovation. It effectively translates a powerful smoothing technique from traditional language modeling into the deep learning paradigm for vision-language tasks. This allows the model to handle the sparsity of n-gram occurrences and learn from infrequent or even out-of-dictionary phrases, making the learning process more robust and comprehensive. This approach could inspire new loss functions in other domains dealing with compositional data and vast vocabularies.

4. **Efficient Inference and Deployment:** By opting for a feed-forward convolutional network structure rather than recurrent networks, the proposed model offers significantly faster inference times. This is a critical advantage for real-world deployment in applications requiring high throughput, such as large-scale image search, content moderation, or real-time visual assistance.

5. **Improved Nuance in Vision-Language Understanding:** The ability to distinguish between nuanced phrases like "city park" and "Park City" or different "Washington" entities highlights the model's capacity to learn finer-grained visual semantics. This goes beyond simple object recognition and moves towards a more comprehensive understanding of visual scenes and their contextual descriptions.

6. **Enhanced Transferability and Zero-Shot Learning:** The strong performance in zero-shot transfer experiments without any finetuning on the target datasets is highly significant. It indicates that the web-trained visual n-gram embeddings capture highly generalizable and robust visual features that can be directly applied to new tasks and domains, a desirable trait for flexible AI systems. The close relation to standard image classification (compared to RNNs) facilitates visual grounding via techniques like CAM.

In the long term, this work lays important groundwork for future research in:
- **More advanced web supervision:** Exploring other forms of web metadata (e.g., geotags, timestamps, social graph information) and more sophisticated methods to handle noise.
- **Continual learning:** As new concepts emerge on the web, visual n-gram models could potentially be updated more efficiently to incorporate them.
- **Multimodal AI:** The approach of bridging vision and language with shared phrase embeddings could be extended to other modalities, or integrated into more complex reasoning systems like visual question answering or scene graph generation.

By providing a scalable, efficient, and nuanced approach to visual understanding from web data, this paper makes a substantial contribution towards developing "open-world" AI systems capable of perceiving and interacting with the world's vast visual information.
