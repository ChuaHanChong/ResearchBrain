# MiCA Learns More Knowledge Than LoRA and Full Fine-Tuning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the RAIR Lab and an independent researcher introduced Minor Component Adaptation (MiCA), a parameter-efficient fine-tuning method for large language models that adapts underutilized minor singular directions of pre-trained weights. MiCA achieved up to 5.9x better knowledge acquisition on novel domain data while using 6-60% fewer trainable parameters than LoRA, without substantially degrading general capabilities.

## Problem

- Adapting large language models to specialized tasks requires substantial computational resources for full fine-tuning.
- Existing parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PeFT) methods, like LoRA, do not explicitly target specific adaptation subspaces, potentially leading to updates overlapping with dominant components.
- This implicit subspace learning may result in inefficient use of representational capacity or shifts in previously acquired knowledge.

## Method

- **MiCA** extends LoRA by explicitly incorporating the Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) of pre-trained weight matrices to identify and adapt minor singular directions.
- It initializes the adapter matrix 'B' using the least significant singular vectors (minor components) from the pre-trained weight matrix 'W', while 'A' is initialized to zero and solely optimized.
- A two-stage fine-tuning strategy combines the task-specific fine-tuned base model with the instruction-tuning delta to retain general instruction-following capabilities.

## Results

- MiCA achieved **61.33%** accuracy on the BLOGS-MC dataset with Llama-2-7B, outperforming LoRA (**58.28%**) by **3.05** percentage points and utilizing approximately **6%** of LoRA's trainable parameters (rank 16 vs 128).
- On the German HISTORY-MC dataset, MiCA attained **39.2%** accuracy, surpassing LoRA (**29.4%**) and full fine-tuning (**30.4%**) for knowledge uptake.
- An ablation study demonstrated that minor-r adaptation (MiCA) consistently outperformed major-r adaptation and random component adaptation on the BLOGS-MC benchmark, validating its hypothesis for effective domain adaptation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Minor singular directions of pre-trained weight matrices offer a plastic and effective subspace for integrating new, task-specific knowledge, as opposed to dominant components saturated with generic knowledge.
> - Constraining adaptation to these spectrally identified minor components enhances knowledge acquisition and significantly reduces the number of trainable parameters.
> - Explicitly selecting adaptation subspaces based on spectral properties of pre-trained weights, rather than implicitly learning them, can lead to more stable and efficient model specialization.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The rapid expansion of large language models (LLMs) has led to challenges in adapting these models to specialized tasks and domains due to the substantial computational resources required for full fine-tuning. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PeFT) methods have emerged as a practical alternative, enabling model adaptation by modifying a comparatively small subset of parameters while preserving the majority of pre-trained weights. Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) is a prominent PeFT approach that introduces trainable low-rank matrices into existing model layers, effectively representing weight updates in a low-dimensional subspace and significantly reducing the number of trainable parameters and memory footprint.

However, existing low-rank adaptation methods, including LoRA, typically do not explicitly address which specific subspace of model representations should be adapted. In standard LoRA, the low-rank factors are optimized freely, leading to an implicitly learned adaptation subspace that may evolve during training. This flexibility does not account for the spectral structure of the pre-trained weight matrix and may result in updates overlapping with dominant components that encode general pre-trained capabilities. Such overlap can potentially lead to interference, inefficient use of representational capacity, or shifts in previously acquired knowledge, even if less prone to catastrophic forgetting than full fine-tuning. While some recent approaches incorporate Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) for initialization, the adaptation parameters often remain free to evolve, potentially drifting towards dominant components. This observation highlights a limitation in current PeFT methods, suggesting that the choice and stability of the adaptation subspace could play a crucial role in efficient specialization.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to introduce Minor Component Adaptation (MiCA), a novel parameter-efficient fine-tuning method that addresses the limitations of existing approaches by explicitly targeting underutilized subspaces of model representations for adaptation. MiCA aims to enhance knowledge acquisition, minimize the parameter footprint, and mitigate catastrophic forgetting more effectively than current methods like LoRA.

The core motivation for MiCA stems from the hypothesis that a subset of minor components, specifically those associated with low singular values in a weight matrix, can offer critical task-specific adaptability. Conventional low-rank approximations prioritize dominant directions, which capture the maximum variance and account for a larger portion of the original matrix weights. MiCA inverts this logic, proposing that less-expressed directions, while seemingly less significant in a variance sense, may offer higher marginal utility for integrating new, task-specific knowledge. This is particularly relevant when the dominant subspace is already highly saturated with generic, pre-trained knowledge.

MiCA draws an analogy to Minor Component Analysis (MCA), which focuses on eigenvectors associated with the smallest eigenvalues, as opposed to Principal Component Analysis (PCA) which targets dominant ones. Minor components are typically orthogonal to the dominant subspace and may contain weak signals or structures that are often overlooked. By adapting these spectrally identified minor components, MiCA seeks to provide a data-driven, semantically grounded mechanism for efficient model adaptation. This approach is intended to preserve core model capabilities more effectively while enhancing domain-specific expressiveness by channeling updates into directions that are hypothesized to be both underused and maximally informative for new learning tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

MiCA extends the architecture of LoRA by explicitly incorporating the Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) of the pre-trained weight matrix. For a given pre-trained weight matrix W ∈ R^(d×d) (or rectangular W ∈ R^(d_out × d_in)), its SVD is expressed as W = U Σ Vᵀ, where U and V are orthogonal matrices of left and right singular vectors, respectively, and Σ is a diagonal matrix containing singular values σ₁ ≥ σ₂ ≥ ... ≥ σ_d ≥ 0.

**Integration with Transformer Layers:**
Similar to LoRA, MiCA integrates into transformer attention layers by adding low-rank matrices A ∈ R^(r×d) and B ∈ R^(d×r). The weight update is defined as ∆W = (α/r) BA, and the final adapted weights are W_final = W + ∆W. The adapters are applied to the query and value projection matrices (q_proj, v_proj) within each transformer block.

**Parameter Selection and Injection Points:**
A key distinction of MiCA lies in its parameter selection and initialization. Instead of randomly initializing both A and B, MiCA specifically selects the least significant singular vectors from the SVD of W to form the basis for updates. For a constrained output space, the procedure involves:
1. Computing the complete SVD of W: W = U Σ Vᵀ.
2. Selecting the last r singular vectors of U, denoted as U_r = U[:, -r:].
3. Initializing B as U_r and A as a zero matrix (A = 0).
This initialization means that the updated matrix ∆W projects into subspaces corresponding to small singular values. During training, only the matrix A is optimized via gradient descent, while the original weights W and the matrix B (derived from the minor singular vectors) are kept frozen. This design constrains adaptation to fixed minor singular directions.

**Training Procedure:**
MiCA employs a two-stage fine-tuning strategy that leverages the separation of pre-training and instruction-tuning phases common in LLMs. Instead of directly fine-tuning an instruction-tuned model, MiCA fine-tunes the base model (pre-trained without instruction-following capabilities) on the target task. After this task-specific fine-tuning, the resulting model weights are additively composed with the delta between the instruction-tuned model and its corresponding base model:
θ_final = θ_FT_base + (θ_instr - θ_base)
Here, θ_FT_base represents the fine-tuned base model, and (θ_instr - θ_base) is the instruction delta derived from the original instruction tuning. This technique assumes a near-linear locality in weight space and aims to retain strong task-specific alignment while reintroducing general instruction-following capabilities without costly end-to-end instruction re-tuning.

**Experimental Setup:**
The effectiveness of MiCA was evaluated through a suite of experiments across various datasets, models, and comparative methods:
- **Datasets:**
 - **BLOGS dataset:** OpenAI blog posts from May and June 2024, expanded via GPT-4 paraphrasing, to introduce novel domain knowledge. A synthetic multiple-choice QA dataset (BLOGS-MC) was generated from this content.
 - **HISTORY dataset:** A 300-page German history book from 2024, representing another source of out-of-distribution knowledge. A custom-built multiple-choice QA dataset (HISTORY-MC) was used for evaluation.
 - **General Knowledge Benchmarks:** TruthfulQA (mc1) and Hellaswag were used to assess adversarial robustness, factuality, and catastrophic forgetting.
- **Models:** Llama-2-7B (knowledge cutoff July 2023) and Qwen2.5-7B (knowledge cutoff October 2023) were used as base models.
- **Comparative Methods:**
 - Full fine-tuning (Full FT): Updating all model parameters.
 - LoRA: The leading parameter-efficient fine-tuning method.
 - Pre-trained models: Llama-2-7B-chat and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct served as non-adapted baselines.
Hyperparameters (adapter rank r, learning rate, training epochs) were optimized independently for each method to ensure fair comparison.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation demonstrated that MiCA consistently achieved improved knowledge acquisition and greater parameter efficiency compared to LoRA and full fine-tuning across various tasks.

#### 4.1. BLOGS dataset Experiments

For the BLOGS dataset, which introduced novel domain knowledge from OpenAI blog posts, MiCA was evaluated on Llama-2-7B and Qwen2.5-7B models.
- **Knowledge Uptake (BLOGS-MC Accuracy):**
 - **Llama-2-7B-chat:** MiCA achieved 61.33% accuracy, surpassing the baseline model (56.18%) by 5.15 percentage points and the optimal LoRA configuration (58.28%) by 3.05 points.
 - **Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct:** MiCA reached 75.63% accuracy, outperforming LoRA (73.87%) and the instruct baseline (72.91%).
- **Parameter Efficiency:** MiCA demonstrated a significantly reduced parameter footprint. For the Llama model, MiCA used an adapter rank (r) of 16, while LoRA required r=128 for its best performance. Additionally, MiCA updates only one of the two LoRA matrices (matrix A while B is frozen), effectively halving its parameter count relative to standard LoRA for the same rank. This resulted in approximately 6% of the trainable parameters of LoRA for the Llama model, while achieving superior or competitive performance. For the Qwen model, both methods achieved optimal BLOGS-MC scores at r=32, but MiCA still required only about 60% of LoRA's parameter count due to the fixed B matrix.
- **General Knowledge Benchmarks:** On TruthfulQA and HellaSwag, MiCA's performance remained comparable to or slightly varied from LoRA and the baseline, indicating that the new knowledge acquisition did not substantially degrade general capabilities.

#### 4.2. HISTORY dataset Experiments

The second continued pre-training test involved the larger German HISTORY dataset, evaluated using Llama-2-7B.
- **Knowledge Uptake (HISTORY-MC Accuracy):** MiCA achieved an accuracy of 39.2% on the custom multiple-choice questions, which was significantly higher than LoRA (29.4%), the fully fine-tuned chat model (30.4%), and the original chat variant (27.4%).
- **Catastrophic Forgetting:** Analysis on the HellaSwag dataset showed similar performance for PeFT methods (approximately 57-58% accuracy), indicating minimal catastrophic forgetting, whereas full fine-tuning led to more significant degradation.
- **Performance Scaling:** A comparison between the smaller BLOGS experiments and the larger HISTORY corpus suggested that the performance gap between MiCA and LoRA widened with increased domain data. This indicates that constraining adaptation to minor singular directions might become more beneficial in higher-signal regimes, potentially reducing the risk of overwriting dominant pre-trained structures.

#### 4.3. Ablation Study: Minor vs. Major Singular Subspaces

An ablation study on Qwen-2.5-7B using the BLOGS dataset compared four variants to determine if MiCA's gains specifically arise from adapting minor singular directions:
1. Instruct baseline (no fine-tuning): 72.91%
2. Major-r adaptation (projection onto top-r singular vectors): 74.21%
3. Minor-r adaptation (MiCA, projection onto bottom-r singular vectors): 75.63%
4. Random components (B initialized from r randomly chosen SVD components): 73.75%
The results demonstrated that while all subspace-restricted adaptations improved over the baseline, Minor-r adaptation (MiCA) consistently outperformed Major-r and Random component adaptation on the BLOGS-MC benchmark. The random-component variant, despite an initial improvement trajectory similar to other restricted methods, plateaued earlier and converged to a lower final accuracy than MiCA. These findings support MiCA's central hypothesis that minor singular directions constitute a more plastic and effective subspace for domain adaptation compared to dominant or randomly selected directions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

MiCA introduces a spectrally grounded approach to parameter-efficient fine-tuning that leverages the minor singular directions of pre-trained weights. The empirical results suggest that this strategy leads to improved knowledge acquisition, potentially reduced catastrophic forgetting, and significantly lower parameter counts when compared to LoRA, even under optimized training hyperparameters. For instance, MiCA demonstrated up to a 5.9x improvement in knowledge acquisition with a minimal parameter footprint of 6-60% relative to LoRA, depending on the model and task.

The significance of MiCA lies in several areas:
- **Enhanced Knowledge Acquisition:** By focusing adaptation on underutilized model subspaces, MiCA appears to integrate new factual knowledge more efficiently without substantially interfering with existing generalized capabilities. This mechanism provides a stable pathway for integrating novel information.
- **Parameter Efficiency:** The ability to achieve superior or comparable performance with a drastically smaller number of trainable parameters offers practical advantages. This reduced parameter footprint translates to lower memory consumption, smaller optimizer states, and decreased communication costs in distributed training environments.
- **Mitigation of Catastrophic Forgetting:** While PeFT methods generally reduce forgetting compared to full fine-tuning, MiCA's targeted adaptation to minor components may further stabilize knowledge retention, as observed in the general knowledge benchmarks.
- **Principled Subspace Selection:** Unlike LoRA, which implicitly learns an adaptation subspace, MiCA explicitly identifies and constrains learning to spectrally defined minor singular directions. The ablation study validates that this specific choice, rather than just any fixed low-rank subspace, contributes to its performance gains.

**Practical Implications:**
MiCA is particularly well-suited for domain specialization scenarios where the primary goal is to integrate new factual knowledge into a pre-trained LLM while carefully preserving its core capabilities. Its reduced parameter footprint makes it attractive for applications with stringent resource constraints, such as on-device adaptation of LLMs or settings involving federated learning where communication and memory are critical factors. This suggests that spectrally constrained adaptation could serve as a foundational method for deploying lightweight, domain-specific LLM variants.

**Future Research Directions:**
While the current work demonstrates consistent improvements on 7B-scale models, further research is warranted to explore MiCA's scaling behavior with respect to larger model sizes, increased dataset volumes, and greater task complexity. Additionally, investigating the potential of applying SVD to the delta weights derived from instruction fine-tuning processes, rather than directly to the base model weights, could extend MiCA's benefits to instruction-based tasks. The computational cost of performing SVD for very large layers is noted as a one-time preprocessing cost, which could be an area for optimization in future work. Finally, combining spectrally constrained adaptation with reinforcement learning objectives is suggested as a promising direction, potentially allowing for synergistic effects in stabilizing domain adaptation and preserving pre-trained capabilities while localizing representational change.
