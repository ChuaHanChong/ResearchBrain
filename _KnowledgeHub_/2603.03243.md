# HoMMI: Learning Whole-Body Mobile Manipulation from Human Demonstrations

> [!summary] Summary
> HoMMI, developed by Stanford University and Toyota Research Institute, enables mobile manipulators to acquire complex bimanual skills directly from robot-free human demonstrations. The framework achieves high success rates, such as 90% for a laundry task and 85% for a delivery task, by effectively bridging the human-to-robot embodiment gap through novel policy and control designs.

## Problem

- Traditional robot teleoperation is slow, expensive, and not scalable for diverse real-world mobile manipulation tasks.
- Existing scalable human demonstration methods (e.g., UMI) lack global task context and active perception capabilities crucial for long-horizon mobile manipulation.
- Transferring egocentric human demonstration data to robots faces significant visual and kinematic embodiment gaps, often requiring extensive robot-specific data or leading to performance degradation.

## Method

- A novel data collection interface extends bimanual **UMI** with an egocentric iPhone, utilizing ==ARKit== to establish a shared, globally consistent ==coordinate frame== across all sensors.
- A ==cross-embodiment hand-eye policy==, built on **Diffusion Policy**, employs an ==embodiment-agnostic 3D visual representation== for the head camera and a relaxed 3D "look-at point" for head actions.
- A ==constraint-aware whole-body inverse kinematics== controller translates high-level policy commands into robust low-level joint and base motions on a **Rainbow Robotics RB-Y1** mobile manipulator, ensuring accuracy, stability, and safety.

## Results

- HoMMI achieved high success rates on complex bimanual mobile manipulation tasks, including **90%** for a laundry task, **85%** for a delivery task, and **80%** for a tablescaping task on a real robot.
- The framework significantly outperformed baselines such as 'Wrist-Only' (original UMI) and 'RGB-Only' (naive egocentric), demonstrating the importance of addressing global context and embodiment mismatch.
- Active head control, driven by the predicted "look-at point," proved critical for successfully performing tasks requiring active search, precise placement, and maintaining policy observability.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Lifting egocentric observations into an embodiment-agnostic 3D visual representation, combined with masking human arms, effectively mitigates the visual discrepancy between human and robot viewpoints.
> - Representing active perception as a 3D "look-at point" for the robot's head, rather than directly mimicking 6-DoF human head poses, successfully transfers human intent while respecting robot kinematic constraints.
> - Expressing all observations and actions within a gripper-centric coordinate frame maintains consistent spatial awareness, preventing drift and inconsistencies from shifting egocentric or world frames.
