# HoMMI: Learning Whole-Body Mobile Manipulation from Human Demonstrations

> [!summary] Summary
> HoMMI, developed by Stanford University and Toyota Research Institute, enables mobile manipulators to acquire complex bimanual skills directly from robot-free human demonstrations. The framework achieves high success rates, such as 90% for a laundry task and 85% for a delivery task, by effectively bridging the human-to-robot embodiment gap through novel policy and control designs.

## Problem

- Traditional robot teleoperation is slow, expensive, and not scalable for diverse real-world mobile manipulation tasks.
- Existing scalable human demonstration methods (e.g., UMI) lack global task context and active perception capabilities crucial for long-horizon mobile manipulation.
- Transferring egocentric human demonstration data to robots faces significant visual and kinematic embodiment gaps, often requiring extensive robot-specific data or leading to performance degradation.

## Method

![[2603.03243-method.png]]
*The cross-embodiment policy integrates 3D head observations and 2D wrist views to predict end-effector trajectories and look-at points.*

- A novel data collection interface extends bimanual **UMI** with an egocentric iPhone, utilizing ==ARKit== to establish a shared, globally consistent ==coordinate frame== across all sensors.
- A ==cross-embodiment hand-eye policy==, built on **Diffusion Policy**, employs an ==embodiment-agnostic 3D visual representation== for the head camera and a relaxed 3D "look-at point" for head actions.
- A ==constraint-aware whole-body inverse kinematics== controller translates high-level policy commands into robust low-level joint and base motions on a **Rainbow Robotics RB-Y1** mobile manipulator, ensuring accuracy, stability, and safety.

## Results

- HoMMI achieved high success rates on complex bimanual mobile manipulation tasks, including **90%** for a laundry task, **85%** for a delivery task, and **80%** for a tablescaping task on a real robot.
- The framework significantly outperformed baselines such as 'Wrist-Only' (original UMI) and 'RGB-Only' (naive egocentric), demonstrating the importance of addressing global context and embodiment mismatch.
- Active head control, driven by the predicted "look-at point," proved critical for successfully performing tasks requiring active search, precise placement, and maintaining policy observability.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Lifting egocentric observations into an embodiment-agnostic 3D visual representation, combined with masking human arms, effectively mitigates the visual discrepancy between human and robot viewpoints.
> - Representing active perception as a 3D "look-at point" for the robot's head, rather than directly mimicking 6-DoF human head poses, successfully transfers human intent while respecting robot kinematic constraints.
> - Expressing all observations and actions within a gripper-centric coordinate frame maintains consistent spatial awareness, preventing drift and inconsistencies from shifting egocentric or world frames.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot learning from human demonstrations aims to enable robots to acquire complex skills by observing human actions. Traditionally, this has involved robot teleoperation, where a human directly controls a robot, generating robot-native data. While this approach minimizes the embodiment gap between human and robot, it is often resource-intensive, slow, and challenging to scale, particularly for complex mobile manipulation platforms in diverse real-world environments.

More recently, approaches like the Universal Manipulation Interface (UMI) have emerged, offering a more scalable solution. UMI utilizes portable handheld grippers equipped with wrist-mounted cameras to collect robot-free demonstrations, primarily focusing on end-effector motions. This method reduces the need for expensive robot teleoperation and allows for data collection in varied settings. However, UMI's reliance on wrist-centric sensing provides only local visual context, limiting the robot's awareness of the broader workspace, its own base motion, and the coordination between two manipulators. This local view is insufficient for tasks requiring navigation, bimanual coordination across a larger space, and active perception to track task progress or search for objects.

The current work, HoMMI, addresses these limitations by augmenting the UMI framework with egocentric (head-mounted) sensing. While egocentric views offer a global context, they introduce a significant "embodiment gap" between human and robot. This gap manifests in two primary ways:
1. **Visual Gap**: Differences in appearance (human vs. robot arms) and viewpoints (human vs. robot height and head placement).
2. **Kinematic Gap**: Discrepancies in body morphology and degrees of freedom (e.g., human neck vs. robot neck), making direct imitation of 6-DoF human head poses often infeasible for a robot.

Prior attempts to incorporate egocentric sensing often required additional robot teleoperation data for action grounding, or were restricted to fixed-base manipulation, thereby limiting their scalability and applicability to mobile manipulation. HoMMI positions itself as an advancement by explicitly bridging this human-to-robot embodiment gap, enabling scalable mobile manipulation learning directly from robot-free human demonstrations, including complex tasks involving whole-body coordination, navigation, and active perception. This research contributes to developing more adaptable and generalizable robot learning systems that can leverage readily available human demonstration data.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of the HoMMI project is to develop a general learning-from-demonstration framework that enables mobile manipulators to learn complex, long-horizon, whole-body manipulation skills directly from robot-free human demonstrations. This overarching goal is driven by several key motivations and specific design objectives:

- **Motivation for Scalability**: Manually programming intricate whole-body coordination for diverse real-world mobile manipulation tasks is prohibitively difficult. Learning from human demonstrations offers a promising alternative. However, traditional robot teleoperation for data collection is expensive, slow, and challenging to deploy for mobile manipulators in varied environments. Existing handheld data collection devices like UMI improve scalability for end-effector motions but lack global context. The authors are motivated to extend this scalability to mobile manipulation by incorporating egocentric sensing in a robot-free manner.

- **Motivation for Bridging the Embodiment Gap**: Incorporating egocentric sensing, while providing crucial global context, introduces a substantial human-to-robot embodiment gap. This gap makes direct policy transfer difficult due to:
 - **Visual Discrepancies**: Differences in appearance (human arms vs. robot arms) and viewpoints (human eye-level vs. robot eye-level, camera placement).
 - **Kinematic Incompatibilities**: Humans and robots have different body morphologies and joint configurations, particularly in the neck. Directly copying 6-DoF human head trajectories can lead to kinematically infeasible robot motions or poor tracking. The authors are motivated to explicitly bridge these gaps to ensure effective policy transfer without relying on robot-specific teleoperation data.

- **Motivation for Whole-Body Coordination**: Generalizable and effective mobile manipulation requires seamless coordination of diverse sensory inputs (e.g., head-mounted and wrist-mounted cameras) and complex action spaces (e.g., arms, torso, head, and base movements). Learning policies that can orchestrate these elements for tasks requiring bimanual coordination, navigation, and active perception is a significant challenge. The authors aim to enable robots to efficiently coordinate their entire body to achieve both precise end-effector tracking for accurate manipulation and effective active perception to intentionally gather task-relevant information.

Based on these motivations, the authors established the following design objectives for the HoMMI system:
1. **Scalability**: To develop a fast, intuitive, and portable demonstration interface for data collection in diverse environments, avoiding the limitations of robot teleoperation.
2. **Transferability**: To overcome both the visual and kinematic embodiment gaps that arise when transferring skills from human demonstrators to robots.
3. **Whole-body coordination**: To enable the robot to efficiently coordinate its whole-body actions to realize precise end-effector tracking for accurate manipulation and effective active perception for gathering task-relevant information.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The HoMMI framework integrates a data collection system, a cross-embodiment policy design, and a constraint-aware whole-body controller to facilitate learning whole-body mobile manipulation from human demonstrations.

#### 3.1. HoMMI Data Collection Interface

To achieve scalable, robot-free data collection for bimanual mobile manipulation, the system extends the UMI gripper design by incorporating egocentric sensing and head motion capture.
- **Hardware**: Three iPhones are used: two mounted on handheld grippers (replicating UMI) and one mounted on a cap for head-mounted egocentric view.
- **Synchronization**: Apple's ARKit multi-device collaboration is utilized to establish a shared, globally consistent coordinate frame across all three iPhones.
- **Data Captured**: Synchronous multimodal trajectories are recorded at 60 Hz, including RGB video, depth maps, 6-DoF poses, and gripper widths from all three iPhones.
- **Design Considerations**: The interface is designed to be intuitive and lightweight, providing direct visual and haptic feedback to the operator while avoiding motion sickness commonly associated with VR-based systems.

#### 3.2. Cross-Embodiment Hand-Eye Policy

An end-to-end visuomotor policy, based on Diffusion Policy, is trained using the collected human demonstration data. The policy conditions on a short observation window and predicts a horizon of actions. To address the embodiment gap, three key algorithmic designs are introduced:

- **4.2.1. Embodiment-Agnostic Visual Representation**:
 - **Problem**: Head-mounted RGB cameras show significant viewpoint and appearance differences between human and robot.
 - **Approach**: Instead of directly using head RGB, egocentric observations are lifted into a 3D representation.
 - **Pointmap Generation**: From each head camera frame, a 3D pointmap (using iPhone depth or stereo depth estimation on the robot) is generated.
 - **Patch-based Encoding**: The pointmap is patchified and downsampled, with each 16x16 patch corresponding to a 3D point. DINO-v3 ViT patch features are extracted from the RGB frame for each patch.
 - **3D Feature Integration**: These patch features are concatenated with a sinusoidal encoding of their corresponding 3D point, linking appearance to 3D geometry and increasing robustness to head pose/height changes.
 - **Appearance Mismatch Mitigation**: Arm points are masked out by transforming pointmaps into left/right gripper frames and discarding points with negative Z-coordinates, as arms originate behind the grippers.
 - **Observation Embedding**: An attention pooling layer processes these tokens to generate a head observation embedding. The overall observation embedding also includes 2D representations for wrist images (finetuned dinov3-vitb16 encoder) and proprioception (end-effector poses, gripper widths).

- **4.2.2. 3D Look-at Point Action Representation**:
 - **Problem**: Mobile robots have different kinematics (e.g., shorter torsos, fewer neck DoF) than humans. Directly mimicking human 6-DoF head poses can result in infeasible robot motions and increased errors.
 - **Approach**: The robot's head motion is controlled via a relaxed "3D look-at point" in space, rather than a full 6-DoF head pose.
 - **Training**: The look-at point is computed as the intersection of the human camera ray with the scene pointmap.
 - **Inference**: A head controller converts the predicted 3D look-at point into a feasible head orientation for the robot. This involves constructing a rotation whose forward axis points towards the look-at point, while ensuring the head's "up" direction is maintained. This allows the robot to actively perceive task-relevant information without being over-constrained by human-specific kinematics.

- **4.2.3. Gripper-Centric Frame for Spatial Awareness**:
 - **Problem**: Egocentric frames shift with head motion and vary due to embodiment differences (height, neck DoF, camera placement), potentially leading to out-of-distribution (OOD) observations and actions.
 - **Approach**: All observations (gripper poses, head pointmaps) and actions (gripper poses, look-at points) are transformed into the left-gripper coordinate frame. This ensures the policy always reasons in a consistent spatial frame anchored to the manipulators, improving spatial awareness for 3D representations and reducing cross-embodiment mismatch.

#### 3.3. Robot System

- **4.3.1. Bimanual Mobile Manipulator Hardware Setup**:
 - **Platform**: Rainbow Robotics RB-Y1 bimanual mobile manipulator, featuring a holonomic base, a 6-DoF torso, two 7-DoF arms, and a 2-DoF neck.
 - **Cameras**: A stereo pair of industrial wide-angle cameras (FLIR BFS-PGE-23S3C-CS) are mounted on the 2-DoF neck for egocentric context. Wrist-mounted cameras (FLIR BFS-PGE-50S5C-C) are installed at locations similar to the human UMI setup.
 - **Grippers**: Fin-ray fingers, identical to the UMI grippers, are used on the end-effectors to align with the data collection setup.
 - **Sensing and Computing**: A distributed architecture with two fiber-connected subnetworks handles high-bandwidth multi-camera streaming and low-latency robot control, with an external workstation running the control stack and policy inference.

- **4.3.2. Constraint-Aware Whole-body Controller**:
 - **Purpose**: To translate the policy's predicted end-effector poses and head look-at points into whole-body joint actions and base motions, respecting robot physical constraints.
 - **Method**: A differential whole-body Inverse Kinematics (IK) solver (using Mink) is implemented, optimizing a quadratic program.
 - **Objective Function**: Minimizes a combination of costs:
 - `C_ee`: High-weight bimanual SE(3) tracking to prioritize accurate end-effector command execution.
 - `C_nominal`: Regularizes toward a human-like nominal posture.
 - `C_current`: Encourages smooth motions by penalizing sudden posture changes.
 - `C_com`: Maintains the center-of-mass over the base for stability.
 - **Constraints**: Includes inequality constraints for configuration bounds, joint velocity limits, base velocity limits, and self-collision avoidance. An equality constraint enforces an upright torso posture.
 - **Look-at Point Integration**: The head look-at point is controlled separately from the whole-body IK objective to prevent interference with bimanual end-effector tracking accuracy. The neck orientation is computed to point towards the predicted look-at point.
 - **Asynchronous Execution**: The IK solver runs at 100 Hz, bridging the 10 Hz policy loop and 500 Hz robot control loop through temporal command interpolation, ensuring smooth trajectory tracking.

- **4.3.3. Asynchronous Policy Inference**:
 - To prevent base jerks and tracking errors, policy inference is decoupled from perception and control.
 - A detached policy server receives timestamped observations, performs inference, and returns an action chunk.
 - A real-time execution bridge aligns observations across sensors by correcting for latency, interpolates proprioception, and prepares synchronized observation windows for the policy.
 - The bridge receives action horizons from the policy, discards stale actions, and streams time-aligned targets (at 10 Hz) to the whole-body controller, accounting for inference and execution latencies.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of HoMMI focused on four core capabilities: cross-embodiment transfer, bimanual/whole-body coordination, long-horizon navigation, and active perception, across three complex mobile manipulation tasks.

#### 4.1. Evaluation Tasks and Performance

**A. Laundry Task**
- **Description**: Robot grasps a cloth bimanually, searches for a bin (potentially out of view), navigates to it, and places the cloth.
- **Capabilities Tested**: Bimanual coordination, whole-body coordination (navigation, rotation, bending), active perception (searching for bin).
- **Performance (Success Rate)**:
 - **Ours (HoMMI)**: 90%
 - Wrist-Only (UMI): 20% (Fails to find bin, poor grasp/placement due to lack of global context)
 - RGB-Only (UMI+Ego): 0% (Fails to grasp, presses table too hard due to appearance/viewpoint mismatch)
 - Head-Only: 0% (Misses cloth grasp, slips due to lack of local contact info)
 - w/o Active Neck: 75% (Fails to accurately place cloth due to lack of active perception)

**B. Delivery Task**
- **Description**: Robot carries a box bimanually, searches for a trolley (potentially out of view), navigates a large workspace, and places the box.
- **Capabilities Tested**: Bimanual coordination (stable box carrying, height coordination), long-horizon navigation (6x6m workspace, accurate approach), active perception (searching, rotating, navigating).
- **Performance (Success Rate)**:
 - **Ours (HoMMI)**: 85%
 - Wrist-Only (UMI): 15% (Incorrect approach/misalignment due to lack of global context)
 - RGB-Only (UMI+Ego): 45% (Fails to turn towards trolley due to infeasible 6-DoF head commands)
 - Head-Only: 5% (Collides box due to low gripper heights, insufficient for manipulation precision)
 - w/o Active Neck: 55% (Lifts box too high for placement due to lack of look-down motion)

**C. Tablescape Task**
- **Description**: Robot grasps two mat edges, lifts, moves forward to unfold, lays mat flat, and retracts hands.
- **Capabilities Tested**: Precise bimanual coordination (grasping edges, maintaining distance/height), whole-body coordination (navigation, significant gripper height adjustments).
- **Performance (Success Rate)**:
 - **Ours (HoMMI)**: 80% (Demonstrates robust recovery, retrying when mat folds back)
 - Wrist-Only (UMI): 0% (Grippers rotate too late, go above mat due to lack of global spatial context)
 - RGB-Only (UMI+Ego): 0% (Presses grippers too hard due to OOD head observations)
 - Head-Only: 0% (Misses contact with mat, demonstrating need for wrist cameras)
 - w/o Active Neck: 55% (Misses grasp, fails to recover due to inability to actively adjust viewpoint)

#### 4.2. Summary of Findings (F1-F6)

- **F1: Wrist-only sensing under-observes global task context and bimanual coordination.** Baselines relying solely on wrist cameras showed poor performance in tasks requiring search, long-horizon navigation, and bimanual spatial awareness, confirming the need for a broader field of view.
- **F2: Head-mounted camera alone is insufficient.** The Head-Only baseline failed consistently in grasping and alignment tasks, indicating that local contact cues from wrist cameras are essential for fine-grained manipulation. HoMMI's combination of head and wrist views, with joint vision encoder finetuning, resulted in cleaner, task-relevant egocentric attention.
- **F3: Naively adding egocentric RGB can degrade performance under embodiment mismatch.** The RGB-Only baseline showed severe failures (0% success on two tasks) due to appearance and viewpoint mismatches in human vs. robot egocentric RGB, and difficulties in tracking 6-DoF head trajectories. HoMMI's embodiment-agnostic 3D visual representation and relaxed 3D look-at point action representation addressed these gaps effectively.
- **F4: Active head control effectively gathers task-relevant information and maintains policy observability.** Disabling active head motion reduced success rates, particularly in tasks requiring searching or precise alignment, confirming the utility of the look-at point based active head control in mimicking human active perception and keeping views in-distribution.
- **F5: Our cross-embodiment hand-eye policy learns task-relevant attention.** HoMMI's egocentric attention maps highlight task-relevant objects and contacts, suggesting that the observation representation and gripper coordinate frame design help the policy focus on crucial information and mitigate the visual embodiment gap.
- **F6: Our system allows transfer from human demonstrators with different body proportions.** Successful transfer from demonstrators of varying heights (167 cm and 182 cm) indicates that the 3D look-at point action representation effectively mitigates height differences between operators.

#### 4.3. Additional Experiments

- **Depth Quality Sensitivity**: Performance on the Laundry task remained robust with Gaussian noise up to 1 cm standard deviation, degrading only significantly with larger perturbations.
- **Robustness to Unseen Lightings and Objects**: HoMMI showed robust generalization, achieving 90.63% success on unseen objects and 93.75% on unseen lighting conditions in the Laundry task.
- **iPhone-ARKit Tracking Error**: The data collection system exhibited an average tracking error of 5.0 mm position and 0.8° rotation against ground truth.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The HoMMI framework presents a significant advancement in learning robot manipulation skills, particularly for mobile manipulators engaging in complex, whole-body tasks. Its core contribution lies in demonstrating that long-horizon, bimanual mobile manipulation can be learned directly from robot-free human demonstrations, circumventing the traditional limitations of teleoperation and enabling more scalable data collection.

The primary significance of HoMMI includes:
- **Enhanced Scalability of Data Collection**: By augmenting the UMI framework with egocentric sensing and leveraging widely available, portable devices (iPhones), HoMMI allows for efficient and intuitive data collection in diverse, in-the-wild environments. This reduces the reliance on expensive and specialized robot teleoperation setups, potentially democratizing access to data for complex robot learning tasks.
- **Effective Bridging of the Human-Robot Embodiment Gap**: The work provides specific technical solutions—an embodiment-agnostic 3D visual representation, a relaxed 3D look-at point action representation, and a gripper-centric coordinate frame—to address the visual and kinematic discrepancies between human demonstrators and robotic platforms. This allows for direct policy transfer without the need for extensive robot-specific fine-tuning or co-training, which has been a major bottleneck in previous egocentric imitation learning approaches.
- **Enabling Complex Whole-Body Coordination**: HoMMI demonstrates the ability to learn and execute tasks requiring intricate coordination of multiple robot components, including two arms, the mobile base, torso, and active head movements. This capability is crucial for mobile manipulation, where navigation and manipulation are tightly coupled and require intelligent interaction with the environment.
- **Robust Active Perception**: The system's design explicitly enables robots to learn and perform active perception behaviors, where the robot intelligently controls its head movements to gather task-relevant information (e.g., searching for objects, maintaining optimal viewpoints). This reactive capability enhances the robot's adaptability and performance in dynamic and partially observable environments.

The potential impact of HoMMI extends to various areas of robotics research and application:
- **Accelerating Robot Skill Acquisition**: By making data collection more accessible and transferable, HoMMI could significantly accelerate the development and deployment of advanced robot skills in real-world settings, from household chores to industrial logistics.
- **Advancing Generalization in Robotics**: The framework's ability to transfer skills across different human body proportions and adapt to unseen objects and lighting conditions suggests a path toward more generalized robot behaviors, reducing the need for extensive domain-specific training.
- **Inspiration for Future Research**: The publicly available code, data, and hardware design for HoMMI are intended to encourage and facilitate further research in mobile manipulation, particularly in areas like long-term memory integration, multi-modal sensing (e.g., force/tactile feedback), and hardware-software co-design to further reduce the embodiment gap.

Overall, HoMMI provides a robust and scalable framework for teaching complex mobile manipulation skills to robots, contributing to the development of more autonomous and capable robotic systems that can operate effectively in human environments.
