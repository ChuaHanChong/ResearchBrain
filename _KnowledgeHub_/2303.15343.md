# Sigmoid Loss for Language Image Pre-Training

> [!summary] Summary
> Google DeepMind researchers introduce Sigmoid Loss for Language-Image Pre-training (SigLIP), an alternative to softmax-based contrastive learning that enables training highly competitive models with significantly fewer computational resources, achieving up to 84.5% zero-shot ImageNet accuracy in two days on four TPUv4 chips. The work also demonstrates that performance for this task saturates around a batch size of 32k, challenging the assumption that ever-larger batch sizes are always beneficial.

## Problem

- Standard softmax-based contrastive loss for vision-language pre-training incurs quadratic memory costs and expensive "all-gather" operations in distributed setups, limiting achievable batch sizes.
- The high computational requirements of current methods restrict access to state-of-the-art language-image pre-training for researchers with limited resources.
- Softmax-based approaches exhibit suboptimal performance at smaller batch sizes due to a limited number of in-batch negative samples.

## Method

- Propose the ==pairwise sigmoid loss== (**SigLIP**), a loss that treats each image-text pair independently, eliminating the need for global ==batch-level normalization== used in ==softmax-based contrastive loss== (InfoNCE).
- Implement an efficient ==chunked distributed training== strategy that avoids expensive "all-gather" operations and reduces memory footprint, enabling much larger effective batch sizes.
- Incorporate a ==learnable bias term== initialized to a low value and adjust optimizer parameters (e.g., "β2") to enhance training stability, particularly with large batch sizes.

## Results

- SigLIP models achieved competitive zero-shot performance, with a SigLiT model reaching **84.5%** ImageNet accuracy in **two days** using only **four TPUv4 chips**.
- The sigmoid loss consistently outperformed softmax for batch sizes below **16k** and matched or slightly exceeded its performance at larger scales, with performance saturating around a batch size of **32k**, enabling training with significantly reduced compute resources.
- Multilingual SigLIP (mSigLIP) set new state-of-the-art results for a Base-sized model on the XM3600 dataset (**34.9%** average R@1), demonstrating its efficacy across diverse languages.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Performance in language-image pre-training saturates around a global batch size of 32k, indicating that indefinitely scaling batch size does not yield proportional gains.
> - The pairwise sigmoid loss demonstrates superior performance over softmax at smaller batch sizes and exhibits increased robustness to noisy web-scale data.
> - Crucial insights for stable training include the introduction of a learned bias term and adjustments to optimizer parameters (e.g., "β2") when dealing with large batch sizes.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of vision-language pre-training has rapidly evolved, primarily driven by the success of models like OpenAI's CLIP and Google's ALIGN. These seminal works demonstrated the viability of training generic computer vision backbones using weak supervision from massive, noisy image-text pairs scraped from the web, effectively replacing traditional pre-training on meticulously labeled multi-class datasets like ImageNet. The core idea is to learn an aligned representation space where image and text embeddings for matching pairs are close, and those for non-matching pairs are far apart.

The standard recipe for achieving this alignment relies on a **softmax-based contrastive loss**, often referred to as InfoNCE. While highly effective, this loss function presents significant challenges, particularly when scaling to the enormous batch sizes often considered beneficial for contrastive learning. The primary limitation is its requirement for **batch-level normalization**, which necessitates a global view of all pairwise similarities within a mini-batch. In distributed training environments, this translates to computationally expensive `all-gather` operations and the materialization of a memory-intensive `|B|x|B|` (batch size squared) matrix of similarity scores. This quadratic memory requirement quickly becomes a bottleneck, limiting the achievable batch size on a given set of hardware resources.

The broader research landscape has seen various attempts to address the efficiency and scalability of language-image pre-training. Some approaches, like LiT (Locked-image Text tuning), focus on freezing a pre-trained image backbone to reduce trainable parameters and computational load. Others, such as FLIP, explore masking techniques for visual tokens to improve efficiency, sometimes at the cost of quality. BASIC and LAION have pushed for larger batch sizes, but typically by employing hundreds of chips or mixing in private datasets, which remains inaccessible to most researchers. Concurrently, alternative pre-training paradigms, such as generative approaches (e.g., GIT, SimVLM, CoCa), have emerged, aiming to learn joint representations through text generation or other multimodal tasks.

This paper positions itself directly within the **contrastive learning paradigm**, aiming to fundamentally improve its efficiency and performance by proposing a novel loss function. By introducing the pairwise **Sigmoid loss**, the authors directly tackle the core limitations of the softmax-based InfoNCE loss, specifically its memory footprint and distributed training complexity. This work seeks to democratize access to high-quality language-image pre-training by enabling larger batch sizes and faster training on more modest hardware setups, thereby making the benefits of large-scale multimodal models accessible to a wider research community. It offers a direct, powerful alternative to the current mainstream approach, building on the success of CLIP/ALIGN while mitigating their practical drawbacks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to **propose and validate a simple pairwise Sigmoid loss for Language-Image Pre-training (SigLIP)**, presenting it as a superior alternative to the widely used softmax-based contrastive loss.

The motivations behind this objective stem directly from the limitations of existing contrastive learning methodologies:

1. **Overcoming Memory and Computational Bottlenecks:** The standard softmax loss requires a global view of pairwise similarities across the entire batch for normalization. In distributed training, this translates to expensive `all-gather` communications and the need to materialize a `batch_size x batch_size` similarity matrix, which consumes a quadratic amount of memory. This severely restricts the maximum effective batch size, especially on limited hardware. The authors aim to introduce a loss that simplifies distributed implementation and significantly boosts memory efficiency.

2. **Improving Performance at Smaller Batch Sizes:** Softmax-based contrastive learning inherently relies on in-batch negative samples. When the batch size is small, the number of available negative examples is limited, which can hinder the learning process. The sigmoid loss, by conceptually decoupling the batch size from the task definition (treating each pair independently), is hypothesized to perform better even with fewer in-batch negatives.

3. **Enhancing Simplicity and Numerical Stability:** The standard softmax implementation requires numerical stabilization (e.g., by subtracting the maximum input value), adding complexity. The sigmoid loss, operating on individual pairs, is inherently simpler and more numerically stable, requiring only a single pass.

4. **Democratizing Access to Large-Scale Pre-training:** A crucial motivation is to make advanced language-image pre-training more accessible. Current state-of-the-art models often require massive computational resources (hundreds or thousands of GPUs/TPUs). By developing a more memory-efficient and performant loss, the authors aim to enable competitive training on significantly fewer accelerators (e.g., four TPUv4 chips), lowering the barrier to entry for many researchers.

Beyond the primary objective of introducing the sigmoid loss, the paper also leverages its unique properties to pursue several secondary research goals:

- **Studying the Impact of Batch Size and Negative-to-Positive Ratio:** The disentanglement of batch size from the loss definition allows for a more controlled investigation into the role of batch size and the optimal ratio of positive to negative examples in contrastive learning, including pushing batch sizes to unprecedented extremes (up to one million).
- **Investigating Robustness to Data Noise:** Given the notoriously noisy nature of web-scraped image-text datasets, the authors aim to explore whether the sigmoid loss inherently provides better robustness against various forms of data corruption, a property previously observed in other classification contexts.
- **Exploring Multilingual Pre-training:** The efficiency gains of the sigmoid loss are applied to multilingual settings to understand its benefits in more complex, diverse data landscapes.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the paper's methodology revolves around replacing the traditional softmax-based contrastive loss with a pairwise sigmoid loss and developing an efficient implementation strategy.

#### 3.1. Softmax Loss (Revisited as Baseline)

The paper first briefly reviews the standard softmax-based contrastive loss, often used in models like CLIP. Given a mini-batch of image-text pairs `(Ii, Ti)`, the objective is to align embeddings of matching pairs while pushing non-matching pairs apart. This is achieved by computing pairwise similarity scores and then applying a softmax normalization, typically twice (once across images for a given text, and once across texts for a given image). This requires gathering all embeddings from all devices in a distributed setup, leading to the aforementioned `|B|x|B|` similarity matrix and `all-gather` operations. A learnable temperature parameter `t` scales the dot products before softmax.

#### 3.2. Sigmoid Loss (Proposed Alternative)

The authors propose the sigmoid loss as a simpler, more efficient alternative. Unlike softmax, which implicitly learns to distinguish a positive pair from all *other* pairs in the batch, the sigmoid loss treats each image-text pair independently. It transforms the problem into a binary classification task for *every possible pair combination* within and across batches.
The loss for a given pair `(Ii, Tj)` is defined as:
`-log(1 / (1 + exp(-z_ij * (t * x_i * y_j + b))))`
where `x_i` and `y_j` are the L2-normalized image and text embeddings, `t` is a learnable temperature (scalar), and `b` is a novel **learnable bias term**. `z_ij` is the label: `1` if `i=j` (matching pair) and `-1` if `i!=j` (non-matching pair). The bias `b` is crucial; initialized to `-10` (with `t` at `log 10`), it ensures training starts near the prior, preventing large initial gradient steps due to the inherent imbalance of many negative pairs to few positive ones.

#### 3.3. Efficient "Chunked" Implementation for Distributed Training

This is a critical innovation that unlocks the scalability of the sigmoid loss. In a distributed setting with `D` devices, each holding a local batch of size `b = |B|/D`, the traditional softmax requires global communication to build the full `|B|x|B|` similarity matrix. The sigmoid loss, being a sum of independent terms, allows for a "chunked" approach:
1. Each device first computes loss components for its *local* positive pairs and `b-1` negative pairs.
2. Then, representations are permuted across devices (e.g., device 1 sends its image embeddings to device 2, device 2 to device 3, etc., cyclically).
3. Each device computes loss components with respect to these "chunked" negative pairs from its neighbors.
4. This process is repeated `D` times until every image embedding on a device has interacted with every text embedding from all devices in the global batch.
This strategy avoids expensive `all-gather` operations and, critically, reduces the peak memory requirement from `|B|^2` (for the full similarity matrix) to `b^2` (for the per-device chunk). This quadratic reduction in memory cost enables training with significantly larger global batch sizes on relatively few devices.

#### 3.4. Model Architectures and Datasets

- **Vision Encoders:** Vision Transformer (ViT) architectures are used, including ViT-B (Base), ViT-L (Large), ViT-g (Gigantic), and So-400m (Shape-Optimized ViT).
- **Text Encoders:** Standard Transformer architectures of corresponding sizes (Base, Large) are employed.
- **Pre-training Strategies:**
 - **SigLiT:** Follows the LiT (Locked-image Text tuning) approach, using a *frozen, precomputed* ViT-g vision backbone and training only the text encoder from scratch. This is highly resource-efficient.
 - **SigLIP:** Trains *both* the image and text encoders from scratch, or fine-tunes a pre-trained image backbone.
- **Datasets:**
 - **LiT image-text dataset:** Used for SigLiT experiments.
 - **WebLI dataset:** A large-scale private dataset. English subsets are used for SigLIP, and the full multilingual (100+ languages) WebLI is used for mSigLIP experiments.
 - **Tokenization:** Standard SentencePiece tokenizers trained on C4 dataset (English) or a larger multilingual vocabulary are used. For multilingual training with very large vocabularies, a "bottlenecked" token embedding is proposed to save memory.

#### 3.5. Optimization and Hyperparameters

- The ScalingViT-Adafactor optimizer is primarily used, with LION also tested.
- For stability with large batch sizes, the `β2` parameter in Adam/AdaFactor is reduced from its default 0.999 to 0.95, which helps prevent instabilities from large gradient spikes.
- A key finding for fine-tuning pre-trained vision backbones is that **disabling weight decay** on these frozen/pre-trained weights significantly improves performance and stability.

#### 3.6. Evaluation Metrics

Models are evaluated on standard zero-shot transfer tasks:
- **ImageNet, ImageNet-v2, ImageNet ReaL, ObjectNet:** For zero-shot image classification accuracy.
- **MSCOCO:** For zero-shot image-to-text (I→T) and text-to-image (T→I) retrieval recall@1.
- **XM3600 dataset:** For massively multilingual zero-shot image-to-text and text-to-image retrieval across 36 languages.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper presents a comprehensive set of experiments validating the efficacy and efficiency of the proposed sigmoid loss for language-image pre-training.

1. **Sigmoid Loss Outperforms Softmax at Smaller Batch Sizes:**
 - For batch sizes smaller than 16k, the sigmoid loss consistently and significantly outperforms the softmax loss in terms of ImageNet zero-shot accuracy for both SigLiT and SigLIP. This is crucial for researchers with limited computational resources.
 - As batch size increases, the performance gap between sigmoid and softmax diminishes, with sigmoid either matching or slightly outperforming softmax at larger scales.
 - The sigmoid loss consistently offers "simplicity, compute savings, and straightforward memory efficient implementation" across all batch sizes.

2. **Saturation of Performance with Extreme Batch Sizes:**
 - The authors successfully trained a SigLiT model with an unprecedented batch size of *one million*, leveraging the memory-efficient chunked implementation.
 - **Crucial finding:** Despite pushing to extreme batch sizes, performance saturation occurs much earlier. For ImageNet zero-shot accuracy, a batch size of **32k** is found to be "surprisingly sufficient" and "nearly optimal" for both sigmoid and softmax losses. Further scaling beyond 32k (e.g., to 256k or 1M) yields only marginal improvements or even leads to performance degradation (e.g., SigLIP at 307k batch size performs worse than at 32k). This challenges the common assumption that "bigger batch size is always better" indefinitely.

3. **Significant Efficiency Gains and Accessibility:**
 - **SigLiT with Limited Resources:** A SigLiT model (frozen ViT-g image encoder, small text encoder) achieved 79.7% ImageNet 0-shot accuracy in just one day using only **four TPUv4 chips**. With a larger g/14 checkpoint, it reached 84.5% in two days. This demonstrates remarkable efficiency and accessibility for competitive results.
 - **SigLIP From-Scratch Training:** When training SigLIP models from scratch (both encoders), the sigmoid loss enabled fitting larger batch sizes on fewer chips (e.g., 4096 batch size vs. 2048 for CLIP on 4 TPUv4 chips). From-scratch SigLIP achieved 72.1% 0-shot accuracy on ImageNet in two days with 32 TPUv4 chips, representing a "significant training cost reduction" compared to CLIP (approx. 2500 TPUv3-days for 72.6%).
 - **Effective Fine-tuning:** For fine-tuning pre-trained vision backbones in SigLIP, disabling weight decay on the pre-trained weights dramatically improves 0-shot and 10-shot ImageNet performance, preventing degradation of visual representations.

4. **Multilingual Pre-training (mSigLIP):**
 - The 32k batch size saturation also holds for multilingual SigLIP (mSigLIP) training across over 100 languages. Scaling beyond 32k actually hurts performance on 36-language cross-modal retrieval tasks (XM3600), while ImageNet 0-shot accuracy plateaus.
 - mSigLIP sets new state-of-the-art results for a Base-sized model on XM3600 (34.9% average R@1, with a scaled-up version reaching 42.6% image retrieval R@1), significantly outperforming previous LiT models.
 - A "bottlenecked" token embedding strategy is proposed to efficiently handle large multilingual vocabularies without excessive memory consumption.

5. **Ablation Studies and Insights:**
 - **Negative Ratio:** Experiments on masking negative examples revealed that simply removing negatives (to balance positive/negative ratio) hurts performance. However, keeping only the *hardest* negative examples almost maintains quality, indicating that learning primarily occurs from these challenging negatives. The overall imbalance in positive-to-negative pairs does not appear to be a major concern for the sigmoid loss.
 - **Learned Bias Term:** The introduction of a learned bias term (`b`) initialized to `-10` is crucial. It ensures the training process starts near the true prior distribution, preventing "dramatic over-correction" in early optimization steps and consistently improving model performance.
 - **Training Stability:** Reducing the `β2` parameter of optimizers (Adam/AdaFactor) from 0.999 to 0.95 is shown to be effective in stabilizing large-batch training by allowing quicker recovery from occasional gradient spikes.

6. **Robustness to Data Noise:**
 - Models trained with the sigmoid loss demonstrate increased robustness to various types of simulated data noise (image corruption, text corruption, and batch alignment shuffling) compared to their softmax counterparts. This property is highly beneficial given the inherent noisiness of web-scale training data.

7. **Overall Performance:**
 - The final SigLIP models, particularly the scaled-up versions, achieve new state-of-the-art results across several zero-shot classification and retrieval benchmarks (ImageNet, ObjectNet, COCO, XM3600), outperforming prior publicly released models like OpenCLIP and original CLIP variants.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research has several significant implications and potential impacts on the field of artificial intelligence:

1. **Democratization of Large-Scale Multimodal Pre-training:** This is perhaps the most profound impact. By introducing a memory-efficient and inherently simpler loss function (Sigmoid loss) coupled with an optimized distributed training scheme, the paper dramatically lowers the computational barrier for achieving competitive language-image models. The ability to train a high-performing model (84.5% ImageNet 0-shot accuracy) in just two days on only *four TPUv4 chips* transforms a resource-intensive task into one accessible to a much broader range of academic institutions and smaller research groups. This could accelerate innovation by enabling more researchers to experiment with and build upon large foundation models.

2. **Fundamental Shift in Contrastive Learning Loss Design:** The sigmoid loss presents a compelling alternative to the ubiquitous softmax/InfoNCE loss, which has dominated contrastive learning. Its pairwise nature and independence from global batch normalization offer a new paradigm that could influence future model architectures and training strategies not just in vision-language, but across other contrastive learning applications (e.g., self-supervised learning for unimodal data).

3. **Challenging the "Bigger Batch is Always Better" Dogma:** The unexpected finding that performance saturates at a relatively moderate batch size (32k), even when extreme scales (1 million) are achievable, is a critical insight. This challenges the prevailing assumption in large-scale machine learning that simply increasing batch size indefinitely yields proportional performance gains. This realization can lead to more efficient resource allocation, as researchers might not need to chase ever-larger batch sizes, instead focusing on other optimization aspects once this sweet spot is reached.

4. **Enhanced Practicality and Robustness for Real-World Applications:**
 - The inherent numerical stability and simpler implementation of the sigmoid loss make it more robust and easier to deploy in complex distributed systems.
 - The demonstrated increased robustness to data noise is highly valuable. Web-scale datasets are inherently noisy, and models that can effectively learn from imperfect data are more reliable for real-world applications where curated datasets are scarce. This directly translates to more robust and deployable AI systems.

5. **Foundation for Future Research:** The paper's detailed ablation studies on batch composition, negative mining, and the role of the bias term provide valuable insights that open new avenues for research into optimizing contrastive learning. The released models and the `big_vision` codebase further serve as strong baselines and tools, fostering collaborative development and exploration within the community.

In essence, "Sigmoid Loss for Language Image Pre-Training" is a landmark paper that not only proposes a technically elegant and highly efficient new loss function but also fundamentally alters the accessibility and practical considerations for training state-of-the-art multimodal AI models.
