# A Survey of Robotic Navigation and Manipulation with Physics Simulators in the Era of Embodied AI

> [!summary] Summary
> A survey systematically analyzes how low-level physics simulator properties affect robotic navigation and manipulation performance and sim-to-real transfer in Embodied AI, providing guidance for selecting and configuring simulation tools. It categorizes existing simulators, benchmarks, and methods, highlighting the critical role of accurate physics and sensory modeling.

## Problem

- Policies trained in simulation often perform poorly in real-world robotic navigation and manipulation tasks due to the persistent "sim-to-real gap."
- Prior surveys on Embodied AI simulators lacked detailed analysis of how low-level simulator properties specifically impact sim-to-real transfer across both navigation and manipulation.
- Simulation-based evaluations frequently fail to accurately predict real-world robotic performance, leading to unreliable deployments and hindering research progress.

## Method

- The authors conduct a systematic survey analyzing how low-level physics simulator properties influence policy learning, evaluation, and sim-to-real transfer for navigation and manipulation tasks.
- The "sim-to-real gap" is dissected into perception and action-dynamics discrepancies, with common mitigation strategies categorized and reviewed.
- A comprehensive resource is provided, covering benchmark datasets, metrics, simulation platforms, and methods, along with practical guidance for tool selection and configuration.

## Results

- The survey identifies that advanced photorealistic rendering and accurate sensor noise models effectively mitigate the perception gap in navigation, while robust physics engines address the action-dynamics gap.
- For manipulation, accurate multi-point contact, friction models, and the emerging field of differentiable physics are found to be crucial for achieving successful real-world transfer of policies.
- A call is made for new evaluation standards, including procedural quality metrics, temporal safety monitoring, and sim-to-real predictivity assessments, to ensure simulated performance genuinely reflects real-world robotic behavior.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Low-level simulator properties, such as collision response, contact solvers, friction models, and sensor accuracy, are critical determinants of sim-to-real transfer success in robotics.
> - Accurate physical modeling, including differentiable physics for manipulation and specialized dynamics for complex navigation, is essential for effectively reducing the sim-to-real gap.
> - Sim-to-real failures are frequently attributable to specific simulator property mismatches, necessitating more nuanced evaluation beyond simple task success rates to include safety and procedural quality.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Embodied Artificial Intelligence (EAI) aims to equip agents, particularly robots, with the capacity to navigate and interact with the physical world. Navigation, enabling safe movement, and manipulation, allowing object interaction, are considered core capabilities in this field. Recent advancements in AI, specifically learning-based methods like Reinforcement Learning (RL) and Imitation Learning (IL), have shown promise for training such agents. However, directly training these agents in real-world environments is often costly, time-consuming, and potentially unsafe due to the complexities of data collection, environmental setup, and hardware risks.

Physics-based simulation has therefore emerged as a foundational tool in robotics research, facilitating scalable data generation, repeatable evaluations, controller prototyping, system debugging, and safer testing prior to hardware deployment. A typical robotics simulator integrates a physics engine (e.g., MuJoCo, Bullet, PhysX) with a higher-level platform that handles scene construction, rendering, sensor simulation, robot models, and interfaces to robotics middleware. Despite the widespread use of simulators, a significant challenge persists: the "sim-to-real gap." This gap refers to the discrepancy where policies trained in simulation may perform suboptimally or fail when deployed on real hardware, due to differences between simulated and real-world sensing, actuation, and dynamics.

The broader research landscape has seen rapid evolution since 2019, driven by increased access to large-scale data and the emergence of model-centric learning paradigms. In navigation, methods have progressed from explicit memory structures to latent-memory approaches, foundation-model-based planning, and learned world models, supported by extensive benchmark ecosystems (e.g., iGibson, Habitat). Similarly, in manipulation, the field has moved from model-free RL and behavior cloning to diffusion policies and Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, enabled by large-scale datasets (e.g., GraspNet, ManiSkill, Open X-Embodiment).

Existing surveys on EAI simulators and navigation typically predate these recent breakthroughs, particularly the rise of large language models (LLMs) and world models. Other surveys offer broad EAI overviews or focus on specific methodologies without a dedicated emphasis on the *properties of physics simulators* that directly influence the sim-to-real gap across *both* navigation and manipulation tasks. This survey addresses this gap by providing a systematic analysis of how low-level simulator properties (e.g., contact solvers, collision response, actuation timing, sensor models) affect learning, evaluation, and, crucially, sim-to-real transfer outcomes in the context of modern EAI.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this survey is the persistent "sim-to-real gap," which undermines the utility of policies trained in simulation when deployed in the real world. While physics simulators are indispensable for EAI research due to their ability to provide scalable data, repeatable evaluations, and safe testing environments, current simulation-based evaluations are often not sufficiently predictive of real-world performance. This deficiency stems from an inadequate understanding of how specific simulator properties influence learning, evaluation, and the subsequent transfer to physical hardware.

The paper identifies several critical issues:
- **Simulator Artifacts**: Policies can exploit artifacts present in simulation but absent in reality (e.g., collision-response issues like wall sliding), leading to policies that perform well in simulation but fail in the real world.
- **Sensitivity to Low-Level Properties**: For manipulation tasks, factors such as contact solvers, friction models, collision geometry, and actuation interfaces can significantly alter object motion and grasp stability, leading to discrepancies. In locomotion, latency, actuator, and foot-ground contact mismatches contribute to the gap. Visual policies are affected by differences in lighting, textures, distractors, and camera perturbations.
- **Challenges in Complex Domains**: Modeling deformable materials, tactile feedback, and underwater environments remains difficult at scale, where material properties, contact dynamics, optical/acoustic effects, and hydrodynamics are critical but hard to accurately simulate.

To address these challenges and provide practical guidance for researchers, the survey sets out to answer three key questions:
1. **Which simulator properties matter most for different tasks in embodied navigation and manipulation?** This objective motivates a detailed analysis of simulator features relevant to specific task requirements, such as rigid-body dynamics for wheeled navigation versus accurate contact and deformable materials for complex manipulation.
2. **Which sim-to-real gaps are captured, mitigated, or left unresolved by different simulator designs?** This focuses on understanding how various simulator designs contribute to or alleviate discrepancies in perception (e.g., sensory observations, lighting) and action-dynamics (e.g., collision response, actuation, contact, friction). The paper categorizes these discrepancies into the "perception gap" and the "action-dynamics gap."
3. **How should researchers select methods and configure simulators for modern navigation and manipulation tasks?** This aims to provide actionable recommendations by analyzing the features of different simulators, their hardware requirements, and their suitability for various benchmark datasets and learning methods.

The survey also explicitly acknowledges the growing interest in learned world models as another approach to modeling environment dynamics. While distinct from analytical physics simulators, the paper highlights that physical consistency and physics-informed representations are crucial for world models, especially for prediction, planning, or manipulation-oriented policy learning, reinforcing that low-level physical assumptions matter regardless of the modeling paradigm.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The survey adopts a structured and comprehensive approach to analyze robotic navigation and manipulation within the context of physics simulators and sim-to-real transfer. The methodology is organized around a taxonomy that covers two central embodied tasks—navigation and manipulation—and for each, examines corresponding tasks, simulators, benchmark datasets, evaluation metrics, and representative methods.

**I. Overall Scope and Focus:**
- The survey distinguishes itself by focusing on simulator properties that have received limited attention in prior surveys, specifically analyzing how these properties interact with task requirements, representation learning, policy learning, and sim-to-real transfer.
- It provides a resource that includes benchmark datasets, metrics, simulation platforms, and methods, along with considerations for hardware constraints, to aid researchers in tool selection.
- The analysis explicitly addresses the "sim-to-real gap," categorizing discrepancies into the "perception gap" (sensory differences like RGB-D images, LiDAR, lighting, noise) and the "action-dynamics gap" (robot-environment interaction differences like collision response, contact, friction, actuation).
- Common mitigation strategies are grouped into calibration and explicit modeling, randomization-based methods (e.g., domain randomization), and transfer-oriented policy learning.

**II. Navigation Analysis (Section 2):**
- **Process Breakdown:** The navigation process in simulators is broken down into four steps: Perception, Memory Building (Explicit: metric maps, topological graphs; Implicit: latent representations, foundation models, world models), Decision-Making (Goal-Driven: PointNav, ObjectNav; Task-Driven: EQA, VLN), and Action Execution. Sim-to-real challenges are linked to visual rendering and physical dynamics at the Perception and Action Execution stages, respectively.
- **Simulators:** Categorized into Indoor, Outdoor (Ground/Wildland, Air, Underwater), and General-purpose. For each category, the survey details how different simulators (e.g., Habitat-Sim, AI2-THOR, CARLA, AirSim, HoloOcean, Isaac Sim) address perception (photorealistic rendering, sensor noise models) and action-dynamics (physics engines like Bullet, PhysX, custom fast physics, hydrodynamic models) challenges.
- **Benchmark Datasets:** Reviewed for both Goal-Driven (e.g., HM3D, ION, MultiON, iGibson) and Task-Driven Navigation (e.g., R2R, ALFRED, DialFRED, OceanGym), highlighting their scale, environment diversity, interaction capabilities, and language conditioning.
- **Evaluation Metrics:** Analyzed for suitability across different tasks. Goal-driven metrics (e.g., Success Rate, Success weighted by Path Length – SPL, Instance-Localization Success Rate – ILSR) focus on task completion and efficiency. Task-driven metrics (e.g., Coverage weighted by Length Score – CLS, Normalized Dynamic Time Warping – nDTW, EQA Efficiency, Instruction Violation Rate – IVR) assess action-instruction alignment and answer accuracy. Metrics are also assessed qualitatively across Scale Invariance, Order Invariance, Safety Compliance, and Energy Efficiency.
- **Methods:** Categorized by memory type: Explicit Memory (Metric Map-Based, Graph-Based) and Implicit Memory (Latent Representation-Based, Foundation Model-Based, World Model-Based), explaining their mechanisms for overcoming partial observability.

**III. Manipulation Analysis (Section 3):**
- **Manipulation Tasks:** Categorized by complexity and required degrees of freedom (DoFs), including Grasping, Dexterous Manipulation, Deformable Object Manipulation, Fragile Object Manipulation, Mobile Manipulation, Open-World Manipulation, and Bimanual Manipulation. The hardware associated with these tasks (grippers, dexterous hands, humanoids) is also discussed.
- **Physics Engines and Simulators:** Compared classical engines (e.g., MuJoCo, PyBullet, Gazebo, Isaac Sim, SAPIEN, CoppeliaSim) with differentiable ones (e.g., Dojo, DiffTaichi, Genesis). The analysis highlights features like GPU acceleration, photorealistic rendering (ray tracing), depth noising, precise contact dynamics (LCP models, soft contact), rigid/soft/fluid dynamics, and support for multi-joint systems. Differentiable simulators are presented as a key advancement for gradient-based optimization.
- **Benchmark Datasets:** Reviewed across rigid-body, deformable object, mobile, language-conditioned, multi-embodiment, and visual perception tasks (e.g., Meta-World, RLBench, SoftGym, OVMM, BEHAVIOR-1K, CALVIN, Open X-Embodiment, GraspNet-1Billion).
- **Methods:** Organized by:
 - **Sensing Modalities:** Vision-only (RGB/RGB-D/point cloud), Contact-only (force/torque, joint torque, tactile), and Vision–contact fusion (Visuo-Tactile/Visual-Tactile). This section discusses how each modality supports different aspects of manipulation (global context vs. local interaction states) and the benefits of fusion.
 - **Policy Learning:** Covers Reinforcement Learning (RL) and Imitation Learning (IL) (including Behavior Cloning and Action Chunking Transformer – ACT), Diffusion Policies (DPs), Vision-Language Models (VLMs), and Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models (e.g., RT-1, RT-2, OpenVLA). The discussion also includes the computational resource profiles and training/inference bottlenecks for these methods.

**IV. Discussion on Unification, Failures, and Future Directions (Section 4):**
- **Unification:** Addresses the growing trend of unifying navigation and manipulation, either through hierarchical system integration or by learning shared action/representation spaces (e.g., UniVLA, MiMo-Embodied), which increases the fidelity demands on simulators.
- **Error Analysis:** Provides a taxonomy of common simulator-induced failure cases, linking them to mismatched simulator properties (geometry/collision, observation/sensing, dynamics/actuation/timing, rigid contact/material, deformable/fragile/tactile objects, evaluation artifacts) and suggesting practical checks or mitigations.
- **Future Directions:** Identifies key areas for advancement, including continual learning, neural ODEs for continuous dynamics modeling, the development of more procedural quality-focused evaluation metrics (beyond goal-oriented success), and the need for safe and trustworthy evaluation frameworks that assess temporal safety violations and motion naturalness.

The methodology is characterized by its comparative analysis of simulators and their properties, its detailed categorization of tasks, datasets, and methods, and its emphasis on practical implications for bridging the sim-to-real gap.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The survey identifies several key findings regarding the role of physics simulators in Embodied AI (EAI), particularly concerning navigation and manipulation, and their impact on sim-to-real transfer:

#### 4.1. The Criticality of Simulator Properties for Sim-to-Real Transfer

- A central finding is that low-level simulator properties, often underemphasized in broader EAI discussions, profoundly affect the success of sim-to-real transfer. These properties include collision response, contact solvers, friction models, actuation timing, integration settings, sensor models, and rendering fidelity.
- Evidence presented (e.g., from Kadian et al., Collins et al., Kataoka et al.) demonstrates that discrepancies in these properties lead to significant differences in real-world policy performance, often resulting in lower absolute success or poor correlation between simulated and real-world rankings.
- The sim-to-real gap is broadly categorized into a **perception gap** (differences in sensory observations) and an **action-dynamics gap** (differences in robot-environment interaction). Effective simulators and training strategies must address both.

#### 4.2. Specialized Requirements for Navigation Simulators

- Navigation simulators are categorized into Indoor, Outdoor (Ground/Wildland, Air, Underwater), and General-purpose, each with distinct needs for visual and physical fidelity.
- **Perception Gap Mitigation:** Modern simulators like Habitat-Sim, AI2-THOR, iGibson, CARLA, Isaac Sim, and ThreeDWorld employ photorealistic rendering techniques (Physically-Based Rendering, ray tracing) and sophisticated sensor noise models (e.g., RGB-D noise, LiDAR models) to minimize the perception gap.
- **Action-Dynamics Gap Mitigation:** Physics engines such as Bullet, PhysX, and custom fast physics engines are integrated to simulate rigid-body dynamics, collision detection, and locomotion. For specialized domains like underwater navigation, simulators like Stonefish, HoloOcean, and OceanSim incorporate hydrodynamics, buoyancy, drag, and specialized sensor models (sonar, DVL, pressure sensors) to address the unique action-dynamics challenges.
- **Memory and Planning:** Navigation methods have evolved from explicit memory structures (metric maps, topological graphs) towards implicit memory approaches, leveraging latent representations, foundation models (LLMs, VLMs), and world models to handle partial observability and long-horizon planning.
- **Benchmark Evolution:** Datasets have advanced from basic spatial navigation to complex object interaction (iGibson, OVMM), open-vocabulary object goals (HM3D-OVON), and language-conditioned tasks (ALFRED, VLN, EQA), demanding more sophisticated agent capabilities and simulator fidelity.
- **Evaluation Limitations:** While metrics like Success Rate (SR) and Success weighted by Path Length (SPL) are common, the survey highlights that they may not capture crucial aspects like instruction fidelity (addressed by CLS, nDTW) or safety (IVR), advocating for more comprehensive, procedurally-oriented metrics.

#### 4.3. Diverse Demands for Manipulation Simulators

- Manipulation tasks range significantly in complexity, from basic grasping to dexterous in-hand manipulation, deformable object handling, and mobile manipulation. Each task imposes higher demands on sensing, control, and simulator fidelity.
- **Contact and Material Physics:** For manipulation, especially contact-rich and deformable object tasks, accurate modeling of multi-point contacts, friction, actuation, and material properties (elasticity, damping) is critical. MuJoCo is noted for its precision in contact dynamics, while simulators like SAPIEN and Isaac Sim leverage PhysX for robust rigid, soft, and fluid dynamics.
- **Differentiable Physics:** A key advancement is the emergence of differentiable physics engines (e.g., Dojo, DiffTaichi, Genesis), which allow for gradient-based optimization of policies and physical parameters. This capability is presented as a method for improving sample efficiency and sim-to-real transfer by directly aligning optimization with physical principles.
- **Multi-Modality in Sensing:** Manipulation policies benefit from diverse sensing modalities. Vision-only approaches excel at global scene understanding and pre-contact planning. Contact-only sensing (force/torque, tactile) is crucial for post-contact stabilization, slip detection, and force regulation. Vision–contact fusion is increasingly adopted to combine global context with local interaction signals, improving robustness in contact-rich tasks.
- **Policy Learning Trends:** Learning-based policies have evolved from classical RL and IL (Behavior Cloning, ACT) to Diffusion Policies (DPs) for modeling complex action distributions, and to Vision-Language Models (VLMs) and Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models (e.g., RT-series, OpenVLA) for generalist, language-conditioned control. Large-scale datasets (Open X-Embodiment) underpin the development of these generalist policies transferable across different robot embodiments.

#### 4.4. Unification and Failure Modes

- The unification of navigation and manipulation into a single embodied agent is a growing trend, leading to hierarchical systems or learned shared action/representation spaces. This unification imposes increased fidelity requirements on simulators, which must now accurately model traversability, visual semantics, object contacts, articulated objects, base-arm coupling, and whole-body dynamics.
- The survey provides a taxonomy of common simulator-induced failure cases, highlighting that sim-to-real failures often stem from specific mismatches in simulator properties (e.g., geometry, sensing, dynamics, contact, deformable physics, or evaluation artifacts) rather than solely from the learning algorithm itself. These failures can manifest as unsafe contacts, perception shifts, unstable locomotion, grasp slip, or incorrect object deformation.

#### 4.5. Future Directions and Implications

- The paper points to continual learning, neural ODEs for continuous dynamics, and enhanced evaluation metrics as crucial future directions.
- A significant finding is the call for **safe and trustworthy evaluation**, moving beyond simple task success rates to report temporal safety violations, motion naturalness, and sim-to-real predictivity. This shift requires property-level runtime monitors and hardware-in-the-loop validation to ensure that simulated performance is genuinely relevant for real-world deployment.

In summary, the survey emphasizes that physics simulators are not monolithic tools but complex systems whose low-level properties critically determine their utility for sim-to-real transfer. Understanding these properties and their task-specific implications is paramount for advancing EAI.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This survey contributes significantly to the field of Embodied AI by providing a detailed and structured analysis of physics simulators, their properties, and their direct implications for robotic navigation and manipulation, particularly in the context of sim-to-real transfer. Its potential impact spans several dimensions:

#### 5.1. Guiding Research and Development

- **Informed Tool Selection:** The survey offers a practical resource for researchers by consolidating information on benchmark datasets, evaluation metrics, simulation platforms, and methods. By dissecting simulator properties like contact solvers, rendering fidelity, and hardware requirements, it enables researchers to make more informed decisions when selecting and configuring simulation tools for specific EAI tasks. This can reduce trial-and-error and optimize resource allocation.
- **Addressing the Sim-to-Real Gap:** By systematically analyzing the perception and action-dynamics gaps, the survey provides a framework for understanding common sources of sim-to-real failures. This understanding can guide the development of more robust policies and more realistic simulators, ultimately leading to higher fidelity sim-to-real transfer.
- **Highlighting Critical Simulator Features:** The emphasis on low-level simulator properties (e.g., accurate contact modeling for manipulation, hydrodynamic forces for underwater navigation, sensor noise models for perception) underscores their importance. This can encourage simulator developers to prioritize these features and provide more transparent documentation of their approximations and limitations.

#### 5.2. Advancing Embodied AI Capabilities

- **Towards Generalist Agents:** The survey contextualizes recent advancements in learning-based methods, foundation models, and VLA models within the simulation paradigm. By connecting these high-level algorithmic trends with low-level simulator capabilities, it provides insights into how simulation can best support the training of increasingly generalist and capable embodied agents.
- **Robustness and Generalization:** The discussion of domain randomization and transfer-oriented policy learning strategies, alongside specific simulator properties, offers practical guidance for improving the robustness and generalization capabilities of learned policies across diverse real-world conditions.

#### 5.3. Improving Evaluation Standards

- **Beyond Success Rates:** A significant impact lies in the call for more nuanced and comprehensive evaluation metrics. By highlighting that traditional goal-oriented metrics (e.g., SR, SPL) can mask critical issues like unsafe contacts or unnatural motion, the survey advocates for procedural quality metrics, temporal safety monitoring, and sim-to-real predictivity assessments. This can drive the community towards more rigorous and trustworthy evaluation protocols.
- **Standardized Benchmarking:** The compiled list of benchmark datasets and metrics provides a consolidated reference, potentially fostering more standardized and comparable research efforts across the EAI community.

#### 5.4. Identifying Future Research Directions

- The survey clearly articulates key future directions, including continual learning for adaptability, neural Ordinary Differential Equations (ODEs) for continuous dynamics, and the need for safe and trustworthy evaluation frameworks. These insights can stimulate new research initiatives aimed at overcoming current limitations and advancing the field.
- The discussion on the unification of navigation and manipulation, especially with humanoid and legged robots, outlines a demanding frontier for simulator development, requiring integrated modeling of whole-body dynamics, balance, and complex contact interactions.

In conclusion, this survey serves as a timely and essential reference for researchers, developers, and practitioners in Embodied AI. By demystifying the intricate relationship between simulator properties and real-world performance, it provides a foundation for building more effective, reliable, and safely deployable embodied agents in the era of advanced AI.
