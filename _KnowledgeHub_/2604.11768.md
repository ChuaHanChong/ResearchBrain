# Identifying Inductive Biases for Robot Co-Design

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Technische Universität Berlin developed a systematic, landscape-analysis-driven method to uncover three inductive biases for robot co-design. Leveraging these insights, they created the Gradient Covariance Particle Filter Optimization (GC-PFO) algorithm, which achieved robot designs up to 54% better than benchmarks with 270 to 950 times higher sample efficiency across diverse soft robot locomotion and manipulation tasks.

## Problem

- Conventional robotic design typically decouples morphology and control, leading to suboptimal integration and requiring extensive adjustments when changes occur.
- Robot co-design, the simultaneous optimization of morphology and control, is a high-dimensional search problem requiring inductive biases for efficiency, but these biases are often empirically derived without clear systematic rationale.
- Existing general optimization algorithms struggle in the complex co-design space due to a lack of explicit knowledge about its underlying structure.

## Method

- The research systematically analyzed co-design landscapes across four soft robot tasks (two locomotion, two manipulation) using ==gradient covariance matrices== to identify consistent patterns.
- Three inductive biases were identified by characterizing how design quality varies across morphology and control parameter spaces.
- A novel algorithm, Gradient Covariance ==Particle Filter Optimization== (**GC-PFO**), was developed to adaptively infer and leverage these identified biases, guiding particle exploration along task-relevant dimensions.

## Results

- GC-PFO consistently found higher-quality co-designs, outperforming state-of-the-art gradient-based and gradient-free benchmarks by **25**% to **54**% across all four tasks.
- The algorithm demonstrated superior sample efficiency, achieving comparable quality to benchmarks with up to **950** times fewer function evaluations.
- GC-PFO successfully discovered high-quality regions of the co-design space that benchmark algorithms failed to reach within the same computational budget.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Robot co-design spaces exhibit low-dimensional quality manifolds within local regions, where quality variations primarily occur along a small subset of dimensions.
> - The effective dimensionality of the co-design space increases as design quality improves, indicating that more dimensions become relevant for further optimization in higher-quality regions.
> - Morphology and control parameters become more tightly coupled in higher-quality co-designs, with gradients increasingly lying along joint morphology-control dimensions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The design of robotic systems commonly involves the independent development of a robot's physical morphology and its control strategy. This decoupled approach contrasts with biological organisms, which exhibit a tight and co-adapted coupling between body and neural control, developed over evolutionary timescales. The separate design paradigm in robotics often leads to challenges, as modifications in one domain frequently necessitate extensive adjustments in the other to maintain system performance.

Co-design, the simultaneous optimization of a robot's morphology and control, has emerged as a strategy to address this challenge, aiming to achieve synergistic interactions between the physical form and its operational intelligence. However, co-design represents a high-dimensional search problem. Its computational complexity necessitates the use of "inductive biases"—assumptions about the problem's structure that guide the search process towards promising solutions. While various heuristics have been proposed for co-design, their underlying structural rationale is often unclear, as they are typically derived empirically. This lack of systematic identification limits their generalizability and the development of new, more effective biases.

Previous research has explored structure in analogous domains, such as biology and general engineering design. Biological systems often demonstrate low-dimensional structures, for instance, in phenotypic variations or motor synergies, which can simplify control and design. Similarly, analyses of engineering optimization landscapes for complex systems (e.g., aerodynamics, solar cells, ship hulls) have revealed "active subspaces" where quality variation is concentrated along a few dimensions. These insights have been leveraged to develop more efficient search algorithms in those specific fields.

In the context of robot co-design, current approaches often rely on standard gradient-based or gradient-free optimization algorithms. However, the "No Free Lunch theorem" suggests that without exploiting problem-specific structure, no single optimization algorithm can consistently outperform others across all problems. Therefore, the effectiveness of these general algorithms in the complex co-design space remains limited without explicit knowledge of its underlying structure. Existing empirically derived inductive biases for co-design, such as the benefits of co-optimization over control-only optimization, the alignment of complexity with task difficulty, or morphological innovation protection, lack a systematic foundation.

This work contributes to the broader landscape by introducing a systematic, landscape-analysis-driven method for identifying inductive biases specifically tailored for robot co-design. By analyzing how quality varies across morphology and control parameter spaces, the paper aims to uncover fundamental patterns that can inform the design of more principled and efficient co-design algorithms. This approach seeks to move beyond empirical heuristics towards a more theoretically grounded understanding of co-design problem structures.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The motivation for this research stems from the observation that biological organisms achieve high performance through the co-adaptation and tight coupling of their morphology and control systems. In contrast, conventional robotic design often separates these two aspects, leading to suboptimal or challenging integration. Co-design, which involves simultaneously optimizing both morphology and control, offers a path to developing robots with synergistic interactions between their physical body and operational intelligence.

However, implementing co-design presents a significant challenge: it is a high-dimensional search problem. Navigating this vast design space efficiently requires "inductive biases"—assumptions about the problem's structure that can guide the search towards regions likely to contain high-quality solutions. Existing inductive biases for co-design are largely derived empirically, meaning their underlying rationale is not systematically understood, limiting their broader applicability and hindering the development of new, more effective search strategies. A systematic method for identifying these biases, tailored to the specific structure of co-design problems, is currently lacking.

Given this context, the key objectives of this research are as follows:

1. **Systematic Identification of Inductive Biases:** To systematically analyze the underlying optimization landscapes of co-design problems. This involves characterizing how the quality of robot designs (morphology and control) varies across the combined design space.
2. **Pattern Discovery in Co-Design Landscapes:** To identify consistent patterns in how quality varies within and across different regions of co-design spaces. This objective aims to uncover fundamental structural properties applicable to diverse tasks, specifically focusing on soft locomotion and manipulation tasks.
3. **Development of an Efficient Co-Design Algorithm:** To leverage the identified landscape insights and inductive biases to devise a new, efficient co-design algorithm. This algorithm is designed to infer and adapt to the specific structure of each task during the search process, as the precise instantiation of these structures may vary.
4. **Demonstration of Effectiveness and Sample Efficiency:** To experimentally validate the developed algorithm against state-of-the-art benchmark optimization algorithms. The objective is to demonstrate that an algorithm leveraging identified inductive biases can achieve superior performance (higher quality co-designs) and significantly greater sample efficiency (fewer function evaluations) compared to methods that do not explicitly exploit such problem structure.

In summary, the research is motivated by the need to make high-dimensional robot co-design tractable and efficient by systematically understanding and exploiting its underlying structural properties, thereby bridging the gap between biological co-adaptation and robotic engineering.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed a multi-faceted approach involving task definition, landscape analysis, inductive bias identification, algorithm development, and experimental validation.

**A. Co-Design Tasks**
To ensure the identified inductive biases are broadly applicable, the analysis was conducted across four distinct soft robot co-design tasks: two locomotion tasks (Loc$_{84}$ and Loc$_{155}$) and two manipulation tasks (Mani$_{212}$ and Mani$_{320}$).
- **Locomotion Tasks:** These involved co-designing a soft robot to travel the furthest distance within a fixed time. Morphology parameters included the length and stiffness of springs, while control parameters defined the amplitude, frequency, and phase of sinusoidal patterns for actuators. Loc$_{84}$ had 72 morphology and 12 control parameters (total 84), and Loc$_{155}$ had 122 morphology and 33 control parameters (total 155).
- **Manipulation Tasks:** These involved co-designing two soft fingers to rotate an elliptical object by 90° (Mani$_{212}$) or 180° (Mani$_{320}$). Morphology parameters defined spring stiffness, block length and width, and finger gap. Control parameters specified finger curvatures over time. Mani$_{212}$ had 200 morphology and 12 control parameters (total 212), and Mani$_{320}$ had 300 morphology and 20 control parameters (total 320).
All co-design spaces were parameterized using continuous variables to facilitate gradient-based landscape analysis. The tasks were designed to be high-dimensional and complex, making exhaustive search infeasible.

**B. Analyzing Co-Design Landscapes**
To understand how quality varies, the researchers first characterized local regions of the co-design space.
- **Visualizing 2D Subspaces:** Initial qualitative analysis involved plotting landscapes across two-dimensional subspaces (one morphology, one control parameter) to visually demonstrate that quality primarily varies along a few dimensions within a region.
- **Gradient-Based Dimension Identification:** For high-dimensional spaces, a more systematic approach was required. The gradient $\nabla f(x)$ of the quality function $f(x)$ at a point $x$ indicates the direction of steepest ascent. To capture regional variability, gradients were sampled at $N$ points within a region. An uncentered covariance matrix $C = E[\nabla f(x) \nabla f(x)^\top] \approx GG^\top$ was computed, where $G$ is a matrix of sampled gradients. The eigenvalue decomposition of $C$ ($C = V \Lambda V^\top$) was then used. The eigenvalues $\lambda_i$ rank dimensions by the variance in quality along them, and the corresponding eigenvectors $v_i$ define these task-relevant dimensions. This was demonstrated by plotting landscapes along eigenvectors, showing that larger eigenvalues correspond to dimensions with greater quality variation.
- **Gradient Sampling Strategy:** Gradients were sampled from 1000 distinct regions for each task. These regions were selected from trajectories of various optimization algorithms to ensure diversity in quality and landscape characteristics. For each region, gradients were sampled from $N$ points (with $N$ greater than the dimensionality of the co-design space, e.g., 100-350 samples) using a normal distribution around the best co-design found in that iteration.

**C. Identifying Inductive Biases**
The analysis of gradient covariance matrices across numerous regions revealed three consistent patterns:
- **Low-Dimensional Manifold:** The eigenspectra were analyzed by calculating the cumulative explained variance for each region. A rapid rise in cumulative explained variance indicated that a small number of eigenvectors captured most of the gradient variance, suggesting that quality varies along a low-dimensional manifold within a region.
- **Effective Dimensionality (ED):** The effective dimensionality, calculated as the Shannon entropy of the normalized eigenspectrum ($\text{ED} = \exp(-\sum_i p_i \ln p_i)$), was used to quantify how gradient variance spreads across dimensions. This metric was plotted against the loss (inverse quality) of the best co-design in each region to observe trends.
- **Morphology-Control Coupling:** To quantify coupling, each gradient was decomposed into its morphology and control components. The Euclidean norm of each component was calculated, normalized by the square root of its dimensionality, and then averaged across all gradients in a region to yield alignment ratios (m for morphology, c for control). These ratios were plotted against loss to observe changes in coupling as quality improved.

**D. Gradient Covariance Particle Filter Optimization (GC-PFO) Algorithm**
The identified inductive biases (low-dimensional manifold, increasing effective dimensionality, and increasing morphology-control coupling with quality) were leveraged to develop a novel co-design algorithm, Gradient Covariance Particle Filter Optimization (GC-PFO).
- **Adaptive Bias Integration:** GC-PFO modifies the Particle Filter Optimization (PFO) algorithm. PFO uses a collection of "particles" that explore the design space. In GC-PFO, information about a region's structure, derived from the gradient covariance matrix, is used to bias particle exploration.
- **Bias Matrix ($\beta$):** The algorithm computes a bias matrix $\beta = V \tilde{\Lambda}$, where $V$ contains the eigenvectors and $\tilde{\Lambda}$ is the normalized diagonal matrix of square roots of eigenvalues ($\tilde{\Lambda} = \Lambda^{1/2} / (\text{tr}(\Lambda^{1/2}) + \epsilon)$). This matrix guides particle updates by emphasizing exploration along task-relevant dimensions identified by the eigenvectors and weighted by the significance of the corresponding eigenvalues.
- **Algorithm Steps (Algorithm 1):**
 1. **Initialize:** R regions, each with P particles.
 2. **Iterate:** For each region:
 - **Evaluate:** Compute losses and gradients for all particles.
 - **Resample:** Particles are resampled based on their quality using a temperature-annealed probability (higher quality, more likely to be resampled).
 - **Identify Task-Relevant Dimensions:** Compute the gradient covariance matrix $C_r$, decompose it ($C_r = V \Lambda V^\top$), and construct the bias matrix $\beta$.
 - **Update Particle:** Particles are updated by adding a Gaussian noise term $\eta$ scaled and rotated by the bias matrix: $x_{r,i} \leftarrow x_{r,i} + \beta \eta$. This effectively directs exploration along the dynamically identified task-relevant dimensions.

**E. Experimental Evaluation**
- **Benchmarks:** GC-PFO's performance was compared against two gradient-based optimizers (SGD with momentum, Adam) and three gradient-free algorithms (PFO, CMA-ES, Genetic Algorithm (GA)).
- **Hyperparameter Tuning:** Extensive hyperparameter tuning was performed for all algorithms (e.g., learning rates, population sizes, elite ratios, step sizes).
- **Performance Metrics:** The mean best loss across 5 runs with different seeds was reported.
- **Exploration Analysis:** To understand performance differences, t-SNE was used to visualize the explored co-design spaces, and the number of function evaluations required by benchmarks to achieve comparable quality to GC-PFO was quantified.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The analysis of co-design landscapes and the subsequent evaluation of the developed algorithm yielded several key findings:

**A. Identified Inductive Biases**
The analysis of gradient covariance matrices across 1000 regions for each of the four co-design tasks revealed three consistent patterns:

1. **Low-Dimensional Manifold:** Within any given region of the co-design space, quality variations primarily occur along a low-dimensional manifold. The eigenspectra analysis (Fig. 5) consistently showed a rapid rise in cumulative explained variance, indicating that a small subset of eigenvectors captures the majority of the variance in gradients. This implies that most dimensions within a local region are less relevant for quality improvement, effectively reducing the active search space.
2. **Increasing Effective Dimensionality with Quality:** Higher-quality regions exhibit greater effective dimensionality (ED). As depicted in Fig. 6, regions yielding lower loss (higher quality) are associated with a higher ED. This indicates that as co-designs improve, the variance in quality is spread across a larger number of dimensions, suggesting that the search must adapt by exploring more dimensions to continue improving performance.
3. **Increasing Morphology-Control Coupling with Quality:** In higher-quality regions, morphology and control parameters become more tightly coupled. Fig. 7 illustrates that in low-quality regions, gradients often align predominantly with control parameters, suggesting that control adjustments are initially more effective for improvement. However, as co-design quality improves (lower loss), the alignment ratios of morphology and control components converge closer to 0.5. This indicates that gradients increasingly lie along joint morphology-control dimensions, highlighting a stronger synergistic interaction between morphology and control in effective designs.

**B. GC-PFO Performance**
The Gradient Covariance Particle Filter Optimization (GC-PFO) algorithm, designed to leverage these identified inductive biases adaptively, demonstrated superior performance compared to benchmark algorithms across all tasks:

- **Overall Outperformance:** GC-PFO consistently found higher-quality co-designs (lower loss) than conventional gradient-based (SGD, Adam) and gradient-free (PFO, CMA-ES, GA) algorithms (Fig. 8).
- **Significant Improvement Margins:** In locomotion tasks (Loc$_{84}$ and Loc$_{155}$), GC-PFO achieved approximately 29% and 54% better performance than the next-best algorithm, PFO. For manipulation tasks (Mani$_{212}$ and Mani$_{320}$), it produced co-designs that were 25% and 39% better than those found by CMA-ES.
- **Gradient-Based Limitations:** SGD and Adam consistently plateaued after a few iterations, suggesting that their fixed-dimensional gradient-descent approach struggled to adapt to the increasing effective dimensionality and coupling observed in higher-quality regions.

**C. Co-Design Space Exploration and Sample Efficiency**
Analysis of the exploration patterns further clarified GC-PFO's effectiveness:

- **Discovery of High-Quality Regions:** t-SNE visualization (Fig. 10a) showed that GC-PFO consistently explored and identified high-quality regions of the co-design space that benchmark algorithms failed to reach within the given computational budget. Benchmarks often remained in lower-quality regions or only reached higher-quality areas through random restarts, which are computationally expensive.
- **Superior Sample Efficiency:** GC-PFO exhibited significantly higher sample efficiency. When given a limited budget of $5 \times 10^4$ function evaluations for GC-PFO, benchmark algorithms required substantially more evaluations to achieve comparable quality (Fig. 10b). Specifically, GA and Adam eventually reached similar quality co-designs for Loc$_{155}$ but required 270 and 950 times more function evaluations, respectively. Other benchmark algorithms were unable to reach comparable quality even with 1000 times more evaluations, underscoring the efficiency gained by explicitly leveraging inductive biases.

**D. Characteristics of Best Co-designs**
The best co-designs generated by GC-PFO for the tasks (Fig. 9) illustrate the practical outcomes of the algorithm:
- **Locomotion:** Morphologies that complement effective leg synchronization for forward movement.
- **Manipulation:** Designs featuring an initial gap closure for robust object grasping, asymmetric finger designs for pivoting, and compliant tips combined with stiff sections for robust object manipulation against external torques.

These results collectively demonstrate the effectiveness of identifying and adaptively leveraging problem-specific inductive biases to navigate complex co-design spaces efficiently and find higher-quality solutions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of robotics and artificial intelligence by addressing a fundamental challenge in the co-design of robot morphology and control: the lack of systematic methods for identifying and exploiting problem-specific structures. The significance and potential impact of this work are multi-faceted:

- **Systematic Inductive Bias Identification:** The paper introduces a systematic, landscape-analysis-driven methodology for uncovering inductive biases in high-dimensional co-design problems. This moves beyond empirical heuristics, providing a more principled framework for understanding why certain design choices or optimization strategies are effective. This methodological innovation can be generalized to other complex design optimization problems where underlying landscape structures are poorly understood.
- **Discovery of Generalizable Co-Design Principles:** The identification of three consistent patterns across diverse soft robot locomotion and manipulation tasks (low-dimensional quality manifolds, increasing effective dimensionality with quality, and increasing morphology-control coupling with quality) suggests that these might be fundamental, generalizable properties of co-design spaces. This insight can form the basis for more robust and universally applicable co-design guidelines and algorithms.
- **Enhanced Co-Design Algorithm Performance:** The developed Gradient Covariance Particle Filter Optimization (GC-PFO) algorithm demonstrates substantial improvements in both the quality of discovered co-designs and sample efficiency compared to state-of-the-art benchmarks. By adaptively inferring and leveraging task-specific structures during the search, GC-PFO enables the discovery of superior robot designs with significantly fewer computational resources. This efficiency is critical for complex robotic systems where physical simulation or real-world evaluation is costly and time-consuming.
- **Inspiration for Novel Representations:** The finding that higher-quality co-designs exhibit tight coupling between morphology and control suggests that future research could focus on developing novel, intrinsically coupled representations for morphology and control. Such representations might inherently capture synergistic interactions, potentially simplifying the co-design problem further and leading to more integrated and bio-inspired robot designs.
- **Guidance for Future Research:** The work opens several avenues for future research. Verifying these identified landscape structures with alternative morphology and control representations is a logical next step to confirm their generality. Furthermore, the paper highlights the potential for extracting task-specific structural information from prior data or expert knowledge, not just online during search, which could lead to even more efficient algorithms.
- **Bridging Biology and Robotics:** By systematically exploring and exploiting the tight coupling between form and function, this research contributes to bridging the gap between biological principles of co-adaptation and engineering design practices in robotics. This could lead to the development of robots that exhibit greater adaptability, robustness, and performance, akin to their biological counterparts.

In summary, this research provides a significant step towards a more principled and efficient approach to robot co-design, offering both a systematic methodology for understanding complex design spaces and a high-performing algorithm that leverages these insights for practical benefit.
