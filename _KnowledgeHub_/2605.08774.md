# ProcVLM: Learning Procedure-Grounded Progress Rewards for Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> ProcVLM is a vision-language model that learns dense progress rewards explicitly tied to procedural task stages in robotic manipulation. It enhances embodied procedural understanding and improves downstream policy learning through better feedback, demonstrating strong one-shot generalization and stabilizing training with noisy real-robot data.

## Problem

- Existing large-scale robot datasets lack dense, procedural progress supervision, typically providing only sparse, trajectory-level success labels.
- Current reward models often rely on sparse outcomes or time-based interpolation, which inaccurately represents progress in complex, long-horizon manipulation tasks.
- There is a need for transferable dense rewards explicitly grounded in the semantic procedural structure of a task, rather than relying on unreliable temporal proxies.

## Method

- **ProcVLM**, a ==progress-aware vision-language model==, was developed to learn and predict ==procedure-grounded progress== as a dense reward signal.
- A scalable, automated pipeline was created to synthesize frame-level procedural annotations (==ProcCorpus-60M==) from raw robot trajectories using large VLMs.
- Continuous progress targets were defined by combining ==subtask-level structure== with intra-subtask visual motion, and the model was pretrained with a ==two-stage strategy== (ProcVQA).

## Results

- **ProcVLM** achieved **superior performance** on the ProcVQA benchmark for embodied procedural understanding, securing the highest **BF1@5** for action segmentation and **VOC/EPR@50** for task progress estimation.
- It demonstrated strong one-shot generalization on unseen RoboFAC tasks, improving **VOC_succ by 85.7%** and reducing **MAE_fail by 38.0%** compared to zero-shot baselines.
- ProcVLM-guided reward fine-tuning yielded significant gains in success rates for downstream policy learning (e.g., **+25.0 pp** in real-robot Stack-Bowls task), stabilizing training with noisy teleoperation data.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - ProcVLM utilizes a 'reasoning-before-estimation' paradigm, inferring current execution stages and remaining atomic actions before predicting a continuous progress score.
> - Dense, continuous progress labels can be automatically derived by combining subtask-level structural duration with local visual changes, offering more nuanced feedback than temporal proxies.
> - Automated, hierarchical synthesis of frame-level procedural annotations enables the creation of large-scale, semantically rich datasets (ProcCorpus-60M) for training progress-aware VLMs.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation has seen advancements with vision-language-action (VLA) models, which facilitate policy generalization through large-scale robot demonstrations. However, a limitation of this demonstration-centric pretraining is the high cost of diverse data collection and challenges in adapting pretrained policies to novel tasks, environments, or failure modes. This has motivated research into reward-guided policy improvement paradigms, where VLA policies can be enhanced through autonomous experience, expert corrections, learned reward feedback, or self-improvement signals.

A central challenge in these reward-guided paradigms is the scarcity of dense, procedural progress supervision within existing robot datasets. Most large-scale corpora typically provide task instructions, observations, actions, and sometimes trajectory-level success labels, but they often lack explicit annotations for procedural structure, such as subtask boundaries or remaining actions. Consequently, current robotic reward or progress models often derive feedback indirectly from sparse outcomes, time-based interpolation, temporal-difference learning, or comparison- and preference-based signals.

The paper argues that treating task progress as a simple function of timestep is insufficient for long-horizon manipulation tasks. Such tasks involve multiple semantic stages with varying rates of progress. A policy might spend significant time on a single step, become stalled, or recover from earlier failures. In these scenarios, temporal progression does not reliably indicate true task advancement. Transferable dense rewards, therefore, require grounding in the explicit procedural structure of a task rather than relying on temporal proxies.

This work addresses this gap by introducing ProcVLM, a model designed to provide dense progress rewards explicitly grounded in the procedural stages of a task. It aims to offer more discriminative feedback that distinguishes between actual task advancement, stagnation, or failure, moving beyond reward signals derived solely from terminal outcomes or time-based proxies. The research aligns with efforts in embodied VLMs for procedural reasoning, aiming to enhance long-horizon understanding, and with advancements in reward modeling for manipulation, specifically by providing denser, more structured feedback than previous VLM-based reward and progress models which often rely on whole-trajectory completion interpolation with limited procedural constraints.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop ProcVLM, a progress-aware vision-language model capable of learning and predicting procedure-grounded progress as a dense reward signal for robotic manipulation tasks. This objective is motivated by several key challenges in current robotic learning paradigms:

1. **Lack of Dense Procedural Supervision**: Existing large-scale robot datasets, while extensive in observations and actions, typically provide only sparse supervision, such as task instructions or overall success/failure labels. They generally lack detailed, frame-level annotations of procedural stages, subtask boundaries, and remaining actions. This sparsity limits the effectiveness of reward-guided policy improvement, which thrives on continuous, informative feedback.

2. **Limitations of Existing Reward Models**: Current approaches to generating dense rewards often rely on:
 - **Trajectory-level success labels**: These provide feedback only at the end of an episode, offering no guidance during execution.
 - **Time-based interpolation**: This method assumes linear progress over time, which is inaccurate for complex, long-horizon tasks where progress rates can vary significantly. It fails to distinguish between genuine advancement, repeated attempts at a single step, stagnation, or recovery from failure.
 - **Temporal-difference learning or comparison-based signals**: While providing some density, these methods may not inherently ground progress in the semantic stages of a procedure, potentially conflating different types of state changes or requiring extensive comparison data.

3. **Need for Procedure-Grounded Feedback**: The motivation is to create a reward signal that is explicitly grounded in the semantic structure of a task, reflecting:
 - **Subtask semantics**: Accurately identifying completed steps, remaining actions, and stage transitions.
 - **Within-stage advancement**: Capturing visual changes that indicate progress within a specific subtask, not just across subtasks.
 - **Discrimination of task states**: Clearly indicating unfinished steps, stagnation, or failure states.

To achieve these objectives, ProcVLM is specifically designed with the following principles and components:

- **Reasoning-Before-Estimation Paradigm**: Instead of direct progress prediction, ProcVLM first infers the current execution stage and remaining atomic actions, and then predicts a continuous progress score conditioned on this explicit procedural reasoning. This aims to make reward prediction more sensitive to actual task progression and specific states.
- **Synthesized Procedural Supervision**: The project aims to automatically construct dense supervision by:
 - Synthesizing frame-level subtask-semantic annotations from raw trajectories.
 - Assigning progress "budgets" based on the structural duration of each subtask.
 - Distributing these budgets within each subtask according to intra-subtask visual changes.
- **Scalable Data Construction**: To train ProcVLM at scale, a key objective is to build a standardized procedural supervision synthesis pipeline and construct a large-scale corpus, ProcCorpus-60M, derived from multiple embodied datasets. This corpus is then used to create ProcVQA for procedure-aware pretraining, centralizing progress estimation alongside auxiliary tasks like action segmentation and future planning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

ProcVLM employs a multi-faceted approach encompassing a scalable data annotation pipeline, a novel method for defining continuous progress targets, and a two-stage pretraining strategy for a vision-language model.

#### 3.1. Procedural Supervision Synthesis

To generate the dense, procedure-grounded supervision required, the authors developed a scalable pipeline utilizing large vision-language models (VLMs) as automatic annotators. This pipeline converts raw manipulation trajectories into frame-wise annotations, forming ProcCorpus-60M.

- **Hierarchical Annotation Pipeline**: The pipeline is designed for efficiency and consistency, decoupling data reading, multimodal preprocessing, VLM inference, and post-processing into queue-connected workers.
 - **Global-to-Local Annotation**: For each episode, a VLM (Qwen3-VL-235B-A22B-Instruct) first infers a high-level task plan and localizes subtask temporal spans across the entire video. This video-level segmentation establishes a global procedural structure, enhancing temporal consistency.
 - **Keyframe Annotation**: These segments are then expanded into frame-wise subtask assignments, and concise reasoning is generated only for visually distinct keyframes (using InternVL3.5-38B).
 - **Post-processing**: Neighboring frames subsequently reuse reasoning from nearby keyframes, maintaining dense supervision without redundant VLM calls. The process outputs JSONL annotations including task-centric scene reasoning, completion states, subtask annotations, and optional 2D bounding box groundings.
- **Data Scaling and Filtering**: ProcCorpus-60M was constructed from 30 diverse real-robot and simulation datasets, including DROID, BridgeData V2, Fractal, RH20T, Table30, selected OXE subsets, LIBERO, RoboTwin 2.0, and GR00T-Teleop-Sim. The corpus totals approximately 400K trajectories and 60M annotated frames. A curated, high-quality refinement subset of about 15K trajectories, selected through manual inspection for subtask segmentation accuracy and task diversity, is also created for a subsequent training stage.

#### 3.2. Learning Procedure-Grounded Progress Rewards

ProcVLM learns continuous progress rewards based on the structured annotations from ProcCorpus-60M.

- **Procedure-Defined Progress Targets**: Continuous progress labels are derived by combining subtask-level structure with intra-subtask visual motion.
 - The progress p(t) at time t is defined as a normalized accumulation of local visual change, weighted by subtask duration.
 - Subtask-level weights w(τ) assign a global progress budget based on a subtask's relative duration, with clipping to soft-anchor around an equal-subtask prior.
 - Local progress rate r(τ) distributes this budget within a subtask according to local visual changes, approximated by adjacent-frame perceptual differences with a numerical stabilizer.
- **Procedure-aware Pretraining (ProcVQA)**: The frame-wise annotations are converted into ProcVQA, a 20B-token VLM training corpus. Pretraining is formulated as multi-task VQA learning over robot observations and task instructions, focusing on three task families:
 - **Subtask-structured progress prediction**: The model outputs remaining atomic subtasks before estimating completion as a continuous percentage, formatted within `<progress>` tags (e.g., `<progress> p% </progress>`).
 - **Action segmentation**: The model predicts a sequence of atomic subtask segments with semantic labels and temporal boundaries from a video and instruction.
 - **Future planning**: Given recent observations and an instruction, the model predicts the immediate next atomic action or lists remaining actions.
- **ProcVLM Architecture and Training Objectives**:
 - **Architecture**: ProcVLM utilizes a compact vision-language backbone initialized from Qwen3-VL-2B-Instruct. This backbone processes task instructions and visual observations, generating textual responses via a standard autoregressive language modeling head.
 - **Progress Value Head**: A three-layer MLP is attached to the shared VLM representations. For progress estimation samples, this branch regresses a scalar completion score from contextual hidden states, employing attention pooling over relevant multimodal and textual representations. This gated design separates general text generation from continuous regression.
 - **Joint Objective**: ProcVLM is trained with a joint objective function: L = L_LM + λ ⋅ I_prog L_value. L_LM is the standard autoregressive language modeling loss applied to all textual responses (including the reasoning and formatted answer for progress prediction). L_value is a regression loss (ℓ_reg) for the continuous progress score ˆp parsed from the `<progress>` tag.
 - **Leakage-Prevention Tail Dropout**: To prevent the value head from directly recovering the ground-truth progress value from the text tokens during teacher forcing, feature-level dropout is applied to the final N_tail hidden states containing the progress answer before value-head pooling.
 - **Two-stage Training**:
 1. **Large-scale procedure-aware pretraining**: On the full ProcVQA dataset (~20B tokens) to learn general procedural representations.
 2. **Refinement**: On the curated 2.8B-token subset to sharpen subtask alignment and progress estimation with higher-quality data.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of ProcVLM was conducted across three primary areas: embodied procedural understanding, generalizable progress reward evaluation, and downstream policy learning.

#### 4.1. Embodied Procedural Understanding

On the ProcVQA benchmark, ProcVLM demonstrated overall strong performance on both in-distribution (ID) and out-of-distribution (OOD) splits.
- ProcVLM achieved the highest BF1@5 for action segmentation, the highest Success rate for future planning, and the highest Value-Order Correlation (VOC) and Effective Progress Resolution (EPR@50) for task progress estimation.
- This performance indicates ProcVLM's capacity for embodied procedural reasoning compared to mainstream VLMs such as Qwen3VL, Qwen3.5, Gemini 3.1 Pro, and GPT-5.4. While its mMAE for action segmentation was not the lowest, its primary segmentation metric (BF1@5) showed clear improvement.

#### 4.2. Generalizable Progress Reward Evaluation

- **Zero-Shot Comparison with Robotic Reward Models**: When compared zero-shot on the ProcVQA-OOD progress estimation subset, ProcVLM achieved the best VOC, suggesting stronger trajectory-internal progress ordering. Robometer attained a higher EPR@50, potentially due to its progress-regression head design, but had a lower VOC. RoboDopamine showed stable performance but lagged in both VOC and EPR@50, which might indicate a tendency to predict coarse value anchors without a dedicated continuous regression mechanism.
- **One-Shot Generalization on RoboFAC**: ProcVLM exhibited rapid adaptation to unseen RoboFAC tasks with minimal demonstrations.
 - With one successful demonstration per task (1-shot Succ. setting), ProcVLM's VOC_succ improved by 85.7%, MCC (for binary success detection) increased by 18.8%, and MAE_fail (for fault localization) decreased by 38.0% compared to its zero-shot performance. These improvements suggest that semantic process reasoning enables generalization beyond the demonstrated success case.
 - In contrast, Robometer showed minimal improvement under the same one-shot setting.
 - ProcVLM maintained substantially lower inference latency compared to larger baseline reward models.
 - Zero-shot ProcVLM showed the best MCC for binary success detection, an advantage further amplified with successful and failed demonstrations.
 - After one-shot adaptation, ProcVLM's MAE_fail became comparable to Robometer, despite Robometer's larger model scale and more extensive reward pretraining.
- **Ablation Studies**:
 - Removing ProcVLM's procedure-aware pretraining led to the most significant degradation in performance, particularly for MAE_fail and MCC, indicating the importance of transferable procedural representations.
 - Removing reasoning-formatted supervision resulted in consistent but milder drops across metrics, suggesting that explicit task-structure reasoning aids in aligning low-level subtask cues with progress labels.
 - A "Base" variant (without pretraining or reasoning) showed severe degradation on MAE_fail and MCC, indicating overfitting to surface-level cues and highlighting the synergy between pretraining and structured reasoning.

#### 4.3. Reward Fine-tuning

ProcVLM-guided Reward Fine-tuning (RFT) was evaluated as a dense reward model for downstream policy learning.
- **Simulation (LIBERO-10)**: RFT yielded moderate early-stage gains of +0.4 to +1.2 percentage points in success rates compared to vanilla supervised fine-tuning (SFT), building on a strong pretrained policy.
- **Real-Robot (Stack-Bowls Task)**: RFT achieved larger gains of +25.0 and +12.5 percentage points at 5K and 10K training steps, respectively. This improvement is attributed to RFT's ability to downweight noisy local behaviors (e.g., repeated grasp retries) in teleoperation-collected data, stabilizing policy fine-tuning by conditioning updates on high-advantage samples.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work presents ProcVLM as a significant contribution to robotic learning by providing a method for generating dense, procedure-grounded reward signals. Its significance and potential impact span several areas:

- **Addressing the Sparse Reward Problem**: ProcVLM directly confronts a fundamental challenge in long-horizon robotic manipulation: the scarcity of dense and informative reward feedback. By learning progress based on procedural structure and intra-stage visual changes, it offers a more nuanced and continuous reward signal than traditional sparse or time-based methods.
- **Enhancing Transferability and Adaptability**: The scalable procedural supervision synthesis pipeline and subsequent pretraining on ProcCorpus-60M enable ProcVLM to learn generalizable procedural representations. This allows for transferable reward modeling that can adapt to new tasks with minimal demonstrations, as evidenced by its strong one-shot generalization performance on the RoboFAC benchmark. Such adaptability is crucial for deploying robots in diverse, real-world scenarios.
- **Improving Policy Optimization**: ProcVLM-guided reward fine-tuning has demonstrated the ability to stabilize and improve downstream policy learning, particularly in the presence of noisy real-robot demonstration data. By providing more discriminative feedback, ProcVLM helps policies focus on effective actions and overcome issues like local optima or over-reliance on suboptimal behaviors present in teleoperated datasets. This can lead to more robust and efficient robot skill acquisition.
- **Advancing Embodied Procedural Reasoning**: The model's performance on ProcVQA benchmarks in tasks like action segmentation, future planning, and progress estimation indicates an improved capacity for embodied procedural understanding. The "reasoning-before-estimation" paradigm, which infers remaining atomic actions prior to estimating progress, provides a structured approach to task comprehension that goes beyond superficial visual correlations.
- **Scalable Data Annotation**: The development of an automated, hierarchical pipeline for synthesizing frame-level procedural annotations from raw robot trajectories offers a scalable solution to the bottleneck of manual data labeling. This approach can facilitate the creation of larger, more semantically rich datasets, which are essential for training increasingly capable generalist robotic systems.
- **Future Robotic Systems**: The ability to provide robust, task-conditioned progress feedback could be instrumental in developing more autonomous and intelligent robotic agents. Such agents could use these dense rewards for self-correction, online adaptation, and more effective exploration in complex environments, moving closer to generalist robotic capabilities.

While the approach is promising, its estimates can be influenced by the quality of subtask decomposition and temporal boundary localization. Future work may explore integrations with diverse reinforcement learning algorithms and further enhancements to the robustness and calibration of continuous progress prediction.
