# Athena-WBC: Capability-Aligned Policy Experts for Long-Tail Humanoid Whole-Body Control

> [!summary] Summary
> XPENG Robotics researchers developed Athena-WBC, a teacher-student pipeline that trains specialized dynamic and balance policy experts with tailored acquisition recipes to overcome "capability bottlenecks" in humanoid whole-body control. This approach enhanced held-out tracking success rates by over 1% on D_AMASS-eval and significantly improved the recovery of challenging, long-tail motions compared to a strong baseline.

## Problem

- Humanoid whole-body controllers struggle to reliably execute "long-tail" motions, such as high-dynamic transitions and balance-critical movements, even after extensive training.
- Existing generalist controllers often exhibit persistent training-set failures that are not solely attributed to insufficient data exposure.
- A "capability bottleneck" exists where standard reinforcement learning training recipes induce conservative control regimes that limit the robot's physical capabilities for demanding motions.

## Method

- A ==two-round teacher training== process identifies residual failures and then trains specialized "dynamic" and "balance" policy experts on these difficult motions with distinct acquisition recipes.
- Dynamic experts remove conservative effort and temporal-control penalties from rewards, instead using an auxiliary second-difference smoothness loss (==Grad-CAPS==) for smooth yet aggressive actuation.
- Balance experts employ a ==gravity continuation curriculum== during training, gradually scaling gravity from a reduced level to nominal, which prolongs early rollouts and aids learning for low-support poses.
- A single student policy is trained via ==DAgger distillation== to mimic the motion-routed teachers' actions, followed by ==PPO==-based reinforcement learning fine-tuning to improve generalization and deployment quality.

## Results

- Athena-WBC improved held-out tracking performance, increasing Success Rate from **98.18%** to **99.26%** on D_AMASS-eval and from **91.81%** to **94.89%** on D_Omni-eval, while consistently reducing tracking errors.
- The multi-teacher student successfully consolidated capabilities, achieving the strongest balance-subset performance (**94.73%** SR, **88.61 mm** MPJPE-W) and improving dynamic-subset recovery over baselines on training data.
- Analysis confirmed that removing conservative reward penalties significantly improved tracking success (e.g., **99.46%** SR on AMASS-eval) but at a substantial cost of increased action variation (Action Rate of **1.46** vs. **0.54**), validating the identified capability bottleneck.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Training-set failures in long-tail humanoid motions are primarily due to a "capability bottleneck," where standard reward functions and training setups inadvertently limit the robot's ability to perform physically feasible yet aggressive or balance-critical actions.
> - Tailoring acquisition recipes, such as modifying reward objectives or introducing specific curricula for specialized policy experts, effectively addresses distinct types of challenging motions (e.g., high-dynamic vs. balance-critical).
> - Auxiliary policy losses (like Grad-CAPS) can achieve a more favorable balance between tracking performance and control smoothness compared to direct reward-level penalties, which tend to suppress necessary aggressive actuation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work addresses a specific challenge within the rapidly advancing field of learning-based humanoid whole-body control (WBC), which has seen progress in enabling robots to track diverse human motion corpora. Previous research, exemplified by systems such as PHC, OmniH2O, GMT, EGM, and SONIC, has focused on achieving high aggregate tracking performance through methods like reinforcement learning, privileged teacher policies, and student distillation. These systems often leverage large datasets, scalable architectures, and substantial computational resources to train generalist controllers.

A common approach to improving performance, particularly for less frequently encountered or difficult "long-tail" motions, involves reallocating training effort. Strategies include adaptive sampling of difficult motions (e.g., GMT, EGM, ZEST) or partitioning motion corpora to train specialized experts (e.g., BumbleBee). These methods primarily focus on data exposure or reducing interference between motion types.

Athena-WBC distinguishes itself by arguing that for certain residual failures, merely reallocating data or training general experts is insufficient. It posits that a "capability bottleneck" exists, where the standard training recipe itself induces control regimes mismatched to the demands of high-dynamic or balance-critical motions. This contrasts with approaches like OmniTrack, which emphasize generating physically consistent references to simplify the tracking problem for a single deployable policy. While OmniTrack demonstrates the power of upstream reference correction, Athena-WBC explores the regime where embodiment difficulty and policy capability still present challenges, even with strong physical-motion generation.

Furthermore, the paper engages with existing work on regularization for deployable control quality (e.g., CAPS, Grad-CAPS, general policy regularizers) and curriculum learning (e.g., Residual Force Control, ZEST, A2CF, Parkour in the Wild). Athena-WBC proposes a nuanced approach to regularization, distinguishing between physical feasibility constraints and conservative control preferences, and employs a gravity curriculum specifically for balance-critical motions. The pipeline also utilizes established teacher-student distillation (DAgger) and RL fine-tuning techniques, similar to Parkour in the Wild, but applies them to consolidate capabilities from specialized experts designed to overcome identified bottlenecks. The work contributes by identifying a specific type of failure (capability bottleneck) and proposing targeted acquisition recipe modifications, complemented by novel evaluation metrics for the high-coverage regime.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to improve the recovery of "long-tail" motions within the training set for humanoid whole-body control, specifically addressing motions that remain unsolved even after extensive training with state-of-the-art generalist controllers. The work also aims to maintain or enhance overall tracking performance and control quality on held-out motions.

The motivation stems from an observation that even strong baselines, such as the SONIC recipe, exhibit residual training-set failures. These failures are not uniformly distributed but concentrate in:
1. **High-dynamic transitions**: Motions involving rapid direction changes, aggressive contact switches, or high momentum requirements.
2. **Balance-critical motions**: Movements characterized by low-support poses, single-leg stances, or slow recovery phases.

The authors identify that these failures are not solely due to insufficient exposure to rare motions. Instead, controlled experiments revealed that a subset of failures persists even when policies are trained specifically on these difficult clips. This led to the hypothesis of a **"capability bottleneck,"** suggesting a mismatch between the inherent demands of certain motions and the "effective capability" induced by the default training recipe.

Specifically, two key issues are identified:
- For high-dynamic motions, standard reward components that penalize control effort and temporal smoothness (e.g., torque magnitude, action rate) can inadvertently suppress aggressive yet physically feasible actuation. This encourages conservative control that may be suitable for general motion but restricts the robot's ability to execute fast, forceful maneuvers.
- For balance-critical motions, early training under nominal gravity often leads to policy collapse before informative tracking states are reached. This hinders the learning process for achieving stable low-support or single-leg poses.

Therefore, the motivation is to develop a training pipeline that adapts the learning "capability" of specialized policy experts to these specific long-tail challenges, rather than merely reallocating training data. This includes modifying reward objectives and introducing targeted curricula, followed by distillation into a single deployable controller.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Athena-WBC pipeline is a compact teacher-student architecture designed to address long-tail humanoid whole-body control failures through capability-aligned policy experts.

#### 3.1. Two-Round Capability-Aligned Teacher Training

The process begins with training privileged teachers that have access to clean simulator and reference-tracking features, which are later distilled into a student with deployable observations.

- **Round 1: General Teacher**: A general privileged teacher (πgenT) is initially trained on the full training motion set (Dtrain). After training, it is evaluated on every training clip using multiple stochastic rollouts (Keval=10). A clip is identified as a residual failure if the general teacher succeeds in fewer than 80% of its rollouts (cSR(πgenT, τm) < ρfail = 0.8). This set of residual failures is denoted Rgen.

- **Round 2: Dynamic and Balance Experts**: Two additional teachers, a dynamic expert (πdynT) and a balance expert (πbalT), are trained *on the same residual set Rgen*. Both experts are exposed to the same difficult motions but employ distinct acquisition recipes tailored to specific capability bottlenecks.
 - **Dynamic Expert**: Targets motions requiring aggressive transitions and large, feasible control responses. Its reward objective (rdyn) retains tracking rewards (rtrack) and physical constraint penalties (rphys) but removes effort (reffort) and temporal-control penalties (rtemp). Physical constraints (joint limits, velocity limits, torque limits, foot slip) are maintained to ensure safety and feasibility. To reintroduce smoothness without conservative reward penalties, the policy mean is regularized with a second-difference smoothness loss (LGradCAPS), which penalizes abrupt changes in action rate. The policy is optimized with L = LPPO + λregLGradCAPS.
 - **Balance Expert**: Focuses on motions where early survivability and low-support stabilization are critical. It employs a gravity continuation curriculum, where gravity (ge) is gradually scaled from a minimum (αmin) up to nominal gravity (g0) during training. This reduced gravity prolongs early rollouts, providing more informative states for learning balance.

- **Adaptive Motion Sampling**: Throughout teacher training, DAgger distillation rollouts, and RL fine-tuning, an adaptive motion sampling mechanism is enabled. This sampler prioritizes clips with larger tracking errors by converting rollout tracking errors into smoothed temporal-bin difficulty scores and then clip-level sampling probabilities, while also retaining a uniform sampling mixture.

#### 3.2. Motion-Routed Teacher Selection

After both rounds of teacher training, the bank of teachers (ΠT = {πgenT, πdynT, πbalT}) is frozen. Each teacher is evaluated on every training clip. For each motion τm, the teacher that empirically tracks it best (k∗(m) = arg maxk: πkT∈ΠT cSR(πkT, τm)) is selected. This fixed motion-level routing table determines which teacher supervises each motion during distillation.

#### 3.3. DAgger Distillation into a Single Student

A single student policy, using only deployable observations, is trained to mimic the actions of the motion-routed teachers via DAgger-style behavior cloning. The student rolls out in simulation, and for each visited state, the action mean of the best-performing routed teacher for that motion is queried (using its privileged observation) and used as the target for the student's action mean. The distillation objective (Ldistill) minimizes the squared difference between the student's action mean (μS) and the routed teacher's action mean (μk∗(m)T), potentially including auxiliary representation losses (Laux).

#### 3.4. RL Fine-tuning

The distilled student is then further fine-tuned using PPO with its deployable observation interface. This stage follows a staged procedure:
1. **Critic-only warm-up**: The actor is frozen, and only the value function is trained. Rollouts are generated by the frozen actor with added Gaussian action noise to expose the critic to a neighborhood around the distilled policy.
2. **Gradual actor unfreezing**: The actor is gradually unfrozen.
3. **Standard PPO fine-tuning**: PPO continues with the fully unfrozen actor under nominal environment dynamics. All teacher-related inputs and training-only curriculum variables are disabled during this stage, which focuses on improving closed-loop tracking and deployment quality.

**Experimental Setup and Evaluation Metrics**
- **Embodiment**: Experiments are conducted on a full-size 80 kg XPENG humanoid robot platform with planetary-roller-screw actuation and closed-chain mechanisms. Comparisons are made against a re-implementation of the SONIC training recipe on this platform.
- **Training Data**: A corpus of 55,482 clips (175.88 hours) from AMASS, Bones-Seed, BEAT, and a curated mocap dataset.
- **Evaluation Splits**: Include the full training set, an IID held-out set (DAMASS-eval), and a hard OOD held-out set (DOmni-eval) for generalization, plus diagnostic long-tail training subsets (Ddynamic, Dbalance).
- **Metrics**:
 - **Success Rate (SR)**: Standard threshold-based tracking success.
 - **Threshold-Robust Success**: Measured by Success–Tolerance Curve (STC) and Threshold-Integrated Success (TIS) to assess robustness across varying error tolerances.
 - **Tracking Errors**: Mean Per-Joint Position Error (MPJPE) and Motion-Salience Weighted MPJPE (MPJPE-W) which weights body parts based on their motion activity in the reference.
 - **Smoothness Metrics**: Action Rate and Action Jerk to quantify control quality.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments evaluated Athena-WBC against baselines including SONIC-Base, a "no-smoothness" diagnostic baseline, a single-teacher student, and the multi-teacher student both before and after RL fine-tuning.

**Q1: Held-out Tracking and Control Quality (Table 3, Figure 8)**
- **Overall Performance**: The RL-finetuned Athena-WBC policy demonstrated improved held-out tracking performance compared to SONIC-Base. For D_AMASS-eval, SR increased from 98.18% to 99.26% and TIS from 0.7631 to 0.8034. For D_Omni-eval, SR increased from 91.81% to 94.89% and TIS from 0.7123 to 0.7449. Tracking errors (MPJPE and MPJPE-W) also consistently decreased across both held-out sets.
- **Capability Bottleneck Confirmation**: The "no-smoothness baseline" achieved the highest default-threshold SR on held-out sets (99.46% on AMASS-eval, 95.33% on Omni-eval). This supported the hypothesis that removing conservative effort and temporal-control penalties facilitates tracking, but at a significant cost: its Action Rate was substantially higher (1.46 vs. 0.54 for SONIC-Base), rendering it non-deployable.
- **Role of Distillation and Fine-tuning**: Both the single-teacher and multi-teacher students improved tracking error metrics over SONIC-Base while maintaining comparable action rates. RL fine-tuning further enhanced threshold-integrated tracking (TIS) and Cartesian tracking accuracy (MPJPE/MPJPE-W), demonstrating its role in generalization and deployment quality.
- **Robustness to Thresholds**: Success–Tolerance Curves (Figure 8) illustrated that while the no-smoothness baseline might have a higher peak SR, the RL-finetuned policy often exhibited stronger performance across a broader range of tolerances, particularly in stricter regimes, leading to superior TIS and lower MPJPE/MPJPE-W.

**Q2: Long-Tail Training-Set Recovery (Table 4)**
- **Baseline Long-Tail Weaknesses**: SONIC-Base showed lower performance on diagnostic long-tail subsets, especially balance-critical motions (90.60% SR on D_balance vs. 98.48% on full train), confirming that aggregate metrics can obscure systematic failures.
- **Capability Bottleneck and Recovery**: The no-smoothness baseline improved full-training MPJPE and SR, and achieved the highest dynamic-subset SR (98.75%), validating that conservative reward regularization hindered long-tail learning.
- **Expert Specialization**: The Grad-CAPS dynamic expert achieved the lowest dynamic-subset errors, while the gravity-curriculum balance expert significantly improved balance-critical tracking (MPJPE-W from 101.99 mm to 90.22 mm). This indicates that the tailored acquisition recipes biased learning towards different operating regimes effectively.
- **Multi-Teacher Student**: This policy successfully consolidated capabilities, achieving the strongest balance-subset performance (94.73% SR, 75.36 mm MPJPE, 88.61 mm MPJPE-W) while maintaining strong overall training performance. This supported the effectiveness of motion-routed distillation.
- **RL Fine-tuning's Role**: RL fine-tuning did not primarily drive training-set long-tail recovery; its main impact was on generalization and held-out performance, as noted in Q1.

**Q3: Tracking–Smoothness Trade-off (Table 5, Figures 9, 10, 11)**
- **Reward-Level Smoothness**: Removing reward-level smoothness penalties (NoSmooth baseline) improved tracking success but significantly increased action variation (Action Rate and Action Jerk). This highlighted the trade-off inherent in reward design.
- **Auxiliary Policy Losses**: CAPS and Grad-CAPS, which use auxiliary policy losses for smoothness, recovered different operating points. CAPS achieved the best Cartesian tracking errors but partially recovered smoothness. Grad-CAPS provided stronger smoothness recovery (Action Rate of 0.5627 and Action Jerk of 0.5117, close to SONIC-Base's 0.5447 and 0.4905, respectively) while still improving tracking over SONIC-Base.
- **Jitter Analysis**: Frequency-domain analysis (Figure 10) confirmed that NoSmooth introduced a clear high-frequency action component (jitter), which CAPS and Grad-CAPS successfully suppressed. Clip-level analysis (Figure 9) showed that NoSmooth's jitter increase was distribution-wide, while Grad-CAPS broadly suppressed high-frequency energy. Time-domain plots (Figure 11) qualitatively illustrated these differences.

#### 4.1. Ablations (Table 6)

- **Teacher Observation Design**: Using "Omni-privileged + motion-privileged + proprioception" for the teacher observation improved teacher acquisition and produced cleaner action labels for student distillation, leading to better held-out student performance despite the student having a fixed deployable observation space.

#### 4.2. Qualitative Results (Figures 4–6c)

- Qualitative case studies demonstrated that Athena-WBC rollouts maintained support and tracked reference motions more closely through critical transitions (e.g., crouch-and-walk, high-kick, single-leg poses, standing up, one-leg stretching), where SONIC-Base often failed during low-support or large-leg-motion phases.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Athena-WBC research contributes to the field of humanoid whole-body control by:

1. **Identifying a "Capability Bottleneck"**: The work explicitly identifies and provides evidence for the training-set long-tail failure as a capability bottleneck, distinct from mere insufficient data exposure. This suggests that for high-dynamic and balance-critical motions, the standard reinforcement learning acquisition recipe can induce a conservative control regime that limits a robot's physical capabilities.
2. **Proposing Capability-Aligned Experts**: Athena-WBC introduces a novel teacher-student pipeline that trains specialized dynamic and balance experts with tailored acquisition recipes. Dynamic experts remove conservative effort/temporal-control penalties from rewards and use auxiliary policy regularization for smoothness. Balance experts utilize a gravity curriculum to improve early-training survivability. This modular approach allows for specific interventions to overcome distinct long-tail challenges.
3. **Demonstrating Performance Improvement**: The pipeline, particularly after RL fine-tuning, shows improved recovery of training-set long-tail motions and better held-out tracking performance compared to a strong SONIC-recipe baseline on an 80kg humanoid robot. This indicates that modifying the learning objective and curriculum for specific motion types can lead to more robust and accurate control.
4. **Introducing Novel Evaluation Metrics**: The paper proposes and utilizes new diagnostics—Success–Tolerance Curves (STC), Threshold-Integrated Success (TIS), and Motion-Salience Weighted MPJPE (MPJPE-W)—for evaluating controllers in the high-coverage regime. These metrics offer a more nuanced understanding of policy performance by assessing robustness across tolerance levels and emphasizing motion-defining body parts, which is crucial when overall success rates are already high.
5. **Informing the Smoothness-Tracking Trade-off**: The analysis of smoothness-placement ablations provides insights into how different regularization strategies affect the trade-off between tracking performance and control smoothness, demonstrating that auxiliary policy losses can achieve a better balance than reward-level penalties alone.

The potential impact of this work includes enabling humanoid robots to execute a wider range of challenging and aggressive motions more reliably. This could be beneficial for applications requiring complex, human-like dexterity and agility, such as assistance robots, logistics, or entertainment. The conceptual distinction between physical feasibility and control conservatism, along with the proposed tailored expert training, offers a framework for designing more capable and robust controllers in the future. The introduced evaluation metrics also provide valuable tools for researchers to analyze and compare advanced WBC systems more effectively, moving beyond single-threshold performance indicators.

However, the work acknowledges limitations, including that long-tail coverage is not fully solved due to potential reference quality issues, the inherent complexity of the multi-stage pipeline, and the current scope of experiments under a relatively small compute budget. Future work involves addressing the coverage-generalization trade-off, mitigating low-frequency sway artifacts, simplifying the pipeline, and expanding real-robot validation.
