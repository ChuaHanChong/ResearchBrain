# Self-adapting Robotic Agents through Online Continual Reinforcement Learning with World Model Feedback

> [!summary] Summary
> An online continual reinforcement learning framework allows robotic agents to autonomously adapt to unforeseen changes in their environment or internal dynamics using a world model's predictive capabilities. The method enabled robots to recover from simulated actuator damage and adapt to real-world friction changes, achieving stable performance within minutes of real-world interaction.

## Problem

- Current learning-based robotic controllers are static and brittle, failing when deployed in dynamic environments with unforeseen changes or system degradations.
- Traditional control methods rely on first-principles modeling and manual design, limiting their applicability to complex, dynamic robotic systems.
- Existing continual reinforcement learning (CRL) methods often struggle with catastrophic forgetting or require separate models for distinct, pre-defined environmental contexts.

## Method

- The framework utilizes the ==DreamerV3== model-based RL algorithm, leveraging its ==world model== for continuous prediction and comparison with actual observations and rewards.
- ==Out-of-distribution (OOD)== events are automatically detected when ==prediction residuals== (==Observation Prediction Residual== and ==Reward Prediction Residual==) exceed a threshold, signaling a change in dynamics.
- Upon OOD detection, the system triggers ==online fine-tuning== of the world model and policy, selectively excluding pre-change data from the ==replay buffer== to focus on current dynamics.
- ==Unsupervised convergence assessment== is performed by monitoring task-level reward and internal learning metrics (dynamics loss, advantage magnitude, value loss) to autonomously determine when adaptation is complete.

## Results

- In the DMC Walker experiment, the agent rapidly adapted to simulated actuator damage within **10,000** steps (**2 minutes** simulation time), regaining stable locomotion.
- A simulated ANYmal quadruped adapted to actuator failure, stabilizing its walking cycle within **5,000** steps (**4 minutes**) and concluding fine-tuning by **26,000** steps.
- A real-world F1Tenth vehicle successfully adapted to both a significant sim-to-real transfer gap and subsequent friction reduction, achieving stable driving within **10,000** real-world steps (**8 minutes**) and optimizing its behavior to match simulation-level rewards by **50,000** steps.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The intrinsic predictive capabilities of a world model can effectively serve as a robust, unsupervised mechanism for detecting changes and OOD events in continuous control tasks.
> - Continual learning in open-set scenarios is achievable by prioritizing adaptation to current dynamics through selective replay buffer management, mitigating catastrophic forgetting for new contexts.
> - A holistic monitoring approach, combining task-level performance with internal world model and policy learning metrics, enables autonomous assessment of adaptation progress and convergence.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research directly addresses a fundamental limitation of contemporary learning-based robotic controllers: their static nature once deployed, which makes them brittle in the face of unforeseen environmental or system changes. It positions itself at the forefront of **Continual Reinforcement Learning (CRL)**, a rapidly evolving field aimed at enabling agents to learn and adapt throughout their operational lifetime, much like biological organisms.

The paper contrasts its approach with traditional control theory methods, such as fault detection, adaptive control, and fault-tolerant control (FTC). While these classical methods offer robustness and sample efficiency, they are constrained by their reliance on first-principles modeling and manual design, limiting their applicability to relatively simple, well-defined systems and environments.

Within the modern Reinforcement Learning (RL) landscape, the authors highlight the diverse and often inconsistent terminology surrounding CRL (e.g., lifelong learning, online learning, novelty adaptation). They situate their work in relation to existing CRL strategies, which typically fall into two categories:
1. **Multi-model or Mixture-of-Experts Approaches:** Earlier works (e.g., da Silva et al., Xu et al., Nagabandi et al.) often develop separate models for different environmental contexts. While these can circumvent "catastrophic forgetting" (the tendency to lose old skills when learning new ones), they require training new models from scratch for every new situation, leading to inefficiencies and scalability issues in open-ended environments.
2. **Single-Model Update Approaches:** Some works, like Nagabandi et al., attempt to update a single dynamics predictor.

This paper distinguishes itself by advancing the single-model update paradigm through the lens of **Model-Based Reinforcement Learning (MBRL)**, specifically leveraging the **DreamerV3** algorithm. Previous research, notably Kessler et al., had already demonstrated DreamerV3's effectiveness for CRL in terms of knowledge transfer and reduced catastrophic forgetting, but primarily in *simple, discrete, and a-priori known environments* with clear task boundaries.

The core contribution of Domberg and Schildbach's work to the broader landscape is pushing CRL beyond these limitations by:
- **Focusing on complex, continuous control problems:** Moving from discrete environments to more realistic robotic scenarios.
- **Addressing small, gradual, and arbitrary changes:** Unlike distinct task boundaries, real-world changes can be subtle and continuous.
- **Achieving fully automated, continuous control, open-set CRL:** This is a significant leap, as it eliminates the need for manual intervention or prior knowledge about potential changes, using the agent's internal world model to automatically detect out-of-distribution (OOD) events and trigger adaptation.

By doing so, the authors aim to bridge the gap between static, pre-programmed robotic behaviors and the dynamic, adaptive capabilities observed in biological intelligence, positioning their work as a foundational step toward truly autonomous and self-improving robotic agents.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to equip robotic agents with the fundamental biological capability of **continual learning throughout their lifetime**, enabling them to autonomously adapt to unforeseen changes during operation. This contrasts sharply with current learning-based controllers, which are typically trained offline, deployed with fixed parameters, and prone to failure when faced with deviations from their nominal training conditions.

The key motivations driving this work are deeply rooted in observations from biological intelligence:
- **Overcoming brittleness of static controllers:** Current robots often "break down" when encountering "out-of-distribution (OOD) events" – situations not encountered during their offline training. Robustness through randomization and larger datasets can only go so far; true autonomy requires online adaptation.
- **Mimicking biological learning mechanisms:** The authors draw inspiration from "violation-of-expectation" and "minimization-of-surprise" theories in neuroscience. These theories propose that higher intelligences use internal models to gauge familiarity with situations and initiate learning when predictions are violated. This internal model also facilitates planning by predicting action consequences. The motivation is to apply these biologically-inspired principles to artificial agents to enable self-reflection and self-improvement.

Based on these motivations, the authors set forth several key objectives:
- **Develop an Online Continual Reinforcement Learning Framework:** Create a system that allows robotic agents to learn and adapt autonomously during deployment.
- **Automated Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Event Detection:** Design a mechanism that leverages the prediction capabilities of a world model to identify when the environment or system dynamics have changed significantly from what was initially learned.
- **Automatic Fine-tuning Triggering:** Ensure that the detection of OOD events automatically initiates a fine-tuning process for the agent's world model and policy, without manual intervention.
- **Unsupervised Adaptation Progress Monitoring and Convergence Assessment:** Establish criteria and metrics, both task-level and internal to the learning algorithm, that allow the system to monitor its own adaptation progress and determine when learning has converged, thereby eliminating the need for external supervision or domain knowledge.
- **Validation Across Diverse Continuous Control Problems:** Demonstrate the framework's general applicability and effectiveness on a variety of challenging robotic tasks, ranging from established benchmarks to high-fidelity simulations and real-world robotic platforms.

In essence, the research aims to move robotic agents beyond fixed, pre-programmed behaviors towards truly adaptive systems capable of self-reflection and continuous self-improvement, replicating a crucial aspect of biological intelligence.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology builds upon a state-of-the-art Model-Based Reinforcement Learning (MBRL) algorithm, **DreamerV3**, to enable autonomous change detection and online adaptation. The approach is conceptually elegant, leveraging the intrinsic predictive capabilities of the world model for continuous self-assessment.

#### 3.1. A. Background: DreamerV3

The core of the methodology relies on DreamerV3, an MBRL algorithm known for its sample efficiency. DreamerV3 jointly learns two main components:
- **Latent World Model (Recurrent State-Space Model - RSSM):** This model learns to predict future states, values, and rewards by capturing the environment's dynamics in a compressed latent space. Given an observation $X_t$ and action $a_t$, an encoder maps $X_t$ to a stochastic latent state $z_t$, while a deterministic recurrent state $h_t$ summarizes past information. The model predicts future latent states ($\hat{z}_{t+1}$) and decodes them into predicted observations ($\hat{X}_t$) and rewards ($\hat{r}_t$).
- **Policy:** The policy is primarily trained on "imagined" interactions generated by the world model within its latent space. This significantly reduces the need for real-world environmental interactions, making the training process highly sample-efficient compared to model-free RL algorithms. The prediction horizon $n$ (set to 15 in this work) balances computational complexity, sample efficiency, and model error.

#### 3.2. B. Change Detection

The central idea for change detection is based on the assumption that a well-trained world model accurately represents its training data distribution. Any significant deviation in its prediction accuracy during deployment signals an **out-of-distribution (OOD) event**—a change in environment dynamics or robot capabilities that the model has not previously encountered. This is inspired by biological "violation-of-expectation" mechanisms.

The detection process involves:
1. **Continuous Prediction:** During policy inference, the world model continuously predicts the next $n$ states and rewards based on current observations and actions.
2. **Residual Calculation:** These predictions are then compared against the actual outcomes experienced by the robot. Two key metrics are computed:
 - **Observation Prediction Residual (OPR):** The average absolute difference between predicted and actual observation variables over the prediction horizon.
 $e^{obs}_{t,x} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n |\hat{x}_{t+i} - x_{t+i}|$ (averaged over all state variables).
 - **Reward Prediction Residual (RPR):** The average absolute difference between predicted and actual rewards over the prediction horizon.
 $e^{rew}_t = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n |\hat{r}_{t+i} - r_{t+i}|$.
3. **Threshold-based Detection:** Since OPR and RPR remain relatively stable for a converged world model in a nominal environment, a simple threshold-based check is used. Any value exceeding three standard deviations from a rolling mean of these metrics is classified as a change event. The combination of OPR and RPR is crucial: OPR reflects general model accuracy, while RPR specifically captures the impact on task performance (reward can be sparse or delayed).

#### 3.3. C. Automatic Adaptation

Once an OOD event is detected, the system automatically initiates a fine-tuning process.
1. **Online Fine-tuning:** DreamerV3's regular training loop is reactivated. The robot continues to operate, collecting new state-transitions and rewards. This new data is used to refit the world model, which in turn adapts the policy in its imaginary latent space.
2. **Replay Buffer Management:** Critically, transitions collected *prior* to the change event are excluded from the replay buffer used for fine-tuning. This decision reflects an "open-set" Continual Reinforcement Learning approach, prioritizing adaptation to the *current* dynamics over strict retention of potentially outdated prior knowledge, thereby mitigating catastrophic forgetting for the new context.
3. **Hyperparameter Consistency:** No alterations are made to DreamerV3's original hyperparameters (e.g., medium model size, train ratio of 16), showcasing the robustness of the core algorithm.

#### 3.4. D. Automatic Convergence Assessment

A significant challenge in online adaptation is knowing when adaptation is complete. The authors propose an unsupervised convergence assessment by monitoring multiple metrics, requiring that they reach desired, stable values without excessive fluctuations:
- **Task-level performance:** The observed **reward** signal.
- **World Model Internal Metrics:**
 - **Dynamics Loss:** Reflects how accurately the world model predicts state-space evolution. A decreasing and eventually stable value indicates a reliable internal representation.
- **Policy Internal Metrics:**
 - **Advantage Magnitude:** Quantifies the potential for policy improvement. High values suggest significant behavioral changes are still being identified, while stabilization at a low, non-zero level implies refinement.
 - **Value Loss:** Measures the accuracy of the model's long-term outcome estimations. Stable fluctuations around a constant level characterize convergence.

The interconnectedness of world model and policy learning is acknowledged, emphasizing the need for holistic monitoring. For instance, policy learning might saturate early if the world model cannot yet represent complex situations, or the world model might converge before the policy fully explores new state-space regions. By considering these metrics holistically, the system can autonomously determine when adaptation is deemed successful and the fine-tuning phase can conclude.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research validated the proposed framework across three distinct continuous control problems, showcasing its general applicability from established benchmarks to real-world scenarios.

#### 4.1. A. DMC Walker Experiment (Actuator Damage)

- **Setup:** A 2D stick-figure humanoid (Walker-walk problem from DeepMind Control Suite). At 5,000 steps, a random joint's gear ratio was halved, simulating actuator damage.
- **Observation:** Immediately after the damage, the robot's reward quickly dropped, and the Reward Prediction Residual (RPR) rose significantly as the Walker struggled to maintain balance.
- **Adaptation:** The method rapidly detected the change and initiated fine-tuning. Within approximately 10,000 steps (2 minutes of simulation time), most metrics, including reward and internal learning signals, asymptotically moved towards their pre-modification values. The Walker regained stable locomotion, albeit with slightly lower average reward due to occasional stumbles. The Observation Prediction Residual (OPR) showed continued variability, attributed to the random nature and varying impact of the joint modifications.
- **Conclusion:** Demonstrated quick detection and recovery from an internal system fault in a simulated continuous control task.

#### 4.2. B. Quadruped Robot ANYmal (Simulated Actuator Failure)

- **Setup:** NVIDIA Isaac Lab simulation with an ANYmal quadruped robot, initially trained to walk at a commanded pace. At 9,000 steps, the velocity limits of three actuators in its right hind leg were reduced to one-third, simulating actuator failure.
- **Observation:** The robot's smooth motion was disrupted, leading to repeated tripping and occasional falls, resulting in a significant drop in reward.
- **Adaptation:** The framework quickly identified the change. On average, within 5,000 steps (4 minutes), the robot's walking cycle stabilized. Full fine-tuning for all runs concluded by 26,000 steps. Internal metrics showed elevated advantage magnitude (indicating increased behavioral exploration) initially, followed by a decrease, and dynamics loss even dropping below its prior level. Value loss remained elevated during fine-tuning before trending downwards.
- **Failed Run:** The inclusion of a "failed run," where metrics never stabilized, effectively illustrated the distinct convergence patterns that enable the automatic evaluation mechanism to differentiate successful adaptation from ongoing learning or failure.
- **Conclusion:** Proved applicability to a more complex, industry-grade robotic platform in high-fidelity simulation, showcasing robust adaptation to a severe actuator failure.

#### 4.3. C. Real-World F1Tenth Model Vehicle (Sim-to-Real Transfer and Friction Change)

- **Setup 1: Sim-to-Real Transfer:** A 1:10 scale F1Tenth model car. The world model and policy were initially trained in simulation for 10 million steps. At 10,000 steps, the trained policy was transferred to the physical car, creating a significant sim-to-real gap.
- **Observation 1:** Upon transfer, the OPR surged immediately, and the reward dropped. The vehicle exhibited jerky driving, frequently approached walls, and crashed.
- **Adaptation 1:** The method recognized the abnormality and initiated fine-tuning. Within approximately 10,000 real-world steps (8 minutes wall-clock time), the car's behavior stabilized. Learning continued, with the vehicle optimizing its driving (e.g., increasing speed, tightening corners). By 50,000 steps, the reward reached simulation-like levels, and the algorithm automatically terminated fine-tuning as metrics converged. Any remaining differences in OPR and dynamics loss were attributed to unmodeled discrepancies between simulation and reality.
- **Setup 2: Friction Reduction:** Immediately after the first adaptation phase, at around 52,000 steps, socks were placed on the vehicle's rear wheels, reducing friction.
- **Observation 2:** Reward dropped by about 20% due to occasional slipping and spinning during cornering. The overall OPR effect was low, but individual state variable logs (e.g., angular velocity prediction) showed significant peaks, suggesting localized prediction errors masked by averaging.
- **Adaptation 2:** Losses quickly returned to pre-socks levels as the policy adapted to drive slightly slower to avoid slipping, resulting in a slightly lower but stable reward.
- **Conclusion:** Demonstrated robust adaptation to both a substantial sim-to-real domain shift and a physical change in system dynamics (friction), validating the method's effectiveness in a real-world, open-ended operational setting.

Across all experiments, the framework consistently demonstrated its ability to automatically detect changes, trigger fine-tuning, and assess convergence using a combination of task-level and internal learning metrics, without requiring manual intervention or prior knowledge of the specific changes.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a highly significant advancement in the field of robotic autonomy and Reinforcement Learning, with profound potential impact across various domains.

#### 5.1. A. Bridging the Gap to Biologically-Inspired Autonomy

The most significant contribution lies in enabling robotic agents to move beyond static, pre-programmed behaviors towards **self-adapting systems capable of continuous learning and self-improvement**, drawing direct inspiration from biological intelligence. By applying neuroscientific theories of "violation-of-expectation" and "minimization-of-surprise" to artificial agents, the work pushes the boundaries of how robots can perceive and react to novel situations, fostering a closer resemblance to natural intelligence. This means robots could autonomously learn from experiences and recover from unexpected faults or environmental changes, rather than merely executing predefined tasks.

#### 5.2. B. Advancing Continual Reinforcement Learning (CRL)

The paper makes a crucial step in CRL by demonstrating a **fully-automated, continuous control, open-set CRL method**. Unlike previous CRL works that often focused on discrete tasks or pre-defined changes, this framework can detect and adapt to arbitrary, potentially gradual, and unforeseen changes in complex, continuous environments. The use of DreamerV3's world model prediction residuals for automatic OOD detection and fine-tuning is a novel and effective mechanism, liberating the adaptation process from external human supervision. The selective exclusion of pre-change data from the replay buffer represents a deliberate strategy for open-ended adaptation, prioritizing current relevance over potentially outdated knowledge.

#### 5.3. C. Enhanced Robustness and Real-World Applicability

The validation across diverse robotic platforms – from a simulated humanoid (DMC Walker), to a complex quadruped (ANYmal) in high-fidelity simulation, and crucially, to a real-world model vehicle (F1Tenth) – underscores the method's practical utility. The successful adaptation to actuator degradation, significant sim-to-real transfer discrepancies, and physical parameter changes (e.g., tire friction) demonstrates a substantial improvement in robotic robustness. This implies that future robots could be deployed in more dynamic and unpredictable environments, such as logistics, disaster response, or planetary exploration, where manual re-programming or external intervention is impractical or impossible.

#### 5.4. D. Foundation for Self-Reflecting and Self-Improving Robots

The ability to monitor adaptation progress using a combination of task-level performance (reward) and internal learning metrics (dynamics loss, advantage magnitude, value loss) for unsupervised convergence assessment is a critical enabler for truly autonomous systems. This self-reflection capability allows the robot to gauge its own learning state and determine when it has successfully adapted, making it a powerful tool for developing intelligent agents that can continuously optimize their behavior throughout their operational life.

#### 5.5. E. Future Trajectories and Impact

The authors envision several high-impact future directions:
- **Scalability to Foundation Models:** The approach could enable sample-efficient "on-the-job finetuning" for larger, generalist multi-task foundation models in RL, allowing these powerful models to adapt to specific real-world deployments with minimal additional data.
- **Adaptive Reward Functions:** Extending the framework to adapt to changes in reward functions could enable efficient re-skilling of agents within the same environment for new objectives.
- **Safety Critical Applications:** Addressing safety concerns during unconstrained exploration is paramount for real-world deployment. Integrating the method with Safe RL techniques and Model Predictive Control could drastically reduce the need for risky "mistakes" during learning, making these adaptive systems viable for safety-critical environments.
- **Optimized Adaptation Speed:** Investigating the effects of incorporating changes during initial training or scaling model size and training ratios could lead to even faster adaptation.

While challenges remain, particularly concerning safety during adaptation, efficiency under extreme distribution shifts, and the long-term retention of all prior skills (a trade-off deliberately made in this work), the presented framework represents a seminal step towards a new generation of robotic agents that are not merely pre-programmed tools, but rather autonomous entities capable of lifelong learning, adaptation, and continuous self-improvement in an ever-changing world.
