# Behavior-Aligned Action Tokenization for Robot Policy Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> BAAT is an action tokenizer for autoregressive robot policies. Soft dynamic time warping picks corresponding action chunks within a mini-batch, and an alignment loss pulls their finite-scalar-quantized coordinates together while joint reconstruction preserves executable detail. A history-conditioned diffusion decoder rebuilds continuous chunks. BAAT averages about 45.2% simulation success across three benchmarks, roughly 7.2 points above OAT.

## Problem

- Existing tokenizers supervise only chunk reconstruction, so nothing specifies which motions across demonstrations should occupy nearby quantized representations for reuse.
- Behavioral correspondence across demonstrations gets little explicit supervision, so similar motions performed at different speeds or phases lack a shared discrete representation.
- Prior alignment signals rely on video, task labels, or temporal adjacency of chunks, rather than action-only correspondence acting on the policy's actual discrete targets.

## Method

- ==Behavioral alignment loss==: GPU-batched ==Soft-DTW== over normalized motion-increment sequences selects the lowest-cost chunk pairs in a mini-batch, then penalizes squared distance between their ==FSQ== coordinates.
- **History-conditioned diffusion decoder** predicts the clean action chunk from quantized coordinates plus executed action history injected via ==FiLM==, trained with masked ==Smooth-L1== reconstruction alongside alignment.
- ==Factorized autoregressive policy==: the frozen tokenizer supplies offline categorical targets over 16 latent slots, each with four FSQ factors predicted in parallel by per-factor heads under ==teacher forcing==.

## Results

- Mean joint-training success **45.2%** versus **38.0%** for OAT, **34.1%** Diffusion Policy, **24.4%** FAST; LIBERO-All **79.0%**, RoboCasa **29.2%**, RoboTwin 2.0 **27.3%**.
- Alignment weight **0.1** raises LIBERO-All policy success from **70.2%** to **79.0%** while replay success drops **80.5%** to **71.5%**; weight **0.5** collapses both to **0.0%**.
- Soft-DTW beats pointwise L2 matching by **6.0** policy and **6.9** replay points; decoder history cuts boundary-jump p95 by **49.6%** and arm MAE by **43.9%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Reconstruction fidelity and downstream control can trade off: the tokenizer with worse replay success gave the better policy, so reconstruction metrics mispick interfaces.
> - Correspondence supervision pays off mainly under multi-task joint training; per-suite training left BAAT below baselines, especially on long-horizon tasks.
> - Alignment strength needs moderation - too much crushes the executable detail the decoder needs, degrading both fidelity and control to total failure.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

Autoregressive robot policies predict discrete labels for chunks of continuous movement, so the way a tokenizer organizes those labels affects what the policy must learn. Different tasks can reuse local motions, such as reaching forward, lifting, or transferring sideways, even when those motions occur at different speeds or pauses. Reconstruction alone can preserve each chunk without specifying which chunks from different tasks should be represented similarly.

BAAT addresses this in two steps. It first finds motions that match when their timing is allowed to stretch or compress. It then encourages their representations to be close while still training a decoder to reconstruct the actions. The intent is to bring similar motions into nearby representations. It does not make their codes identical or claim that the full tasks share semantics.

The retrieval does not isolate alignment as the cause of the neighborhoods or establish independently callable skills.

### 2. Matching motions, not task labels

BAAT decides which chunks should be represented similarly by comparing their motion. Each action chunk is represented as normalized motion increments in a platform-specific matching space.

Chunks are then compared with Soft-DTW, a smoothed dynamic-time-warping distance that permits monotone temporal alignment. BAAT uses it to choose the lowest-scoring chunk pairs within a minibatch. These pairs are weak behavioral correspondences, not task-semantic labels.

Chunks are encoded and quantized with FSQ, which discretizes scalar coordinates into fixed levels. Alignment applies to the quantized scalar coordinates of selected pairs, encouraging closeness but not identical categorical codes. This alignment is trained jointly with an action-reconstruction objective, so the representation is encouraged to retain executable detail as it organizes corresponding motions.

The trained tokenizer and decoder are then frozen, and expert chunks are tokenized offline. An autoregressive policy learns to predict their categorical labels from observations and optional task input. At deployment, predicted labels map back to quantized coordinates. A history-aware diffusion decoder then reconstructs continuous action chunks using executed action history.

### 3. A policy gain with a fidelity trade-off

The paper measures two kinds of success:

- **Policy success** is closed-loop task success.
- **Replay success** is success when actions reconstructed from demonstration-encoded tokens are executed.

The strongest evidence comes from a controlled LIBERO-All ablation. On LIBERO-All, changing only the alignment weight from zero to 0.1 raises policy success from 70.2% to 79.0%. Over the same change, replay success falls from 80.5% to 71.5%. The measured result is therefore a policy-success gain alongside a replay-fidelity cost, not an across-the-board improvement in action reconstruction.

Higher alignment is not uniformly better. At weight 0.3, policy success is 57.9%. At 0.5, both replay and policy success are 0.0%. The authors say this is consistent with stronger alignment conflicting with executable action detail, but the failures do not diagnose representation collapse.

Alignment weights $\lambda_{\text{align}}$ of 0, 0.05, 0.1 (BAAT), 0.3, and 0.5 on LIBERO-All. At 0, reconstruction MSE, replay SR, and policy SR are $4.672 \times 10^{-4}$, 80.5%, and 70.2%. At 0.1 they are $5.773 \times 10^{-4}$, 71.5%, and 79.0%. At 0.5, replay and policy SR are both 0.0%.

A second check replaces Soft-DTW with pointwise L2 matching. On LIBERO-All, Soft-DTW reaches 79.0% policy success and 71.5% replay success, versus 73.0% and 64.6% for pointwise L2. This is consistent with timing-tolerant matching helping, but the experiment does not directly measure pair quality. Replay success with Soft-DTW also remains below the 80.5% no-alignment reference.

### 4. What the results establish

Under joint training, mean simulation policy success is 45.2% for BAAT and 38.0% for OAT, a 7.2-percentage-point difference. The mean is an unweighted average across LIBERO-All, RoboCasa, and RoboTwin 2.0, with three seeds and 50 final-checkpoint rollouts per task in each run. Joint training also changes demonstration volume and diversity, so this comparison alone does not isolate alignment.

BAAT makes behavioral correspondence an explicit source of tokenizer supervision. In the controlled ablation, moderate alignment improves policy success despite a replay-success decrease. The evaluations cover selected tasks from three simulation benchmarks and two real-robot tasks, not general-purpose VLA pretraining or broad out-of-distribution generalization.
