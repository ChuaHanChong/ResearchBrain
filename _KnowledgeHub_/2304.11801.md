# Efficient Robot Skill Learning with Imitation from a Single Video for Contact-Rich Fabric Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> A one-shot imitation-from-video approach for contact-rich fabric manipulation. Prior knowledge (keypoint state representation, forward/inverse dynamics, constrained action space) is learned from random exploration in a contact-free simulation; a single human demonstration video yields a state alignment reward via IoU progress estimation and learned registration; sampling-based MPC then executes safely, reaching 81.9% and 72.7% simulated success rates.

## Problem

- Standard imitation learning needs state-action pairs from kinesthetic teaching or teleoperation, requiring operator training and large datasets impractical for high-dimensional deformable-object tasks.
- Existing one-shot methods either fine-tune with real-robot RL, unsafe and sample-hungry, or need highly realistic simulators plus sim-to-real tricks, or assume task-specific priors.
- Fabric manipulation work targets folding, flattening, or shaping in structured scenes; contact-rich fabric-object tasks rely on customized primitives, strong initial-configuration assumptions, or simulation-only validation.

## Method

- Prior knowledge module: ==FCN keypoint representation==, ==MLP forward/inverse dynamics==, and a constrained action space (gripper separation thresholds, action-edge angle limit, workspace, collision), trained on 10,000 random simulation transitions.
- ==State alignment reward==: ==IoU== (or center distance) temporal-progress estimation picks a subgoal frame, a learned registration network scores positive rotation-plus-translation distance, and a negative term penalizes overshooting the demonstration's final state.
- **State Alignment-Driven Sampling MPC**: an inverse-dynamics prior action sequence seeds ==Gaussian sampling==, the cost function filters infeasible candidates, elite samples update the noise distribution, and an IoU-based termination classifier ends episodes.

## Results

- In simulation the method reaches **81.9%** success covering a box and **72.7%** hanging, versus **6.6%** and **2.4%** for the strongest baseline TCN; Zero and CFM score below **2%**.
- Ablations: removing MPC drops box success to **0.0%**, removing the action prior to **14.4%**, and removing the cost constraint to **3.0%**, confirming each component is necessary.
- Real dual-Panda experiments: **12/15**, **12/15**, **14/15** success under object translation and **7/10**, **8/10**, **9/10** under arbitrary pose change, each episode taking 20-40 seconds.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Chamfer distance to the goal can look good while the symbolic object-object relation fails; pick metrics that test task semantics, not geometry.
> - Priors that transfer are scene-independent ones (representation, dynamics, safety constraints), so a crude simulator suffices and no sim-to-real realism is needed.
> - Seeding a sampling-based planner with an inverse-dynamics guess shrinks the search space enough for real-time bimanual control; unseeded sampling rarely finds feasible actions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper addresses challenges in robot skill learning, particularly the efficiency and safety of acquiring complex manipulation abilities. Classical policy search algorithms and reinforcement learning (RL) methods typically necessitate extensive exploration of the environment. This exploration is often time-consuming and expensive to conduct on real physical platforms, and for complex or unstructured environments, it can pose safety risks to the robot and its surroundings.

Imitation learning (IL) has emerged as a strategy to mitigate the exploration problem by allowing robots to learn from demonstrations. However, many conventional IL approaches, such as behavior cloning (BC) or inverse reinforcement learning (IRL), require large databases of state-action pairs. Collecting such comprehensive datasets can be problematic for high-dimensional visuomotor policies or complex manipulation tasks. Furthermore, methods that still rely on additional real-world explorations for fine-tuning often face the same sample efficiency and safety concerns as direct RL.

To simplify the demonstration process, imitation from observation (IfO) has gained attention, where only observation sequences are provided. This removes the need for detailed proprioceptive action data, which can be difficult to collect (e.g., through kinesthetic teaching or teleoperation). However, IfO introduces the challenge of inferring actions from observations alone and often still requires numerous demonstrations to ensure generalizability.

This paper positions itself within the subfield of "one-shot imitation learning," aiming to enable robots to learn from a single demonstration and generalize to new situations. Previous one-shot methods often integrate with RL for robustness, but these frequently require high-fidelity simulations to bridge the sim-to-real gap, or they rely on strong task-specific assumptions (e.g., known parameters or proximity to a goal pose). Meta-learning approaches for one-shot learning can also demand extensive diverse demonstrations to learn a general policy.

The authors specifically target "contact-rich fabric manipulation," a domain that presents significant difficulties due to the deformable nature of fabrics (infinite degrees of freedom), bimanual manipulation requirements, and the necessity of establishing symbolic relationships with rigid objects. These characteristics make it difficult for conventional end-to-end imitation learning due to data hunger and the prohibition of random explorations in contact-rich environments. Existing methods for deformable object manipulation often focus on shaping tasks without complex object interactions or make strong assumptions about initial configurations. Sim-to-real RL for fabric manipulation also typically requires substantial simulation verisimilitude.

This work contributes to the landscape by proposing a modular approach that aims to overcome the need for extensive real-world data collection and exploration, explicit sim-to-real transfer mechanisms for specific tasks, or highly realistic simulations. It seeks to enable efficient learning and immediate deployment of robot skills for *novel scenes* within a task family by combining general prior knowledge learned in a simplified simulation with a single observational demonstration, thereby advancing efficient and safe robot skill acquisition.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an efficient one-shot imitation-from-video strategy that allows robots to learn and execute complex manipulation skills, particularly in contact-rich fabric scenarios, with minimal real-world interaction and no risky explorations.

The motivation for this objective stems from several limitations observed in current robot skill learning paradigms:
- **High Cost and Time Consumption of Exploration:** Classical policy search algorithms and many RL approaches require extensive trial-and-error exploration. Implementing this on real robots is time-consuming, expensive, and resource-intensive due to the need for human supervision and manual resets.
- **Safety Concerns in Real-World Exploration:** Random explorations in unstructured or contact-rich environments can lead to dangerous states, potential collisions, and damage to the robot or its surroundings, making them impractical for real-world deployment.
- **Data Scarcity for Complex Tasks:** Learning high-dimensional visuomotor policies for tasks like fabric manipulation demands vast amounts of data. Acquiring large datasets of state-action tuples is challenging for complex, unstructured manipulation tasks.
- **Limitations of Existing Imitation Learning:** While imitation learning reduces exploration, many methods still require large demonstration databases. One-shot imitation methods are data-scarce and often depend on strong assumptions or require significant sim-to-real tuning.
- **Inefficiencies of Sim-to-Real RL:** Current sim-to-real RL approaches often necessitate creating a simulation environment that precisely mirrors the real task and additional techniques to bridge the reality gap, which can be complex and task-specific. This paper aims for generalization to *novel scenes* not present in simulation, rather than just transferring a policy for a known task.
- **Specific Challenges of Contact-Rich Fabric Manipulation:** This domain is critical in various industries and households but is difficult for robots due to the infinite degrees of freedom of fabrics, the need for bimanual coordination, and the complexities of establishing stable symbolic relationships with rigid objects. These factors highlight the need for robust generalization and high-level planning beyond simple trajectory following.

To address these motivations, the authors set out to achieve the following specific objectives:
1. **Formulate Efficient Robot Skill Learning:** Define the problem as adapting from general prior knowledge of a task *family* (learned in a simulated environment) to a *new scene* within that family, guided by a single demonstration.
2. **Develop State Alignment-Based Reward Function:** Design a mechanism to extract temporal abstraction from a single demonstration video, serving as a dense reward signal for imitation without requiring precise state-action pairs.
3. **Integrate Sampling-Based Model Predictive Control (MPC):** Utilize an MPC framework to generate promising and safe motions in real-time imitation, explicitly incorporating systematic safety constraints and leveraging the learned prior knowledge.
4. **Conduct Comprehensive Experimental Evaluation:** Provide detailed numerical simulations and real-world hardware experiments to validate the effectiveness, efficiency, and robustness of the proposed approach, particularly in the challenging context of contact-rich fabric manipulation.

By achieving these objectives, the research aims to enable robots to deploy novel tasks immediately after observing a single demonstration, without extensive and risky physical explorations.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed approach, named "efficient robot skill learning," is structured into three main modules: Prior Knowledge Learning, Demonstration Learning, and Model Predictive Control. The overall idea is to learn generalizable priors in a simplified simulation scene (Q1) of a task family (Q) and then adapt this knowledge to a novel scene (Q2) within the same family using a single demonstration video.

**A. Prior Knowledge Learning**
This module aims to equip robots with fundamental knowledge that is generalizable across different scenes of a task family. Data collection for this phase is performed in a *contact-free simulation scenario* (e.g., Fig. 3a) to avoid the complexities and costs of real-world data collection and detailed fabric-rigid object interaction modeling.
- **Data Acquisition:** 10,000 transitions (observation, state, action, next observation, next state) are generated by sampling random actions within a defined valid action space.
- **State Representation ($f_R$):** The raw observation, a masked RGB image of the fabric, is transformed into a customized M-keypoint representation. A Fully Convolutional Network (FCN) is trained to output 2D pixel coordinates of these keypoints, which are then converted to 3D by querying depth values (Fig. 3c). This provides a semantic, compact representation of the fabric's configuration.
- **Dynamic Models:** Two fully-connected neural networks are trained based on the predicted keypoints:
 - **Forward Dynamics ($f_D$):** Predicts the next state $S^{[t+1]}$ given the current state $S^{[t]}$ and action $A^{[t]}$ ($S^{[t+1]} = f_D(S^{[t]}, A^{[t]})$).
 - **Inverse Dynamics ($f_I$):** Predicts the action $A^{[t]}$ required to transition from state $S^{[t]}$ to a target state $S^{[t+1]}$ ($A^{[t]} = f_I(S^{[t]}, S^{[t+1]})$).
 Both models are trained using Mean Squared Error (MSE).
- **Constrained Action Space:** A set of rules defines a safe and feasible action space $A_V^{[t]}$ for dual-arm manipulation, which is critical for contact-rich tasks:
 - Maintaining an appropriate distance between grippers to avoid over-stretching or severe self-occlusion (Eq. 5).
 - Limiting the angular difference between individual actions and their corresponding fabric edge vectors (Eq. 6).
 - Ensuring the fabric remains within the workspace.
 - Preventing gripper collisions with rigid objects.

**B. Demonstration Learning**
This module focuses on extracting temporal abstraction and defining a reward function from a single *observation-only* demonstration video for a novel task scene.
- **State Extraction:** The learned representation model $f_R$ is used to extract the keypoint states $\{S_E^{[j]}\}_{j=1}^T$ from the T-length demonstration video $\{O_E^{[j]}\}_{j=1}^T$.
- **Rigid Object-Associated Coordinate Frame:** All states are represented in a coordinate frame tied to the pose of the rigid object in the scene. This makes the imitation platform-agnostic and simplifies generalization across different robot setups.
- **State Alignment-Based Reward Function ($R_E$):** The reward function is designed to guide the robot to follow the state sequence of the demonstration. It considers two main aspects:
 - **Temporal Progress Estimation:** Measures the similarity between the current robot state $S^{[t]}$ and the demonstration states $\{S_E^{[j]}\}$ using Intersection over Union (IoU) of the fabric masks. If no overlap, Euclidean distance between object centers is used. The index $J$ of the most similar demonstration state is identified (Eq. 7, 8).
 - **Spatial Distance to Goal:**
 - **Positive Reward Component:** Quantifies the distance to the *next subgoal* $S_E^{[J+1]}$ in the demonstration. A neural network $f_A$ (Fig. 3d) is trained to register the current state $S^{[t]}$ with the subgoal $S_E^{[J+1]}$ and output their relative rotation (R) and translation (T). The positive distance $D_P$ integrates these values (Eq. 9, 10).
 - **Negative Reward Component:** Penalizes the robot if its current state passes beyond the *final state* $S_E^{[T]}$ of the demonstration ($D_N$) (Eq. 11), preventing "overshooting" or manipulating in the wrong direction.
 The overall reward $R_E$ combines the temporal progress estimate with weighted positive and negative distance terms (Eq. 12).

**C. Model Predictive Control (MPC)**
The MPC module generates real-time, safe, and efficient control policies for the novel task by integrating the learned priors and the state alignment-based reward function. The problem is formulated as a constrained optimization (Eq. 4).
- **Prior Action Sequence Generation ($\hat{X}$):** To improve the efficiency of the sampling-based MPC, a baseline h-length action sequence $\hat{X}$ is generated at each timestep. This is done by iteratively using the inverse dynamics model $f_I$ to estimate the action needed to reach the next subgoal $S_E^{[J+1]}$ (obtained from the temporal progress estimation) and then using the forward dynamics $f_D$ to predict the resulting state for the next iteration (Eq. 13, Fig. 4b).
- **Sampling-Based Optimization:**
 - A set of $N$ candidate action sequences $X$ is generated by adding noise (sampled from a multivariate Gaussian distribution $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \Sigma)$) to the prior action sequence $\hat{X}$.
 - For each candidate $X$, the accumulated reward $R(X;S^{[t]})$ and accumulated cost $C(X;S^{[t]})$ over a horizon $h$ are evaluated (Eq. 14), using the forward dynamics $f_D$ to predict future states and the defined cost function (Eq. 2) to evaluate safety.
 - **Constraint Handling:** Only "feasible" solutions (those with zero accumulated cost, meaning no constraint violations) are selected.
 - **Parameter Update:** The mean ($\mu$) and covariance ($\Sigma$) of the noise distribution are updated iteratively based on the top-performing feasible solutions (Eq. 15), narrowing the search space towards better policies. The iteration stops when a feasible solution is found and the variance converges (Eq. 16).
- **Receding Horizon Control:** Only the first action from the optimal sequence is applied to the robot, and the process is repeated at the next timestep.
- **Termination Classifier:** An episode is terminated when: (1) no feasible action is found; (2) maximum steps are exceeded; or (3) the task is deemed complete. Task completion is classified when the IoU between the current state and the final demonstration state $S_E^{[T]}$ exceeds a threshold *and* the temporal progress estimation matches $S_E^{[T]}$.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates its approach through numerical simulations and real-world hardware experiments, focusing on contact-rich fabric manipulation tasks.

**A. Simulation Setting and Prior Model Performance**
- **Environment:** Simulations were conducted using DeDo, modeling fabric as a 256-vertex mesh and robot grippers as virtual anchors. Prior knowledge was learned in a contact-free simulation environment.
- **Prior Model Training:** The state representation, dynamic models (forward and inverse), and the registration model for demonstration learning exhibited low training and validation errors, indicating effective learning of these foundational components (Fig. 6).
- **Qualitative Results:** Examples from the "covering a box" (Box) and "hanging a towel" (Hang) tasks (Fig. 7) show the robot successfully manipulating fabric to align with demonstration states, interacting with rigid objects, and achieving desired final configurations (e.g., lifting fabric to avoid collisions, then releasing).

**B. Comparisons with Baselines**
The proposed method was quantitatively compared against three baselines: Zero, CFM, and TCN. All methods shared the same simulated prior dataset and single demonstration video for novel tasks.
- **Success Rate (Table I):** The proposed method demonstrated a significantly higher success rate in both tasks: 81.9% for "Box" and 72.7% for "Hang." In contrast, the baselines achieved much lower success rates (Zero: 1.8%/0.0%, CFM: 0.0%/1.4%, TCN: 6.6%/2.4%). This indicates the method's effectiveness in achieving the symbolic object-object relationships necessary for task completion.
- **Chamfer Distance (Fig. 8a,b):** While the proposed method achieved high success rates, its Chamfer distance error (a metric for geometric similarity) was comparable to or sometimes slightly higher than TCN and Zero for specific time steps. The authors interpret this as a consequence of their constraint-aware approach, which prioritizes safe, step-by-step alignment towards the desired symbolic relationship rather than merely minimizing the direct geometric distance to the final goal, which other methods might optimize locally.
- **Analysis of Baselines:**
 - CFM's poor performance was attributed to its lack of explicit consideration for spatial and temporal connections between states in complex sequential tasks.
 - TCN struggled with data scarcity from a single demonstration to accurately encode temporal distances.
 - Zero tended to get trapped in local distance minima without sufficient long-term prediction.

**C. Ablation Study**
An ablation study was conducted to assess the necessity of key components:
- **No MPC:** Using only inverse dynamics as a controller resulted in 0% success for "Box" and 3.0% for "Hang" (Table I), showing a strong decrease in accumulated reward (Fig. 8c,d). This highlights the importance of predictive control for long-term impacts.
- **No Prior:** Removing the prior action sequence, meaning the MPC iterated from scratch, led to 14.4% success for "Box" and 7.4% for "Hang." This indicated that the large search space prevented the algorithm from finding solutions efficiently within a finite iteration period.
- **No Cost:** Removing the cost constraint function in MPC led to 3.0% success for "Box" and 2.2% for "Hang." This resulted in constraint violations and premature episode termination, demonstrating the critical role of safety constraints.
These results confirmed that all three components (predictive control, prior action sequence, and cost constraints) were integral to the method's performance.

**D. Failure Cases in Simulation**
Two typical failure modes were identified:
- **Complex Interactions:** The controller sometimes struggled with complex interactions between the fabric and rigid objects, which were not explicitly considered in the prior dynamics model. This could lead to the fabric passing the rigid object, failing to meet termination conditions, and incurring penalization (Fig. 9a).
- **Computational Efficiency:** In scenarios where the fabric was close to the rigid object, the action space became more restricted, making it challenging for the sampling-based MPC to find a feasible solution within the allocated real-time control period (Fig. 9b).

**E. Physical Experiments**
The method was validated on real-world robot hardware (two Franka Emika Panda arms with an RGB-D camera) for three contact-rich fabric manipulation tasks: "Box-Small," "Hang," and "Box-Big" (Table II, Fig. 11). Human hands provided single demonstration videos.
- **Task Variations:** Tasks included variations in fabric size and rigid object dimensions, testing robustness against deformable object changes.
- **Difficulty Levels:** Experiments included "Translation" (changing object position) and "Transformation" (changing object pose arbitrarily).
- **Success Rates (Table II):** The system achieved high success rates:
 - **Translation:** 12/15 for Box-Small, 12/15 for Hang, 14/15 for Box-Big.
 - **Transformation:** 7/10 for Box-Small, 8/10 for Hang, 9/10 for Box-Big.
 These quantitative results support the efficiency and robustness of the approach in physical scenarios, enabling immediate task execution without additional real-world explorations.
- **Observations from Real-world Experiments:**
 - Success rates decreased when rotation (Transformation) was involved. This was attributed to kinematic feasibility limits of the robot arms, which constrained manipulation space and made alignment more difficult than in simulation where virtual anchors were used.
 - Occlusion during grasping sometimes reduced keypoint detection accuracy by the $f_R$ model, suggesting potential improvements by incorporating temporal information for object tracking.
- **Real-world Failure Cases:** Similar to simulation, one failure mode involved the fabric being manipulated past the rigid object due to the sim-to-real gap and the dynamics models not fully accounting for complex interactions (Fig. 12a). Another failure mode involved the state representation model struggling to detect keypoints accurately due to self-occlusion (Fig. 12b).

In summary, the main findings indicate that the proposed three-module approach successfully enables efficient one-shot robot skill learning for contact-rich fabric manipulation. It achieves high success rates in both simulated and real-world scenarios, outperforming baselines and demonstrating the necessity of its component modules, particularly in handling the symbolic nature and safety constraints of the tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of robot skill learning, particularly for complex and contact-rich manipulation tasks, by addressing several long-standing challenges:

- **Enhanced Efficiency and Reduced Exploration:** The primary impact is enabling efficient robot skill acquisition. By learning general prior knowledge in a simplified simulated environment and adapting to novel tasks with a *single observational demonstration*, the method substantially reduces the need for extensive, time-consuming, and costly real-world robot explorations. This makes robot learning more practical and accessible.
- **Improved Safety in Robot Learning:** Eliminating random explorations in physical environments mitigates the inherent safety risks associated with trial-and-error learning, especially for delicate or safety-critical applications. The integration of systematic safety constraints within the MPC framework further enhances safe operation.
- **Generalization to Novel Scenes:** The approach's ability to generalize from prior learning in one scene to *novel scenes* within a task family (e.g., different rigid objects or configurations) using only a single video is a key advancement. This contrasts with methods requiring highly specific sim-to-real tuning or large diverse datasets for meta-learning.
- **Overcoming Sim-to-Real Challenges:** By not assuming exact dynamic consistency between training (simulated contact-free) and evaluation (real-world contact-rich), the method offers a practical way to leverage simulation without needing highly verisimilar environments or complex sim-to-real transfer techniques for every new task variant.
- **Advancing Deformable Object Manipulation:** The validation in contact-rich fabric manipulation, a historically challenging domain due to high degrees of freedom and complex interactions, demonstrates the robustness and applicability of the method to a critical area of robotics. This could lead to more capable robots in domestic tasks (e.g., laundry, bed-making) and industrial applications (e.g., manufacturing involving textiles).
- **Human-like Learning Paradigm:** The one-shot imitation from video aligns with how humans often learn new skills, by observing a single demonstration rather than extensive trial-and-error or numerous precise examples. This abstraction offers a promising direction for intuitive robot programming.
- **Modular and Adaptable Framework:** The modular design, separating prior knowledge learning, demonstration interpretation, and constrained policy execution, offers a flexible framework that could be adapted to other complex manipulation domains beyond fabric, provided suitable state representations and dynamic models can be learned.

In the long term, this work could contribute to more autonomous and versatile robots that can rapidly acquire and adapt skills in dynamic, unstructured environments with minimal human intervention, reducing deployment costs and expanding the range of tasks robots can perform effectively and safely. The identified limitations, such as handling complex rigid object interactions and improving keypoint detection under occlusion, also provide clear directions for future research, suggesting that this work forms a strong foundation for continued development.
