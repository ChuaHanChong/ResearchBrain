# Hiking in the Wild: A Scalable Perceptive Parkour Framework for Humanoids

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Tsinghua University and Shanghai Qi Zhi Institute developed a scalable perceptive parkour framework for humanoid robots, enabling robust traversal of complex, unstructured terrains at speeds up to 2.5 m/s through zero-shot sim-to-real transfer. The framework demonstrated high success rates across diverse environments like stairs, gaps, and high platforms, significantly improving foothold safety and locomotion agility.

## Problem

- Reactive proprioception-only locomotion policies limit humanoid robots from proactively avoiding hazards in complex, unstructured environments.
- Existing perceptive locomotion methods struggle with state estimation drift in mapping-based approaches or lack scalability and robustness in prior end-to-end visual policies.
- Challenges in ensuring safe foothold placement, mitigating reward hacking during training, and achieving reliable zero-shot sim-to-real transfer for dynamic humanoid tasks.

## Method

- A single-stage, end-to-end reinforcement learning policy (PPO) directly mapping raw depth inputs and proprioception to joint actions, avoiding intermediate mapping.
- Utilizing a highly realistic and efficient ego-centric depth simulation with bidirectional depth alignment, extensive noise modeling, and temporal depth aggregation for robust zero-shot sim-to-real transfer.
- Incorporating a Terrain Edge Detector with Volumetric Point Penetration Penalization for safer foothold placement, and a position-based velocity command strategy with Flat Patch Sampling to mitigate reward hacking and promote goal-directed exploration.

## Results

- Achieved robust real-world traversal of diverse and challenging terrains (e.g., stairs, 32 cm platforms, 50 cm gaps, grassy slopes) with the Unitree G1 humanoid robot via zero-shot sim-to-real transfer.
- Demonstrated high-speed locomotion agility, reaching a maximum running speed of 2.5 m/s, with high success rates across various terrains (e.g., 100% for large box, ramp, platform up/down, stair up, and gap traversal).
- The edge-aware penalization mechanism visibly improved foothold safety by encouraging feet placement away from terrain edges and generalized to novel terrain topologies without explicit engineering.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Direct perception-to-action learning, combined with carefully designed depth simulation and noise modeling, enables robust zero-shot sim-to-real transfer for highly dynamic humanoid locomotion.
> - Implicit, learning-based safety mechanisms, such as edge contact penalization, can effectively guide robots to safer footholds and generalize across diverse, unseen terrain types.
> - Integrating position-based velocity commands and adversarial motion priors is crucial for mitigating reward hacking and teaching natural, agile, and stable parkour behaviors for humanoids in complex environments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of legged locomotion, particularly for humanoid robots, has seen significant advancements, shifting from traditional model-based control methods (e.g., Model Predictive Control) to data-driven Reinforcement Learning (RL). This transition allows robots to better handle unmodeled dynamics and achieve more agile, robust behaviors.

Initially, "blind locomotion" policies, relying solely on proprioception (internal body state sensing), established a baseline for robust movement on irregular terrains like grass or gravel. However, these reactive approaches have intrinsic limitations; they cannot proactively avoid large hazards like deep gaps or high steps, making them vulnerable to catastrophic failures in complex, unstructured environments.

The integration of exteroceptive perception (external environmental sensing) into the control loop is the natural next step. Existing perceptive locomotion approaches generally fall into two categories:
- **Mapping-based methods (LiDAR or depth-based heightmaps):** These build environmental representations (e.g., 2.5D elevation maps, voxel grids) that depend heavily on precise state estimation. However, in "the wild," position sensors are prone to drift, LiDAR can suffer from low frequency and motion distortion during dynamic movements, and depth-image reconstructions often lack scalability to unseen wild environments, limiting robots to low-speed movements on simpler structures.
- **End-to-end approaches:** These aim to directly map sensory inputs (e.g., raw depth images) to actions. While promising, they often struggle with scalability, training complexity, and the problem of "reward hacking" (agents exploiting rewards in unintended ways, like spinning in place instead of traversing obstacles). Some previous works using "virtual obstacles" were implemented case-by-case, lacking generalization.

"Hiking in the Wild" directly addresses these critical bottlenecks. It builds upon recent successes in dynamic humanoid control (e.g., dancing, backflips, mimicking human motions) but distinguishes itself by tackling the more complex challenge of "hiking in the wild," which demands active perception and adaptation rather than merely tracking predefined motions. The paper positions itself as a scalable, end-to-end framework that overcomes the limitations of state estimation drift in mapping-based methods and the scalability/training challenges of previous end-to-end approaches, particularly regarding robust foothold placement and efficient exploration. By adopting a single-stage RL policy that maps raw depth inputs and proprioception directly to joint actions, it seeks to enable high-frequency, dynamic, and robust locomotion in truly unstructured environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of "Hiking in the Wild" is to develop a **scalable, end-to-end perceptive parkour framework** that enables humanoid robots to achieve **robust and agile locomotion in complex, unstructured, and diverse real-world environments**. The authors explicitly state the necessity of transitioning from reactive proprioception to proactive perception for safe and effective "hiking in the wild."

The core motivations driving this research stem from the identified limitations in the current state of humanoid locomotion:
1. **Addressing State Estimation Drift:** Existing mapping-based methods (LiDAR, heightmap reconstruction) heavily rely on precise state estimation, which is notoriously difficult to maintain in dynamic, unstructured outdoor environments. The authors aim for a system that does not depend on external localization or map reconstruction, making it more resilient to sensor inaccuracies.
2. **Overcoming Scalability and Training Complexity:** Previous end-to-end RL approaches often face challenges in scaling to diverse terrain skills and handling the high dimensionality of visual data. Some methods involving virtual obstacles were implemented on a case-by-case basis, lacking generalizability. The goal is to create a unified framework that can handle a wide variety of terrains without extensive manual reconfigurations.
3. **Ensuring Foothold Safety:** Humanoid robots are inherently less stable than quadrupeds. Stepping precariously on terrain edges (e.g., the lip of a stair) can lead to catastrophic slippage and falls. Traditional model-based planners can generate precise footholds but are brittle to map errors. The motivation is to develop a robust, learning-based mechanism to implicitly guide the robot to safe, stable footholds.
4. **Mitigating Reward Hacking:** A common issue in training RL agents for locomotion in complex environments is "reward hacking," where agents find unintended ways to maximize rewards (e.g., spinning in place) rather than learning desired traversal behaviors. The authors seek a strategy to ensure meaningful exploration and goal-directed movement, especially concerning speed control.
5. **Achieving Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer:** To make the framework practical, it must be capable of transferring policies trained in simulation directly to real-world robots with minimal or no fine-tuning. This requires robust simulation, realistic sensor modeling, and an architecture that can generalize well across the sim-to-real gap.

In essence, the paper aims to provide a comprehensive solution that allows humanoid robots to "look ahead," perceive their environment in real-time, plan proactively, ensure safety, and learn efficient locomotion skills, ultimately enabling them to reliably traverse challenging terrains at high speeds, similar to how a human hiker would navigate.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The "Hiking in the Wild" framework leverages a **single-stage, end-to-end reinforcement learning (RL) scheme** using the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm. The policy directly maps raw depth inputs and proprioception to joint actions, eschewing intermediate mapping or state estimation modules. The architecture employs an asymmetric actor-critic setup for training stability.

**A. Observation and Action Spaces:**
- **Observation Space (Actor):** Comprises proprioceptive signals (base angular velocity, projected gravity vector, velocity commands, joint positions, joint velocities, last action) and perceptive signals (raw depth images). A sliding window of historical steps (8 frames) captures temporal dependencies. Stochastic noise is injected during training for sim-to-real robustness.
- **Observation Space (Critic):** Includes noise-free actor observations plus base linear velocity, aiding training in simulation.
- **Action Space:** Target joint positions (29-DoF for the Unitree G1 humanoid). Joint torques are computed via PD control (τ = kₚ(aₜ - qₜ) - k_d˙qₜ).
- **Termination Criteria:** Episodes terminate on time-out, terrain boundary exceedance, illegal torso contact, unstable orientation, or insufficient root height, guiding efficient exploration.
- **Reward Functions:** A composite reward R = r_task + r_reg + r_safe + r_amp encourages command tracking, energy efficiency, constraint satisfaction, and natural locomotion styles, respectively.

**B. Ego-centric Depth Simulation:**
Central to zero-shot sim-to-real transfer is a highly realistic and efficient depth simulation:
- **Parallelized Ray-casting:** Utilizes NVIDIA Warp for GPU-accelerated ray-casting to synthesize high-fidelity depth observations in real-time, accounting for camera intrinsics and extrinsics, and scene geometry (terrain + robot's visual meshes). It transforms radial distance to orthogonal depth.
- **Bidirectional Depth Alignment and Noise Modeling:** Two pipelines (F_sim for simulation, F_real for real-world) ensure processed depth distributions are harmonized.
 - **F_sim:** Degrades ideal depth by applying sequential stochastic operations: cropping/resizing, range-dependent Gaussian noise, disparity artifact synthesis (masking "white regions"), Gaussian blur (motion blur), clipping/normalization, and Out-of-Distribution (OOD) perturbation (replacing observations with random noise) for robustness against sensor glitches.
 - **F_real:** Refines raw real-world depth by cropping/resizing, depth inpainting (to fill "black regions"), and Gaussian blur (to suppress jitter).
- **Temporal Depth Aggregation via Strided Sampling:** Instead of dense history, depth input uses a strided temporal sampling strategy (m frames with stride ℓ) to capture long-term context while being computationally efficient. A single-frame delay emulates sensor latency. The policy incorporates a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architecture to handle high-dimensional visual data and diverse terrain skills, allowing high-frequency (60 Hz) depth processing.

**C. Terrain Edge Contact Penalization:**
To enhance foothold safety and prevent slippage on edges:
- **Terrain Edge Detector:** Identifies sharp terrain edges in the input triangular mesh by comparing dihedral angles between adjacent faces against a threshold. It then processes raw edges through greedy concatenation to filter noise and merge segments, creating a spatial collision grid.
- **Volumetric Point Penetration Penalization:** A set of "volume points" distributed within each foot's collision manifold. NVIDIA Warp queries penetration depth of these points relative to the constructed edge spatial grid. A penalty term (r_vol = - Σ ||dᵢ|| ⋅ (||vᵢ|| + ϵ)) is applied, penalizing penetration depth (dᵢ) weighted by point velocity (vᵢ), encouraging stable, centered foot placements.

**D. Position-based Velocity Command:**
To mitigate reward hacking and promote goal-directed exploration:
- **Target Generation via Flat Patch Sampling:** Identifies reachable, stable "flat patches" on the terrain mesh (regions where terrain height difference within a radius r is below a threshold δ) using ray-casting. These serve as feasible navigation targets.
- **Position-based Velocity Command Generation:** Periodically selects a flat patch as a target. Desired linear (vₓ) and angular (ω_z) velocities are calculated based on the target's relative position in the robot's base frame (vₓ = clip(k_v ⋅ x_g, 0, v_max), ω_z = clip(k_ω ⋅ atan2(y_g, x_g), -ω_max, ω_max)). Velocity limits are adaptively adjusted based on terrain type. To ensure in-place turning skills, a small subset of agents on flat terrain receive pure turning commands (vₓ=0, random ω_z).

**E. Policy Training with Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP):**
To improve gait style and natural locomotion:
- The AMP framework is used, with a discriminator trained on a reference dataset D.
- **Reference Dataset:** Comprises synthetic walking patterns (MPC controller), high-quality human motion capture data (NOKOV), and high-speed running motions (LAFAN dataset). Human motions include complex tasks like climbing platforms and stairs. GMR retargets human trajectories to the robot.
- **Mode Collapse Mitigation:** Walking and running policies are trained separately using different datasets.
- **Temporal Features:** The discriminator uses a short sequence of past states (S_t = [s_t-n..., s_t]) instead of a single state pair, better capturing motion dynamics.
- **Loss and Rewards:** Least-squares (MSE) loss for the discriminator, with gradient penalty and weight decay for stability. The policy receives a quadratic style reward (r_t = max(0, 1 - 0.25(D(S_t) - 1)²)) for smoother gradients and stable convergence.

**F. Experiment Configurations:**
- **Training:** NVIDIA Isaac Sim and Isaac Lab on RTX 4090 GPU, using a 29-DoF Unitree G1 humanoid. History length for proprioception and depth is 8 frames.
- **Hardware (Real-world):** Unitree G1 with factory-integrated Intel RealSense D435i depth camera (60 Hz, 480x270 raw, downsampled to 36x18 for policy input).
- **Onboard Deployment:** Policy runs at 50 Hz on NVIDIA Jetson Orin NX, using onnxruntime for inference. Depth acquisition and processing are asynchronous at 60 Hz. Post-training is used for high-speed running and specific terrains.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The extensive experiments, conducted in both simulation and real-world environments with a full-size Unitree G1 humanoid robot, demonstrate the robustness and effectiveness of the "Hiking in the Wild" framework.

**A. Real-world Deployment and Zero-shot Sim-to-Real Transfer:**
- The trained policies were successfully deployed directly onto the humanoid robot using zero-shot sim-to-real transfer, showcasing the efficacy of the ego-centric depth simulation and noise modeling.
- The robot achieved **robust traversal** of diverse and challenging terrains, including stairs (ascent/descent), high platforms (up to 32 cm ascent/descent), grassy slopes, and discrete gaps (50 cm width).
- It demonstrated **high-speed locomotion agility**, reaching a maximum running speed of **2.5 m/s**.
- The high-frequency depth perception (60 Hz) was crucial for enabling these dynamic tasks, allowing rapid posture and gait adjustments.
- **Quantitative Success Rates:** Across 10 trials for each terrain and gait combination, the policy maintained a very high success rate (Figure 8), demonstrating remarkable robustness to real-world sensory noise and physical discrepancies. For example, it achieved 100% success rates for large box, ramp, platform down, stair up, platform up, and gap traversal, with minor dips for small box (99.09%) and stair descent (99.95%).
- **Long Duration Test:** The robot successfully walked for 4 minutes continuously across multiple staircases and flat surfaces without falls or human intervention, highlighting system stability over prolonged periods.

**B. Edge-aware Penalization Mechanism:**
- **Improved Foothold Safety:** With edge-aware penalization enabled, the robot visibly learned to place its feet away from terrain edges, seeking larger, safer contact areas (Figure 9).
- **Quantitative Comparison (Simulation):** Comparing against a "No Edge" baseline (Table I), the proposed method showed improved success rates and mean feet landing area percentages, particularly on edge-dense terrains:
 - Stair Descent: Success Rate 99.95% (vs 99.82%), Landing Area 0.94 (vs 0.87).
 - Deep Gap: Success Rate 100.0% (vs 99.94%), Landing Area 0.96 (vs 0.94).
 - Small Box: Success Rate 99.09% (vs 93.17%), Landing Area 0.96 (vs 0.95).
 - While mean landing areas were generally high due to time spent on flat ground, the observed improvements demonstrate a substantial positive impact specifically in edge-dense scenarios.
- **Real-world Impact:** By encouraging maximum landing area and edge avoidance, the mechanism significantly reduced real-world risks like slippage and falls (Figure 10), contributing to greater robustness during physical deployment (Figure 7 shows stable footholds with redundant safety margins).
- **Scalability:** The Terrain Edge Detector successfully generalized to new, unseen terrain types like "Stones" and "Stakes" without any manual feature engineering or parameter tuning (Figure 11), confirming its scalability to arbitrary terrain topologies.

**C. Ablation Study on Training Recipe:**
A comprehensive ablation study (Table II) highlighted the individual contributions of the proposed components:
- **Full Method (Ours):** Achieved the highest success rates and generally the lowest mean reaching times across all terrain types, confirming that the integration of all components is essential.
- **Without Depth History:** Showed a significant drop in success rate for "Small Box" (1.66% vs 99.09%) and increased mean reaching times, especially on "Small Box" (11.68s vs 8.03s) and "Stair up" (6.98s vs 5.35s), demonstrating the critical role of temporal depth context for agile, complex traversal.
- **Without Position-based Command:** Led to a notable decrease in success rate for "Small Box" (90.68% vs 99.09%) and generally higher mean reaching times, suggesting that the Flat Patch Sampling and position-based command generation effectively mitigate reward hacking and ensure robust navigation.
- **Without MoE (Vanilla MLP):** Also resulted in a lower success rate for "Small Box" (84.51% vs 99.09%) and slightly longer reaching times, indicating the Mixture-of-Experts architecture is beneficial for processing high-dimensional visual data and handling diverse skills.
- **Without AMP (No Motion Prior):** Caused a complete failure (0.00% success rate) on "Small Box" and generally higher mean reaching times, underscoring the vital role of Adversarial Motion Priors in learning natural, robust, and stable locomotion gaits required for complex terrains.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Hiking in the Wild" framework represents a significant advancement in humanoid robotics, pushing the boundaries of autonomous locomotion in complex and unstructured environments. Its key significance and potential impacts are:

1. **Enabling True "Wild" Locomotion:** By developing a scalable, end-to-end perceptive framework that does not rely on external state estimation or pre-built maps, this work moves humanoid robots closer to reliably operating in dynamic, previously unseen real-world conditions beyond controlled lab settings. This is crucial for applications in disaster relief, exploration, logistics, and service robotics in unengineered environments.
2. **Robust and Agile Parkour Capabilities:** The demonstrated ability to traverse diverse terrains (stairs, gaps, high platforms, grassy slopes) at high speeds (up to 2.5 m/s) with high success rates showcases a new level of agility and robustness for humanoid robots. This opens possibilities for more dynamic and versatile task execution.
3. **Advancements in Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The success of zero-shot sim-to-real transfer, enabled by a meticulous ego-centric depth simulation and noise modeling, is a critical contribution. It significantly reduces the immense cost and effort typically associated with deploying learned policies on physical hardware, accelerating research and development in real-world robotics.
4. **Novel Safety Mechanisms:** The introduction of the Terrain Edge Detector coupled with Volumetric Point Penetration Penalization provides an implicit, learning-based approach to ensure safe and stable footholds. This robust, scalable mechanism for handling terrain edges is vital for humanoid stability and avoids the brittleness of explicit planning susceptible to map errors.
5. **Efficient and Stable RL Training:** The Flat Patch Sampling strategy combined with position-based velocity command generation effectively addresses the pervasive "reward hacking" problem in RL. This ensures that agents learn meaningful, goal-directed behaviors, leading to faster convergence and more robust policies. The use of temporal state sequences and MSE loss for AMP also contributes to stable gait learning.
6. **Open-Sourced Research Infrastructure:** The decision to open-source the training and deployment code is highly significant. It will enable other researchers and developers to reproduce the results, build upon this work, and deploy similar capabilities on their own hardware with minimal modifications. This accelerates collective progress in the humanoid robotics community.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
The authors candidly acknowledge two primary limitations, which also outline avenues for future research:
1. **Limited Omnidirectional Perception:** Relying solely on a single forward-facing depth camera restricts backward or lateral movement capabilities. Future work could integrate multiple cameras to achieve omnidirectional perception and corresponding agility, further expanding the robot's maneuverability.
2. **Mode Collapse in Multi-Task Learning:** Training a wide variety of terrains and gait modes simultaneously can lead to performance degradation or "mode collapse" compared to specialized policies. Investigating advanced multi-task reinforcement learning techniques will be crucial to developing a single, unified policy capable of robustly handling all scenarios without compromise.

Overall, "Hiking in the Wild" presents a highly impactful framework that not only pushes the technical capabilities of humanoid robots but also provides valuable insights and tools for the broader robotics community.
