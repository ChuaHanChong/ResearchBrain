# FUSE: Quantifying Uncertainty in Vision-Language Models by Bayesian Fusing Epistemic and Aleatoric Uncertainty

> [!summary] Summary
> FUSE quantifies vision-language model uncertainty by fusing two sources: aleatoric embedding ambiguity, obtained by wrapping a frozen CLIP encoder in sparse Gaussian-process latent-variable adapters, and epistemic response diversity, summarized as the log-determinant of a confidence-weighted Gram matrix of sampled response embeddings. A linear-Gaussian Bayesian update combines them in closed form into one scalar score, giving state-of-the-art calibration and selective answering on three VQA benchmarks.

## Problem

- Next-token likelihood and logit-based confidence miss semantic ambiguity and cross-modal grounding, so a model's reported confidence can be high on hallucinated answers.
- Sampling-based uncertainty methods score only generation-level response diversity, ignoring uncertainty already present in the frozen encoder's latent representation of the image-text pair.
- Existing multimodal scores stay heuristic rather than probabilistic, lacking a principled way to combine representation-level and output-level evidence into one calibrated quantity.

## Method

- A ==sparse variational GPLVM adapter== maps frozen CLIP image and text embeddings to Gaussians sharing one latent point, trained with ==ELBO reconstruction== plus ==symmetrized-KL alignment== losses.
- ==Aleatoric prior==: log-determinants of the two predictive covariances form a scalar statistic, regressed by OLS on held-out error rates to define a Gaussian prior over latent uncertainty.
- ==Epistemic likelihood==: log-determinant of a per-token-confidence-weighted ==Gram matrix== of n sampled response embeddings, proven linear-Gaussian in latent uncertainty, yielding a ==closed-form conjugate posterior== and z-score statistic.

## Results

- On VQAv2, OKVQA and AdVQA, FUSE reaches AUROC **0.883/0.761/0.796**, beating the strongest baseline UMPIRE at **0.862/0.750/0.773** and EigenScore at **0.850/0.741/0.766**.
- Calibration improves too: ECE drops to **0.037/0.037/0.041** versus UMPIRE's **0.042/0.041/0.044**, and selective-answering AURAC rises to **0.940/0.770/0.793** from **0.916/0.761/0.761**.
- Ablations: dropping response weighting cuts VQAv2 AUROC to **0.864**, mean-only posterior gives **0.880**, jointly tuned parameters **0.872**; most variants still beat the baselines.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Uncertainty has separable input and model components; combining a representation-level prior with generation-level evidence predicts correctness better than either source alone.
> - Log-determinant of a response Gram matrix is exactly linear in latent uncertainty, which makes conjugate Gaussian fusion available instead of heuristic score mixing.
> - Training-free uncertainty scoring still needs labelled data: prior regression, weight scaling and the abstention threshold all come from a held-out calibration set.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language Models (VLMs) have demonstrated advanced capabilities in integrating visual and textual information for open-domain reasoning across various applications, including visual question answering (VQA), multimodal retrieval, and embodied task planning. These models leverage Transformer-based architectures and are trained on extensive web-scale datasets. However, despite their progress, VLMs often produce unreliable or inconsistent outputs, such as visual hallucinations or overconfident textual reasoning. This unreliability is particularly critical in high-stakes domains like healthcare, finance, and autonomous systems, where understanding model limitations is paramount for trust and safety.

Uncertainty Quantification (UQ) is an essential component for assessing model reliability. Prior research in deep learning established foundational UQ paradigms, including Monte Carlo Dropout for epistemic uncertainty, Deep Ensembles, and Bayesian neural networks for weight uncertainty. Aleatoric uncertainty, which stems from inherent data ambiguity, has also been addressed through multi-task loss functions. Calibration methods, such as post-hoc transformations, aim to align model confidence with empirical accuracy.

However, these classical UQ methods often do not translate directly or effectively to multimodal large language models (MLLMs). In MLLMs, token-level likelihoods or entropy measures often correlate weakly with semantic correctness or visual grounding. The "confidence" being calibrated may not align with the actual event of interest (e.g., grounding quality). Existing MLLM UQ methods typically operate at the generation level, estimating uncertainty by evaluating the semantic consistency of stochastic outputs (e.g., entropy-based metrics, self-consistency scores). These approaches often overlook uncertainty inherent in the model's latent representation itself, and they frequently treat uncertainty scores as heuristic rather than principled probabilistic quantities.

This work addresses these limitations by proposing a unified Bayesian framework that explicitly disentangles and fuses two complementary sources of uncertainty: aleatoric uncertainty from input data ambiguity and epistemic uncertainty arising from the model's generative process. By integrating these distinct uncertainty sources in a principled manner, FUSE aims to provide a more comprehensive and calibrated measure of uncertainty in VLM outputs, bridging a gap in current MLLM UQ methodologies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from the observed unreliability of Vision-Language Models (VLMs) in critical applications, where accurate quantification of uncertainty in model outputs is essential. Current VLMs, despite their advanced capabilities, are prone to generating inconsistent or erroneous responses, such as hallucinations or overconfident predictions that do not reflect their true accuracy. This lack of reliable uncertainty estimation undermines trust and safety, especially in domains requiring high degrees of interpretability and control.

The paper identifies two fundamental and complementary sources of uncertainty in VLMs that, when properly modeled and combined, can lead to a more robust and interpretable measure of model confidence:

1. **Aleatoric Uncertainty:** This type of uncertainty originates from the inherent ambiguity or noise within the input data itself or the task context. Examples include underspecified questions, visually ambiguous regions in an image, or multiple plausible text-image alignments. The authors argue that this input-level ambiguity leads to uncertainty in the VLM's latent representations. Existing VLM encoders (e.g., CLIP) typically produce deterministic embeddings, which do not inherently capture this form of uncertainty. A key objective is therefore to adapt these deterministic embeddings into probabilistic representations that can quantify this intrinsic data-level ambiguity.

2. **Epistemic Uncertainty:** This uncertainty arises from the model's own limitations, lack of knowledge, weak grounding, or variability in predictions for a fixed input. It reflects the model's variability due to its internal generative mechanisms and the stochastic nature of its output. For VLMs, this can manifest as diverse or inconsistent responses when the model is prompted multiple times with the same input. The motivation here is to capture this model-level variability as "evidence" of its internal uncertainty.

The key objectives of this work are:
- **Formalize and disentangle:** To provide a conceptual and mathematical framework for distinguishing and quantifying both aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty within VLM representations and generative outputs.
- **Unified probabilistic modeling:** To develop a principled Bayesian inference framework that can analytically combine these two distinct sources of uncertainty. This aims to overcome the limitations of existing methods that often treat uncertainty heuristically or focus on only one source.
- **Closed-form, calibrated measure:** To derive a single, scalar uncertainty score that is interpretable and well-calibrated, meaning it accurately reflects the probability of the model's output being correct. This score should be usable for downstream applications like selective prediction.
- **Empirical validation:** To demonstrate through experiments that the proposed framework outperforms existing baselines in terms of uncertainty calibration, selective accuracy, and the correlation between uncertainty and error across various visual question answering benchmarks.
- **Lightweight and training-free inference:** To ensure the uncertainty quantification process for VLMs is lightweight and does not require extensive retraining of the VLM itself.

By addressing these objectives, the research aims to enhance the reliability, interpretability, and safety of VLM deployments in real-world scenarios where understanding when to trust model predictions is critical.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The FUSE framework quantifies uncertainty in Vision-Language Models by combining two distinct sources of uncertainty—aleatoric and epistemic—within a unified Bayesian inference mechanism. The overall approach involves: (i) deriving a data representation prior from embedding-level aleatoric uncertainty, (ii) constructing a likelihood from response-level epistemic uncertainty, and (iii) fusing these components to yield a calibrated posterior uncertainty score.

#### 3.1. Aleatoric Uncertainty via VLM Embeddings Prior

The first component of FUSE addresses aleatoric uncertainty, which arises from inherent ambiguity in the input data. This is achieved by adapting deterministic embeddings from a frozen VLM encoder (e.g., CLIP) into probabilistic distributions.

1. **Probabilistic Embedding Generalization:** For an input query `q = (I, T)` (image and text), the frozen VLM encoder provides deterministic embeddings `z_I` and `z_T`. FUSE generalizes these to uncertainty-aware distributions by introducing a shared latent variable `x ∈ R^Q` (`Q ≪ D`) that represents the "true" semantic meaning of the data in a lower-dimensional latent space.
2. **Gaussian Process (GP) Decoders:** Two GP decoders, `G_M(x)` for `M ∈ {I, T}`, are learned to map the latent `x` to the higher-dimensional embedding spaces (`z_M = G_M(x) + ε_M`). Each dimension `k` of `G_M` is modeled by a scalar GP prior.
3. **Sparse Variational GPLVM:** To handle scalability, a sparse variational GPLVM approach is used, employing a small set of inducing points. The parameters of a Gaussian variational posterior over inducing values are optimized by maximizing an Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO) (`L_emb`).
4. **Cross-Modal Alignment:** Since both modalities share the same latent `x`, their predictive embedding distributions (`F_I` and `F_T`) are aligned by penalizing the symmetrized KL divergence between them (`L_KL`). The total objective `L_total = λ_1 L_emb + λ_2 L_KL` learns the latent points, inducing locations, variational parameters, and GP hyperparameters.
5. **Inferring Probabilistic Embeddings:** For a new input `z*`, its latent representation `x*` is inferred. This allows obtaining a probabilistic embedding `N(μ*, Σ*)` whose predictive covariance `Σ*` quantifies the aleatoric uncertainty.
6. **Data Representation Prior:** These modality-specific uncertainties (`F_I = N(μ_I, Σ_I)` and `F_T = N(μ_T, Σ_T)`) are summarized into a scalar statistic `s`, chosen as a variance-based metric (e.g., `s = 1/(2D) [log det(Σ_I) + log det(Σ_T)]`). This statistic is then used to define a Gaussian prior for a scalar uncertainty variable `u ∈ R`: `u ~ N(m_0, σ_0^2)`, where `m_0 = α_0 + β_0 s`. The parameters `(α_0, β_0, σ_0^2)` are calibrated on a held-out set by regressing empirical error rates on `s`. This prior captures the uncertainty solely contributed by the input representation, independent of VLM inference.

#### 3.2. Epistemic Uncertainty via Response Semantics Evidence

The second component quantifies epistemic uncertainty, stemming from the VLM's generative variability.

1. **Model Response Definition:** Given an uncertainty level `u`, each sampled `d`-dimensional response embedding `r_i` is modeled as `r_i | u ~ N(0, e^u Σ_r)`, where `Σ_r` is a fixed base geometry of the response space. This implies that higher `u` leads to greater variability among responses. Responses are normalized to remove arbitrary scaling.
2. **Semantic Scatter and Evidence:** For `n` sampled and whitened responses `r_1, ..., r_n`, a matrix `R = [r_1, ..., r_n]^T` is formed. The semantic scatter matrix is defined as `S = RR^T`, and the semantic evidence `~V := log det S`. The determinant of `S` is proportional to the squared volume of the ellipsoid spanned by the responses, thus `~V` measures semantic dispersion.
3. **Linearity and Gaussian Likelihood (Unweighted):** The paper proves that `~V = nu + log det Λ`, where `Λ` is a Wishart random matrix. This demonstrates an exact linear dependence of `~V` on `u`. Furthermore, `~V | u` follows a Gaussian distribution `N(a(n,d) + nu, v(n,d))`, where `a` and `v` are analytical functions of `n` (number of samples) and `d` (embedding dimension), derived from the moments of `log χ^2` distributions via Bartlett decomposition of the Wishart distribution.
4. **Weighted Responses:** To account for heterogeneous reliability of sampled responses, uncertainty weights `w_i` are introduced. `w_i = exp(2α(1-p_i))`, where `p_i` is the average per-token log-probability of response `y_i`. Responses with lower confidence receive higher weights. The weighted semantic scatter `S_w = W^(1/2) RR^T W^(1/2)` (where `W = diag(w_i)`) and its log determinant `~V = log det S_w` are used. A similar theorem proves that `~V` still follows a linear-Gaussian model `~V = nu + a_w(ν_eff, d, n, w̄) + ε_w`, where `ε_w ~ N(0, v(d,n))`, and `a_w` depends on `w̄` (mean weight) and `ν_eff` (effective degrees of freedom).

#### 3.3. Bayesian Fusion of the Uncertainty Sources

With both the Gaussian prior `p(u) ~ N(m_0, σ_0^2)` (from aleatoric uncertainty) and the linear-Gaussian likelihood `p(~V | u) ~ N(a + nu, τ^2)` (from epistemic uncertainty), FUSE applies Bayesian inference to combine them.

1. **Closed-Form Posterior:** The conjugacy of the Gaussian prior and likelihood yields a closed-form Gaussian posterior `p(u | ~V) ~ N(m_post, σ_post^2)`, where `m_post` and `σ_post^2` are analytically derived from `m_0, σ_0^2, a, n, τ^2, ~V`. This posterior captures the combined uncertainty.
2. **Fused Uncertainty Statistic:** A single scalar uncertainty score `u*` is defined as `u* = m_post + z_α σ_post`, where `z_α` is a hyperparameter determining a z-score-based bound on the uncertainty.

#### 3.4. Practical Calibration and Hyperparameters

Before deployment, several parameters are calibrated on a held-out development set with ground truth VQA labels:
- **Prior parameters:** `(α_0, β_0, σ_0^2)` are estimated by maximizing the marginal log-likelihood of `~V | s`, which integrates out `u`. This step does not require correctness labels.
- **Uncertainty weights parameter:** `α` in `w_i` is tuned to balance incoherent samples.
- **Decision threshold:** `θ*` for `u*` is set to control the conditional risk `R(θ) = P(correct | u* ≤ θ)` below a user-specified maximum tolerable error rate `r`, while maximizing coverage.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates FUSE using LLaVA-v1.5-13b with CLIP-ViT-L-336px on three VQA datasets: VQAv2, OKVQA, and AdVQA. It compares FUSE against several baselines, including UMPIRE, Neighborhood Consistency, LN-Entropy, Semantic Entropy, EigenScore, LoFreeCP, and CLM.

#### 4.1. Uncertainty as Correctness Prediction

The performance of FUSE in predicting the correctness of VLM outputs is evaluated using discrimination and calibration metrics.

- **Discrimination:** FUSE demonstrates superior discriminative power, achieving the highest AUROC scores across all three datasets (0.883 on VQAv2, 0.761 on OKVQA, 0.796 on AdVQA), indicating that it more effectively ranks incorrect answers as more uncertain than correct ones. It also consistently shows higher True Positive Rates (TPR) at various False Positive Rate (FPR) thresholds (e.g., 0.644 TPR at 0.1 FPR on VQAv2). This suggests that FUSE can reliably distinguish between correct and incorrect VLM responses.
- **Calibration:** FUSE significantly improves uncertainty calibration. It achieves higher Pearson and Spearman correlation coefficients between binned mean uncertainty and binned empirical error (e.g., Pearson 0.945 on VQAv2, 0.945 on OKVQA, 0.974 on AdVQA). Furthermore, FUSE yields the lowest Expected Calibration Error (ECE) across all datasets (0.037 on VQAv2, 0.037 on OKVQA, 0.041 on AdVQA). These results indicate that FUSE’s predicted uncertainty scores closely match the actual empirical error rates, providing a well-calibrated measure of model confidence. FUSE's robustness is particularly noted on challenging datasets like OKVQA and AdVQA.

#### 4.2. Selective Answering

FUSE's ability to support selective answering, where the model abstains from uncertain queries, is evaluated using the Area Under the Rejection–Accuracy Curve (AURAC). FUSE achieves the highest AURAC scores across all datasets (0.940 on VQAv2, 0.770 on OKVQA, 0.793 on AdVQA). This demonstrates that FUSE provides a more reliable abstention signal, enabling the model to preferentially reject queries likely to be incorrect and thus improve accuracy on the answered subset.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies

Ablation studies investigate the contribution of different components and design choices within FUSE:
- **Ablations on Uncertainty Sources:** Comparing "Prior-Only," "Evidence-Only," and "Fused" versions reveals that fusing both aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty significantly enhances performance across all metrics. While "Evidence-Only" is a strong baseline (similar to UMPIRE), the "Fused" approach consistently outperforms it. This highlights the importance of integrating both uncertainty sources.
- **Ablations on Fused Uncertainty Statistic Construction:** Variants like "Logistic u*" (fitting a logistic function), "Mean-only u*" (using only the posterior mean), and "Quantile θ*" (using a quantile-based threshold) generally perform better than baselines, but slightly below the full FUSE model. This suggests that the chosen z-score-based metric for `u*` and the risk-control thresholding contribute to optimal performance.
- **Global Tuning vs. Analytical Calculation:** "Global Tuning," which jointly tunes all prior and likelihood parameters using labeled correctness, performs strongly. This indicates that while analytical calculation provides a principled foundation, supervised tuning can absorb dataset-specific idiosyncrasies.
- **OLS for ~V:** Using a simple Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) fit for the semantic evidence `~V` instead of theory-motivated likelihood calibration still performs well, but slightly below the full FUSE model, supporting the utility of the Wishart-inspired moment structure.
- **Unweighted Semantic Evidence:** Using unweighted semantic evidence results in a marginal decrease in performance compared to the weighted version, indicating the benefit of modulating each response's contribution based on its token-level probability.
- **Number of Generations:** FUSE achieves strong performance with fewer sampled responses per instance compared to baselines, suggesting greater sample efficiency, although all methods benefit from more generations.
- **Evaluation Criteria:** FUSE consistently surpasses baselines across different ROUGE-L correctness criteria, demonstrating robustness to varying definitions of answer correctness.

#### 4.4. Qualitative Results

Qualitative examples illustrate FUSE's behavior. In successful cases, FUSE assigns low uncertainty to correct answers and high uncertainty to incorrect ones, even when next-token likelihoods might be ambiguous. For instance, in an example of a "Motorcycle racing" image, FUSE correctly assigns low uncertainty despite a relatively low NTL score. In a "bus energy" VQA example, FUSE assigns high uncertainty even though the VLM consistently produced "Electricity," possibly due to missing visual information or alternative plausible answers. Failure modes primarily involve numerical answers, where FUSE sometimes confidently outputs incorrect numerical values (e.g., "1940" for a plane made in 1930), which is an area for future work.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The FUSE framework presents a significant advancement in the field of uncertainty quantification for Vision-Language Models (VLMs) by introducing a principled Bayesian method to fuse two complementary sources of uncertainty: aleatoric and epistemic.

The key significance of this work lies in its **rigorous and unified probabilistic modeling** of uncertainty. By explicitly disentangling and combining data-level ambiguity (aleatoric) and model-level variability (epistemic), FUSE moves beyond heuristic or partial uncertainty measures common in current MLLM UQ. The analytical derivation of a closed-form posterior uncertainty statistic provides a theoretically grounded and interpretable measure of confidence. This mathematical foundation differentiates FUSE from many existing sampling-based or heuristic approaches.

The **improved calibration and discrimination performance** demonstrated across various VQA benchmarks is critical. Well-calibrated uncertainty estimates are essential for understanding when to trust VLM outputs, which directly impacts their deployability in high-stakes or safety-critical domains such as robotics, healthcare, and autonomous systems. In such applications, knowing "what the model doesn't know" is as important as its predictive accuracy. The consistent outperformance against a range of baselines on metrics like AUROC, ECE, and AURAC suggests that FUSE provides a more reliable signal for downstream decision-making.

The ability of FUSE to support **selective answering** with high accuracy on answered subsets is a practical benefit. Systems can leverage FUSE's uncertainty scores to abstain from answering highly uncertain queries, thereby maintaining higher trustworthiness and accuracy for the responses they do provide. This capability is valuable for human-in-the-loop systems or scenarios where VLM outputs require verification.

Furthermore, the methodology's reliance on **lightweight adaptation** (a small GP adapter for the frozen VLM encoder) rather than extensive retraining of the entire VLM is a practical advantage. This makes FUSE applicable to existing large-scale VLMs without incurring substantial computational costs, contributing to its broader utility and adoption. The analytical nature of the Bayesian fusion also means that the uncertainty calculation for inference is efficient.

Potential impacts include:
- **Enhanced Reliability and Trust:** By providing more trustworthy uncertainty estimates, FUSE can increase confidence in VLM deployments, making them safer and more reliable in sensitive applications.
- **Improved Decision-Making:** Downstream systems can use the FUSE score to make more informed decisions, such as deferring to human experts for high-uncertainty cases or triggering alternative safety protocols.
- **Advanced Robotic and Embodied AI:** The paper explicitly mentions the goal of deploying FUSE to robotic applications for 3D scene understanding. Reliable uncertainty in VLM-powered robotic perception and planning could lead to more robust and adaptive autonomous systems.
- **Foundation for Future UQ Research:** FUSE's principled decomposition of uncertainty sources and Bayesian fusion approach can serve as a foundational framework for future research in multimodal uncertainty quantification, potentially inspiring similar methods for other modalities beyond vision-language or more complex generative models.

While the current work focuses on vision-language models and relies on CLIP-like encoders, the conceptual framework for decomposing and fusing uncertainty sources holds potential for generalization to other multimodal architectures. Addressing limitations such as the need for adapter retraining and handling numerical responses will be important steps for broader impact.
