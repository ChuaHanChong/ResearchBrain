# Test-Time Gradient Guidance of Flow Policies in Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from UC Berkeley and Physical Intelligence introduce QGF (Q-Guided Flow), a reinforcement learning algorithm that achieves policy optimization entirely at test time. This method guides a pre-trained flow policy using gradients from a learned Q-function, demonstrating performance competitive with or superior to state-of-the-art training-time RL methods on continuous control tasks, while improving computational efficiency compared to Best-of-N sampling.

## Problem

- Traditional reinforcement learning (RL) training, especially with expressive generative policies like flow models, suffers from instability, hyperparameter sensitivity, and high computational cost (e.g., backpropagation through time).
- Existing test-time policy improvement methods for RL, such as Best-of-N (BFN) sampling, are computationally prohibitive for high-dimensional actions, and naive critic gradient guidance struggles with unreliable gradients from out-of-distribution intermediate actions.
- Integrating expressive generative models into RL pipelines often requires specialized training objectives that undermine their scalability and stability benefits seen in supervised learning.

## Method

- **QGF** pre-trains a ==reference flow policy== using behavioral cloning (supervised learning) and a Q-function critic using Implicit Q-learning (IQL), decoupling policy acquisition from reward optimization.
- During test-time inference, QGF guides the flow policy's iterative denoising process by applying gradients from the Q-function, but crucially, it computes these gradients on a first-order approximation of the *fully denoised action*, avoiding out-of-distribution critic queries and expensive backpropagation.
- The method empirically omits the Jacobian term from the gradient estimation, which is shown to yield lower variance and better performance compared to its inclusion.

## Results

- QGF consistently outperforms existing gradient-based test-time guidance algorithms (e.g., QFQL, BPTT) and is competitive with, or superior to, state-of-the-art training-time RL algorithms (e.g., FQL, QAM) across diverse OGBench environments.
- QGF demonstrates significantly higher computational efficiency than Best-of-N (BFN) sampling, outperforming BFN with N=4 samples while using substantially less compute, and combined with BFN, achieves performance of BFN N=16 at reduced cost.
- The algorithm scales effectively to harder, long-horizon goal-conditioned tasks and benefits substantially from larger model architectures, showing nearly **4x** performance improvement when scaling from 800k to **3.2M** parameters.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Policy optimization can be effectively and stably performed entirely at test time by leveraging a pre-trained generative policy and a critic, sidestepping the instabilities of traditional actor-critic training.
> - Accurate and stable gradient estimation for guiding generative models requires querying the critic on actions that are within its training distribution, which QGF achieves by approximating a fully denoised action rather than using noisy intermediate steps.
> - Simplified gradient estimators, specifically a first-order approximation for the denoised action and omitting the Jacobian, can lead to lower variance and improved performance in practice for generative policy guidance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) presents a framework for optimizing reward-maximizing behavior. A persistent challenge in scaling RL algorithms, particularly in offline or off-policy settings, relates to the complexity and instability inherent in policy optimization. Unlike supervised learning, which optimizes toward fixed targets, RL typically involves an iterative process of learning a value function and then optimizing an actor to maximize this learned function. This coupled training dynamic can render RL policy training sensitive to hyperparameters and susceptible to optimization instability.

The integration of expressive policy classes, such as diffusion and flow models, further complicates this challenge. These models are capable of representing complex, multimodal action distributions and have demonstrated effective scaling in standard supervised learning contexts, such as behavioral cloning (BC). However, their incorporation into RL pipelines often necessitates the design of specialized training objectives or backpropagation through multi-step denoising processes. Such approaches can undermine the scalability and stability that these generative models offer in supervised settings, leading to issues like computational expense, instability, and high variance in gradients.

Existing approaches to policy improvement in RL using expressive generative models include:
- **Specialized Training Objectives:** Modifying RL training objectives to accommodate the specific characteristics of diffusion or flow models.
- **Backpropagation Through Time (BPTT):** Computing gradients by backpropagating through the iterative denoising steps of generative models, which can be computationally intensive and unstable.
- **Test-Time Optimization:** Methods that refine actions at inference time without altering policy parameters. This category includes:
 - **Best-of-N (BFN) Sampling:** Generating multiple actions from a policy and selecting the one with the highest estimated value. This can be prohibitively expensive in high-dimensional action spaces.
 - **Gradient Guidance:** Using the gradient of a Q-function to steer action generation. Prior attempts in this area face challenges such as unreliable gradients when querying the Q-function with intermediate, "noisy" actions (out-of-distribution for the critic), or the computational burden and instability of BPTT.

This paper addresses the tension between the expressivity of flow models and the stability requirements of RL. It investigates whether policy improvement can be achieved predominantly at test time, thus sidestepping the training-time instabilities associated with actor-critic methods and complex backpropagation. The proposed method, QGF, aims to provide an effective, stable, and scalable test-time gradient guidance mechanism for flow policies, addressing the limitations observed in previous gradient-based approaches.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a reinforcement learning algorithm, termed QGF (Q-Guided Flow), that performs policy optimization entirely at test time. This approach aims to guide a pre-trained reference flow policy towards higher-value actions using gradients from a learned value function critic, without requiring any additional policy learning or parameter updates during reward optimization.

The motivation for pursuing this objective stems from several challenges in current RL practices, particularly when utilizing expressive policy classes like diffusion and flow models:

- **Instability and Complexity of Traditional RL Training:** Conventional RL algorithms, especially in the offline or off-policy settings, typically involve an alternating process of learning a value function and optimizing a policy to maximize this function. This coupled dynamic is often characterized by instability, sensitivity to hyperparameters, and complex optimization landscapes, which can hinder scalability.
- **Challenges of Expressive Policies in RL Pipelines:** While generative models such as diffusion and flow models have demonstrated strong performance and scalability in supervised imitation learning (e.g., behavioral cloning, BC) due to their ability to model complex, multimodal action distributions, their integration into RL frameworks is difficult. It frequently necessitates the development of specialized training objectives or computationally expensive and unstable backpropagation through their iterative denoising processes.
- **Desire for Decoupled and Stable Training:** The authors seek an alternative paradigm where the actor (flow policy) is trained using a standard, stable supervised learning objective (behavioral cloning), and reward maximization is applied dynamically at inference time. This approach leverages the robustness of supervised learning for policy acquisition and isolates the reward optimization process, avoiding the instabilities associated with training an actor against an evolving critic.
- **Limitations of Existing Test-Time RL Methods:**
 - **Best-of-N (BFN) Sampling:** While effective, BFN can be computationally prohibitive in scenarios involving high-dimensional action spaces, as it requires generating and evaluating multiple full action sequences.
 - **Naïve Critic Gradient Guidance:** Directly applying critic gradients to intermediate, noisy actions generated during the denoising process (e.g., ∇_a_t Q(s, a_t)) is problematic. Critics are typically trained on fully denoised actions, making queries at noisy, out-of-distribution (OOD) intermediate actions unreliable and prone to biased guidance. Such gradients may exploit the Q-function on OOD inputs, leading to suboptimal actual performance despite high estimated Q-values.
 - **Backpropagation Through Time (BPTT) for Gradients:** A more principled approach would involve backpropagating the critic gradient through the entire denoising ODE (∇_a_t Q(s, ODE(a_t))). However, this is computationally expensive and can suffer from high variance and instability, as demonstrated in empirical analyses within the paper.

By addressing these limitations, the research aims to establish test-time gradient guidance as a practical, scalable, and effective strategy for optimizing expressive generative policies in continuous control, offering a stable alternative to conventional actor-critic training.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, QGF (Q-Guided Flow), is an RL algorithm designed to perform policy optimization entirely at test time. It builds upon a foundation of pre-training a reference flow policy via behavioral cloning and a value function critic using Implicit Q-learning (IQL).

**Theoretical Basis:**
The work is framed within the context of a KL-regularized reward maximization objective. The optimal policy for this objective is given by 𝜋(𝑎 ∣ 𝑠) ∝̂ 𝜋(𝑎 ∣ 𝑠) ⋅ exp(𝑄(𝑠, 𝑎))^(1/𝛽), where ̂𝜋 is the reference policy and 𝛽 is a regularization weight. This formulation implies that the score function of the optimal policy can be expressed as ∇_a log 𝜋(a|s) = ∇_a loĝ𝜋(a|s) + (1/𝛽) ⋅ ∇_a Q(s, a). This equation suggests that a base policy can be guided by adding a term proportional to the Q-function gradient to its own score function.

**Training Phase:**
1. **Reference Flow Policy (v_θ):** A time-dependent velocity field v_θ(x, t) is trained using flow matching (Equation 2) with a standard behavioral cloning objective. This model learns to transport samples from a simple noise distribution to the target data distribution of actions observed in an offline dataset. At inference, actions are generated by integrating an ordinary differential equation (ODE) defined by v_θ.
2. **Value Function Critic (Q(s, a)):** A Q-function is trained using Implicit Q-learning (IQL) (Equations L_Q and L_V). IQL is selected because it allows for learning a Q-function for near-optimal policies using only actions from the dataset, thereby decoupling value learning from policy extraction and avoiding the need for on-policy sampling. A state value function V_ψ is also trained via expectile regression.

**Test-Time Inference (QGF Algorithm 1):**
QGF guides the iterative denoising process of the pre-trained flow policy using the critic gradient without altering the policy's parameters. The process proceeds as follows:
1. **Initialization:** Start with a noisy action `a_0` sampled from a Gaussian distribution.
2. **Iterative Denoising (for t = 0, δ..., 1-δ):**
 - **Approximate Denoised Action (̂a_1):** At each time step `t` during the denoising process, QGF computes a first-order approximation of the *fully denoised action* `̂a_1`. This is achieved by taking a single, large Euler integration step from the current noisy action `a_t` following the local velocity field `v_θ`: `̂a_1 = a_t + v_θ(s, a_t, t) ⋅ (1 - t)`. This approximation avoids running the full, multi-step denoising process.
 - **Critic Gradient Estimation:** The gradient for guidance, `g`, is then calculated by taking the gradient of the Q-function with respect to this approximated denoised action `̂a_1`: `g = ∇_̂a_1 Q(s, ̂a_1)`.
 - **Jacobian Approximation:** Conceptually, the gradient with respect to `a_t` should involve the Jacobian of `̂a_1` with respect to `a_t` (i.e., `(𝜕̂a_1 / 𝜕a_t)ᵀ ∇_̂a_1 Q(s, ̂a_1)`). However, the paper empirically finds that replacing this Jacobian term with the identity matrix (J ≈ I) yields better performance and lower variance. Thus, the gradient `g` is applied directly.
 - **Guided Denoising Step:** The next noisy action `a_{t+δ}` is updated by combining the original flow velocity `v_θ(s, a_t, t)` with the estimated critic gradient `g`, scaled by a guidance weight `(1/𝛽)`: `a_{t+δ} ← a_t + δ ⋅ (v_θ(s, a_t, t) + (1/𝛽)g)`.
3. **Final Action:** After iterating through all denoising steps, the final `a_1` is returned as the guided action.

**Distinguishing Features and Design Choices:**
- **Avoidance of OOD Critic Queries:** QGF avoids querying the critic on intermediate noisy actions `a_t` (which are out-of-distribution for a critic trained on clean actions). Instead, it queries the critic on an *approximation of a fully denoised action* `̂a_1`, which is closer to the critic's training distribution.
- **Efficiency over BPTT:** The method bypasses the computational expense and instability of backpropagating through the entire denoising ODE (BPTT). The first-order approximation for `̂a_1` is computationally cheaper.
- **Empirical Justification for Approximations:** The paper provides empirical evidence that the specific approximations (first-order `̂a_1` and omitting the Jacobian) are not merely compromises for efficiency but actually lead to lower-variance gradient estimators and better performance. The first-order approximation is hypothesized to be more effective at mode selection, allowing for deviations from the exact dataset distribution to select higher-value modes.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical evaluation of QGF was conducted across a suite of seven environments from OGBench (five tasks each), including manipulation tasks with high-dimensional action spaces and long horizons, using datasets ranging from 100M to 1B transitions. The chunking setting (h=5) for actions was used, increasing the complexity of the action space.

### 5. Performance Against Baselines (Fig. 5)

- **Test-Time Methods:** QGF demonstrably outperforms all previously established gradient-based test-time guidance algorithms, including QFQL, BPTT, and a novel RobustQ baseline. It also surpasses other test-time methods like CFGRL and GradStep. This suggests the efficacy of QGF's proposed gradient estimator.
- **Training-Time Methods:** Remarkably, QGF, which operates solely at test time, exhibits competitive performance with, and in some cases slightly superior performance to, state-of-the-art training-time RL algorithms such as FQL, EDP, QAM, DAC, and QSM+BC. This outcome indicates that stable supervised policy training combined with dynamic test-time optimization can rival methods that explicitly train policies for reward maximization.
- **QGF-Jacobian vs. QGF:** The variant of QGF that includes the Jacobian in its gradient estimator (QGF-Jacobian) consistently performs worse than QGF, supporting the empirical decision to omit the Jacobian for improved performance and reduced gradient variance.

### 6. Compute Requirements and Scaling with Test-Time Compute (Fig. 6 & 7)

- **Efficiency:** QGF is shown to be significantly more computationally efficient than Best-of-N (BFN) sampling. BFN requires orders of magnitude more GFLOPs per generated action.
- **BFN Comparison:** QGF, on its own, outperforms BFN with N=4 samples, despite BFN utilizing substantially more compute. When further test-time compute is available, combining QGF with BFN (QGF+BFN, N=4) achieves performance comparable to BFN with a much larger budget (N=16), demonstrating that QGF can serve as an effective base for further performance gains.

### 7. Scaling to Harder Tasks and Model Architectures (Fig. 8 & 9)

- **Goal-Conditioned RL:** On harder, long-horizon goal-conditioned tasks (e.g., puzzle-4x6-1B, cube-octuple-1B), QGF consistently emerges as the top-performing method, while QFQL and BPTT struggle. This indicates the robustness and scalability of QGF's gradient estimator to complex environments.
- **Model Size Scaling:** QGF benefits substantially from increasing model size. When scaling from 800k to 3.2M parameters, QGF shows a nearly 4x improvement in performance, whereas a training-time baseline (QAM) shows no notable gain. At very large model sizes (12.7M parameters), QGF exhibits less susceptibility to overfitting compared to QAM.

### 8. Analysis of Gradient Estimators (Fig. 2, 3, 4, 17)

- **OOD Gradient Limitations:** Illustrative examples and empirical data demonstrate that the "OOD gradient" (∇_a_t Q(s, a_t)), utilized by methods like QFQL, misguides the flow. Despite producing actions with artificially high Q-values, these actions are far from optimal and out-of-distribution relative to the dataset, resulting in poor actual performance.
- **BPTT Instability:** Backpropagation Through Time (BPTT) for gradients, while theoretically more sound than OOD gradients, shows high sensitivity to noise and instability, especially with increased guidance weights, leading to suboptimal results.
- **QGF Gradient Characteristics:** The QGF gradient estimator demonstrates the least variance and lowest sensitivity to noise among the evaluated gradient methods (Fig. 3). This characteristic translates to QGF being a more effective Q-value optimizer, aligning with the performance of a BFN oracle (Fig. 4).
- **First-Order Approximation Efficacy:** The use of a single Euler integration step for the approximate denoised action (̂a_1) in QGF, instead of the full denoising chain (QGF-chain), is shown to be more effective. This is hypothesized to be due to QGF's improved ability to select beneficial action modes, rather than strictly adhering to the exact dataset distribution.

### 9. Compatibility with Different Critics (Fig. 10)

- QGF is demonstrated to be compatible with various critic training methodologies. When integrated with a more performant critic (e.g., a QAM-based Q-function trained with Q-bootstrapping), QGF's performance improves further, surpassing the QAM policy itself, highlighting its effectiveness as a policy extraction mechanism.

### 10. Significance and Potential Impact

The research introduces QGF as a novel reinforcement learning algorithm, making several contributions to the field of continuous control and generative policy modeling.

**1. Practical and Stable Policy Optimization:** QGF demonstrates that competitive policy improvement can be achieved entirely at test time. This approach decouples the challenging aspects of reward maximization from policy learning, allowing the policy to be pre-trained stably with standard behavioral cloning via flow matching, a supervised learning objective. This sidesteps the inherent instabilities and complexities associated with traditional actor-critic training, offering a more robust and practical pipeline for RL.

**2. Effective Utilization of Expressive Generative Policies:** The work provides a method to harness the representational capabilities of flow models for RL without inheriting the difficulties of integrating them into conventional RL training loops. By guiding the denoising process with a carefully constructed critic gradient, QGF effectively steers these expressive policies towards high-value actions, a problem that prior gradient-guidance methods struggled with due to issues like out-of-distribution (OOD) critic queries or the computational expense and instability of backpropagation through time (BPTT).

**3. Improved Performance and Scalability:** QGF not only outperforms existing test-time RL methods but also achieves performance competitive with, or superior to, state-of-the-art training-time RL algorithms. This is particularly significant for challenging, long-horizon tasks and with larger model architectures, where QGF exhibits favorable scaling properties and reduced susceptibility to overfitting compared to some training-time baselines. This suggests that test-time gradient guidance can be a highly effective strategy for complex control problems.

**4. Advancement in Gradient Estimation for Generative Models:** The proposed gradient estimator within QGF, which relies on a first-order approximation of the denoised action and empirically omits the Jacobian, is shown to possess lower variance and higher stability. This contributes to a deeper understanding of how to effectively use critic gradients to guide iterative generative processes, addressing practical limitations observed in naive OOD gradients and BPTT approaches.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Simplified RL Workflows:** Researchers and practitioners can potentially adopt QGF to simplify their RL workflows, leveraging stable supervised learning for policy acquisition and deferring reward optimization to inference time. This could accelerate development and deployment of RL agents in complex domains.
- **Broader Adoption of Generative Policies:** By mitigating the integration challenges, QGF could facilitate wider adoption of powerful generative models, such as flow and diffusion models, in various continuous control applications, including robotics and simulation.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The success of QGF opens new avenues for research into test-time policy optimization. Future work might explore optimizing the inference-time compute for very large critics, investigate alternative training recipes for the base reference policy to maximize QGF's efficiency, or extend this guidance paradigm to other types of generative models and decision-making problems.
- **Robustness in Offline RL:** Given its strong performance in offline RL settings, QGF offers a promising approach for extracting robust and performant policies from fixed, mixed-quality datasets, which is crucial for real-world applications where online interaction is limited or costly.

In conclusion, QGF presents a compelling case for test-time gradient guidance as a practical, scalable, and effective paradigm for integrating expressive generative policies into reinforcement learning, marking a step towards more stable and performant RL systems.
