# SEES: A Self-Evolving Embodied System via Failure-Guided VLA Policy Adaptation

> [!summary] Summary
> SEES is a self-evolving system that improves VLA policies on long-horizon tasks without extra expert demonstrations. It decomposes tasks into atomic skills routed to family-shared LoRA adapters, uses a learned termination head to attribute failures, picks the most frequent failing stage as bottleneck, and trains it with online PPO in simulation from restored pre-task states and LLM-generated success predicates. It lifts LIBERO average success from 53.1% to 65.4%.

## Problem

- VLA policies trained on data biased toward cheap single-stage demonstrations stay unreliable on long-horizon tasks, where one weak atomic skill breaks many multi-stage tasks.
- Human-in-the-loop and intervention methods still need experts to diagnose bottlenecks and collect extra demonstrations, which is costly and impractical for robots already deployed in homes.
- Autonomous data collection and modular adapter methods broaden experience or protect old skills but do not prioritize the specific atomic skills that most often block long-horizon success.

## Method

- ==Failure-guided bottleneck identification==: a learned ==termination head== on the frozen VLA marks atomic-stage completion, failures go to the first blocking stage, and the most frequent one is selected per task.
- ==LLM-based RL task builder==: restores simulator states recorded just before the bottleneck stage and generates a ==Boolean success predicate== from documented simulator APIs, defining sparse first-completion rewards.
- ==Family-level policy evolution==: related atomic skills share one action-head ==LoRA adapter== trained with ==PPO== on pooled bottleneck tasks, registering only checkpoints that show positive validation gain on targets.

## Results

- On RoboCasa365 after **three** rounds, GR00T N1.5 rises from **10.2%** to **13.1%** Composite-Seen and **3.5%** to **6.1%** Composite-Unseen; RLDX-1 Unseen rises from **6.0%** to **7.1%**.
- Across **four** LIBERO suites, average success climbs from **53.1%** to **65.4%** under a small **rank-8** LoRA budget, and sibling tasks never trained with PPO improve in every suite.
- Bottleneck selection lifts ScrubCuttingBoard success from **18%** to **64%**, versus **28%** random and **34%** highest-success selection; family LoRA beats atomic-specific LoRA on siblings, **80.0%** versus **72.0%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Spending a fixed RL budget on the most frequent failing stage beats spreading it randomly or polishing reliable skills, so failure statistics make an effective curriculum signal.
> - Sharing one adapter across related skills turns targeted practice into transfer, while isolated per-skill adapters improve only their own targets and can slightly hurt sibling tasks.
> - Splitting long tasks into separately prompted subtasks can hurt at first when those subtasks lack standalone training data, so modular execution needs adaptation before it pays off.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

SEES addresses a practical weakness of vision-language-action (VLA) robots. A long household job can fail because one small, recurring capability, such as scrubbing a board, breaks. Finding that weak point and collecting new expert demonstrations can be costly.

On ScrubCuttingBoard, the baseline loses its sponge and times out at scrubbing. The round-3 SEES policy completes scrubbing and the remaining stages. This is one controlled replay, not a success-rate estimate.

The authors' approach is to learn from where execution breaks, practice there in simulation, and share the update with related skills. They frame this as a failure-guided self-evolution loop that needs no *additional expert demonstrations*. It does require an interactive simulator and online RL.

Both runs of ScrubCuttingBoard begin from the same initial state and use a matched inference seed. Top, before evolution (official base): the robot drops the sponge, times out while scrubbing, and execution stops. The final “Execution stopped” panel is not a recorded downstream frame. Bottom, after evolution (R3 family policy): the robot scrubs successfully and completes the full task, using the full validated family routing. This is a controlled example, not a rate comparison.

### 2. Turning failures into practice

**Splitting the job into stages.** A frozen vision-language planner decomposes the instruction into ordered subtasks. A language agent then routes each subtask to a predefined skill family. Related subtasks keep their own instructions but use the same family adapter. A learned termination head marks when each stage is complete.

**Finding the bottleneck.** Across rollouts, SEES assigns a failure to the first reached stage that blocks continuation. Downstream stages blocked by it are not counted. For each composite task, SEES selects the stage that fails most frequently.

**Practicing from saved states.** For each selected stage, the system saves simulator states from just before that stage began. These include states from attempts that eventually succeeded as well as failed ones. Restoring those states lets RL begin at the weak stage rather than replaying earlier steps.

**Checking success.** An LLM writes a Boolean success check from the instruction, the relevant object and fixture identifiers, and documented simulator queries. During RL, success must hold for three consecutive steps. A sparse reward is issued at the first confirmed completion.

**Updating the shared adapter.** PPO updates a low-rank adapter for the selected family, starting from its latest accepted checkpoint. The best checkpoint is registered only if its mean success on the selected bottleneck tasks improves. Unseen tasks are excluded from adaptation and checkpoint selection. The backbone stays frozen.

### 3. Do the chosen target and the sharing matter?

**Target selection.** One controlled test used a single ScrubCuttingBoard task, with the same base policy, two rounds, and 40 PPO steps per round. Measured by held-out full-task success:

- Selecting the most frequent bottleneck reached 32/50 (64%).
- Random selection reached 14/50 (28%).
- Training the currently highest-success stage reached 17/50 (34%).

The selected bottleneck changed from Scrub to Navigate after the first update. This supports failure-based selection in that controlled task, not a universal guarantee.

Held-out full-task success by selection strategy: base policy 9/50 (18%); random, Release then Navigate, 14/50 (28%); highest-success stage, Pick up then Release, 17/50 (34%); bottleneck, Scrub then Navigate, 32/50 (64%). The strategies share the base policy and use two rounds with 40 PPO steps per round.

**Shared adapters.** A separate five-task pick-and-place comparison tested sharing. On two sibling tasks excluded from RL, family-level LoRA reached 80.0% mean success, versus 72.0% for atomic-specific LoRAs. On the three selected bottlenecks, the means were 74.0% and 70.7%.

The training budgets differed in structure. The family adapter had 40 training steps in total, using mixed initializations from three bottlenecks. Each separate atomic adapter had 40 steps on its own bottleneck. The comparison therefore matches steps per adapter, not total training across all separate adapters.

This contrasts with [[2603.09298|CORAL]], which assigns a separate LoRA expert to each task to prevent interference.

### 4. Contribution and limits

SEES couples failure attribution to targeted RL updates and shares one adapter across related skills. In the controlled tests, both the bottleneck choice and the family sharing improved results. The adaptation experiments are in simulation, and SEES does not eliminate simulation or RL computation. Real-to-sim construction and hardware screening are proposed as future work.
