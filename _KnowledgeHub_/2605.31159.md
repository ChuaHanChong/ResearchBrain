# Trust-Region Behavior Blending for On-Policy Distillation

> [!summary] Summary
> Trust-Region Behavior Blending (TRB) improves on-policy distillation by guiding student model prefix generation towards the teacher's policy during early training through a constrained, annealed blending approach. This method enhances the quality of student-generated rollouts, leading to higher average pass@1 scores on math-reasoning benchmarks and accelerating initial learning compared to vanilla on-policy distillation.

## Problem

- Autoregressive model knowledge distillation faces exposure bias, where training on fixed prefixes conflicts with self-generated sequences during inference.
- While on-policy distillation (OPD) addresses exposure bias by using student-generated prefixes, it struggles in early training stages.
- Nascent student models often produce low-quality or irrelevant prefixes, diluting the effectiveness of teacher supervision and impeding efficient knowledge transfer.

## Method

- Trust-Region behavior Blending (**TRB**) is introduced to control the behavior policy during early OPD prefix collection, specifically for next-token prediction.
- **TRB** formulates a per-prefix behavior policy that minimizes KL divergence to the teacher policy while staying within a defined KL trust region of the student policy.
- This results in a closed-form geometric mean blending of student and teacher policies, where the blending strength (KL budget 'ε') is linearly annealed to zero over a predefined warmup horizon.

## Results

- TRB achieved the highest average pass@1 scores on math benchmarks: **33.2%** for the Qwen3-1.7B student and **44.4%** for the Qwen3-0.6B student, outperforming vanilla OPD and other baselines.
- Early training analysis showed TRB accelerated initial pass@1 score increases, demonstrating faster and more stable learning trajectories compared to vanilla OPD and persistent teacher-guidance methods.
- Prefixes generated by TRB during warmup exhibited lower teacher token-mean entropy and higher inherent quality, indicating more structured and teacher-aligned rollouts that provide better learning contexts.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Proactively guiding the student's rollout behavior towards the teacher during early OPD phases enhances learning stability and accelerates initial performance gains.
> - An annealed warmup schedule for teacher guidance is more effective than persistent application, indicating that strategic, temporary intervention is optimal for balancing exploration and exploitation.
> - The TRB approach offers an efficient local trade-off, achieving a first-order reduction in teacher KL divergence for a second-order student KL divergence cost when intervention budgets are small.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research contributes to the field of knowledge distillation (KD) for large language models (LLMs), specifically addressing challenges within on-policy distillation (OPD). Traditional knowledge distillation methods for autoregressive models often suffer from exposure bias. This bias arises because students are trained on fixed or teacher-provided prefixes but must generate their own sequences during inference, leading to a mismatch between training and inference conditions (Bengio et al., 2015; Agarwal et al., 2024).

On-policy distillation (OPD) was introduced to mitigate this exposure bias by having the student model generate its own prefixes during training, with teacher supervision then applied to these student-generated trajectories (Gu et al., 2023; Agarwal et al., 2024). This approach ensures that the student learns under a distribution of prefixes it actually encounters. However, a limitation of OPD is its potential brittleness in early training stages. When the student model is initially weak, it may generate low-quality or irrelevant prefixes. Teacher supervision applied to such prefixes can be inefficient or even misdirect learning (Xu et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026).

This paper introduces Trust-Region behavior Blending (TRB) to specifically address the issue of weak early student rollouts in OPD. TRB distinguishes itself from other related efforts:
- **Stabilizing teacher supervision methods** (e.g., Veto (Jang et al., 2026), Entropy-Aware OPD (Jin et al., 2026), TIP (Xu et al., 2026)) typically modify the learning signal *after* a prefix has been visited by changing the target distribution or divergence. TRB, in contrast, intervenes *prior* to prefix generation by controlling the behavior policy used for sampling.
- **Methods bridging the student-teacher gap** (e.g., SKD (Xu et al., 2025), MiCoTA (Ding et al., 2025)) might inject teacher tokens or introduce assistant data. TRB's mechanism is distinct, as it optimizes a teacher-guided behavior policy under an explicit student-centered KL constraint, without directly altering the target or injecting tokens after generation.

Therefore, this work positions itself by providing a method that proactively guides the student's rollout behavior towards the teacher during the critical early phases of OPD, thereby improving the quality of the prefixes on which the student subsequently learns.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the effectiveness of on-policy distillation by improving the quality of prefixes generated during the initial stages of student model training.

The motivation for this objective stems from a recognized challenge in existing OPD methodologies. While OPD effectively addresses the prefix mismatch problem by training the student on prefixes sampled from its own policy, this approach can become problematic early in training. A nascent student policy is prone to generating weak, low-quality, or off-topic prefixes. When teacher supervision is applied to these suboptimal prefixes, the learning signal can be diluted or potentially harmful, impeding efficient knowledge transfer from the teacher to the student. Prior work has highlighted that OPD's success is contingent upon whether student-visited trajectories carry usable teacher signals (Li et al., 2026).

The authors propose that direct intervention at the point of prefix generation, specifically by guiding the behavior policy during early rollouts, can lead to a more stable and effective learning process. This intervention aims to steer the student towards generating prefixes that are more aligned with the teacher's expertise, thereby providing a more relevant context for distillation. A crucial aspect of this motivation is to implement this guidance in a controlled manner, preventing the behavior policy from deviating too far from the student's current policy and ensuring that the intervention is temporary, allowing the student to eventually rely on its own policy for rollouts once it has developed sufficient competence.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the proposed methodology is Trust-Region behavior Blending (TRB), designed to control the behavior policy during the collection of rollout prefixes in the early stages of OPD. TRB operates by defining a teacher-guided sampling distribution for next-token prediction at each prefix, while ensuring this distribution remains within a constrained proximity to the student's current policy.

#### 3.1. Per-Prefix Behavior Policy

At each generation prefix `h`, TRB determines a behavior policy `μ*(· | h)`. This policy is formulated to be as close as possible to the teacher policy `π_T(· | h)` while remaining within a specified Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence trust region centered around the student policy `π_S(· | h)`. The optimization problem is defined as:

`μ*(· | h) = arg min_μ D_KL(μ || π_T)`
`s.t. D_KL(μ || π_S) ≤ ε`
`Σ_a μ(a) = 1, μ(a) ≥ 0`

Here, `ε ≥ 0` represents the allowed local deviation from the student policy. The objective minimizes the KL divergence to the teacher, making the behavior policy `μ` more teacher-like, while the constraint `D_KL(μ || π_S) ≤ ε` ensures that the deviation from the student's current policy remains bounded.

#### 3.2. Closed-Form Solution

The optimization problem presented in Section 4.1 has a closed-form solution. The optimal behavior policy `μ_β(a | h)` is expressed as a geometric mean blending of the student and teacher policies:

`μ_β(a | h) = (π_S(a | h)^(1-β) * π_T(a | h)^β) / Z_β(h)`

where `β ∈` is an interpolation coefficient that controls the blending strength, and `Z_β(h)` is a normalization term.
The specific value of `β`, denoted as `β*(h)`, is chosen as the largest feasible value that satisfies the KL trust region constraint `D_KL(μ_β || π_S) ≤ ε`.
- If `ε = 0`, the behavior policy `μ*` defaults to the student policy `π_S`.
- If the teacher policy `π_T` itself is within the trust region (i.e., `D_KL(π_T || π_S) ≤ ε`), then `μ*` becomes `π_T`.
- Otherwise, `β*(h)` is determined through a binary search procedure over the interval ``. This is feasible because the KL divergence `D_KL(μ_β || π_S)` is monotonically nondecreasing with respect to `β`.
The authors also note that for small trust-region budgets `ε`, this blending approach offers an efficient local trade-off, achieving a first-order reduction in teacher KL divergence for a second-order student KL divergence cost.

#### 3.3. Annealed Warmup

TRB is designed as a warmup method, meaning its application is temporary and diminishes over time. The KL budget `ε` is annealed linearly to zero over a predefined warmup horizon `K`. The annealing schedule is given by:

`ε_k = ε_0 * (1 - k/K)` for `k ≤ K`

Here, `ε_0` is the initial KL budget, and `k` is the current training step. This schedule ensures that the behavior policy starts with more teacher guidance (higher `ε_0`) and gradually transitions back to pure student sampling (`ε = 0`) by the end of the warmup phase. This annealing is inspired by concepts from scheduled sampling (Ross et al., 2011; Bengio et al., 2015).

#### 3.4. Preservation of the OPD Objective

A key aspect of TRB is that it exclusively modifies the *behavior policy μ* used for generating prefixes. The per-prefix reverse-KL OPD loss objective remains fixed:

`L_OPD(θ) = E_h~P_μ [D_KL(π_θ(· | h) || π_T(· | h))]`

The only change is that prefixes `h` are sampled from the TRB-guided behavior policy `μ` instead of directly from `π_S` during warmup. The reverse-KL term is estimated on a truncated student top-k support (k=16).

#### 3.5. Computational Considerations

TRB introduces increased training-time cost during the warmup phase. This is because it requires online teacher decoding and concurrent residency of both student and teacher models in memory to construct the blended policy. In vanilla OPD, teacher log-probabilities can be computed in a batched pass after student rollouts. However, this overhead is temporary, as memory usage and computational profile return to standard student-only generation after the warmup period.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of TRB focused on its ability to improve final OPD outcomes compared to vanilla OPD and various alternative baselines, particularly strong or persistent off-policy methods. The study was conducted across two math-reasoning distillation settings: Qwen3-1.7B-Base distilled from Qwen3-8B, and Qwen3-0.6B-Base distilled from Qwen3-4B models. Performance was measured using the pass@1 metric on several math benchmarks, including MATH500, Olympiad, AMC, AIME, and GSM8K.

#### 4.1. Benchmark Comparison (Table 1)

TRB achieved the highest average pass@1 score across both model-pair settings. Specifically, it recorded an average of 33.2% for the Qwen3-1.7B student and 44.4% for the Qwen3-0.6B student. This performance surpassed vanilla OPD (32.3% and 44.0% respectively) and all other compared baselines, including Veto, Interleaved teacher injection (SKD), Temperature warmup, SFT warmup, and Fixed-ε blending. While some baselines demonstrated superior performance in specific benchmark categories, TRB maintained the strongest overall average. The direct comparison with Fixed-ε blending, which uses the same per-prefix solver but without annealing, suggests that the annealed warmup schedule of TRB is more effective than persistent application of teacher guidance.

#### 4.2. Early-Training Comparisons (Figure 2)

Analysis of training trajectories revealed that TRB, along with other teacher-guided interventions (SKD, SFT warmup, Fixed-ε blending), facilitated a faster initial increase in pass@1 scores compared to vanilla OPD. This indicates that early behavior-side guidance can accelerate learning. However, several methods that initiated quickly, such as SFT warmup and persistent fixed-ε blending, did not sustain their initial advantage, failing to achieve as high final scores as TRB. This finding supports the utility of TRB's controlled, annealed guidance approach over constant or more aggressive early interventions.

#### 4.3. Teacher Token-Mean Entropy (Figure 3)

During the warmup phase, TRB-generated prefixes exhibited lower teacher token-mean entropy compared to prefixes from vanilla OPD. This suggests that TRB effectively steers the student towards generating more structured or predictable sequences that align more closely with the teacher's policy. After the warmup period concluded, the teacher token-mean entropy for TRB largely converged with that of vanilla OPD, yet TRB maintained a higher benchmark performance. This indicates that the critical impact of TRB is concentrated during the initial learning phase, shaping the trajectory in a way that yields lasting benefits.

#### 4.4. Early Rollout Quality Analysis (Figure 4, Figure 7, Figure 8)

Further diagnostics explored the quality of early rollouts.
- A controlled step-0 probe (Figure 4) indicated that TRB prefixes led to a higher success rate (defined as correctly solving the problem when continued by either the teacher or student model) across all tested prefix truncation lengths, compared to vanilla-OPD prefixes. This provides evidence that TRB improves the inherent quality of the starting states for learning.
- Figure 7 showed that increasing the TRB trust-region budget `ε` correlated with higher mean teacher log-probability on sampled rollouts and increased mean verifier reward. Concurrently, the AUROC of the teacher-support score (for ranking correct vs. incorrect rollouts) decreased. This suggests that TRB moves rollouts towards states that are intrinsically better and more teacher-aligned, potentially making the distinction between correct and incorrect paths less pronounced from a teacher-support perspective, yet yielding higher quality overall.
- A qualitative example (Figure 8) illustrated that an early pure-student rollout quickly drifted off-topic, whereas a TRB rollout (with `ε = 0.01`) maintained relevance to the problem's arithmetic structure, albeit with some noise. This visual evidence supports the claim that TRB guides the student towards more problem-relevant prefix generation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The findings of this research suggest several significant implications and potential impacts within the field of knowledge distillation and large language model training.

#### 5.1. Enhanced On-Policy Distillation Effectiveness

TRB offers a method to address a critical fragility in on-policy distillation (OPD): the generation of low-quality prefixes by weak student models during early training. By demonstrating improved average performance across diverse math-reasoning benchmarks, TRB suggests that controlled, teacher-guided intervention in the behavior policy during warmup can lead to more stable and effective knowledge transfer. This could broaden the applicability and reliability of OPD as a technique for compressing or accelerating LLMs.

#### 5.2. Efficient and Targeted Teacher Guidance

The success of TRB's annealed warmup schedule, particularly in outperforming persistent blending, indicates that targeted and temporary teacher guidance is more beneficial than continuous intervention. This finding contributes to the understanding of optimal teacher-student interaction dynamics in online learning settings, suggesting that a strategic 'kick-start' can be more impactful than prolonged steering. It highlights that the most effective intervention may occur when the student-visited prefixes are most misaligned with the teacher, gradually returning control as the student matures.

#### 5.3. Contributions to Online Learning Dynamics

The methodology and results provide insights into the dynamics of online learning for LLMs. The analysis of teacher token-mean entropy and early rollout quality demonstrates that TRB effectively shifts the distribution of encountered prefixes towards higher-quality, teacher-aligned states during warmup. This implies that carefully managing the initial data distribution can significantly influence the subsequent learning trajectory and final performance. The theoretical analysis of the trust-region solution, showing a first-order improvement in teacher closeness for a second-order student deviation cost, further grounds the efficiency of this approach for small intervention budgets.

#### 5.4. Practical Implications for LLM Training

While TRB introduces a temporary increase in computational overhead during the warmup phase (due to online teacher decoding and co-residency of models), its demonstrated efficacy in improving final student model performance suggests it could be a valuable tool for practitioners. Given that the overhead is transient, it may be deemed acceptable for achieving superior distillation outcomes. This work encourages exploration of similar transient, behavior-modifying strategies in other online LLM training paradigms, such as reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), where initial policy quality is also a known challenge.

#### 5.5. Limitations Acknowledged by Authors

The authors acknowledge that the study's scope is confined to two specific math-reasoning OPD settings using Qwen3-Base student-teacher pairs and a correctness-based evaluation protocol. Therefore, the direct transferability of the specific warmup schedules to other domains or teacher-student gaps is not claimed. Additionally, the increased training-time cost during warmup is noted as a limitation, though temporary.
