# Self-Corrective Task Planning by Inverse Prompting with Large Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> InversePrompt, developed by researchers at Chung-Ang University and Korea University, introduces a self-corrective task planning approach that enhances the reliability and interpretability of Large Language Model (LLM)-generated robot task plans. This method utilizes a novel 'inverse prompting' strategy to enable LLMs to identify and rectify their own errors through a multi-step, logic-grounded validation process, achieving up to 30% higher success rates in benchmark scenarios and enabling more robust real-world robot task completion.

## Problem

- Large Language Models (LLMs) often generate plausible-sounding but inaccurate or infeasible task plans for robots, which can lead to unsafe operations or task failures.
- Existing external validation methods for LLM-generated robot plans are limited by reliance on predefined rules, requiring extensive human effort, or incurring high computational costs.
- Prior LLM-based self-correction methods for robot planning often suffer from 'naïve' or 'single-step' reasoning, providing vague feedback that fails to guide effective re-planning for complex tasks.

## Method

- InversePrompt introduces an iterative self-correction framework where an LLM generates an initial task plan and then validates each step in a loop.
- The core innovation is 'inverse prompting,' where the LLM is prompted to generate the inverse action for a given step, apply it to the resulting state, and compare the outcome to the original state.
- If a discrepancy is found between the inverted state and the original state, the difference is explicitly used as detailed, multi-step feedback to guide the LLM's re-planning process.

## Results

- InversePrompt consistently achieved higher success rates in benchmark environments, averaging **78%** with GPT-4o-mini and **89%** with Gemini-**1.5**-Flash, representing up to a **30%** improvement over baseline 'No Validator' methods.
- The method demonstrated higher accuracy in the self-correction process, with up to **88%** true positives and true negatives for Gemini-**1.5**-Flash, indicating more reliable error detection compared to standard self-correction.
- Successfully validated in real-world Blocksworld and Cooking scenarios with a Franka Emika Panda robot arm, enabling the robot to autonomously correct infeasible plans and complete tasks where other self-correction methods failed due to vague feedback or undetected errors.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Verifying an action's logical consistency by applying its inverse and checking if the original state is restored proves effective for detecting planning errors.
> - Explicitly quantifying the difference between the inverted state and the original state provides precise, grounded, and actionable feedback for the Large Language Model during re-planning.
> - A multi-step, structured reasoning process, like inverse prompting, significantly improves the LLM's ability to generate accurate and interpretable self-correction feedback.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) into robot task planning is a rapidly evolving and significant area within artificial intelligence and robotics. LLMs have demonstrated remarkable abilities in understanding context, generating complex sequences, and leveraging vast commonsense knowledge, making them attractive for complex, long-horizon robot tasks that require flexible decision-making. Researchers have explored using LLMs for grounding language in robotic affordances, embodied reasoning, and converting natural language instructions into actionable robot plans (e.g., PDDL formulations).

However, a critical challenge in deploying LLMs for real-world robotic applications is their propensity to produce "plausible-sounding but inaccurate or infeasible" outputs. This phenomenon, often referred to as "hallucination," is particularly problematic in robotics, where errors can lead to non-executable plans, unsafe operations, or task failures. The broader research landscape has converged on two main strategies to mitigate these planning errors:

1. **External Validators:** These methods typically involve predefined error sets, rule-based systems, or retrieval of knowledge from external sources (e.g., news articles, Wikipedia, or even other LLMs) to verify the generated plans. While effective for known error types, they are limited by their reliance on fixed rules, requiring substantial human effort for construction and lacking adaptability to novel scenarios. Accessing external knowledge sources can also incur high computational costs.
2. **Self-Correction Methods:** Inspired by human problem-solving, these approaches empower the LLM itself to identify and correct its own mistakes iteratively. Recent works have started applying self-correction to robot task planning. However, the paper argues that existing self-correction methods often rely on "naïve reasoning processes" or "single-step reasoning," lacking the thorough grounding and explicit justifications needed for complex, structured robotic tasks. This can lead to the generation of unhelpful feedback and a higher likelihood of failed corrections, especially in long-horizon planning.

"Self-Corrective Task Planning by Inverse Prompting" directly addresses this gap. It positions itself as an advancement within the self-correction paradigm by introducing "inverse prompting." By incorporating a multi-step reasoning process, explicitly validating logical coherence through "inverse actions," and generating clearer, interpretable feedback, this work aims to significantly improve the reliability and robustness of LLM-based robot task planning. It moves beyond simple error detection to provide deeper, more actionable insights into *why* a plan is faulty, thereby bridging the gap between plausible language generation and executable robotic actions. The paper’s contribution lies in enhancing the reasoning capabilities of LLMs for self-correction in a domain where accuracy and physical feasibility are paramount.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust and interpretable self-corrective task planning approach for robots, leveraging the capabilities of Large Language Models. The authors aim to improve the success rate of LLM-generated robot task plans by enabling the LLM to identify and rectify its errors more effectively.

This objective is driven by several key motivations:
- **Addressing LLM Inaccuracies in Robotics:** While LLMs excel at generating complex language, they frequently produce outputs that sound plausible but are factually or logically incorrect, a significant hurdle for robotic applications where plans must be physically executable and safe.
- **Limitations of Existing Validation Methods:**
 - **External Validators:** These methods, whether rule-based or reliant on external knowledge, are often constrained by the completeness of their predefined error sets, require extensive human effort for construction, and can be computationally expensive or inflexible to new scenarios.
 - **Prior Self-Correction Approaches:** Existing LLM-based self-correction methods for robot planning often suffer from "insufficient reasoning." They typically employ a single-step reasoning process, providing vague or poorly grounded feedback that is not sufficiently explicit or detailed to guide effective re-planning. This can lead to a "plausible-sounding but inaccurate" feedback loop, hindering true error correction, especially in long-horizon tasks.
- **Enhancing Interpretability and Reasoning:** There is a strong motivation to make the LLM's error detection and correction process more transparent and interpretable. By providing clear justifications and structured reasoning steps, the feedback becomes more actionable for the LLM itself during re-planning, leading to more successful task completion. The paper highlights the need for "thorough groundings or steps that support a clear reasoning process" to overcome the shortcomings of prior self-correction methods.
- **Reducing Reliance on External Resources:** The authors aim for a truly self-contained correction mechanism, minimizing the need for additional human effort, predefined error sets, or external computational resources during the iterative planning and refinement process.

In essence, the motivation is to empower LLMs to critically evaluate their own robotic plans, not just superficially, but through a deep, logical consistency check inspired by mathematical inversion, thereby leading to more reliable and autonomously generated executable action sequences for robots.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, **InversePrompt**, is a self-corrective task planning framework that integrates a novel inverse prompting strategy within an iterative planning and refinement loop.

**A. Overall Framework:**

1. **Initial Plan Generation:** Given a natural language task goal, an LLM first translates it into a Planning Domain Definition Language (PDDL) formulation, which includes an initial state ($s_0$) and a goal state ($s_{goal}$). The LLM then generates an initial sequence of task plans $P = \{(s_1, a_1), \dots, (s_T, a_T)\}$, where $a_t$ is an action and $s_t$ is the resulting state.
2. **Iterative Self-Correction:** The core of InversePrompt lies in its iterative self-correction process. Instead of executing the entire plan immediately, each step of the generated plan (action $a_t$ and its resulting state $s_t$) undergoes validation.
 - **Feasibility Evaluation:** The LLM evaluates the feasibility and correctness of the current action and its resulting state.
 - **Feedback Generation:** If an infeasibility or error is detected, the LLM generates specific feedback.
 - **Re-planning:** This feedback is then used in a re-planning phase to refine the original plan.
3. **Repetition:** This self-correction and re-planning loop continues until a feasible action sequence is identified or a predefined maximum number of repetitions is reached.
4. **Real-world Integration:** The framework also accounts for real-world execution. During robot execution, actual observed states are compared with the planned states. Discrepancies serve as further feedback to the planner, allowing for dynamic revision of plans to handle unexpected failures.

**B. Inverse Prompting (The Novelty):**

The key innovation is the "inverse prompting" strategy, designed to enhance the LLM's reasoning capabilities by enabling multi-step justification generation, drawing inspiration from the inversion principle in mathematics (e.g., verifying $x+y=z$ by checking if $z-y=x$).

The inverse prompting process for validating an action $a_t$ that transforms state $s_{t-1}$ to $s_t$ involves three primary steps:

1. **State Verification:** The LLM first verifies the generated state $s_t$ (the outcome of action $a_t$ from $s_{t-1}$). This ensures that the action produced the expected state transitions.
2. **Inverse Action Application:**
 - The LLM is prompted to generate the **inverse action** $(a_t)^{-1}$ corresponding to the executed action $a_t$. The paper notes that in PDDL, many actions have direct inverses (e.g., "pick" and "putdown"; "stack" and "unstack").
 - This inverse action $(a_t)^{-1}$ is then conceptually applied to the *validated* current state $s_t$. This operation yields an "inverted state," denoted as $\hat{s}_{t-1} = s_t + (a_t)^{-1}$.
3. **Original State Confirmation / Difference Analysis:**
 - The crucial step for validation: The LLM compares the *inverted state* ($\hat{s}_{t-1}$) with the *original state* ($s_{t-1}$) from which $a_t$ was executed.
 - If $\hat{s}_{t-1}$ matches $s_{t-1}$, it implies the action $a_t$ was logically consistent and feasible, as its inverse successfully restored the previous state.
 - If $\hat{s}_{t-1}$ *does not* match $s_{t-1}$ (i.e., $s_t + (a_t)^{-1} \neq s_{t-1}$), then an error is detected. The *difference* between $\hat{s}_{t-1}$ and $s_{t-1}$ is explicitly used as detailed feedback for the re-planning phase. This difference provides a clear, grounded explanation of *why* the original action or state was incorrect.

**Example (Fig. 2):** If "pick ball1 room2" was attempted when the robot was not in "room2", the inverse action "drop ball1 room2" from the resulting state would lead to an inverted state where the robot is still in "room2", not its original "room1". The difference (robot-at robot1 room2) & (robot-at robot1 room1) clearly highlights the robot's initial position as the cause of the error.

This multi-step, inverse reasoning process allows the LLM to generate richer, more accurate, and explicitly justified feedback compared to standard prompting, which often provides only a single, less coherent explanation. By explicitly validating the logical flow of plans through inversion, InversePrompt significantly enhances the reliability of the self-correction mechanism.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper presents extensive experimental results demonstrating the superior performance of InversePrompt compared to existing LLM-based task planning methods. The evaluation was conducted across benchmark datasets and validated in real-world robotic environments.

**A. Benchmark Experiments:**

1. **Setup:**
 - **Environments:** Ballmoving, Blocksworld, and Cooking, with varying complexities (N=3 and N=4, representing the number of balls/blocks/pots/ingredients). Each scenario had 20 randomized test cases.
 - **LLMs:** GPT-4o-mini (approx. 8 billion parameters) and Gemini-1.5-Flash (larger, details not public). Different temperature settings were used for translation and refinement to control randomness.
 - **Comparison Methods:**
 - "No Validator": Baseline, single planning stage with LLM.
 - "External Validator": Rule-based system with predefined conditions.
 - "Self-corr. w/o IP": Self-correction using standard prompting.
 - **Evaluation:** Success or failure assessed by a rule-based simulation system. Max 10 refinement steps.

2. **Results:**
 - **Overall Success Rate (Table I):** InversePrompt consistently achieved the highest success rates.
 - For GPT-4o-mini, InversePrompt averaged 78% success, compared to 54% for "No Validator," 70% for "External Validator," and 61% for "Self-corr. w/o IP." This represents a **24% improvement** over the baseline and **8% over external validation**.
 - For Gemini-1.5-Flash, InversePrompt averaged 89% success, compared to 59% for "No Validator," 89% for "External Validator," and 72% for "Self-corr. w/o IP." This represents a **30% improvement** over the baseline and **17% over standard self-correction**.
 - Notably, in scenarios like Ballmoving N=3 (both models) and Blocksworld N=4 (Gemini-1.5-Flash), InversePrompt achieved 100% success.
 - **Self-Correction Process Accuracy (Table II):** InversePrompt demonstrated higher accuracy in correctly identifying successes (true positives) and failures (true negatives) in the generated plans.
 - For GPT-4o-mini, InversePrompt averaged 67% accuracy, 11% higher than "Self-corr. w/o IP" (56%).
 - For Gemini-1.5-Flash, InversePrompt averaged 88% accuracy, 16% higher than "Self-corr. w/o IP" (73%).
 - This higher accuracy confirms that the inverse prompting method significantly improves the LLM's ability to reliably detect its own errors.
 - **Number of Attempts for Correction (Fig. 4):** InversePrompt generally required fewer refinement attempts to reach a successful plan. This indicates that the feedback generated by InversePrompt is more interpretable and accurate, leading to more efficient corrections. In contrast, "Self-corr. w/o IP" sometimes showed fewer attempts but this was often due to its failure to detect errors, leading to early termination with a flawed plan.
 - **Qualitative Feedback (Fig. 2):** The paper provides a crucial qualitative example. Standard prompting generated vague and incorrect feedback ("ball1 is not in room2"). In stark contrast, InversePrompt's multi-step reasoning provided precise, grounded feedback identifying the exact error ("robot1 is in room1, not room2"), which is highly actionable for re-planning.

**B. Real-world Experiments:**

1. **Setup:**
 - **Robot:** 7-DOF Franka Emika Panda robot arm.
 - **Scenarios:** Blocksworld and Cooking, validating the generated action sequence in each environment state.
 - **Comparison:** Against existing methods employing standard prompting ("Self-corr. w/o IP").

2. **Results (Fig. 5):**
 - **Blocksworld:** The initial plan generated an infeasible action "(Unstack B4 B3)".
 - "Self-corr. w/o IP" detected an error but provided unhelpful feedback, leading to another infeasible plan "(Pickup B1)" and ultimately task failure due to collision.
 - InversePrompt provided "clear explanation with detailed justification," leading to a correctly revised action sequence and successful task completion.
 - **Cooking:** The initial plan included an incorrect action "(Pick granola)" when the robot's arm was already holding a mushroom.
 - "Self-corr. w/o IP" failed to detect this error, mistakenly validating it, resulting in the robot executing the action and a collision.
 - InversePrompt provided detailed feedback specifying "the arm is holding a mushroom," allowing for successful re-planning and task completion.

The real-world experiments robustly validate InversePrompt's ability to handle unexpected failures and generate effective corrective feedback in practical robotic scenarios, leading to more successful task executions compared to standard self-correction methods.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Self-Corrective Task Planning by Inverse Prompting" paper makes a significant contribution to the field of LLM-driven robotics by directly addressing one of the most critical challenges: the generation of accurate and executable plans. Its significance and potential impact are multifaceted:

1. **Enhanced LLM Reliability and Trustworthiness:** By enabling LLMs to perform rigorous self-validation through inverse prompting, the work substantially increases the reliability of LLM-generated outputs, particularly in high-stakes applications like robotics where errors have physical consequences. This improved trustworthiness is crucial for wider adoption of LLMs in autonomous systems.
2. **Improved Interpretability and Debugging:** The multi-step reasoning process and the use of difference analysis (between the inverted and original states) provide explicit, grounded justifications for detected errors. This makes the LLM's feedback highly interpretable, not only for the LLM itself during re-planning but also for human developers who need to understand why a plan failed. This interpretability simplifies debugging and refinement of complex robotic policies.
3. **Reduced Human Effort and Cost:** Traditional methods relying on external validators (rule-based or external knowledge) often require significant human expertise to define error rules or retrieve information, and can incur computational costs. InversePrompt's self-contained, iterative correction mechanism reduces this reliance, moving towards more autonomous and cost-effective robot planning systems.
4. **Robustness to Unexpected Failures:** The ability of InversePrompt to iteratively validate each step of a plan against the current state and observed real-world conditions makes it highly robust to unexpected failures during execution. This adaptability is vital for deploying robots in dynamic and unpredictable environments.
5. **Advancement in LLM Reasoning Capabilities:** Beyond robotics, the "inverse prompting" strategy itself is a notable contribution to the broader field of LLM research. It demonstrates a powerful technique for eliciting deeper, more logical, and verifiable reasoning from LLMs, moving beyond superficial plausibility towards structural consistency. This could have implications for other domains requiring logical coherence, such as code generation, mathematical theorem proving, or complex decision-making.
6. **Paving the Way for More Autonomous Robots:** By making LLM-based planning more accurate, robust, and self-correcting, this research brings us closer to truly autonomous robotic systems that can not only generate complex plans but also intelligently identify and recover from their own mistakes without constant human oversight. This is fundamental for applications ranging from industrial automation and logistics to assistive robotics and exploration.

In conclusion, InversePrompt represents a significant step forward in making LLMs more dependable tools for robot task planning. Its innovative use of inverse reasoning directly tackles the "hallucination" problem, offering a practical and effective solution that enhances both performance and safety in real-world robotic deployments.
