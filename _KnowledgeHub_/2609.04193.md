# GIFT: Guided Intermediate Feature Training via Action-Oriented Structural Supervision for Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> GIFT (Guided Intermediate Feature Training) is an architecture-flexible framework that shapes a robot policy's intermediate visual features with three training-time objectives: geometry alignment to a frozen VGGT teacher, object-centric affordance regression, and instruction-conditioned goal-region segmentation. Instantiated in a VLA and two world-action models without changing action generation, it lifts zero-shot LIBERO-Plus success by 4.6-12.6 points and RoboCasa by 8.4-12.6 points.

## Problem

- Action-only supervision in vision-language-action policies rewards background and camera shortcuts, never forcing features to retain metric relations, interaction configurations, or instruction-conditioned targets.
- World-action models optimize future RGB latents for pixel reconstruction, over-representing appearance while omitting the 3D and object-semantic structure that interaction actually requires.
- Prior structured-guidance methods validate single factors within one policy family or predictive interface, leaving unclear whether a common feature-guidance principle transfers across action formulations.

## Method

- **Geometry guidance** distills a frozen ==VGGT== teacher into shallow layer-6 tokens via decomposed direction and log-magnitude losses (==angular plus scale alignment==), discarding the teacher at deployment.
- **Affordance guidance** regresses a 20-D per-slot interaction target, role ID plus anchor-relative object pose plus object-frame end-effector pose and closure, from K learned queries with masked ==Smooth-L1==.
- **Goal guidance** decodes instruction-conditioned binary masks over task-relevant regions with ==BCE== plus ==soft Dice==; the default no-injection design keeps all auxiliary predictions out of the action head.

## Results

- Zero-shot LIBERO-Plus: GIFT-VLA, GIFT-WAM-Fast, GIFT-WAM-IDM reach **79.6%**, **72.6%**, **87.8%**, beating matched baselines by **4.6**, **12.6**, **5.2** points; IDM tops all listed methods.
- RoboCasa averages hit **61.4%**, **83.6%**, **82.3%** (gains of **12.6**, **9.0**, **8.4**), with articulated-object tasks improving **21.3** and **24.6** points for the two WAM variants.
- Removing injection helps everywhere (VLA **76.7** to **79.6** on LIBERO-Plus); real-robot success rises **35.0** to **57.5%** and **52.5** to **87.5%**, with **27.5%** vs **5.0%** under perturbation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Shaping shared intermediate features is sufficient; routing auxiliary predictions into the action head adds inference cost and consistently hurts, especially for semantics-centered VLAs.
> - The three signals specialize predictably: geometry rescues viewpoint shifts, affordance rescues robot-initialization shifts, goal rescues background and sensor-noise shifts, and they compose.
> - Gains concentrate where in-distribution benchmarks are saturated, so near-ceiling scores hide representation deficits that only distribution shift and precision-critical real tasks expose.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

General-purpose robot policies often operate within two primary paradigms: Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies and World-Action Models (WAMs). VLA policies leverage pre-trained vision-language models to transfer semantic understanding to action prediction, while WAMs integrate predictive visual dynamics with control. Both approaches aim for language-conditioned manipulation that can generalize across various tasks.

Despite advancements, a recognized limitation in these models is what the authors term the "action-sufficiency gap." This refers to a mismatch between the visual richness provided by models (e.g., from large pre-trained vision models or pixel-level reconstruction in world models) and the actual utility of this visual information for precise robot control. Intermediate features, while visually rich, may retain task-irrelevant visual redundancy or omit critical physical and task structures, leading to suboptimal performance, particularly under distribution shifts in viewpoints, object layouts, language, appearance, and robot initializations.

Prior work has attempted to bridge this gap through various forms of auxiliary supervision. These include geometry-oriented approaches that inject 3D representations or align features with geometric teachers, and object- or goal-oriented methods that integrate affordance reasoning or reconstruct instruction-relevant regions. Examples include DreamVLA predicting dynamic, spatial, and semantic knowledge, Flex-π supervising RGB, 3D pointmaps, and object-level semantics, and GuidedVLA assigning factors like object, skill, and geometry to specialized decoder heads. However, these methods often focus on specific guidance signals within particular policy families, attention layouts, or predictive interfaces. This limits understanding of whether a common principle for guiding intermediate features could preserve complementary physical and task structures across diverse policy architectures, and whether such structuring alone could improve control without adding auxiliary predictions directly into the action generation process.

GIFT aims to provide a unified framework that addresses this question by focusing on structuring intermediate features with recurring control-relevant information (geometry, affordance, and goals) in an architecture-flexible manner, thus contributing to the generalizability and robustness of robotic manipulation policies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate whether the "action-sufficiency gap" in robot policies can be effectively bridged by guiding the intermediate features of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) and World-Action Models (WAMs) to preserve control-relevant structures. The authors define this gap as the discrepancy between the extensive visual information these models process and the specific utility of that information for precise robot control. They posit that existing end-to-end action supervision or visual prediction objectives often fail to guarantee that intermediate features retain the most crucial information for robotic control, such as metric relations, object-centric interaction configurations, or instruction-conditioned targets.

The core motivation is to develop an architecture-flexible, unified principle for explicitly structuring these intermediate features. The authors organize this guidance around three complementary types of information frequently encountered in robotic manipulation tasks:

1. **Geometry:** Information governing motion feasibility, such as the spatial layout of objects and surfaces. Existing models may prioritize appearance over 3D structure relevant for collision avoidance and path planning.
2. **Affordance:** Encoding instruction-relevant entities and object-centric end-effector configurations. This involves identifying which objects are important for an instruction and understanding the specific ways the robot's end-effector should interact with them (e.g., grasping poses, closure states) relative to the object itself.
3. **Goals:** Grounding instructions in task-relevant action regions within the visual scene. This provides dense image-space localization of where the intended interaction or manipulation should occur.

The key hypothesis is that by translating these structures into explicit training-time constraints on intermediate visual features – through geometry alignment, affordance prediction, and goal-region reconstruction – a policy can learn more functionally structured representations. Furthermore, the research specifically aims to determine if structuring these features alone can improve control without the necessity of injecting auxiliary predictions as additional conditioning signals into the policy's action head at inference time. This "no-injection" design is critical for demonstrating that the benefits arise from improved internal representations rather than simply providing more inputs to the action generator.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

GIFT (Guided Intermediate Feature Training) is presented as an architecture-flexible framework designed to learn intermediate features that are specifically guided to preserve control-relevant structures in robotic manipulation.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation and Overview

A robot policy learns to predict an action chunk $\hat{A}_t$ from intermediate features $Z_t$ and native action conditions $C_t$. GIFT focuses on shaping $Z_t = E_\theta(O_t, l, s_t)$ by attaching three auxiliary training objectives to selected intermediate visual tokens, without modifying the action generator $A_\phi$ or the native action conditions $C_t$ in its default "no-injection" form. Geometry guidance is applied to shallow features (layer $r=6$), while affordance and goal segmentation are applied to semantically richer final-layer features (layer $r=-1$).

#### 3.2. Structured Intermediate Guidance

- **Geometry Guidance:**
 - **Purpose:** To instill knowledge about scene geometry and motion feasibility into intermediate features.
 - **Approach:** A frozen VGGT geometric foundation model acts as a training-time teacher. For a given set of input views, the teacher produces patch features $G_t$. The student's shallow visual tokens $Z^{(r)}_t$ are passed through a lightweight `D_geo` decoder (a two-layer MLP and a scalar scale head) to predict aligned features $\hat{g}_{t,i}$ and log-scales $\hat{\rho}_{t,i}$.
 - **Losses:** Two components are used:
 - **Angular Alignment Loss ($L_{ang}$):** Minimizes the angular difference between the unit directions of the predicted and teacher features, ensuring orientation alignment.
 - **Scale Alignment Loss ($L_{scale}$):** Minimizes the squared difference between predicted log-scales and teacher log-scales, preserving magnitude information.
 - **Benefit:** The geometric teacher is only used during training; at deployment, there is no additional computational cost or modality.

- **Affordance Guidance:**
 - **Purpose:** To encode instruction-conditioned, structured object-end-effector relations.
 - **Approach:** The interaction affordance is defined as a 20-D target $u_{t,k}$ for each entity slot $k$. This target includes a role ID (e.g., padding, end-effector, anchor object), the pose of entity $k$ relative to an "anchor" object, the pose of its associated end-effector relative to entity $k$, and the end-effector closure state. An affordance decoder `D_aff` (summarizing shared visual tokens with $K$ learned queries) predicts these targets from final-layer visual tokens.
 - **Loss:** Element-averaged Smooth L1 distance is used for supervision.
 - **Benefit:** This anchor-relative and object-centric parameterization explicitly links instruction-relevant entities with interaction configurations, reducing dependence on global camera frames.

- **Goal Guidance:**
 - **Purpose:** To provide dense image-space localization by identifying task-relevant action regions.
 - **Approach:** For each input view, a binary target mask $M_t$ is constructed from instruction-relevant manipulated objects or regions. A goal decoder `D_goal` predicts an instruction-specific, low-resolution mask $\hat{M}_t$ from final-layer visual tokens and the language instruction.
 - **Loss:** A combination of binary cross-entropy and soft Dice loss is used.
 - **Benefit:** Complements affordance by identifying *where* interaction should occur in the image.

#### 3.3. Instantiation Across Policy Paradigms

GIFT's objectives are designed to be agnostic to the action parameterization, allowing its application to diverse policy architectures:

- **GIFT-VLA (Semantics-Centered Direct Action Policy):** Builds on StarVLA-OFT with a Qwen3-VL backbone. Geometry guidance is applied to shallow visual tokens. Affordance and goal decoders operate on final-layer visual tokens. An optional BridgeAttention action head is used to support injection analysis, but in the default no-injection mode, affordance and goal tokens are masked from the action head's cross-attention. Action prediction uses direct $\ell_1$ regression.

- **GIFT-WAM-Fast (Direct Action from World-Model Features):** Based on Fast-WAM, which uses a Mixture-of-Transformers architecture with video and action diffusion transformers. GIFT objectives are applied to the current-frame portion of the video stream. The native fast-mode training objective (video denoising and action denoising via flow matching) remains unchanged.

- **GIFT-WAM-IDM (Future-Conditioned Inverse Dynamics):** Based on Fast-WAM-IDM, using the same video and action experts as Fast-WAM but conditioning action generation on a predicted future trajectory. GIFT supervises current-frame tokens in the same layers as GIFT-WAM-Fast. The native objective is a sum of video and action flow-matching losses, where the action term is conditioned on future video features.

#### 3.4. Optional Auxiliary-Feature Injection

The authors investigate two designs:
- **No-Injection (Default):** Auxiliary geometry, affordance, and goal predictions are *excluded* from the action inputs. They only shape the shared intermediate features through backpropagated losses, demonstrating the effect of representation shaping.
- **Injection:** For completeness, an injection variant is implemented where hidden features from the affordance and goal decoders are projected and concatenated with the model-specific action condition $C_t$, exposing them directly to the action generator during training and inference. Geometry is not injected as it's absorbed into the native visual stream.

#### 3.5. Overall Training Objective

The total loss for GIFT is $L_{GIFT} = L_{native} + \lambda_{geo}L_{geo} + \lambda_{aff}L_{aff} + \lambda_{goal}L_{goal}$, where $L_{native}$ is the policy's original action/world model loss, and $\lambda$s are non-negative scalar weights for the auxiliary objectives.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of GIFT involved extensive simulation experiments on LIBERO, LIBERO-Plus, and RoboCasa benchmarks, followed by real-world robotic experiments.

#### 4.1. Simulation Evaluation

- **Performance on LIBERO (In-Distribution Control):**
 - GIFT-VLA achieved 97.9% overall success, improving StarVLA-OFT by 1.3 percentage points.
 - GIFT-WAM-Fast and GIFT-WAM-IDM reached 97.7% and 98.5%, respectively, slightly outperforming their Fast-WAM and Fast-WAM-IDM baselines (by 0.1 and 0.5 points).
 - These results suggest that GIFT's structured intermediate supervision does not compromise the in-distribution control capabilities of the base policies, despite limited headroom on this benchmark.

- **Zero-Shot Robustness on LIBERO-Plus (Distribution Shifts):**
 - GIFT demonstrated substantial gains in zero-shot transfer performance across seven distribution shifts (camera viewpoint, robot initialization, language, lighting, background, noise, layout).
 - GIFT-WAM-IDM achieved the highest overall success rate of 87.8%, surpassing ACoT-VLA by 1.2 points and its baseline Fast-WAM-IDM by 5.2 points. It also showed leading performance under lighting and sensor-noise perturbations.
 - GIFT-VLA reached 79.6%, improving StarVLA-OFT by 4.6 points, and achieved the highest score under background changes.
 - GIFT-WAM-Fast achieved 72.6%, outperforming Fast-WAM by 12.6 points.
 - The consistent improvements across all three policy paradigms under various perturbations indicate that the common representation basis enhances robustness.

- **Performance on RoboCasa (Different Embodiment and Dynamics):**
 - GIFT-WAM-Fast achieved the best average success rate of 83.6%, improving Fast-WAM by 9.0 points. It also led in pick-and-place tasks.
 - GIFT-WAM-IDM reached 82.3%, an 8.4-point gain over Fast-WAM-IDM, and achieved the best results on articulated interaction tasks.
 - GIFT-VLA reached 61.4%, improving StarVLA-OFT by 12.6 points.
 - The pronounced gains, especially on articulated-object tasks for WAM variants, suggest the utility of structured geometry and affordance cues even under richer contact dynamics and different robot embodiments.

#### 4.2. Ablation Study and In-depth Analysis

- **Contribution of Individual Guidance Signals:** Each of the geometry, affordance, and goal guidance signals individually contributed to improving robustness.
 - **Geometry:** Consistent improvements under camera-viewpoint changes (e.g., 10.4, 13.7, 4.4 points for GIFT-VLA, GIFT-WAM-Fast, GIFT-WAM-IDM respectively), suggesting its role in motion feasibility across viewpoint shifts.
 - **Affordance:** Significant gains under robot-initialization shifts (e.g., 3.8, 21.9, 5.0 points), indicating its role in adapting to new robot starting configurations by capturing entity roles and object-centric interactions.
 - **Goal:** Strong improvements under camera, background, and sensor-noise perturbations, suggesting its role in maintaining task relevance despite visual variations.
 - Combining all three signals yielded the best overall success rates across all configurations.

- **Effect of Auxiliary-Feature Injection:**
 - The "no-injection" variants (where auxiliary predictions only supervise intermediate features) generally performed better or comparably than their "injection" counterparts (where predictions are explicitly routed to the action head). For GIFT-VLA, no-injection yielded 79.6% vs 76.7% on LIBERO-Plus, and 61.4% vs 58.6% on RoboCasa. For WAM variants, differences were smaller but still favored no-injection.
 - This indicates that improvements largely stem from shaping the internal representations rather than requiring additional inference-time inputs.

- **Encoded Information and Attention Maps:**
 - Qualitative visualizations confirmed that GIFT's intermediate features preserve coherent scene layout (geometry), recover plausible end-effector configurations anchored to target objects (affordance), and consistently isolate task-relevant regions (goal) even under perturbations.
 - Attention map visualizations showed that GIFT policies maintain stronger and more consistent focus on manipulated objects, target receptacles, and contact-relevant regions throughout rollouts, whereas baselines exhibited more diffuse or irrelevant attention.

- **Failure Cases:** Guidance can become unreliable under severe sensor noise (geometry failure), misidentification of task-relevant objects (affordance failure), or when out-of-distribution visual changes lead to incorrect mask predictions (goal failure). This implies the effectiveness depends on the accuracy of structured intermediate feature preservation under encountered distribution shifts.

#### 4.3. Real-World Experiments

- **Performance under Original Settings:**
 - GIFT-VLA improved StarVLA-OFT from 35.0% to 57.5% average success across four tasks (color-conditioned placement, layer-conditioned placement, articulated-object manipulation, bimanual test-tube insertion).
 - GIFT-WAM-IDM improved Fast-WAM-IDM from 52.5% to 87.5% average success.
 - Notable gains in precision-sensitive tasks like bimanual test-tube insertion.

- **Robustness under Perturbations:**
 - GIFT-VLA and GIFT-WAM-IDM maintained significantly higher success rates (27.5% and 67.5% respectively) compared to baselines (StarVLA-OFT: 5.0%, Fast-WAM-IDM: 15.0%) under progressively stronger unseen lighting, background, object changes, and task-relevant object pose perturbations.
 - GIFT-WAM-IDM achieved 7/10 success on Task 2 (layer-conditioned placement) under both perturbation levels and 8/10, 5/10 on Task 4 (bimanual test-tube insertion) under Level 1 and Level 2 perturbations, respectively.
 - The larger performance margins in real-world perturbed settings, particularly for precision-sensitive tasks, highlight the value of explicit geometry and affordance supervision for robust spatial relations and interaction poses.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presents GIFT as a framework that addresses a fundamental challenge in robotic manipulation: the "action-sufficiency gap," where visually rich features often lack the specific physical and task structure necessary for precise control.

**Key significance points include:**

- **Unified and Architecture-Flexible Principle:** GIFT establishes a reusable principle for structuring intermediate features that is applicable across diverse policy architectures, specifically demonstrated with semantics-centered VLA policies and two variants of WAMs (direct action and inverse-dynamics). This contributes to a more generalized approach for representation learning in robotics, moving beyond model-specific auxiliary interfaces.
- **Enhanced Zero-Shot Robustness:** The consistent and substantial improvements in zero-shot transfer performance across various distribution shifts in simulation (LIBERO-Plus) and under real-world perturbations (lighting, background, layout, object pose changes) indicate that GIFT policies are more robust and generalizable to unseen environments. This directly translates to more reliable robot deployment in varied real-world scenarios.
- **Improved Precision in Manipulation:** The significant gains observed in articulated-object interaction tasks and high-precision bimanual manipulation (both in simulation and real-world) suggest that guiding intermediate features with geometry, affordance, and goal information is particularly beneficial for tasks requiring fine-grained control and understanding of interaction dynamics.
- **Efficiency through Representation Shaping:** The ablation studies confirm that the performance improvements primarily stem from shaping the internal, shared intermediate features themselves, rather than merely injecting auxiliary predictions as additional inputs to the action head. This implies that GIFT enhances the fundamental representation quality for control, potentially simplifying model architecture and reducing inference-time overhead by not requiring auxiliary decoders to be run for action conditioning.
- **Diagnostic Understanding of Control-Relevant Information:** By breaking down guidance into geometry, affordance, and goal, the work provides insights into which types of information are critical for robustness against specific types of distribution shifts (e.g., geometry for camera changes, affordance for robot initialization). This offers a more structured approach to understanding and addressing challenges in robot policy learning.

**Potential impact includes:**

- **More Capable General-Purpose Robots:** By providing a method to learn more functionally structured and robust representations, GIFT can contribute to the development of robot policies that are more capable of performing complex manipulation tasks reliably in dynamic and varied environments.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The framework serves as a foundation for exploring additional forms of guidance for intermediate features, scaling up training with diverse datasets, and improving the robustness of guidance signals under even more severe perceptual distribution shifts.
- **Bridge between Perception and Control:** GIFT acts as a direct interface between abstract visual knowledge (e.g., from VLMs or video models) and the concrete physical requirements of robot control, helping to close the "action-sufficiency gap" by ensuring that learned features are truly actionable.
