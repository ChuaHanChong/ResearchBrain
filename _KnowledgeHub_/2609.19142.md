# PointZero: 3D Point Track Completion for Learning Transferable 3D Dynamics

> [!summary] Summary
> PointZero is a robot-free pre-training objective and model for 3D dynamics: given one RGB-D frame and one to three sparse 3D point trajectories, a diffusion transformer with Perceiver-IO visual compression predicts dense future 3D tracks of every observed point. Trained on 2.9M synthetic deformable, articulated and rigid frames, it beats adapted particle and graph dynamics baselines and, after post-training, improves action-conditioned dynamics prediction and imitation learning.

## Problem

- Scene-specific 3D dynamics models such as graph-based and particle-grid neural dynamics fit one model per object from hundreds of interactions, so they learn no transferable prior across objects.
- Large-scale video and visual pre-training for robotics learns 2D representations or pixel/latent video predictors that cannot explicitly represent metric 3D scene motion.
- Recent scalable 3D world models such as PointWorld need calibrated robot interaction data with action labels, making collection expensive and excluding vast unlabeled web video from the training pool.

## Method

- **3D point track completion** objective: condition on one RGB-D point cloud plus one to three partial 3D trajectories, predict all points' 10-frame futures; labels need only point tracks, no robot actions.
- **PointZero** architecture: a ViT-Base ==diffusion transformer== whose per-point trajectory queries cross-attend to point, partial-track and ==Perceiver-IO==-compressed ==DINOv2== tokens, trained with ==v-prediction flow matching== or ==JiT-style x-prediction==, weighting later timesteps more.
- 2.9M-frame synthetic dataset (FleX cloth, Genesis PartNet-Mobility articulation, Kubric rigid) plus post-training recipes: swap track tokens for end-effector pose tokens with ==LoRA==, or add an action head for imitation learning.

## Results

- On held-out synthetic data every variant beats all baselines on every metric: flow-matching mean-of-10 mean distance error **3.59** vs **9.01 cm** (PGND) deformable, **2.40** vs **8.25 cm** (PTv3) articulated.
- Post-trained on the PGND benchmark, PointZero beats PGND on **4/6** scenes (best-of-10), while from-scratch training trails on nearly all metrics; zero-shot it averages **3.8 cm** error versus **5.2 cm** for PTv3.
- Imitation with 20 demonstrations plus 100 actionless videos: best or tied on **6/7** tasks, losing only Paper (**70%** vs **90%** for 3PoinTr); pre-training lifts simulated average success from **80.5%** to **88.2%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Point tracks can stand in for action labels: conditioning on a few moving points gives action-like supervision, so dynamics pre-training could use any tracked video instead of robot logs.
> - A generic transformer predicting whole trajectories beats grid, graph and particle inductive biases across rigid, articulated and deformable objects, suggesting such structure limits cross-material generality.
> - Sparse conditioning leaves future motion ambiguous, so generative objectives beat regression; but several headline tables report best-of-10 oracles, which overstate single-sample accuracy.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

PointZero addresses a central problem in robotics and embodied artificial intelligence: how to build world models that predict the three-dimensional evolution of objects under interaction. Existing approaches to dynamics learning generally fall into several categories.

One category consists of physics-based simulators, including mass-spring systems, finite-element methods, position-based dynamics, and material-point methods. These methods can represent physical processes explicitly, but their accuracy depends on correctly estimating material properties, physical parameters, and initial states. Such estimation is difficult when observations are incomplete or noisy.

A second category consists of learned, scene-specific dynamics models. Graph neural networks and particle-based models have been used to predict the motion of deformable objects, rigid bodies, and articulated mechanisms. These models often require substantial interaction data collected for a particular object or scene. Their architectures may also rely on assumptions such as manually designed graph structures, local interactions, or predefined material categories. These assumptions can limit transfer across object types.

A third category involves large-scale visual pre-training. Video prediction, masked image modeling, DINO-style representation learning, and related methods have improved visual representation quality and robot learning efficiency. However, many of these systems operate primarily in image or latent-feature space and do not explicitly represent metric three-dimensional motion. Recent 3D world models have begun addressing this gap, but some require calibrated robot-interaction data and action labels, which restricts scalability and prevents the direct use of much web video.

PointZero contributes to this emerging area by proposing a robot-free pre-training objective based on 3D point tracks. Rather than conditioning on a robot action, the model receives a small number of observed point trajectories and predicts the future trajectories of all visible points. The authors argue that this provides action-like information while avoiding embodiment-specific labels. The approach is related to prior work on point-track prediction for manipulation, particularly 3PoinTr, but is positioned more broadly as a general 3D dynamics pre-training method for deformable, articulated, and rigid objects.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

#### 2.1. Primary Objective

The primary objective is to learn a transferable prior over 3D scene dynamics without requiring robot action annotations. Given one RGB-D observation and one to three complete trajectories for selected 3D points, PointZero predicts dense future trajectories for all points in the observed scene.

The task can be represented as follows. An RGB image, depth map, foreground mask, and camera intrinsics are used to construct an initial 3D point cloud. A small set of point tracks supplies information about how part of the scene is moving. The model must infer how the remainder of the scene will evolve over a ten-frame prediction horizon.

#### 2.2. Motivation

The motivation is based on the observation that robot data is costly and difficult to scale. Robot demonstrations require hardware, calibration, embodiment-specific action labels, and often task-specific data collection. In contrast, videos of people or objects interacting are abundant, including potentially large web-based collections. If 3D point trajectories can be estimated from such videos, they could provide supervision for learning dynamics without requiring robot actions.

The proposed objective is also task-agnostic. Training sequences do not need to represent successful task completions. Arbitrary interactions, exploratory movements, and free-form object manipulation can provide useful information about object motion. In addition, point tracks can describe objects with different state dimensionalities. A rigid object may require only a few degrees of freedom, whereas cloth or other deformable materials require a much higher-dimensional representation. A point-based formulation can represent both within a common framework.

The paper further investigates whether the learned representation transfers to two downstream applications: robot-conditioned 3D dynamics prediction and imitation learning. This distinction is important because strong performance on the pre-training task alone would not establish that the model has learned a useful dynamics prior.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Input Representation

The RGB-D observation consists of an RGB image \(I\), depth map \(D\), foreground mask \(M\), and camera intrinsic matrix \(K\). The masked depth map is unprojected into an observed point cloud containing \(N_p\) points. Each point is represented in metric 3D coordinates.

The conditioning input contains \(N_a\) partial point tracks, where the experiments use one, two, or three tracks. Each track provides a 3D coordinate at every time step. The target is a dense trajectory tensor containing the future positions of every observed point.

#### 3.2. PointZero Architecture

PointZero combines point-cloud geometry, partial trajectories, image features, and diffusion-based trajectory generation in a transformer architecture.

For each observed point, the initial 3D position is repeated over the future time steps and embedded with a shared multilayer perceptron. The partial point-track coordinates are separately embedded, with learned time and trajectory-index embeddings added to distinguish different temporal positions and conditioning tracks.

Visual features are extracted from the RGB image using DINOv2. Because dense image features can create a large token sequence, the authors compress them into a smaller set of learned visual tokens with a Perceiver-IO module. This module uses self-attention among latent queries and cross-attention to the DINOv2 feature map.

The prediction component is a diffusion transformer. During training, the future trajectories are perturbed with Gaussian noise, and the transformer receives both the noisy trajectory and the repeated initial positions. Cross-attention keys and values are formed by concatenating the point tokens, partial-track tokens, and visual tokens. Alternating self-attention and cross-attention layers allow the model to combine local point information with global scene and conditioning information.

The reported base model uses a ViT-Base-sized transformer with 768-dimensional tokens, 12 attention heads, and 12 layers. The Perceiver-IO encoder uses four query tokens in the pre-training configuration.

#### 3.3. Training Objectives

The authors compare three objectives.

1. **Direct regression** predicts the future trajectory directly using a weighted squared error. Later time steps receive greater weight to emphasize long-horizon prediction.

2. **Flow matching** constructs a linear interpolation between Gaussian noise and the ground-truth trajectory. The model predicts the velocity required to move from the noisy trajectory to the target.

3. **JiT-style prediction** predicts the denoised trajectory directly and trains the model through the corresponding implied velocity. This avoids using a learned low-dimensional autoencoder for the 3D trajectory representation.

At inference time, the generative variants use four forward-Euler updates. Multiple sampled trajectories can be generated, reflecting the possibility that incomplete observations correspond to multiple physically plausible futures.

#### 3.4. Dataset

The authors create a synthetic dataset containing approximately 2.9 million image frames. It covers three categories:

- **Deformable objects**, including procedurally generated towels, T-shirts, and shorts, simulated with NVIDIA FleX under actions such as folding, lifting, dropping, and pushing.
- **Articulated objects**, obtained from PartNet-Mobility and simulated in Genesis with revolute or prismatic joints.
- **Rigid objects**, simulated with a modified Kubric environment, including collision-based motion and scenes with up to three objects.

Camera locations and intrinsics are randomized, and the data includes RGB-D observations and dense ground-truth point trajectories. The training mixture emphasizes deformable and articulated objects, with an approximate category ratio of 3:3:1.

For real-world zero-shot evaluation, the authors collect 124 interactions involving 14 objects: six articulated, five deformable, and three rigid. FoundationStereo provides depth estimates and CoTracker3 provides point tracking. Human-object contact trajectories are manually initialized and then propagated.

#### 3.5. Post-Training

For action-conditioned dynamics prediction, the partial point-track tokens are replaced with tokens encoding robot end-effector pose, including six-degree-of-freedom pose and gripper state. The model is fine-tuned separately for each scene in the PGND benchmark.

For imitation learning, the point-track conditioning stream is removed and a lightweight action head is added to predict end-effector action trajectories. The model can be jointly supervised using robot actions and predicted point tracks. Experiments use 20 action-labeled demonstrations per task, with an additional 100 actionless videos in the main comparison.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Synthetic Point-Track Completion

On approximately 32,000 held-out synthetic scenes, all PointZero variants outperform the adapted GBND, ParticleFormer, PGND, and Point Transformer v3 baselines across deformable, articulated, and rigid categories.

For example, PointZero-FM-mean-10 obtains mean distance errors of 3.59 cm for deformable objects, 2.40 cm for articulated objects, and 27.26 cm for rigid objects. The corresponding PGND errors are 9.01 cm, 8.70 cm, and 67.58 cm. The rigid-object errors are larger overall, reflecting the difficulty of predicting abrupt collision and contact behavior.

Generative objectives perform better than direct regression. On deformable objects, PointZero-FM-mean-10 obtains an MDE of 3.59 cm compared with 4.08 cm for regression. The authors interpret this as evidence that multiple future trajectories may be plausible given sparse conditioning information. Best-of-ten oracle results are lower still, although these numbers assume access to the ground truth when selecting the best sample and therefore should not be treated as ordinary single-prediction performance.

#### 4.2. Real-World Zero-Shot Evaluation

When trained only on synthetic data and evaluated on unseen real-world objects, PointZero-FM and PointZero-JiT outperform the baselines on 11 of 12 reported category-level metrics. PointZero-FM achieves MDEs of 2.923 cm for deformable objects, 1.720 cm for articulated objects, and 2.322 cm for rigid objects. The results indicate a degree of sim-to-real transfer despite differences between simulated and real scenes.

Qualitatively, the model more closely follows the input point trajectories and better preserves object-specific structure. The paper reports that competing methods sometimes predict nearly zero motion or struggle with articulated motion. PointZero also better maintains cloth surface area and avoids excessive deformation of rigid objects.

In a separate zero-shot evaluation on the PGND benchmark, PointZero-FM achieves an average MDE of approximately 3.8 cm across six scenes, compared with approximately 5.2 cm for the strongest baseline, PTv3. This corresponds to an approximate 26 percent reduction in average MDE.

#### 4.3. Robot-Conditioned Dynamics

After fine-tuning on scene-specific robot end-effector trajectories, PointZero outperforms the PGND baseline on four of six scenes and is competitive on the remaining scenes. For example, on the Bread scene, PointZero-FT obtains an MDE of 1.5 cm compared with 2.0 cm for PGND. On the Rope and Sloth scenes, it obtains 3.3 cm and 3.9 cm, respectively, compared with 3.9 cm and 4.3 cm for PGND.

Training the same architecture from scratch performs worse, particularly on scenes such as Rope and Sloth. This comparison supports the claim that the pre-trained representation, rather than only the transformer architecture, contributes to downstream performance.

#### 4.4. Imitation Learning

PointZero achieves the highest or joint-highest success rate on six of seven manipulation tasks. In simulation, success rates are 99.8 percent for Blockstack, 93.1 percent for Microwave, and 95.9 percent for Glass. In real-world tasks, it achieves 100 percent on Drawer and Cup, 70 percent on Paper, and 90 percent on Sock.

In controlled pre-training ablations using only 20 demonstrations and no additional actionless videos, pre-training increases average simulated success from 80.5 percent to 88.2 percent when downstream point-track supervision is retained. Without downstream point-track supervision, the pre-trained model achieves 80.0 percent compared with 74.1 percent for the scratch model. These results indicate that the proposed objective can improve policy learning even when the final policy is trained with relatively limited robot data.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

PointZero’s principal contribution is conceptual and methodological: it separates large-scale 3D dynamics pre-training from robot action labels. By treating sparse 3D point trajectories as conditioning signals and dense trajectories as targets, the work provides a common representation for rigid, articulated, and deformable motion.

If reliable 3D point tracks can be obtained from ordinary videos, the objective could support the use of much larger and more varied data sources than robot-interaction datasets alone. The reported transfer to robot-conditioned prediction and imitation learning suggests possible benefits for data efficiency, scene adaptation, and cross-object generalization.

However, the conclusions are subject to several limitations. Most pre-training data is synthetic, and the real-world evaluation is relatively small. The dataset does not fully capture clutter, contact forces, friction, hand-object interaction, material diversity, or long-horizon behavior. The model also requires RGB-D observations, foreground segmentation, and partial trajectories, which may be difficult to obtain reliably in unconstrained settings. Finally, several results use best-of-ten sampling oracles, and the downstream evaluations are limited to a small number of tasks and scenes.

Overall, the paper presents 3D point-track completion as a practical intermediate objective between passive video learning and action-conditioned robotics. Its results support further investigation of scalable, robot-free learning of transferable 3D dynamics priors.
