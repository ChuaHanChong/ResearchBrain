# CosmoH2G: A Hand-to-Gripper Transfer Dataset and Baseline Method for Object Manipulation with Complex Spatial Movements

> [!summary] Summary
> CosmoH2G transfers human hand demonstrations from monocular RGB-D video into robot gripper action sequences. A UMI handheld-gripper acquisition pipeline yields 6,189 paired episodes over 1,254 objects rich in rotations and flips. A two-stage diffusion Transformer first predicts start and terminal gripper keyframes, then generates the orientation sequence while translation is optimized from grasp-region alignment, reaching 83.87% simulated success versus 77.42% for the strongest baseline.

## Problem

- Rule-based kinematic retargeting maps hand keypoints to grippers with fixed heuristics that break down as morphological disparity grows during large rotations and flips.
- Trajectory-conditioned methods depend on object-centric 2D or 3D flow tracking, which drifts under rotation-induced occlusion and discards the fine-grained hand-pose dynamics complex tasks need.
- Existing paired human-robot datasets come from cumbersome teleoperation in clean scenes with near-planar pick-and-place, so trained models never learn complex spatial reorientation.

## Method

- **Scalable paired acquisition pipeline**: a ==UMI== handheld gripper mimics each human demo, and off-the-shelf reconstruction, segmentation and pose tracking register 3D hand, object and 6-DoF gripper motion, keeping pairs above 0.9 trajectory similarity.
- **Stage I** trains a ==denoising Transformer== to generate single-frame start and terminal 6-DoF gripper poses from separate ==Point Transformer== hand and object features plus a coarse contact map, using a ==three-keypoint SE(3) action representation==.
- **Stage II** factorizes sequence generation: ==diffusion== learns only orientation conditioned on Stage-I keyframes, while translation initializes from the hand trajectory and is refined by contact, penetration, smoothness and IK objectives.

## Results

- In simulation it reaches **7.53 degree** grasp-orientation error, **83.87%** success, **0.9672** trajectory similarity and **10.27 degree** placement error, beating retrained Track2Act at **10.67 degrees**, **77.42%**, **0.9015** and **12.48 degrees**.
- On the real Galaxea R1 Lite it holds **70.43%** success and **0.9413** trajectory similarity against Track2Act's **60.22%** and **0.8732**; object slip during large rotations explains the sim-to-real drop.
- Ablations: single-stage Denoising Transformer, Diffusion Policy and ACT cap at **64.52%** success; keypoint actions beat quaternion (**72.58%**) and matrix (**69.89%**); removing the contact map still outperforms every baseline.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Factorizing an end-effector sequence into learned orientation plus geometrically optimized translation avoids the compounding drift that afflicts fully learned pose-sequence generation.
> - Predicting sparse keyframes before densifying converts a hard long-horizon mapping into two easier ones; single-stage formulations underperform regardless of backbone choice.
> - Parameterizing gripper pose as three 3D keypoints puts conditioning and prediction in one space, removing cross-domain mapping and beating quaternion or matrix representations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot learning is increasingly leveraging human demonstrations to acquire skills, offering a more cost-effective and scalable alternative to solely robot-collected data. This approach, often referred to as learning from demonstration or imitation learning, addresses the challenges of data scarcity and diversity in robotic tasks. However, a significant limitation within this domain is the disparity between human morphology and robot kinematics. Existing methods for transferring human hand motions to robotic grippers are often restricted to simpler, planar manipulation tasks, such as basic pick-and-place or pushing. These methods struggle with complex spatial movements that are common in human dexterity, including intricate trajectories, rotations, and object flips. Such complex movements are critical for robots to perform advanced manipulation tasks in diverse, cluttered environments.

Current paradigms for human-to-robot transfer generally fall into two categories:
1. **Rule-based retargeting**: These methods rely on hand-crafted kinematic heuristics to map keypoints from human hands to robot end-effectors. While straightforward, they exhibit limitations when faced with substantial morphological differences between human hands and robot grippers, particularly during complex spatial movements where direct mapping becomes inaccurate.
2. **Trajectory-conditioned policies**: These approaches aim to guide robots using embodiment-agnostic, object-centric motion paths extracted from human demonstrations. This can involve optimization-based or learning-based techniques. However, accurately extracting reliable 3D trajectories in cluttered scenes, especially when objects undergo significant reorientation or occlusion, presents a notable challenge. Furthermore, these methods often overlook the fine-grained dynamics and subtle pose adjustments inherent in human hand movements, which are crucial for precise spatial manipulation and collision avoidance.

This research addresses these gaps by proposing a data-driven approach that moves beyond rigid kinematic rules and solely object-centric trajectories. It focuses on incorporating fine-grained human hand-pose guidance to enable the transfer of complex spatial manipulations to robotic grippers, an area where existing methods show performance degradation. The work aims to provide both a specialized dataset and a robust methodology to facilitate advancements in this challenging aspect of robot learning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable robotic grippers to perform complex spatial manipulation tasks by effectively transferring intricate human hand motions. The authors identify several motivations for pursuing this objective:

1. **Addressing Limitations of Existing Transfer Methods**: Current human-to-robot transfer methods are largely confined to simple, planar tasks. They frequently fail to replicate complex spatial movements, such as object rotations or flips, which are ubiquitous in human demonstrations and essential for sophisticated robotic manipulation. This limitation stems from two main issues:
 - **Morphological Disparities**: Hand-crafted kinematic rules often cannot adequately bridge the embodiment gap between a five-fingered human hand and a two-finger robot gripper, especially during complex movements.
 - **Trajectory Extraction Challenges**: Extracting reliable and fine-grained object-centric or gripper-centric trajectories from human videos is difficult in cluttered environments, and such trajectories may not fully encode the nuanced hand dynamics necessary for precise manipulation.

2. **Necessity of Fine-Grained Hand-Pose Guidance**: The paper argues that successful transfer for complex tasks requires more than just object trajectories; it necessitates guidance from fine-grained human hand poses. Such guidance is critical for tasks requiring precise spatial adjustments, like reorienting a gripper to prevent collisions or maintaining functional contact while rotating a tool. Existing methods that rely solely on object-centric trajectories often miss these critical hand-pose dynamics.

3. **Lack of Adequate Datasets**: The absence of large-scale, high-quality paired human hand-gripper datasets featuring complex spatial movements hinders the development of data-driven solutions. Previous paired datasets either use cumbersome teleoperation (limiting scalability and task complexity) or focus on simpler tasks in controlled environments, making them unsuitable for learning complex, in-the-wild human demonstrations.

To address these motivations, the authors propose two key insights:
- **Shift from hand-crafted to data-driven mapping**: Moving away from rigid, explicit kinematic rules towards an implicit, data-driven approach that learns complex correspondences from extensive data.
- **Emphasis on hand-pose guidance**: Advocating for the use of human hand-pose data, specifically hand-gripper paired data, to learn fine-grained pose dynamics, rather than relying solely on trajectory-only or object-centric information.

These insights motivate the development of a specialized dataset and a corresponding learning framework designed to effectively capture and transfer complex spatial manipulation skills.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology of CosmoH2G involves a scalable data acquisition pipeline to create a specialized dataset and a two-stage data-driven framework for hand-to-gripper transfer.

#### 3.1. Data Acquisition and 3D Motion Data Extraction

The authors developed a scalable pipeline to collect paired human hand and robot gripper demonstrations, focusing on complex spatial movements.

- **Collection Protocol**:
 - **Human Hand Manipulations**: Collectors actively varied hand gestures and contact areas on objects, encompassing a wide spectrum of natural grasps beyond simple pinches. Each manipulation episode included complex orientation transformations, such as in-plane rotations and vertical flips, guided by spatial markers to ensure high spatial complexity.
 - **Gripper Imitation**: A handheld gripper, UMI (Universal Manipulation Interface), was used to mimic human hand motions. This device allowed for easier control and operation, enabling the gripper to replicate intricate human hand movements.
 - **Data Recording**: Monocular RGB-D videos were captured from a fixed viewpoint using a depth camera.
 - **Quality Control**: Object poses at the start and end frames were verified for consistency between human and UMI demonstrations; misaligned trials were filtered.

- **Dataset Statistics**: The resulting dataset comprises 6,189 manipulation episodes across 1,254 unique objects, exhibiting significantly higher spatial complexity (quantified by total rotation angle) compared to existing benchmarks. Objects were categorized across five types: Food, Decor, Beauty, Necessities, and Toys, to enhance generalization.

- **3D Motion Data Extraction**: To bypass complex calibration, 3D motion data was directly extracted from the recorded RGB-D videos:
 - **Object Reconstruction**: Object meshes were reconstructed from multi-view images using ReconViaGen.
 - **Registration and Tracking**: FoundationPose++ was employed to register and track objects, hands, and the UMI gripper in 3D scenes using RGB-D frames, mesh models, and initial masks.
 - **Hand Data**: Hand meshes and masks were reconstructed for each sampled frame using Wilor, avoiding tracking due to slight pose variations.
 - **Gripper Data**: UMI manipulation videos were annotated for start/terminal frames, and its 6-DOF pose sequence was registered and tracked, serving as robot action data.
 - **Dataset Pairing**: A 3D trajectory similarity score (exceeding 0.9) was used to ensure spatial alignment between hand and UMI trajectories, resulting in high-quality paired data.

#### 3.2. Problem Formulation

Given a monocular RGB-D video of human demonstration, the goal is to transfer the demonstrated task to a robot by generating a 6-DOF end-effector pose sequence. The input 3D data includes object points (at start/terminal frames) and a hand points sequence. The model generates 6-DOF grasping poses (for start/terminal frames in Stage I) and a full continuous gripper orientation sequence (in Stage II), with the position sequence derived and optimized separately.

#### 3.3. Two-Stage Learning Framework

The proposed framework consists of two stages, designed to mitigate the challenges of learning complex, continuous action sequences directly. Both stages use Transformer-based diffusion models.

- **Stage I: Generating Starting and Terminal Action (Sparse Gripper Keyframes)**:
 - This stage independently predicts single-frame 6-DOF gripper poses for the initial and terminal frames.
 - **Conditioning**: The model is conditioned on hand points, object points, and a contact map (a per-point soft contact likelihood on the object surface, derived from hand-object distance).
 - **Point Features**: Two separate Point Transformer encoders extract global features from object and hand point clouds.
 - **Action Representation**: Gripper pose is parameterized by three keypoints (gripper center, left, and right fingertips), projected into a shared 3D-point space, simplifying learning by avoiding cross-domain mapping.
 - **Model**: A denoising Transformer processes concatenated features and noisy actions, generating clean actions through multi-step denoising.
 - **Loss Function**: Mean Squared Error (MSE) between ground-truth and generated actions.

- **Stage II: Predicting Action Sequences (Full Continuous Sequence Generation)**:
 - This stage aims to generate the full continuous gripper action sequence, focusing on learning orientation.
 - **Decoupled Strategy**:
 - **Orientation Sequence Generation**: A denoising Transformer learns to generate the gripper orientation sequence (represented by relative keypoint positions). It conditions on object points, contact map, the full hand points sequence, and the starting/terminal poses generated by Stage I.
 - **Position Sequence Optimization**: The gripper's central translational trajectory is derived from the hand manipulation sequence, initialized based on the prior that hand and gripper contact regions are spatially aligned. This initial sequence undergoes a three-step optimization:
 1. **Grasping Optimization**: Refines gripper positions at start/terminal frames to ensure stable grasping, penalizing perpendicular distance to contact points, object penetration, and large position corrections.
 2. **Smooth Trajectory Refinement**: Optimizes intermediate frames for trajectory smoothness while staying close to the initialized positions, penalizing abrupt motions.
 3. **Inverse Kinematics (IK) Optimization**: Each gripper pose is optimized via an IK solver to ensure kinematic feasibility, minimizing end-effector residual, joint-limit violations, and regularizing poses to stay close to the initialization.

#### 3.4. Implementation Details

Both stages utilize a Transformer-based diffusion model with Point Transformer encoders producing 256-dimensional features. Training employs AdamW with a fixed learning rate of $10^{-4}$ and a batch size of 32 for 20,000 steps. A cosine schedule is used for the 1000-timestep diffusion process, and DDPM sampling with 500 steps is used for inference.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of CosmoH2G was conducted in both simulation (GalaxeaManipSim with R1 Lite embodiment) and on a real robot (Galaxea R1 Lite), using a test set of 186 unseen cases across 40 novel objects. The focus was on pick-and-place tasks with unseen hand motions.

#### 4.1. Evaluation Metrics

The following metrics were used:
1. **Grasping Orientation Accuracy (GOA)**: Angular deviation between executed and ground truth grasping poses. Lower values indicate higher accuracy.
2. **Trajectory Similarity (TS)**: Normalized Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) distance between the 3D position trajectories of the ground truth and executed paths. Higher values (closer to 1) indicate greater similarity.
3. **Success Rate (SR)**: Percentage of trials where the final object placement is within 10% of the desktop width from the target. Higher values indicate higher success.
4. **Target Orientation Placement Accuracy (TOPA)**: Angular error between the achieved and desired orientations of the manipulated object. Lower values indicate higher precision.

#### 4.2. Quantitative Comparison to Baselines

CosmoH2G was compared against two types of baselines:
- **Optimization-based**: MimicFunc and 3DFlowAction, which calculate and then optimize action sequences based on object transformations or retargeted hand poses.
- **Learning-based**: Im2Flow2Act and Track2Act (retrained on the CosmoH2G dataset), which generate robot actions conditioned on tracked 2D trajectory flows.

**Observations:**
- **Superior Performance**: CosmoH2G consistently outperformed all baselines across all metrics in both simulation and real-robot experiments. The improvements were substantial, especially in trajectory similarity and success rate.
- **Real-Robot vs. Simulation**: Real-robot performance was slightly lower than simulation, primarily attributed to physical factors like object slipping during large rotations or premature object movement before lifting.
- **Baseline Limitations**:
 - MimicFunc and 3DFlowAction suffered from tracking degradation under complex spatial movements and errors from 2D tracking, respectively.
 - Even after fine-tuning on the dataset, Track2Act showed suboptimal predictions, particularly when calculations deviated significantly from desired actions.
 - Rule-based retargeting methods were explicitly noted to fail to grasp objects in the complex tasks.
- **Contact Map Impact**: The paper noted that even without the contact map input, CosmoH2G still outperformed all baselines, indicating that the gains primarily stem from the framework's formulation rather than solely additional input information.

#### 4.3. Ablation Results

Ablation studies validated key components of the CosmoH2G framework in simulation.

- **Single-Stage Versus Two-Stage**:
 - Direct end-to-end learning using Denoising Transformer, Diffusion Policy, or ACT architectures resulted in insufficient accuracy (e.g., SRs of 62.90%, 58.60%, 64.52% respectively, compared to 83.87% for CosmoH2G). This confirmed the necessity of the two-stage framework for precise grasping in complex manipulations.

- **Model Condition**:
 - **Without contact map**: A slight performance drop was observed (SR 80.11% vs. 83.87%, GOA 9.58° vs. 7.53°), indicating that the contact map provides valuable geometric cues.
 - **Without ending action**: A more significant drop in performance, particularly for placement accuracy (TOPA 21.79° vs. 10.27°, SR 76.34% vs. 83.87%), highlighting the importance of the terminal pose constraint for accurate placement orientation.

- **Representation of Robot Action**:
 - Representing actions as Homogeneous Transformation Matrix or Quaternion-Translation pairs led to lower performance (SRs 69.89%, 72.58% respectively, compared to 83.87% for keypoints). The three-keypoint representation, which explicitly denotes contact regions and orientation in 3D-point space, was found to be more effective.

- **Post-optimization**:
 - **Direct (pos + ori)**: End-to-end generation of the full pose sequence without decoupled optimization (SR 81.72%, TOPA 13.23°).
 - **Computed (pos + ori)**: Deriving both position and orientation purely from geometric heuristics and optimization (SR 67.20%, TOPA 26.78°). This variant suffered from inaccurate orientations due to lack of a trustworthy geometric prior and difficulty in optimizing in a high-dimensional pose space.
 - **Ours (gen. ori + opt. pos)**: The proposed strategy of generating orientation and optimizing position performed best (SR 83.87%, TOPA 10.27°). This validated the effectiveness of the decoupled strategy.

#### 4.4. Generalization Analysis

- **Demonstrators**: CosmoH2G generalized well across adult hands of varying scales (SR 83.33% for adult males, 91.67% for adult females), comparable to the original test set. However, performance degraded on children's hands (SR 33.33%), whose scales were outside the training distribution, indicating a boundary to hand-scale generalization.
- **Camera Viewpoints**: Performance remained stable across different camera viewpoints (first-person, egocentric top-down, third-person, exocentric top-down), with SR ranging from 75% to 90%. This insensitivity is attributed to the use of 3D data extracted from videos as input.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The CosmoH2G research presents several significant contributions and potential impacts on the field of robot manipulation and learning from human demonstrations:

1. **Novel Dataset for Complex Manipulation**: The introduction of the CosmoH2G dataset is a key contribution. It is, to the authors' knowledge, the first hand-gripper dataset specifically designed for object manipulations involving complex spatial movements (e.g., rotations, flips), significantly surpassing existing benchmarks in terms of motion complexity. This dataset provides a valuable resource for developing and evaluating advanced robot learning algorithms that require nuanced spatial understanding.

2. **Scalable Data Acquisition Pipeline**: The paper details a scalable paired hand-gripper data acquisition pipeline, featuring a rigorous UMI-based collection protocol and an efficient 3D motion data annotation scheme. This pipeline addresses the challenge of acquiring large-scale, high-quality paired human-robot data, which has historically been a bottleneck for data-driven robot learning approaches. Its efficiency and focus on manipulation complexity could inspire future data collection efforts.

3. **Effective Two-Stage Data-Driven Framework**: The proposed two-stage framework (CosmoH2G) for hand-to-gripper transfer demonstrates a practical and robust design for handling complex manipulations. By decomposing the problem into predicting sparse keyframe poses (Stage I) and then generating the continuous action sequence with a decoupled orientation learning and position optimization strategy (Stage II), the framework mitigates issues like cumulative drift and improves precision. This architectural approach offers a valuable baseline and design pattern for future research in cross-embodiment learning.

4. **Advancement in Complex Spatial Manipulation**: The consistent superior performance of CosmoH2G over traditional baselines in both simulation and real-robot experiments, especially for tasks involving intricate spatial movements, indicates a notable step forward in enabling robots to perform more dexterous and human-like manipulations. This capability is crucial for deploying robots in unstructured and dynamic environments where objects often require complex reorientation.

5. **Foundation for Future Research**: The dataset, pipeline, and algorithm are made public, providing a foundation for further research in data-driven cross-embodiment learning. Future work could build upon this by exploring unified one-stage direct transfer as datasets scale, integrating closed-loop feedback for real-time error correction, and incorporating explicit collision avoidance mechanisms. The work opens avenues for developing more generalizable and robust robotic manipulation skills derived from diverse human demonstrations.
