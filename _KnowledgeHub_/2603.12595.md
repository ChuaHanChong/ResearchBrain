# Swap-guided Preference Learning for Personalized Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Yonsei University and KIST introduced Swap-guided Preference Learning (SPL), a framework designed to resolve posterior collapse in personalized reinforcement learning from human feedback. SPL enhances personalized reward modeling by ensuring informative user-specific latent representations, leading to improved preference prediction accuracy, such as 62.21% on UltraFeedback-P-4 with Llama-3.1-8B, surpassing prior methods.

## Problem

- Current Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) methods often assume a universal reward function, neglecting diverse human preferences and potentially leading to biased AI systems.
- Variational Preference Learning (VPL), designed for personalized alignment, suffers from a previously unreported posterior collapse, rendering user-specific latent variables uninformative.
- Posterior collapse in VPL is driven by encoder instability from noisy human feedback and the decoder's self-sufficiency when processing prompt-response pairs.

## Method

- The Swap-guided Preference Learning (SPL) framework leverages the "==mirroring property==" observed in preference swaps to address posterior collapse; Swap-guided Base Regularization guides the encoder to disentangle directional and background preference information into the latent variable's mean and log-variance.
- Preferential Inverse Autoregressive Flow (P-IAF) is employed to enrich the latent distribution while maintaining disentanglement by conditioning transformations on separated swap-reversal and swap-invariant contexts.
- Adaptive Latent Conditioning allows the reward decoder to dynamically adjust the influence of the user-specific latent on reward prediction, improving robustness.

## Results

- SPL successfully mitigates posterior collapse, achieving high Active Units (70-**97%**) on complex datasets where VPL showed **0%** AU.
- The framework consistently yielded higher preference prediction accuracy, such as **62.21%** on UltraFeedback-P-4 with Llama-3.1-**8B**, surpassing VPL (**57.14%**) and other baselines.
- SPL demonstrates minimal computational and memory overhead compared to VPL, and ablation studies confirmed that all proposed components are essential for preventing collapse and improving accuracy, with robustness to noisy data and sparse preference pairs.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Existing RLHF frameworks often fail to account for diverse human preferences, relying on a single reward function that can lead to biased AI systems.
> - Variational Preference Learning (VPL) is prone to a "posterior collapse" phenomenon, causing user-specific latent variables to become uninformative.
> - A key insight is that successful latent representations of preferences exhibit a "mirroring property" under preference swaps, where the latent mean reverses sign while log-variance remains invariant; disentangling this swap-reversal (directional) and swap-invariant (background) information within the latent space is crucial for mitigating posterior collapse and achieving robust personalization.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) has become a prevalent method for aligning large-scale Artificial Intelligence (AI) systems, particularly Large Language Models (LLMs), with human values. This approach typically involves training a reward model on human comparison data, which then guides a policy optimization process. However, a fundamental limitation in most existing RLHF frameworks is the assumption of a single, universal reward function. This assumption, rooted in models like the Bradley–Terry–Luce (BTL) model, overlooks the inherent diversity of human preferences. Consequently, models trained under this paradigm may exhibit biases, favoring majority preferences and potentially disadvantaging under-represented groups, thereby limiting true personalization and fairness.

The research community has begun to explore "personalized alignment" or "pluralistic alignment" to address this limitation. This emerging field seeks to train AI systems to adapt to individual or group-specific preferences rather than a monolithic human value system. Variational Preference Learning (VPL) represents a leading approach in personalized alignment. VPL extends the traditional RLHF framework by introducing user-specific latent variables, inferred from an individual user's preference data. These latent variables are then decoded into personalized reward functions, enabling AI systems to adapt more flexibly to diverse users without requiring predefined categorization.

This paper identifies and addresses a previously unreported failure mode in VPL: posterior collapse. While posterior collapse is a known issue in Variational Autoencoders (VAEs), its manifestation in preference learning frameworks, particularly VPL, had not been documented. Posterior collapse in VPL causes the user-specific latent variables to become uninformative, effectively ignored by the reward decoder, which subsequently reverts the model to an implicit single-reward paradigm. This undermines the core objective of personalized alignment. This work, Swap-guided Preference Learning (SPL), aims to resolve this posterior collapse, thereby enriching user-specific latent representations and enhancing the personalization capabilities of RLHF systems. By addressing this specific failure mode, SPL advances the state of personalized alignment, enabling more faithful and pluralistic preference modeling in AI systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust framework for personalized alignment in Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) that effectively captures diverse human preferences without succumbing to posterior collapse. This overarching goal is driven by several key motivations:

Firstly, current RLHF methods predominantly rely on a single-reward assumption, meaning they attempt to represent all human preferences with a universal reward function. While mathematically convenient, this assumption fails to acknowledge the pluralistic nature of human values. Human preferences are heterogeneous, varying significantly across individuals or groups. The consequence of this simplification is that AI models can develop systematic biases, often aligning with majority preferences and inadvertently marginalizing minority viewpoints. This reduces fairness and limits the practical applicability of AI systems in diverse user populations. The objective is therefore to move beyond this single-reward paradigm towards personalized alignment, where AI systems can adapt their behavior to individual user preferences.

Secondly, Variational Preference Learning (VPL) has been identified as a promising approach for personalized alignment. VPL attempts to encode user-specific latent variables from preference data, which are then used to predict personalized rewards. However, the authors observed a significant practical limitation in VPL: posterior collapse. This phenomenon, where the encoder's latent variable becomes uninformative and is effectively ignored by the reward decoder, has been previously recognized in Variational Autoencoders (VAEs) but not in preference learning contexts until this work. In VPL, posterior collapse leads to the latent variables failing to capture user-specific information, causing the decoder to predict preferences without relying on these personalized features. The model then defaults to an implicit single-reward model, negating the benefits of personalized alignment and perpetuating the issue of overlooking minority preferences.

The investigation into the nature of posterior collapse in VPL revealed two contributing factors:
1. **Encoder instability:** Noisy and ambiguous human feedback, combined with the inherent difficulty of compressing complex textual preference data into a concise latent embedding, often leads to unstable latent learning. This instability can cause the reward decoder to disregard the latent pathway.
2. **Decoder self-sufficiency:** The reward decoder often receives sufficient information from the complete prompt-response pair alone to maximize the likelihood objective. This capability reduces its reliance on the latent variable, rendering the latent uninformative and leading to its collapse.

To understand how to counteract this collapse, the authors conducted a swap experiment. They inverted preference pairs (swapping chosen and rejected responses) to simulate a user with opposite preferences. In cases where VPL did *not* collapse, the mean of the latent posterior (μ) for the original user and the swapped user exhibited a sign-reversal (μ ≈ −μ_swap), while the log-variance (ℓ) remained approximately invariant (ℓ ≈ ℓ_swap). This "mirroring" property suggested that μ captures swap-reversal information (directional preferences), while ℓ captures swap-invariant information (background context). This disentanglement makes the latent variable crucial for the decoder.

Based on these observations, the key objective is to leverage this structural property of preference data to design a variational framework that explicitly guides the encoder to produce meaningful, disentangled user-specific latents, thereby mitigating posterior collapse and enabling effective personalized reward modeling. This is achieved through the introduction of Swap-guided Preference Learning (SPL) and its three core components: Swap-guided Base Regularization, Preferential Inverse Autoregressive Flow (P-IAF), and Adaptive Latent Conditioning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed Swap-guided Preference Learning (SPL) framework is a variational approach designed to overcome posterior collapse in personalized preference learning by leveraging the structural properties of preference pair data. It consists of three main components: Swap-guided Base Regularization, Preferential Inverse Autoregressive Flow (P-IAF), and Adaptive Latent Conditioning.

#### 3.1. Encoding User Preferences into a Latent

The encoding process in SPL aims to transform user preference data into a user-specific latent variable `z` that faithfully captures personalized characteristics and avoids collapse.

- **Swap-guided Base Regularization:** This component addresses the initial observation that successful latent encoding exhibits a "mirroring" property when preferences are swapped. For a given user `h` with preference dataset `D_h`, the encoder outputs an approximate posterior `q_ψ(z | D_h) = N(μ, ℓ)` (where `ℓ = log σ^2`). A fictitious "swapped" user `h_swap` is constructed by inverting all preferences in `D_h`, yielding `D_h_swap`, from which the encoder outputs `N(μ_swap, ℓ_swap)`. The base regularization loss, `L_guide`, is designed to enforce `μ ≈ -μ_swap` and `ℓ ≈ ℓ_swap`. This is achieved by minimizing `(1/2)(1 + cos(μ_h, μ_h_swap))` to encourage antiparallel means, and `η(1/2)(1 - cos(ℓ_h, ℓ_h_swap))` to encourage parallel log-variances. This loss term explicitly guides the encoder to disentangle swap-reversal (mean) and swap-invariant (log-variance) information, making the base latent variable `z_0` informative.

- **Preferential Inverse Autoregressive Flow (P-IAF):** While the base regularization ensures a disentangled Gaussian `z_0`, a simple Gaussian distribution may not be expressive enough to capture complex, multi-modal user preferences. To enrich the posterior distribution, SPL employs an Inverse Autoregressive Flow (IAF), which applies a sequence of invertible transformations to `z_0` to yield a more complex distribution `z_K`. However, a standard IAF might entangle the disentangled signals from `z_0`. To prevent this, P-IAF is introduced. The key innovation is to decompose the encoder's additional context vector `c` (derived from `D_h`) and `c_swap` (from `D_h_swap`) into:
 - **Swap-reversal context (`c_d`):** `(1/2)(c - c_swap)`. This component primarily captures directional preference signals.
 - **Swap-invariant context (`c_s`):** `(1/2)(c + c_swap)`. This component captures background information.
 The P-IAF then applies transformations `z_k = μ_k(z_{k-1}, c_d) + σ_k(z_{k-1}, c_s) ⊙ z_{k-1}`. By conditioning the shift function `μ_k` only on `c_d` and the scale function `σ_k` only on `c_s`, P-IAF reduces cross-context coupling between swap-reversal and swap-invariant signals. This design helps preserve the mirroring property from the base distribution throughout the flow, ensuring that `z_K` remains expressive while retaining its preference-disentangled structure. Theoretical justification provided in the appendix indicates that P-IAF yields a tighter bound on swap probability error compared to a standard IAF, thereby maintaining the mirroring property effectively.

#### 3.2. Decoding Personalized Rewards from Latents

The reward model `r_ϕ(x, y, z_K)` is designed to score prompt-response pairs `(x, y)` conditioned on the learned user-latent `z_K`.

- **Adaptive Latent Conditioning:** To ensure that the latent variable `z_K` is actively used and its contribution dynamically adjusted, the decoder incorporates an adaptive latent conditioning mechanism, inspired by feature modulation techniques like FiLM. `z_K` is mapped to a scale (`γ`) and shift (`β`) vector. These `γ` and `β` vectors then modulate the prompt-response embeddings `e` within the decoder's layers (e.g., `e' = γ ⊙ e + β`). This allows the reward model to dynamically amplify or attenuate the influence of the latent variable on reward prediction. When `z_K` contains strong, clear preference signals, its contribution is enhanced; when `z_K` is uncertain or less informative, its influence is reduced, allowing the base reward model to dominate. This adaptive mechanism enhances robustness and accelerates training, particularly in data-scarce scenarios.

#### 3.3. Objective Function of SPL

The overall objective function for SPL minimizes a loss `L(ϕ, ψ)` that comprises three terms:
1. **Reconstruction Loss (`L_recon`):** This is the negative log-likelihood of observed preferences, conditional on `z_K`, similar to the standard VPL objective. It ensures that the model accurately predicts human choices.
2. **KL-Divergence Loss (`L_KL`):** This term regularizes the learned posterior `q_ψ(z_K | D_h)` towards a prior distribution `p(z_K)` (typically a standard Gaussian). It prevents overfitting and encourages the latent space to have a generalizable structure.
3. **Guidance Loss (`L_guide`):** As described above, this term applies the swap-guided base regularization to the base posterior `q_ψ(z_0 | D_h)`, with a weighting factor `λ`. This term is critical for mitigating posterior collapse and enforcing the desired mirroring property.

The total objective is formulated as: `L(ϕ, ψ) = -ELBO + λL_guide`, where `ELBO` combines `L_recon` and `L_KL`. By optimizing this objective, SPL aims to learn personalized reward functions `r_ϕ(·, ·, z_K)` that are robustly conditioned on informative, user-specific latent variables, enabling personalized alignment.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted to evaluate SPL focused on two key aspects: its ability to mitigate posterior collapse and its effectiveness in improving preference prediction accuracy.

#### 4.1. Mitigation of Posterior Collapse

- **Quantitative Assessment (Active Units):** The Active Units (AU) metric was used to quantify posterior collapse. AU measures the percentage of latent dimensions exhibiting variability above a predefined threshold across the evaluation set. An AU of 0% indicates complete collapse.
- **Results:** VPL consistently exhibited posterior collapse, particularly on the complex UltraFeedback-P-4 (UF-P-4) dataset and with the Llama-3.1-8B model, showing 0% AU across various KL-divergence weights (β). In contrast, SPL demonstrated no posterior collapse in any of the tested settings, consistently maintaining high AU percentages (e.g., 70-97%), even on challenging datasets like UF-P-4. This indicates that SPL successfully prevents the latent variables from becoming uninformative. Furthermore, SPL was significantly less sensitive to the tuning of `β` compared to VPL, highlighting its stability.
- **Qualitative Assessment (Latent Space Visualization):** UMAP and t-SNE visualizations of the latent embeddings (`z`) on the UF-P dataset demonstrated that SPL produced more compact and distinctly separated clusters for different user preference types compared to VPL. VPL embeddings often collapsed into a single, undifferentiated cluster, making preference types indistinguishable. SPL's ability to maintain clear separation in the latent space confirms that its components (Swap-guided Base Regularization and P-IAF) effectively encode user-specific preferences.

#### 4.2. Preference Prediction Accuracy

- **Performance Comparison:** SPL was compared against baselines including BTL (standard RLHF), DPL (Distributional Preference Learning), and VPL. Experiments were conducted on a simple Pets dataset and complex UF-P datasets (UF-P-2 and UF-P-4) using Llama-3.2-3B and Llama-3.1-8B models.
- **Results:** Across all datasets and models, SPL consistently achieved higher preference-prediction accuracy. For instance, on the UF-P-4 dataset with Llama-3.1-8B, SPL achieved 62.21% accuracy, significantly outperforming VPL (57.14%), DPL (57.66%), and BTL (57.40%). On the Pets dataset, SPL achieved 100% accuracy, while VPL (99.67%) and other baselines were lower. This indicates that SPL not only prevents collapse but also enables the model to make more accurate personalized preference predictions.

#### 4.3. Computational Efficiency

- **Overhead:** Despite its advanced architecture, SPL introduces only minimal computational and memory overhead compared to VPL. Training computation time (samples/s, GPU hour) and peak memory usage for SPL were marginally higher than VPL, indicating that its gains are achieved efficiently.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies and Robustness

- **Effect of Components:** Ablation studies on UF-P-4 with Llama-3.1-8B confirmed the individual contributions of SPL's components. Removing `L_guide` or P-IAF individually, or adaptive latent conditioning, led to reduced accuracy and/or re-emergence of posterior collapse. The combination of `L_guide` and P-IAF showed a substantial increase in accuracy (from 59.10% to 62.14%) and active units (from 11.03% to 94.24%).
- **P-IAF vs. Standard IAF:** Comparisons with VPL-IAF (VPL with standard IAF) and SPL-IAF (SPL with standard IAF instead of P-IAF) demonstrated that while a standard IAF can prevent collapse in some cases, P-IAF's specific conditioning on swap-reversal and swap-invariant contexts is crucial for learning clearly separated and identifiable user-latent embeddings, leading to better accuracy.
- **Robustness to Noise:** Adaptive latent conditioning was shown to be essential for robustness to noisy preference labels. In a setting where 25% of preference pairs were flipped, SPL maintained high accuracy (61.41%) and active units, whereas models without adaptive latent conditioning or `L_guide` experienced significant accuracy drops or collapse.
- **Effect of P-IAF Depth (K):** An optimal P-IAF depth of K=2 was found, providing the best balance between expressivity and performance. Deeper flows (K=4) led to a slight drop in performance, suggesting that excessive complexity can be detrimental.
- **Fewer Preference Pairs:** SPL effectively encoded user preferences even when provided with a limited number of preference pairs (e.g., 2, 3, or 4 pairs), outperforming VPL, which reverted to standard RLHF-like accuracy in such sparse data regimes.

In summary, the main findings establish that SPL successfully mitigates posterior collapse in personalized preference learning, leading to more informative and stable user-specific latent representations. This, in turn, translates into higher preference prediction accuracy across various datasets and models, with minimal computational overhead and improved robustness to noisy feedback.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The significance of Swap-guided Preference Learning (SPL) lies in its direct addressal of a critical limitation in personalized alignment methods for RLHF, namely posterior collapse in Variational Preference Learning (VPL). By providing a robust solution to this problem, SPL makes a substantial contribution to the field, with several potential impacts:

Firstly, SPL significantly advances the practical viability of personalized RLHF. The ability to learn expressive, user-specific latent variables without posterior collapse means that AI systems can genuinely adapt to individual human preferences. This moves beyond the current paradigm of single, universal reward functions that often lead to biased and non-inclusive AI behaviors. The consistent improvement in preference prediction accuracy across diverse and complex datasets suggests that SPL can enable more accurate and nuanced understanding of individual users.

Secondly, the framework's components—Swap-guided Base Regularization, Preferential Inverse Autoregressive Flow (P-IAF), and Adaptive Latent Conditioning—offer novel methodological contributions. The identification and leveraging of the "mirroring property" in preference swaps to guide latent space construction is an innovative approach to ensure that latent variables are informative and disentangled. P-IAF's ability to maintain this disentanglement through complex transformations, and adaptive latent conditioning's role in dynamically adjusting latent influence, are crucial for robust and efficient learning. These techniques could potentially be adapted and applied to other areas of generative modeling and representation learning where disentanglement and personalized conditioning are desired.

Thirdly, the demonstrated robustness of SPL to varying hyperparameters (like KL-divergence weight `β`) and to noisy preference data is highly impactful for real-world applications. Human feedback is inherently noisy and often sparse. A method that can reliably learn personalized preferences under such conditions is essential for deploying personalized AI systems in practical settings, where collecting perfectly clean and extensive feedback from every user is impractical. The effectiveness of SPL even with a few preference pairs further underscores its utility in scenarios with limited user interaction data.

The paper also outlines the broader potential impact beyond reward modeling for LLMs. By explicitly conditioning a policy on user-specific latents (`π_θ(y | x, z_k)`), SPL can enable the training of personalized policies that generate distinct behaviors for different users. This goes beyond simple prompt-based adaptation and allows for deeper, more intrinsic behavioral shifts. This concept can extend to other domains where preferences are difficult to summarize, such as generative models for images or audio, and control settings in robotics, as suggested by the authors.

In terms of social effects, by enabling personalized alignment, SPL has the potential to foster more inclusive and equitable AI systems. Instead of imposing a monolithic "human value" derived from majority preferences, AI can learn to cater to diverse individual needs and values, thereby reducing systematic biases and increasing fairness for under-represented populations. This could lead to AI assistants that are more helpful, relevant, and trustworthy to a wider range of users.

A noted limitation is the current focus on independent, single-turn comparison data. Future work could explore extending the framework to natural, multi-turn dialogue, which would further enhance its real-world applicability and reduce the burden of data collection on users.

Overall, SPL represents a significant step towards creating truly personalized and robust AI systems that can effectively learn from and adapt to the diverse preferences of human users, addressing a fundamental challenge in the development of human-aligned AI.
