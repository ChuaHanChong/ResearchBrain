# Deciding What to Model: Value-Equivalent Sampling for Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> A new reinforcement learning algorithm, Value-Equivalent Sampling for Reinforcement Learning (VSRL), is presented to enable agents to learn "satisficing" models in complex environments by integrating rate-distortion theory with the value equivalence principle. The algorithm provides information-theoretic Bayesian regret bounds that connect model complexity and agent capacity to sub-optimality, achieving an improved regret bound for tabular MDPs that matches the state-space dependence of best-known PSRL bounds.

## Problem

- Traditional RL algorithms aim to learn a perfect environment model for optimal solutions, often assuming agents can fully represent complex dynamics.
- Real-world environments frequently exceed the representational and computational capacity of agents, making perfect modeling infeasible.
- Current methods struggle to efficiently balance model simplicity with achieving sufficiently good, rather than strictly optimal, performance.

## Method

- **VSRL** integrates ==rate-distortion theory== with the ==approximate value equivalence principle== to enable agents to select which parts of an environment to model.
- The algorithm modifies ==Posterior Sampling for Reinforcement Learning (PSRL)== by planning with a lossy, compressed, approximately ==value-equivalent MDP==.
- It employs ==distortion functions==, such as those based on ==Bellman error== or optimal ==Q-function== differences, to quantify the fidelity loss of using a simplified model.

## Results

- VSRL provides general information-theoretic Bayesian regret bounds for finite-horizon, episodic MDPs, accounting for both learning regret and irreducible sub-optimality from model compression.
- For tabular MDPs, utilizing a distortion function based on optimal Q-functions (dQ⋆d_{Q^\star}dQ⋆) leads to an improved Bayesian regret bound of O~(Γ∣S∣∣A∣KH)O(√(\Gamma|S||A|KH))O~(Γ∣S∣∣A∣KH), matching the state-space dependence of best-known PSRL bounds.
- The framework also offers capacity-sensitive performance guarantees, expressing regret bounds in terms of an agent's information acquisition capacity (RRR) and the minimum achievable distortion (D(R)D(R)D(R)).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Perfect environment models are often unnecessary; simpler, value-equivalent surrogate models can be sufficient for effective planning and near-optimal behavior.
> - Rate-distortion theory offers a formal, information-theoretic framework to quantify the trade-off between model simplicity (compression) and acceptable sub-optimality.
> - Optimal Q-functions can act as an information bottleneck, facilitating more efficient model compression and learning when distortion is measured through differences in optimal Q-values.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement learning (RL) is fundamentally challenged by exploration, where an agent must balance utilizing its current knowledge with acquiring new information to achieve optimal long-term performance. Historically, provably-efficient RL algorithms have predominantly addressed exploration through two mechanisms: optimism in the face of uncertainty and posterior sampling. Optimism-based methods initialize with biased value estimates, encouraging visits to all state-action pairs until accurate estimates are formed. Posterior sampling methods, such as Thompson sampling, involve drawing samples from a prior belief over the Markov Decision Process (MDP) and acting optimally, subsequently updating the posterior based on observed experience. While both paradigms have established theoretical foundations for efficient learning, posterior sampling methods have shown favorable properties in both theoretical and practical contexts.

However, existing analyses primarily focus on learning optimal solutions for environments that an agent can fully represent and identify. Real-world RL scenarios often involve computationally-bounded agents interacting with highly complex environments, whose dynamics may exceed the agent's representational capacity. This necessitates a shift from strictly optimal solutions to "satisficing" solutions, which are sufficiently good but potentially sub-optimal. The concept of satisficing has been explored in multi-armed bandit problems, where identifying an $\epsilon$-optimal arm might be more efficient than relentlessly pursuing the absolute optimal arm, especially as the number of arms increases.

This work extends the concept of satisficing from multi-armed bandits to the full RL setting. It builds upon the "value equivalence principle," which suggests that model-based RL agents need not learn a perfect model of the environment if a simpler, "value-equivalent" surrogate model can facilitate effective planning. This principle acknowledges that certain facets of the environment might be irrelevant for achieving a particular behavioral objective. The paper positions itself as augmenting Posterior Sampling for Reinforcement Learning (PSRL) to accommodate these satisficing solutions, leveraging rate-distortion theory—a tool from information theory typically used in data compression—to formalize the notion of a simplified yet useful model. This approach contrasts with prior work that uses rate-distortion theory for state abstraction or policy entropy control by focusing on the total information an agent acquires about the environment model. It also relates to the broader field of information theory and control, where agents operate under limited observational capacity.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to develop a provably-efficient reinforcement learning algorithm that can learn "satisficing" solutions in complex environments where identifying the true, optimal model might be infeasible due to agent limitations. This involves addressing the trade-off between model simplicity (learnability) and bounded sub-optimality.

The motivation for this objective stems from several considerations:
- **Computational Boundedness of Agents:** Real-world agents have finite computational resources and cannot perfectly model overwhelmingly complex environments. An agent's representational capacity may be insufficient to capture all aspects of true environment dynamics.
- **Irrelevant Information in Complex Environments:** Many complex environments contain information that is irrelevant to the agent's task or for achieving near-optimal behavior. For example, in a "multi-resolution MDP" where an environment consists of numerous constituent MDPs with varying reward magnitudes, only a subset of these MDPs might significantly contribute to overall value. Traditional algorithms like PSRL would relentlessly attempt to learn all components, irrespective of their impact, leading to inefficiencies.
- **Time and Resource Constraints:** The pursuit of an exactly optimal model often demands an inordinate amount of time and data. In practical applications, it may be more pragmatic to achieve a satisfactory level of performance efficiently rather than striving for perfect optimality at a prohibitive cost.
- **Formalizing Approximate Value Equivalence:** Prior work introduced the value equivalence principle, demonstrating that simplified models can suffice for effective planning. This paper aims to operationalize an approximate version of this principle by defining a distortion measure that quantifies the fidelity loss when using a surrogate model.
- **Information-Theoretic Guarantees:** The authors aim to provide information-theoretic regret bounds for an algorithm that learns simplified models. Such bounds can characterize performance not just in terms of sub-optimality but also with respect to agent capacity (e.g., how many bits of information an agent can acquire). This provides a framework for understanding the fundamental limits of learning under resource constraints.

The goal is to design an algorithm that, in a data-driven and automated fashion, implicitly identifies which parts of a complex environment are crucial to model (e.g., the "more important" MDPs in the multi-resolution example) to achieve a desired sub-optimality gap or the best possible outcome under agent capacity constraints.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology integrates rate-distortion theory with the value equivalence principle to enable a reinforcement learning agent to decide what to model in complex environments.

**A. Problem Formulation:**
The problem is formulated as a finite-horizon, episodic Markov Decision Process (MDP) where the agent interacts with an unknown true MDP ($M^\star$). The agent maintains a prior distribution over $M^\star$ and aims to minimize Bayesian regret over $K$ episodes. Unlike traditional approaches, the agent does not necessarily aim to learn $M^\star$ perfectly.

**B. Rate-Distortion Theory:**
Rate-distortion theory, a branch of information theory, provides a mathematical framework for lossy data compression. For a given information source $P(X)$ and a distortion function $d(X, Z)$ that quantifies fidelity loss when $Z$ is used in place of $X$, the rate-distortion function $R(D)$ specifies the minimum mutual information $I(X; Z)$ required to achieve an expected distortion $E[d(X, Z)] \le D$. This framework is adapted to model the environment: $M^\star$ is the information source, and a simplified MDP ($\tilde{M}$) is the compressed representation.

**C. The Value Equivalence Principle and Distortion Functions:**
The concept of approximate value equivalence is leveraged to define the distortion function. Two MDPs, $M$ and $\hat{M}$, are considered "value-equivalent" if they induce identical Bellman updates for a given policy class $\Pi$ and value function class $\mathcal{V}$. This work extends this to *approximate* value equivalence, quantified by a distortion function.
Two primary distortion functions are considered:
1. **$d_{\Pi,\mathcal{V}}(M, \hat{M})$:** This measures the supremal squared Bellman error between $M$ and $\hat{M}$ across all states, for policies in $\Pi$ and value functions in $\mathcal{V}$:
 $d_{\Pi,\mathcal{V}}(M, \hat{M}) = \sup_{\pi \in \Pi, V \in \mathcal{V}} ||B_M^\pi V - B_{\hat{M}}^\pi V||_\infty^2$.
 This captures the error in Bellman updates across a range of policies and value functions. As $D \to 0$, this collapses to exact value equivalence.
2. **$d_{Q^\star}(M, \hat{M})$:** This alternative distortion function focuses solely on the optimal action-value functions:
 $d_{Q^\star}(M, \hat{M}) = \sup_{h \in [H]} ||Q_{M,h}^\star - Q_{\hat{M},h}^\star||_\infty^2$.
 This measures the supremal squared difference between optimal Q-functions over the horizon $H$.

The rate-distortion function $R_{\Pi,\mathcal{V},k}(D)$ (or $R_{Q^\star,k}(D)$) is then defined as the minimum mutual information $I_k(M^\star; \tilde{M})$ such that the expected distortion $E_k[d(M^\star, \tilde{M})]$ is bounded by $D$, conditioned on the history $H_k$.

**D. Value-equivalent Sampling for Reinforcement Learning (VSRL) Algorithm:**
The proposed algorithm, VSRL (Algorithm 2), modifies Posterior Sampling for Reinforcement Learning (PSRL). In each episode $k$:
1. It computes an optimal compressed MDP $\tilde{M}_k$ that achieves the rate-distortion limit $R_k(D)$ given the current belief $P(M^\star \in \cdot | H_k)$ and a distortion threshold $D$. This $\tilde{M}_k$ represents an approximately value-equivalent, lossy compression of $M^\star$.
2. It samples $M^\star \sim P(M^\star \in \cdot | H_k)$.
3. It then samples the *compressed* MDP $M_k \sim P(\tilde{M}_k \in \cdot | M^\star = M^\star)$, where this conditional distribution is derived from the rate-distortion optimization.
4. The optimal policy $\pi^{(k)}$ for this *sampled compressed MDP* $M_k$ is computed and executed in the true environment.
5. The observed trajectory $\tau_k$ updates the history $H_{k+1}$, leading to a new posterior $P(M^\star \in \cdot | H_{k+1})$.

The core distinction from PSRL (Algorithm 1) is that VSRL plans with respect to a *lossy compressed model* $M_k$ rather than a sample directly from the posterior over $M^\star$. This allows the agent to forgo learning granular details of $M^\star$ that are deemed irrelevant by the distortion criterion.

**E. Capacity-Sensitive Guarantees:**
The framework also allows for capacity-sensitive performance guarantees. Instead of specifying a distortion threshold $D$, an agent's "capacity" can be defined as a limit $R$ on the number of bits of information it can acquire ($I_k(M^\star; \tilde{M}) \le R$). The inverse of the rate-distortion function, the distortion-rate function $D(R)$, is then used to determine the minimum achievable expected distortion for a given capacity $R$. This enables performance guarantees that directly consider agent resource limitations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides several theoretical results, primarily in the form of Bayesian regret bounds, to characterize the performance of the VSRL algorithm.

**A. Regret Decomposition and Information-Theoretic Bound ($d_{\Pi,\mathcal{V}}$ distortion):**
- **Theorem 1:** This theorem decomposes the Bayesian regret incurred by VSRL. It states that the total regret can be bounded by a sum of two terms: the expected regret incurred while trying to learn the *simplified* MDP $\tilde{M}_k$ (i.e., the difference between the optimal value in $\tilde{M}_k$ and the value achieved by the executed policy in $\tilde{M}_k$), plus a penalty term $2KH\sqrt{D}$. This penalty term quantifies the irreducible sub-optimality accepted by learning an approximately value-equivalent model instead of the true one, directly tied to the distortion threshold $D$.
- **Theorem 2:** This result establishes an information-theoretic bound on the first term of the regret decomposition (the "satisficing regret"). If the information ratio (ratio of squared expected regret to information gained about $\tilde{M}_k$) is uniformly bounded by $\Gamma$, then the sum of expected satisficing regrets is bounded by $\sqrt{\Gamma K R_{\Pi,\mathcal{V},1}(D)}$. Here, $R_{\Pi,\mathcal{V},1}(D)$ is the rate-distortion function at the initial episode.
- **Corollary 1 (Main Result):** Combining Theorems 1 and 2, this corollary provides the primary information-theoretic Bayesian regret bound for VSRL using the $d_{\Pi,\mathcal{V}}$ distortion function:
 $\text{BAYESREGRET}(K, \pi^{(1)}, \dots, \pi^{(K)}) \le \sqrt{\Gamma K R_{\Pi,\mathcal{V},1}(D)} + 2KH\sqrt{D}$.
 This bound holds for any finite-horizon, episodic MDP, without requiring finite state-action spaces.

**B. Properties of the Rate-Distortion Function:**
- **Lemma 1:** This lemma shows a dominance relationship for the rate-distortion function. If policy and value function classes are nested (e.g., $\Pi' \subseteq \Pi$ and $\mathcal{V}' \subseteq \mathcal{V}$), then $R_{\Pi,\mathcal{V},k}(D) \ge R_{\Pi',\mathcal{V}',k}(D)$. This indicates that shrinking the scope of policies and value functions over which value equivalence is assessed allows for more aggressive compression (i.e., fewer bits of information are needed for the same distortion).
- **Lemma 2 (Generalized Fano's Inequality):** This result quantifies the error probability of sampling a compressed MDP $\tilde{M}$ that does not meet the distortion constraint $D$. It shows that to avoid such an error with reasonable probability, the rate-distortion function $R_{\Pi,\mathcal{V},k}(D)$ should be approximately $\log(1/\delta)$, where $\delta$ is related to the probability of an MDP being approximately value-equivalent.

**C. Specialization to Tabular MDPs ($d_{\Pi,\mathcal{V}}$ distortion):**
- **Theorem 3:** For tabular MDPs and with $D=0$ (meaning exact value equivalence is targeted), VSRL with $d_{\Pi,\mathcal{V}}$ distortion has a Bayesian regret bound of $O(|S|\sqrt{\Gamma|A|K})$. This bound depends on the size of the state space ($|S|$), action space ($|A|$), number of episodes ($K$), and the horizon ($H$). This initial bound is comparable to weaker guarantees previously established for PSRL.

**D. Improved Regret with $d_{Q^\star}$ Distortion:**
- **Theorem 4:** When using the $d_{Q^\star}$ distortion function, the regret decomposition is modified:
 $\text{BAYESREGRET}(K, \pi^{(1)}, \dots, \pi^{(K)}) \le E[\sum_{k=1}^K E_k[V_{\tilde{M}_k,1}^\star - V_{\tilde{M}_k,1}^{\pi^{(k)}}]] + 2K(H+1)\sqrt{D}$.
 The penalty term is slightly different compared to Theorem 1.
- **Corollary 2:** This provides the main information-theoretic bound for VSRL using $d_{Q^\star}$:
 $\text{BAYESREGRET}(K, \pi^{(1)}, \dots, \pi^{(K)}) \le \sqrt{\Gamma K R_{Q^\star,1}(D)} + 2K(H+1)\sqrt{D}$.
- **Lemma 3:** This crucial lemma shows that for $D=0$ and with $d_{Q^\star}$ distortion, a Markov chain $M^\star \to Q_{M^\star}^\star \to \tilde{M}_k$ is induced. This means that the optimal action-value functions act as an information bottleneck between the true MDP and its compressed representation.
- **Theorem 5:** Leveraging Lemma 3 and the data-processing inequality, the Bayesian regret bound for tabular MDPs with $D=0$ and $d_{Q^\star}$ distortion is improved to $\tilde{O}(\sqrt{\Gamma|S||A|KH})$. This bound matches the dependence on $|S|$ in the best-known Bayesian regret bounds for PSRL, suggesting that $\Gamma$ could be $O(H^2)$. This implies that focusing on optimal value functions (via $d_{Q^\star}$) can lead to more efficient learning than considering a broader class of Bellman updates.

**E. Capacity-Sensitive Performance Guarantees:**
- **Corollary 3 & 4:** These corollaries translate the regret bounds into a capacity-sensitive form. If an agent has a capacity of $R$ bits of information, the regret bounds are expressed in terms of $R$ and the distortion-rate function $D(R)$. For example, using $d_{\Pi,\mathcal{V}}$, the regret is $\le \sqrt{\Gamma KR} + 2KH\sqrt{D_{\Pi,\mathcal{V},1}(R)}$. This provides guarantees for finding the best model given a limit on agent capacity, rather than a desired sub-optimality.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work "Deciding What to Model: Value-Equivalent Sampling for Reinforcement Learning" offers a principled, information-theoretic framework for addressing the challenges of reinforcement learning in complex, real-world environments with computationally bounded agents.

**A. Theoretical Advancements:**
- **Formalization of Satisficing in RL:** It extends the concept of satisficing from multi-armed bandits to the full RL setting, providing a formal definition grounded in rate-distortion theory and the value equivalence principle. This allows for a structured approach to identifying simplified yet effective models.
- **Information-Theoretic Regret Bounds:** The derivation of information-theoretic Bayesian regret bounds for VSRL is a significant contribution. These bounds are general, holding for any finite-horizon, episodic MDP, and are not restricted to tabular settings. This contrasts with many prior works that focus on discrete state-action spaces.
- **Twofold Performance Guarantees:** The framework offers two perspectives on performance guarantees:
 1. **Bounded Sub-optimality:** For a given desired sub-optimality gap (distortion $D$), VSRL can identify the simplest environment compression that achieves this performance.
 2. **Bounded Capacity:** Given an agent's information-acquisition capacity ($R$), VSRL can find the best possible environment compression within that capacity. These capacity-sensitive guarantees are particularly relevant for real-world applications where computational resources are limited.
- **Insights into Model Simplification:** The work clarifies how different choices of distortion functions (e.g., $d_{\Pi,\mathcal{V}}$ vs. $d_{Q^\star}$) and policy/value function classes impact the achievable compression and learning efficiency, linking model complexity to performance. The result showing the optimal Q-functions as an information bottleneck highlights a specific mechanism for efficient model learning.

**B. Potential Impact:**
- **Foundation for Resource-Constrained RL:** The framework provides a theoretical underpinning for designing RL agents that are explicitly aware of their computational limitations. This could lead to more robust and deployable agents in scenarios with vast, irrelevant state spaces or limited training data.
- **Guiding Representation Learning:** The ideas presented, particularly the use of rate-distortion theory to compress environment models, could inform the development of more principled representation learning techniques in deep RL. Current deep RL often learns latent state representations; this work suggests a theoretical lens through which these representations could be optimized for value-preserving information.
- **Informing Practical Algorithms:** While VSRL is theoretical, its insights could influence the design of practical algorithms. For instance, it could guide exploration strategies in model-based deep RL methods like MuZero, which already optimize value-equivalent loss functions, by providing a formal mechanism for deciding *what* aspects of the environment model are important to learn.
- **Understanding Multi-Task/Contextual RL:** The connections drawn to meta-RL and contextual MDPs suggest that the principles of lossy compression could be relevant for learning efficient, task-specific models during meta-exploration, especially when combined with probabilistic inference for latent variable models.

**C. Limitations and Future Work (as identified by the authors):**
- **Practical Instantiation:** The primary challenge is translating VSRL into a practical, scalable algorithm for high-dimensional settings. This involves overcoming barriers related to representing epistemic uncertainty (e.g., in deep Bayesian RL), efficiently computing the rate-distortion function (especially for continuous state spaces), and synthesizing optimal policies for sampled compressed MDPs in complex environments.
- **State Abstraction:** The paper proposes an extension, "Compressed-VSRL," to explicitly incorporate state abstraction, which could allow for even greater compression and address the complexity of infinite state spaces by learning abstract models. This area is noted as a subject for future theoretical and practical investigation.
- **Information Ratio Bounds:** The regret bounds depend on a uniform upper bound for the information ratio ($\Gamma$), whose exact form for the RL setting is left to future work, although a connection to $H^2$ is suggested for tabular MDPs.

In summary, this paper contributes a rigorous theoretical framework that re-evaluates the objectives of reinforcement learning, shifting focus from perfect environment modeling to learning sufficiently accurate and compressed representations tailored to agent capabilities and performance requirements. Its significance lies in providing a foundational perspective for the design of efficient and resource-aware RL systems.
