# GaP: A Graph-as-Policy Multi-Agent Self-Learning Harness For Variational Automation Tasks

> [!summary] Summary
> GaP (Graph-as-Policy) is a multi-agent coding harness that turns a natural-language Variational Automation task into a directed computation graph of perception, planning and control nodes drawn from a 51-skill Modular Open Robot Skill Library, then rehearses and refines that graph in physics simulation before edge deployment. Across 8 new benchmarks it reaches 0.93-0.99 success, far above VLA and TAMP baselines.

## Problem

- Model-free VLA policies collapse under object pose variation, falling from 0.96 on fixed-pose LIBERO to roughly 0.20 once target and container positions are perturbed.
- Single-agent Code-as-Policy systems emit free-form Python, so growing context windows invite hallucinated skills, constraint violations, and cheating through trivially easy success metrics.
- Classical TAMP pipelines cannot find feasible plans for cubic objects, tall baskets, or varied orientations, while fixed automation still demands costly per-task human engineering.

## Method

- **MORSL**, a Modular Open Robot Skill Library of 51 skills spanning ==GraspGen==, ==cuRobo==, ==SAM2== and ==Grounding DINO==, each declaring typed inputs, outputs and preconditions under agentic tool-use conventions.
- An **Orchestration Agent** partitions the task into semantic segments; **Skill Agents** synthesize local subgraphs of atomic nodes, which the orchestrator wires and statically validates into one executable ==computation graph==.
- ==Rehearsal-based graph optimization== samples N instances from the ==belief space==, rolls them out in parallel simulation, isolates geometric failure causes, then has agents swap nodes, rewire edges and retune parameters.

## Results

- Over **5500** simulated grocery trials GaP scores **0.93-0.99** on every pose variation, against **0.20** for both VLA baselines and **0.24** for the TAMP baseline on mixed-all.
- Real robot runs give **25/25** order fulfillment, **28/30** packing and **18/20** popcorn versus **8/25**, **10/30** and **0/20** for TAMP, at **67s** versus **95s** per pick-and-place.
- Self-learning raised Make Popcorn from **33%** to **94%** in sim over **10** iterations; removing the graph structure or the multi-agent split drops success to zero.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Structured graph output beats free-form code generation: typed nodes plus static edge validation are what stop multi-agent LLM programs from crashing at runtime.
> - Bounding the task class pays more than model scale; a known workcell and pose distribution let simulation rehearsal substitute for demonstration data.
> - Wrapping a VLA in a classical alignment graph more than doubles its success, implying failures stem from out-of-distribution framing rather than missing manipulation skill.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is positioned within the field of robotic automation, specifically addressing a class of tasks termed "Variational Automation (VA)." The authors define VA as tasks where robots persistently perform varying instances with non-trivial variations in object geometry and pose, differentiating it from "fixed automation" (identical tasks with minimal variation) and "generalist robotics" (broad variety of tasks in highly unstructured environments).

Prior research in robot learning often focuses on generalist robotics, utilizing model-free end-to-end Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. While these models have advanced, they frequently struggle to achieve the high levels of reliability required for commercial and industrial applications, particularly for persistent tasks. Conversely, fixed automation, which achieves high reliability and throughput, necessitates substantial human effort for setup and tuning, an effort that escalates with increasing task variation.

The paper integrates recent advancements in Large Language Models (LLM) and Vision-Language Models (VLM) for "agentic coding," which involves LLMs generating robot control code. Earlier approaches, such as Code-as-Policy (CaP), have explored this paradigm, but often suffer from limitations like unstructured code generation, context window overflow in complex tasks, hallucinations, and "cheating" by agents. Multi-agent coding systems can exacerbate these issues.

GaP draws inspiration from established robotic control architectures like Task and Motion Planning (TAMP) systems, which use graphical hierarchies for safety, and the Robot Operating System (ROS), which is built on a computation graph structure. These traditional modular approaches emphasize reusability and composability. GaP aims to bridge the gap between model-based interpretable programming and model-free adaptability by imposing a structured, graph-based policy representation on agentic coding, thereby enhancing reliability and interpretability for VA tasks. This structured approach aims to mitigate the challenges observed in less constrained agentic coding methods and improve the applicability of robot learning to specialized, yet variable, automation scenarios.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a system that enables robots to reliably perform "Variational Automation" (VA) tasks in commercial and industrial settings. VA tasks are characterized by requiring persistent execution with significant variations in object geometry and pose, distinguishing them from fixed automation, which involves repetitive identical motions, and generalist robotics, which focuses on diverse tasks in highly unstructured environments.

The motivation for GaP stems from several identified limitations in the current state of robotic automation and learning:
1. **Reliability Gap in Model-Free Policies for VA Tasks:** While model-free Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies have shown capabilities in generalist robotics, they frequently fail to achieve the consistent reliability necessary for persistent execution in commercial and industrial VA applications. These applications demand high success rates and throughput, which current generalist models often cannot provide consistently when faced with object variations.
2. **High Human Effort in Fixed Automation:** Traditional fixed automation, while reliable, requires substantial human expertise and time for setup and tuning. This effort becomes even more pronounced and costly when configuring systems for VA tasks that involve variations, as each instance might require careful human intervention or re-programming. The goal is to reduce this human effort.
3. **Challenges in Agentic Coding Systems:** Recent advancements in Large Language Models (LLMs) and Vision-Language Models (VLMs) have enabled agentic coding for robotics, where LLMs generate robot control code. However, these systems, particularly unstructured single-agent approaches like earlier Code-as-Policy (CaP) implementations, face issues such as:
 - **Hallucinations:** Agents generating non-existent skills or incorrect code.
 - **Constraint Violations:** Difficulty in adhering to complex operational constraints.
 - **Context Window Limitations:** Struggles with managing large context windows for complex tasks, leading to loss of coherence.
 - **"Cheating":** Agents creating trivial success metrics or artificial solutions.
 The motivation is to develop an effective "harness" to steer these coding agents to produce robust and reliable robot control systems.
4. **Integration of Paradigms:** The research is motivated by the potential to combine the interpretable, structured programming of traditional model-based robotics (like Task and Motion Planning and ROS) with the open-world adaptability of model-free policies and the code-generation capabilities of LLMs. GaP aims to harness the strengths of each paradigm to create a system that is both flexible and reliable.

By introducing a graph-structured policy and a multi-agent self-learning harness, GaP aims to address these motivations by providing a modular, interpretable, and iteratively refinable framework for robust robot control in variational automation settings.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

GaP proposes a novel architecture that represents robot policies as directed computation graphs, generated and refined through a multi-agent self-learning harness. The methodology involves defining the VA task class, establishing a modular skill library, and employing a simulation-based self-learning process.

#### 3.1. Variational Automation (VA) Task Class Formulation

A VA Task `T` is formalized as a tuple `⟨L, E, R, O, X, B, J⟩`:
- `L` (Language Space): Natural language instructions for task decomposition.
- `E` (Environment): Known, stationary workspace with a world frame and occupancy map.
- `R` (Robot and Sensor Configuration): Robot URDF, joint limits, gripper, camera specifications.
- `O` (Object Set): Possible objects, including rigid objects with 3D models and articulated entities with joint limits.
- `X` (State Space): Product space of robot configurations, object poses, and articulated entity states.
- `B` (Belief Space): Distribution of object poses and states, sampled as `x_i ∼ p(x | X)`. This introduces variations (e.g., uniform sampling over volumes, empirical distributions).
- `J` (Reward Function): Multi-objective function `J = w_s · I(success) + w_t · Φ`, where `I(·)` is a success indicator and `Φ` is throughput (success rate / cycle time), weighted by `w_s` and `w_t`.
The goal is to synthesize an execution graph `G*` that maximizes `E_{x_i∼B}[J(π(a | I, G))]` for any task instance `τ_i = ⟨o_i, x_i⟩`.

#### 3.2. Policy Representation via Directed Execution Graphs

A robot policy `π(a | x, T)` is represented as a directed computation graph `G = (V, E)`.
- **Nodes (V):** Each node `n ∈ V` is a functional primitive (manipulation, perception, code segment) with a typed input/output signature. Nodes are grouped into "skills," which are natural-language specifications instructing an LLM agent on how to configure and compose atomic nodes.
- **Edges (E):** Directed edges `e = (n_i, n_j)` represent data flow and execution logic dependencies. Data edges route outputs to inputs; control edges are conditional branches.

#### 3.3. GaP System Architecture (Multi-Agent Harness)

The architecture operates in two main phases: graph generation and self-learning.
- **Graph Generation:**
 - **Orchestration Agent:** Given a VA task description, it partitions the high-level objective into semantic segments (e.g., "turn on the knob").
 - **Skill Agents:** Instructed by the orchestrator, these agents synthesize localized, functional subgraphs for their assigned segments, drawing from the Modular Open Robot Skill Library (MORSL).
 - **Aggregation:** The orchestrator combines and wires these subgraphs into an initial executable computation graph `G`.
- **Modular Open Robot Skill Library (MORSL):**
 - A collection of 51 initial atomic and composite skills covering various robotic functionalities.
 - **Perception:** Includes models like SAM2/3, Grounding DINO, OWL-ViT, Molmo, general-purpose VLMs (15 skills).
 - **Grasp Planning:** Incorporates Contact GraspNet, GraspGen, M2T2 (5 skills).
 - **Motion Planning:** Utilizes cuRobo and cuRobov2 (8 skills).
 - **Vision Utilities:** 2D and 3D utilities with NumPy and OpenCV (15 skills).
 - **Control Primitives:** Cartesian Linear Motion Planning, ROS Translator, Visuomotor Interactive Perception Policies (8 skills).
 - Skills declare inputs, outputs, semantic parameters, and pre-conditions using agentic tool-use conventions (e.g., Anthropic's Skill.md), enabling agents to invoke and wire them.

#### 3.4. Self-Learning Through Internal Simulation Rehearsal (Algorithm 1)

This iterative process refines the computation graph using simulation feedback.
1. **Graph Initialization:** An initial graph `G_0` is generated by the multi-agent harness based on the task language. A simulation environment is built.
2. **Scene Variational Sampling (Step 1):** For each iteration, `N` parallel task instances (`{ˆs_i}^N_{i=1}`) are sampled from the belief space `B`, introducing variations in object poses and configurations.
3. **Parallel Rehearsal (Step 2):** Each sampled task instance `ˆs_i` is executed using the current graph `G_{j-1}` in an internal simulator (NVIDIA Isaac Sim). The simulator renders visualizations for perception nodes, simulates physics, and computes contacts.
 - **Failure Analysis:** If a rollout fails, GaP analyzes physical execution data (robot and object states before/after each node) to identify geometric root causes of failure (e.g., gripper not in contact, object outside target area). This analysis generates structured feedback `F_i`.
4. **Graph Refinement (Step 3):** The aggregated failure feedback (`{F_1, ..., F_N}`) is provided to the LLM agents in a "Graph Update" step. Agents iteratively modify the graph architecture (e.g., swapping functionally equivalent nodes, changing edges, updating code parameters) to improve success rates and throughput.
5. **Iteration:** This loop continues for `M` iterations or until performance plateaus.
The optimized graph `G*` is then deployed to an edge-based interpreter for persistent execution on a physical robot without the agents.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates GaP against baselines across 8 new Variational Automation (VA) benchmarks, comprising 4 simulation and 4 real-world tasks.

#### 4.1. Variational Automation Benchmarks

The benchmarks include six inspired by LIBERO for kitchen tasks:
- (I-a,b) Fulfill Grocery Orders (Sim and Real): Locating and placing a target item into a basket, with variations in X-Y position, basket swap, item permutation, and mixed combinations.
- (II-a,b) Pack Grocery Items (Sim and Real): Picking 6 objects and placing them into a container.
- (III-a,b) Make Popcorn (Sim and Real): Sequential manipulation (turn on stove, pick pan, place on stove, remove pan, turn off stove).
The remaining two are for datacenter and industrial applications:
- (IV) Insert USB-C Cables (Real): Inserting/extracting cables in 6 sockets with variations in port pose and goal conditions.
- (V) Wash Crates (Sim): Cooperative bimanual grasping, lifting, flipping, and placing a crate.

#### 4.2. Benchmarks I and II: Fulfill Grocery Orders and Pack Grocery Items (Simulation & Real)

- **Simulation Results (Table 1):** GaP consistently achieved high success rates (0.93–0.99) across all positional and permutation variations for both grocery tasks, demonstrating robust positional generalization. In contrast, baseline VLA models (π0.5, MolmoAct2), while performing well on fixed LIBERO tasks (0.96–0.97), saw significant performance drops (as low as 0.10–0.26) under positional variance. TipTop also struggled with motion planning for many instances.
- **VLA Policy Enhancement:** When GaP was used to guide the robot's wrist camera to a pre-grasp pose above the target object before handing over to VLA policies, the success rates of π0.5 and MolmoAct2 improved substantially (e.g., MolmoAct2 with GaP achieved 0.66 for "mixed all" compared to 0.20 for MolmoAct2 alone), suggesting GaP can enhance the robustness of existing VLA models.
- **Real-World Results (Table 2):** GaP demonstrated high transferability and reliability in physical experiments, achieving a perfect 25/25 success rate for Fulfill Grocery Orders and 28/30 for Pack Grocery Items. TipTop, again, performed poorly (8/25 and 10/30) due to motion planning failures for varied object orientations and complex geometries. GaP exhibited practical execution times, e.g., 14.1 seconds for parallel perception and 36.4 seconds for grasping and transport.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies

- **Graphless Generation:** Replacing the structured graph output with a single LLM generating raw Python scripts resulted in a zero success rate due to inevitable syntax and interface errors, even when high-level logic was correct.
- **Single LLM Agent:** Condensing GaP’s specialized authoring agents into a single LLM also led to a zero success rate, with all trials failing static structural verification. A single LLM struggled to manage global data flow, local logic, and verification simultaneously, often introducing errors and failing to converge even with iterative feedback.
- **Graph Validation:** GaP's internal graph validation mechanism was found to be critical for ensuring graph edge connectivity and preventing runtime crashes by verifying wiring and type checks.

#### 4.4. Benchmark III: Make Popcorn (Simulation & Real)

- **Initial Performance:** The graph generated initially had a 33% success rate, mainly failing at the pan pick-and-place sub-task.
- **Self-Learning Impact:** Through 10 iterations of self-learning (Figure 2), GaP iteratively refined the graph. This involved:
 1. Replacing an initial grasp skill with a more robust one combining GraspGen with an oriented bounding box planner.
 2. Adjusting perception prompts to specifically localize the pan handle.
 3. Finetuning the offset for pan placement on the stove surface.
- **Final Performance:** Self-learning boosted the success rate to 94% in simulation and 90% (18/20) in real-world trials, with physical failures attributed to IK errors or accumulated kinematic error.

#### 4.5. Benchmark IV: Insert Cables (Real)

- GaP leveraged a computation graph composed of modular ROS execution nodes (align, touch, insert, extract).
- Achieved a 0.93 success rate (121/130) across varied port poses (5 cm and 15° increments) and different text-prompted goals (individual, ascending/descending, odd/even indexed ports).
- This demonstrates GaP's capability to integrate with and orchestrate classical ROS-based robot functionalities for robust, accurate completion of complex, long-horizon tasks with real-world variations.

#### 4.6. Benchmark V: Wash Crates (Simulation)

- GaP’s performance closely matched an expert hand-engineered solution for cooperative bimanual crate handling.
- GaP achieved a 0.953 success rate (143/150) and a sustained throughput of 18 successes per hour over a 3-hour continuous run, compared to the hand-engineered solution’s 0.987 success rate and 19 successes per hour (Table 4).
- This suggests that GaP can autonomously generate bimanual policies with reliability and throughput comparable to manually tuned systems.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The GaP framework introduces a structured approach to agentic robotics, aiming to bridge the gap between traditional robot engineering (Good Old Fashioned Engineering, or GOFE) and modern model-free Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies. The significance and potential impact of this work can be summarized as follows:

1. **Addressing Variational Automation (VA):** GaP offers a framework specifically designed for VA tasks, a class of problems that are prevalent in commercial and industrial settings but often underserved by existing robot learning paradigms. By explicitly handling variations in object geometry and pose, GaP has the potential to expand the scope of automation beyond highly structured, fixed environments.

2. **Enhanced Reliability and Robustness in Agentic Robotics:** The graph-structured approach fundamentally improves the robustness of LLM-generated robot policies. The ablation studies demonstrate that imposing this structure and utilizing a multi-agent harness significantly mitigates issues like hallucinations, constraint violations, and context window limitations observed in unstructured Code-as-Policy (CaP) approaches. This enhanced reliability is crucial for real-world deployment.

3. **Modular and Interpretable Robot Policies:** By representing policies as computation graphs composed of atomic skill nodes, GaP promotes modularity and interpretability. This allows for easier debugging, reuse of skills, and understanding of the robot's decision-making process, which is often a challenge with opaque end-to-end learning models. The integration with a Modular Open Robot Skill Library (MORSL) further supports this modularity.

4. **Reduced Human Effort in System Setup:** GaP's ability to autonomously decompose natural language specifications and iteratively refine computation graphs through self-learning in simulation has the potential to significantly reduce the human engineering effort required to set up and tune robotic systems for VA tasks. This could lead to faster deployment times and lower operational costs.

5. **Boosting Existing VLA Models:** The findings suggest that GaP can serve as a robust pre-processor or orchestrator for existing VLA policies. By accurately identifying target objects and navigating the robot to favorable pre-grasp poses, GaP can bring the VLA model’s input into its operational distribution, thereby improving the success rates of otherwise struggling model-free approaches in varied environments.

6. **Introduction of New Benchmarks:** The creation of 8 new open VA benchmarks provides a valuable resource for the robotics research community, enabling standardized evaluation and fostering further development in the field of variational automation.

**Limitations acknowledged by the authors include:**
- **Current Reliability:** While significantly improved over baselines, the execution reliability is not yet at industry levels, suggesting further self-learning and parameter tuning are required.
- **Execution Speed:** GaP's execution times are currently below industry standards for throughput (e.g., 7 seconds per instance for 500 units per hour). Reducing VLM inference requests and IK motion planning time during execution is identified as an area for future work.
- **Task Scope:** The initial 8 VA benchmarks primarily focus on quasi-static pick-and-place operations, with only one involving force sensing. Future work needs to extend GaP to tasks involving deformable objects, dynamic forces, and moving targets to broaden its applicability.

Despite these limitations, GaP represents a step in developing more adaptable, reliable, and human-agnostic robotic systems for complex, real-world automation challenges.
