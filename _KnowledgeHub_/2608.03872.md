# EvoHIL: Self-Evolving Reward and Flow-Matched Policy Optimization for Robust Human-in-the-Loop Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> EvoHIL presents a Human-in-the-Loop Reinforcement Learning framework that dynamically adapts its reward model, action generation, and visual understanding to environmental changes. This unified approach maintained high success rates (e.g., 1.00 SR up to 90% illumination shift on USB insertion) and improved motion smoothness (e.g., 49.0% smoother on candy push) across varied robot manipulation tasks, enhancing robustness to deployment-time shifts.

## Problem

- Existing HIL-RL systems use fixed visual success classifiers, which degrade in changing scenes, leading to corrupted terminal rewards and unstable learning.
- Traditional per-step Gaussian policies often produce temporally inconsistent or jerky robot movements, especially in contact-rich manipulation tasks requiring fine-grained control.
- Vision-based policies are sensitive to appearance shifts (e.g., illumination changes), requiring extensive new real-world interaction to adapt to new visual conditions.

## Method

- **Self-Evolving Reward (SER)** adapts the terminal-success classifier online based on explicit human confirmations, preventing self-reinforcing errors and ensuring stable reward signals.
- **Action Flow Stabilization (AFS)** replaces single-step policies with a conditional ==flow-matching== policy that predicts short, temporally coherent action chunks, incorporating ==FPO-style surrogate loss== and smoothness terms.
- ==Retention-Aware Offline Fine-tuning== uses 'relit' replay data (images rendered under new lighting) and anchored regularization to adapt the policy to visual domain shifts while preserving performance in the original domain without new robot interaction.

## Results

- EvoHIL maintained a **1.00** success rate on USB insertion across both original and **90%** shifted-light conditions, significantly outperforming baselines that degraded sharply under shifts.
- The Self-Evolving Reward classifier's balanced accuracy consistently recovered to **~96%** (candy push) and **~91%** (medicine-box stow) after scene changes, while static reward functions remained low.
- Action Flow Stabilization reduced command irregularity by **44.5%** (mean first-difference L2) and increased aggregate smoothness by **49.0%** on candy push, leading to smoother end-effector trajectories and improved task completion efficiency.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Dynamic, human-confirmation-driven adaptation of reward models allows HIL-RL systems to maintain accurate task success understanding and recover performance in changing environments.
> - Generating robot actions as temporally coherent chunks via flow-matching policies significantly improves motion smoothness and regularity compared to independent, per-step command generation.
> - Combining relit replay data with anchored regularization enables efficient offline adaptation to visual shifts, preserving source-domain competence while learning new visual conditions without additional real-world interaction.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

Human-in-the-loop reinforcement learning (HIL-RL) represents an approach for training robots to perform complex, contact-rich manipulation tasks with limited real-world interaction. Existing HIL-RL systems leverage human demonstrations and real-time teleoperated interventions to facilitate learning. These approaches are particularly valuable in scenarios where autonomous exploration might be unsafe or inefficient, allowing human operators to guide behavior, correct errors, and confirm task completion.

However, the current landscape of HIL-RL systems typically uses human feedback to refine a policy while maintaining fixed underlying interfaces for reward generation, action representation, and visual domain understanding. This architectural constraint introduces limitations, especially in deployment scenarios characterized by environmental changes or task variability. Prior work in reward learning, generative action policies, and visual adaptation addresses these aspects individually. Reward learning methods include those based on preferences, visual events, or positive-unlabeled data. Generative action policies, such as diffusion and flow-matching models, aim to produce more coherent and multimodal actions. Visual adaptation techniques often employ data augmentation or domain randomization to improve robustness to appearance shifts.

This work contributes to the HIL-RL landscape by integrating adaptive mechanisms for reward generation, action representation, and visual domain coverage within a unified framework. It addresses the coupled nature of these limitations, aiming to improve robustness to scene changes and appearance shifts while maintaining temporal consistency in robot actions, an area where independent command generation can fall short. By combining these adaptive strategies, EvoHIL seeks to extend the applicability and robustness of HIL-RL systems beyond static training environments, moving towards more adaptable and resilient robotic manipulation in varied operational conditions.

### 2. Key objectives and motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a unified framework that enables HIL-RL systems to adapt to changing deployment conditions. The authors identify three interconnected limitations in existing HIL-RL systems that become apparent during deployment and motivate their approach:

1. **Static Visual Reward Models:** Current HIL-RL systems typically train a visual success classifier before online learning and then fix it as the sole source of terminal rewards. During deployment, scene changes or environmental shifts can cause this static classifier to make errors, mislabeling successes or failures. While human confirmations can highlight these errors, they do not update the fixed reward model. This leads to corrupted terminal labels, which can destabilize the Bellman targets used for policy learning and hinder continued learning in new environments. The motivation is to enable the reward model to evolve and adapt to changes, reflecting accurate task completion criteria dynamically.

2. **Temporally Inconsistent Action Generation:** The common use of per-step Gaussian actors, which sample each action command independently, often results in temporally inconsistent or jerky motion, especially in contact-rich manipulation tasks that require coordinated corrections over multiple control steps. Sparse success rewards provide insufficient information for learning fine-grained, temporally coherent action sequences. This limitation affects the smoothness and efficiency of robot operation. The motivation is to generate temporally coherent action chunks rather than independent commands, leading to smoother and more effective robot movements.

3. **Sensitivity of Vision-Based Policies to Appearance Shifts:** Vision-based policies are inherently tied to the appearance distribution observed during their initial interaction and training. Changes in illumination, background, or object appearance, even without altering task geometry or semantics, can significantly degrade policy performance. Continued optimization solely on the original replay data cannot provide the necessary visual coverage for these shifted conditions. This makes deployment in varied real-world settings challenging. The motivation is to enable policies to adapt to visual domain shifts without requiring extensive new robot interaction, thereby enhancing robustness to environmental variations.

These three limitations are considered coupled because errors or rigidities in one interface can propagate and negatively impact others. For instance, an erroneous terminal label (reward issue) impacts the critic's evaluation, which in turn affects the policy update (action issue), and this process is exacerbated if the visual inputs (visual sensitivity issue) are also distorted. The overall motivation is to develop a staged, adaptive framework (EvoHIL) where the reward model, action generator, and visual domain are adapted in a coordinated manner within a common actor-critic workflow, preserving the integrity of each supervision source while allowing for dynamic adjustment to deployment-time changes.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

EvoHIL is presented as a staged framework designed to address the aforementioned limitations by adapting the reward model, action generator, and visual domain within a human-in-the-loop actor-critic workflow. The system's three main components are Self-Evolving Reward (SER), Action Flow Stabilization (AFS), and Retention-Aware Offline Fine-tuning. These components operate with distinct data paths and training stages but interact through a common critic.

**A. Self-Evolving Reward (SER)**
SER focuses on adapting the binary terminal-success classifier online.
- **Deployed Reward Calculation:** The deployed reward relies on a classifier's success probability, $p_{\text{dep}}(o)$, and human confirmations ($h_t^+$). A reward is emitted if $h_t^+=1$ or if $p_{\text{dep}}(o)$ exceeds a high threshold ($\tau = 0.95$) for $K_{\text{conf}}=2$ consecutive frames.
- **Label-Source Isolation:** A critical design choice is that classifier predictions *cannot* independently become positive training labels. The proxy target for training the classifier, $\tilde{y}_t$, is defined solely by explicit human confirmation ($h_t^+$). This prevents self-reinforcing errors where confident false positives might corrupt the training data. Unconfirmed frames are treated as provisional weak negatives.
- **Conservative Online Update:** The classifier is updated using a weighted cross-entropy loss, augmented with random crops and photometric jitter for trusted positives, and a fixed negative-to-positive ratio for sampling provisional negatives. A consistency term relative to a frozen baseline classifier ($\theta_0$) is added to the loss. A new classifier is only deployed if its balanced accuracy on a held-out split exceeds a threshold (0.92). The deployed parameters are updated using an exponential moving average (EMA, $\beta=0.1$) to limit abrupt changes to the reward distribution. Crucially, rewards already stored in replay are not rewritten, ensuring a stable historical reward signal for the critic.

**B. Action Flow Stabilization (AFS)**
AFS replaces the traditional single-step Gaussian policy with a conditional flow-matching policy that predicts short action chunks.
- **Flow Policy and Sampling:** AFS learns a velocity field $v_\theta(X_\tau, \tau | s)$ conditioned on the observation, flow time $\tau$, and an intermediate action chunk $X_\tau$. The policy generates an $H$-step action chunk, but only the first $E < H$ actions are executed before replanning (receding-horizon control). This aims to improve temporal coherence.
- **Source-Aware Replay Segments:** Each primitive transition stores metadata, including trajectory index, chunk-start flag, policy version, and action source. At sampling time, consecutive actions are assembled into an $H$-step chunk, respecting episode, source, or policy-version boundaries. This allows different objectives to apply to different segments (e.g., FPO only for policy-generated segments, BC-flow for expert segments).
- **Off-Policy Critic and Advantage:** An execution-prefix ensemble critic is trained using macro transitions. For an executed prefix $C_i$ of length $e_i$, the critic $Q_j(s_i, C_i, m_i)$ evaluates this entire segment. The target $y^{(e_i)}_i$ aggregates primitive rewards $R^{(e_i)}_i$ and bootstraps from the next prefix sampled from the *current* actor. The actor advantage, $A_i$, is derived from the difference between the Q-value of the data's executed prefix and the value of the current actor's sampled prefix.
- **Flow Policy-Gradient Surrogate:** An FPO-style surrogate loss is applied only to the executed prefixes of policy-generated replay segments. It uses a ratio $\rho_i(\theta)$ based on the difference between the current flow-matching loss and a reference loss from a Polyak-averaged target actor. This ratio is clipped and weighted by the advantage.
- **Expert Imitation and Replay Smoothness:** All valid demonstration and intervention chunks contribute to a BC-flow imitation loss, weighted by positive advantages. A smoothness term is added to the actor objective, penalizing large first and second differences within a predicted chunk, and enforcing overlap-consistency between successive replans to maintain trajectory continuity.

**C. Retention-Aware Offline Fine-tuning**
This phase aims to adapt the policy to shifted visual conditions while preserving competence in the source domain, without additional robot interaction.
- **Transition-Preserving Relit Replay:** Recorded trajectories are reused, but only their images are relit by an external world model under new lighting conditions. The original action, reward, and termination labels remain unchanged. This creates `relit` transitions that provide visual coverage for the shifted domain while maintaining the original control labels, assuming geometry and object state are preserved.
- **Dual-Domain Replay with Retention Ratio:** The replay buffer mixes `source-light` online replay (excluding intervention transitions) and `relit` online replay using a retention ratio $\alpha$ (default 0.75). This provides Bellman coverage for both source and shifted distributions. A separate prior-data buffer collects expert behavior (original-light demonstrations and relit human interventions).
- **Anchored Regularization:** On expert samples, the current critic and actor networks are regularized towards frozen reference parameters ($\theta_0$) from the start of fine-tuning. A critic anchor distills the execution-prefix Q-values of the frozen critic. An actor anchor aligns the current and frozen velocity fields for the same expert chunk, flow time, noise, and interpolation point. This constrains adaptation, limiting deviation from source-domain behavior. The FPO term is disabled during this phase, with the actor anchor, expert BC-flow, and smoothness terms driving actor updates.

**D. Coupling Through the Critic:**
SER and AFS interact solely through the critic. SER's updates to the deployed reward model alter the rewards stored in subsequent transitions, which then influence the macro TD target in the critic and, consequently, the advantage calculation that drives the AFS actor updates. This design aims to preserve the task objective without introducing auxiliary reward-shaping terms.

### 4. Main findings and results

The experiments were conducted across six manipulation tasks on two robot embodiments (Franka FR3 and SO-101) under a controlled lighting shift, evaluated against baselines including HIL-SERL, HG-DAgger, BC, IBRL, and ACT. Evaluation metrics included task success rate (SR), agreement with human-confirmation labels, motion smoothness, and episode completion time.

**A. Shift Adaptation and Source-Domain Retention (using Gaussian SAC backbone)**
- **Complementary Effects of Anchors:** On USB insertion (FR3), a single feature anchor led to high shifted-light SR (0.97) but reduced original-light SR (0.57). Conversely, an action-mean anchor maintained original-light SR (1.00) but showed lower shifted-light SR (0.80). The combination of both anchors achieved 1.00 SR in both original and shifted light, with shorter completion times (2.75s original, 2.48s relit). This suggests both anchors contribute to balancing source retention and shift adaptation.
- **Retention Ratio Selection:** A sweep of the original-domain retention ratio ($\alpha$) for the replay buffer indicated that anchoring significantly reduced sensitivity to this ratio. Without anchoring, success rates varied widely. With anchoring, original-light SR remained high (near 1.00 for $\alpha \in [0.35, 1.0]$) and shifted-light SR was consistently higher (0.72-1.00). The best observed operating point was $\alpha=0.75$, yielding 1.00 SR in both domains.
- **Comparison with Online Gaussian Reference:** Over 30k offline fine-tuning iterations on USB insertion, the unanchored SAC objective was unstable, with shifted-domain SR oscillating and original-domain SR degrading. The anchored objective maintained stable and high SRs (above 0.77 original, reaching 1.00 shifted at several checkpoints), exceeding the best online policy's 0.50 shifted-light SR.
- **Replay and Anchors Complementarity:** A 2x2 study showed that neither replay mixing (at $\alpha=0.75$ without anchors) nor anchors (with $\alpha=0$ for replay) alone achieved the performance of their combination (1.00/1.00 SR for original/relit). This suggests that the buffer mixture provides cross-domain coverage, while anchors are associated with retaining source competence during adaptation.

**B. SER: Confirmation-Label Agreement Recovers Across Scene Shifts**
- **Online Recovery After Each Scene Transition:** On candy push and medicine-box stow (SO-101), the deployed SER classifier's balanced accuracy with human-confirmation proxy labels initially decreased after scene changes but consistently recovered to high levels (e.g., ~96% for candy push, ~91% for medicine-box stow at training end). In contrast, the agreement of the original static reward function decreased significantly and remained low (e.g., ~46% for candy push, ~40% for medicine-box stow). This indicates the SER effectively adapts to new scenes using human feedback.
- **Conservative Updates Without Self-Relabeling:** The recovery pattern is consistent with label-source isolation, preventing classifier outputs from directly relabeling their inputs as positive, thus avoiding inflation of agreement through self-training.

**C. AFS: Temporal Regularity and Task Outcomes**
- **Reduced Command Irregularity:** The AFS flow policy exhibited lower command irregularity compared to a Gaussian actor on both candy push and medicine-box stow. For candy push, AFS reduced the mean first-difference L2 by 44.5% and the high-frequency power ratio by 58.3%, increasing the aggregate smoothness score by 49.0%. Similar improvements were observed for medicine-box stow. This supports the claim that action chunking and temporal regularization enhance movement smoothness.
- **Illustrative Workspace Trajectories:** Visual examples indicated that the complete EvoHIL system followed more direct and smoother end-effector paths compared to baselines.
- **Contribution of AFS Components:** Ablation studies on candy push showed that all AFS components (FPO-style surrogate, expert BC-flow, and smoothness term) contributed to performance, with the full AFS achieving the best success-duration balance (0.97 SR, 5.44s duration). Removing any component led to slightly lower SR or longer durations.
- **Distinct Roles of Evolving Sources:** Comparing ablations on candy push and medicine-box stow at 60% illumination shift showed that removing SER had a larger impact on success rate, while removing AFS (keeping SER) resulted in longer completion times. The complete system showed the largest improvement in both metrics.

**D. Multi-Embodiment Evaluation Under Illumination Shift**
- **Trends Across Full Sweep:** Across a 0-100% illumination gradient for all six tasks, EvoHIL maintained high success rates with gradual declines only under extreme shifts. Baselines, however, showed sharp degradation outside the training condition. For example, on USB insertion, EvoHIL maintained 1.00 SR up to 90% shift, while HIL-SERL dropped to 0.00.
- **Comparison at 60% Operating Point:** At the 60% illumination shift, EvoHIL recorded the highest success rate for all six tasks and both embodiments. For FR3 tasks (USB insertion, RAM insertion, table wiping, circuit breaker), EvoHIL's SRs ranged from 0.83 to 1.00, significantly higher than HIL-SERL (0.13 to 0.50). On SO-101 tasks (candy push, medicine-box stow), EvoHIL achieved 0.93 and 0.73 SR, respectively, outperforming ACT (0.32 and 0.45). EvoHIL also achieved shorter mean durations for four of the six tasks, indicating efficient task completion in addition to higher success.

### 5. Significance and potential impact

This research presents EvoHIL, a framework designed to enhance the robustness and adaptability of human-in-the-loop reinforcement learning for robotic manipulation under changing environmental conditions. The findings suggest that by integrating adaptive mechanisms for reward learning, action generation, and visual domain adaptation, HIL-RL systems can achieve improved performance in dynamic deployment settings.

The key contributions and their potential impact include:
- **Robustness to Scene Changes:** The Self-Evolving Reward (SER) mechanism demonstrates the ability to adapt the success classifier online based on human confirmations. This dynamic adaptation can mitigate performance degradation caused by appearance shifts or other scene changes, ensuring that the robot's understanding of task success remains accurate during deployment. This has implications for long-term robot autonomy and deployment in varied, uncontrolled environments where manual recalibration of reward models is impractical.
- **Improved Motion Coherence:** Action Flow Stabilization (AFS), through its flow-matching policy and action chunking, is shown to reduce command irregularity and improve the smoothness of robot trajectories. This can lead to more predictable, efficient, and potentially safer robot operations, especially in contact-rich manipulation tasks where precise and coordinated movements are critical. Enhanced temporal coherence may also reduce wear on robot hardware and improve human perception of robot competence.
- **Adaptation to Visual Shifts without New Interaction:** The Retention-Aware Offline Fine-tuning phase, utilizing relit replay data and anchored regularization, enables the policy to adapt to new visual conditions without requiring additional real-robot interaction. This mechanism addresses a significant challenge in robotics, as collecting real-world interaction data is often time-consuming and expensive. By leveraging existing data and simulated visual variations, this approach can accelerate adaptation to new lighting, textures, or other appearance shifts, making robot deployment more efficient.
- **Unified Adaptive Framework:** EvoHIL integrates these three adaptive components within a common actor-critic workflow, where they interact through the critic while preserving the sparse reward semantics. This unified approach suggests a more holistic solution to the coupled challenges of deployment drift in HIL-RL, rather than addressing each limitation in isolation.

**Limitations:** The paper acknowledges several limitations, which represent avenues for future research. SER's evaluation of agreement with a human-confirmation proxy does not represent an unbiased measure of latent task success accuracy. The FPO-style surrogate used in AFS is an empirical CFM-loss ratio, not an exact importance ratio, which may affect its theoretical guarantees. The reliance on an external relighting backend for visual adaptation means its fidelity limits the coverage of illumination shifts in replay, and the assumption of preserved task geometry, object state, and temporal correspondence during relighting may not always hold. Furthermore, the comparison with baselines is not strictly resource-matched, as only EvoHIL uses relit observations and offline updates, which could affect the interpretation of the performance gains. The evaluation focuses on targeted adaptation to predefined shifts rather than zero-shot generalization to unseen conditions.
