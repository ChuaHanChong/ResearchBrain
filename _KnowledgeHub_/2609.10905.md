# Planning along Differentiable Charts of Constraint Manifolds with General-Purpose IK Solvers

> [!summary] Summary
> This work differentiates through black-box analytic inverse kinematics by applying the inverse function theorem to the augmented forward-kinematics Jacobian, recovering IK gradients without editing solver code. A least-squares domain extension with residual damping preserves gradient signal outside the reachable workspace, and a log-determinant boundary reachability constraint is active only at the workspace edge. Runtime nearly matches bespoke autodiff solutions, validated on hardware.

## Problem

- Kinematic equality constraints confine feasible configurations to a measure-zero manifold, forcing sampling planners into specialized projection and trajectory optimizers into initial-guess-dependent nonlinear equality handling.
- Automated IK meta-solvers such as IKFast generate optimized code that is not autodiff-compatible, so most robots lack the gradients required for parameterized trajectory optimization.
- Solvers return no solution outside the reachable workspace, so nonlinear optimizers that transiently leave it fail catastrophically; direct reachability constraints stay active everywhere, hurting solver behavior.

## Method

- ==Augmented forward kinematics== returns end-effector pose plus self-motion parameters; the ==inverse function theorem== turns its Jacobian into IK gradients via a linear solve, avoiding explicit matrix inversion.
- A ==least-squares domain extension== defines IK outside the workspace, with sensitivity-analysis gradients approximated by ==pseudoinverse==, ==Levenberg-Marquardt==, ==residual damping==, ==anisotropic damping==, or ==full Newton== variants.
- A ==boundary reachability constraint== uses a ==log-determinant manipulability measure== on the kinematic Jacobian, becoming active only at the reachable-workspace boundary rather than across the whole feasible interior.

## Results

- Against forward-mode autodiff on **10,000** sampled configurations, median gradient error stayed below **1e-13**; the inverse-function-theorem route became faster than autodiff beyond partial-vector size **64**.
- With boundary reachability plus residual damping, convex-region growth ran in **1.15 s** versus **1.08 s** for bespoke autodiff, while direct reachability degraded to **4.11 s** with zero gradients.
- On UR5e grasp selection, the parameterized formulation reached **63.2%** single-start success versus **46.5%** for the configuration-space baseline; **20** bimanual hardware trials held constraint violation at **0.555 mm** mean.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Implicit differentiation through an inverse map recovers gradients from the forward map's Jacobian, making closed-source or code-generated solvers usable inside gradient-based pipelines without rewriting them.
> - Constraints that stay active across the whole feasible interior degrade optimizer behavior; reformulating them to activate only at the boundary yields large practical speedups.
> - Gradient quality outside the feasible domain matters most for counterexample-search routines; residual-scaled damping recovers usable descent directions where zeroing gradients stalls the solver.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

This paper addresses motion planning for robotic manipulators subject to kinematic equality constraints. Examples include bimanual manipulation with a fixed relative pose between two end effectors, opening a door while maintaining contact, and whole-body motion with both feet fixed to the ground. Such constraints restrict feasible robot configurations to a lower-dimensional subset, commonly called a constraint manifold, embedded within the full configuration space.

Traditional planning methods represent these constraints implicitly through equations such as \(F(q)=0\). Sampling-based planners must then generate samples directly on the manifold or project unconstrained samples onto it. Trajectory optimizers can impose the equality constraints directly, but their performance can depend strongly on the quality of the initial trajectory and the numerical behavior of the constraint Jacobians. These methods may also require post-processing to restore constraint satisfaction.

An alternative is to construct an explicit parameterization of the constraint manifold. Analytic inverse kinematics provides a natural mechanism for doing so: an end-effector pose, together with continuous and discrete self-motion variables, can be mapped to a joint configuration. Optimization can then occur in lower-dimensional task-space coordinates while collision, joint-limit, stability, and other costs remain evaluated in configuration space.

Previous work demonstrated the usefulness of this strategy for constrained bimanual planning and optimization-based inverse kinematics. However, handwritten analytic IK implementations are difficult to produce and maintain, whereas widely used automated solvers such as IKFast generally generate optimized code without support for automatic differentiation. This creates a practical divide between bespoke analytic IK solutions, which may be differentiable, and general-purpose automatically generated solutions, which typically are not.

The paper proposes a way to compute derivatives of a black-box analytic IK mapping without modifying its implementation. It also addresses a second problem: analytic IK solvers often return no solution outside the reachable workspace, while nonlinear optimizers routinely evaluate intermediate points that are temporarily infeasible. The paper therefore combines inverse-function-theorem differentiation with least-squares domain extension and a reachability constraint designed to act primarily at the workspace boundary.

The work lies at the intersection of constrained motion planning, differential kinematics, analytic inverse kinematics, manifold parameterization, and nonlinear trajectory optimization.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The paper has three closely related objectives.

#### 2.1. Differentiating through general analytic IK functions

The primary objective is to obtain the Jacobian of an analytic IK mapping even when the IK implementation is a black box and does not support automatic differentiation. Gradient-based optimizers require derivatives of the mapping from task-space variables to joint angles. Without these derivatives, costs and constraints expressed in configuration space cannot be propagated back to the minimal decision variables.

The authors observe that the forward kinematics Jacobian is readily available from standard robotics software. They use the inverse function theorem to recover the local derivative of the IK function from an appropriately augmented forward kinematics map.

#### 2.2. Preserving optimization behavior outside the reachable workspace

An analytic IK function is normally defined only for reachable end-effector poses. During optimization, however, iterates can leave the reachable workspace. If the solver returns “no solution,” the optimization procedure may fail before it has an opportunity to correct the infeasible iterate. Returning a zero gradient is also inadequate because it removes the direction information needed to move back toward feasibility.

The paper therefore seeks a domain extension that returns an approximate configuration for unreachable targets and supplies useful approximate gradients. The extension is based conceptually on a least-squares inverse kinematics problem that finds the configuration whose augmented forward kinematics is closest to the requested pose and self-motion value.

#### 2.3. Enforcing reachability in an optimizer-friendly way

A direct reachability constraint measures the discrepancy between the requested end-effector pose and the pose produced by the IK solution. Although valid, this constraint remains active throughout the feasible workspace, including its interior. The authors propose a boundary-based alternative using the determinant of the kinematic Gram matrix, \(JJ^T\). The constraint penalizes configurations near singularities, which correspond to the boundary of the reachable workspace under the assumptions used in the paper.

Together, these objectives are intended to make minimal-coordinate planning usable with automatically generated analytic IK solvers rather than only with custom differentiable implementations.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Analytic IK as a chart of the constraint manifold

The paper represents an analytic IK function as

\[
IK: U \times \Psi \times K \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^n,
\]

where \(U\) is the reachable set of end-effector poses, \(\Psi\) contains continuous self-motion parameters, and \(K\) enumerates discrete IK branches such as elbow-up and elbow-down solutions. During a particular optimization problem, a discrete branch \(\kappa\) is fixed.

For a redundant manipulator, the end-effector pose alone does not uniquely determine the joint configuration. The self-motion parameter is therefore included as an additional output of an augmented forward kinematics map:

\[
FK_A(q) = (FK(q), FK_\psi(q), \kappa).
\]

Here, \(FK(q)\) is ordinary forward kinematics, while \(FK_\psi(q)\) recovers the self-motion parameter associated with the chosen IK representation. The augmented map has the same number of effective coordinates as the joint configuration, allowing the inverse function theorem to be applied locally.

#### 3.2. Inverse-function-theorem gradients

If the augmented forward kinematics Jacobian is full rank, the inverse function theorem gives

\[
D IK = [D FK_A]^{-1}.
\]

In practice, the authors do not explicitly invert the matrix. Given derivatives of the desired pose and self-motion variables with respect to another variable \(y\), they solve the linear system

\[
D FK_A(q)\frac{\partial q}{\partial y}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\frac{\partial X}{\partial y}\\
\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial y}
\end{bmatrix}.
\]

This produces the required Jacobian-vector product.

The paper gives a detailed bimanual example. One arm is treated as the controlled arm, and the second arm is assigned an end-effector pose determined by the first arm through a fixed relative transform. The subordinate arm’s joint angles are obtained by analytic IK from this pose and a self-motion parameter. The derivative of the subordinate arm’s joint configuration is computed by solving a linear system involving the subordinate arm’s augmented Jacobian and the forward-kinematic Jacobian of the controlled arm.

This approach is analogous to differential inverse kinematics in that it uses a local kinematic Jacobian, but the augmented formulation maintains consistency with the selected global IK branch and self-motion parameterization.

#### 3.3. Domain extension through least-squares IK

At unreachable target poses, the paper defines a conceptual extended inverse kinematics map:

\[
\widetilde{IK}(X,\psi)
=
\arg\min_q
\|FK_A(q)-(X,\psi)\|_2^2.
\]

For reachable targets, this agrees with the analytic IK solution. For unreachable targets, it returns a configuration whose augmented forward kinematics is as close as possible to the requested target.

Sensitivity analysis of this least-squares problem yields a derivative containing both a Gauss–Newton term and residual-weighted kinematic Hessians. Since computing the complete expression can be expensive, the authors evaluate several approximations:

1. **Zero gradients**, which provide no gradient outside the reachable set.
2. **Pseudoinverse gradients**, which retain information in nonsingular directions.
3. **Levenberg–Marquardt damping**, with either constant damping or singular-value thresholding.
4. **Residual damping**, which increases damping as the task-space residual grows.
5. **Anisotropic damping**, which applies damping according to residual magnitude along individual singular directions.
6. **Full Newton approximation**, using available portions of the kinematic Hessian.

The least-squares solution itself is not computed directly in the experiments. Instead, the authors use practical approximations such as arccosine clipping, geometric subproblem least-squares solutions, and a bisection search toward a canonical reachable target.

#### 3.4. Boundary reachability constraint

To prevent the optimizer from accepting an approximate unreachable solution, the paper introduces a boundary reachability constraint. Let \(J\) be the ordinary end-effector kinematic Jacobian evaluated at the projected configuration. The proposed constraint is

\[
-\log\det(JJ^T+\epsilon I) \leq \tau.
\]

The determinant decreases near singular configurations. Under the paper’s assumptions, the boundary of the reachable workspace maps back to critical configurations where the Jacobian loses rank. Thus, constraining this log-determinant is intended to keep the solution away from the workspace boundary while avoiding an always-active pose-matching constraint.

The gradient of this constraint requires the kinematic Hessian and the derivative of the extended IK solution. This is computationally more involved than direct reachability, but the experiments indicate that its optimization behavior can be better.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Accuracy and computational cost of the IFT gradients

The first experiment compares the proposed gradients with forward-mode automatic differentiation through a bespoke differentiable IK implementation. Across 10,000 randomly sampled reachable configurations, the median derivative error was below \(10^{-13}\), and the 95th-percentile error was below \(10^{-12}\). These results indicate numerical agreement between the inverse-function-theorem calculation and direct autodiff in reachable, nonsingular regions.

Autodiff was faster for small derivative-vector sizes. For larger partial-vector sizes, the IFT method became faster because the cost of constructing the augmented Jacobian remained effectively constant. The reported runtimes were approximately 35.6 microseconds for standard autodiff and 363.5 microseconds for the IFT calculation in the relevant benchmark, although the relative advantage changed with derivative size.

#### 4.2. Downstream planning performance

The authors evaluated graph-of-convex-sets planning with IrisNp2, trajectory optimization, and TOPPRA retiming. With direct reachability constraints, some IFT variants caused substantial IrisNp2 slowdowns. For example, zero-gradient and pseudoinverse approaches required approximately 4.1 seconds, compared with 1.14 seconds for the autodiff baseline.

Residual-informed methods performed better. Under boundary reachability, IrisNp2 runtimes were approximately 1.15–1.75 seconds for residual damping, anisotropic damping, zero gradients, or pseudoinverse variants, while the autodiff boundary-reachability baseline took 1.08 seconds. The full Newton variant took 1.17 seconds. These results support the importance of both meaningful gradients outside the workspace and a reachability constraint that does not remain active in the feasible interior.

Trajectory-optimization runtimes were generally comparable across methods, partly because these experiments used feasible initial guesses. TOPPRA showed a moderate cost increase with IFT gradients because it uses small partial vectors for which direct autodiff was more efficient.

#### 4.3. Generic EAIK solver

The authors used EAIK with a UR5e in an optimization-based grasp-selection task involving pose, joint-limit, collision, and grasp-alignment constraints. Across 100 problem instances and multiple initializations, the configuration-space baseline achieved a 46.5% success rate from one initialization and 86% with three initializations.

The IFT formulation with direct reachability achieved 61.4% and 93%, respectively. The boundary-reachability formulation achieved 63.2% and 90%. The minimal-coordinate formulations produced slightly higher objective costs, which the authors attribute partly to objective distortion caused by the coordinate transformation. Median runtimes were similar, but mean runtimes were higher for the IFT methods, suggesting occasional difficulty certifying convergence when gradients outside the reachable workspace were approximate.

#### 4.4. Hardware demonstration with IKFast

The final experiment used IKFast-generated solutions for the two arms of a Rainbow RB-Y1 robot. The parameterization included base and torso joints, the desired poses of both grippers, and a self-motion parameter for each arm. The task required the robot to pick up a box from one of 20 floor positions arranged in a \(4\times5\) grid and place it on a nearby table while maintaining a fixed relative transform between the grippers.

The complete procedure combined optimization-based grasp selection, collision-free sampling, bidirectional RRT with shortcutting, trajectory optimization, TOPPRA retiming, and static-stability constraints. The average total planning time was 56.1 seconds, with a maximum of 135.0 seconds. Optimization-based IK took an average of 19.4 seconds, and trajectory optimization took an average of 5.6 seconds.

The robot completed all 20 reported pick-and-place trials. The mean measured relative-pose violation was 0.555 mm in translation and 1.597 mrad in rotation. Maximum violations were 2.235 mm and 6.809 mrad, respectively. The demonstration indicates that the method can support constrained manipulation using an automatically generated IK solver and ordinary joint-position tracking.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The paper provides a general mechanism for making analytic IK parameterizations compatible with gradient-based optimization without rewriting the underlying IK solver. This could reduce the engineering effort required to apply minimal-coordinate planning to robot arms for which only automatically generated analytic solutions are available.

The domain-extension strategy is also relevant to practical nonlinear optimization. Because temporary infeasibility is common during optimization, returning approximate configurations with informative gradients may prevent solver failure and improve recovery toward the reachable set. The boundary reachability constraint offers an alternative to enforcing pose-matching residuals everywhere.

The approach does not eliminate all limitations. It depends on continuity of the selected IK branch, full rank of the augmented Jacobian away from singularities, and reliable tracking of self-motion branches. Solvers that do not globally order solutions into consistent branches can cause discontinuities. Cuspidal manipulators and representational singularities remain difficult cases. Approximate gradients may also impair line-search-based optimizers, and the proposed log-determinant constraint requires Hessian calculations and problem-specific tuning.

Overall, the paper connects general-purpose analytic IK software with constrained trajectory optimization and demonstrates the connection in simulation and on hardware. Its principal contribution is a derivative and feasibility-handling framework that broadens the set of IK implementations usable for manifold-based robot motion planning.
