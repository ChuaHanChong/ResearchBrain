# PVRA: A Pointwise Key-point Voting Framework for Robotic Assembly

> [!summary] Summary
> PVRA is a supervised RGB-D keypoint-voting framework that jointly predicts per-point semantic assembly roles (target/base/background) plus target 6-DoF pose and 6-DoF assembly pose via Manhattan-distance offset regression to CAD-defined keypoints, achieving 89.3% Step Acc@0.15d on a progressive gear-reducer assembly benchmark without needing ground-truth masks or known target-base relationships.

## Problem

- Prior assembly pose estimators like GPAT assume single-step global assembly, ignoring intermediate steps and physical constraints needed for progressive multi-step assembly.
- Sequence-aware methods such as Instance Encoded Transformer rely on fixed assembly sequences, limiting robustness under assembly ambiguities or variable part orderings.
- SE(3)-equivariant and geometric-prior methods like Neural Shape Mating cannot learn temporal dependencies or inter-part relationships essential for progressive assembly reasoning.

## Method

- ==Semantic Role Segmentation Module (SEG)== predicts per-point probability over target, base, and background classes, letting the same object switch semantic roles across assembly steps.
- ==Key-point Offset Prediction module (KpOF)== regresses ==Manhattan distance== offsets from visible points to 8 CAD-annotated keypoints, recovering target pre-assembled 6-DoF pose via optimal transformation.
- ==Assembly Key-point Offset module (AKpOF)== predicts offsets to the same keypoints in assembled state from base-role points, recovering assembly pose, jointly trained with SEG under a weighted combined ==focal-and-offset loss==.

## Results

- PVRA reaches Step Acc@0.15d of **0.893** versus 0.344 for CAD-ICP-PCA and 0.286 for the same baseline using PVRA-predicted masks on the Nema17 test split.
- PVRA attains SLA-AUC of **0.757**, exceeding FoundationPose with PVRA-predicted masks at 0.727, despite FoundationPose scoring higher (0.989) under ground-truth masks.
- FoundationPose with PVRA masks processed **53 fewer samples** than PVRA out of 280, showing PVRA's greater robustness to sparse, non-dense segmentation boundaries.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Registration-based pose methods like ICP degrade sharply under partial, occluded pointclouds typical of progressive assembly, favoring learned keypoint-voting approaches instead.
> - High single-threshold accuracy from mask-dependent pose estimators can mask inconsistency; AUC-style metrics across thresholds better reveal step-level reliability under imperfect segmentation.
> - End-to-end inference of both object and assembly pose without prior knowledge of target-base relationships trades some peak accuracy for deployment robustness and generality.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic assembly aims to enable robots to autonomously combine parts into a final product, a task that remains complex for current robotic systems despite being trivial for humans. Research in this domain spans various applications, including manufacturing, service robotics, medical applications, and space exploration. Traditional approaches to assembly planning, such as Assembly/Disassembly Sequence Planning (ASP/DASP) and Assembly/Disassembly Path Planning (APP/DAPP), primarily focused on deterministic action planning and trajectory optimization based on geometric modeling and combinatorial reasoning.

With advancements in robotics and artificial intelligence, a shift has occurred towards perception-driven and learning-based methods. Computer vision techniques like object detection, classification, and semantic segmentation provide basic scene understanding, while 6 Degrees of Freedom (6DoF) object pose estimation has become crucial for robotic grasping and placement tasks. However, multi-object *progressive assembly* presents more complex requirements that extend beyond basic object-centric perception. These include:
- **Spatial awareness:** To localize pre-assembled and post-assembled object poses.
- **Temporal awareness:** To determine optimal assembly sequences.
- **Inter-part relational awareness:** To ensure structural coherence.
- **Physical feasibility awareness:** To account for stability under gravity.

Existing learning-based methods for assembly largely fall into categories such as Assembly Sequence Planning (e.g., ASAP, PAST, which often use "assembly by disassembly" principles), and Assembly Pose Estimation (APE). APE methods include generalized part assembly (e.g., GPAT), progressive part assembly (e.g., Instance Encoded Transformer, RGL-Net, Dynamic Graph Learning), and those leveraging geometric priors (e.g., Neural Shape Mating, SE(3)-Equivariance). While some approaches address inter-part relationships or sequential reasoning, they often make simplifying assumptions, such as fixed assembly sequences or neglecting intermediate steps and physical constraints. Many also rely on complete point clouds or mesh files, which are not always available from real-world RGB-D sensor data that typically produces partial and noisy point clouds.

Keypoint-based methods, exemplified by PVNet and PVN3D, have shown promise in overcoming challenges associated with partial and noisy point clouds by learning on unique sub-samples of data. However, these methods primarily focus on object localization, not the higher-level "assembly cognition" required for progressive tasks.

This work positions itself within this landscape by identifying a gap: the need for object-centric perception to evolve towards learning assembly dependencies to predict meaningful *actionable outputs* for autonomous assembly manipulation. The authors propose a framework that specifically targets this "assembly task awareness," integrating capabilities for 6DoF assembly pose estimation and semantic role segmentation within a progressive assembly context, building upon the robustness of keypoint-based perception.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the observation that while modern computer vision has enabled partial autonomy in robotic manipulation, executing progressive assembly tasks demands a more specialized set of skills beyond mere object perception. The authors argue that existing object-centric perception methods often lack the ability to understand and reason about the complex spatial, temporal, and relational dependencies inherent in progressive assemblies. This deficiency limits robots to primitive pick-and-place operations rather than truly task-aware manipulation.

The central objective is to develop a learning framework that can enable "assembly task awareness" in robotic entities, interpreted as the ability to perceive an assembly configuration and reason about its dependencies to produce actionable outputs.

The specific problem addressed is defined as follows: Given an RGB-D input of an assembly scene with multiple objects in disassembled or partially assembled states, the goal is to formulate a progressive assembly approach. At each step `t` of the assembly process, the method should identify the *target object* (`O_target`), localize its 6DoF pose (`Q_t`) in the camera optical frame, and estimate its 6DoF *assembly pose* (`T_t`) relative to the camera optical frame.

To manage the complexity of this problem, the authors establish four specific assumptions for the progressive assemblies considered in this work:
1. **Fixed assembly sequence:** The order in which objects are assembled is predetermined.
2. **Single and explicitly defined target object and its 6DoF pose per assembly step:** Each step focuses on one specific object to be assembled.
3. **Assembly objects are stably positioned under gravity:** Physical stability is assumed.
4. **Only one object is in contact with the target object when it’s assembled (base object):** Simplifies contact relationships.

These assumptions are explicitly stated to enable testability and reproducibility while acknowledging the complex and ambiguous nature of assembly tasks that extend beyond the immediate scope of this research.

The key objectives and contributions of the paper are:
- To propose a 3D keypoint-based modular learning framework designed to foster assembly task awareness in robotic systems.
- To introduce an augmented set of metrics specifically tailored for benchmarking assembly-aware role segmentation and pose estimation, which differ from standard object-centric metrics.
- To implement and evaluate the proposed framework on a custom object assembly dataset, thereby demonstrating its practical application.
- To conduct a comparative evaluation of the proposed model against existing object-centric baselines, assessing its performance in the context of progressive assembly.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, PVRA (Pointwise Key-point Voting Framework for Robotic Assembly), is a supervised RGB-D point-cloud model designed to perceive an assembly scene and generate assembly task-aware actionable outputs. It is structured as a modular learning framework (Fig. 1). PVRA does not constitute a complete control system for executing assembly tasks but rather provides the necessary perceptive outputs for a downstream autonomous agent. The model adapts the RGB-D feature fusion strategy from PVN3D but customizes its prediction heads and supervision for the progressive assembly task.

#### 3.1. RGB-D Feature Extraction and Fusion

The initial step involves processing the RGB-D input. The depth image is back-projected to generate a 3D point cloud, which is then sampled into `N` points. Each point is associated with a set of features: its 3D coordinates, RGB color values, and surface normals. A pre-trained Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) is utilized to extract semantic features from the RGB image, while a PointNet++ feature extractor processes the sampled point cloud. The features derived from these two streams are then fused into a shared feature representation, denoted as `F_t` for `N` visible points from an assembly step `H_t`.

#### 3.2. Semantic Role Segmentation Module (SEG)

This module is responsible for predicting a per-point semantic probability map, `S_t`. This map distributes probabilities over three distinct object classes: `target`, `base`, and `background`. A key distinction from conventional semantic segmentation is that these roles are dynamic and dependent on the current assembly step; an object might be a `target` in one step and a `base` in a subsequent step. The segmentation head outputs point-wise role logits, where `S_t(p) ∈ R^C` represents the probabilities for point `p` belonging to one of the `C` classes.

#### 3.3. Key-point Offset Prediction Module (KpOF)

The KpOF module predicts a set of Manhattan distances, `L_t`, from the visible points (`{p_i}`) to a predefined set of 3D keypoints of the target object. These keypoints represent the target object in its *pre-assembled* state. This head's learning is supervised exclusively by points classified as having the `target` role. The 6DoF pose of the target object is subsequently recovered by computing the optimal transformation between the predicted keypoints and the corresponding object-model keypoints.

#### 3.4. Assembly Key-point Offset Prediction Module (AKpOF)

Similar to KpOF, the AKpOF module predicts Manhattan distance offsets, `D_t`, from the visible points (`{p_i}`) to the same set of predefined 3D keypoints of the target object, but this time, the keypoints represent the target object in its *assembled* state. This head is supervised only by points classified as having the `base` role. The target 6DoF assembly pose is then recovered by determining the optimal transformation between the predicted keypoints and the object-model keypoints.

#### 3.5. Key-point Sampling

Keypoints for all assembly objects are manually annotated using their respective CAD files, leveraging a farthest point sampling algorithm. Eight keypoints are initialized per object, chosen as a pragmatic balance between sufficient spatial representation and manageable output dimensionality. The networks learn scale-normalized distance offsets, ensuring object scale independence. This approach aligns with prior 3D keypoint-based pose estimation methods, providing sparse geometric anchors for offset inference.

#### 3.6. Learning Objective

PVRA employs a joint learning objective across its three prediction heads (SEG, KpOF, and AKpOF). The modules collectively learn the same progressive assembly task. The combined loss function `L` is defined as `(2 * L_SEG + L_KpOF + L_AKpOF) / 4`. The segmentation head utilizes focal loss, while the offset prediction heads use an offset loss.

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Dataset:** The Nema17 gear-reducer progressive assembly dataset was used for training and validation. This simulated dataset comprises 431 assembly instances involving 5 distinct assembly objects across 4 assembly steps, totaling 8620 instances. It includes ground-truth object poses, assembly poses, RGB-D frames, and CAD files.
- **Data Split:** The dataset was divided into 60% for training, 20% for validation, and 20% for testing.
- **Hardware:** Training was performed on a high-performance computing cluster equipped with 4 NVIDIA Tesla V100 GPUs.
- **Baselines:** PVRA was compared against two CAD-based pose estimation baselines:
 - **CAD-ICP-PCA:** Initializes point cloud registration using mask centroids and principal component axes, followed by ICP (Iterative Closest Point) refinement.
 - **FoundationPose:** A modern CAD-based RGB-D pose estimator.
 Both baselines estimate the 6DoF pose of the target and base objects and then calculate the assembly pose using known base-target transformations. A critical difference is that these baselines are evaluated under two conditions: using ground-truth (GT) object masks (ideal segmentation) and using PVRA-predicted masks (sparse points), while PVRA inherently predicts role labels on sampled RGB-D points.

**Metrics:**
The evaluation utilized an augmented set of metrics designed for assembly-aware tasks:
- **Step-Segmentation Accuracy (SSA):** Measures the average Intersection-over-Union (IoU) for the target and base roles, reflecting PVRA’s ability to correctly identify active target and base objects.
 `SSA = (1/2) * (IoU_target + IoU_base)`
- **Step-Localization Accuracy (SLA):** Quantifies pose accuracy using the normalized Maximum Symmetry-Aware Surface Distance (MSSD). Continuous local-Z symmetries are accounted for in Nema17 objects to avoid penalizing equivalent rotations. For a target-object diameter `d`, the target error (`e_target`), assembly error (`e_assembly`), and combined step error (`e_step`) are computed.
 `e_target = MSSD_target / d`, `e_assembly = MSSD_assembly / d`, `e_step = (1/2) * (e_target + e_assembly)`
 Results are reported as `Step Acc@0.15d` (accuracy at a fixed threshold) and AUC (Area Under the Curve) values for `Target AUC`, `Assembly AUC`, and `SLA-AUC` (summarizing threshold accuracy from 0 to 0.50d).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Quantitative Results

The evaluation on the Nema17 test split, summarized in Table 2, provides insights into the performance of PVRA and the baseline methods.

- **Sample Counts:** The number of samples evaluated varied for each method due to different failure mechanisms (e.g., PVRA failing during keypoint voting, CAD-ICP-PCA failing due to insufficient segmented points, FoundationPose rejecting small masks). PVRA processed 280 samples.
- **Step-Segmentation Accuracy (SSA):** PVRA achieved an SSA of 0.833, indicating its capability to correctly segment target and base roles within assembly steps. Baselines did not report this metric as they assume prior segmentation or use ground-truth masks.
- **Pose Accuracy (AUC and Acc@0.15d):**
 - **FoundationPose with Ground-Truth Masks (FP-GT):** Achieved the highest accuracy, with `Target AUC` of 0.941, `Assembly AUC` of 0.921, `SLA-AUC` of 0.927, and `Step Acc@0.15d` of 0.989. This indicates that with ideal object segmentation, FoundationPose is highly accurate, especially at strict thresholds (Fig. 2).
 - **PVRA:** Demonstrated competitive performance, achieving `Target AUC` of 0.745, `Assembly AUC` of 0.790, `SLA-AUC` of 0.757, and `Step Acc@0.15d` of 0.893. Notably, PVRA showed a sharp improvement in accuracy between 0.05d and 0.15d (Fig. 2).
 - **FoundationPose with PVRA-predicted Masks (FP-PVRA):** Its performance significantly dropped compared to using GT masks, with `SLA-AUC` declining to 0.727 and `Step Acc@0.15d` to 0.771. This highlights FoundationPose's dependency on highly accurate, dense object masks.
 - **CAD-ICP-PCA (ICP-GT and ICP-PVRA):** Performed considerably worse than the learning-based methods, both with ground-truth and PVRA-predicted masks. `SLA-AUC` values were 0.379 (ICP-GT) and 0.337 (ICP-PVRA), with `Step Acc@0.15d` at 0.344 and 0.286 respectively. This suggests that CAD registration methods are less effective with partial point clouds obtained from RGB-D sensors in a progressive assembly context.

#### 4.2. Qualitative Results

Figure 3 presents qualitative comparisons for two Nema17 test instances.
- **PVRA:** The qualitative examples show PVRA providing reasonably accurate target and assembled-target pose estimates, with `e_step` values indicating successful assembly under the `Acc@0.15d` threshold.
- **FoundationPose (FP):** With ground truth-level performance on typical cases, it achieves very low MSSD errors, demonstrating high accuracy under ideal conditions.
- **CAD-ICP-PCA (ICP):** In the qualitative examples, ICP frequently yielded high MSSD errors, often resulting in "fail" status regarding the `Acc@0.15d` threshold, confirming its poor quantitative performance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents PVRA, a keypoint-based framework designed to address critical challenges in robotic assembly by introducing an "assembly task awareness" capability. The significance of this work can be understood through several points:

**Addressing a Research Gap:** The paper identifies that traditional object-centric perception is insufficient for the demands of progressive robotic assembly, where understanding spatial, temporal, and inter-part relational dependencies is paramount. PVRA directly addresses this gap by moving beyond generic object pose estimation to explicitly learn and predict assembly-aware actionable outputs.

**Enabling Advanced Robotic Manipulation:** By providing robust per-point semantic role segmentation (target, base, background) alongside 6DoF pose estimation for both pre-assembled (target) and assembled states, PVRA offers the foundational perceptive capabilities for robots to execute more intelligent and nuanced assembly tasks. This allows for manipulation capabilities that extend beyond simple pick-and-place, enabling task-aware reasoning and execution in complex multi-step assembly sequences.

**Robustness in Challenging Conditions:** The evaluation demonstrates that PVRA maintains competitive accuracy even when dealing with partial point clouds from RGB-D inputs and when object boundaries are under-represented or uncertain in segmentation. This robustness is critical for real-world robotic applications, where occlusions, sensor noise, and imperfect segmentation are common. The framework's ability to operate effectively without requiring dense object silhouettes, a limitation observed in some baseline methods, enhances its practical applicability.

**Contribution to Benchmarking and Metrics:** The introduction of augmented metrics like Step-Segmentation Accuracy (SSA) and Step-Localization Accuracy (SLA), incorporating symmetry-aware surface distances, provides a more appropriate and comprehensive means to benchmark performance in progressive assembly tasks. These metrics are specifically designed to capture the unique requirements of assembly-aware perception, contributing to a standardized evaluation framework for future research.

**Foundation for Future Research:** PVRA provides a modular learning framework and demonstrated a concept of assembly cognition. This approach can serve as a basis for further research into more complex assembly scenarios, such as handling assembly ambiguities, variable sequences, or integrating physical constraints more explicitly. While currently evaluated on a synthetic dataset, the work establishes a reproducible benchmark and highlights the next critical step of addressing domain transferability to real-world environments, which will involve tackling sensor noise, calibration errors, and lighting variations.

In summary, PVRA contributes a viable approach to integrate context-aware perception into robotic assembly, showing that a model trained to understand assembly dependencies can achieve accuracy comparable to, and in some contexts, more robust than, object-centric pose estimation methods. This advancement has the potential to significantly improve the autonomy and adaptability of robots in complex assembly operations.
