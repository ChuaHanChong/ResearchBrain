# MotionJEPA: Preventing Temporal Feature Collapse by Capturing Visual Changes in Latent Space

> [!summary] Summary
> MotionJEPA is a JEPA latent world model that adds DISReg, a regularizer that predicts the embedding of the temporal difference image from consecutive frame embeddings, with SIGReg applied to both the image and difference latents. It needs no action labels or pixel reconstruction and keeps both fast-moving and static features. It achieves the lowest probe error on three synthetic games and 81.8% mean planning success under static-background distractors.

## Problem

- Standard JEPA training is biased toward slow or static features, so fast-moving dynamics get suppressed even when the representation avoids total collapse.
- SIGReg-based world models like LeWM regularize only along the batch dimension, letting static backgrounds dominate and suppressing moving objects such as the Pong ball.
- Inverse-dynamics anti-collapse methods need action labels and encode only action-controlled features, ignoring unlabeled scene dynamics and struggling when actions affect observations with delay.

## Method

- **DISReg** ==difference-image branch==: ==DiffEnc== encodes the pixel difference between consecutive frames, and an ==inverse-dynamics==-style ==DiffPred== predicts that embedding from the two frame embeddings via ==MSE==.
- ==Dual SIGReg== terms: a static term on image embeddings (weight 0.25) and a term on difference embeddings (weight 2) prevent collapse of both latent spaces, with prediction weight 0.5.
- The **MotionJEPA** total loss combines DISReg with the standard ==JEPA== forward-prediction MSE. DiffEnc and DiffPred are used only in training and discarded at inference, adding no runtime cost.

## Results

- On Pong, Dino and Golf, MotionJEPA reaches average MLP-probe NMSE of **0.004**, **0.021** and **0.061**, the only method keeping every feature group below **0.1**.
- Under wood-texture static distractors, mean success with 25-step CEM planning reaches **81.8%** versus **20.4%** for LeWM and **77.2%** for IDM. Combining it with IDM reaches **86.4%**.
- At the 50-step horizon MotionJEPA averages **70.3%** versus **59.3%** for IDM, with Reacher at **98.0%**. Removing the static term drops the 25-step mean to **65.4%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Avoiding total collapse is not enough: distribution regularizers can silently suppress fast features without it showing in training loss, so explicit latent probing is necessary.
> - Pixel differences give a free, label-free supervisory signal for dynamics, avoiding inverse dynamics' bias toward encoding only action-controlled degrees of freedom.
> - Change-aware and action-aware objectives are complementary, and static-distribution regularization is still needed alongside dynamics supervision, since dropping it degrades planning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

The paper addresses representation learning for visual world models, particularly Joint Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPAs). JEPAs learn an encoder that maps observations into latent representations and a predictor that forecasts future latent states, usually conditioned on actions and recent observations. Unlike generative video models, they do not reconstruct pixels. This can reduce the computational burden and encourage the model to represent abstract, task-relevant state variables rather than visual detail.

A central problem in this area is representational collapse. Complete collapse occurs when different observations are mapped to nearly identical latent vectors. More subtle feature suppression can also occur: the latent space remains statistically diverse, but only a subset of the available factors is retained. The paper focuses on temporal feature collapse, in which slowly changing or static features dominate the representation while faster-moving features are discarded.

This issue has been observed in JEPA-based systems and is related to the broader concept of slow-feature bias. Because consecutive frames are often similar, predicting one latent state from another favors information that changes slowly. Static backgrounds, scoreboards, and other predictable visual elements may therefore be encoded more reliably than moving objects or rapidly changing physical states.

Existing approaches address collapse in several ways. SIGReg and related variance or distribution-based losses impose statistical constraints on latent embeddings. Other methods use stop-gradient teachers or inverse dynamics, requiring the representation to preserve action-relevant information. Inverse-dynamics methods, however, require action labels and primarily encourage features that are directly linked to the agent’s actions. The authors argue that this can omit visually changing but indirectly relevant or delayed dynamics. MotionJEPA is positioned as an action-independent alternative that uses image differences to encourage the preservation of visual change.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

The main objective is to develop a JEPA training method that represents both static and dynamic aspects of a scene without relying on pixel reconstruction or action labels for the anti-collapse signal.

The motivation has several components. First, the authors distinguish between complete collapse and feature suppression. A model may avoid mapping every input to a constant while still neglecting important feature groups. Consequently, a distributional regularizer that merely maintains global variance may not ensure that all relevant dynamics are represented.

Second, the desirable temporal scale of a feature depends on the downstream task. Short-horizon planning may require rapidly changing object positions or velocities, whereas long-horizon reasoning may benefit from slowly changing context. A task-agnostic world model should therefore avoid imposing an overly narrow temporal hierarchy.

Third, action supervision is not always available or sufficient. Inverse dynamics can preserve controllable degrees of freedom, but it cannot directly encourage the representation of dynamics that are not immediately attributable to an action. It may also be less effective when an action produces an observable consequence after a delay.

The paper introduces Difference Image and Single image embedding Regularization, or DISReg. Its static component regularizes the ordinary image embedding and retains the distribution-shaping role of SIGReg. Its dynamic component encodes the difference between consecutive frames and requires consecutive image embeddings to predict that difference embedding. This is intended to ensure that changing visual information is present in the image latent without forcing that latent to have a particular distribution along the temporal dimension.

The authors evaluate whether this approach reduces feature suppression, preserves geometrically simple latent trajectories, and improves model-based planning when static backgrounds act as distractors.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. MotionJEPA Architecture

The model receives an offline dataset of visual trajectories and, where applicable, action sequences. An image encoder \(Enc_\theta\) maps an observation \(o_t\) to an image embedding \(z_t\). A forward predictor estimates the next latent state:

\[
\hat{z}_{t+1} = Pred_\phi(z_{t-H+1:t}, a_{t-H+1:t}),
\]

where \(H\) is the history length.

The authors use the same core architecture as LeWorldModel. The image encoder is a ViT-Tiny with 14-by-14 patches, producing a 192-dimensional embedding through the final class token and a one-layer MLP with batch normalization. The forward predictor is a six-layer causal transformer with 16 attention heads and learned positional embeddings.

DISReg adds two training-only components. A difference-image encoder produces:

\[
d_t = DiffEnc_\alpha(o_{t+1}-o_t).
\]

A difference predictor receives two consecutive image embeddings and predicts the difference embedding:

\[
\hat{d}_t = DiffPred_\beta(z_t,z_{t+1}).
\]

The difference predictor is a three-layer MLP with 512-unit hidden layers, layer normalization, and GELU activations.

#### 3.2. Training Objectives

Both the image embedding \(z\) and difference embedding \(d\) receive SIGReg losses:

\[
L_z = SIGReg(z), \qquad L_d = SIGReg(d).
\]

The dynamic prediction loss is the mean squared error between the encoded difference image and the predicted difference embedding:

\[
L_{pred} = MSE(d_t,\hat{d}_t).
\]

The complete DISReg objective is:

\[
L_{DISReg}=\lambda_zL_z+\lambda_dL_d+\lambda_{pred}L_{pred}.
\]

The MotionJEPA objective adds the standard JEPA forward-prediction loss:

\[
L_{MotionJEPA}=L_{DISReg}+MSE(z_{t+1},\hat{z}_{t+1}).
\]

The default weights are \(\lambda_z=0.25\), \(\lambda_d=2\), and \(\lambda_{pred}=0.5\). The authors explain that setting \(\lambda_d=0\) removes the dynamic anti-collapse signal, while setting \(\lambda_z=0\) emphasizes motion at the expense of broader state information.

#### 3.3. Experimental Design

The first evaluation uses three deterministic synthetic environments: Pong, Dino, and Golf. These environments contain explicitly defined, separable state variables, including moving objects, agent-controlled objects, obstacles, scores, clouds, and backgrounds. Their true state dimensions are substantially smaller than the 192-dimensional latent space, reducing the possibility that limited latent capacity causes the observed collapse.

Models are trained from scratch on 10 million randomly sampled four-frame episodes. The evaluation compares MotionJEPA with LeWM, SMWM, ForwardOnly, Random, and several LeWM variants that apply SIGReg across time, flatten batch and time dimensions, or use stop-gradient targets. Hyperparameter-optimized versions of the baselines are also included.

The authors freeze the trained models and train probes to recover known environment states from image embeddings and five-step predicted latent rollouts. They use normalized mean squared error (NMSE), where a value near 1 indicates failure comparable to predicting the target mean. High-capacity residual MLP probes measure whether information is present in the representation, rather than whether it is linearly accessible. Linear probing is also reported in the supplement.

A separate planning evaluation uses PushT, TwoRoom, OG-Cube, and Reacher. Static wood-texture backgrounds are added to these environments, with a different crop fixed throughout each rollout. Models are evaluated using cross-entropy-method planning over 25- and 50-step horizons. The study also tests an ablation without the static latent regularizer and a combination of MotionJEPA with inverse-dynamics supervision.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Feature Preservation

ForwardOnly collapses across the synthetic environments, with feature-probe NMSE values generally near or above 1. Standard LeWM often retains static or slow features while suppressing dynamic ones. For example, it encodes the Pong scoreboard but not the ball, and in Dino it preserves the sky and hearts more reliably than the cactus or dinosaur.

SMWM preserves features directly associated with actions, such as Pong bats, but suppresses other changing information. This supports the authors’ claim that action-conditioned inverse dynamics provides incomplete coverage of scene dynamics.

MotionJEPA obtains the lowest average residual-MLP probe error in all three environments. For direct image embeddings, its average NMSE is 0.004 on Pong, 0.021 on Dino, and 0.061 on Golf. It is the only evaluated method with a balanced probing loss below 0.1 for every reported feature category in these environments. On Golf, for example, it retains the moving bar obstacles that other methods frequently suppress.

The five-step rollout results show a similar pattern. MotionJEPA achieves average NMSE values of 0.036, 0.055, and 0.164 for Pong, Dino, and Golf, respectively. These results indicate that the preserved information is also usable by the forward predictor rather than being limited to the single-frame encoder.

The training analysis shows that collapse is not necessarily reflected in the overall JEPA loss. In Dino, static sky and slowly changing heart features are learned early, while information about the dinosaur can initially appear and later disappear in standard LeWM. This demonstrates that latent probing or downstream evaluation is needed to identify feature suppression.

#### 4.2. Latent Geometry

The authors measure latent path straightness as the ratio of the encoded trajectory’s arc length to the direct distance between its endpoints. A value of 1 indicates a straight path; higher values indicate curvature.

MotionJEPA produces relatively straight trajectories compared with methods that impose distribution constraints across time. Its path-straightness scores are 2.756 for Pong, 5.789 for Dino, and 3.305 for Golf. SMWM is straighter in Pong and Golf, but it retains fewer feature groups. In contrast, LeWM-Time and LeWM-Flat can produce values above 20 in some environments, suggesting more curved or entangled latent paths.

Visualizations in Golf show that MotionJEPA maps ball positions to nearly straight trajectories and represents periodic obstacle variables as simple loops. Standard LeWM often collapses obstacle states into a small region, while LeWM-Flat retains more information but with greater curvature and entanglement.

#### 4.3. Downstream Planning

Under static-background distractors, standard LeWM achieves mean success rates of 20.4% for 25-step planning and 11.8% for 50-step planning. LeWM-Flat improves these means to 41.7% and 31.9%, respectively.

MotionJEPA reaches mean success rates of 81.8% for 25-step planning and 70.3% for 50-step planning. Its gains are particularly pronounced on Cube and Reacher at the longer horizon, where it achieves 63.2% and 98.0%, compared with 24.4% and 2.0% for LeWM.

Inverse-dynamics supervision alone obtains mean success rates of 77.2% and 59.3%. Combining MotionJEPA with inverse dynamics raises the means to 86.4% and 71.6%. This indicates that the two objectives preserve partially complementary information. On TwoRoom, performance is close to saturation for the dynamics-aware methods.

The ablation with \(\lambda_z=0\) performs worse, with mean success rates of 65.4% and 49.2%. This supports the need to retain the static term alongside the dynamic term. The static term appears to preserve broader state and contextual information rather than allowing the representation to focus only on changes.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The paper contributes a training objective for JEPA-style world models that targets feature suppression rather than only complete representational collapse. Its central design choice is to predict an embedding of a temporal difference image, allowing dynamic information to influence the image representation without directly prescribing the image embedding’s temporal distribution.

This approach may be useful for model-based control, planning, and self-supervised video representation learning, especially when data are unlabeled or action annotations are incomplete. The planning experiments suggest that preserving dynamic state can improve robustness to static visual distractors, a condition that can cause conventional JEPA models to prioritize background appearance over controllable scene content.

The method also provides a practical framework for analyzing representations through feature probes and latent geometry. However, the evidence is primarily based on synthetic environments and four benchmark control tasks with artificially inserted wood-texture backgrounds. Its performance on natural videos, complex observations, delayed effects, and real robotic data remains untested in the presented work.

DISReg adds a difference-image encoder and a difference predictor, increasing training parameters and computation. These modules are discarded during inference, so they do not increase deployment-time cost, but their training overhead may matter for large-scale video models. Difference images may also be sensitive to camera motion, illumination changes, or independently moving background elements, issues that require evaluation beyond the controlled settings used here. Overall, the paper presents MotionJEPA as a mechanism for balancing static context and temporal change, with results indicating improved feature coverage and planning robustness under the tested conditions.
