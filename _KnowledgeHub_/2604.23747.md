# SFT-then-RL Outperforms Mixed-Policy Methods for LLM Reasoning

> [!summary] Summary
> This research re-evaluates mixed-policy optimization methods for large language model (LLM) reasoning by identifying and correcting bugs in widely used supervised fine-tuning (SFT) training frameworks. It demonstrates that a correctly implemented SFT-then-reinforcement learning (RL) pipeline consistently outperforms current mixed-policy approaches on mathematical reasoning benchmarks, often with greater computational efficiency.

## Problem

- Recent mixed-policy optimization methods for LLM reasoning reported superior performance over the traditional SFT-then-RL pipeline.
- The established SFT-then-RL pipeline's efficacy was potentially underestimated due to systemic bugs in open-source SFT training frameworks.
- Limitations in on-policy RL (sparse rewards) and SFT alone (incomplete reasoning development) motivated the exploration of mixed-policy methods.

## Method

- Identified and corrected two critical bugs in ==DeepSpeed== (CPU-offloaded optimizer) and ==OpenRLHF== (loss aggregation) frameworks that silently degraded SFT performance.
- Re-evaluated the standard ==SFT-then-RL pipeline== against five prominent ==mixed-policy methods== (LUFFY, ReLIFT, SRFT, Prefix-RFT, HPT) using robust, bug-fixed baselines.
- Conducted experiments on **Qwen2.5-Math-7B** and **Llama-3.1-8B** across diverse mathematical and general knowledge benchmarks, analyzing training dynamics and computational efficiency.

## Results

- Fixing identified bugs improved Qwen2.5-Math-7B SFT performance by **+5.7 points** on average, aligning OpenRLHF's SFT with independent implementations.
- The corrected SFT-then-RL on Qwen2.5-Math-7B achieved an average score of **57.0**, outperforming all five mixed-policy methods by up to **+3.8 points**.
- On Llama-3.1-8B, the corrected SFT-then-RL achieved **43.7**, exceeding published mixed-policy methods by **+22.2 points**, with a truncated 50-step RL version using fewer FLOPs than several mixed-policy methods.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Systematic bugs in common SFT training frameworks significantly suppress baseline performance, leading to misleading comparisons with novel methods.
> - A well-executed SFT phase efficiently bootstraps LLMs for reasoning tasks, providing a strong initial policy that significantly benefits subsequent RL refinement.
> - The correctly implemented SFT-then-RL paradigm is often more computationally efficient while achieving higher performance than complex mixed-policy optimization methods.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of large language model (LLM) reasoning, particularly for complex mathematical and scientific tasks, has seen substantial advancements. The standard approach for training these models involves a two-stage pipeline: supervised fine-tuning (SFT) followed by reinforcement learning (RL). SFT is used to impart domain knowledge and desired output formatting through expert-generated demonstrations, drawing on methodologies popularized by efforts such as DeepSeek-R1 and Kimi k1.5. Subsequently, reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards (RLVR) refines the model's policy to achieve higher-reward reasoning strategies.

Recently, a trend has emerged exploring "mixed-policy optimization methods" that deviate from this sequential pipeline. These methods interleave or blend supervised and reinforcement learning signals during training. The primary motivation for such approaches is to address limitations observed in both SFT and RL. On-policy RL can suffer from sparse rewards in challenging problems where models rarely produce correct solutions initially. Concurrently, SFT alone may not fully develop a model's intrinsic reasoning capacity. By integrating on-policy rollouts with off-policy expert demonstrations, mixed-policy methods aim to combine SFT's ability to learn from demonstrations with RL's capacity to refine existing reasoning. A growing body of research, including methods like LUFFY, ReLIFT, SRFT, Prefix-RFT, and HPT, has reported improvements over traditional SFT and SFT-then-RL baselines, often claiming state-of-the-art results on mathematical reasoning benchmarks.

This present work positions itself as a critical re-evaluation of these mixed-policy methods. It investigates the underlying baselines against which these methods claim superiority, suggesting that many such claims might be based on "faulty baselines." By identifying and correcting identified bugs in widely used SFT training frameworks, the paper aims to provide a clearer comparative landscape and assess the true efficacy of the SFT-then-RL pipeline versus contemporary mixed-policy approaches.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to scrutinize and re-evaluate the reported superior performance of recent mixed-policy optimization methods for large language model (LLM) reasoning when compared to the established SFT-then-RL pipeline. The authors hypothesize that numerous recently published research papers reporting improvements with mixed-policy methods may be relying on baselines that are systematically weakened by underlying technical issues in shared training frameworks.

The key motivations driving this investigation are:

- **Challenging Reported Gains:** The consistent claims of improved performance by mixed-policy methods over the standard SFT-then-RL pipeline warrant thorough examination, especially if these claims could be influenced by deficiencies in the baseline implementations.
- **Identifying Systemic Bugs:** The authors aim to identify specific bugs within widely adopted open-source SFT training frameworks that could silently degrade SFT performance. Such bugs, if widespread, could systematically deflate baselines across multiple independent studies and projects.
- **Restoring Baseline Integrity:** By identifying and correcting these potential bugs, the research seeks to establish a robust and accurate SFT baseline. This corrected baseline is essential for a fair comparison against novel mixed-policy methods, enabling a more accurate assessment of their true contributions.
- **Assessing Pipeline Efficiency:** The study is motivated to compare the computational efficiency of the corrected SFT-then-RL pipeline against mixed-policy methods, particularly examining whether a strong SFT initialization can lead to more efficient and shorter subsequent RL training phases.
- **Cross-Framework Validation:** The authors highlight the importance of cross-framework validation in empirical machine learning research to guard against silent bugs that can influence results without being immediately apparent. This work serves as a practical demonstration of this principle.
- **Generalizability Across Models:** The research aims to verify whether the findings concerning baseline integrity and pipeline performance hold true across different model families, specifically by extending experiments to Llama-3.1-8B in addition to Qwen2.5-Math-7B.

Ultimately, the paper seeks to clarify the relative strengths and efficiencies of the standard SFT-then-RL approach versus mixed-policy methods for LLM reasoning by ensuring that comparisons are made against correctly implemented and high-performing baselines.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a systematic approach to identify, validate, and quantify the impact of bugs in SFT training frameworks and then re-evaluate the performance of the standard SFT-then-RL pipeline against contemporary mixed-policy methods.

#### 3.1. Bug Identification and Correction

The authors identified two specific bugs affecting the quality of SFT:

- **CPU-Offloaded Optimizer Bug:** This bug was found in DeepSpeed (version v0.18.9), specifically within its ZeRO Stage 1 or 2 configuration when using a CPU-offloaded Adam optimizer for gradient accumulation. The bug caused only the gradients from the first micro-batch to be copied to the CPU-side optimizer, effectively dropping gradients from subsequent micro-batches. This issue impacted frameworks leveraging DeepSpeed in this configuration, including TRL, OpenRLHF, and Llama-Factory. The fix involved relocating the `copy_gradients_to_cpu` call to execute after every micro-batch’s accumulation, ensuring all gradients are correctly aggregated.
- **Loss Aggregation Bug:** This bug, present in OpenRLHF (version v0.9.10), Llama-Factory, and earlier versions of `verl`, resulted from incorrectly calculating the average cross-entropy loss. Instead of computing a true per-token mean across all active tokens in a batch (and across distributed ranks), it averaged per-mini-batch means. Since mini-batches or distributed ranks often contain varying numbers of active tokens due to different prompt and response lengths in SFT, this method incorrectly weighted mini-batches, distorting the total loss. The correction involved aggregating token-level loss sums and counts globally before computing the final mean.

The fixes for both bugs were submitted upstream to their respective projects.

#### 3.2. Experimental Setup

To conduct their evaluation, the authors established a controlled experimental environment:

- **Base Models:** Experiments primarily used Qwen2.5-Math-7B (with increased context length and RoPE theta as per previous works) and Llama-3.1-8B to assess generalization across model families.
- **Training Dataset:** The OpenR1-Math-46k-8192 dataset was utilized, comprising approximately 46k prompts paired with high-quality reasoning demonstrations, filtered for length and correctness. This dataset is common among the mixed-policy methods being re-evaluated.
- **Evaluation Protocol:** Models were evaluated on six in-distribution mathematical reasoning benchmarks (AIME24, AIME25, AMC, MATH-500, Minerva, Olympiad) using avg@32 or pass@1 metrics. Additionally, three out-of-distribution benchmarks (ARC-Challenge, GPQA-Diamond, MMLU-Pro) covering science and general knowledge were used to assess generalization. Math-Verify served as the verifier during training and testing.
- **Training Frameworks:** The `verl` framework was used for corrected baselines. OpenRLHF, paired with DeepSpeed, was used for the bug reproduction experiments. Distributed training was managed with PyTorch Fully Sharded Data Parallel (FSDP).

#### 3.3. Baseline and Method Comparison

The core of the methodology involved:

- **Corrected Baselines:** The authors implemented and trained the standard SFT-then-RL pipeline using GRPO for 500 steps, ensuring the identified bugs were fixed and using robust SFT hyperparameters. SFT was trained for 3 epochs.
- **Mixed-Policy Methods:** Five mixed-policy methods were selected for comparison: LUFFY, ReLIFT, SRFT, Prefix-RFT, and HPT. For LUFFY and ReLIFT, the authors attempted full reimplementation into `verl` using original hyperparameters, while correcting the loss aggregation bug within their SFT/RL components if applicable. Other methods were compared against their reported scores.
- **Bug Impact Decompositon:** A dedicated experiment was run using OpenRLHF with progressive bug fixes (baseline, +fix loss aggregation, +fix optimizer, +fix both) and compared against the `verl` SFT implementation. This allowed quantifying the contribution of each bug to SFT performance degradation. Training loss and gradient norms were monitored to analyze training stability.
- **Hyperparameter Sensitivity Analysis:** The impact of SFT hyperparameters (e.g., learning rate, batch size) on baseline performance was investigated by reproducing SRFT's SFT baseline with its reported hyperparameters and comparing it to the tuned configuration.
- **Training Efficiency Analysis:** A truncated SFT→RL variant, trained for only 50 RL steps with an increased learning rate, was evaluated. Its performance was compared against the full 500-step RL run and mixed-policy methods, alongside an estimation of total FLOPs to assess computational cost.

This comprehensive approach allowed for both direct empirical comparison and a mechanistic understanding of how framework bugs and hyperparameter choices influenced reported results.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several key findings that challenge existing perceptions regarding the performance of mixed-policy methods for LLM reasoning.

#### 4.1. Impact of Identified Bugs on SFT Performance

The study identified two critical bugs that significantly degraded the performance of SFT baselines:

- **CPU-Offloaded Optimizer Bug:** This bug, primarily in DeepSpeed's gradient accumulation for CPU-offloaded optimizers, was found to be the dominant factor. Fixing this bug alone on Qwen2.5-Math-7B improved the average SFT score from 48.3 to 53.4 points on a subset of benchmarks. This fix also corrected significantly lower reported gradient norms in the buggy configuration, bringing them in line with GPU-resident optimizers and the `verl` baseline.
- **Loss Aggregation Bug:** This bug in OpenRLHF, which incorrectly weighted per-mini-batch losses, contributed a smaller but measurable additional improvement. Fixing it on top of the optimizer bug further raised the average score from 53.4 to 54.0. Beyond score improvement, fixing this bug also stabilized training dynamics, reducing variability in the SFT training loss curve.

Together, these fixes brought the performance of OpenRLHF's SFT (54.0) in line with the independently implemented `verl` SFT (53.8), demonstrating that the original "buggy" baselines were indeed significantly suppressed.

#### 4.2. Performance of Corrected SFT-then-RL Pipeline

Once the SFT baselines were corrected, the standard SFT-then-RL pipeline demonstrated superior performance:

- **Qwen2.5-Math-7B (In-Distribution):** The corrected SFT-then-RL pipeline achieved an average score of 57.0 on in-distribution mathematical benchmarks. This outperformed all five evaluated mixed-policy methods, surpassing the next-best method (SRFT at 53.2) by +3.8 points. Notably, the corrected SFT alone (52.2) already surpassed LUFFY (46.3), ReLIFT (48.8), and Prefix-RFT (51.8).
- **Qwen2.5-Math-7B (Out-of-Distribution):** On out-of-distribution tasks, the SFT-then-RL pipeline (59.9) remained competitive, ranking second only to SRFT (62.5), indicating good generalization capabilities.
- **Llama-3.1-8B:** The findings were even more pronounced on Llama-3.1-8B. The corrected SFT-then-RL achieved an average score of 43.7, outperforming every published mixed-policy method by a substantial margin of +22.2 points (closest competitor: HPT at 21.5). The corrected SFT alone (33.9) exceeded all reported mixed-policy results. This difference was attributed to Llama's less specialized pre-training for math, making the knowledge injection from SFT even more critical.

#### 4.3. Training Dynamics and Efficiency

The analysis of training dynamics and efficiency further supported the SFT-then-RL paradigm:

- **Training Dynamics:** For Qwen2.5-Math-7B, the RL phase of SFT-then-RL started with a high training reward (above 80%), leveraging the knowledge acquired during SFT. Mixed-policy methods, by contrast, never reached this initial reward level. On Llama-3.1-8B, the gap was starker, with mixed-policy methods remaining below 30% training reward while SFT-then-RL started around 60% and continued to climb. This suggests that SFT efficiently bootstraps mathematical reasoning before RL refinement.
- **Response Length and Entropy:** SFT-then-RL on Qwen initially produced verbose responses from SFT, which RL then refined to shorter, more efficient solutions. Mixed-policy methods showed gradually increasing response lengths, some diverging towards context limits on Llama-3.1-8B. Policy entropy remained relatively constant for SFT-then-RL, consistent with refining an already strong policy.
- **Training Efficiency (FLOPs):** A truncated SFT-then-RL pipeline with only 50 RL steps (compared to 500) achieved an average score of 55.6 on in-distribution benchmarks, still outperforming most mixed-policy methods. Crucially, this truncated pipeline required fewer FLOPs (3.63 × 10^19) than LUFFY (6.65 × 10^19) and ReLIFT (8.76 × 10^19), demonstrating improved performance with reduced computational cost.

#### 4.4. Hyperparameter Sensitivity

Beyond framework bugs, the study also found that suboptimal SFT hyperparameters could weaken baselines. Reproducing the SFT stage of SRFT with its reported hyperparameters resulted in a weaker baseline (48.3 average score) compared to the tuned configuration (53.8), further inflating the apparent gains of mixed-policy methods.

In summary, the research demonstrated that once common bugs in SFT frameworks are corrected and optimal hyperparameters are applied, the standard SFT-then-RL pipeline delivers higher performance and greater computational efficiency than the evaluated mixed-policy methods.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research carries several significant implications for the field of large language model (LLM) reasoning and empirical machine learning practices more broadly:

- **Reaffirmation of SFT-then-RL Paradigm:** The findings strongly reaffirm the efficacy and efficiency of the traditional two-stage SFT-then-RL pipeline. The study demonstrates that, when correctly implemented, this sequential approach not only matches but surpasses the performance of current mixed-policy methods for LLM reasoning on both in-distribution and out-of-distribution tasks across different model families (Qwen2.5-Math-7B and Llama-3.1-8B). This restores confidence in the established paradigm and provides a robust benchmark for future work.
- **Critical Re-evaluation of Mixed-Policy Methods:** The work critically questions the reported state-of-the-art results and perceived advantages of numerous recently published mixed-policy optimization methods. It suggests that their reported gains over SFT-then-RL may not stem from inherent methodological superiority but rather from comparisons against systematically deflated baselines. This necessitates a re-evaluation of these methods' contributions within the research community.
- **Highlighting the Importance of Baseline Integrity:** A core impact of this paper is to underscore the crucial importance of meticulous implementation and rigorous validation of baselines in empirical machine learning research. Silent bugs, even in widely used open-source frameworks, can lead to widespread misinterpretations of research outcomes. This work serves as a cautionary tale and a call for greater scrutiny in reproducing and validating foundational components.
- **Impact on Open-Source Frameworks:** The identification and upstream submission of fixes for critical bugs in widely used frameworks such as DeepSpeed and OpenRLHF (and by extension, frameworks like TRL and Llama-Factory that inherit these issues) have a direct positive impact on the robustness and reliability of the broader ML ecosystem. These corrections will lead to more accurate training and reproducible research for countless users.
- **Efficiency Gains in Training:** The demonstration that a well-executed SFT can significantly bootstrap an LLM, allowing for a much shorter and more efficient subsequent RL phase (e.g., 50 steps instead of 500, with fewer FLOPs), has practical implications for researchers and practitioners. This suggests that resources can be optimized by focusing on a high-quality SFT initialization, leading to faster experimentation and deployment cycles.
- **Generalizability of Findings:** The verification of these findings across different model families (Qwen and Llama) and the observation that the performance gap is even more pronounced for models less natively proficient in math (like Llama) enhance the generalizability of the conclusions. This suggests that the quality of SFT is particularly vital for models that require substantial domain-specific knowledge injection.
- **Call for Cross-Framework Validation:** The study advocates for routine cross-framework validation of empirical results. Relying on a single framework, even a popular one, can obscure underlying bugs. Independent implementations, as exemplified by the `verl` baseline, can serve as essential checks against systematic errors.

In conclusion, this research significantly shifts the understanding of LLM reasoning training paradigms by demonstrating that the perceived advantages of complex mixed-policy methods were largely artifacts of flawed baselines. It reinforces fundamental principles of scientific rigor, emphasizing the necessity of robust baselines and meticulous implementation for valid and impactful research in machine learning.
