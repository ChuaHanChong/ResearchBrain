# Achieve What You Imagined: Learning to Align Actions with Visual Plans

> [!summary] Summary
> A post-training framework for world-action models: it treats the model's imagined video as a visual goal and uses the gap to the outcome of its own actions as reward. A frozen Ctrl-World model predicts what Cosmos3's generated actions would actually produce. Pixel-level trajectory and goal-frame agreement drive Flow Policy Optimization on the action head only. Success on four UR5 tasks rises from 43.4% to 75.1%.

## Problem

- Binary terminal rewards are too sparse for weak base policies in low-data settings, and adding task-matched demonstrations shifts the burden back to collecting expert data.
- Learned visual or vision-language reward evaluators give denser feedback but need extra pipelines for data preparation, training, calibration and deployment of task-specific success detectors.
- Joint world-action models train video and action generation separately, so imagined task completion often does not match what the generated actions actually achieve; RL for flow policies cannot handle high-order solvers.

## Method

- ==Closed-loop cross-model rollout==: Cosmos3 generates an imagined video plus an action chunk, and a frozen ==Ctrl-World== model predicts the action's consequence video, which supplies the next observation.
- ==Cross-model reward==: ==negative pixel MSE== between imagined and consequence frames (trajectory term) plus a terminal goal-frame term, using discounted suffix returns and per-chunk ==group-relative advantage normalization==.
- Action-head-only ==Flow Policy Optimization==: a clipped surrogate ratio built from masked conditional flow-matching losses on action coordinates, treating Cosmos3's native ==UniPC== sampler as a black box.

## Results

- During supervised fine-tuning, imagined-video success climbs from **18% to 50%** while the action-conditioned rollout stays at **6-13%**, a **41-point** imagination-consequence gap at 15k steps.
- Real-robot mean success rises from **43.4% to 75.1%**, versus **61.4%** for pi0.5, **8.8%** for VLA-JEPA and **4.0%** for Fast-WAM. Laptop goes **22% to 68%**, Cup **33.3% to 83.3%**.
- Mean task completion time drops **6-17%** across tasks, with **25.1%** fewer control steps on cup nesting. Trajectory-only and goal-only reward ablations each work, and combining them works best.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A world-action model often knows what success looks like before it can act on it, so the gap between its own imagination and its actions' outcome is a reward signal it gets for free.
> - Freezing the models that produce the reward and updating only the action head stops the policy from gaming the reward by changing the predictors.
> - Success measured inside the world model peaks and then falls during RL, so model-space metrics should not stand in for real-robot evaluation or decide when to stop training.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A robot receives an instruction and a starting image. From the same instruction and starting state, a model's imagined video shows a red duck being placed in a basket, while its proposed action fails to grasp the duck. The video is a useful visual target, but it does not prove the action will work. This paper tests whether that mismatch can serve as training feedback.

The paper's illustrative model-mismatch example. The imagined sequence shows the robot completing the duck placement. The lower illustration depicts failure, with the duck still outside the basket.

### 2. Comparing the imagined future with the action's consequence

A world-action model (WAM) jointly generates future video and robot actions. The video can depict task completion even when its action chunk does not imply the same outcome. The authors therefore treat the visual prediction as a goal-conditioned proposal, not an executable motor plan. Feedback is hard to obtain in low-data learning. Terminal rewards are sparse when a weak policy rarely succeeds, and a learned visual evaluator adds its own training and calibration pipeline.

The method has four parts:

- **Proposal.** Cosmos3, the WAM used here, generates a goal-conditioned video prediction and an action chunk from the current observation and instruction.
- **Predicted consequence.** The action commands are converted to Ctrl-World's control interface. Frozen Ctrl-World then predicts the visual consequence of those actions.
- **Rollout.** That consequence is used as the next observation in the model-mediated closed-loop rollout. The WAM's imagined video is used as the comparison target and is not fed back as the next observation.
- **Rewards.** The trajectory reward is the negative pixel mean-squared error between matched-time, matched-view frames of the two predicted videos. The terminal-goal reward is the negative pixel MSE between Ctrl-World's final predicted frame and the task's predefined visual goal frame, applied at the final chunk. Higher reward means smaller pixel discrepancy.

Group-relative advantages and a clipped surrogate from prior work, Flow Policy Optimization ([[2507.21053|Flow Matching Policy Gradients]]), update only the action head. FPO uses conditional flow-matching losses as a surrogate for policy change, not an exact likelihood ratio. This allows Cosmos3's native UniPC sampling procedure to be retained. The video-generation parameters and Ctrl-World remain frozen. The feedback comes without online robot interaction or a separately trained task-specific reward model.

Method overview. The imagined visual plan is compared with a frozen World Model's action-conditioned rollout, and final frames are separately compared with a goal frame. The two similarity signals form reward and lead to an action-head update through Flow Policy Optimization.

### 3. What the tests show

The first test checks whether the gap appears in model outputs. The authors used SFT checkpoints at 500, 5k, 10k, and 15k steps. At each, they sampled 100 paired rollouts from 20 initializations. The same frozen instruction-conditioned classifier scored the terminal frames of the WAM video and of the frozen world model's consequence prediction.

The WAM video's model-space success rises from 18% to 50%, while action-conditioned predictions stay between 6% and 13%. At 15k steps the difference is 41 percentage points. This is a discrepancy between model-generated predictions, not a real-robot success difference.

The second test runs post-training on four physical UR5 tasks:

- laptop closing (40 demonstrations)
- specified-color duck placement (200)
- Push-T to a target pose (150)
- nesting four cups (100)

The reported mean is the equal-weight average of the four task success rates, not a trial-weighted pooled rate. It rises from 43.4% for the authors' SFT initialization to 75.1% after RL. That is 13.7 percentage points above π₀.₅ at 61.4%, under the paper's adaptation protocol. The method is higher than π₀.₅ on all four evaluated tasks.

Real-robot success rates (%) for Laptop, Duck, Push-T, Cup, and Avg. Mean values are 43.4% for Ours (SFT), 61.4% for π₀.₅, and 75.1% for Ours (RL). The Ours (RL) row exceeds π₀.₅ in each task column.

### 4. Contribution and limits

In these four tasks, the imagined future served as a visual target, and comparing it with predicted action consequences supplied useful feedback for the action head. The post-training feedback came from model-generated rollouts, while the success gains were measured on physical robots with 40–200 demonstrations per task.

A visual prediction is not itself physics validation, and the authors say future work should improve assessment of physical feasibility. The results do not prove that the discrepancy alone caused the improved execution. They also do not show that model-space success always predicts real-world success.
