# GHOST: Grounded Human Motion Generation with Open Vocabulary Scene-and-Text Contexts

> [!summary] Summary
> GHOST tackles text-and-scene-conditional human motion generation by replacing the closed-vocabulary scene encoder in HUMANISE cVAE with an open-vocabulary Point Transformer distilled from CLIP-aligned 2D segmentation teachers, adding bounding-box and category regularization losses; achieves up to 30% goal-object distance reduction and is preferred 63.27% of the time in a perceptual study.

## Problem

- Closed vocabulary scene encoders require learning text-scene associations from scratch, since their output space mismatches the open vocabulary text encoder's embedding space.
- HUMANISE cVAE shows a bias toward generating motions centered in the scene, with failure cases placing the human far from the intended goal object.
- No prior text-and-scene-conditional motion generation method leverages internet-scale vision-language pretraining knowledge for grounding, forcing grounding to be learned solely from the limited HUMANISE dataset.

## Method

- Pretrains a ==Point Transformer== U-Net scene encoder via ==knowledge distillation== from an open vocabulary 2D segmentation teacher (LSeg, OpenSeg, or OVSeg), aligning scene features to ==CLIP== text space using the ==OpenScene cosine-similarity loss==.
- Fine-tunes the distilled scene encoder jointly with a ==cVAE== motion generator built on the HUMANISE motion module, fusing text and scene features via ==self-attention==.
- Introduces two novel regularization losses, a ==bounding-box corner regression loss== and a ==9-class ScanNet category classification loss==, that sharpen goal-object awareness beyond the prior center-point regression alone.

## Results

- On the HUMANISE dataset, the OpenSeg variant reduces goal object distance by **41.8 cm** on the walk subset and **27.6 cm** overall (**1.008 m** to **0.732 m**) versus the HUMANISE cVAE baseline.
- All 27 participants in a 2AFC perceptual study preferred GHOST OpenSeg outputs, yielding a **63.27%** overall preference rate against the baseline's **36.73%**.
- Ablations show swapping the text or scene encoder back to closed-vocabulary/BERT versions hurts most (distance rises to **1.021-1.425 m**), while removing bbox or class losses causes smaller but still measurable degradation (**0.982-1.011 m**) versus the full model's **0.952 m**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Aligning multimodal conditioning signals in a pretrained shared embedding space before fusion can substitute for learning cross-modal grounding purely from a task-specific dataset.
> - Auxiliary geometric regularizers, like regressing object size and category, provide a cheap way to inject spatial grounding signal beyond simple center-point targets.
> - The open-vocabulary teacher choice matters less than the architectural shift itself, since all three distilled variants (LSeg, OpenSeg, OVSeg) substantially outperform the closed-vocabulary baseline.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The generation of human pose and motion within 3D scenes is a foundational task in several domains, including visual effects, video games, virtual and augmented reality, and robotics. This capability is essential for creating realistic and expressive human animations that harmoniously integrate with their 3D environments, taking into account geometry, lighting, and physical attributes. However, a recurring limitation in existing methods has been the lack of precise control over the motion generation process, often relying on coarse assumptions about object location within a scene.

Concurrent advancements in text-conditional generation have revolutionized synthetic data generation across various modalities, such as images, videos, 3D scenes, 3D character shapes, and human motion. These developments have enabled more intuitive control over generation through natural language. Nevertheless, many text-conditional motion generation methods do not explicitly incorporate 3D scene context. Bridging the gap between these two modalities—scene understanding and text-based control—is a critical area for developing more sophisticated motion synthesis approaches.

The HUMANISE dataset and its accompanying Conditional Variational Autoencoder (cVAE) architecture represent the primary prior work in text-and-scene-conditional human motion generation. This dataset combines AMASS motions with ScanNet scenes and compositional text annotations. Despite its contributions, the HUMANISE cVAE demonstrated limitations, such as a reported average distance of approximately 1 meter from the goal object during motion sampling and qualitative failures where characters were positioned far from the intended interaction point, often biased towards the center of the scene. The paper attributes this issue to the use of a scene encoder pre-trained for closed-vocabulary semantic segmentation, which predicts a fixed set of categorical labels. This creates a mismatch with the open-vocabulary nature of the text encoder, which can embed a broader range of descriptions. Consequently, the scene encoder is compelled to learn text-scene grounding from scratch on the HUMANISE dataset, which, despite its size, is insufficient for robust grounding in this complex multimodal task.

This work addresses the challenge of inaccurate motion grounding by integrating open-vocabulary scene understanding into the motion generation framework. It builds upon recent progress in Vision-Language Models (VLMs) like CLIP and open-vocabulary 3D scene understanding methods such as OpenScene. These models enable the alignment of visual and text modalities in a shared feature space, facilitating querying with natural language prompts without predefined semantic categories. By leveraging these advancements, the proposed method aims to establish a more robust and semantically informed connection between textual descriptions and 3D scene context for human motion generation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance text-and-scene-conditional human motion generation by improving the precision of motion grounding and placement within complex 3D scenes. The authors aim to enable virtual humans to interact with specific objects described in natural language, accurately localized within their environment.

The motivation for this work stems from several limitations observed in existing methodologies:

- **Inaccurate Motion Grounding:** Prior work, specifically the HUMANISE cVAE, exhibits a bias towards generating human motions centered within the scene or at a significant distance from the intended goal object. This reduces the realism and utility of generated animations, as humans often interact with specific, spatially defined objects.
- **Mismatch between Modalities:** The current state-of-the-art methods typically employ a scene encoder pre-trained for closed-vocabulary semantic segmentation. This design choice leads to a discrepancy between the fixed, categorical output space of the scene encoder and the flexible, open-vocabulary feature space of text encoders, which can represent a diverse range of objects and concepts through natural language.
- **Inefficient Grounding Learning:** Due to the aforementioned mismatch, existing systems are often forced to learn text-scene grounding from scratch during the conditional motion generation training phase. Although datasets like HUMANISE are large, they are not tailored for teaching fine-grained text-scene relationships in an open-vocabulary manner, leading to sub-optimal grounding performance.
- **Lack of Spatial Awareness in VLMs:** While Vision-Language Models (VLMs) like CLIP are powerful for associating images and text, they often prioritize identifying foreground objects and lack strong inductive biases for encoding precise 3D spatial relationships (e.g., "where" an object is located relative to others or the human). Current open-vocabulary 3D scene understanding methods, such as OpenScene, also primarily focus on "what" objects are present rather than their precise interaction locations.
- **Need for Finer Control:** The ability to control human motion generation with both descriptive language and explicit 3D scene context is crucial for practical applications in computer graphics, robotics, and virtual environments. Achieving this requires a robust mechanism for the model to understand and leverage the spatial semantics of the scene in relation to textual prompts.

To address these motivations, the authors propose a framework that establishes a strong initial text-scene alignment through open-vocabulary pretraining and subsequently refines this grounding during conditional motion generation using novel regularization techniques.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors introduce GHOST (Grounded Human Motion Generation with Open Vocabulary Scene-and-Text Contexts), a framework built upon a Conditional Variational Autoencoder (cVAE) architecture designed to model the conditional probability of human motion parameters (Θ) given a tokenized language description (L) and an RGB-colored scene point cloud (S). The methodology primarily focuses on improving the text-scene grounding component of the cVAE.

#### 3.1. Problem Definition and Notations

The goal is to generate a sequence of human motion parameters Θ (global translation, global orientation, body pose) of length T, conditioned on a text description L and a scene point cloud S. The differentiable SMPL-X body model is used to obtain human meshes M_t for each motion frame.

#### 3.2. Proposed Solution Overview

GHOST largely adopts the motion module of the HUMANISE cVAE but significantly modifies the condition module to enhance text-scene grounding. The approach consists of two main steps: pretraining and training.

##### 3.2.1. Motion Module

The motion module's architecture remains consistent with the original HUMANISE cVAE. It comprises a motion encoder (Enc_ψ) and a motion decoder (Dec_ϕ). The encoder utilizes a bidirectional GRU layer, concatenates its output with the conditional latent (z_c), and includes a residual block and linear layers to output the mean (μ) and covariance (Σ) of a Gaussian distribution. A latent variable (z) is sampled using the reparametrization trick. The decoder combines z and z_c via a linear layer, incorporates sinusoidal positional embedding, a transformer decoder, and a final linear output layer to reconstruct the motion (Θ̂).

##### 3.2.2. Condition Module

The condition module (Cond_ω) processes the tokenized text (L) and scene point cloud (S) to output the conditional latent (z_c). Key architectural differences from HUMANISE cVAE include:
- **Text Encoder:** The text encoder is adopted from an open-vocabulary image segmentation model, with its parameters frozen during fine-tuning.
- **Scene Encoder (E_3D):** A Point Transformer (PT) U-Net architecture is used, featuring 5 encoder stages (transition down modules and PT Blocks) and 5 decoder stages (transition up modules and PT Blocks with residual skip connections). A downsampling module uses farthest point sampling and average pooling. The output head includes ReLU and a linear layer.
- **Fusion Module:** Text and scene features are concatenated, passed through a Self-Attention layer, dense ReLU, and linear layers to obtain a fused scene feature. This is then concatenated with the SMPL-X body shape (β) and transformed by a linear layer to produce z_c.

##### 3.2.3. Pretraining Phase

This phase focuses on establishing a robust text-scene relationship before motion generation begins.
- **Knowledge Distillation:** Instead of closed-vocabulary pretraining, the PT U-Net scene encoder (E_3D) is pretrained through knowledge distillation using the OpenScene loss. This involves maximizing the cosine similarity (L_cos) between the scene encoder's output and text-aligned 2D pixel features generated by an open-vocabulary image segmentation teacher model (E_2D).
- **Teacher Models:** The framework is designed to be plug-and-play, accommodating various open-vocabulary image segmentation models as teachers, specifically LSeg, OpenSeg, and OVSeg. The corresponding CLIP text encoder parameters of the teacher model are adopted and frozen. This step aligns the 3D scene representations with the shared CLIP text feature space.

##### 3.2.4. Training Phase

During this phase, the conditional motion generator is trained, and the pretrained scene encoder is fine-tuned.
- **Loss Function:** The overall objective function (L) comprises a reconstruction loss (L_rec) and a regularization loss (L_reg).
 - **Reconstruction Loss (L_rec):** An ℓ1 loss calculated for global translation (L_t), global orientation (L_r), body pose (L_θ), and canonical mesh vertices (L_M).
 - **Regularization Loss (L_reg):** Includes:
 - Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence (L_kl) to encourage a standard Gaussian latent distribution for z.
 - Action classification loss (L_action) and goal object center coordinate regression loss (L_center), similar to HUMANISE.
 - **Novel Grounding Losses:**
 - **Goal object bounding box corner regression (L_bbox):** A mean squared error loss to regress the axis-aligned bounding box coordinates of the goal object, enhancing spatial awareness of object size and extent.
 - **Goal object classification (L_class):** A cross-entropy loss for classifying the goal object into one of 9 ScanNet categories, improving awareness of the object's semantic category.
- **Fine-tuning:** The scene encoder weights are fine-tuned during this stage, while the text encoder remains frozen. This allows the model to learn 3D spatial relationships ("where") beyond just object identification ("what").

#### 3.3. Experimental Setup

- **Dataset:** HUMANISE (19,648 AMASS motion sequences aligned with 643 ScanNet scenes, 1.2 million frames) for four actions: walk, sit, stand up, and lie. SMPL-X model parameters (72 per frame), motion sequences padded to T=120 frames, and N=32,768 scene vertices sampled from meshes. Text annotations follow Sr3D template.
- **Training Details:** Models trained for 150 epochs using Adam optimizer (learning rate 10^-4, batch size 24). Specific weights (λ) were manually tuned for the various loss components. Fine-tuning of the scene encoder used a learning rate of 10^-5.
- **Teacher Models for Distillation:** Three variants of GHOST were implemented, each distilled from a different open-vocabulary image segmentation teacher: LSeg (with CLIP ViT-B/32), OpenSeg (with CLIP ViT-L/14@336px), and OVSeg (with CLIP ViT-L/14).
- **Baseline:** The unmodified HUMANISE cVAE.
- **Ablation Study:** Conducted on the 'walk' subset to evaluate the impact of replacing the GHOST text encoder with BERT, replacing the GHOST scene encoder with a closed-vocabulary variant, and individually removing the L_bbox or L_class regularization losses.

#### 3.4. Evaluation Metrics

- **Generation (Goal Object Distance):** The primary metric is `d(L, S)`, the mean distance between K generated humans and the goal object. This is computed using the positive Signed Distance Function (SDF) of the human mesh evaluated at the goal object's points. The minimum distance is taken, and negative values (penetration) are clamped to zero. Evaluated at the last frame for walk, sit, lie, and the first frame for stand up, with K=10 samples.
- **Perceptual Study:** A Two-Alternative Forced Choice (2AFC) user study with 27 participants. Participants evaluated 60 pairs of videos (one from HUMANISE cVAE, one from GHOST OpenSeg) generated from 20 text-scene combinations, selecting the video that better aligned with the text description.
- Additional metrics for motion reconstruction quality and diversity were referenced in the supplementary material but not detailed in the main paper.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides quantitative and qualitative evidence for the effectiveness of the GHOST framework in improving text-and-scene-conditional human motion generation.

#### 4.1. Quantitative Results

- **Goal Object Distance (Table 1):** All three implementations of the GHOST framework demonstrated significant reductions in the goal object distance metric compared to the HUMANISE cVAE baseline.
 - The **GHOST OpenSeg** variant achieved the most substantial improvement, reducing the distance by 41.8 cm on the 'walk' action subset and by 27.6 cm across the entire dataset (from 1.008 m to 0.732 m).
 - For the 'lie' subset, GHOST LSeg showed the greatest reduction, marginally outperforming OpenSeg.
 - These results indicate that GHOST models consistently placed the generated human characters closer to the intended goal objects.

- **Perceptual Study (Table 2):** A 2AFC user study involving 27 participants provided evidence for the improved perceptual quality and textual alignment of GHOST's generated motions.
 - Motions generated by the **GHOST OpenSeg** model were preferred over those from the HUMANISE cVAE baseline in 63.27% of cases.
 - All 27 participants unanimously preferred the motions generated by the GHOST model when forced to choose, suggesting a clear subjective improvement in motion grounding and coherence with the text descriptions.

- **Ablation Study (Table 3):** An ablation study conducted on the 'walk' action subset highlighted the importance of GHOST's components:
 - Replacing GHOST's text encoder with a BERT text encoder (a closed-vocabulary alternative) resulted in the highest goal object distance (1.425 m), indicating the critical role of the open-vocabulary text-scene alignment.
 - Replacing GHOST's open-vocabulary scene encoder with a closed-vocabulary counterpart also led to a significant increase in goal object distance (1.021 m), reinforcing the value of open-vocabulary scene understanding.
 - The proposed regularization losses, `L_bbox` (bounding box regression) and `L_class` (goal object classification), also contributed to performance. Removing `L_bbox` (setting λ_bbox = 0) increased the distance to 1.011 m, and removing `L_class` (setting λ_class = 0) increased it to 0.982 m. This indicates that while the open-vocabulary pretraining has the largest impact, these specific regularization terms further refine grounding. Adjusting the weight of `L_class` also showed sensitivity, with λ_class = 1.0 yielding 0.970 m, slightly better than 0.982 m for λ_class = 0, and 0.995 m for λ_class = 0.1, compared to the optimal 0.952m with λ_class = 0.5.

#### 4.2. Qualitative Results

- **Comparison with Baseline (Figures 1, 4):** Qualitative comparisons illustrated that the HUMANISE cVAE often failed to accurately locate the goal object, tending to position the human character towards the scene's center. In contrast, the GHOST OpenSeg model successfully identified the goal object, placed the human closer to it, and generated plausible interactions. Attention maps further indicated the baseline's bias towards the scene center, while GHOST demonstrated better grounding.
- **Ablation Study (Figure 5):** Qualitative results from the ablation study visually supported the quantitative findings, showing that the full GHOST model with both bounding box and classification regularization losses resulted in more accurate human placement near the goal object compared to variants where these losses were removed. The bounding box loss appeared particularly crucial for localizing the target object by inferring its size.

#### 4.3. Computational Analysis

- GHOST variants exhibited larger parameter counts, ranging from 1.5× to 3.9× more parameters than the HUMANISE cVAE baseline, with the scene encoder being 1.6× larger and producing 16× to 24× larger representations.
- Despite the increased complexity, motion sampling time was only 1.3× longer, approximately 0.19 seconds on an NVIDIA A100 GPU.

#### 4.4. Limitations

The paper acknowledges several limitations of the current GHOST solution:
- Despite improvements, the model still exhibited some goal identification errors, incorrect fine-grained orientations of the generated human, and occasional scene penetrations.
- Attention maps could still show a bias towards the center of the scene in some instances.
- The framework's generalization capabilities to more natural texts and a broader range of actions require further investigation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The GHOST framework represents an advancement in the field of text-and-scene-conditional human motion generation by directly addressing the challenge of accurate motion grounding.

**Advancement in Grounding:** By integrating an open-vocabulary scene encoder and pretraining it through knowledge distillation from existing 2D open-vocabulary semantic segmentation models, the research establishes a robust and semantically rich connection between textual descriptions and 3D scene geometry. This approach mitigates the limitations of closed-vocabulary systems that struggle with diverse, natural language instructions and complex scenes. The alignment of text and 3D scene features in a shared CLIP space enables more precise understanding of interaction targets.

**Improved Motion Localization and Realism:** The significant reduction in goal object distance and the positive results from the perceptual study demonstrate that GHOST generates human motions that are more accurately localized and contextually appropriate. This leads to more realistic and plausible human-scene interactions, which is critical for applications requiring high fidelity and immersion.

**Flexible and Extensible Framework:** The plug-and-play design for integrating different open-vocabulary teacher models (LSeg, OpenSeg, OVSeg) makes GHOST adaptable to future advancements in 2D segmentation and vision-language understanding. This modularity allows the framework to benefit from ongoing research in related fields without requiring extensive re-engineering.

**Broader Practical Implications:** The capability to generate contextually aware human motions with precise control has direct implications for various industries and research areas:
- **Visual Effects and Video Games:** Enables automated creation of more convincing character animations and interactions within virtual worlds.
- **Virtual and Augmented Reality:** Facilitates the populating of virtual environments with intelligent agents that respond realistically to user commands and scene elements.
- **Robotics:** Can inform human-robot interaction design, allowing robots to better anticipate and understand human actions and intentions within shared environments.
- **Synthetic Data Generation:** Provides a tool for generating high-quality synthetic datasets of human-scene interactions, which can be used to train other vision and robotics models.

**Catalyst for Future Research:** The work highlights areas for further exploration, such as leveraging more advanced Vision-Language Models, incorporating contact optimization for enhanced physical realism, and extending grounding to non-goal objects and more complex natural language instructions. The success of GHOST in improving localization is anticipated to encourage further research into sophisticated techniques for human motion generation.
