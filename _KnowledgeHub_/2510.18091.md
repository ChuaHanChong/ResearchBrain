# Accelerating Vision Transformers with Adaptive Patch Sizes

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Carnegie Mellon University and KAIST developed Adaptive Patch Transformers (APT) to accelerate Vision Transformers by dynamically adjusting patch sizes based on image content. This approach yields substantial speedups in both training and inference across various visual tasks while fully maintaining or slightly improving downstream performance.

## Problem

- Vision Transformers (ViTs) suffer from quadratic computational complexity due to self-attention, making them slow and expensive for high-resolution images.
- Standard ViTs use fixed-size image patches, wasting computation on visually homogeneous or redundant regions and failing to allocate finer-grained attention to complex areas.
- Existing token reduction methods often introduce practical inefficiencies (e.g., irregular shapes, padding) or require extensive fine-tuning, limiting their real-world applicability and training speedups.

## Method

- ==APT== implements a ==content-aware, multi-scale patchification strategy==, using entropy to dynamically assign larger patches to homogeneous regions and smaller patches to detailed areas.
- A novel ==patch aggregation mechanism== employs a zero-initialized MLP to convert variable-sized patches into uniform embeddings, enabling APT to rapidly adapt to pre-trained ViT models with minimal fine-tuning.
- Dynamic token counts are efficiently handled through ==sequence packing and block-diagonal attention masks==, which are optimized by modern attention backends like FlashAttention.

## Results

- Achieved up to an **86%** speedup in ImageNet training for ViT-L at 448x448 resolution and up to **23%** throughput increase for LLaVA-1.5-13B on VQA benchmarks, maintaining or exceeding baseline performance.
- Reduced input tokens by **30%** and attained a **30%** speedup in object detection (COCO) and an **11%** speedup in semantic segmentation (ADE20K) using EVA-02 backbones, matching baseline accuracy.
- APT effectively allocates patches, using fewer tokens for homogeneous regions and more for complex areas, confirming its content-aware processing and achieving rapid convergence (**1 epoch**) for fine-tuning pre-trained models.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Adopting a variable-granularity, content-aware tokenization strategy, similar to those in NLP, can drastically improve the computational efficiency of Vision Transformers.
> - A zero-initialized MLP enables a "plug-and-play" capability, allowing APT to be applied to existing pre-trained ViT backbones and converge in as little as one fine-tuning epoch.
> - Input-level token reduction, when guided by image content, provides more robust and practical speedups compared to fixed-ratio or layer-level token merging/pruning methods.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision Transformers (ViTs) have become a prominent architecture in visual recognition. However, a significant limitation of standard ViTs is their quadratic computational cost with respect to the input sequence length, largely due to the fixed-size patchification of images. This approach generates a disproportionately large number of tokens for high-resolution images, leading to substantial computational redundancy, especially in visually homogeneous areas.

Prior research has attempted to address this efficiency bottleneck through several strategies. One common approach involves reducing the number of tokens by merging similar ones or pruning uninformative tokens using auxiliary predictors. While these methods aim to reduce theoretical Floating Point Operations (FLOPs), they often face practical drawbacks. Fixed reduction ratios may not adapt optimally to varying image complexities, potentially leading to insufficient reduction in simple scenes or detrimental information loss in complex ones. Furthermore, pruning during the forward pass can introduce padding and irregular data shapes, which can negate practical speedups on modern hardware optimized for fixed-size computations.

In contrast, language models frequently employ adaptive tokenizers, such as Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) and SentencePiece, which dynamically assign tokens of varying lengths based on subword frequency. This content-aware tokenization has been shown to reduce input sequence size while often improving performance, suggesting a potential paradigm for vision models.

The broader landscape of ViT acceleration also includes methods focusing on alternative attention mechanisms (e.g., linearized or local window attention), compressed representations, and quantization. More directly related to the current work are methods that exploit image redundancy by pruning or merging tokens. However, many token pruning methods necessitate learning which tokens to remove, thereby negating training speedups and often restricting batch sizes. Token merging approaches, while accelerating training, frequently combine a constant number of tokens regardless of input complexity, which can be suboptimal.

Existing efforts in adaptive patch sizing for ViTs have explored models capable of using different patch sizes, but often restrict a single patch size per image. Other works allow varying patch sizes within an image, but may rely on a fixed total number of patches, neglect semantic variability, depend on attention scores (hindering efficient kernel use), or require training from scratch. More recent multi-scale approaches, such as MS-ViT, learn a gating network to determine patch sizes and use separate patch embedding networks for each size, but typically require extensive fine-tuning and may not accelerate training.

This work positions itself within this landscape by introducing a content-aware, adaptive patch sizing mechanism for ViTs that aims to provide significant wall-clock speedups for both training and inference. It seeks to overcome the limitations of prior token reduction and adaptive patching methods by avoiding fixed reduction ratios, enabling compatibility with efficient attention implementations like FlashAttention, allowing for application to pre-trained models with minimal fine-tuning, and extending its benefits to a wider array of downstream visual tasks beyond classification.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to significantly accelerate Vision Transformer (ViT) inference and training by introducing an adaptive patch sizing mechanism. This mechanism, named Adaptive Patch Transformer (APT), aims to reduce the total number of input tokens processed by the transformer.

The core motivation behind APT stems from the observation that conventional ViTs partition input images into uniformly sized patches, irrespective of the visual content within those patches. This uniform division leads to several inefficiencies:
1. **Redundancy in Homogeneous Regions:** Large areas of an image that are visually uniform (e.g., a clear sky, a plain background) are broken down into numerous small patches, each consuming computational resources. These patches often convey minimal unique information.
2. **Excessive Sequence Lengths for High-Resolution Images:** For high-resolution inputs, the fixed patch size policy results in an extremely long sequence of tokens. Since the computational cost of self-attention scales quadratically with sequence length, this drastically increases processing time and memory consumption.
3. **Mismatch with Visual Complexity:** The uniform approach assigns the same amount of computation to simple, redundant regions as to complex, detail-rich areas, which is an inefficient allocation of resources.
4. **Inspiration from Language Models:** Language models benefit from adaptive tokenizers that vary token lengths based on subword frequency, reducing sequence size and improving efficiency. The authors hypothesize that a similar content-aware approach could be beneficial for vision models.

Building on these motivations, the key objectives for APT are:
- **Content-Aware Patch Allocation:** To assign larger patch sizes to visually homogeneous areas (low complexity/redundancy) and smaller patch sizes to visually complex or detailed regions. This strategy aims to preserve critical information where it is needed while drastically reducing redundant tokens elsewhere.
- **Significant Speedup:** To achieve a substantial wall-clock speedup in both ViT inference and training, particularly for larger models and higher-resolution images, quantified by increased throughput and reduced training time.
- **Performance Preservation:** To maintain or closely match the downstream performance (e.g., accuracy in classification, mAP in detection, mIoU in segmentation) of standard ViTs, ensuring that efficiency gains do not come at the cost of model quality.
- **Ease of Integration:** To enable the application of APT to previously fine-tuned ViT models with minimal additional training, ideally converging to baseline performance in a small number of epochs.
- **Broad Task Applicability:** To demonstrate the effectiveness of APT across a diverse range of computer vision tasks, including image classification, visual question answering (VQA), object detection, and semantic segmentation, showcasing its generalizability beyond simple recognition tasks.
- **Efficient Implementation:** To integrate adaptive tokenization with modern, efficient attention mechanisms (e.g., FlashAttention) and handling dynamic input sizes without incurring prohibitive overheads.

By achieving these objectives, APT aims to make ViTs more scalable, computationally efficient, and broadly applicable, thereby reducing the computational burden for developing and deploying state-of-the-art visual recognition systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Adaptive Patch Transformer (APT) introduces a content-aware patchification strategy to reduce the input sequence length for Vision Transformers (ViTs) while preserving performance. The methodology involves three main components: deciding patch sizes, aggregating embeddings from different-sized patches, and efficiently handling dynamic input sizes.

#### 3.1. Deciding Patch Sizes

APT aims to vary patch sizes within a single image based on content, moving away from the standard uniform patch division.
- **Multi-Scale Patches:** A fixed number of patch scales, $S$, is defined. Patches can have sizes $2^i p \times 2^i p$, where $p \times p$ is the smallest base patch size (e.g., 16x16) and $i$ ranges from 0 to $S-1$. For example, with $S=3$ and $p=16$, patches can be $16 \times 16$, $32 \times 32$, or $64 \times 64$. All patches adhere to a quadtree-like hierarchical structure.
- **Entropy as a Measure of Complexity:** The information content or compressibility of a patch is quantified using Shannon entropy, $H(P) = -\sum p_i \log_2 p_i$. Here, $p_i$ represents the probability of pixel intensity $i$. Pixel intensities are binned to approximate this for discrete values. Lower entropy indicates higher redundancy and thus greater compressibility.
- **Hierarchical Patchification Algorithm:**
 1. The image is initially divided into patches at the coarsest scale (largest patch size, $2^S p \times 2^S p$).
 2. For each patch at this coarsest scale, its entropy is computed.
 3. Patches with entropy below a predefined threshold, $\tau_S$ (a tunable hyperparameter), are retained at this large scale.
 4. The regions corresponding to patches *above* the threshold are then recursively subdivided into patches of the next finer scale ($2^{S-1} p \times 2^{S-1} p$).
 5. This process is repeated for progressively finer scales, computing entropy and applying a scale-specific threshold ($\tau_i$), until the smallest base patch size ($p \times p$) is reached. All remaining regions are assigned patches of this base size. This ensures content-rich regions are represented by smaller, more numerous tokens, while homogeneous regions are covered by fewer, larger tokens.

#### 3.2. Patch Aggregation

After generating patches of various sizes, APT needs to convert them into a uniform embedding space (dimension $d_{embed}$) suitable for the transformer encoder.
- **Combining Strategies:** The approach combines resizing and sub-patch information.
- **Base Patch Embedding:** The standard linear layer $E$ is used to embed the smallest $p \times p$ patches directly.
- **Larger Patch Processing:** For a larger patch $P_i$ of size $2^i p \times 2^i p$:
 1. It is resized to the base $p \times p$ size, and its embedding is computed using the standard linear layer $E(Resize_p(P_i))$. This provides a coarse representation.
 2. Concurrently, the larger patch $P_i$ is conceptually divided into its constituent $p \times p$ sub-patches $\{P_j\}$. Each sub-patch $P_j$ is then embedded using the standard layer $E(P_j)$.
 3. These sub-patch embeddings are aggregated using a series of convolutional downsampling layers, Conv2d(i), which reduce the collection of sub-patch embeddings back to a single embedding of size $p \times p$. This process aims to retain higher-resolution details from the sub-patches.
 4. The aggregated sub-patch embeddings and the resized large patch embedding are combined. This combination occurs via a **ZeroMLP** (a single linear layer initialized with zero weights). The ZeroMLP allows the model to gradually incorporate the higher-resolution details from the aggregated sub-patches without degrading initial performance, facilitating rapid convergence during fine-tuning (e.g., in just one epoch). The final embedding for $P_i$ is: $E(P_i) = ZeroMLP(\text{Conv2d}^{(i)}(\{E(P_j) | P_j \subset P_i\})) + E(\text{Resize}_p(P_i))$.

#### 3.3. Dynamic Input Sizes

Since the number of tokens varies per image, APT employs strategies to handle dynamic input sequences efficiently.
- **Sequence Packing:** For a batch of images with varying token counts $\{N_1, N_2, \ldots, N_B\}$, all tokens are concatenated into a single long sequence. A block-diagonal attention mask is constructed to ensure that tokens only attend to other tokens from the same original image. This method is compatible with efficient attention backends like FlashAttention and xFormers, incurring minimal overhead. After network processing, the output sequence is split back into individual image representations.
- **Positional Encodings:** Positional encoding interpolation, adapted from NaViT, is used. The base $p \times p$ grid is assigned initial positional encodings. For larger patches of size $sp \times sp$, their positional encodings are obtained by sampling and interpolating from the original $H/p \times W/p$ encoding map, maintaining spatial consistency across scales.

#### 3.4. Adaptation to Downstream Tasks

APT extends its applicability beyond image classification to dense visual tasks.
- **Feature Map Reconstruction:** For tasks like object detection or semantic segmentation that require a feature map with the original image's aspect ratio, tokens corresponding to larger patches ($2^i p \times 2^i p$) are repeated $2^{2i}$ times. This creates a differentiable feature map that can be upsampled by transposed convolutions.
- **Window Attention Compatibility:** For tasks using window attention, the image is divided into windows that are multiples of the largest patch size. The adaptive patching strategy is applied within each window, resulting in windows with variable token counts. Sequence packing and attention masks are then used for efficient processing within these windows.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research demonstrates that Adaptive Patch Transformers (APT) offer significant speedups in Vision Transformer (ViT) training and inference while maintaining or exceeding performance across various visual tasks.

#### 4.1. Image Classification

- **Full Fine-Tuning:** When fine-tuning from a self-supervised pre-trained backbone (MAE checkpoints), APT consistently matched baseline accuracy across different ViT models and resolutions. For example, on ViT-L with 448x14 resolution, APT achieved an 86% speedup, reducing training wall-clock time from 31.4 hours to 16.9 hours, effectively nearly halving the training duration without accuracy degradation (86.3% vs. 86.4%). Speedups were more pronounced at higher resolutions and for larger models, where attention computation dominates training time.
- **Short Fine-Tuning (1-epoch):** When applied to an already fine-tuned ImageNet checkpoint, APT incurred a small initial accuracy drop. However, with just one additional epoch of fine-tuning, enabled by the zero-initialized MLP, APT recovered and matched or slightly exceeded the original model's performance. For instance, ViT-H 336/14 showed 88.4% accuracy (APT) versus 88.5% (baseline) with a 50% speedup (263 vs. 175 Img/s throughput). APT consistently outperformed other input-level merging baselines (Random, Resizing) and advanced layer-level merging methods (EViT, ToMe, DTEM), even when these were re-implemented with FlashAttention for fairer comparison. APT exhibited a superior trade-off between accuracy and throughput across different compute budgets.

#### 4.2. Visual Question Answering (VQA)

- APT was applied to the vision backbone of LLaVA-1.5 models (7B and 13B parameters). By reducing visual tokens, APT accelerated both the vision backbone and cross-attention layers.
- It achieved a 22-23% increase in throughput (e.g., LLaVA-1.5-7B: 4.51 Img/s vs. 3.70 Img/s) while matching or slightly exceeding the baseline performance across a range of VQA benchmarks (VQAv2, GQA, SQA-I, VQAT, POPE, MME, MMB, MMBC, MMV). This indicates that APT does not compromise the visual acuity required for complex vision-language understanding.

#### 4.3. Dense Visual Tasks (Object Detection and Semantic Segmentation)

- **Object Detection:** Using an EVA-02 backbone with window attention and a ViTDet-style head on the COCO dataset at 1536x1536 resolution, APT reduced input tokens by up to 30%. This resulted in a significant speedup (e.g., EVA-02-L: 2.17 Img/s vs. 1.62 Img/s, +30%) while maintaining mAP and AP50 performance (e.g., EVA-02-L APT: 62.07 mAP vs. 62.28 baseline mAP). This demonstrates APT's effectiveness even with high-resolution inputs and window attention.
- **Semantic Segmentation:** For semantic segmentation on ADE20K, using an EVA-02 backbone with a UperNet head, APT delivered an 11% speedup (e.g., EVA-02-L 640: 2.83 Img/s vs. 2.55 Img/s). It matched baseline performance for metrics like pixel accuracy (aAcc) and mean Intersection over Union (mIoU) (e.g., EVA-02-L 640 APT: 86.82 aAcc, 60.01 mIoU vs. 86.83 aAcc, 60.05 mIoU baseline). This indicates that APT can handle fine-grained, pixel-level tasks without sacrificing visual detail.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies

- **APT Overhead:** Without any token reduction (setting a threshold that retains all patches), APT incurs approximately 10% overhead compared to a standard ViT. However, the benefits from token reduction (typically 20%+ speedups) significantly outweigh this overhead, leading to a net gain. The entropy computation is performed on the CPU dataloader, amortizing its cost.
- **Zero-initialization:** The ZeroMLP mechanism was found to be crucial. It provided the best off-the-shelf accuracy for newly introduced components and facilitated the strongest performance after a single epoch of fine-tuning, compared to simple residual connections or non-zero initialized layers.
- **Thresholds:** The entropy threshold is a key tunable parameter. Increasing the threshold generally increases throughput due to more aggressive token reduction. However, beyond certain values (e.g., $\tau \approx 6.0$), accuracy begins to degrade significantly, suggesting a limit to compressibility without information loss. Object detection tasks typically required lower thresholds due to their reliance on precise edge localization.
- **Scorer Analysis:** Comparing entropy, Laplacian, and upsampling-based scorers for patch compressibility, the entropy scorer performed comparably or slightly better at higher token reductions, although differences were generally small.
- **Augmentation Analysis:** Data augmentations like random erasing can lead to fewer tokens being retained by APT, as they often create more homogeneous regions. This suggests that APT might yield even higher speedup gains during training compared to inference.

#### 4.5. Visualizations

Qualitative visualizations confirm that APT consistently allocates larger patches to visually homogeneous regions (e.g., plain backgrounds, blurry areas) and smaller, finer-grained patches to complex areas rich in detail (e.g., faces, textured objects). The number of patches varies naturally with image complexity, illustrating the content-adaptive nature of the approach.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Adaptive Patch Transformer (APT) presents a significant advancement in the efficiency of Vision Transformers (ViTs), with several implications for the broader vision community:

- **Alleviating ViT Scalability Bottlenecks:** APT directly addresses a fundamental limitation of traditional ViTs: the quadratic scaling of self-attention with respect to input sequence length and the inefficient fixed-size patchification. By dynamically adjusting patch sizes based on content, it offers a principled way to manage token counts, especially crucial for high-resolution images and larger models.
- **Substantial Computational Savings:** The reported speedups (e.g., 40% on ViT-L, 50% on ViT-H, and up to 86% reduction in training wall-clock time) are substantial. These gains translate directly into reduced computational costs, energy consumption, and environmental impact associated with training and deploying large-scale ViT models. This can make advanced vision models more accessible to researchers and practitioners with limited computational resources.
- **Preservation of Performance:** Crucially, APT achieves these efficiency gains without compromising downstream task performance. Its ability to maintain or even slightly exceed baseline accuracy across diverse benchmarks (ImageNet, VQA, object detection, semantic segmentation) ensures that its benefits are practical and deployable.
- **Ease of Adoption:** The methodology's compatibility with pre-trained ViT backbones and its rapid convergence (as little as one epoch of fine-tuning) significantly lowers the barrier to adoption. Users can integrate APT into existing workflows with minimal disruption and retraining effort, extending the lifespan and utility of their pre-trained models.
- **Broadened Applicability:** Demonstrating effectiveness across image classification, visual question answering, object detection, and semantic segmentation highlights APT's generalizability. This indicates that content-aware tokenization can enhance efficiency in a wide spectrum of vision tasks, including those requiring fine-grained spatial understanding.
- **Methodological Innovation:** The combination of hierarchical entropy-based patch allocation, the novel patch aggregation scheme with a zero-initialized MLP, and efficient handling of dynamic input sizes via sequence packing offers a robust and well-engineered solution. The zero-initialized MLP is particularly impactful, enabling seamless integration and rapid fine-tuning.
- **Inspiration for Future Research:** The paper identifies several limitations, such as the reliance on a hand-crafted heuristic for patch size determination and empirically tuned hyperparameters. This provides clear avenues for future work, potentially inspiring research into learned adaptive tokenization or more robust parameter-free methods. The stated limitation regarding image generation tasks also suggests a promising direction for extending APT's utility.

In summary, APT represents a practical and effective solution for accelerating Vision Transformers, mitigating a critical bottleneck in their scalability. Its demonstrated efficiency and robust performance across various tasks suggest it has the potential to become a standard component in the development and deployment of future ViT-based computer vision systems.
