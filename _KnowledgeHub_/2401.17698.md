# Bi-ACT: Bilateral Control-Based Imitation Learning via Action Chunking with Transformer

> [!summary] Summary
> Bi-ACT extends Action Chunking with Transformer to bilateral leader-follower teleoperation, feeding follower joint angles, angular velocities, torques and two RGB camera views into a CVAE-transformer that predicts chunks of the leader's next angle, velocity and force commands at 100 Hz. Adding force to both data collection and control lets the arm adapt grip to object hardness and weight, reaching 100% success on pick-and-place of trained objects and 80-100% on seven untrained ones.

## Problem

- ALOHA-style leader-follower data collection uses position control only, so demonstrations carry no force information and policies cannot discern object hardness or weight.
- Existing bilateral control-based imitation learning relies mostly on LSTM backbones and omits image input, limiting robustness to changes in the operating environment.
- Single-step imitation policies suffer compounding error and struggle with temporally correlated confounders such as operator pauses during demonstrations.

## Method

- **Bi-ACT** augments the ==ACT== ==CVAE-transformer== input with a 15-dimensional follower state (angle, angular velocity, torque across five joints) plus gripper and overhead 360x640 RGB images.
- ==Action chunking== outputs a k x 15 tensor of the leader's future angle, velocity and force commands, which the ==four-channel bilateral controller== converts into per-joint currents.
- Data collection runs the leader-follower pair under ==four-channel bilateral control== with ==disturbance== and ==reaction-force observers==, giving the operator haptic feedback while recording position and force at 1000 Hz, downsampled to 100 Hz.

## Results

- Pick-and-place over ten trials per object: **100%** total success on both trained objects and on table tennis ball and eye cream, and **80-90%** on Canele, soccer ball, honey bottle, bell pepper and glue jar.
- Removing force input dropped eye cream and glue jar success from **100%** and **80%** to **50%** each, and softball from **100%** to **80%**, isolating force as the source of the gain.
- The long-horizon put-in-drawer task, roughly **19.5-22.4 s** per episode trained on **50** demonstrations, scored **100%** across all five stages: open, pick, move, place, close.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Force feedback matters most for deformable, irregular or liquid-filled objects with shifting weight distribution; vision alone suffices only when the grasp point is visually obvious.
> - Action chunking is what makes a force-aware policy viable in practice, sustaining 100 Hz command output that current-level bilateral control requires.
> - Evidence is narrow: one 4-DOF arm, ten trials per object, and the only ablation is force on/off, so generality across platforms remains unverified.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic control has increasingly embraced learning from human demonstration, often termed behavior cloning or imitation learning (IL), as an intuitive and adaptable framework for complex tasks. This paradigm offers a compelling alternative to traditional, explicit programming of robot behaviors, especially in unstructured or dynamic environments.

Historically, imitation learning systems face several critical challenges: the need for high-quality expert data, the design of appropriate learning model architectures, and robust robot system integration. Data acquisition for IL frequently relies on teleoperated systems, ranging from virtual reality setups to smartphone interfaces and keyboard inputs. More recently, leader-follower systems like ALOHA, Mobile ALOHA, and GELLO have shown remarkable success in collecting robot joint angles and image data for complex manipulation tasks. However, a significant limitation of these systems, particularly ALOHA, is their reliance on position control, which means they do not capture or utilize force information. This prevents them from discerning object properties like hardness or weight.

Parallel to these developments, another line of research has explored imitation learning based on bilateral control. Pioneered by researchers like Sakaino and his collaborators, this approach leverages bilateral control to collect both position and force information during human demonstrations. While highly valuable for tasks requiring tactile feedback and nuanced interaction (e.g., cutting cucumbers), these models have predominantly employed recurrent neural networks like Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM). A key limitation of most existing bilateral control-based IL methods is their infrequent integration of visual data, which can limit their robustness to changes in the operating environment.

In terms of model architectures, early IL efforts often used RNNs or LSTMs for time-series data. However, these models, along with single-step action policies, are susceptible to the "compounding error" problem, where small prediction inaccuracies accumulate over time, leading to significant deviations. The advent of the Transformer architecture, particularly its application in Action Chunking with Transformer (ACT), marked a significant leap. ACT mitigates compounding errors by predicting sequences or "chunks" of future actions (e.g., k time steps) rather than just the next single step. While ACT successfully integrates visual and joint position data, it, like ALOHA, does not incorporate force information during data collection or robot operation.

This paper, "Bi-ACT," directly addresses the critical gaps in the existing research landscape. It synthesizes the strengths of two distinct, yet complementary, approaches: the force-aware capabilities of bilateral control-based imitation learning and the vision-enabled, error-mitigating architecture of ACT. By integrating position, velocity, *and force* information with image data into a Transformer-based action chunking model, Bi-ACT aims to create a more comprehensive and robust imitation learning system that can adapt to objects of varying physical properties while maintaining visual awareness and mitigating compounding errors.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to develop a more robust and efficient control mechanism for autonomous robotic arms, particularly for complex manipulation tasks. This is achieved by synergizing principles from bilateral control and the Action Chunking with Transformer (ACT) model.

The key motivations and specific objectives driving the development of Bi-ACT are:

- **Addressing the Limitations of Existing IL Methods:**
 - **Lack of Force Information in ACT-like Models:** Current state-of-the-art models like ACT and ALOHA, while effective with visual and positional data, do not incorporate force feedback. This prevents robots from understanding and adapting to physical properties like the hardness or weight of objects.
 - **Limited Visual Robustness in Bilateral Control IL:** While bilateral control offers excellent force feedback, many existing imitation learning methods based on it (often using LSTMs) do not extensively utilize image data, limiting their adaptability to changing visual environments.
- **Integrating Multimodal Sensory Data:** The authors aim to create a model that can effectively process and learn from a rich combination of sensory inputs, including images (from both gripper and overhead cameras), joint angles, joint velocities, and critically, *joint forces/torques* from the follower robot.
- **Enhancing Predictive Capability for Bilateral Control:** The model is designed to predict the subsequent steps for the *leader robot's* joint angles, angular velocities, and forces. This predictive capability is crucial for effectively implementing bilateral control in the follower robot, enabling more nuanced and responsive maneuvering in autonomous operation.
- **Mitigating Compounding Errors:** By adopting the action chunking mechanism from ACT, the paper seeks to reduce the effective task horizon and diminish the accumulation of errors inherent in single-step prediction policies, thereby improving the long-term stability and success rate of complex tasks.
- **Enabling Adaptation to Object Properties:** A primary motivation for incorporating force information is to allow the robot to adapt its manipulation strategy based on an object's hardness and weight, a capability not possible with position-only control.
- **Achieving High-Frequency and Robust Motion Generation:** The goal is to achieve a high action update frequency (100Hz) while handling multimodal data, ensuring fast and robust motion generation for real-world robotic manipulation.

In essence, Bi-ACT seeks to combine the "brains" (Transformer-based action chunking and visual processing) with the "feel" (bilateral control with force feedback) to create a more intelligent, adaptive, and dexterous robotic manipulation system.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed Bi-ACT method innovatively integrates bilateral control principles with the Action Chunking with Transformer architecture to achieve more robust and force-aware robotic manipulation.

**Controller Design:**
The underlying control system is designed to handle both position and force for each joint axis. Joint angle information is acquired from encoders, and angular velocity is derived by differentiating this data. To estimate relevant force components, a disturbance observer (DOB) is used to calculate disturbance torque ($\hat{\tau}_{dis}$), and a force reaction observer (RFOB) estimates the torque response value ($\tau_{res}$). This robust controller forms the foundation for precise manipulation and force interaction.

**Bilateral Control:**
The core principle of bilateral control, central to this work, involves sharing position and force information between a human operator (leader) and the robot (follower). The control objectives are twofold: achieving position tracking between the leader and follower ($\theta_l - \theta_f = 0$) and maintaining an action-reaction relationship for forces ($\tau_l + \tau_f = 0$). During the autonomous operation phase, the trained neural network model effectively substitutes the human leader. The follower robot receives predictions of the leader's desired response (from the model) and uses these as commands to execute the task. This means the model learns the dynamic interaction of the leader system, which inherently includes force feedback.

**Data Collection:**
High-quality demonstration data is critical for imitation learning. Bi-ACT utilizes a bilateral control setup for data collection, allowing the human operator to intuitively control a leader robot while simultaneously "feeling" the follower robot's interaction with the environment. This significantly enhances the quality of the collected demonstration data.
The data recorded includes:
- Joint angles, angular velocities, and torques (forces) from both the leader and follower robot arms.
- RGB images from an overhead camera and a gripper-mounted camera on the follower robot.
Data was initially collected at a high frequency (robot control at 1000Hz, cameras at 200Hz) and then downsampled to 100Hz for training, matching the model's inference cycle. The inclusion of force data specifically allows the model to learn demonstrations where object weight and texture are considered.

**Learning Architecture (Bi-ACT Model):**
The Bi-ACT model builds upon the Action Chunking with Transformer (ACT) framework but introduces a novel dimension by incorporating force data. The model architecture (illustrated in Figure 4) receives the following inputs:
- **Visual Input:** Two RGB images (360x640 resolution), one from the gripper camera and one from the overhead camera.
- **Proprioceptive/Force Input:** The current state of the follower robot's joints, comprising joint angles, angular velocities, and *forces (torques)* for each of its five joints, forming a 15-dimensional vector.
The model, which is based on a Transformer architecture, processes these multimodal inputs. Utilizing the action chunking paradigm, the policy generates an output as a `k x 15` tensor. This tensor represents the predicted sequence of the *leader's* actions (joint angles, angular velocities, and forces) over the next `k` time steps. This temporal aggregation of actions is crucial for mitigating the compounding error problem common in single-step policies.

**Execution to Robot Arm:**
During autonomous operation, the predicted `k` steps of the leader's actions (joint angle, velocity, and torque for each joint) are transformed into the necessary current commands for the follower robot's joints. This transformation is facilitated by the bilateral control system. To ensure responsive and precise control, the action result update frequency is set at 100Hz. The model executes its predictions every `k` time steps, generating the necessary commands to maintain the 100Hz control loop. This continuous prediction and execution loop enables the robot to autonomously perform tasks, adapting to varying object properties and completing long-duration sequences.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The effectiveness of Bi-ACT was rigorously evaluated through real-world experiments using an OpenMANIPULATOR-X robotic arm (5 DOF, 1000Hz control cycle), equipped with overhead and gripper RGB cameras. Two distinct manipulation tasks were designed to assess different aspects of the model's performance: Pick-and-Place and Put-in-Drawer.

**A. Pick-and-Place Task:**
This task aimed to evaluate the model's generalization capabilities to untrained objects and its ability to handle variations in object properties (shape, weight, texture).
- **Data Collection:** 50 episodes were recorded using two trained objects: a foam ball (light, high hardness) and a softball (heavier, very low hardness). Each task lasted 8.4-9.3 seconds, yielding over 44,184 time steps.
- **Testing:** The model was tested on the two trained objects and seven untrained objects with diverse characteristics (Table I): table tennis ball, eye-cream package, Canele, soccer ball, plastic bell pepper, honey bottle, and glue jar.
- **Results (Table II):**
 - **Bi-ACT (proposed, with force):** Demonstrated exceptional performance. It achieved a 100% success rate for trained objects (Softball, Foam ball) and two untrained objects (Table Tennis ball, Eye Cream). For the remaining five untrained objects (Canele, Soccer Ball, Honey Bottle, Plastic Bell Pepper, Glue Jar), success rates ranged from 80% to 90%. This highlights the model's strong generalization and adaptability to previously unseen objects with varied physical properties.
 - **Bi-ACT (without force):** This baseline model showed a significant performance degradation, particularly with objects where force feedback is crucial. While it performed well with smaller, visually distinct objects like the foam ball and table tennis ball (100% success), its accuracy dropped considerably for objects with irregular shapes, deformability, or inconsistent weight distribution. For instance, its success rate for the Eye Cream and Glue Jar plummeted to 50%, compared to 100% and 80% for the proposed method, respectively. The paper explicitly states that the model without force struggled with objects containing liquid (eye cream, glue jar) due to inconsistent weight, underscoring the vital role of force feedback.

**B. Put-in-Drawer Task:**
This task evaluated the proposed model's performance on long-duration, multi-step manipulation, including complex interactions with the environment (drawer handle).
- **Data Collection:** 50 episodes were recorded, each lasting 19.5-22.4 seconds, totaling 97,972 time steps. The task involved grasping a small, friction-prone drawer handle, pulling to open, placing an object inside, and then closing the drawer.
- **Results (Table III):**
 - **Bi-ACT (proposed, with force):** Consistently achieved a 100% success rate across all five sub-steps (Open, Pick, Move, Place, Close) over five trials. This demonstrates the model's robustness and reliability in executing complex, sequential tasks that demand precise manipulation and sustained control over an extended period. The ability to handle the drawer's handle, which requires subtle force control to avoid derailing or failure to open, is a key indicator of the proposed method's efficacy.

**Summary of Findings:**
The experiments conclusively demonstrate that integrating force information via bilateral control into an Action Chunking Transformer model (Bi-ACT) significantly enhances the robot's ability to perform manipulation tasks. Bi-ACT exhibits superior generalization to untrained objects, particularly those requiring an understanding of physical properties like hardness and weight, which purely visual or position-controlled methods cannot achieve. Its success in a complex, long-duration task further validates its robustness and precision for real-world applications. The qualitative analysis emphasizes that force feedback is crucial for managing objects with intricate geometries, varying consistencies, or dynamic weight characteristics, making Bi-ACT a more versatile solution compared to previous approaches.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Bi-ACT: Bilateral Control-Based Imitation Learning via Action Chunking with Transformer" paper presents a significant advancement in the field of robotic manipulation, offering solutions to key limitations of existing imitation learning paradigms.

**Significance of the Work:**
- **Novel Multimodal Integration:** The most significant contribution of Bi-ACT is its pioneering integration of bilateral control (which provides crucial force/torque information) with the visual awareness and temporal action chunking capabilities of the Transformer architecture. This effectively bridges a critical gap where prior state-of-the-art methods like ACT lacked force feedback, and traditional bilateral control-based IL often lacked visual robustness or the advanced temporal modeling of Transformers.
- **Enhanced Understanding of Physical Properties:** By incorporating joint forces/torques into the learning process, Bi-ACT enables the robot to "feel" and react to the physical properties of objects, such as their hardness, deformability, and weight. This is a crucial step towards more human-like and versatile robotic dexterity, as demonstrated by its superior performance with objects like eye cream and glue jars compared to models without force data.
- **Improved Generalization and Robustness:** The comprehensive multimodal input (images + position + velocity + force) allows Bi-ACT to generalize effectively to untrained objects with varying characteristics. The action chunking mechanism, inherited from ACT, inherently mitigates the compounding error problem, leading to more stable and robust long-duration task execution, as evidenced by the 100% success rate in the Put-in-Drawer task.
- **High-Frequency, Responsive Control:** Operating at 100Hz with the ability to predict future action chunks ensures that the robot's movements are not only accurate but also smooth and responsive, which is vital for real-time interaction in dynamic environments.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Advancement in Robotic Dexterity:** Bi-ACT paves the way for robots that can perform more delicate, precise, and adaptive manipulation tasks. This is crucial for handling fragile items, assembling intricate components, or interacting with unpredictable environments.
- **Industrial Automation:** The ability to adapt to varying object properties (e.g., slight variations in product dimensions, material consistency) can significantly enhance the flexibility and reliability of robots in manufacturing and logistics. This could lead to more adaptive assembly lines, improved quality control, and more efficient handling of diverse product lines.
- **Service Robotics and Healthcare:** Robots capable of understanding and responding to tactile feedback can be invaluable in service roles, assisting with daily tasks that involve delicate objects or human interaction. In healthcare, this could translate to more precise surgical assistance or adaptive patient handling.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** Bi-ACT provides a robust baseline and a new direction for future research in several areas, as highlighted by the authors:
 - **Increased Robustness and Adaptability:** Further research can focus on making Bi-ACT even more resilient to environmental variations like lighting changes, occlusions, and dynamic objects.
 - **Deeper Multimodal Sensory Integration:** Integrating additional sensory inputs such as dedicated touch sensors (haptics), auditory cues, or proprioceptive data beyond joint states could lead to an even more holistic perception and decision-making for robots.
 - **Generalization Across Diverse Robotic Platforms:** Testing and adapting the Bi-ACT framework to a wider array of robotic manipulators and sensor configurations will be crucial for its widespread applicability and commercialization.

In conclusion, Bi-ACT represents a significant step towards creating more intelligent and adaptable robotic systems, capable of performing complex manipulation tasks with a nuanced understanding of their physical environment. Its novel combination of bilateral control and Action Chunking with Transformer is poised to influence future developments in imitation learning and robotic control.
