# lmgame-Bench: How Good are LLMs at Playing Games?

> [!summary] Summary
> LMGAME-BENCH introduces a comprehensive benchmark for evaluating LLM agents across diverse video games, incorporating a "gaming harness" to mitigate issues like brittle perception and data contamination. It demonstrates that reinforcement learning training within game environments can transfer learned skills to novel game scenarios and external planning and agentic tasks.

## Problem

- Directly deploying LLMs into game environments often leads to low performance and poor discriminability, making it hard to evaluate their true capabilities.
- Existing benchmarks lack a quantitative framework for interpreting LLM gaming performance across diverse skill sets.
- Evaluation reliability is compromised by issues like prompt sensitivity and potential data contamination from LLM pre-training data.

## Method

- Introduces "**lmgame-Bench**", a benchmark featuring six diverse ==video games== with a unified Gym-style API for LLM agent evaluation.
- Deploys a "gaming harness" that includes perception modules for symbolic state representation and memory modules for long-horizon planning.
- Implements principled methods for detecting and mitigating data contamination and utilizes a two-stage prompt optimization pipeline for standardization.

## Results

- The gaming harness significantly improved LLM performance and discriminability across games, with **86.7%** of runs surpassing random baselines.
- Correlation analysis and latent ability decomposition confirmed that games evaluate distinct compositional LLM capabilities, with O3 and O1 models consistently performing highest among tested LLMs.
- Reinforcement learning training on games resulted in performance improvements on unseen game variations and demonstrated generalizability to external planning tasks (e.g., at least **10%** on Blocksworld) and agentic tasks (at least **6%** on WebShop).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Scaffolding modules, such as perception and memory harnesses, are crucial for LLMs to achieve discriminable performance and engage in complex reasoning within game environments.
> - Video games effectively probe compositional LLM capabilities, with different genres correlating with unique blends of skills like spatial reasoning, long-context language understanding, and planning.
> - Reinforcement learning conducted in game environments can yield transferable skills, demonstrating performance gains in unseen games and improving capabilities in external planning and agentic tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper positions `lmgame-Bench` at the intersection of large language models (LLMs), reinforcement learning (RL), and artificial intelligence benchmarking. Games have historically served as a critical proving ground for AI, from early chess programs to modern RL breakthroughs like AlphaGo and the widespread adoption of OpenAI Gym. Recent advancements have extended this tradition, exploring the use of LLMs as agents trained via RL to navigate complex, interactive environments, including video games. This convergence naturally leads to games emerging as promising benchmarks for evaluating LLM agents, as they inherently demand skills like perception, reasoning, and long-horizon planning—precisely the faculties expected from capable LLM agents.

However, the authors identify significant gaps and limitations in the existing landscape. Current efforts often "lack quantitative understandings of how to interpret gaming performance" for LLMs, and direct deployment of LLMs into games frequently results in "low performance, often close to that of random action-taking baselines." This underperformance is attributed to challenges such as brittle vision perception and difficulties with long-horizon decision-making. Furthermore, while some gaming benchmarks exist, they tend to be domain-specific (e.g., grid-based puzzles, text-based narratives) or focus on qualitative observations rather than systematic, quantitative analysis across diverse capabilities. Similarly, general LLM agentic benchmarks, though valuable, often require substantial engineering effort for specialized tasks (e.g., code editing, web browsing), limiting their scalability and breadth of skill coverage.

`lmgame-Bench` directly addresses these limitations. It contributes to the research landscape by:
- **Standardizing Evaluation:** It introduces a principled framework for evaluating state-of-the-art LLMs on a diverse suite of popular video games, filling the gap in comprehensive quantitative understanding.
- **Bridging Performance Gaps:** By introducing a "gaming harness" with perception and memory scaffolds, it overcomes the critical issues of brittle vision and long-horizon planning that hinder direct LLM performance, thereby enabling more effective and discriminative evaluations.
- **Ensuring Reliability:** It tackles crucial practical concerns like data contamination and prompt sensitivity, which are vital for trustworthy and comparable benchmark results in the LLM domain.
- **Uncovering Latent Capabilities:** Through correlation analysis and latent ability decomposition, the work moves beyond mere performance metrics to quantitatively illuminate the underlying LLM capabilities that contribute to success in different game genres, providing deeper insights than isolated skill tests.
- **Demonstrating Training Potential:** Crucially, it explores the generalizability of RL training on games, showing that skills acquired in gaming environments can transfer to unseen games and, more significantly, to external planning and agentic tasks. This positions games not just as evaluation tools but as valuable, scalable training environments for developing more broadly capable LLM agents, a relatively underexplored aspect in the current LLM-agent paradigm.

In essence, `lmgame-Bench` aims to elevate video games from challenging environments to robust, insightful, and generalizable benchmarks and training grounds for the next generation of LLM-powered AI agents, thereby pushing the boundaries of agentic AI research.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core motivation behind `lmgame-Bench` stems from the increasing expectation that modern large language models, when operating as agents, should master complex cognitive faculties such as perception, memory, and planning. Video games, by their very nature, inherently demand these exact skills, making them a "natural" and "promising" testbed for evaluating advanced AI capabilities.

However, the authors identify several critical shortcomings in the existing approach to using games for LLM evaluation:
1. **Low Performance and Discriminability:** Directly "dropping" LLMs into game environments often leads to "low performance, often close to that of random action-taking baselines." This makes it numerically challenging to differentiate between models, as even advanced reasoning models struggle with foundational issues like brittle vision perception and long-horizon decision-making.
2. **Lack of Quantitative Understanding:** There's a notable absence of quantitative frameworks to interpret gaming performance in the context of LLMs, making it difficult to understand *what* game performance signifies about a model's underlying abilities.
3. **Evaluation Reliability Issues:** Existing methods are susceptible to practical challenges such as prompt sensitivity (small changes in prompts leading to large performance swings) and potential data contamination (models memorizing game assets or solutions from their training data rather than genuinely reasoning).

Given these motivations, the paper sets forth several key objectives for `lmgame-Bench`:

- **To introduce `lmgame-Bench` as the first comprehensive benchmark** that utilizes popular video games (including platformer, puzzle, and narrative genres) to evaluate state-of-the-art LLMs, specifically designed to function both with and without supportive scaffolds.
- **To overcome major challenges in LLM gaming evaluation:** This involves developing and integrating a "gaming harness" comprising lightweight perception and memory modules. These modules are intended to mitigate the limitations of raw vision perception and facilitate long-horizon planning, thereby enabling a more effective evaluation environment.
- **To ensure benchmark reliability:** This includes implementing principled methods for detecting and mitigating data contamination and employing a standardized prompt optimization technique to reduce prompt variance and enhance result comparability.
- **To quantitatively understand LLM performance in games:** The authors aim to go beyond mere scores by providing a framework for analyzing how game performance correlates with other widely recognized LLM capabilities, revealing the unique blend of skills each game probes.
- **To demonstrate the generalizability of game-based RL training:** A significant objective is to show that performing reinforcement learning on games within `lmgame-Bench` can lead to performance improvements not only on unseen game variations but also on external, out-of-domain planning and agentic tasks. This aims to establish games as valuable training environments, not just evaluation tools.

In essence, the paper is motivated by the need for a robust, interpretable, and scalable benchmark that can accurately assess and even enhance the complex problem-solving abilities of modern LLM agents in dynamic, interactive environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

`lmgame-Bench` is designed as a comprehensive benchmark to evaluate LLM agents on a diverse set of video games while addressing critical challenges that hinder effective evaluation.

#### 3.1. Benchmark Design

The backbone of `lmgame-Bench` comprises six well-known video games, chosen to ensure broad coverage of diverse cognitive skills:
- **Platformer:** Super Mario Bros. (visual perception, 2D spatial reasoning, goal-directed planning with partial observability)
- **Puzzle:** Tetris (visual perception, spatial reasoning, long-horizon planning), Sokoban (visual perception, spatial reasoning, long-horizon planning, low fault tolerance), Candy Crush (visual perception, spatial reasoning, long-horizon planning with limited moves), and 2048 (visual perception, spatial reasoning, goal-directed planning).
- **Narrative:** Ace Attorney (long-context language understanding, causal & deductive reasoning, long-horizon, low-fault-tolerance decision-making).

The benchmark provides a unified Gym-style API for interaction, formalizing game processes as partially or fully observable Markov Decision Processes (MDPs).
- **Observation Space Representations:** Since games are graphical, `lmgame-Bench` focuses on converting UI inputs into symbolic representations or textual descriptions to facilitate LLM understanding and minimize raw perception errors. For grid-based games, this means text-based tables (e.g., "Box at (2,3)"); for text-rich games, it involves extracting dialogues and describing visual elements.
- **Action Space:** The system supports multi-turn interactions where the LLM generates actions in response to current game states.
- **Difficulty:** Games are categorized by fault tolerance (low, medium, high) and state-action space complexity.
- **Metrics Design:** Performance is quantified using game-specific scores categorized into:
 - **Progression Rewards:** For games with indefinite or linear progression (e.g., Mario's x-axis movement, cumulative score in Tetris/2048).
 - **Long-Horizon Rewards:** For games where intermediate progress is hard to quantify, and success requires a series of non-obvious steps (e.g., total boxes pushed onto targets in Sokoban, correct actions in Ace Attorney).

#### 3.2. Benchmark Effectiveness Enhancement

To address challenges like low discriminability, contamination risk, and prompt variance, `lmgame-Bench` introduces several mitigation techniques:

- **Gaming Harness:** This modular system significantly boosts model performance and discriminability.
 - **Perception Modules:** Converts multimodal UI inputs (images, sprites) into structured symbolic or textual representations. For grid games, it generates text-based tables. For graphical/text-rich games, it uses advanced VLMs (like o3) to extract and describe key visual elements and indicators.
 - **Memory Modules:** Supports long-horizon planning by maintaining a transient memory (past N states/actions) and a reflection module. The reflection module, powered by a powerful LLM (o3), encodes "lessons learned" to avoid past failures and guide future actions, helping to narrow the action space.
 - **Reasoning Modules:** Explicitly supports models that employ chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning, allowing for evaluation with or without long CoT traces.

- **Data Contamination Detection and Mitigation:**
 - The authors recognize that popular games' assets/solutions might exist in LLM pre-training data.
 - **Vision-level (Super Mario Bros.):** Assessed by prompting models to reorder shuffled RGB frames. Findings indicated low reordering accuracy and no significant correlation with game performance, suggesting minimal reliance on memorized visual sequences.
 - **Text-level (Ace Attorney):** Tested for reproduction of public fan transcripts. A strong correlation between output similarity and performance was found initially.
 - **Mitigation:** Structured prompt-based interventions were applied: instructing models to forget prior knowledge, requiring detailed causal reasoning, requesting self-evaluation for memorization, and modifying inputs (entity masking, paraphrasing context/contradictions). These interventions effectively removed the correlation between memorization and performance.

- **Prompt Standardization:** To reduce performance variance caused by prompt sensitivity.
 - **Two-stage optimization:**
 1. **Empirical Prompt Engineering:** Utilizes best practices from agent prompt design (e.g., including game trajectory, reflection, and current state).
 2. **DSPy-based Optimization:** Leverages DSPy's SIMBA optimizer and Chain-of-Thought module. It iteratively refines prompts using game rewards as evaluation metrics, optimizing for maximum cumulative rewards across multiple target models. This approach significantly reduces performance variance compared to empirically designed prompts.

#### 3.3. Training Generalizability Study

To assess if game-based training can generalize, the authors conducted RL training on simplified versions of Sokoban and Tetris.
- **Framework:** Finetuned Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct using RAGEN, a multi-turn RL framework for LLMs, incorporating explicit thinking tokens in prompts.
- **Evaluation:** The trained models were evaluated on:
 - **In-domain:** Same game with different settings (e.g., larger Sokoban boards, more Tetris block types).
 - **Cross-game:** Other games within `lmgame-Bench`.
 - **Out-of-domain:** Planning tasks (Blocksworld), math/coding benchmarks (GSM8K, BIRD), and agentic tasks (WebShop). For math/coding, a multi-turn self-correction mechanism was simulated.

This comprehensive methodology ensures that `lmgame-Bench` not only provides a robust platform for evaluating LLM agent capabilities but also offers deep insights into the nature of these capabilities and their potential for generalization.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of 13 leading LLMs across six diverse games within `lmgame-Bench` yielded several significant findings:

- **Initial LLM Performance Challenges:** Without the gaming harness, most models performed poorly, often achieving scores close to random baselines (e.g., 75% of models scored zero on Sokoban and Ace Attorney). This low discriminability highlighted the inherent difficulties LLMs face with raw game inputs and long-horizon tasks, underscoring the necessity of `lmgame-Bench`'s design.

- **Harness Effectiveness (Scaffolds are Crucial):** The introduction of the gaming harness dramatically improved model performance and discriminability.
 - **Overall Boost:** With the harness, 86.7% of game runs surpassed the random baseline, a significant improvement from 40% without. Paired-sample t-tests confirmed statistically significant performance gains for five out of six games (Candy Crush, Sokoban, 2048, Ace Attorney, Tetris), validating the harness’s utility.
 - **Module-Specific Impact:** Perception modules were particularly effective in grid-based games (like Sokoban) by providing symbolic representations, unlocking planning capabilities not seen with raw visual inputs. Memory modules proved invaluable for long-horizon games (like 2048), enhancing decision-making, especially for models with weaker zero-shot abilities. The combination of both modules yielded the strongest results, amplifying differences between models.
 - **Stability:** The harness also led to a consistently lower coefficient of variation (CV) for most games, indicating more stable and reliable performance.

- **Model Differentiation:** Despite being challenging, `lmgame-Bench` effectively differentiated models. O3 and O1 consistently achieved the top-2 best performance across games, followed by reasoning-capable models like Gemini-2.5-pro-preview and Claude-3.7. Among models without explicit "reasoning" capabilities, GPT-4.1 led the pack.

- **Data Contamination Insights:**
 - **Vision-level (Super Mario Bros.):** Analysis showed low accuracy in reordering shuffled frames and no significant correlation with game performance, suggesting models rely on local perception rather than memorized visual sequences.
 - **Text-level (Ace Attorney):** Initially, a strong correlation existed between models reproducing public fan transcripts and their game performance. However, after applying structured prompt-based mitigation (entity masking, paraphrasing, enforced reasoning), this correlation disappeared. Post-mitigation performance instead aligned with independently judged reasoning quality, validating the effectiveness of the anti-contamination measures.

- **Prompt Standardization Success:** The two-stage prompt optimization pipeline, particularly the DSPy-based iterative refinement, successfully reduced performance variance across different empirically optimized prompt initializations (e.g., 33.8% to 63.5% reduction in 2048), leading to more robust and comparable benchmark results.

- **Understanding Gaming Performance (Correlation and Latent Abilities):**
 - **Correlation Analysis:** Spearman’s rank correlation revealed that different games probe unique blends of LLM capabilities. Sokoban strongly correlated with math and coding benchmarks, while Tetris and 2048 aligned with pattern recognition. Candy Crush showed ties to algorithmic reasoning (coding), and Ace Attorney strongly correlated with language understanding, confirming its text-heavy nature.
 - **Latent Ability Decomposition:** Low-rank matrix factorization identified underlying "latent abilities." Games like Sokoban engaged symbolic and physical reasoning, Ace Attorney leveraged long-context language reasoning, and Tetris/2048 primarily relied on mathematical and spatial reasoning. This analysis confirmed that games evaluate *compositional* capabilities rather than isolated skills.
 - **Linear Modeling:** Supported these findings, showing that long-horizon games were driven by math/coding, spatial reasoning games by physics/visual understanding, and text-heavy games by language skills.

- **Training Generalizability (RL on Games):**
 - **Cross-Game and Planning Transfer:** RL training on simplified Sokoban and Tetris significantly improved performance on larger Sokoban boards, boosted Blocksworld planning tasks (by at least 10%), and enhanced zero-shot performance on Tetris (up to 8% from Sokoban training). This demonstrated that learned spatial reasoning and planning heuristics generalized effectively across similar board rules and settings.
 - **Mixed Generalization:** While substantial gains were observed in planning and spatial reasoning, training on games did *not* transfer to math (GSM8K) or coding (BIRD) tasks, suggesting these require distinct reasoning skills. However, game-trained models showed at least 6% improvement on the multi-turn WebShop benchmark, indicating that skills derived from grid games can benefit real-world decision-making in agentic contexts. The use of "thinking tokens" during training generally improved generalization, especially for planning tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

`lmgame-Bench` represents a significant contribution to the field of AI, particularly in the evaluation and training of large language model agents. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Robust and Interpretable LLM Agent Evaluation:** By addressing fundamental challenges like brittle vision perception, prompt sensitivity, and data contamination, `lmgame-Bench` provides a more reliable and trustworthy benchmark for assessing the true capabilities of LLM agents in interactive environments. The quantitative analysis, including correlation studies and latent ability decomposition, moves beyond simple performance scores, offering a deeper understanding of *which* underlying LLM capabilities (e.g., spatial reasoning, long-context understanding, planning) are critical for success in different game genres. This interpretability is crucial for guiding future model development.

- **Advancing Agentic AI Research:** The benchmark’s focus on perception, memory, and planning in diverse game settings directly aligns with the core requirements of general-purpose AI agents. By providing a unified, Gym-style API and a suite of games ranging from platformers to narrative adventures, `lmgame-Bench` offers a scalable and skill-diverse environment that can accelerate progress in developing more intelligent and adaptive LLM agents beyond narrow, domain-specific tasks.

- **Games as Generalizable Training Environments:** Perhaps the most profound impact of this work lies in its demonstration that video games can serve as effective training environments for LLMs, yielding skills that generalize to unseen games and, critically, to *external planning and agentic tasks* like Blocksworld and WebShop. This finding suggests a powerful and scalable paradigm for teaching LLMs complex sequential decision-making and problem-solving heuristics. Instead of relying solely on massive text corpuses or highly specialized real-world data, game environments offer a structured, reward-rich setting for acquiring and refining agentic behaviors, potentially paving the way for more efficient and generalizable AI training.

- **Guiding Model Development:** The correlation analysis can inform LLM developers about specific areas for improvement. For instance, models struggling with Sokoban might need enhancements in symbolic reasoning or long-horizon planning, while those underperforming in Ace Attorney might require better long-context understanding or deductive reasoning capabilities. This diagnostic power can lead to more targeted and effective architectural or training data innovations.

- **Driving Future Research:** The paper also candidly highlights remaining limitations, such as high performance variance in partially observable games like Super Mario Bros. and substantial computational costs for long reasoning chains. These identified challenges serve as clear directions for future research, prompting explorations into more robust perceptual models, efficient inference techniques, and methods to handle environmental stochasticity.

- **Community Adoption and Collaboration:** By making the evaluation code publicly available, the authors foster an open research environment, encouraging wider adoption of `lmgame-Bench` as a standard, facilitating comparative studies, and accelerating collaborative efforts in the rapidly evolving field of LLM agents.

In conclusion, `lmgame-Bench` is more than just a collection of game environments; it is a meticulously designed framework that offers a robust, insightful, and forward-looking approach to evaluating and training LLM agents, with significant implications for shaping the development of general artificial intelligence.
