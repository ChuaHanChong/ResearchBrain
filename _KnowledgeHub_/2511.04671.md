# X-Diffusion: Training Diffusion Policies on Cross-Embodiment Human Demonstrations

> [!summary] Summary
> Cornell University researchers developed X-DIFFUSION, a framework for training robot diffusion policies using cross-embodiment human demonstrations. The method selectively integrates human actions into training based on noise levels, allowing robots to learn manipulation skills despite dynamic incompatibilities. It achieved an average 16% improvement in task success rates over existing baselines across five real-world manipulation tasks.

## Problem

- High cost and time associated with collecting large-scale, high-quality robot demonstration data.
- Significant embodiment mismatch between humans and robots makes direct utilization of abundant human video demonstrations challenging, often leading to infeasible robot actions.
- Naive co-training of robot and human demonstrations can degrade policy performance due to robots attempting to replicate dynamically infeasible human execution styles.

## Method

- **X-Diffusion** unifies human and robot state and action spaces by ==retargeting human hand motions== to the robot's end-effector, including 7-DOF pose and gripper state.
- Introduces a ==cross-embodiment equivalence principle==, defining a ==minimum indistinguishability step (k*)== where noised human and robot action distributions become similar (adapting ==Ambient Diffusion==).
- Trains a ==noised human-robot action classifier== to identify k* for each human action sequence and integrates it into ==Diffusion Policy== training, applying the ==denoising loss== for human data only when the current noise level exceeds k*.

## Results

- X-DIFFUSION consistently achieved higher average success rates across **five real-world manipulation tasks** (Close Drawer, Pan On Plate, Push Plate, Mug On Rack, Bottle Upright) compared to robot-only and state-of-the-art cross-embodiment baselines.
- The framework demonstrated an average improvement of **16%** in task success rates over baselines, preventing the performance degradation often seen with naive co-training.
- It effectively leveraged a higher fraction of human demonstrations, including those manually identified as kinematically or dynamically infeasible for direct robot execution, showing better data utilization than filtering approaches.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Dynamically infeasible human actions can be conceptualized as 'noisy' versions of feasible robot actions, where embodiment-specific differences are masked by sufficient Gaussian noise.
> - By selectively training on human demonstrations at high noise levels (beyond k*), a robot policy can extract valuable high-level task intent and object interaction cues without learning problematic low-level human execution details.
> - This adaptive integration of human data allows for more comprehensive utilization of human demonstrations, even those deemed infeasible by manual filtering, transforming them into useful coarse guidance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in "X-DIFFUSION" is situated within the field of robot learning, specifically addressing challenges in imitation learning (IL) from human demonstrations. Imitation learning is a flexible method for teaching robots new skills by observing examples. However, a significant bottleneck in IL is the high cost and time required to collect large-scale, high-quality robot demonstration data.

Human video demonstrations offer a scalable and accessible alternative to robot data, as they are generally easier and faster to collect. However, directly utilizing human demonstrations for training state-of-the-art robot IL methods is challenging due to inherent differences in embodiment between humans and robots. These differences extend beyond physical appearance to kinematics, dynamics, and typical execution styles, making many human actions physically infeasible or inefficient for a robot to perform.

Prior research has attempted to bridge this human-robot embodiment gap through various approaches:
- **Motion Retargeting:** Several methods focus on mapping human motions, often extracted via 3D hand-pose estimation, to a robot's end-effector action space [3–5, 7–10]. While this unifies the representation of actions, it does not guarantee the physical realizability or dynamic feasibility of the retargeted motions on the robot. Human executions often involve contact strategies and dynamics fundamentally mismatched with a robot's capabilities.
- **Reward Extraction for RL:** Some reinforcement learning (RL) approaches leverage human data by defining rewards based on reference motion tracking or object-centric signals [31–34]. These methods typically require realistic simulators or potentially costly and unsafe real-world interactions.
- **One-Shot Imitation:** Other works explore learning from single human demonstrations, often relying on paired human-robot videos, unified visual embeddings, or prompting pre-trained policies [38–44]. These can require substantial base datasets or paired data, which can be expensive to acquire.
- **Learning from Sub-Optimal Data in Generative Models:** Parallels exist in generative modeling, where training on mixed-quality datasets can degrade model performance. Solutions include filtering low-quality samples or extracting signals from noisy data [15–18]. A particularly relevant development is Ambient Diffusion, which strategically incorporates low-quality data into diffusion model training only at higher-noise timesteps.

X-DIFFUSION directly addresses the limitation that human actions, even when retargeted, may remain dynamically infeasible for a robot. It aims to integrate the rich object-interaction cues and task intent from human demonstrations without transferring embodiment-specific, infeasible execution strategies. By adapting principles from Ambient Diffusion, X-DIFFUSION positions itself as an advancement in cross-embodiment learning, enabling the maximal utilization of human data while ensuring the dynamic feasibility of learned robot policies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a learning framework that enables robots to effectively learn manipulation skills from human demonstrations, even when the human actions are dynamically incompatible or physically infeasible for direct robot execution. The motivation stems from several key observations and challenges:

- **Scalability of Human Data:** Human video demonstrations represent a highly scalable and readily available source of training data for robot learning. Leveraging this data efficiently could significantly reduce the burden of collecting expensive robot-specific demonstrations.
- **Embodiment Mismatch as a Core Barrier:** A fundamental challenge is the "dynamics gap" or "embodiment mismatch" between humans and robots. Although kinematic retargeting methods can translate human hand motions into a robot's action space, the underlying physics and interaction strategies (e.g., how to grasp, push, or slide an object) often differ significantly. For instance, a human might dexterously slide fingers under a plate, while a robot gripper might be better suited to push or slide it across a surface.
- **Dilemma of Using Mismatched Data:** This mismatch presents a dilemma: should human demonstrations that are dynamically infeasible for a robot be entirely discarded, or can their valuable "coarse guidance" (object interaction cues, task intent) still be utilized? Naive co-training, which simply combines human and robot datasets, often leads to degraded policy performance because the robot policy attempts to replicate infeasible human execution styles. Conversely, discarding mismatched data might mean losing potentially useful information.
- **Inspiration from Generative Modeling:** The authors draw inspiration from generative modeling, particularly Ambient Diffusion. This method demonstrated that low-quality data can be effectively used in diffusion models by incorporating it only at high-noise timesteps, where its "low-quality" characteristics are obscured by noise and its underlying "high-quality" information becomes indistinguishable from true high-quality data.
- **Core Insight: Human Actions as Noisy Robot Actions:** The central insight of X-DIFFUSION is to conceptualize dynamically infeasible human actions as "noisy" versions of feasible robot actions. In the context of a diffusion process, adding sufficient Gaussian noise to both human and robot actions can progressively obscure embodiment-specific differences, making them indistinguishable at higher noise levels. At these higher noise levels, the underlying task structure and high-level guidance from human demonstrations are preserved, while the problematic low-level execution details are masked.

Based on this, the specific objectives are:
1. To develop a mechanism to identify when human actions, under increasing noise, become sufficiently similar to robot actions that they can be safely incorporated into robot policy training.
2. To integrate this mechanism into a Diffusion Policy framework to selectively train on human demonstrations, ensuring that useful signals are extracted without transferring infeasible behaviors.
3. To validate this approach on real-world robot manipulation tasks, demonstrating improved performance over existing methods that either ignore human data or incorporate it indiscriminately.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

X-DIFFUSION introduces a cross-embodiment learning framework that integrates human demonstrations into Diffusion Policy training by selectively applying the learning signal based on the noise level of the human actions. This approach is inspired by Ambient Diffusion and hinges on the idea that embodiment differences fade under sufficient noise.

#### 3.1. Unifying State and Action Spaces

To enable co-training, both human and robot demonstrations are represented in a shared state and action space:
- **State (s_t):** The robot's state `s_t` is composed of proprioceptive information `q_t` (7-DOF end-effector position, rotation, and gripper state) and visual observations. For visual input, task-relevant objects are segmented using Grounded SAM 2, and a keypoint rendering of the end-effector pose is overlaid on each frame to reduce the visual domain gap.
- **Action (a_t):** Actions are represented as the next proprioceptive state, `q_t+1`.
- **Human Data Retargeting:** For human demonstrations, which are assumed to involve a single hand with an open grasp observed by calibrated RGB cameras, 2D hand keypoints are detected using HaMeR and triangulated to 3D in the robot frame. The grasp point is derived from thumb and index fingertips, and orientation is obtained by fitting a local hand frame and retargeting it to the robot end-effector. The gripper state is inferred from the distance between thumb and index keypoints.

#### 3.2. Cross-Embodiment Equivalence under Noise

The core principle is that the forward diffusion process, which progressively adds Gaussian noise to action sequences, can homogenize the distributions of human and robot actions.
- **Forward Diffusion Process:** Given a clean action sequence `A_0_t` (either human or robot), the forward process `q` generates progressively noisier versions `A_1_t, ..., A_K_t` by adding Gaussian noise at each step `k`, controlled by `β_k`.
- **Minimum Indistinguishability Step (k*):** The authors define `k*` as the earliest diffusion step where the distributions of noised human actions (`p_k_H`) and noised robot actions (`p_k_R`) become sufficiently similar such that they cannot be reliably distinguished. Formally, `k*` is the minimum `k` at which the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence `D_KL(p_k_H || p_k_R)` falls below a small threshold `ϵ`. This concept posits that beyond `k*`, human demonstrations can safely supervise robot policy learning without transferring infeasible motions because embodiment-specific details are obscured by noise.

#### 3.3. Training a Noised Human-Robot Action Classifier

To determine `k*` for each human action sequence, a classifier `c_θ(·|k, A_k_t, s_t)` is trained.
- **Classifier Function:** This classifier takes the current diffusion step `k`, the noised action sequence `A_k_t`, and the current state `s_t` as input. It outputs the probability that the action originated from a robot (`y=1`) rather than a human (`y=0`).
- **Classifier Training:** The classifier is optimized using a binary cross-entropy loss (Eq. 2). Training samples are drawn equally from both the human (`D_H`) and robot (`D_R`) datasets, despite `D_H` being typically much larger, to prevent bias towards the human label.
- **Determining k* for Human Actions:** For each human action sequence `A_t`, its `k*(A_t)` is defined as the minimum diffusion step `k` at which the classifier assigns at least a 50% probability of that noised action `A_k_t` being a robot action (Eq. 3). This effectively identifies the point where the human action, obscured by noise, becomes indistinguishable from a robot action according to the classifier.

#### 3.4. Classifier Integration into Diffusion Policy Training

The Diffusion Policy aims to learn a denoising network that models the reverse diffusion process, recovering clean action sequences from noise.
- **Selective Loss Application:** Instead of naively applying the denoising loss to all human actions at all noise levels, X-DIFFUSION selectively integrates human actions. The denoising loss for a human action `A_t` is only applied when the current diffusion step `k` is greater than or equal to its `k*(A_t)`.
- **X-DIFFUSION Loss (L_X-DP):** The total loss (Eq. 4) combines the standard denoising loss for robot demonstrations (applied at all noise levels) with the selectively applied loss for human demonstrations. This ensures that robot-feasible human actions (low `k*`) provide precise supervision across many diffusion steps, while dynamically infeasible human actions (high `k*`) contribute only coarse, high-level guidance at higher noise levels, thus avoiding the transfer of problematic low-level execution details.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of X-DIFFUSION was conducted across five real-world manipulation tasks: Close Drawer, Pan On Plate, Push Plate, Mug On Rack, and Bottle Upright. For each task, 5 robot demonstrations and 100 human demonstrations were collected. The robot utilized was a 7-DOF Franka Emika Panda arm. Performance was assessed based on average success rates over 10 real-world rollouts per task.

#### 4.1. Comparison with Cross-Embodiment Learning Baselines (Figure 4)

X-DIFFUSION was compared against several baselines:
- **Diffusion Policy:** Trained only on the 5 robot demonstrations.
- **Point Policy:** Co-trained a Diffusion Policy on all human and robot data, using object and hand keypoints as state.
- **Motion Tracks:** Co-trained a Diffusion Policy on all human and robot data, unifying action space as hand keypoints but using raw image observations.
- **DemoDiffusion:** A diffusion-based method that uses a human policy for initial denoising steps and a robot policy for later steps, but does not train the robot policy on human demonstrations directly.

The results demonstrated that X-DIFFUSION consistently achieved higher average success rates across all five manipulation tasks compared to all tested baselines. Naive co-training approaches (Point Policy, Motion Tracks) showed minimal to no improvement over robot-only training, and in the case of Point Policy, even led to a degradation in performance. This suggests that simply combining human and robot data without careful consideration of embodiment mismatch can be detrimental. Qualitatively, these baselines often failed by attempting to execute human actions that were kinematically or dynamically infeasible for the robot, such as grasping objects from the side instead of from above in tasks like Push Plate or Pan On Plate (Figure 5). X-DIFFUSION, by selectively integrating human data based on its learned classifier, avoided these failure modes, effectively leveraging human data from a wider state distribution.

#### 4.2. Systematic Ablation of Co-Training Data Choices (Figure 6)

An ablation study further investigated the impact of different human data utilization strategies:
- **ROBOT ONLY:** Trained solely on robot demonstrations.
- **NAIVE:** Trained on all available robot and human demonstrations (`D_R ∪ D_H`).
- **FILTERED:** Trained on robot demonstrations and a manually curated set of robot-feasible human demonstrations (`D_R ∪ D_H+`). Approximately 50% of the original human demonstrations were discarded in this manual filtering process due to kinematic or dynamic failures when replayed on the robot via Inverse Kinematics.
- **X-DIFFUSION:** Trained on `D_R ∪ D_H`, but selectively incorporating human data beyond its minimum indistinguishability step `k*`.

The results showed that the FILTERED policy outperformed the NAIVE policy, confirming that training on infeasible human demonstrations negatively impacts policy performance. Crucially, X-DIFFUSION consistently outperformed the FILTERED policy across all tasks. This indicates that X-DIFFUSION's adaptive inclusion of human data, even from trajectories deemed infeasible by manual filtering, is more effective. The classifier-based approach allows X-DIFFUSION to extract useful signals from *all* human demonstrations by incorporating them at appropriate noise levels, where they become indistinguishable from robot actions (Figure 3).

#### 4.3. Quantifying Transfer Learning from Human Data (Figure 7)

The study also quantified the extent and benefit of transfer learning from human data within X-DIFFUSION.
- **Fraction of Human Data Integrated:** X-DIFFUSION demonstrated a task-dependent integration of human data, with a higher fraction incorporated at increased noise levels. Tasks like Mug On Rack and Pan On Plate integrated a larger proportion of human data throughout the diffusion process, suggesting better dynamic compatibility. Conversely, Bottle Upright integrated substantially less human data, indicating that its human demonstrations were less dynamically compatible with robot execution.
- **Performance Gain:** When compared to the robot-only baseline, X-DIFFUSION consistently achieved positive performance gains across all tasks. Tasks that integrated a larger fraction of human data (e.g., Mug On Rack) showed more substantial performance improvements than tasks that integrated less (e.g., Bottle Upright). This highlights that the benefits of transfer learning are task-dependent and directly correlated with the degree of alignment between human and robot dynamics, which X-DIFFUSION adaptively manages.

These findings collectively establish X-DIFFUSION as a robust framework for leveraging human demonstrations, demonstrating its ability to extract valuable guidance without suffering from the detrimental effects of embodiment mismatch that affect prior co-training approaches.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The X-DIFFUSION framework represents a significant contribution to the field of robot learning, particularly in addressing the long-standing challenge of cross-embodiment learning from human demonstrations. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Effective Utilization of Human Data:** X-DIFFUSION provides a principled and effective method for incorporating readily available human video demonstrations into robot policy training. This is crucial for scaling up robot learning, as human data is far more abundant and less costly to acquire than robot-specific demonstrations. The ability to use human data that might otherwise be discarded as "infeasible" by manual filtering methods further enhances data efficiency.
- **Robustness to Embodiment Mismatch:** The core innovation of X-DIFFUSION lies in its ability to selectively integrate human actions based on their dynamic compatibility with the robot, as determined by their indistinguishability under progressive noise. This mechanism prevents the robot policy from learning kinematically or dynamically infeasible behaviors, a common failure mode in previous cross-embodiment approaches.
- **Consistent Positive Transfer:** Unlike many prior co-training methods that often exhibit negative transfer (where adding human data degrades performance), X-DIFFUSION consistently yields positive transfer, improving robot task success rates across diverse manipulation tasks. This reliability makes it a valuable tool for accelerating skill acquisition in robots.
- **Theoretical Foundation:** By adapting the principles of Ambient Diffusion, X-DIFFUSION is grounded in a theoretical understanding of how to learn from "low-quality" or mismatched data distributions. This provides a formal basis for its selective training strategy.
- **Improved Robot Performance:** The empirical results demonstrate that X-DIFFUSION outperforms both robot-only policies and several state-of-the-art cross-embodiment learning baselines, achieving an average improvement of 16% in task success. This improved performance indicates a practical advancement in robot capabilities.
- **Future Scalability:** The framework opens avenues for leveraging truly large-scale, uncurated human video datasets, including those from the internet. This could significantly broaden the range of skills robots can learn and enable more generalized capabilities, moving towards robots that can learn complex tasks by simply observing humans in diverse, real-world scenarios.

### 6. Limitations and Future Work

The authors acknowledge that the current work is evaluated on a limited number of robot and human demonstrations collected in a calibrated multi-camera environment. Future research aims to extend X-DIFFUSION to training on larger-scale datasets and to learn from unstructured, internet-scale human videos. Overcoming these limitations would further solidify the framework's real-world applicability and impact.
