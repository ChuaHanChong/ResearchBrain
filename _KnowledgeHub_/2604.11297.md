# The Past Is Not Past: Memory-Enhanced Dynamic Reward Shaping

> [!summary] Summary
> The OpenMOSS framework introduces Memory-Enhanced Dynamic reward Shaping (MEDS) for large language models, integrating historical error patterns into reward design to address policy collapse. This approach improves performance on mathematical reasoning benchmarks by up to 4.37 pass@128 points while enhancing exploration diversity.

## Problem

- Reinforcement learning for Large Language Models (LLMs) often suffers from 'policy collapse,' leading to reduced sampling diversity and repetitive generation of similar errors.
- Existing regularization methods, such as entropy regularization, are frequently insufficient in vast LLM action spaces and do not explicitly target the recurrence of specific failure patterns.
- LLMs can become entrenched in suboptimal reasoning trajectories, hindering effective exploration and the correction of persistent mistakes over training.

## Method

- The ==Memory-Enhanced Dynamic reward Shaping (MEDS)== (**MEDS**) framework reuses layer-wise logits, specifically from the latter half of Transformer layers, to construct 'logic features' representing LLM reasoning trajectories.
- A per-prompt error memory stores historical logic features of responses, which are then grouped into clusters using HDBSCAN, a density-based clustering algorithm, to identify prevalent error patterns.
- Rewards for new responses are dynamically adjusted by applying a penalty proportional to the size of the error cluster they fall into, thereby discouraging the reproduction of common historical mistakes.

## Results

- MEDS consistently achieved superior performance across three LLM scales (Qwen3**-1.7B**, Qwen**2.5**-Math**-7B**, Qwen3**-8B**) and five mathematical reasoning benchmarks, showing average improvements of up to **4.13** pass@1 and **4.37** pass@**128** points.
- The framework enhanced exploration behavior by increasing both 'Within-Step Diversity' and 'Across-Step Diversity' of reasoning patterns, as measured by an LLM-based evaluator and a lower 'Top**-1** Eigen Ratio' of logit distributions.
- The choice of using logits from the last **14** Transformer layers for clustering yielded optimal performance, with a strong correlation observed between higher clustering quality (agreement with semantic annotations) and improved downstream model performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Theoretically, dynamically penalizing repeated errors helps reallocate probability mass away from frequently sampled error patterns, leading to an improved expected return in reinforcement learning.
> - Layer-wise logits, particularly from later Transformer layers, serve as an efficient and effective internal representation for capturing the implicit reasoning trajectories and underlying logical structures of LLM responses.
> - Density-based clustering on logit features provides a viable proxy for identifying semantic similarity in reasoning patterns, enabling targeted reward shaping based on the prevalence of specific error types.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of Large Language Models (LLMs) has seen significant advancements through reinforcement learning (RL), where models are iteratively optimized to maximize expected returns based on reward signals. This approach is widely applied across various domains, including mathematical reasoning, code generation, and multimodal tasks. A critical challenge in on-policy RL optimization, however, is the tendency for the learned policy to converge into a narrow set of behaviors, leading to reduced sampling diversity and repetitive generation of similar errors. This phenomenon, often termed "policy collapse" or "mode collapse," hinders effective exploration and can entrench models in suboptimal reasoning trajectories.

Traditional methods to mitigate this issue include KL regularization, which constrains policy shifts, and entropy regularization, which encourages randomness in the policy distribution. While entropy regularization aims to promote output diversity, its effectiveness in LLM reasoning can be limited by the vast action space. Moreover, it often addresses randomness at a distribution level without explicitly targeting the recurrence of specific failure patterns across different rollouts or training steps. Once an LLM drifts into a particular error pattern, it may continue to produce variations of that same mistake, even with stochastic exploration.

Reward shaping is a common technique to guide model behavior, but existing approaches often operate on current-batch differences or track solution patterns over limited training history. Some methods cluster rollouts by strategy or promote diversity among correct trajectories but do not consistently leverage long-term historical error information. Other works have explored adaptive reward adjustments using external models, which can incur substantial computational overhead. The broader research landscape also includes efforts to understand and utilize the "inner thoughts" or internal reasoning processes of LLMs, often through analyzing layer-wise logits to gain fine-grained insights into how models arrive at conclusions. This line of research has inspired the use of logits for analysis and supervision in various contexts.

This paper introduces a Memory-Enhanced Dynamic reward Shaping (MEDS) framework, which addresses the limitation of existing methods by explicitly incorporating historical behavioral signals, specifically recurrent error patterns, into the reward design. By dynamically penalizing these persistent mistakes, MEDS aims to provide more targeted supervision than general entropy regularization, thereby enhancing exploration and preventing the model from becoming trapped in stable error basins over the course of training. The work integrates concepts from efficient representation learning (using logits) and density-based clustering to create a scalable, history-aware reward adjustment mechanism.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from a recognized failure mode in reinforcement learning for Large Language Models: the reduction of sampling diversity, where the optimized policy repeatedly generates similar or identical erroneous behaviors. This "policy collapse" has several detrimental effects:
1. **Ineffective Exploration:** Repetitive responses waste on-policy samples and limit the model's ability to explore the vast solution space, potentially preventing it from discovering optimal or more robust reasoning paths.
2. **Entrenchment of Errors:** When training converges on a particular error pattern, even with slight variations in expression, the model becomes entrenched in self-reinforcing, faulty reasoning trajectories. This makes it difficult to correct persistent mistakes.
3. **Limitations of Existing Solutions:** While entropy regularization encourages randomness, it often operates at a distribution level and injects stochasticity within the current policy. This approach can be insufficient in the context of LLM reasoning, where the action space is extensive. Crucially, it does not explicitly differentiate or penalize *recurring* failure patterns across different rollouts or over extended training periods. A policy might still repeatedly sample from an "error basin" even with high entropy, as long as the distribution within that basin remains high entropy.

Inspired by human learning, where individuals typically impose stronger psychological penalties on recurring mistakes to reinforce avoidance, the authors propose a mechanism to dynamically penalize such repetitive failures in LLMs. The key objectives of this work are:

1. **Develop a dynamic reward shaping framework:** To design a novel framework, MEDS, that moves beyond static reward functions by actively incorporating historical behavioral signals to modify rewards.
2. **Identify and leverage historical error patterns:** To store and utilize intermediate model representations (specifically, layer-wise logits) to capture features of past rollouts. These features should allow for the identification of frequently recurring error patterns.
3. **Implement density-based error clustering:** To use a clustering algorithm (HDBSCAN) to group similar historical error patterns. This allows for the quantification of how prevalent certain errors are.
4. **Dynamically adjust rewards:** To penalize rollouts more heavily if they are assigned to more prevalent error clusters. This aims to actively discourage the reproduction of known, common mistakes.
5. **Enhance exploration and mitigate recurrent errors:** The overarching goal is for this dynamic penalty mechanism to facilitate the correction of persistent errors, encourage broader exploration, help the model escape local optima, and ultimately improve performance by reducing repeated mistakes.
6. **Ensure computational efficiency:** To achieve these goals with minimal additional computational overhead, by reusing information (layer-wise logits) already generated during the model's forward pass.
7. **Provide theoretical grounding:** To offer a theoretical analysis demonstrating that additional penalties on repeated errors can improve overall performance.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Memory-Enhanced Dynamic reward Shaping (MEDS) framework consists of three main stages: Logic Feature Extraction, Memory-based Clustering, and Reward Shaping.

#### 3.1. Preliminaries

The work operates within the standard reinforcement learning setup for LLMs. An LLM is modeled as a policy $p_{\theta}$ that generates a response sequence $y$ for a given input $x$. A task-specific reward function $r(x, y)$ assigns a scalar reward to each response. Training involves maximizing an expected return within a trust region, typically employing a KL-regularized objective to constrain policy updates relative to a reference policy $p_{ref}$.

#### 3.2. Theoretical Benefits of Penalizing Repeated Errors

The paper provides a theoretical analysis to justify the benefits of penalizing repeated errors. Theorem 1 posits that introducing an adjusted reward signal $\mu_2 = r(x, y) - \lambda c(y)$ (where $c(y)$ is a non-decreasing indicator of repeated errors and $\lambda > 0$ is a penalty coefficient) will result in an updated policy $q_2$ that achieves an expected return $J(q_2)$ at least as high as the policy $q_1$ updated with the original reward $\mu_1 = r(x, y)$. The proof demonstrates that this penalty helps reallocate probability mass away from frequently sampled error patterns, leading to a non-decreasing expected return. The indicator function $c(y)$ effectively serves as a memory for the reward model, dynamically adjusting scores based on historical sampling behavior.

#### 3.3. Logic Feature Extraction

To implement the repetition indicator function $c(y)$ efficiently, MEDS reuses the layer-wise logits generated by the LLM during its forward pass.
- **Target Token Selection:** For a given response $y$, the logits are extracted from $y^*$, which is defined as the first token of the final answer within the response sequence.
- **Feature Construction:** At each Transformer layer $n$, the logit corresponding to $y^*$ is denoted as $l^{*(n)} \in \mathbb{R}$. These scalar logits are then aggregated across specific layers to form a feature vector.
- **Layer Selection:** The authors observed that earlier layers typically capture simpler semantic information, while later layers reflect more complex reasoning. Consequently, logits from the *latter half* of the Transformer layers (from $N/2$ to $N$, where $N$ is the total number of layers) are concatenated to form the logic feature representation $f(y) \in \mathbb{R}^{N/2}$.
- **Efficiency:** This approach introduces minimal additional computational overhead because it reuses internal representations that are already computed during the model's inference. This feature vector is hypothesized to capture the model's implicit reasoning trajectory and underlying logical structure.

#### 3.4. Cluster-based Reward Shaping

With the logic feature representations $f(y)$ constructed, the system performs dynamic reward shaping:
- **Error Memory:** For each unique input prompt $x$, a per-prompt error memory $G_x$ is maintained. This memory stores the logic feature representations $f(y)$ of all responses sampled across historical rollouts for that specific prompt.
- **Clustering:** HDBSCAN, a density-based clustering algorithm, is applied to the set $G_x$. This algorithm dynamically determines the number of clusters and groups responses with similar logic features into clusters $C_1, C_2, \dots, C_K$, with a separate $C_{noise}$ for outliers.
- **Penalty Calculation:** For a newly generated response $\tilde{y}$, its feature $f(\tilde{y})$ is assigned to a cluster $C_k$. A penalty is then computed based on the size of this cluster $|C_k|$. The penalty function is defined as $\min(\alpha \log(|C_k| + 1), \beta)$, where $\alpha$ controls the penalty strength and $\beta$ sets an upper bound. The logarithm ensures that penalties increase with cluster size but with diminishing returns, and the addition of 1 handles cases with small cluster sizes.
- **Reward Adjustment:** The final adjusted reward $\tilde{r}(x, \tilde{y})$ for the policy update is calculated as $\tilde{r}(x, \tilde{y}) = r(x, \tilde{y}) - \text{penalty}$. This modified reward signal guides the policy to avoid producing responses that fall into historically prevalent error clusters.

#### 3.5. Experimental Setup

- **Base Models:** Experiments were conducted using three Transformer-based LLMs: Qwen3-1.7B, Qwen3-8B, and Qwen2.5-Math-7B. These models represent different scales and training methodologies (with and without explicit reasoning processes).
- **Training Data:** The training corpus combined the DAPO-Math-17K dataset with levels 3-5 of the MATH dataset. All problems were formatted using the Qwen-Math template.
- **RL Framework:** All reinforcement learning training was implemented based on the verl framework.
- **Clustering Parameters:** HDBSCAN was configured with a minimum cluster size of 2, a minimum number of samples of 1, and Euclidean distance as the metric. Logits from the last 14 Transformer layers were concatenated and L2-normalized.
- **Penalty Coefficients:** $\alpha$ and $\beta$ hyperparameters were set (e.g., $\alpha=0.1, \beta=0.2$ for Qwen3-1.7B/Qwen2.5-Math-7B; $\alpha=0.02, \beta=0.04$ for Qwen3-8B).
- **Training Details:** Prompt-level batch size of 512, 16 rollouts per prompt, maximum prompt length 1024 tokens, varying maximum response lengths. Training utilized H200 hardware.
- **Baselines:** Performance was compared against the base model (without fine-tuning) and RL algorithms including GRPO, DAPO, and GRPO with Entropy Advantage.
- **Evaluation:** Models were evaluated on five mathematical reasoning benchmarks: AIME24, AMC23, MATH500, Minerva, and OlympiadBench. The key metrics were pass@1 and pass@128, with 128 samples per problem.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of the MEDS framework across various settings and provide insights into its mechanisms.

#### 4.1. Overall Performance

- **Consistent Superiority:** MEDS consistently achieved the best overall performance compared to the base model and other RL baselines (GRPO, DAPO, GRPO w/ Entropy Adv) across all three tested model scales (Qwen3-1.7B, Qwen2.5-Math-7B, Qwen3-8B) and all five mathematical reasoning benchmarks (AIME24, AMC23, MATH500, Minerva, OlympiadBench).
- **Quantitative Gains:** The framework demonstrated performance improvements of up to 4.13 pass@1 points and 4.37 pass@128 points on average. The largest observed pass@128 improvement was on Qwen3-8B, increasing from 70.81 to 82.67 on a single dataset, representing a relative gain of approximately 17%.
- **Scaling Benefits:** The benefits of MEDS appeared more pronounced with larger base models, suggesting its effectiveness scales with the model's capabilities.
- **Pass@k Curves:** Visualizations of pass@k (for $k \in \{1, 2, \dots, 128\}$) further confirmed that MEDS generally matched or outperformed baselines across the entire range of $k$.
- **Computational Efficiency:** MEDS incurred a minimal computational overhead compared to DAPO, indicating that its performance gains were achieved without substantial increases in training time.

#### 4.2. Impact on Exploration Behavior

- **Increased Diversity (LLM-based Evaluation):** Using Claude-Haiku-4.5 as an evaluator for both "Within-Step Diversity" (diversity of rollouts for the same prompt at a given step) and "Across-Step Diversity" (novelty of reasoning patterns in later steps compared to earlier ones), MEDS consistently obtained higher exploration scores than baselines. This suggests that MEDS encourages the generation of a wider range of distinct reasoning paths.
- **Representational Diversity (Top-1 Eigen Ratio):** Analysis of the covariance structure of stored logits revealed that MEDS yielded consistently lower "Top-1 Eigen Ratio" values. This metric, which measures the proportion of variance explained by the dominant principal direction in logit space, indicates a more uniform spread of logits and less concentration on a single, dominant error direction, supporting the claim of increased exploration diversity at a representation level.

#### 4.3. Logits Reflect Reasoning Patterns

- **Qualitative Analysis:** A case study demonstrated that layer-wise logit vectors capture underlying logical reasoning structures. Responses exhibiting similar reasoning patterns showed correlated logit trajectories, particularly in later Transformer layers. Conversely, responses following different reasoning paths remained distinguishable. This indicates that logits are not merely reflecting surface-level similarity or final answers but rather intermediate reasoning processes. For instance, responses leading to the same incorrect answer could still have distinct logit patterns, while responses with different answers might share similar reasoning structures.

#### 4.4. Logits Provide Correct Clustering Signals

- **Quantitative Validation:** To validate that logit-based clustering aligns with semantic reasoning, an automated annotation procedure using Claude-Haiku-4.5 was employed. The LLM proxy categorized negative responses into 11 predefined error types. Comparing the agreement between LLM-based and logit-based clustering for response pairs, the logit clustering achieved a substantial positive correlation of 61.2%. This suggests that logits serve as a viable proxy for identifying logical similarity in reasoning patterns.

#### 4.5. Clustering Quality Matters

- **Feature Construction Impact:** Experiments comparing various logit aggregation strategies for clustering demonstrated that the choice of features influences both clustering quality and downstream performance. Using logits from the *last 14 layers* as clustering features yielded the best performance. Other variants utilizing clustering information still outperformed the vanilla DAPO baseline.
- **Correlation with Performance:** A notable finding was the strong correlation between clustering consistency (as measured by agreement with LLM annotations) and the resulting downstream model performance. Configurations that achieved higher agreement with the LLM-based semantic clustering also exhibited greater performance gains, reinforcing the hypothesis that better recognition of repeated error patterns directly translates to improved model outcomes. The degenerate case of assigning all logits to a single cluster resulted in performance worse than DAPO, further highlighting the importance of meaningful clustering.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of reinforcement learning for LLMs by addressing the challenge of policy collapse and repetitive error generation. The significance and potential impact of MEDS are multifaceted:

1. **Mitigation of Recurrent Errors:** By explicitly incorporating historical error patterns into reward design, MEDS offers a targeted mechanism to discourage the repetition of specific faulty reasoning, moving beyond generic randomness encouragement. This can lead to more robust and less brittle LLM agents.
2. **Enhanced Exploration:** The dynamic penalty system incentivizes the LLM to explore a wider variety of reasoning paths, thereby increasing behavioral diversity. This broader exploration can help models escape local optima and discover more effective or novel solutions, particularly in complex reasoning tasks like mathematics.
3. **Efficient Use of Internal Representations:** The method leverages existing layer-wise logits as a lightweight and efficient proxy for capturing reasoning trajectories. This avoids the computational overhead associated with training auxiliary reward models or requiring extensive external annotations, making it practical for large-scale LLM training.
4. **Theoretical Foundation:** The provided theoretical analysis supports the effectiveness of dynamic penalties based on historical repetition, lending a formal basis to the empirical observations.
5. **Improved Performance:** Consistent performance gains across multiple LLM scales and mathematical reasoning benchmarks demonstrate the practical utility of MEDS in improving model accuracy (pass@1) and solution coverage (pass@128).
6. **Insights into LLM Reasoning:** The work further validates the utility of logits as a signal for understanding and influencing the internal reasoning processes of LLMs, suggesting that these internal states can serve as actionable features for control and supervision.
7. **New Research Directions:** MEDS opens avenues for future research in memory-enhanced reinforcement learning, adaptive reward functions, and the development of more sophisticated methods for leveraging internal model states and historical data to guide LLM training. This could include exploring more complex aggregation functions for logits or dynamic weighting of historical information.

Overall, MEDS represents a step toward more intelligent and adaptive LLM agents that can learn from their past mistakes to explore more effectively and achieve higher performance in complex tasks.
