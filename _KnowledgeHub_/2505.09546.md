# Distilling Realizable Students from Unrealizable Teachers

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Cornell University present CritiQ and ReTRy, two distinct methods designed to enable student policies with partial observations to learn from teachers possessing privileged full-state information. These methods achieve higher learning efficiency and success rates in robotic tasks by strategically managing teacher queries and dynamically refining training resets.

## Problem

- Robotic agents operating with partial observations struggle to learn from teacher policies that rely on privileged, full-state information, leading to information asymmetry.
- Directly imitating an "unrealizable" teacher, whose optimal actions depend on hidden context, causes state aliasing where multiple teacher states map to a single student observation, resulting in conflicting guidance and policy degradation.
- Existing distillation methods either compromise teacher optimality by forcing realizable demonstrations or introduce conflicting training signals by combining imitation and reinforcement learning, both leading to inefficiencies.

## Method

- CritiQ, an imitation learning algorithm, selectively queries the teacher only at "critical states" where the student is at risk of entering an unrecoverable trajectory, avoiding the accumulation of conflicting labels.
- ReTRy, a ==reinforcement learning== algorithm, dynamically expands the set of effective reset states by rolling out the teacher from states previously visited by the student, thereby ensuring resets are always to recoverable states.
- Both methods formalize the problem using a Contextual Markov Decision Process (CMDP) to address state aliasing, with CritiQ using a discriminator to identify critical states and ReTRy iteratively refining the reset distribution.

## Results

- CritiQ and ReTRy consistently outperformed baseline methods (Behavior Cloning, DAgger, and standard RL) in simulated drawer search, block push, and navigation tasks, with ReTRy achieving near-perfect success rates (close to **100%**).
- ReTRy demonstrated robust real-world transfer by achieving a **100%** success rate in a physical drawer search task using a Hello Robot Stretch, matching its simulation performance.
- ReTRy proved more robust for long-horizon tasks and achieved full exploration, while CritiQ showed faster initial exploration and higher data efficiency, though it experienced a performance drop in real-world scenarios due to sim-to-real distribution shifts.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Blindly imitating a teacher with privileged information under state aliasing leads to policy degradation, as conflicting teacher actions for the same student observation accumulate and destabilize learning.
> - Strategic interaction, such as selective querying, can mitigate information asymmetry by only seeking teacher guidance when necessary, preventing the amplification of inconsistent training signals.
> - Leveraging the teacher to generate recovery trajectories from student-explored states allows for more efficient exploration and robust learning, as the student can always restart from a known path to success.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robotics research frequently addresses the challenge of enabling agents to operate effectively in real-world environments characterized by partial observations and limited exploration capabilities. A common strategy to address this disparity between simulated training environments (which often provide privileged, full-state information) and real-world deployment is teacher-student policy distillation. In this paradigm, a teacher policy, typically trained with access to complete state information, guides a student policy that operates under realistic, often incomplete, sensing constraints. This approach has found applications in various domains, including quadrupedal locomotion, robotic manipulation, and visual navigation.

A central problem within this framework is information asymmetry. The teacher's privileged state information often leads to guidance that is "unrealizable" in the student's limited observation space. This condition, referred to as state aliasing, occurs when multiple distinct teacher states map to the same student observation, resulting in conflicting optimal actions for the student given a single perceived input. Directly imitating such a teacher can lead to policy degradation, distributional shifts, and suboptimal trajectories because the student lacks the context to resolve these ambiguities.

Existing methodologies to mitigate information asymmetry generally fall into two categories. One approach involves modifying the teacher's policy to generate demonstrations that are realizable by the student, often at the cost of optimality. The other strategy combines imitation learning (IL) with reinforcement learning (RL), allowing the student to explore independently to infer missing information. However, both categories present trade-offs: constraining the teacher can limit the overall performance ceiling, while blending IL and RL can introduce conflicting training signals that destabilize the learning process.

This work contributes to the broader landscape by proposing alternative solutions that do not modify the teacher's policy or solely rely on conflicting IL/RL signals. Instead, it focuses on strategic student-teacher interaction. The paper aims to improve the efficiency and performance of policy distillation by enabling the student to query the teacher selectively and manage its exploration through informed resetting, thereby navigating the challenges of information asymmetry more effectively. This positions the work as an advancement in robust learning for real-world robotic systems, particularly those facing the sim-to-real gap under partial observability.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop effective methods for policy distillation where a student policy, operating with partial observations, learns from a teacher policy that has access to privileged, full-state information. The core motivation stems from the fundamental challenge of information asymmetry between the teacher and student.

The specific challenges driving this research include:
1. **State Aliasing and Unrealizable Teacher Guidance:** In scenarios where the teacher possesses privileged information (e.g., exact object location in a cluttered room), its optimal trajectory and actions may be directly dependent on this information. A student, lacking this privilege, observes a "collapsed" state where multiple distinct teacher states appear identical. This state aliasing means that the teacher might provide different optimal actions for what appears to be the same student observation, leading to conflicting labels if the student simply imitates. The paper highlights that blindly imitating such an "unrealizable" teacher can cause the student's trajectory to diverge, resulting in distributional shift and degraded performance.
2. **Inefficiencies of Existing Approaches:**
 - **Modifying the Teacher:** Previous work has attempted to adapt the teacher's policy to produce realizable but often sub-optimal demonstrations for the student. This compromises the teacher's original optimality, limiting the potential performance of the student.
 - **Combining Imitation Learning and Reinforcement Learning:** Other methods blend imitation learning with reinforcement learning, allowing the student to explore and infer missing information. However, this can lead to conflicting signals during training, destabilizing the learning process and reducing efficiency.
3. **Limitations of Standard Imitation Learning (DAgger):** The paper specifically motivates the need for new methods by analyzing the limitations of DAgger, a common imitation learning algorithm, in the presence of state aliasing. It posits that DAgger's mechanism of repeatedly querying the teacher at all visited states exacerbates state aliasing by aggregating conflicting expert actions for the same student state, causing the "realizability error" to monotonically increase and leading to poor performance.

Against this background, the key insight motivating the proposed methods is that the student should not blindly imitate the teacher but instead strategically interact with it. This strategic interaction involves two main facets:
- **Selective Querying:** The student should query the teacher only when necessary, specifically when it is at risk of entering an unrecoverable trajectory. This prevents the accumulation of conflicting labels at aliased states.
- **Strategic Resetting:** The student should leverage teacher "recovery states" for efficient exploration, allowing it to reset training to states where a path to success is known, even if the student's own exploration leads it to novel, but recoverable, regions.

Based on this motivation, the research sets out to achieve the following specific objectives:
1. **Formalize the Problem:** Provide a formal description of teacher-student distillation under information mismatch, specifically within a Contextual Markov Decision Process (CMDP) framework.
2. **Propose CritiQ:** Develop an imitation learning algorithm, Critical State Query (CritiQ), that adaptively determines when the student should query the teacher for corrections, focusing on critical states to manage information asymmetry.
3. **Propose ReTRy:** Introduce a reinforcement learning algorithm, Resetting to Teacher Recovery (ReTRy), that selects effective initial training points by resetting the student to teacher recovery states, thereby enabling sample-efficient exploration.
4. **Analyze Performance Bounds:** Demonstrate how CritiQ and ReTRy theoretically improve performance bounds compared to standard algorithms like DAgger under state aliasing conditions.
5. **Empirical Validation:** Validate the proposed methods in both simulated and real-world robotic tasks, demonstrating improvements in training efficiency and final performance over standard teacher-student baselines.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research formulates the problem of policy distillation under privileged information as a Contextual Markov Decision Process (CMDP). In this framework, the teacher policy operates on a privileged state `˜s = (c, s)`, where `c` is the hidden context and `s` is the observable state. The student, in contrast, observes only `s`, where `s = f(˜s)` represents a surjective mapping, indicating that multiple `˜s` (different contexts) can map to the same `s`. This inherent state aliasing is identified as the core challenge, as it leads to inconsistent teacher actions for a given student observation, rendering direct imitation problematic. The paper formalizes that under such aliasing, algorithms like DAgger lead to poor performance.

To address this, the authors propose two distinct methods: Critical State Query (CritiQ) for imitation learning and Resetting to Teacher Recovery (ReTRy) for reinforcement learning.

#### 3.1. A. Critical State Query (CritiQ)

CritiQ is an imitation learning method designed to mitigate the problem of state aliasing by selectively querying the teacher.

1. **Overview:** Unlike DAgger, which queries the teacher at all states visited by the student, CritiQ queries only at "critical states." A critical state is defined as a state where the student is about to take an action that could lead to an unrecoverable trajectory, and for which no prior recovery supervision exists. The rationale is that indiscriminate querying in aliased states leads to conflicting labels, destabilizing student policy learning. By limiting queries to critical states, CritiQ aims to provide necessary corrections without amplifying inconsistent data.
2. **Algorithm (Algorithm 1):**
 - The student policy (`π`) is initialized via behavior cloning from a pre-collected teacher dataset. A discriminator (`G_ϕ`) is also initialized.
 - In each training iteration, the student policy `π_i` is rolled out to generate a trajectory of states.
 - For each state `s_t` in the student's trajectory, the discriminator `G_ϕ(s_t)` evaluates whether `s_t` is a critical state. The paper defines a critical state as a state visited by the student that the expert has not previously visited (or for which the discriminator score falls below a threshold `κ`).
 - If a state is deemed critical, the teacher (`π*`) is queried for the correct action `a*_t`, and the pair `(s_t, a*_t)` is added to the aggregated dataset `D`.
 - The student policy `π_i+1` is then updated to minimize an imitation loss `ℓ(D, π)` while also being trained to "fool" the discriminator (maximizing `G_ϕ(τ)` for its own trajectories), via a loss `L_S(D, G_ϕ) = ℓ(D, π) - λE_{τ~π}[G_ϕ(τ)]`.
 - Concurrently, the discriminator `G_ϕ` is updated to better distinguish between teacher and student trajectories using a standard adversarial loss `L_G(π, π*)`.
 - This iterative process refines both the student policy and the discriminator, with the best-performing student policy selected based on validation data.
3. **Analysis:** The paper analyzes DAgger's performance bound, noting that in CMDPs, the imitation loss `ϵ` includes a realizability error `δ` due to state aliasing. DAgger's repeated querying leads to a monotonically increasing `δ` because conflicting expert actions for the same aliased student state accumulate in the dataset (Eq. 5, 6). CritiQ addresses this by limiting teacher queries to `S_critical`, ensuring `|D_t(s)| ≈ |D_t-1(s)|` for non-critical states (Eq. 7), thereby slowing the growth of `δ`. The appendix further clarifies that critical state selection involves a "necessary condition" (student entering an unseen region) and an "optimization criterion" (minimizing aliasing impact while maximizing useful data augmentation and maintaining state connectivity to the expert dataset).

#### 3.2. B. Resetting to Teacher Recovery (ReTRy)

ReTRy is a reinforcement learning method aimed at improving sample efficiency under partial observability by iteratively refining the reset distribution.

1. **Overview:** While RL inherently avoids the aliasing problem by learning from trial and error, training RL agents under partial observability is challenging due to the student's lack of privileged information for efficient exploration. Traditional resets often rely on fixed expert-visited states. ReTRy overcomes this by dynamically expanding the set of reset states: the teacher is rolled out from states *visited by the student*, thereby augmenting the reset distribution to cover states the student is likely to encounter. This ensures that resets are always to "recoverable" states.
2. **Algorithm (Algorithm 2):**
 - The student policy (`π`) and two datasets, `D_π*` (teacher-visited states for resets) and `D_π` (student-visited states), are initialized.
 - In each iteration, the student is initialized by sampling a state `s_0` from `D_π*`.
 - The student `π_i` then rolls out from `s_0`, collecting its trajectory `τ_π`. These student-visited states are aggregated into `D_π`.
 - The student policy `π_i+1` is updated using a standard policy gradient method based on `τ_π`.
 - Crucially, after the student's rollouts, the teacher `π*` is then rolled out *from states sampled from the student-visited dataset `D_π`*. This generates new recovery demonstrations `τ_π*` which are added to `D_π*`, thus expanding the set of available reset states for the next student iteration.
 - This iterative process dynamically refines the reset distribution, ensuring that it adapts to the student's evolving exploration.
3. **Analysis:** The Performance Difference Lemma (PDL) highlights that efficient RL training depends on the reset distribution `d(s)` closely approximating the optimal realizable policy's state visitation distribution `d_π_opt(s)`. If the teacher's reset distribution `d_π*(s)` is unrealizable for the student due to aliasing, the density ratio `d_π*(s)/d_π_opt(s)` can be unbounded, leading to slow convergence. ReTRy addresses this by iteratively refining `D_π*` through teacher rollouts from student-visited states. By progressively blending teacher and student rollouts, ReTRy aims to keep this density ratio bounded, leading to tighter performance bounds and faster convergence.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of CritiQ and ReTRy was evaluated across both simulated and real-world robotic tasks using a Hello Robot Stretch mobile manipulator. The tasks included:
- **Drawer Search:** Finding an object in one of three drawers (simulated and real). The teacher knows the object's location; the student only tracks opened drawers.
- **Push Block Search:** Pushing a block to one of three goal locations (simulated). The teacher knows the correct goal; the student tracks visited locations.
- **Navigation Search:** Navigating to a room containing an object out of four possibilities (simulated). The teacher knows the object's room; the student tracks visited rooms.

Baselines included Behavior Cloning (BC), DAgger, and Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) for RL. Success rate was the primary evaluation metric.

**Q1. Can student policies learn effectively from unrealizable teachers?**
- **Simulation Results:** Both CritiQ and ReTRy consistently outperformed baseline methods across all three simulated tasks.
 - BC performance was near-random, indicating its failure when a single teacher trajectory cannot provide recovery from errors.
 - DAgger exhibited complete failure, attributed to the accumulation of conflicting labels from aliased teacher states, leading to policy divergence.
 - Standard RL (SAC) achieved a 0% success rate on the Drawer and Navigation tasks, struggling with sparse rewards and large exploration spaces under partial observability. It reached 65% on the Block Push task, which has a comparatively smaller exploration space.
 - ReTRy achieved near-perfect success rates (close to 100%) by effectively leveraging its teacher state reset strategy.
 - CritiQ demonstrated strong performance, achieving high success rates (e.g., 78% on Drawer, 97% on Block Push, 95% on Navigation), though its performance was slightly lower than ReTRy on the more challenging Drawer task.

**Q2. Do CritiQ and ReTRy enable effective exploration?**
- **Exploration Level:** Evaluation of the "exploration level" (quantifying how thoroughly agents explore possible goal options, e.g., opening drawers) showed that:
 - Standard RL struggled significantly with exploration, often converging to a "no-exploration" level due to sparse rewards.
 - Both CritiQ and ReTRy demonstrated improved exploration over training time.
 - ReTRy eventually achieved full exploration but required a longer training duration.
 - CritiQ showed more rapid improvement in exploration during early training stages but saturated at a mid-level of exploration.

**Q3. Do these policies transfer to real-world settings?**
- **Real Robot Drawer Search:**
 - ReTRy maintained a 100% success rate in the real-world drawer search task, consistent with its simulation performance.
 - CritiQ experienced a performance drop, from 78% success in simulation to 66% in reality. This was attributed to a distribution shift from simulation to real-world dynamics and the compounding errors inherent in behavior-cloned policies, particularly after opening multiple drawers. Despite this drop, CritiQ still significantly outperformed baseline methods, which failed to generalize to high-exploration regimes in the real world.

**Q4. Why does ReTRy outperform CritiQ?**
- ReTRy's superior robustness is attributed to its foundation in reinforcement learning, which allows it to learn from sparse rewards and systematically structure exploration. This approach helps ReTRy avoid compounding errors more effectively.
- CritiQ, relying on behavior cloning and a discriminator, is susceptible to: (1) early-stage discriminator errors that can misclassify critical states, introducing noise into the training set, and (2) the inherent fragility of behavior cloning in long-horizon, high-variance tasks, where errors can accumulate.

**Q5. When should CritiQ or ReTRy be used?**
- **CritiQ:** Suitable when the teacher policy is near-optimal and training time is a significant constraint, especially in high-dimensional observation spaces where data efficiency is crucial.
- **ReTRy:** More robust for long-horizon tasks and can tolerate sub-optimal teachers due to its RL foundation, which promotes better generalization and recovery. However, it typically requires longer training durations and may face scalability challenges in extremely large exploration spaces.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research addresses a fundamental challenge in the field of learning-based control for robotics: the efficient and robust distillation of policies from highly capable, privileged teachers to partially observable student agents. The presence of information asymmetry and state aliasing has historically limited the effectiveness of standard imitation learning and made direct reinforcement learning challenging for real-world robotic tasks.

The introduction of CritiQ and ReTRy represents a significant methodological contribution. CritiQ offers an imitation learning solution that intelligently manages expert queries, preventing the degradation of policy learning caused by conflicting labels in aliased states. This approach is particularly relevant for scenarios where data efficiency is paramount and a near-optimal teacher is available. ReTRy provides an reinforcement learning framework that accelerates exploration by dynamically refining reset distributions. This method allows the student to benefit from expert recovery trajectories even when exploring novel states, leading to more robust and generalized policies, especially for long-horizon tasks.

The empirical validation across both simulated and real-world robotic manipulation and navigation tasks underscores the practical relevance and effectiveness of the proposed methods. Their demonstrated ability to significantly outperform standard baselines (Behavior Cloning, DAgger, and even vanilla RL in some cases) in complex, partially observable environments highlights their potential to bridge the sim-to-real gap more reliably. ReTRy's 100% success rate in the real-world drawer task is a notable indicator of its robustness.

The insights into the trade-offs between CritiQ and ReTRy (e.g., CritiQ's data efficiency vs. ReTRy's long-term robustness) provide valuable guidance for practitioners in selecting appropriate learning strategies based on task characteristics and available resources. By offering principled ways to navigate unrealizable teacher guidance, this work has the potential to:
- **Enhance Robot Autonomy:** Enable robots to learn more effectively from demonstrations, leading to more capable and adaptable autonomous systems in unstructured real-world settings.
- **Improve Sim-to-Real Transfer:** Provide tools that make policy transfer from simulation to reality more robust, reducing the need for extensive real-world data collection and training.
- **Advance Foundation Models in Robotics:** Offer frameworks that could contribute to the development of more generalizable robotic policies, potentially informing efforts to distill complex behaviors from large-scale models or human demonstrations under realistic sensing constraints.

While the research acknowledges limitations, such as CritiQ's sensitivity to discriminator accuracy and the compounding errors inherent in behavior cloning, it also outlines clear directions for future work, including more principled critical state detection. This indicates an ongoing effort to refine these methods and extend their applicability, further solidifying their potential impact on the field of robotic learning.
