# DIFFTACTILE: A Physics-based Differentiable Tactile Simulator for Contact-rich Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Carnegie Mellon University, MIT, and other institutions developed DIFFTACTILE, a physics-based differentiable tactile simulator that integrates FEM for sensors, MLS-MPM for multi-material objects, and Position-Based Dynamics for cables. The system effectively reduces the sim-to-real gap by optimizing physical parameters with real-world data and demonstrates improved efficiency for learning contact-rich robotic manipulation skills.

## Problem

- Existing robotic simulators often lack high-fidelity contact modeling for soft bodies and tactile sensors, frequently relying on rigid-body approximations.
- Simulators struggled to support diverse object materials (elastic, elastoplastic, cables) and complex, dynamic, contact-rich manipulation tasks.
- A significant sim-to-real gap and inefficient skill learning methods hampered the practical application of tactile sensing in robotics.

## Method

- DIFFTACTILE combines Finite Element Method (FEM) for soft tactile sensor deformation, Moving Least Square Material Point Method (MLS-MPM) for diverse object types (rigid, elastic, elastoplastic), and Position-Based Dynamics (PBD) for cables, all within a differentiable Taichi framework.
- A penalty-based contact model handles interactions between FEM-modeled sensors and various object types, accounting for normal forces and friction.
- A learning-based pixel module, approximated by an MLP, reconstructs the optical response of vision-based tactile sensors, accurately capturing high spatial variations.

## Results

- System identification achieved lower pixel-wise tactile marker Mean Squared Errors (MSE) in Real2Sim evaluations (e.g., 3.08 ± 1.27 vs. 3.54 ± 1.73 for press-slide) compared to baselines, demonstrating a closer match to real-world tactile responses.
- The learning-based optical simulation produced more accurate images with lower L1 error (7.94 vs. 16.1) and higher SSIM (0.999 vs. 0.998) compared to prior methods like Taxim.
- For grasping elastic and elastoplastic objects, tactile feedback reduced slipping distance (e.g., from 0.18 ± 0.06 to 0.07 ± 0.04 for elastic objects) and improved position accuracy, leading to more stable grasps; it also outperformed baselines in various contact-rich manipulation tasks, such as object reposing (60.82 vs. 4.89 for SAC).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating distinct physics models (FEM, MLS-MPM, PBD) within a unified differentiable framework enables comprehensive and physically accurate simulation of complex, multi-material contact-rich scenarios.
> - Differentiable physics allows for gradient-based system identification, effectively minimizing the sim-to-real gap by refining simulator parameters with real-world tactile and force data.
> - Providing dense and accurate tactile feedback within a differentiable simulator significantly enhances the efficiency of learning manipulation skills for contact-rich tasks compared to sampling-based or model-free methods.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robotic manipulation tasks often require fine-grained interaction with diverse objects, a capability that tactile sensing can provide by offering detailed information on contact geometries and forces. While the efficacy of tactile sensing has been recognized for tasks such as grasping fragile objects, operating in occluded environments, and detecting incipient slip, its integration with physics-based simulation for complex, contact-rich robotic manipulation remains an area of ongoing development.

Existing robotic simulators often exhibit limitations concerning tactile sensing. Many either lack tactile simulation entirely or primarily focus on interactions with rigid bodies, neglecting the inherent deformability of tactile sensors and many manipulated objects. Simulators like Tacto (Wang et al., 2022) and Tactile-Gym (Church et al., 2022; Lin et al., 2022) are built upon rigid-body physics engines such as PyBullet, which approximate soft body contact dynamics, potentially compromising physical fidelity. Other approaches, such as those by Xu et al. (2023), also leverage rigid-body dynamics for efficiency but may lack accuracy for soft contacts.

Finite Element Method (FEM)-based simulators (Narang et al., 2021; Kim et al., 2022) offer higher physical accuracy for soft body dynamics, including tactile sensors. However, these methods have primarily been applied to manipulating rigid objects. Taxim (Si & Yuan, 2022) approximated FEM for tactile sensors in quasi-static scenarios, applied successfully to tasks like object shape estimation and grasp stability prediction, but did not thoroughly explore highly dynamic manipulation tasks. Tacchi (Chen et al., 2023) used the Moving Least Square Material Point Method (MLS-MPM) for soft body deformation but did not emphasize differentiability or comprehensive contact dynamics.

Differentiable physics-based simulation has emerged as a methodology for efficient gradient-based policy learning and system identification, with examples like PlasticineLab (Huang et al., 2021) for soft body manipulation and FluidLab (Xian et al., 2022) for fluid manipulation, often utilizing MLS-MPM. However, applying differentiability specifically to physically accurate, multi-material tactile simulation in dynamic, contact-rich scenarios has been less explored. Previous differentiable tactile simulations (e.g., Xu et al., 2023) still approximated soft body contact via rigid-body dynamics.

The optical simulation of vision-based tactile sensors has also seen development, moving from model-based methods to data-driven approaches. While some data-driven methods use generative models for style transfer (Higuera et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2022), requiring extensive real-world data, pixel-based approaches like Taxim (Si & Yuan, 2022) offer data efficiency but may make assumptions limiting applicability to sensors with low spatial variance.

DIFFTACTILE addresses these gaps by integrating a physics-based, differentiable framework that supports dense and accurate tactile feedback across diverse contact modes and object materials. It combines FEM for sensor modeling, MLS-MPM for various object types, and Position-Based Dynamics (PBD) for cables, alongside a penalty-based contact model. Furthermore, it incorporates a learning-based optical simulation capable of handling high spatial variation. This combination aims to provide a unified platform for studying complex, contact-rich manipulation tasks, offering both physical fidelity and the advantages of differentiability for sim-to-real transfer and efficient skill learning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to introduce DIFFTACTILE, a physics-based differentiable tactile simulation system designed to enhance robotic manipulation capabilities. This enhancement is sought through the provision of dense and physically accurate tactile feedback, which is crucial for robots to achieve human-level manipulation skills across a variety of tasks.

The motivation for developing DIFFTACTILE stems from several identified limitations in existing robotic and tactile simulators:
1. **Limited Contact Modeling Fidelity:** Prior tactile simulators often simplify the modeling of stress and deformation in materials during contact. Many primarily focus on manipulating rigid bodies or use approximations that compromise the physical accuracy required for soft body interactions, which are inherent to tactile sensing. There is a need for a simulator that emphasizes high-fidelity, physics-based contact modeling to accurately represent the complex contact geometries, forces, and dynamics of soft bodies.
2. **Lack of Material Diversity:** Existing simulators frequently restrict interactions to rigid objects. To support more versatile manipulation applications, a simulator capable of modeling diverse object types—including elastic, elastoplastic, and cable-like deformable objects—is necessary.
3. **Enhancing Dynamic Manipulation Tasks:** While some progress has been made in simulating tactile sensors for quasi-static scenarios, the simulation of highly dynamic manipulation tasks involving complex object interactions (e.g., grasping deformable or fragile objects, manipulating cables, opening articulated cases) has not been thoroughly explored.
4. **Bridging the Sim-to-Real Gap:** Physics-based simulation is a significant tool in robotics, but discrepancies between simulation and real-world physics (the "sim-to-real gap") often hinder the direct transfer of learned policies. Differentiable physics offers a mechanism for system identification, allowing the refinement of simulator physical properties using real-world data, thereby reducing this gap.
5. **Improving Skill Learning Efficiency:** Traditional sampling-based methods for robot learning can be computationally expensive. Differentiable simulation provides fine-grained gradient information, which can guide optimization processes, leading to more efficient learning of tactile-assisted grasping and contact-rich manipulation skills.
6. **Accurate Optical Response Simulation:** For vision-based tactile sensors, accurately simulating the optical response to contact is essential. This simulation needs to capture high spatial variations efficiently, moving beyond model-based or data-intensive generative approaches.

In summary, the key objectives are to:
- Develop a physics-based differentiable tactile simulator that accurately models soft body interactions and contact dynamics.
- Support a broad range of object materials, including rigid, elastic, elastoplastic, and cables.
- Incorporate a learning-based method for high-fidelity optical response simulation.
- Leverage differentiability for system identification to narrow the sim-to-real gap.
- Facilitate efficient gradient-based learning of tactile-assisted manipulation skills for contact-rich tasks.

DIFFTACTILE is presented as a platform to address these motivations, enabling comprehensive study of contact-rich manipulations that leverage dense tactile feedback and differentiable physics.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

DIFFTACTILE is a physics-based differentiable tactile simulation system designed to model soft contact, including force distribution, surface deformation, and optical response. The system is implemented using Taichi, which provides parallel GPU computing and automatic differentiation capabilities.

The methodology is structured around four key modules:

#### 3.1. Tactile Sensor Simulation (FEM-based Soft Body Model)

The deformation of the tactile sensor's soft elastomer under contact forces is modeled using the Finite Element Method (FEM). The elastomer is discretized into tetrahedron elements. Boundary conditions (position or velocity control) are applied at the sensor's base. Given that many tactile sensor elastomers, including the one simulated, are hyper-elastic, the Neo-Hookean constitutive model is employed to capture the material's non-linear properties.
The energy density function $\Psi(I_1, J) = \frac{\mu}{2}(I_1 - 3) - \mu\log(J) + \frac{\lambda}{2}\log^2(J)$ and the first Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor $P(F) = \mu(F - F^{-T}) + \lambda\log(J)F^{-T}$ define the material behavior, where $F$ is the deformation gradient, $I_1$ is the first isotropic invariant, $J$ is the determinant of $F$, and $\mu, \lambda$ are Lamé parameters.
Tactile outputs, such as visual images and marker motions for vision-based sensors, are generated by extracting the deformed surface mesh from the FEM solution. Marker locations are interpolated from surface node locations, and these 3D markers are then projected to a 2D image plane using the sensor's camera model. The system allows for customization of sensor shape, size, and material by replacing the input mesh or constitutive model.

#### 3.2. Optical Simulation (Learning-based Pixel Module)

The optical response of a vision-based tactile sensor is reconstructed using a data-driven approach. The sensor's surface is represented as a height function $z = f(x, y)$, and the spatially-varying reflectance function is modeled as a 4D vector-valued function. This function's input consists of the 2D viewing direction $(d = \theta, \phi)$ and 2D surface normals $(x = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}, \frac{\partial f}{\partial y})$, and its output is the change in reflected color $c = (r, g, b)$.
A multilayer perceptron (MLP) $f_{\theta}$ approximates this reflectance function. Its input is augmented with positional encoding $\gamma(p) = \sin(2^0\pi p), \cos(2^0\pi p)..., \sin(2^{L-1}\pi p), \cos(2^{L-1}\pi p)$ for both viewing direction and surface normals to enable fitting data with high-frequency variations. The deformation caused by contact indentation is approximated using pyramid Gaussian kernels, as previously proposed in related work.

#### 3.3. Object Simulation (Multi-Material Models)

To support a wider range of manipulation tasks, DIFFTACTILE models objects beyond rigid bodies using different physics-based methods:
- **Rigid, Elastic, and Elastoplastic Objects:** The Moving Least Square Material Point Method (MLS-MPM) is employed for these object types.
 - **Elastic Objects:** Both corotated linear elasticity and Neo-Hookean elasticity models are implemented.
 - **Elastoplastic Objects:** The von Mises yield criterion is used to model plasticity occurring after elastic deformation.
 - **Rigid Objects:** These are initially treated as elastic using MLS-MPM, and then rigidity constraints are added by calculating object transformations and enforcing shape preservation.
 - **Articulated Objects:** These are approximated by an MLS-MPM-based approach where different materials are assigned to different parts (e.g., joints simulated as soft and thin, other parts as rigid).
- **Cable Objects:** For objects like cables and clothes, Position-Based Dynamics (PBD) (Müller et al., 2007) is incorporated. PBD constrains stretch, bending, and self-collision to simulate cable behavior.

#### 3.4. Penalty-Based Contact Model

Contact dynamics between the tactile sensors and various objects are handled using a penalty-based contact model.
- **Collision Detection:** At each simulation step, contact collision is checked by pairing surface triangle meshes from FEM (sensor) with surface nodes/particles from the object (MLS-MPM or PBD).
- **Force Calculation:** For each penetrating pair (negative signed distance field $d$), a normal penalty force is applied to both mesh nodes and particle nodes.
 The normal force is defined as $f_n = -(k_n + k_d v_n)dn$, where $k_n$ is contact stiffness, $k_d$ is contact damping, $v_n$ is relative normal velocity.
 Static or dynamic friction forces are also applied tangentially based on relative velocities and normal forces: $f_t = - \frac{v_t}{||v_t||} \min(k_t||v_t||, \mu||f_n||)$, where $k_t$ is friction stiffness, $\mu$ is the friction coefficient, and $v_t$ is relative tangential velocity.
 The total contact force $f = f_n + f_t$ is then applied as an external force to both the triangle mesh nodes and the particle nodes.

#### 3.5. FEM-MPM/PBD Coupling

The simulation integrates these different physical models:
- **FEM-MPM Coupling:** FEM is mesh-based, while MLS-MPM is a meshless hybrid Lagrangian-Eulerian method. Contact collision checking and force modeling occur between FEM surface mesh nodes and MLS-MPM Eulerian grids for efficiency. The process involves pre-computing internal elastic forces for FEM, advancing MPM particles to grids, checking collisions and calculating external contact forces, and then post-contact advection of positions and velocities for both FEM and MPM elements.
- **FEM-PBD Coupling:** Similar to FEM-MPM, PBD particles replace MPM particles for contact detection and modeling. After contact, PBD objects solve stretch, bending, and self-collision constraints, followed by velocity advection.
External boundaries, such as tables and walls, are also considered to constrain object positions.

The entire system benefits from the auto-differentiation capabilities of Taichi, allowing for gradient-based optimization of system parameters and trajectories.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of DIFFTACTILE was evaluated across several experiments, encompassing system identification, optical simulation, and various contact-rich manipulation tasks.

#### 4.1. System Identification

This experiment aimed to reduce the sim-to-real gap by optimizing the simulator's physical parameters (Lamé parameters $\mu, \lambda$ for the FEM sensor model, and contact parameters $k_n, k_d, k_t, \mu$ for the contact model) using real-world tactile observations and force readings. Data sequences included press-slide, press-twist-z, and press-twist-x interactions with acrylic and tape surfaces.

- **Experimental Setup:** Real-world sequences of 6-axis force readings, sensor poses, and tactile images were collected. The optimization objective combined pixel-wise tactile marker distances and three-axis force errors.
- **Results:** The gradient-based trajectory optimization method ("Ours") was compared against baselines: Random, RNN-based estimation, and CMA-ES.
 - In Sim2Sim evaluations, "Ours" achieved pixel-wise tactile marker Mean Squared Errors (MSE) of 0.53 ± 0.35 (press-slide), 0.42 ± 0.24 (press-twist-z), and 0.58 ± 0.28 (press-twist-x), outperforming all baselines which had higher MSEs (e.g., Random: 1.69 ± 1.10, 1.15 ± 0.51, 1.43 ± 0.62; CMA-ES: 0.59 ± 0.12, 0.47 ± 0.15, 0.61 ± 0.20).
 - In Real2Sim evaluations, "Ours" again showed lower MSEs (3.08 ± 1.27, 2.38 ± 0.86, 3.99 ± 2.89), demonstrating an improved ability to match real-world data compared to baselines (e.g., Random: 3.54 ± 1.73, 2.59 ± 0.99, 4.47 ± 3.31; CMA-ES: 3.42 ± 1.47, 2.67 ± 1.22, 4.99 ± 4.30).
These results indicate that the differentiable physics approach effectively refines simulator parameters, leading to a closer match with real-world tactile responses.

#### 4.2. Optical Simulation

This evaluation focused on the accuracy of the learning-based method for reconstructing the optical response of a vision-based tactile sensor.

- **Experimental Setup:** A dataset of 250 real-world deformations from a 4mm spherical indenter was collected, with 200 for training and 50 for testing.
- **Results:** The proposed method was compared to Taxim (Si & Yuan, 2022) using image similarity metrics.
 - "Ours" achieved lower L1 (7.94 vs 16.1) and MSE (56.10 vs 85.74) errors, and higher SSIM (0.999 vs 0.998) and PSNR (39.42 vs 38.47) values.
 - This indicates that the method produces more accurate and detailed optical responses, effectively capturing various contact geometries.

#### 4.3. Grasping

This task assessed the simulator's capability in achieving stable and adaptive grasps of objects with diverse properties (shape, size, weight, material) using two tactile sensors as gripper fingertips.

- **Experimental Setup:** Four objects from the EGAD dataset were used, each assigned elastic or elastoplastic material properties. Grasping trajectories involved gripping and lifting, optimized with gradient-based trajectory optimization to minimize position loss ($L_{pos}$), deformation loss ($L_{deform}$), and slipping loss ($L_{slip}$).
- **Results:** Performance was evaluated with and without tactile feedback.
 - For elastic objects, tactile feedback reduced $L_{pos}$ from 0.04 ± 0.03 to 0.01 ± 0.01 and slipping distance ($D_{slip}$) from 0.18 ± 0.06 to 0.07 ± 0.04.
 - For elastoplastic objects, tactile feedback reduced $L_{pos}$ from 0.70 ± 0.53 to 0.24 ± 0.01 and $D_{slip}$ from 0.26 ± 0.09 to 0.20 ± 0.05. $L_{deform}$ also showed slight improvements or similar performance.
These findings suggest that incorporating tactile feedback contributes to improved grasping quality, leading to more stable and less deforming grasps for both elastic and elastoplastic objects.

#### 4.4. Contact-Rich Manipulation

Four additional manipulation tasks were evaluated: surface following, cable straightening, case opening, and object reposing, representing interactions with rigid, deformable, and articulated objects.

- **Experimental Setup:** The tasks involved learning manipulation skills using state rewards and tactile rewards. Comparisons were made between gradient-based trajectory optimization ("Ours"), sampling-based CMA-ES, and model-free reinforcement learning algorithms (SAC, PPO).
- **Results:** Task-specific evaluation metrics (e.g., traveling distance for surface following, aggregate distance for cable straightening, orientation changes for case opening/object reposing) were used.
 - "Ours" with tactile feedback demonstrated significantly better performance across most tasks compared to baselines. For instance, in Object Reposing, "Ours" achieved a score of 60.82 ± 0.00 (with tactile), substantially higher than PPO (4.64 ± 0.15), SAC (4.89 ± 0.11), or CMA-ES (4.50 ± 0.05). In Surface Following, "Ours" (with tactile) reached 51.67 ± 12.86, while PPO, SAC, and CMA-ES ranged from 1.33 to 14.00.
 - Generally, scenarios utilizing tactile feedback ("w/ tactile") exhibited improved performance compared to those without ("w/o tactile") across various methods and tasks, indicating the value of tactile sensing in contact-rich manipulation.
These results demonstrate that the differentiable physics framework of DIFFTACTILE enables efficient skill learning for complex, contact-rich manipulation tasks, and tactile sensing provides beneficial information for these tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

DIFFTACTILE introduces a comprehensive physics-based differentiable tactile simulator that addresses several critical challenges in robotic manipulation and simulation. Its significance lies in its ability to combine high-fidelity physics modeling with differentiability, thereby offering a platform that can advance skill learning and address the sim-to-real gap.

The potential impacts of DIFFTACTILE are multifaceted:

1. **Enhanced Robotic Manipulation Capabilities:** By providing dense and physically accurate tactile feedback, DIFFTACTILE facilitates the development of robots capable of performing more dexterous and adaptive manipulations. This includes handling fragile or deformable objects, operating in environments with occlusions, and executing complex contact-rich tasks that require precise interaction forces and geometries.
2. **Reduction of the Sim-to-Real Gap:** The differentiable nature of the simulator allows for gradient-based system identification. This capability enables the refinement of physical parameters within the simulation using real-world data, leading to a closer match between simulated and real-world behaviors. A reduced sim-to-real gap is critical for effectively transferring policies learned in simulation to physical robots, saving time and resources associated with real-world experimentation.
3. **Improved Efficiency in Skill Learning:** Differentiable physics provides gradient information that can guide optimization algorithms more efficiently than traditional sampling-based or model-free reinforcement learning methods. This is evidenced by the "Ours" method's performance in manipulation tasks, which significantly outperformed baselines in learning efficiency. This efficiency is crucial for tackling increasingly complex robotic tasks where training times can be a major bottleneck.
4. **Support for Diverse Contact Modes and Materials:** Unlike many prior simulators limited to rigid bodies or simplified material models, DIFFTACTILE's integration of FEM for sensors, MLS-MPM for various object types (rigid, elastic, elastoplastic), and PBD for cables allows for the simulation of a broad spectrum of contact interactions. This versatility broadens the applicability of tactile simulation to a wider array of real-world manipulation scenarios.
5. **Foundation for Future Research:** DIFFTACTILE is presented as a valuable platform for studying contact-rich manipulations. Its modular design and differentiable components can serve as a foundation for further research in areas such as:
 - **Advanced Dexterous Manipulation:** Integrating the simulator into frameworks for dexterous robotic hands could enable more sophisticated in-hand manipulation studies.
 - **Multi-Modal Robot Learning:** Combining tactile feedback with other modalities, such as vision, within the simulation could lead to more robust and capable robotic policies.
 - **Physics-Informed AI:** The framework aligns with the growing trend of leveraging physics-based models within AI systems for improved generalization and data efficiency.

In conclusion, DIFFTACTILE represents a significant advancement in tactile simulation, offering a robust and flexible tool for researchers and engineers. Its contributions to physically accurate multi-material contact modeling, differentiable physics for system identification and efficient skill learning, and high-fidelity optical simulation collectively push the boundaries of what is achievable in contact-rich robotic manipulation.
