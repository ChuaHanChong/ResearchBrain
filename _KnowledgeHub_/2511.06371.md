# Towards Adaptive Humanoid Control via Multi-Behavior Distillation and Reinforced Fine-Tuning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Harbin Engineering University, TeleAI, and collaborating institutions developed an Adaptive Humanoid Control (AHC) framework that enables a single policy to perform diverse locomotion behaviors, including fall recovery and walking, and adapt to challenging terrains. The framework produced natural, human-like motions and demonstrated robust sim-to-real transfer on a Unitree G1 robot, autonomously recovering from falls and continuing its tasks.

## Problem

- Existing humanoid robot control methods often require separate policies for each specific skill, limiting generalization and adaptability across different behaviors and terrains.
- Directly training a multi-skill, multi-terrain policy using reinforcement learning (RL) for humanoids is challenging due to conflicting policy gradients from diverse reward functions and difficulties in convergence.
- Integrating highly diverse skills, such as robust fall recovery and human-like locomotion, into a unified, autonomously switching policy for humanoid robots remained an area needing further exploration.

## Method

- A two-stage training process, beginning with multi-behavior distillation, consolidates knowledge from independently trained fall recovery and walking policies into a single basic multi-behavior controller using DAgger and a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architecture.
- The distilled policy is then fine-tuned through reinforced fine-tuning to enhance terrain adaptability, utilizing behavior-specific critics, a terrain curriculum, and PCGrad to mitigate gradient conflicts in multi-task RL.
- ==Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP)== are integrated into both stages to guide the learning process towards generating natural, human-like, and stable motions for both recovery and locomotion.

## Results

- The AHC framework significantly outperformed baseline methods in simulation across various terrains, achieving higher locomotion success rates (e.g., **0.975** on slope vs. **0.599** for HOMIE) and fall recovery success rates (e.g., **1.000** on slope vs. **0.978** for HoST).
- AHC generated smoother, more natural recovery and walking motions compared to baselines, evidenced by qualitative observations and stable joint velocity acceleration profiles during recovery.
- The AHC policy was successfully deployed on a physical Unitree G1 humanoid robot without fine-tuning, demonstrating robust autonomous recovery from falls and seamless transition back to stable locomotion on flat and inclined surfaces.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Multi-behavior distillation effectively integrates diverse skills and mitigates initial gradient conflicts, leading to a more robust basic policy with enhanced individual behaviors.
> - Combining behavior-specific critics with PCGrad (Projecting Conflicting Gradients) during reinforced fine-tuning is crucial for balanced and stable multi-task learning, preventing tasks with smaller reward scales from being neglected.
> - Adversarial Motion Priors provide an effective mechanism to learn naturalistic and smooth motions, reducing the need for complex hand-engineered reward functions for achieving human-like movement quality.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of humanoid robot control has seen advancements, particularly with the application of reinforcement learning (RL) to achieve complex locomotion behaviors such as walking, running, and standing up. Existing research often focuses on training independent policies for each specific skill. While these behavior-specific controllers can excel in their designated tasks (e.g., dedicated policies for standing up, jumping, or walking), they typically exhibit limited generalization abilities across different behaviors and adaptability to varied terrains.

Directly extending this paradigm to multi-skill settings, where a single controller manages diverse behaviors, presents significant challenges. These challenges primarily stem from conflicts in policy gradients arising from different reward functions and environmental requirements for each skill, hindering the convergence of multi-skill policies. While some prior work has explored multi-skill learning for quadruped robots using techniques like Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) or policy distillation, and for humanoids combining highly similar behaviors or requiring explicit control signals, integrating highly diverse skills like fall recovery and general locomotion into a unified, autonomously switching policy for humanoid robots remains an area requiring further exploration.

This paper addresses these limitations by proposing a framework designed to enable humanoid robots to learn a diverse set of human-like locomotion behaviors, including fall recovery and walking, and to adapt these behaviors across various challenging terrains within a single, unified controller. It aims to overcome the generalization and adaptability constraints of single-skill policies and the training complexities of direct multi-task RL for humanoids.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an Adaptive Humanoid Control (AHC) framework that enables humanoid robots to perform a diverse set of human-like locomotion behaviors and adapt these behaviors robustly across various terrains. This overarching goal is broken down into several specific objectives, driven by identified limitations in current humanoid control strategies:

- **Integrate Multiple, Diverse Behaviors into a Unified Policy:** Current methods frequently require separate policies for distinct skills (e.g., standing up, walking, running). The authors aim to consolidate fundamental, yet diverse, behaviors such as robust fall recovery and human-like locomotion into a single controller, moving beyond behavior-specific solutions.
- **Enable Adaptive Behavior Switching:** A key motivation is to allow the humanoid robot to autonomously switch between different behaviors based on its current state and environmental context. For instance, the robot should be able to recover from a fall and seamlessly transition into walking without requiring external intervention or a change in controller.
- **Enhance Terrain Adaptability:** Existing controllers often perform robustly only on specific terrains. This work seeks to improve the generalization of locomotion and recovery skills across a wider range of challenging terrains, including slopes, hurdles, and discrete surfaces.
- **Mitigate Challenges of Multi-Task Reinforcement Learning (RL):** Directly training a multi-skill, multi-terrain policy using RL is challenging due to issues such as conflicting policy gradients from different reward functions, gradient imbalance, and difficulty in achieving stable convergence. The authors are motivated to develop a training methodology that effectively addresses these problems.
- **Achieve Human-like Motion Quality:** Beyond functional performance, the research aims to ensure that the learned behaviors exhibit natural, human-like motion qualities, leveraging techniques like Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP) to guide policy learning towards smoother and more plausible movements.
- **Demonstrate Real-World Applicability:** A critical objective is to validate the effectiveness and robustness of the proposed control framework not only in simulation but also through successful deployment on a physical humanoid robot, such as the Unitree G1, without requiring additional fine-tuning.

These objectives are motivated by the need for more versatile and autonomous humanoid robots capable of operating effectively in complex and unpredictable real-world environments, overcoming the brittle performance and limited generalization typically associated with single-skill or less integrated control approaches.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Adaptive Humanoid Control (AHC) framework employs a two-stage training process: multi-behavior distillation followed by reinforced fine-tuning.

#### 3.1. Preliminaries and Problem Definitions

Each behavior-specific control problem is modeled as a Markov Decision Process (MDP). The adaptive humanoid control problem is formulated as a multi-task RL problem, where each task (e.g., recovery, walking) corresponds to an MDP differing primarily in its reward function and state space, while operating under a unified environment. The policy takes robot proprioception `s_prop` as input, which includes base angular velocity, gravity vector, velocity command, joint positions, joint velocities, and the last action. Behavior-specific policies are initially trained with privileged information (`s_priv`) such as ground friction, motor gains, and base mass, which is not available to the final deployed policy. Actions `a_t` are converted into PD target positions to compute motor torques.

#### 3.2. Multi-Behavior Distillation (Stage 1)

This stage focuses on learning a basic multi-behavior controller that integrates fundamental skills and addresses initial gradient conflicts inherent in direct multi-task RL.

- **Training Behavior-Specific Policies (Teachers):**
 - **Falling Recovery Behavior Policy (`π_br`):** A policy for robustly recovering from various fallen postures on flat terrains. It is trained using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and leverages multiple critics, similar to prior work (HoST), but with an emphasis on flat terrain recovery. To promote natural and smooth recovery motions, an Adversarial Motion Prior (AMP) is incorporated. This involves a discriminator that distinguishes between reference human recovery motions and robot-generated motions, providing a style reward. The robot is initialized in supine or prone positions with joint noise to encourage robust learning.
 - **Walking Behavior Policy (`π_bw`):** A policy for human-like locomotion on flat terrain in response to velocity commands. This policy is also trained with PPO and integrates an AMP-based reward to encourage stable, human-like walking gaits.
- **Behavior Distillation:** The knowledge from the independently trained `π_br` and `π_bw` is distilled into a single basic multi-behavior policy (`π_d`).
 - **Technique:** DAgger (Dataset Aggregation) is used for distillation.
 - **Architecture:** `π_d` is an Mixture-of-Experts (MoE)-based architecture, allowing it to assign different experts to distinct behaviors. It takes only proprioception as input, eliminating the need for privileged information.
 - **Loss Function:** The distillation loss `L_πd` is a supervised loss that minimizes the squared difference between the actions predicted by `π_d` and the actions of the appropriate teacher policy (`π_br` or `π_bw`), depending on the robot's current state (e.g., determined by base height).
 - **Benefits:** This process consolidates diverse behaviors, mitigates initial gradient conflicts, and is observed to enhance individual behaviors (e.g., `π_d`'s walking becomes more robust to near-falls, and its stand-up transitions more smoothly to walking).

#### 3.3. Reinforced Fine-Tuning (Stage 2)

This stage fine-tunes the distilled multi-behavior policy (`π_d`, now denoted `π_ft`) to enhance its adaptability to diverse and complex terrains.

- **Initialization and Objective:** `π_ft` is initialized with `π_d`. The goal is to further improve fall recovery and walking performance on complex terrains (e.g., slopes, hurdles, discrete blocks), while maintaining human-likeness using AMP.
- **Multi-Task RL Formulation:** The problem is treated as a multi-task RL problem, with shared actor parameters and behavior-specific critics.
- **Behavior-Specific Critics and Shared Actor:**
 - **Rationale:** To prevent tasks with larger reward scales from dominating the learning process and to provide more accurate value estimates for task-specific reward functions.
 - **Implementation:** Each task (recovery, walking) is assigned a separate critic network, which learns the value function for its specific reward. The actor network, responsible for generating actions, shares parameters across tasks.
- **Eliminating Gradient Conflict (PCGrad):**
 - **Rationale:** Even with behavior-specific critics, the shared actor can still experience conflicting gradients from different tasks.
 - **Technique:** Projecting Conflicting Gradients (PCGrad) (Yu et al. 2020) is applied. If the gradients from two tasks conflict (negative cosine similarity), one gradient is projected onto the normal plane of the other, effectively removing the conflicting component while preserving progress in non-conflicting directions.
 - **Process:** Task-specific actor gradients are computed on dedicated GPUs, then communicated to a main process where PCGrad is applied, and the updated parameters are broadcast back.
- **Terrain Curriculum:** To improve learning efficiency and adaptability, a terrain curriculum is implemented. This involves gradually increasing the difficulty of the terrain types (flat, slope, hurdle, discrete) based on the robot's performance, with automatic level adjustment. Max slope inclination is 16.6°, hurdle heights up to 0.1m, and discrete block heights from 0.03m to 0.15m.

#### 3.4. Training Details (Appendices)

- **PPO Parameters:** Standard PPO formulation with clipped surrogate loss and Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE). Adam optimizer is used, with a learning rate of 1e-3 for stage 1 and 1e-4 for stage 2. Rollouts of 32 steps, 5 learning epochs, 4 minibatches per epoch. Discount factor γ = 0.99, GAE λ = 0.95.
- **AMP Details:** Discriminator `D_ϕ` takes a 5-step temporal window of 20 joint positions as input. It is trained with a least square GAN formulation and a gradient penalty to ensure natural behaviors. The discriminator output `d` is converted to a smooth surrogate reward `r_style`.
- **Domain Randomization:** Extensive domain randomization is applied in both stages to enhance sim-to-real transfer and robustness, including variations in restitution, friction, base CoM offset, mass payload, link mass, PD gains, actuation offset, motor strength, action delay, and initial joint angles.
- **Hardware:** Training is performed in IsaacGym using 4096 parallel environments on NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPUs. Deployment is on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot at 50Hz, with a 500Hz PD controller.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study evaluated the AHC framework through extensive experiments in both simulation and real-world deployment.

#### 4.1. Simulation Results

- **Terrain Adaptability (Table 1):**
 - AHC significantly outperformed baseline methods (HOMIE for locomotion, HoST for recovery) across all evaluated terrains: flat, slope, hurdle, and discrete. For locomotion, AHC achieved higher success rates (e.g., 0.975 on slope, 0.922 on hurdle, 0.969 on discrete compared to HOMIE's 0.599, 0.407, 0.442) and greater average traversing distances. For recovery, AHC showed higher success rates (e.g., 1.000 on slope, 0.985 on hurdle compared to HoST's 0.978, 0.911).
 - The `Multi-Behaviors Policy` (π_d, after distillation) demonstrated improved robustness on complex locomotion terrains (hurdle, discrete) compared to behavior-specific policies (π_br, π_bw), underscoring the benefits of integrating skills.
 - The `AHC` policy (after reinforced fine-tuning) further improved performance over the distilled `Multi-Behaviors Policy` on most terrain types, particularly for slope and discrete terrains, validating the two-stage training approach. AHC's ability to autonomously recover from falls and resume traversal was a key factor in its superior performance on challenging terrains.

- **AMP for Standing-Up (Figures 3 & 4):**
 - Qualitative analysis (Figure 3) showed that AHC, incorporating AMP, generated smoother and more natural recovery motions (e.g., leg folding, arm support, trunk lifting) from both lying and prone postures, compared to HoST (without AMP) which exhibited uncoordinated and jerky movements.
 - Quantitative analysis of joint velocity acceleration (Figure 4) revealed that AHC produced stable joint actuation with fewer abrupt fluctuations in key hip and knee joints during recovery, in contrast to HoST. This indicated AMP's effectiveness in guiding the policy towards stable and naturalistic motions.

- **Ablation on PCGrad and Behavior-Specific Critics (Table 2, Figures 5 & 6):**
 - **Gradient Conflict (Table 2):** PCGrad consistently increased the average cosine similarity between task gradients during fine-tuning (e.g., from 0.247 to 0.519 with a shared critic; from 0.334 to 0.535 with behavior-specific critics), demonstrating its effectiveness in mitigating gradient conflicts. Behavior-specific critics also contributed to higher gradient similarity.
 - **Value Learning Stability (Figure 5):** Policies utilizing behavior-specific critics (AHC-BC-w/o-PC and AHC) exhibited lower and more stable value loss curves during training compared to shared-critic variants (AHC-SC), suggesting better value function approximation due to decoupled learning for different reward scales.
 - **Balanced Multi-Task Learning (Figure 6):** AHC (with both PCGrad and behavior-specific critics) achieved higher and more balanced episode returns for both recovery and locomotion tasks. Shared-critic variants tended to neglect the locomotion task due to its smaller reward magnitude. AHC also demonstrated faster convergence. These results supported the combined efficacy of PCGrad and behavior-specific critics in balanced multi-task optimization.

#### 4.2. Deployment Results (Figure 7)

The AHC policy was successfully deployed on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot in real-world environments without any additional fine-tuning.
- **Recovery:** The robot consistently recovered from various fallen postures (supine, prone) on both flat and inclined surfaces, even with moderate external disturbances. It demonstrated natural and coordinated motions during recovery and transitioned smoothly into a stable, walking-ready posture.
- **Locomotion:** The robot walked stably on flat ground and inclined surfaces, effectively tracking velocity commands. It demonstrated resilience by withstanding external pushes during walking.
- **Autonomy:** In instances where the robot did fall during walking, it autonomously performed the recovery maneuver and subsequently resumed locomotion, showcasing strong resilience and long-horizon autonomy.
These real-world experiments indicated effective sim-to-real transfer and robust, cohesive integration of recovery and locomotion behaviors.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of humanoid robot control in several significant ways and holds substantial potential for future impact:

- **Advancement in Adaptive Humanoid Control:** The proposed Adaptive Humanoid Control (AHC) framework offers a novel and effective method for creating a unified controller for humanoid robots that can handle diverse, fundamental behaviors (such as fall recovery and locomotion) and adapt to varied, challenging terrains. This represents a step beyond behavior-specific controllers, which are inherently limited in generalization and robustness.
- **Enhanced Autonomy and Resilience:** By enabling autonomous switching between recovery and locomotion and demonstrating robust performance on complex terrains, the AHC framework significantly enhances the autonomy and resilience of humanoid robots. This capability is crucial for robots operating in dynamic, unpredictable environments where falls or unexpected terrain changes are common. The robot's ability to self-recover and continue its task reduces the need for human intervention and increases operational uptime.
- **Methodological Innovations for Multi-Task RL:** The paper demonstrates the effectiveness of a two-stage training approach (multi-behavior distillation followed by reinforced fine-tuning) for complex multi-skill learning in robotics. It effectively addresses long-standing challenges in multi-task reinforcement learning, such as gradient conflicts and imbalances, through the strategic use of Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures, behavior-specific critics, and gradient projection techniques (PCGrad). These methods provide a blueprint for future multi-skill RL endeavors in other complex robotic systems.
- **Learning Naturalistic Motions:** The incorporation of Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP) is shown to be highly effective in guiding the learning process towards producing natural, human-like, and stable motions for both recovery and locomotion. This is significant because hand-designing reward functions to achieve such motion quality is notoriously difficult. AMP reduces the need for laborious reward engineering, potentially accelerating the development of more sophisticated and aesthetically pleasing robot behaviors.
- **Successful Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The successful deployment of the AHC policy on a real-world Unitree G1 humanoid robot without additional fine-tuning highlights the robustness of the learned policy and the efficacy of the training methodology (including extensive domain randomization) in bridging the sim-to-real gap. This practical validation is critical for the broader adoption of RL-based control in real-world robotic applications.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** This work lays a foundation for future research in several directions. It encourages exploration into augmenting the robot's perceptual capabilities with external sensors (e.g., depth cameras, LiDARs) to allow for even greater environmental awareness and interaction. Furthermore, it opens avenues for expanding the repertoire of learned behaviors beyond recovery and basic locomotion, moving towards more complex manipulation, navigation, and human-robot interaction skills, ultimately contributing to the development of more general-purpose humanoid robots.
