# FastStair: Learning to Run Up Stairs with Humanoid Robots

> [!summary] Summary
> FastStair introduces a planner-guided, multi-stage reinforcement learning framework that enables humanoid robots to run up and down stairs with both high speed and stability. This method leverages parallel foothold planning and achieves robust real-world performance, including winning the Canton Tower Robot Run Up Competition by climbing a 33-step spiral staircase in 12 seconds with a peak velocity of 1.65 m/s.

## Problem

- Humanoid robots struggle to simultaneously achieve high agility (speed and dynamic motion) and strict stability when navigating challenging terrains like stairs.
- Model-free reinforcement learning often leads to overly conservative or unstable behaviors on stairs due to implicit stability rewards conflicting with high-velocity objectives.
- Model-based planners provide explicit stability guarantees but typically result in slow, conservative motions and are computationally intensive for integration with large-scale reinforcement learning.

## Method

- A DCM-based foothold planner is reformulated into a GPU-parallel discrete search, achieving a 25x speedup and enabling real-time, explicit stability guidance within the RL training loop.
- A three-stage reinforcement learning framework (Pre-training, Post-training, ==LoRA Fine==-tuning) is developed: pre-training emphasizes safety via foothold-tracking rewards, post-training specializes policies for low and high-velocity bands, and LoRA fine-tuning unifies these experts for smooth, full-range speed transitions.
- The policy's observation space integrates proprioceptive data with privileged information, including terrain elevation maps and the optimal foothold from the parallel planner, to inform control decisions.

## Results

- The parallel foothold planner achieved a latency of approximately 4 ms for 4096 environments, demonstrating a **25x** speedup compared to recent parallel MPC implementations.
- FastStair consistently achieved the highest success rates and lowest velocity-tracking Mean Absolute Error in simulations compared to baseline and adversarial motion prior (AMP) methods.
- The LimX Oli humanoid robot, utilizing FastStair, successfully navigated a 33-step spiral staircase in 12 seconds with a peak velocity of **1.65** m/s in a real-world competition, exhibiting stable high-speed ascent and emergent step-skipping behavior.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Reformulating model-based optimization into a parallel discrete search can overcome computational bottlenecks, making explicit stability guidance tractable for large-scale reinforcement learning.
> - A multi-stage learning approach effectively reconciles the agility-stability trade-off by initially imbuing the policy with planner-derived safety priors, then specializing for speed, and finally unifying policies for seamless performance across velocity ranges.
> - Integrating Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) layers allows for the merging of speed-specialized 'expert' policies into a single network, enabling smooth and robust transitions across diverse velocity commands.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of humanoid robotics has seen significant advancements, particularly in locomotion control, yet matching human agility and stability on complex terrains remains a formidable challenge. Staircases, being a pervasive feature of human environments, represent a critical benchmark for humanoid adaptability. This paper addresses a central dilemma in this domain: the inherent trade-off between achieving high agility (speed, dynamic motion) and ensuring strict stability (avoiding falls) for humanoid robots on stairs.

The existing research landscape broadly bifurcates into two main paradigms for locomotion control:

- **Model-Free Reinforcement Learning (RL):** Recent years have seen RL emerge as a powerful tool for generating dynamic and versatile humanoid locomotion on various terrains, including flat ground and rough patches. However, the paper points out a crucial weakness when applied to stairs: RL's reliance on *implicit stability rewards*. These rewards (e.g., "alive," "posture," "gravity-projection") often conflict with task-level objectives like high-velocity tracking. This conflict frequently forces RL-based policies to prioritize safety over speed, resulting in conservative behaviors typical of current RL stair climbers, or requiring complex perception-augmented approaches. The "fragility" of RL on stairs, particularly regarding stability at high speeds, is a key problem this work seeks to overcome.

- **Model-Based Foothold Planners:** In contrast, classical model-based methods, such as those based on the Divergent Component of Motion (DCM) or Model Predictive Control (MPC), provide *explicit stability guarantees*. These planners calculate feasible footholds by encoding contact feasibility and balance criteria as hard kinodynamic constraints. While excelling at maintaining stability, especially on discrete terrains like stairs, these methods typically use simplifying assumptions and conservative safety margins to ensure real-time computability. This rigor, however, inevitably restricts the solution space, leading to "conservative motion that limits speed" and hinders dynamic agility.

The authors identify a critical gap: while combining model-based planners to *guide* RL exploration seems a natural strategy to leverage the strengths of both, such guidance often transfers the planner's inherent conservatism to the learned policy, making it safe but not agile enough for high-speed tasks. This "persistent tension between planner-guided safety and learned agility" is the core problem that FastStair aims to resolve.

Furthermore, the paper distinguishes itself from other learning-based approaches:
- **Residual-based methods:** These typically use a vision-based network to provide corrective residuals to a pre-existing "blind" policy. FastStair is a more integrated approach, potentially offering higher peak agility.
- **End-to-end frameworks:** While unifying perception and control, these often suffer from severe exploration bottlenecks, especially on discrete terrains without explicit priors. FastStair incorporates such priors.
- **Specialized training strategies:** Methods like curriculum learning or adversarial motion priors (AMP) have been explored. FastStair directly compares against AMP, noting that such methods often fail to ensure strict dynamical feasibility at high speeds, which FastStair explicitly addresses.

In summary, FastStair positions itself at the forefront of humanoid locomotion research by attempting to bridge the critical divide between safety-guaranteeing model-based control and agility-generating model-free RL, specifically addressing the challenges of high-speed stair climbing without sacrificing either attribute. It also innovates in making model-based guidance computationally tractable for large-scale RL training.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of the FastStair project is to enable humanoid robots to perform **fast and stable stair ascent**, mirroring human capabilities, which remains an extremely challenging task due to the simultaneous demands for high agility and strict stability.

The key motivations and more specific objectives driving this research can be broken down as follows:

- **Reconciling Agility and Stability:** The primary motivation is to resolve the fundamental trade-off between agility (speed, dynamic movement) and stability (preventing falls) that plagues current humanoid locomotion methods on challenging terrains like stairs. Existing approaches either prioritize stability at the cost of speed (model-based) or struggle with stability at high speeds (model-free RL).
- **Overcoming RL's Fragility on Stairs:** Model-free RL, while dynamic, often relies on implicit stability rewards that frequently conflict with high-velocity tracking rewards on stairs. This leads to unsafe or overly conservative behaviors. An objective is to introduce explicit stability guidance to make RL policies more robust on stairs.
- **Mitigating Planner-Induced Conservatism:** While model-based planners offer explicit stability guarantees, their strict constraints inherently lead to conservative, slow motions. The motivation is to integrate these planners for safety without transferring their conservatism to the learned policy, thus enabling higher speeds.
- **Enabling Computationally Efficient Planner Guidance for RL:** Traditional model-based optimization is often computationally intensive and not suitable for the massive parallelism required in modern RL training environments. A key objective is to develop a method to incorporate real-time, explicit stability guidance from a planner into the RL training loop with negligible computational overhead.
- **Addressing Action Distribution Discrepancies Across Speeds:** A single learned policy often struggles to efficiently track a wide range of commanded speeds due to distinct action distributions required for low-speed precision versus high-speed dynamics. The goal is to create a policy that performs robustly across the entire commanded-speed spectrum.
- **Ensuring Smooth Transitions Between Speed Regimes:** When separate "expert" policies are trained for different speed ranges, directly switching between them can cause control discontinuities and instability during deployment. A motivation is to create a unified policy that allows for smooth, stable transitions across different commanded velocities.
- **Demonstrating Real-World High-Performance:** Ultimately, the project aims to deploy the developed controller on a physical humanoid robot, demonstrating robust, high-speed, perception-enabled stair climbing in challenging real-world environments, as evidenced by the Canton Tower Robot Run Up Competition.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

FastStair proposes a novel **planner-guided, multi-stage learning framework** to achieve fast and stable stair ascent. The approach synergistically combines model-based planning with model-free reinforcement learning, specifically designed to address the aforementioned challenges. The framework is structured into three distinct training stages: Pre-training, Post-training, and LoRA Fine-tuning.

**A. Parallel Foothold Planning (Integrated into Pre-training)**

The first key methodological innovation is the integration of a parallel, model-based foothold planner into the RL training loop.
1. **DCM for Stair Climbing:** The framework adapts the Divergent Component of Motion (DCM) model, based on the Variable-Height Inverted Pendulum (VHIP) dynamics, to account for stair climbing. This allows for analytical expressions and simpler constraint formulations compared to ALIP-based MPC methods, making it suitable for optimization.
2. **DCM-based Foothold Optimization:** A cost function (Eq. 2) is formulated to minimize deviations from nominal step targets and DCM offset, while penalizing terrain steepness. The decision variable is the candidate foothold location `u_T`. Crucially, the feasible foothold set `P` is derived from a local elevation map.
3. **Steepness Score Calculation:** Terrain steepness `S(u_T)` is computed using a GPU-parallelized Sobel operator on the elevation map, followed by a local neighborhood aggregation matching foot dimensions. This identifies suitable contact patches.
4. **Optimization-to-Search Reformulation:** To overcome the computational bottleneck of solving large-scale optimization problems in real-time during parallel RL training, the authors reformulate the optimization into a *parallel discrete search*. By leveraging the equality constraint (Eq. 3), each candidate foothold from the discretized elevation map is analytically mapped to a DCM offset. The cost function is then evaluated in parallel across all candidates using vectorized tensor operations on the GPU. The optimal foothold is found via an `argmin` reduction within a constrained search window. This "optimization-as-search" paradigm achieves approximately 25 times speedup compared to recent parallel MPC implementations, ensuring negligible computational overhead.

**B. Three-Stage Reinforcement Learning Framework**

The core of FastStair is its multi-stage RL training process, built upon an actor-critic network architecture (adapted from) and a modified reward function.

1. **Observation Space:** The policy receives **proprioceptive observations** (robot states, speed commands, joint data, previous action history, gait clock signals, terrain elevation map) and **privileged observations** (ground-truth linear velocity, base height, external forces, joint accelerations/torques, and critically, the *optimal foothold from the parallel planner*).

2. **Reward Function:** The baseline reward function from is used, but significantly modified by:
 - Introducing a **foothold-tracking reward** (`r_foot = exp(-10 ||p_f - p_d_f||)`): This reward explicitly guides the swing foot (`p_f`) towards a desired instantaneous position (`p_d_f`), which is interpolated from the optimal planar foothold `u_T` provided by the parallel planner. This is the mechanism by which the model-based guidance influences RL.
 - Adjusting reward scalars across the three stages (Table I) to prioritize different aspects of locomotion.

3. **Pre-training Stage (Stage 1):**
 - **Objective:** To imbue the policy with fundamental stability and safety, leveraging the explicit geometric guidance of the planner.
 - **Approach:** The **foothold-tracking reward** is assigned a dominant weight (0.6). This forces the policy to prioritize targeting safe contact regions (e.g., stair tread centers) over risky edge placements, effectively encoding the geometric intuition of the model-based planner.
 - **Outcome:** A "safety-focused base policy" capable of stable traversal, but likely with increased speed-tracking error due to the strong emphasis on foothold accuracy.

4. **Post-training Stage (Stage 2):**
 - **Objective:** To recover velocity-tracking precision and achieve higher climbing speeds, mitigating the conservatism from pre-training.
 - **Approach:** The reward weights are adjusted, significantly increasing the weight of the **speed-tracking term** and reducing the foothold-tracking weight. Crucially, the command space is partitioned into two distinct regimes: a high-velocity band ([0.8, 1.6] m/s) and a low-velocity band ([-0.3, 0.8] m/s). Two separate "expert" policies are trained, initialized from the pre-trained base policy, each specializing in one of these regimes. This "divide-and-conquer" strategy prevents mode collapse observed when trying to train a single policy over the full range.
 - **Outcome:** Two speed-specialized policies that perform well in their respective velocity ranges but are discrete.

5. **LoRA Fine-tuning Stage (Stage 3):**
 - **Objective:** To create a unified policy that transitions smoothly and robustly across the entire speed spectrum, addressing control discontinuities from direct expert switching.
 - **Approach:** The parameters of both high-speed and low-speed experts are integrated into a single network using **Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) layers**. LoRA layers are applied to branches within the network. This integrated model is then fine-tuned over the *full commanded speed range* ([-0.3, 1.6] m/s). A rule-based switcher, driven by the user command, activates the corresponding LoRA branch (e.g., high-speed expert for `v_d_x > 0.8 m/s`).
 - **Outcome:** A single, unified policy that operates robustly across the full velocity range, ensuring stable and smooth transitions.

**C. Elevation Maps Reconstruction:**
For terrain awareness, the policy relies on egocentric local elevation maps. In simulation, ground-truth heights are used. For physical deployment, an Intel RealSense D435i depth camera and a real-time perception pipeline reconstruct a local terrain grid (1.8m x 1.2m at 5cm resolution).

**D. Robot Platform and Deployment:**
All experiments are conducted on the **LimX Oli humanoid robot** (55 kg, 1.65 m, 31 DoF). Training uses IsaacLab with 4096 parallel environments and extensive domain randomization (mass, inertia, friction, PD gains, external perturbations) to bridge the sim-to-real gap. Onboard computing for deployment utilizes an NVIDIA Jetson Orin NX and a Rockchip RK3588-based SBC for decoupled perception and control workloads.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental results demonstrate the superior performance and robustness of the FastStair framework, both in simulation and on a physical humanoid robot.

- **Computational Efficiency of Parallel Foothold Planning:** The "optimization-to-search" reformulation of the DCM-based planner proved highly efficient. It achieved a latency of approximately **4 ms per step for a batch of 4096 environments** on an NVIDIA RTX 4090. This represents an **approximate 25x speedup** compared to recent parallel MPC implementations, confirming its suitability for integration into large-scale RL.

- **Foothold Tracking:**
 - The pre-trained policy (Stage 1) exhibited lower foothold-tracking error compared to the final unified policy. This indicates that the explicit foothold-tracking reward successfully guided the policy to prioritize safe, precise foot placement, leveraging the planner's geometric intuition (Fig. 6).
 - The final unified policy, while slightly less accurate in foothold tracking, achieved better velocity tracking, demonstrating the intended trade-off where initial safety guidance is later refined for agility.

- **Full-Range Velocity-Tracking (Simulation):**
 - In MuJoCo simulations, the unified policy effectively tracked velocity commands across its full operational range, from 0 m/s (stationary) up to 1.6 m/s, maintaining stability during both stair ascent and descent (Fig. 7). This validates the multi-stage training framework's ability to produce a versatile policy.

- **Training Framework Evaluation (Simulation Success Ratio):**
 - Evaluated on a 25 cm step height staircase with speeds from 0.5 to 2.0 m/s (including out-of-distribution speeds).
 - **FastStair (Ours) achieved the highest success rate** across all velocities, consistently outperforming the Baseline, Pre-trained (ablation), and AMP methods (Fig. 8). Notably, it maintained over 70% success even at 2.0 m/s, demonstrating strong generalization.
 - The Pre-trained policy significantly outperformed baselines, highlighting the value of parallel foothold planning in enhancing stability and traversal success.
 - The AMP method, while performing adequately at lower speeds (≤ 1.0 m/s), degraded sharply at higher speeds, often stalling, attributed to a lack of high-speed climbing data in its reference and conservative reward structure.

- **Training Framework Evaluation (Velocity-Tracking Mean Absolute Error - MAE):**
 - FastStair achieved the **lowest MAE (approximately 0.5 m/s)** in velocity tracking on stairs with 25 cm step height, demonstrating its superior fidelity across uniformly sampled commands (Fig. 9).
 - The Pre-trained policy had a slightly higher MAE than the Baseline, which is consistent with its design intent to prioritize foothold accuracy (safety) over velocity tracking during the initial stage. This further justifies the need for subsequent stages to recover speed performance.
 - The AMP method yielded the largest MAE, primarily due to its stagnation behavior at high-velocity commands.

- **Real-World Deployment on LimX Oli:**
 - The final unified policy was successfully deployed on the full-scale LimX Oli humanoid robot, running at 100 Hz on onboard computers without additional fine-tuning (Fig. 1, 10).
 - The robot performed **stable stair ascent and descent across the full velocity range (0 to 1.5 m/s)** on outdoor staircases (15 cm step height).
 - At high speeds, the robot exhibited an **emergent step-skipping behavior**, clearing two steps in a single stride, indicating highly dynamic and agile locomotion.
 - **Champion Performance in Canton Tower Robot Run Up Competition:** The most significant real-world validation was the robot's success in the Canton Tower Robot Run Up Competition. It **negotiated a 33-step spiral staircase (17 cm rise per step) in just 12 seconds, reaching a peak velocity of 1.65 m/s**.
 - Further real-world tests included continuous negotiation of steeper staircases (20 cm step height) and dynamic turning maneuvers between flight segments, all completed successfully.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The FastStair framework represents a significant advancement in humanoid locomotion, with the potential to profoundly impact the capabilities and real-world deployment of bipedal robots.

- **Breakthrough in Agility-Stability Trade-off:** The most significant contribution is the successful reconciliation of high agility and strict stability for humanoid robots on challenging stairs. This addresses a long-standing, fundamental problem in legged robotics, pushing the boundaries of what these robots can achieve in complex, human-centric environments. The demonstrated speeds up to 1.65 m/s on stairs are exceptionally high for full-scale humanoids and represent a new state-of-the-art.

- **Novel Multi-Stage Learning Framework:** The proposed three-stage learning framework is a novel and effective paradigm for combining the complementary strengths of model-based planning and model-free reinforcement learning. It intelligently uses explicit safety priors for initial guidance, then specializes policies for performance, and finally unifies them to ensure seamless operation. This multi-stage approach, particularly the use of LoRA for expert merging, offers a powerful blueprint for developing versatile and robust controllers for other complex robotic tasks.

- **Enabling Scalable Model-Based Guidance:** The reformulation of DCM-based foothold optimization into a GPU-parallel discrete search is a critical technical innovation. By achieving a 25x speedup, this "optimization-to-search" paradigm makes it computationally feasible to integrate explicit, real-time stability guarantees into large-scale, parallel reinforcement learning, overcoming a major bottleneck that previously limited the practical fusion of model-based and model-free methods. This could generalize to other forms of explicit priors in RL training.

- **Enhanced Real-World Capabilities:** The successful deployment on the LimX Oli robot in demanding outdoor environments, including steep and spiral staircases, and the champion win at the Canton Tower Robot Run Up Competition, unequivocally demonstrate the practical efficacy and robustness of FastStair. This moves humanoid robots significantly closer to reliable operation in unstructured, dynamic real-world settings, which is essential for applications ranging from logistics and inspection to disaster response and personal assistance.

- **Generalizable Methodology for Complex Locomotion:** The principles demonstrated by FastStair – leveraging model-based priors to guide RL, specializing policies for different operating regimes, and then unifying them for seamless operation – are highly generalizable. This methodology could be adapted to other challenging terrains (e.g., highly uneven ground, narrow beams, inclined ramps) or other complex dynamic tasks for various robotic platforms, facilitating the development of more versatile and adaptive intelligent agents.

- **Future Impact on Humanoid Robotics:** By enabling humanoids to ascend and descend stairs at speeds approaching human capabilities, FastStair contributes to making these robots truly "humanoid" in their environmental adaptability. This work paves the way for a future where bipedal robots can navigate our built environments with unprecedented agility and reliability, unlocking new applications and pushing the boundaries of autonomous robotics. The emergent behavior of step-skipping at high speeds also highlights the potential for learned policies to discover highly efficient, dynamic gaits that might be difficult to engineer manually.

In conclusion, FastStair not only provides a solution for a specific hard problem in robotics but also introduces a robust and scalable framework for integrating different control paradigms, setting a new benchmark for high-speed, stable, and perception-enabled stair climbing in humanoid robots.
