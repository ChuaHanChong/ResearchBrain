# Uncertainty-Based Smooth Policy Regularisation for Reinforcement Learning with Few Demonstrations

> [!summary] Summary
> SPReD (Smooth Policy Regularisation from Demonstrations) is a framework that integrates demonstrations into reinforcement learning by using an ensemble of Q-networks to quantify uncertainty and apply a continuous, rather than binary, imitation weight to demonstration actions. This method significantly enhances sample efficiency and robustness to suboptimal demonstrations in sparse-reward robotics tasks, outperforming existing baselines by up to 14x in success rate.

## Problem

- Reinforcement learning (RL) in complex, real-world tasks often requires extensive, costly environmental interactions, especially with sparse rewards, making exploration difficult.
- Existing demonstration-based RL methods, like the Q-filter, rely on single-point Q-value estimates and make binary imitation decisions, leading to high training variance and poor performance with limited or suboptimal demonstrations.
- Designing dense reward functions is complex, while sparse rewards, despite simplicity, intensify the exploration problem, highlighting the need for robust demonstration integration.

## Method

- Utilizes an ensemble of critic networks to quantify ==epistemic uncertainty== in Q-value estimates for both demonstration and policy actions.
- Introduces a smooth, state-adaptive weight (p ∈ [0, 1]) that continuously modulates a behavior cloning loss, reflecting the statistical confidence in a demonstration's superiority over the current policy.
- Develops two complementary weighting approaches: SPReD-P (Probabilistic Advantage Weighting based on Gaussian assumptions) and SPReD-E (Exponential Advantage Weighting scaled by the Interquartile Range for robust advantage assessment).

## Results

- SPReD methods consistently achieved superior sample efficiency, with up to a **14x** improvement in success rate on complex FetchStack2 tasks using 100 demonstrations (**0.920** vs. **0.064** for RLPD).
- Demonstrated high robustness to varying demonstration quality and quantity, maintaining consistent performance with suboptimal or limited demonstrations where the Q-filter's performance sharply declined, sometimes performing worse than RL without demonstrations.
- Empirically confirmed a significant reduction in policy gradient variance and observed adaptive weighting behavior, with weights clustering around **0.5** in early high-uncertainty phases and approaching 0 for inferior actions as the policy improved.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Quantifying Q-value estimation uncertainty using ensembles allows for more informed and adaptive utilization of demonstrations, moving beyond fixed or binary imitation.
> - Continuous, rather than binary, regularisation based on this uncertainty significantly reduces policy gradient variance, leading to more stable and efficient learning in deep RL.
> - The adaptive weighting mechanism enables agents to automatically filter out misleading or suboptimal demonstrations as the policy improves, preventing negative transfer and allowing performance to potentially surpass that of the provided demonstrations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has demonstrated effectiveness in addressing complex sequential decision-making problems, particularly in domains such as robotics and games. However, training RL agents for complex tasks, especially in real-world settings, often necessitates extensive environmental interactions, which can be costly, time-consuming, and risky. The challenge is exacerbated in environments characterized by sparse rewards, where agents receive minimal feedback, making exploration difficult. While dense reward functions can provide more consistent feedback, their design often requires significant domain expertise and can become increasingly complex for intricate dynamics. Sparse rewards, despite their simplicity and reduced susceptibility to local optima, intensify the exploration problem.

To mitigate these challenges, researchers have explored the integration of pre-collected expert or suboptimal demonstrations. Early approaches in demonstration-based RL involved prioritized replay mechanisms, which can accelerate learning but often require extensive parameter tuning and exhibit limited adaptability to varying demonstration quality. Other methods utilized weighted behavior cloning (BC) with predetermined decay factors, leading to a reduction in demonstration influence over time irrespective of its ongoing utility. More recent advancements aimed to address limitations posed by suboptimal or limited demonstrations through techniques such as reduced Q-values for undemonstrated actions or variance-based guidance signals, though some were restricted to discrete action spaces. Constrained policy optimization formulations also emerged, offering soft constraints on policy exploration, but often involving considerable computational complexity.

A notable advancement in this field is the Q-filter approach, which selectively applies behavior cloning only when demonstration actions are estimated to yield higher Q-values than the current policy's actions. This method has become foundational for RL with demonstrations, particularly in continuous control tasks. However, the authors identify two primary limitations of the Q-filter: its reliance on single-point Q-value estimates that do not account for inherent estimation uncertainty, and its use of binary imitation decisions (either full imitation or none). These limitations can lead to high variance during training and become particularly problematic when demonstrations are limited or suboptimal, which is a common scenario in practical applications.

The broader research landscape also includes the quantification of uncertainty in RL, typically categorized into aleatoric (environmental randomness) and epistemic (model knowledge limitations) uncertainty. Uncertainty quantification has been applied to various aspects of RL, including exploration-exploitation trade-offs, safety constraints, and offline learning. Methods for estimating uncertainty range from bootstrapping and ensemble techniques to MC-dropout. While some online RL approaches incorporate uncertainty with demonstrations, many still make binary decisions regarding demonstration usage.

This work, Smooth Policy Regularisation from Demonstrations (SPReD), positions itself by directly addressing the aforementioned limitations of the Q-filter and the broader gap in applying continuous, uncertainty-aware regularisation in demonstration-based RL. It reframes demonstration utilization as a distributional comparison problem, aiming to provide a more stable and adaptive learning framework, especially in scenarios with few or imperfect demonstrations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to address the fundamental question: "When should an agent imitate a demonstration versus follow its own policy?" This question is particularly pertinent in reinforcement learning environments with sparse rewards, where demonstrations can accelerate learning but their effective integration remains challenging.

The motivation for SPReD stems from identified limitations in existing demonstration utilization methods, notably the Q-filter approach. While the Q-filter introduced a mechanism for selectively leveraging demonstrations, the authors highlight two critical shortcomings:
1. **Neglect of Estimation Uncertainty**: The Q-filter relies on single point estimates of Q-values when comparing demonstration actions to policy actions. This approach does not account for the inherent uncertainty in these Q-value estimates, which can be substantial, especially in the early stages of learning or with limited data. This lack of uncertainty awareness can lead to unreliable decisions regarding demonstration utility.
2. **Binary Imitation Decisions**: The Q-filter makes a binary choice: either fully imitate a demonstration action (if its Q-value is higher) or entirely ignore it. This hard, discontinuous decision-making process introduces high variance into the policy gradients during training. Such discontinuities can destabilize learning, particularly as the policy approaches optimality, where nuanced guidance from demonstrations would be most beneficial.

These limitations become more pronounced and problematic in practical scenarios involving limited or suboptimal demonstrations, where the Q-value estimates might be less reliable or the demonstrated actions themselves are not consistently optimal.

To overcome these issues, the key objectives of SPReD are:
- To explicitly model Q-value distributions for both demonstration and policy actions, thereby quantifying the associated uncertainty.
- To develop uncertainty-aware methods that allow for a continuous, rather than binary, application of regularisation weights, reflecting the statistical confidence in a demonstration's superiority.
- To reduce gradient variance during training, leading to more stable and efficient policy learning.
- To enable robust learning that adapts systematically to varying levels of demonstration quality and quantity, allowing agents to filter out misleading information and potentially surpass the performance of provided demonstrations.

By achieving these objectives, SPReD aims to provide a more principled and effective framework for integrating demonstrations into RL, particularly for complex robotic tasks with sparse rewards.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

SPReD (Smooth Policy Regularisation from Demonstrations) is a framework designed to enhance reinforcement learning by addressing the limitations of binary demonstration utilization, particularly for continuous control tasks with sparse rewards. The core approach reformulates demonstration-based regularisation as a distributional comparison problem, explicitly accounting for uncertainty in Q-value estimates.

**Ensemble-Based Q-Value Modeling:**
The central tenet of SPReD is the use of an ensemble of *m* independent critic networks. For each state-action pair in the demonstration buffer, this ensemble provides *m* distinct Q-value estimates for two types of actions:
1. The demonstration action, *a_d*: {Q_i(s_d, a_d)} for *i* ∈ [1, *m*].
2. The current policy's action, *π_ϕ(s_d)*: {Q_i(s_d, *π_ϕ(s_d)*)} for *i* ∈ [1, *m*].
The variability across these ensemble estimates quantifies the epistemic uncertainty in the value approximations. The specific uncertainty measure used is the empirical mean and variance across the ensemble, assuming a Gaussian distribution for SPReD-P, or the interquartile range (IQR) for SPReD-E.

**Smooth Policy Regularisation Mechanism:**
Instead of making binary decisions to accept or reject a demonstration, SPReD introduces a state-adaptive weight *p* ∈. This weight continuously modulates a behavior cloning (BC) loss, with its value reflecting the statistical confidence that a demonstration action outperforms the current policy action. The weighted BC loss is formulated as:
*L_WBC* = E_((s_d, a_d) ~ B_D) [*p*(s_d, a_d) ||*π_ϕ(s_d)* - *a_d*||²]
This *L_WBC* term is then integrated into the actor's objective function in the TD3 framework:
*L(ϕ)* = -*λ₁* E_((s,a) ~ B) [*Q_θ*(s, *π_ϕ(s)*)] + *λ₂* *L_WBC*
Where *λ₁* and *λ₂* are hyperparameters balancing policy improvement and demonstration imitation, and *Q_θ*(s, *π_ϕ(s)*) represents the ensemble mean Q-value for the policy action. This continuous weighting mechanism provides a smoother regularisation effect that adapts to the quality of demonstrations and the uncertainty in value estimates.

**Two Complementary Weighting Approaches:**

1. **SPReD-P: Probabilistic Advantage Weighting**
 - **Assumption**: Q-value estimates from the ensemble are modeled as Gaussian distributions. The mean and variance of these distributions are empirically estimated from the ensemble members for both demonstration and policy actions.
 - **Weight Calculation**: The weight *p_P* is computed as the probability that the Q-value of the demonstration action is greater than the Q-value of the current policy action:
 *p_P* = P(*Q*(s_d, a_d) > *Q*(s_d, *π_ϕ(s_d)*)) = *Φ* ( ( *Q̂*(s_d, a_d) - *Q̂*(s_d, *π_ϕ(s_d)*)) / √(*σ̂*_d² + *σ̂*²))
 where *Φ* is the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal distribution, *Q̂* denotes the empirical mean, and *σ̂²* denotes the empirical variance of the ensemble's Q-value estimates.
 - **Adaptive Behavior**: This formulation allows *p_P* to vary continuously. When Q-value estimates have high variance (early in training), *p_P* tends towards 0.5, allowing partial learning. As uncertainty decreases, *p_P* becomes more decisive, approaching 0 or 1.

2. **SPReD-E: Exponential Advantage Weighting**
 - **Focus**: This method scales imitation strength based on the magnitude of the advantage, relative to estimation uncertainty, without requiring an explicit Gaussian assumption.
 - **Advantage Measure**: The advantage *A* is calculated as the difference between the mean Q-values of the demonstration action and the current policy action, derived from the ensemble:
 *A* = E_i∈[m] [*Q_i*(s_d, a_d)] - E_i∈[m] [*Q_i*(s_d, *π(s_d)*)]
 - **Weight Calculation**: The advantage is transformed into a weight using an exponential function, inspired by advantage-weighted behavioral cloning:
 *p_E* = clip(*e^(A/β)* - 1, 0, 1)
 where *β* is a scaling factor that controls sensitivity to advantage magnitude. *β* is set as a proportion (*α*) of the interquartile range (IQR) of the mixture Q-value distribution (comprising both demonstration and policy Q-value distributions). This makes *β* state-adaptive and robust to outliers, ensuring *p_E* = 0 for inferior demonstrations, proportional weighting for uncertain cases, and strong imitation for clearly superior ones.

**Integration with TD3 and HER:**
SPReD is implemented with the off-policy actor-critic algorithm TD3, suitable for continuous state and action spaces. For sparse-reward, goal-conditioned tasks, Hindsight Experience Replay (HER) is employed to improve exploration by relabelling unsuccessful episodes with achieved goals. The ensemble critics are also used for target value computation, following the REDQ approach, to mitigate overestimation bias.

**Computational Considerations:**
Despite using ensembles, SPReD aims for computational efficiency. The ensemble computations are stacked into batched tensor operations, allowing parallel execution on GPUs. The actor update occurs less frequently than critic updates to allow Q-value estimates to stabilize. The computational overhead is reported to be minimal, with throughput comparable to standard TD3.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates SPReD across eight challenging robotics tasks from OpenAI Gym (Fetch and Shadow Dexterous Hand environments), which feature sparse binary rewards and complex multi-goal structures. Additional experiments are conducted on locomotion tasks with dense rewards. The evaluation metrics primarily include success rates and learning curves, demonstrating sample efficiency and asymptotic performance.

**Performance Against Baselines:**
- **Superior Sample Efficiency**: SPReD-P and SPReD-E consistently outperformed all evaluated baselines, including TD3 (without demonstrations), EnsTD3 (ensemble TD3 without demonstrations), Q-filter, EnsQ-filter, RLPD, and three variants of AWAC. This outperformance was evident across all tasks, particularly in complex manipulation tasks.
- **Significant Gains in Complex Tasks**: In tasks like FetchStack2, SPReD methods achieved up to a 14x improvement in success rate (0.920 vs. 0.064 for RLPD) after 1 million interactions (10 million for stacking tasks), even with only 100 demonstrations. In FetchSlide, SPReD-E achieved a 50% higher success rate (0.240) than the next best method (0.160).
- **Asymptotic Performance**: Beyond initial sample efficiency, SPReD methods consistently matched or exceeded the asymptotic performance of all baselines across the environments.
- **AWAC Limitations**: AWAC variants struggled significantly in this setting, which is attributed to its design for large offline datasets for pretraining, a condition not met with the few demonstrations available in these experiments.

**Impact of Demonstration Quality:**
- **Robustness to Suboptimal Demonstrations**: SPReD variants maintained consistent high performance across varying demonstration quality levels (expert, suboptimal, severely suboptimal) in tasks like FetchPickAndPlace and FetchPush.
- **Q-filter Sensitivity**: The performance of the standard Q-filter dropped sharply with decreasing demonstration quality. In an extreme case (FetchPickAndPlace with 1 expert and 99 random trajectories), Q-filter performed worse than TD3 without any demonstrations, suggesting it amplified misleading information.
- **Adaptive Weighting**: SPReD's adaptive, continuous weighting mechanism automatically reduced the influence of inferior demonstration actions as the policy improved and uncertainty decreased, allowing it to filter out misleading information without explicit quality estimation.

**Impact of Demonstration Sample Size:**
- **Effectiveness with Limited Data**: SPReD methods demonstrated superior performance even with extremely limited data (e.g., 10 demonstration episodes).
- **Trade-off between Variants**: SPReD-P exhibited greater robustness to smaller sample sizes, while SPReD-E achieved better asymptotic performance when provided with larger or higher-quality demonstration sets.

**Theoretical Property Validation:**
- **Gradient Variance Reduction**: Empirical results (Figure 7) confirmed Lemma 5.1, showing that SPReD's smooth weighting significantly reduced the gradient variance of actor updates compared to binary imitation decisions, contributing to training stability.
- **Adaptive Behavior and Diminishing Weights**: Observations of the weighting mechanisms (Figure 8) demonstrated SPReD's ability to adapt to uncertainty (weights clustering around 0.5 in early, high-uncertainty phases) and to autonomously down-weight suboptimal demonstrations as the policy improved (weights approaching 0 for inferior actions), aligning with Properties 5.1 and 5.2.
- **Gaussian Assumption Validity**: Empirical comparisons (Figure 6) indicated that the Gaussian approximation used by SPReD-P outperformed nonparametric alternatives, validating the modeling choice.

**Computational Efficiency:**
- SPReD maintained computational efficiency comparable to standard TD3. Despite using 5x more critics (10 critics vs. 2 for TD3), SPReD incurred only a 3.8% decrease in throughput. In contrast, RLPD, another ensemble-based method, required approximately twice the computation time.

**Robustness to Noisy Rewards:**
- SPReD maintained its leading performance even in environments with noisy rewards, where methods like the Q-filter failed to learn effectively due to disturbed Q-value estimates.

**Ablation Studies:**
- **Ensemble Size**: An ensemble size of 10 critics provided a favorable trade-off between performance and computational cost, with diminishing returns beyond this point for most tasks. More complex tasks showed greater benefit from larger ensembles.
- **Isolated Contributions**: Continuous regularisation was identified as the primary driver of performance improvement, even with a minimal ensemble. Ensembles provided complementary value, especially crucial for SPReD-E's exponential scaling to accurately estimate uncertainty.
- **Normalization Constant (α) for SPReD-E**: The IQR-based *β* parameter choice for SPReD-E demonstrated better empirical performance than a theoretically derived *σ√2π* variant, particularly for suboptimal demonstrations, attributed to IQR's robustness to outliers.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of SPReD represents a notable advancement in the field of reinforcement learning from demonstrations, particularly for scenarios characterized by sparse rewards and limited or suboptimal expert data.

**Addressing a Core Challenge:** SPReD addresses a fundamental problem in demonstration-based RL: how to effectively determine when and to what extent an agent should imitate demonstrated actions versus exploring its own policy. By moving beyond binary imitation decisions, the framework offers a more nuanced and continuous approach.

**Enhanced Sample Efficiency:** The consistent and substantial performance gains, exemplified by up to 14x success rates in complex manipulation tasks, underscore SPReD's ability to significantly improve sample efficiency. This is critical for real-world robotic applications where interaction time is costly and potentially hazardous.

**Robustness to Data Quality and Quantity:** A key strength of SPReD is its robustness to varying demonstration quality and quantity. The adaptive weighting mechanisms enable the agent to extract valuable information even from imperfect demonstrations and to autonomously diminish the influence of suboptimal actions as its own policy improves. This reduces the stringent requirement for perfectly expert and abundant demonstrations, making RL from demonstrations more practical and accessible.

**Improved Training Stability:** The theoretical and empirical validation of reduced policy gradient variance offered by continuous, uncertainty-aware weighting contributes to more stable and reliable training processes. This is particularly important for deep RL, where high variance can hinder convergence and overall performance.

**Theoretical Foundation:** The framework is supported by theoretical properties that explain its adaptive behavior under varying uncertainty levels, its ability to filter out suboptimal demonstrations, and the connection between its probabilistic and exponential weighting schemes. These theoretical insights strengthen the understanding of why continuous regularisation is beneficial.

**Broad Applicability:** The simplicity and off-policy nature of SPReD's integration with TD3 suggest its potential applicability to a broader range of off-policy RL algorithms. This could lead to widespread improvements in various continuous control domains.

**Practical Implications for Robotics:** The positive results in challenging robotics tasks (Fetch and Shadow Dexterous Hand) highlight SPReD's direct relevance to real-world robotic control, potentially accelerating automation across industries, improving robot learning in complex manipulation scenarios, and reducing the need for extensive manual reward engineering or expert supervision.

**Limitations and Future Work:** The paper acknowledges several limitations, including the need for future research into automatic balancing between demonstration guidance and exploration throughout training, as demonstration influence can sometimes impede later-stage learning in highly dexterous tasks. Hyperparameter sensitivity, sample complexity in extremely high-dimensional tasks, theoretical convergence properties, and addressing the sim-to-real gap also remain areas for continued refinement. Addressing these aspects would further solidify the foundation and applicability of demonstration-based RL methods like SPReD.
