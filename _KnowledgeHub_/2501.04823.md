# Learning Robot Safety from Sparse Human Feedback using Conformal Prediction

> [!summary] Summary
> Stanford University researchers developed a sample-efficient framework that learns subjective robot safety from sparse human feedback, leveraging full conformal prediction to identify regions of unsafe states with statistical guarantees on future error coverage. This approach reduced collision rates from 52% to 14% in simulated quadcopter navigation and enabled more cautious behavior in hardware experiments.

## Problem

- Formalizing robot safety constraints is challenging due to the complexity and uncertainty of real-world environments.
- Identifying unsafe behaviors in complex, data-driven policies like visuomotor control is often non-trivial.
- Safety can be a subjective preference, making it difficult to define universal, objective safety metrics.

## Method

- A framework using ==full conformal prediction== with a ==nearest neighbor score function== identifies a region of ==suspected unsafe states (SUS)== from a small dataset of human-flagged unsafe robot trajectories.
- The score function is extended to leverage safe trajectories, enhancing the discrimination between safe and unsafe states.
- The learned SUS region forms the basis for a ==warning system== and a policy modification strategy, such as a ==backup safety mode== in Model Predictive Control (MPC).

## Results

- The developed warning system successfully maintained theoretical miss rate guarantees while minimizing false alarms, especially when utilizing both safe and unsafe trajectory data.
- Integrating the warning system with a backup safety mode reduced empirical collision rates from **52%** to **14%** for an MPC quadcopter policy in simulation.
- Hardware flight tests on a quadcopter demonstrated that the modified policy adopted more cautious behaviors consistent with human-flagged unsafe trajectories from simulation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Full conformal prediction offers finite-sample, distribution-free statistical guarantees on the coverage of future unsafe states, effectively handling data scarcity.
> - Sparse human feedback, specifically trajectory terminations for unsafe events, is sufficient for learning subjective safety preferences without complex annotations.
> - The method can be enhanced by learned state representations (e.g., PCA, KPCA) for better discrimination in high-dimensional or visual data.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Ensuring robot safety is a fundamental challenge in the development and deployment of autonomous systems. Traditional methods often rely on mathematically formalizing safety constraints, which can be difficult or impossible in complex, uncertain environments (e.g., surgical robots interacting with deformable tissue), with intricate policies (e.g., visuomotor policies from images), or when safety is subjective (e.g., user tolerance for quadcopter speed). This difficulty highlights a need for data-driven approaches to learn safety.

Existing research in learning robot safety from data can be broadly categorized into several approaches:
- **Learning from Expert Demonstrations:** These methods infer constraints from optimal, safe demonstrations, assuming access to a reward function or deriving constraints from optimality conditions. This work differs by not requiring optimal demonstrations or known reward functions and by offering guaranteed coverage of unsafe states without parametric or distributional assumptions.
- **Learning from Binary Feedback:** Some approaches query a binary safety function during reinforcement learning to fit a safety-constrained policy or a safety critic. Others pretrain a safety critic offline and learn a recovery policy. This paper distinguishes itself by focusing on sample efficiency with very few unsafe samples (e.g., 25 rather than hundreds) from offline batch data, aiming for significant safety improvement in a single iteration, and relying on sparse human feedback rather than extensive online interaction.
- **Learning from Human Interaction:** These methods involve humans providing interventions, corrections, or outlining safe boundaries. While this work also uses human feedback, it simplifies the human's task to merely terminating trajectories when they are deemed unsafe, a less demanding interaction than direct control or detailed interventions.

The paper also situates itself within the growing application of **conformal prediction** in robotics. Conformal prediction is a distribution-free statistical method used to quantify uncertainty and improve safety. Previous applications include anomaly detection (calibrating false alarm rates or miss rates) and collision avoidance (producing uncertainty sets for predicted motion). This work leverages conformal prediction for anomaly detection in a novel way, learning a region of suspected unsafe states directly from observed safe and unsafe states, rather than calibrating a predefined safety function. Critically, it employs *full conformal prediction* to avoid the need for a separate calibration dataset, which is beneficial when unsafe data points are scarce. Furthermore, it extracts the first unsafe state encountered in a trajectory to provide trajectory-level error probability guarantees.

In summary, this research addresses the limitations of predefined safety constraints by proposing a sample-efficient, data-driven method for learning subjective robot safety from sparse human feedback, using novel theoretical developments in conformal prediction. It aims to provide formal statistical guarantees on future error coverage, which is a distinguishing feature compared to many existing learning-based safety approaches.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this work stems from the inherent difficulties in formalizing robot safety constraints:
- **Environmental Complexity and Uncertainty:** Many robotic environments are too complex or uncertain to be accurately modeled, making it challenging to mathematically define all possible unsafe scenarios. Examples include a surgical robot's interaction with deformable tissues or a quadcopter navigating in a dynamic, partially known space.
- **Policy Complexity:** Modern robot policies, especially those derived from deep learning (e.g., visuomotor control policies), can be highly complex. It is often non-trivial to identify what specific inputs or internal states might lead to unsafe behavior.
- **Subjectivity of Safety:** For many human-robot interaction scenarios, safety is not an absolute, objective metric but rather a subjective preference that varies among users or contexts. For instance, different users might have different tolerances for a robot's speed or proximity.

Given these challenges, the core objective of this research is to **learn about robot safety directly from human-supplied feedback**, rather than relying solely on mathematical formalisms. Specifically, the authors aim to:

1. **Develop a Sample-Efficient Method for Identifying Unsafe Regions:** From a limited number of human-flagged unsafe robot trajectories (and potentially safe ones), identify a region of states, possibly in a learned latent space, that is likely to contain future policy errors. The method needs to be sample-efficient because unsafe events are often rare, and human labeling can be time-consuming.
2. **Provide Statistical Guarantees on Future Error Coverage:** Ensure that the identified "suspected unsafe sublevel" (SUS) region C(ϵ) is statistically guaranteed to contain at least a user-specified fraction (1 − ϵ) of future policy errors. This guarantee should hold without strong distributional assumptions and even with few unsafe samples.
3. **Create a Calibrated Warning System:** Utilize the learned SUS region to develop a warning system that alerts users to impending unsafe behavior. This system should mimic the human's safety preferences with a guaranteed maximum miss rate (probability of failing to alert when the human would deem it unsafe).
4. **Enable Policy Improvement:** Integrate the learned safety knowledge to modify or improve the original robot policy. This involves using the warning system to trigger a backup safety mode or incorporate avoidance constraints, thereby making the robot's operation safer in practice.
5. **Generalize to Different Robot Systems and Data Types:** Demonstrate the applicability of the approach across various robot control paradigms (e.g., Model Predictive Control, visuomotor policies) and data modalities (e.g., 9D state vectors, RGB images).

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on leveraging sparse human feedback with conformal prediction for safety learning and policy modification.

#### 3.1. Problem Setting and Data Collection

The robot operates in a state space `X` with transition dynamics `T`. An unknown subset `A ⊂ X` represents truly unsafe states. A given policy `π: X → U` is executed, generating trajectories `τ`. Trajectories terminate upon reaching a goal `G`, entering `A`, or timing out. `D_τ` is the distribution over resulting trajectories. `τ(-1)` denotes the terminal state, which is in `A` if the trajectory is unsafe.

Humans observe `P` policy executions and flag `N` trajectories as unsafe by terminating them when they first become unsafe. The final state of these `N` unsafe trajectories constitutes the "error states" dataset `D = {x_1..., x_N}`. These `N` states are treated as Independent and Identically Distributed (IID) samples from an unknown policy error distribution `F`. The objective is to construct a set `C(ϵ)` that covers at least `1 − ϵ` of future error states `x_{N+1} ~ F`.

#### 3.2. Nearest Neighbor Conformal Prediction (NNCP)

The core of the approach involves using full conformal prediction with a nearest neighbor score function.

- **Score Function:** For a candidate state `x`, the score `s(D; x)` is defined as `min_{x'∈D} d(x', x)`, where `d` is any user-defined dissimilarity measure (e.g., Euclidean distance). Lower scores indicate greater similarity to known unsafe states.
- **Closed-Form Conformal Prediction (Theorem V.1):** A novel theoretical contribution enables closed-form computation of the SUS region `C(ϵ)` without reserving a separate calibration dataset (common in split conformal prediction). By reusing the `N` error states in `D`, the method calculates `N` intra-data nearest neighbor values `α_i = min_{x'∈D, x'≠x_i} d(x', x_i)`. A threshold `r` is then set as the `k`-th order statistic `α_{(k)}` of these values, where `k = ⌈(N + 1)(1 − ϵ)⌉`. The SUS region is defined as `C(ϵ) = {x | s(D; x) ≤ r}`. This guarantees `Pr[x_{N+1} ∈ C(ϵ)] ≥ 1 − ϵ` for future error states `x_{N+1} ~ F`.
- **Geometric Interpretation (Theorem V.2):** `C(ϵ)` can be equivalently described as a union of `N` sublevel sets: `C(ϵ) = ∪_{i=1}^N {x | d(x_i, x) ≤ r}`. For Euclidean distance, this implies `C(ϵ)` is a union of `N` closed balls with shared radius `r`, each centered at an error state `x_i`.
- **Overcoverage Bounds (Theorems V.3 & V.4):** The paper also provides theoretical upper bounds on the coverage, showing `Pr[x_{N+1} ∈ C(ϵ)] ≤ 1 − ϵ + 2/(N+1)` for symmetric `d` and `Pr[x_{N+1} ∈ C(ϵ)] ≤ 1 − ϵ + 1/(N+1) + 1/N` for asymmetric `d`. These bounds demonstrate the tightness of the finite-sample generalization guarantees.

#### 3.3. Unsafe-Safe Nearest Neighbor Extension

To improve discrimination between safe and unsafe data, safe trajectories are also utilized. Let `D_F` be the error states and `D_G` be states from safe trajectories. The score function is modified to `s(D_F; x) = min_{x'∈D_F} d(x', x) − min_{y'∈D_G} d(y', x)`. This "margin-style" score can be recast into the nearest neighbor form with an asymmetric dissimilarity measure `d_tilde(x', x) = d(x', x) − min_{y'∈D_G} d(y', x)`. Theorem V.1 then applies, and for squared Euclidean distance, `C(ϵ)` becomes a union of `N` polyhedra, each defined by `M` half-space constraints (where `M` is the number of safe points).

#### 3.4. Probabilistic Interpretation and Representation Learning

- **Conformal p-Value:** Beyond binary classification, a conformal p-value `ϵ*` is defined as the smallest `ϵ` for which `x ∉ C(ϵ)`. A larger `ϵ*` suggests a state `x` is more unsafe. This provides a continuous safety measure.
- **Representation Learning:** The efficacy of `d` can be enhanced by transforming the original state `x` to `ϕ(x)` using representation learning models (e.g., PCA, KPCA, autoencoders, CLIP). This allows for better discrimination of safe and unsafe data. The conformal guarantee remains valid if `ϕ` is trained independently of the error states `D`.

#### 3.5. Warning System

The offline-computed `C(ϵ)` (via `r = α_{(k)}`) is used online to augment the policy. If, during execution, the current state `x` enters `C(ϵ)` (i.e., `s(D; x) ≤ r`), an alert is triggered.
- **Trajectory-Level Guarantee (Theorem VI.1):** The single-state guarantee extends to trajectories: `Pr(τ yields no alert | τ reaches A) ≤ ϵ`. This implies `Pr(τ reaches A without warning) ≤ ϵβ`, where `β` is the probability of the original policy reaching `A`.

#### 3.6. Distribution Shift and Multiple Labelers

The paper analyzes the impact of distribution shift between data collection and deployment.
- **Coverage Loss (Theorem VIII.1):** If `x_{N+1}` is sampled from a modified distribution `F_tilde`, the coverage loss is bounded by `1 − ϵ − (N/(N+1)) * (2d_{TV}(F, F_tilde) − d_{TV}(F, F_tilde)^2)`, where `d_{TV}` is the total variation distance. This bound implies robustness to shifts that do not drastically change the error distribution.
- **Mixture Distributions (Theorem VIII.2):** For mixture distributions, the total variation distance is bounded by `1/2 ||w − w_tilde||_1`, allowing for analysis of shifts in failure mode frequencies or disagreements among multiple labelers.
- **Robustness via Transformations:** Using transformations `ϕ` can reduce `d_{TV}(F_ϕ, F_tilde_ϕ)`, potentially making `C(ϵ)` more robust to shifts.

#### 3.7. Policy Modification

The warning system is used to improve policy safety by avoiding `C(ϵ)`. This is implemented in an MPC setting.
- **Backup Safety Mode:** The original policy `π` is used. If its open-loop plan `x_{1:H}` predicts entry into `C(ϵ)` at timestep `t_w`, the robot switches to a backup policy `π_B`.
- **Steering to Safe Data:** `π_B` identifies the nearest historical safe trajectory `τ_B` from a pre-collected backup dataset `D_B` (trajectories avoiding `C(ϵ)`) and steers towards a sequence of states from `τ_B`.
- **Avoiding C(ϵ):** `π_B` incorporates constraints to avoid `C(ϵ)` by treating `C(ϵ)` (represented as a union of balls/polyhedra) as learned obstacles in its trajectory optimization.
- **Return to Original Policy:** The robot returns to `π` if it is sufficiently close to `τ_B` and `t_w` timesteps have passed, or after a maximum number of backup iterations. This approach maintains the calibration of the warning system by preventing distribution shift until an alert occurs.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents findings from both simulation and hardware experiments, demonstrating the capabilities of the proposed method for warning systems and policy modification.

#### 4.1. Warning System Experiments

- **Model Predictive Control (MPC) with Unknown Obstacles (Simulation):**
 - An MPC policy was tasked with navigating a quadcopter to a goal in an environment with unknown obstacles. The original policy exhibited a 52% collision rate in demonstrations.
 - Using `N=25` unsafe samples, the warning system (with `ϵ=0.2`) was implemented. It successfully detected impending collisions, resulting in 0% collisions during testing when an alert mechanism was active.
 - **Performance Comparison (Figure 6):** Three variants of the nearest neighbor conformal approach were compared: `Unsafe-Safe` (using both safe and unsafe data), `Unsafe` (only unsafe data), and `-Safe` (only safe data). All variants maintained the theoretical miss rate guarantee (`Pr(no alert | unsafe) ≤ ϵβ`). The `Unsafe-Safe` approach consistently yielded fewer false alarms (higher `Pr(no warning | safe)`) across varying `ϵ`, demonstrating better discrimination.
 - **Comparison with Machine Learning Baselines (Figure 7):** Random Forest (RF), Support Vector Machine (SVM), and Neural Network (NN) classifiers (with and without class balancing) were evaluated. While the best-performing RF with class balancing showed similar performance to the proposed method, the Nearest Neighbor Conformal Prediction (NNCP) approach offered advantages: no parameter tuning, explicit geometric interpretability of `C(ϵ)`, and the ability to use all `N` unsafe samples for calibration (unlike split conformal prediction baselines, which require holding out data).
- **Visuomotor Quadcopter Policy (Simulation):**
 - A visuomotor policy, trained via behavior cloning in a Gaussian Splat simulator, was tasked with flying a quadcopter through a gate. Human labelers flagged 45 out of 200 trajectories as unsafe.
 - The warning system was applied to image data, leveraging learned representations (PCA, KPCA, autoencoder, CLIP). All representation learning methods preserved the miss rate guarantee (Theorem VI.1).
 - **Performance Comparison (Figure 9):** PCA and KPCA, simpler linear and kernel methods, outperformed the autoencoder and pre-trained CLIP in reducing false alarms with the limited available data. This suggests that for small datasets, simpler, task-specific representations can be more effective.
 - **Runtime Safety Monitoring:** The conformal p-value provided a continuous, interpretable safety score, allowing for runtime monitoring where increasing p-values indicated approaching unsafe conditions (Figure 10).

#### 4.2. Distribution Shift Analysis

- **Empirical Verification (Figure 11):** Experiments showed that `C(ϵ)` remained robust to changes in the relative frequencies of different failure modes (e.g., collisions with different obstacles), provided these modes were represented in the initial training data `D`. If a failure mode was entirely absent from the training data, the miss rate increased significantly, consistent with theoretical bounds.

#### 4.3. Policy Modification Experiments

- **Simulation Experiments:**
 - The MPC policy with unknown obstacles (52% collision rate) was modified using the backup safety mode. This reduced the empirical collision rate to 14%. The backup policy successfully steered the quadcopter around obstacles upon alert.
 - **Comparison with Alternatives (Figures 12 & 13):**
 - `Constrained MPC` (directly avoiding `C(ϵ)`) resulted in a 32% collision rate.
 - `Penalized MPC` (allowing cost-penalized entry into `C(ϵ)`) resulted in a 30% collision rate.
 - `Critic Recovery` (an offline pretraining approach from prior work) resulted in a 50% collision rate, suggesting potential issues with out-of-distribution queries in a limited-data setting.
 - The proposed `Backup MPC` consistently achieved a lower error rate across varying `ϵ` values, approximately matching the heuristic bound of `ϵβ`. This indicated its ability to reduce actual policy errors by effectively utilizing the warning system and historical safe data.
- **Hardware Experiments:**
 - An initial obstacle-aware MPC policy was further refined using human video feedback collected in a Gaussian Splat simulator (52 unsafe trajectories from 100). The learned `C(ϵ)` covered main obstacles (table, ladder, gate) and reflected preemptive human labeling (Figure 14).
 - **Flight Tests:** 30 hardware flight tests across 6 start-goal configurations demonstrated that the modified policy navigated more cautiously, avoiding unsafe behaviors flagged by the human (e.g., flying too low over the table, going through the ladder).
 - **Sim-to-Real Gap:** While the modified policy generally kept p-values below the `ϵ` threshold, occasional violations were observed in hardware, attributed to the sim-to-real gap (e.g., unmodeled dynamics, state estimation errors). Nevertheless, the trajectories still avoided severe unsafe events.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework for learning robot safety that addresses critical challenges in deploying autonomous systems, particularly in contexts where safety is subjective, difficult to formalize, or involves rare failure events.

The key significance and potential impact include:

- **Statistically Guaranteed Safety:** The primary contribution is the development of a sample-efficient method that provides finite-sample, distribution-free statistical guarantees on the coverage of future unsafe states. This formal assurance is valuable for safety-critical applications, distinguishing it from many heuristic or empirically-validated learning-based safety approaches.
- **Sample Efficiency and Data Scarcity:** By employing novel theory for full conformal prediction with nearest neighbors, the method avoids holding out calibration data and works effectively with a small number of unsafe examples. This is crucial for situations where unsafe events are rare, and extensive data collection or human labeling is impractical.
- **Interpretability and User Control:** The resulting SUS region `C(ϵ)` offers a clear geometric interpretation (e.g., union of balls or polyhedra), which can be visualized and understood by human operators. The conformal p-value provides a calibrated, continuous measure of safety, enabling runtime monitoring and a deeper understanding of policy behavior. Users can directly control the acceptable miss rate (`ϵ`).
- **Practical Applicability:** Demonstrated in both high-fidelity simulation and real-world quadcopter hardware, the approach shows practical utility across different control paradigms (MPC, visuomotor) and state representations (numerical, image-based). The ability to improve existing policies through a backup safety mechanism makes it immediately actionable.
- **Robustness to Subjectivity and Uncertainty:** The reliance on sparse human feedback allows the system to learn subjective safety preferences that are difficult to formalize mathematically. The analysis of distribution shift offers insights into the method's robustness and how its guarantees might degrade under changing operating conditions, providing guidance for deployment.
- **Foundation for Future Safety Research:** This work lays a foundation for future advancements in robot safety, including extending to dynamic unsafe sets, enabling transferability across environments (e.g., learning body-frame safety rules), and integration with other advanced learning paradigms like reinforcement learning where the calibrated p-value could serve as a penalty. It also opens avenues for re-calibrating safety models without extensive re-collection of data.

Overall, this work advances the field of trustworthy autonomy by offering a principled and practical method for integrating human intuition about safety into robot control systems with quantifiable statistical assurances.
