# UI-Voyager: A Self-Evolving GUI Agent Learning via Failed Experience

> [!summary] Summary
> Tencent Hunyuan researchers developed UI-Voyager, a self-evolving GUI agent that learns from failed experiences through a two-stage optimization pipeline. The model achieved an 81.0% Pass@1 success rate on the AndroidWorld benchmark, exceeding human-level performance of 80.0% with a 4B parameter model.

## Problem

- Autonomous mobile GUI agents struggle with inefficient learning from failed interaction trajectories, often discarding valuable error-related data.
- Conventional Reinforcement Learning (RL) faces ambiguous credit assignment due to sparse, trajectory-level rewards in long-horizon GUI tasks, hindering effective policy optimization.
- Manual re-annotation and curation of failed trajectories is impractical and resource-intensive, limiting the scalability of training pipelines for real-world mobile deployment.

## Method

- UI-Voyager implements a two-stage self-evolving optimization pipeline: ==Multi-Round Rejection Fine-Tuning (RFT)== and ==Multi-Round Group Relative Self-Distillation (GRSD)==.
- RFT iteratively generates diverse tasks, executes them, and uses ==rejection sampling== to collect high-quality successful trajectories for supervised fine-tuning, providing a warm-start policy.
- GRSD addresses credit assignment by detecting =="fork points"== where successful and failed trajectories diverge, using successful paths to generate dense, ==step-level self-corrective training samples== for fine-tuning.

## Results

- UI-Voyager (4B model) achieved an **81.0%** Pass@1 success rate on the AndroidWorld benchmark, surpassing baseline MLLMs, including larger models, and exceeding human-level performance (**80.0%**).
- Rejection Fine-Tuning (RFT) alone improved the Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct model's Pass@1 score from **37%** to **73.2%** across four rounds, demonstrating its efficacy.
- Group Relative Self-Distillation (GRSD) further enhanced the RFT-trained model's success rate from **73.2%** to **81%**, outperforming traditional RL methods like GRPO and PPO in efficiency and final performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Learning from failed experiences by identifying divergence points between successful and unsuccessful trajectories provides precise, step-level supervision, transforming sparse rewards into actionable feedback.
> - A self-evolving pipeline, where data generation and model refinement co-evolve, can significantly reduce reliance on manual data annotation and continuously improve agent capabilities.
> - Rejection fine-tuning acts as an effective warm-start mechanism for complex agentic tasks, bootstrapping performance before more targeted self-correction methods are applied.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of autonomous Graphical User Interface (GUI) agents has garnered increasing attention, particularly with the advancements in Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs). These models enable agents to perceive, comprehend, plan, reason, and operate digital interfaces autonomously, representing a step toward general, human-like intelligent agents. Mobile interfaces constitute a significant and challenging domain for GUI agents due to their diverse screen layouts, varied interaction styles (e.g., clicks, swipes, text input), limited visual context, and dynamic state transitions. Numerous efforts have focused on integrating MLLMs into mobile phones to create powerful mobile GUI agents, leading to practical applications in various industries.

Despite progress in general GUI agents, mobile-oriented agents continue to face specific challenges. Existing methodologies often exhibit inefficient learning from failed interaction trajectories. In real-world mobile interactions, failures are common, especially in complex tasks, yet conventional training pipelines frequently underutilize this experience. Another significant issue is the ambiguous credit assignment inherent in Reinforcement Learning (RL) algorithms when dealing with sparse rewards in long-horizon GUI tasks. The typical trajectory-level rewards (simple success or failure signals) do not provide granular information about which specific step caused a task failure, thereby hindering effective policy optimization.

This work addresses these identified limitations by proposing a novel framework, UI-Voyager, designed to enhance the learning efficiency and credit assignment mechanisms for mobile GUI agents. By introducing a two-stage self-evolving optimization pipeline, UI-Voyager aims to enable continuous co-evolution of training data and model capabilities, offering a method for learning from failed experiences and transforming sparse rewards into dense, step-level supervision without requiring extensive manual data annotation. This positions UI-Voyager within the broader landscape as a contribution towards developing more robust, efficient, and self-improving mobile GUI agents.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations for developing UI-Voyager stem from two fundamental challenges observed in current autonomous mobile GUI agents:

1. **Inefficient Learning from Failed Trajectories:** During mobile interactions, particularly for complex tasks, agents frequently generate trajectories that do not lead to task completion. Conventional training approaches often discard or underutilize these "failed" experiences. This represents a significant inefficiency in data usage, as valuable information about undesirable actions or decision points is lost. The motivation here is to devise a mechanism that can extract actionable insights from these failed attempts, allowing the agent to learn from its mistakes and improve its policy more effectively. This is crucial for real-world deployment where failure is an intrinsic part of the learning process, and manual re-annotation of failed trajectories is impractical and resource-intensive.

2. **Ambiguous Credit Assignment under Sparse Rewards for Long-Horizon GUI Tasks:** Many GUI tasks involve a sequence of multiple interaction steps. In such long-horizon scenarios, the reward signal is typically sparse, often a binary indication of success or failure at the very end of the task. This sparse reward structure creates a challenge for credit assignment: if a task fails, it is difficult for the agent to identify which specific action or sequence of actions, potentially much earlier in the trajectory, was responsible for the failure. Conversely, if a task succeeds, it is equally challenging to attribute credit precisely to the most impactful actions. This ambiguity hinders stable and efficient policy optimization, as the agent receives a uniform, undifferentiated signal across all steps of a long trajectory, regardless of their individual contribution to success or failure. The motivation is to transform this coarse-grained, trajectory-level feedback into more precise, step-level supervision, thereby providing the agent with clearer signals for policy refinement.

To address these challenges, UI-Voyager aims to:
- **Establish a self-evolving learning paradigm:** Develop a framework where the agent continuously generates data, learns from it, and improves its capabilities in an autonomous loop, reducing reliance on manual data curation.
- **Leverage rejection fine-tuning:** Implement a mechanism to collect high-quality successful trajectories efficiently, thereby bootstrapping the agent's initial performance and providing a reliable starting point.
- **Introduce a novel self-distillation approach:** Design a method that can identify critical decision points (fork points) where successful and failed trajectories diverge, and use successful paths to directly correct erroneous actions in failed ones. This objective directly targets the credit assignment problem by creating dense, step-level learning signals.
- **Achieve high performance with model efficiency:** Develop an agent that can achieve state-of-the-art performance on complex mobile GUI benchmarks using a compact model architecture, demonstrating efficiency in both learning and deployment.
- **Minimize manual annotation:** Reduce the need for human supervision and data labeling, making the training process more scalable and autonomous.

These motivations collectively drive the development of UI-Voyager as a step towards more capable, adaptable, and efficient autonomous mobile GUI agents.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

UI-Voyager implements a two-stage self-evolving optimization pipeline designed to address the challenges of inefficient learning from failed trajectories and ambiguous credit assignment.

#### 3.1. Overview and Task Formulation

The interaction within GUI tasks is modeled as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP). The agent perceives visual screenshots and linguistic instructions (observations, $O$), interacts through a predefined action space ($A$)—including `click`, `swipe`, `input text`, `open app`, `navigate back`, `navigate home`, `wait`, `status`, and `answer`—and transitions between underlying states ($S$). At each step, the agent's policy ($\pi$) determines the next action based on the current instruction, observation, and historical context. Task completion is verified by a rule-based system using Android Debug Bridge (adb) commands, providing a sparse, trajectory-level reward.

#### 3.2. Agent Architecture

The UI-Voyager architecture is built upon Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct as its backbone model and operates through two iterative stages:

**Stage 1: Multi-Round Rejection Fine-Tuning (RFT)**
RFT employs a closed-loop self-evolving training pipeline to enhance both training data quality and model capabilities.

- **Trajectory Generation:** To ensure diversity and novelty in training data, a seed task generator synthesizes new tasks by perturbing parameters (e.g., temporal constraints, quantities, entities) within original task templates. GUI agents then automate the execution of these tasks within the mobile environment, creating raw interaction trajectories. This automated process facilitates high-throughput data generation without manual annotation.
- **Rejection Sampling:** Generated trajectories are filtered by a rule-based verifier. Only "successful" trajectories—those that achieve the predefined goal or pass the task-completion criteria—are retained. This filtering ensures that the collected dataset for supervised fine-tuning (SFT) consists of high-quality, correct interaction sequences.
- **Iterative Training:** In the initial iteration, various Qwen3-VL models generate trajectories, with Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct serving as the base model for SFT. In subsequent rounds, the refined model from the previous iteration becomes the agent for generating new trajectories. These new trajectories are then filtered via rejection sampling, and the resulting high-quality samples are used to fine-tune the model for the next round. Each iteration introduces new tasks to prevent overfitting and maintain novelty.

**Stage 2: Multi-Round Group Relative Self-Distillation (GRSD)**
GRSD is designed to address the credit assignment problem by converting sparse trajectory-level rewards into dense step-level supervision. This stage is applied after RFT provides a warm-start policy.

- **Key Insight:** Unlike conventional RL algorithms (e.g., GRPO, PPO) that struggle with sparse, trajectory-level rewards by assigning uniform advantage estimates, GRSD identifies "fork points" where a group of rollouts for the same task initially share the same screen states but then diverge due to different actions. A successful trajectory within this group can serve as a "teacher" to correct a failed one at these critical divergence points.
- **Fork Point Detection:** This mechanism aims to extract step-level supervision by comparing a successful trajectory ($\tau^+$) with a failed trajectory ($\tau^-$) for the same task.
 - **Cross-Trajectory State Matching:** An observation equivalence function determines if two screenshots depict the same screen state. The Structural Similarity Index (SSIM) is used for this, applied after preprocessing steps (cropping status bar, resizing, grayscale conversion). A mean-hash pre-filter quickly eliminates obviously dissimilar pairs before SSIM computation.
 - **Transition Alignment:** Before matching a failed step $j$ to a successful step $i$, a check is performed: if $o^+_i$ matches $o^-_j$ AND $o^+_{i+1}$ matches $o^-_{j+1}$, the trajectories are considered aligned, and the current failed step $j$ is skipped as uninformative for correction, advancing the minimum successful index for subsequent matches.
 - **Teacher Step Selection:** For each remaining failed step $j$, the algorithm searches for a successful step $i$ that satisfies two conditions: (1) `SAME(o+_i, o-_j)` (observation equivalence) and (2) `DIVERGE(i, j)` (subsequent observations $o^+_{i+1}$ and $o^-_{j+1}$ are not nearly identical, meaning the actions had different effects). Among valid candidates, the successful step with the highest SSIM score (breaking ties by smallest successful-step index) is chosen as the teacher. A monotonicity constraint ensures that later failed steps only match to later or same successful steps.
- **Step-Level Self Distillation:** For each identified fork point $(j, i^*(j))$, a self-corrective training sample is constructed. This sample consists of the failed trajectory's prompt and contextual history at step $j$, combined with the correct action and reasoning from the successful trajectory at step $i^*(j)$. The training objective is a standard autoregressive next-token prediction loss over these constructed samples, effectively distilling precise step-level knowledge. GRSD replaces traditional GRPO and PPO objectives in this stage, providing targeted self-correction.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of UI-Voyager was conducted on the AndroidWorld benchmark, which includes 116 diverse tasks across real-world mobile applications.

#### 4.1. Overall Performance

UI-Voyager, utilizing a 4B parameter model, achieved an 81.0% Pass@1 success rate on the AndroidWorld benchmark. This performance surpasses all reported baseline methods, including models with significantly larger parameter counts, and exceeds the reported human-level performance of 80.0% on the same benchmark. The results were averaged over 64 independent runs to ensure reproducibility.

#### 4.2. Comparative Performance

- **Superiority over Larger Models:** UI-Voyager (4B) outperformed considerably larger models such as MAI-UI-235B-A22B (76.7%), UI-Tars-2 (73.3%), and Qwen3-VL-235B-A22B (63.7%). This indicates a substantial improvement in learning efficiency relative to model size.
- **Superiority over Comparable-Sized Models:** When compared to models of similar parameter scale, UI-Voyager (4B) demonstrated a significant advantage over Step-GUI-4B (63.9%) and Qwen3-VL-4B (45.3%).

#### 4.3. Analysis of Rejection Fine-Tuning (RFT)

- **Performance Boost:** RFT demonstrated consistent and substantial improvements in agent performance. Across four iterative rounds, the Pass@1 score of the Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct model increased from 37% to 73.2%. This illustrates the efficacy of the self-evolving process in mutually enhancing data quality and model capabilities.
- **Efficiency compared to RL:** Direct application of traditional RL algorithms like GRPO and PPO, when initialized from the Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct base model, showed slow and inefficient performance improvements. These methods required approximately 175 training steps to reach a success rate of 64.0%, a level achieved by a single RFT iteration. This finding supports the role of RFT as an effective "warm-start" strategy for complex, long-horizon agentic tasks.

#### 4.4. Analysis of Fork Point Detection

Case studies on tasks like "BrowserMaze" and "SystemBluetoothTurnOff" visually demonstrated the functionality of the fork point detection mechanism.
- In the "BrowserMaze" task, a fork point was identified where both successful and failed trajectories shared an identical screen state (Step 12). The failed trajectory made an invalid "Right" action, while the successful one correctly took a "Down" action. The mechanism accurately pinpointed this divergence.
- In the "SystemBluetoothTurnOff" task, the fork point occurred at the initial state (Step 0), where both trajectories started from the same home screen. The failed trajectory used an incorrect upward swipe to open settings, while the successful one correctly swiped down to access quick settings. This example showed the detection mechanism's ability to identify divergences even at the very beginning of a task.

These visualizations indicated that fork point detection effectively identifies critical decision moments where trajectories diverge, providing a foundation for targeted corrections.

#### 4.5. Analysis of Self-Corrective Samples

The construction of self-corrective samples at identified fork points was illustrated. For instance, in the "BrowserMaze" task, at the fork point (Step 12), the failed trajectory's incorrect reasoning ("continue moving 'X' right and then down") and action ("Tap on the 'Right' button") were replaced with the successful trajectory's correct reasoning ("move 'X' down towards the bottom-right corner") and action ("Tap on the 'Down' button"). This process transforms failed experiences into high-quality supervised data, enabling the agent to learn directly from its specific errors and refine its decision-making.

#### 4.6. Comparison of GRSD with RL Methods

- **Performance Gap:** Starting from the RFT-trained model (73.2% success rate), GRSD significantly improved the success rate to 81%. In contrast, GRPO and PPO, when applied to the same starting model, exhibited slower progress and plateaued around 76%.
- **Reason for Effectiveness:** This performance disparity was attributed to GRSD's precise fork point detection and self-distillation mechanisms, which provide dense step-level supervision. Standard RL baselines, limited by the absence of effective credit assignment and mechanisms for distilling actionable insights from failed trajectories, struggled to achieve comparable gains, especially in sparse-reward environments.
- **Effectiveness on Hard Tasks:** On a selection of ten tasks where the baseline RFT model initially showed low success rates, GRSD consistently achieved the highest success rates, substantially outperforming both PPO and GRPO. This demonstrated GRSD's ability to learn efficiently from failed trajectories even in challenging scenarios with sparse rewards.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

UI-Voyager addresses two central challenges in the field of autonomous mobile GUI agents: the inefficient utilization of failed interaction trajectories and the ambiguous credit assignment under sparse rewards characteristic of long-horizon tasks.

The proposed two-stage self-evolving framework, comprising Rejection Fine-Tuning (RFT) and Group Relative Self-Distillation (GRSD), demonstrates a methodological advancement. RFT enables a continuous, autonomous co-evolution of training data and model capabilities, reducing the dependency on labor-intensive manual data annotation. This aspect has the potential to significantly lower the cost and effort associated with developing and improving GUI agents, making the training process more scalable and practical for real-world applications.

GRSD introduces a mechanism for generating dense, step-level supervision from otherwise sparse reward signals. By precisely identifying "fork points" where successful and failed trajectories diverge, the method allows successful paths to serve as teachers for correcting erroneous actions in failed ones. This approach directly tackles the credit assignment problem, providing more granular and informative feedback to the agent. The empirical results, notably achieving an 81.0% Pass@1 success rate on the AndroidWorld benchmark with a 4B model, highlight UI-Voyager's performance and efficiency. Surpassing human-level performance and outperforming larger baseline models with a more compact architecture indicates a significant step towards developing high-performing GUI agents that are also resource-efficient.

The detailed analysis, including ablation studies and case studies, validates the contributions of RFT for warm-starting policy learning and GRSD for targeted error correction. These contributions have implications for the development of more robust and intelligent autonomous systems capable of operating complex digital interfaces.

Potential future impacts and directions, as identified by the authors, include extending the UI-Voyager framework to other GUI tasks beyond AndroidWorld, exploring more adaptive reasoning and self-correction mechanisms, and enhancing the efficiency and robustness of mobile GUI agents in real-world environments. The discussion also highlights practical considerations such as the challenges of real-time execution and SSIM-based matching due to temporal misalignments and transient visual perturbations, suggesting avenues for improvement through time-aware matching or integration with other structural signals. Addressing the limitations of predefined action spaces through hierarchical action modeling could further improve transfer robustness to more granular control settings. Overall, UI-Voyager presents a foundation for developing self-improving, efficient, and highly capable GUI agents.
