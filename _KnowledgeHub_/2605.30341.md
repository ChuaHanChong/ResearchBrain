# GPIC: A Giant Permissive Image Corpus for Visual Generation

> [!summary] Summary
> GPIC, a collaborative effort from Stanford University and other institutions, introduces a permissive, stable, large, and accessible image corpus comprising 100 million images with high-quality synthetic text captions, designed as a modern benchmark for visual generative modeling. It provides a new foundation for reproducible research and evaluation using the FD-DINOv2 metric, addressing the limitations of prior benchmarks.

## Problem

- Modern visual generative models require substantially larger and more diverse datasets with rich text captions, which existing public benchmarks do not adequately provide.
- The long-standing ImageNet-1K benchmark is no longer relevant for evaluating contemporary text-conditioned generation due to metric saturation and a divergence from current modeling practices.
- Reproducibility and open scientific comparison in visual generation are hindered by the widespread reliance on proprietary or unstable datasets in state-of-the-art research.

## Method

- Constructed **GPIC** by curating 100 million images from Flickr and Wikimedia under strictly ==permissive licenses==, ensuring legal clarity for both research and commercial use.
- Developed a robust four-stage pipeline including ==VLM-based quality== and safety filtering, conservative deduplication, and high-quality synthetic captioning using Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct.
- Introduced a new benchmarking protocol utilizing FD-DINOv2, a DINOv2-feature-based Fréchet Distance, for evaluating against a held-out test set, complemented by a reproducible pixel-space flow matching baseline.

## Results

- GPIC delivers 100 million training images, 200,000 validation images, and 1 million test images, distributed in three nested scales (Nano, Lite, Full) totaling 12.9 TB of sharded data.
- A pixel-space flow matching model (JiT-T2I) trained on GPIC-Full achieved an FD-DINOv2 score of 76.25 (with CFG 6.25), establishing a transparent and reproducible baseline.
- The dataset and its associated evaluation protocol are designed to address critical limitations in current benchmarking, offering a new foundation for advancing visual generative modeling.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A modern visual generation benchmark requires permissiveness, stability, large scale with rich text captions, and accessibility to foster open and reproducible research effectively.
> - Existing evaluation metrics like ImageNet-1K FID have saturated, necessitating the development of more sensitive and human-aligned metrics like FD-DINOv2 for assessing current generative capabilities.
> - Leveraging high-quality synthetic captions generated by advanced Vision-Language Models can provide superior conditioning signals for generative models compared to potentially noisy or misaligned source metadata.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of "GPIC: A Giant Permissive Image Corpus" is situated within a rapidly evolving landscape of visual generative modeling. Modern generative models for images and videos, particularly those at the forefront of the field, have demonstrated capabilities that demand increasingly large, diverse, and well-annotated datasets. Current state-of-the-art open-weight models are trained on hundreds of millions to billions of examples, and proprietary models operate at comparable or greater scales. Furthermore, visual generation has largely shifted from basic class-conditioning signals towards dense, rich text captions, enabling more nuanced and controllable content creation.

Historically, the ImageNet-1K benchmark served as a critical testbed, driving significant advancements in class-conditional visual generation for over a decade. However, the authors identify two primary issues that have rendered ImageNet-1K less relevant for contemporary research:
1. **Drift from Modern Practice:** Modern visual generative models require larger, more diverse training corpora with rich text conditioning, a departure from ImageNet-1K's class-conditional structure and scale. Consequently, research conclusions drawn from ImageNet-1K may not directly transfer to practical modern settings.
2. **Metric Saturation and "Goodharting":** Over a decade of optimization on ImageNet-1K has led to the saturation of FID scores, with some methods achieving lower FID scores than even held-out real images. This phenomenon, referred to as "Goodharting," indicates that the metric has ceased to be a reliable measure of true generative capability.

While industrial laboratories frequently report results on text-conditioned image and video generation, they often rely on proprietary or unstable datasets. This practice impedes reproducibility, open scientific comparison, and the broader advancement of the field.

In response to these challenges, the authors propose four key properties for a modern benchmark dataset for visual generation:
- **Permissive:** Images, metadata, and annotations must be released under licenses permitting both research and commercial use without restrictions on derived artifacts.
- **Stable:** The dataset must remain unchanged over time, addressing issues like "link rot" prevalent in URL-indexed datasets.
- **Large:** It must be sufficiently scaled with rich text captions to train and evaluate modern visual generative models.
- **Accessible:** The dataset needs to be easily downloadable in a sharded format, obviating the need for complex crawling infrastructure or memory-intensive resharding.

GPIC is introduced as a comprehensive solution designed to satisfy all four of these criteria, aiming to establish a new foundation for open, reproducible, and scalable research in visual generative modeling, thereby addressing critical limitations in current benchmarking practices.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this work is to introduce GPIC, a "Giant Permissive Image Corpus," designed to serve as a robust, modern benchmark dataset for visual generative modeling. This objective is motivated by several pressing needs within the visual AI research community:

1. **Addressing Data Demands of Advanced Generative Models:** Modern visual generative models exhibit an increasing appetite for vast quantities of diverse data coupled with rich, dense conditioning signals, such as free-form text captions. Existing public datasets and benchmarks are not adequately scaled or structured to meet these requirements.
2. **Overcoming Limitations of Existing Benchmarks:** The long-standing ImageNet-1K benchmark, while historically significant, has become less relevant for evaluating contemporary visual generative models. Its class-conditional nature does not align with the text-conditioned paradigm, and its evaluation metric (FID) is saturated, making it difficult to discern meaningful progress. A new benchmark is needed that reflects current modeling practices and offers a more informative evaluation.
3. **Fostering Reproducibility and Open Science:** Many state-of-the-art results in text-conditioned generation are reported using proprietary or unstable datasets. This lack of public access and consistency hinders reproducibility, prevents fair scientific comparisons, and impedes open collaboration. The authors are motivated to provide a dataset that enables researchers to replicate and build upon frontier work.
4. **Ensuring Legal Permissiveness and Stability:** A critical motivation is to provide a dataset that is not only large and relevant but also legally permissible for both research and commercial use. This eliminates legal ambiguities that often accompany internet-sourced data. Furthermore, by centrally hosting the dataset as fixed shards rather than URL indices, the authors aim to prevent data drift and ensure long-term stability for consistent benchmarking.
5. **Providing Scalable and Accessible Infrastructure:** The practical challenge of managing and accessing extremely large datasets is a barrier for many researchers. The motivation includes offering GPIC in an easily downloadable, sharded format, thereby reducing the infrastructure burden for researchers.

In essence, the key motivation is to provide a "permissive, stable, large, and accessible" resource that enables open, accessible, and reproducible research in visual generative modeling, pushing the field forward with a unified and robust evaluation framework.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The construction of GPIC involved a four-stage pipeline encompassing source pool acquisition and licensing, image filtering, deduplication, and high-quality captioning, followed by careful split construction and release. A dedicated benchmarking protocol was also developed.

**Dataset Construction Pipeline:**
1. **Source Pool and Licensing:**
 - Images were collected from Flickr and Wikimedia, strictly adhering to permissive licenses: CC BY, CC0, Public Domain, and No-Known-Restrictions. This ensures both research and commercial use of the dataset and derived artifacts.
 - Provenance and attribution metadata (key, dimensions, timestamp, license details) are retained for each image. URLs were deliberately excluded to prevent reliance on external links and avoid future link rot.
 - The initial pool comprised 110,569,761 images, with Flickr contributing 87.7% and Wikimedia 12.3%.

2. **Image Filtering:**
 - **Resolution and Aspect Ratio:** Images with extreme resolutions or aspect ratios, or those with the longest side smaller than 256 pixels, were removed (approx. 0.01% of the source pool).
 - **VLM-based Quality Filtering:** The Qwen3-VL-4B vision-language model was used to filter out images deemed of poor visual quality or limited semantic content, such as near-blank, severely blurred, underexposed, or overexposed images (approx. 0.3% removed).
 - **Safety Filtering:** A conservative safety filter, also employing Qwen3-VL-4B, was applied to remove images flagged as unsafe (approx. 0.35% removed).

3. **Deduplication:**
 - Acknowledging the prevalence of duplicates and near-duplicates in internet-sourced image collections, a conservative deduplication strategy was implemented to retain distinct but visually similar images while removing clear copies.
 - **Feature Extraction:** SSCD (Self-Supervised Copy Detection) features were extracted for all images.
 - **Similarity Search:** FAISS was used for approximate nearest-neighbor search based on SSCD features.
 - **Threshold Calibration:** Manual inspection of nearest-neighbor pairs across SSCD similarity ranges informed the selection of removal thresholds, prioritizing avoidance of false removals.
 - **Collision Models:** Predictive collision models, based on power-law trends observed across subsets of the data (ranging from 108K to 3.4M images), estimated the number of removals at full scale for different similarity thresholds. A threshold of θ = 0.95 was chosen for conservative removal.
 - **Full-Corpus Deduplication:** A two-tier rule was applied:
 - Pairs with SSCD similarity > 0.9625: The lower-resolution image was removed.
 - Connected components with at least five images and similarity between 0.90 and 0.9625: Only the highest-resolution image in the component was retained.
 - Post-deduplication, 101.3 million images remained. An SHA-256 hash check confirmed no exact duplicates.

4. **Captioning GPIC:**
 - **Synthetic Captions:** GPIC employs high-quality synthetic captions generated by a VLM, rather than potentially noisy or misaligned source metadata.
 - **Caption Formats:** Four levels of detail were generated: 'tag' (unordered keywords), 'short' (1-2 sentences), 'medium' (2-3 sentences), and 'long' (5-7 sentences). These were distributed as 1% tag, 45% short, 45% medium, and 9% long.
 - **Model Selection:** Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct was selected after evaluating 2B, 4B, 8B, and 30B-A3B models on a microbenchmark of 1,520 GPIC images. This microbenchmark used human-annotated reference captions and an LLM-as-a-judge pipeline across five axes (overall summary, counting, spatial understanding, attribute binding, OCR) to assess quality and throughput. Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct offered the best quality-throughput trade-off, with full-corpus captioning requiring approximately 1,500 H100 GPU-hours.
 - **Prompts:** Specific prompts for each caption type are provided for reproducibility.

**Split Construction and Release:**
- **Dataset Splits:** GPIC is partitioned into 100M training images, 200K validation images, and 1M test images. Each split maintains the original Flickr/Wikimedia source distribution and the global caption-type distribution.
- **Benchmark Scales:** Three nested tiers for the training set are provided: GPIC-Nano (1M images), GPIC-Lite (10M images), and GPIC-Full (100M images), allowing for scalable development and training.
- **Packaging:** GPIC is released on Hugging Face as 8,000 tar shards (totaling 12.9 TB for GPIC-Full), containing images, captions, and metadata. Shards are balanced in image count and caption-type composition, supporting large-scale streaming training. The dataset is released under the MIT license.

**Benchmarking Protocol:**
- **Metrics:** The primary evaluation metric is FD-DINOv2, which uses DINOv2 features instead of Inception features in the Fréchet Distance calculation. This addresses the saturation of ImageNet-1K FID and exhibits better correlation with human judgments. Auxiliary metrics include Precision, Density, Recall, and Coverage.
- **Evaluation Procedure:** Models are evaluated by generating 50,000 images using a provided fixed set of 50,000 test captions, sampled from the 1M GPIC test set. These generated samples are compared against precomputed reference statistics from the full 1M GPIC test set. A PyTorch evaluation suite, gpic-eval, is provided.
- **Held-out Test Statistics:** Unlike ImageNet-1K's use of training set statistics, GPIC evaluates against a held-out test set to prevent detection failures related to memorization or overfitting.
- **Oracle References:** Real-vs-real distances are provided for GPIC subsets against the 1M GPIC test set, offering reference points for interpreting model performance and monitoring benchmark saturation.
- **Compliance Guidelines:** Explicit disclosure is required for models using DINOv2-based objectives or auxiliary networks trained on other datasets, as these constitute material deviations from the standard protocol and can affect result interpretability.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper includes the establishment of a reference baseline for visual generative modeling on GPIC-Full, using a pixel-space flow matching model.

**Reference Baseline Model:**
- A JiT (Just-in-Time) model, a pixel-space flow matching architecture with a Transformer backbone, was chosen as the baseline. This choice was based on its single-stage training, lack of tokenizer pretraining, and absence of auxiliary losses.
- Specifically, the JiT-T2I (PixGen-XXL/16 1.1B) architecture, leveraging Qwen3-1.7B for text conditioning, was employed.

**Experiment Setup:**
- The JiT-T2I model was trained on the GPIC-Full dataset (100M text-image pairs) for one epoch.
- Training was conducted at 256 × 256 resolution with a global batch size of 256.
- The optimizer used was AdamW with a learning rate of 10⁻⁴, betas of 0.9 and 0.95, and no weight decay. A constant learning rate schedule with 0.1% warmup was applied.
- Images were randomly cropped (scale 0.8 to 1.0) and resized to 256 × 256. The maximum text length for conditioning was 300 tokens.
- Training required approximately 40 hours on a single 8×H100 node.
- For evaluation, images were generated for the provided 50,000 test captions using Euler sampling with 50 steps. Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG) scales of 1.75, 4.0, and 6.25 were tested.

**Quantitative Results (Table 3):**
The baseline achieved the following performance metrics against the 1M GPIC test set:
- **CFG 1.75:** FD: 204.01, Precision: 0.917, Recall: 0.530, Density: 1.034, Coverage: 0.806
- **CFG 4.00:** FD: 87.80, Precision: 0.933, Recall: 0.765, Density: 1.012, Coverage: 0.906
- **CFG 6.25:** FD: 76.25, Precision: 0.942, Recall: 0.792, Density: 1.014, Coverage: 0.908

The results indicate that increasing the CFG scale improved the FD-DINOv2 score (lower is better), as well as recall and coverage for this specific baseline model. The best FD of 76.25 was observed at a CFG scale of 6.25. This value is noted to be higher than CFG values commonly used for class-conditional ImageNet-1K evaluations using FID.

**Qualitative Results (Figure 11):**
Qualitative samples generated by the JiT-T2I model for prompts from the held-out Test-50K subset are presented. These samples demonstrate the model's ability to generate diverse images spanning various categories, including animals, vehicles, natural scenes, architecture, and indoor environments, conditioned on the provided text prompts, with visual variations across different CFG scales.

**Pretraining Loss (Figure 10):**
The pretraining loss curve for the JiT-T2I baseline on GPIC-Full is provided, illustrating the training progression over iterations for one epoch.

The authors emphasize that this baseline serves as a reproducible reference point, rather than an optimized performance benchmark, for future comparisons on GPIC.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

GPIC represents a significant contribution to the field of visual generative modeling, addressing critical gaps in existing datasets and evaluation protocols. Its significance and potential impact stem from its core design principles and features:

1. **Enabling Open and Reproducible Research:** By providing a "permissive, stable, large, and accessible" dataset, GPIC aims to democratize access to high-quality, large-scale image-text data. This facilitates open scientific inquiry, reproducibility of results, and fair comparisons between different generative models, which was previously hindered by reliance on proprietary or unstable datasets.
2. **Modernizing Benchmarking:** GPIC introduces a contemporary benchmark that aligns with the data demands and conditioning signals of modern visual generative models, particularly text-to-image generation. It moves beyond the limitations of ImageNet-1K, which had become saturated and less relevant, offering a new, unsaturated evaluation setting.
3. **Robust Evaluation Protocol:** The adoption of FD-DINOv2 as the primary metric, combined with a held-out test set for reference statistics, provides a more reliable and human-aligned measure of generative quality and diversity. This structured evaluation protocol helps to prevent metric "Goodharting" and promotes advancements that truly reflect improved generative capabilities.
4. **Legal Clarity and Stability:** The meticulous attention to permissive licensing for all images, along with the retention of provenance metadata, provides a clear legal basis for both academic and commercial use. Releasing the dataset as frozen tar shards eliminates issues like link rot and dataset drift, ensuring long-term stability for consistent research.
5. **Scalability and Accessibility:** The centralized hosting on Hugging Face and the provision of data in a sharded format significantly reduce the practical barriers for researchers to access and utilize large datasets. The tiered benchmark scales (Nano, Lite, Full) also allow for flexible development and experimentation across different computational budgets.
6. **Resource for Broader Multimodal Research:** Beyond its primary focus on text-to-image generation, GPIC’s high-quality image-text resource is poised to support a wide array of broader multimodal research, contributing to advancements in areas requiring large-scale visual-language understanding and generation.

**Broader Impact and Limitations (Societal Considerations):**
The authors acknowledge that GPIC, like other large-scale image corpora, carries inherent societal risks. These include:
- **Misuse for Harmful Generation:** The potential for malicious actors to leverage powerful generative models trained on GPIC for creating harmful or unethical content.
- **Memorization of Training Content:** The risk that generative models might memorize and reproduce specific images from the training set, raising concerns about privacy and intellectual property.
- **Amplification of Source-Platform Biases:** The possibility that biases present in the source image platforms (Flickr and Wikimedia) could be amplified by models trained on GPIC.

The authors have implemented several mitigation strategies:
- **Legal Basis and Attribution:** Every image in GPIC has a verifiable legal basis for redistribution, and all license and attribution metadata are retained.
- **VLM-Generated Captions:** Using captions generated by Qwen3-VL-4B helps avoid the direct release of potentially toxic or personally identifying language often found in scraped alt text.
- **Frozen Tar Shards:** Distributing GPIC as frozen tar shards prevents silent dataset drift and protects against data poisoning attacks that could occur with URL-indexed datasets.
- **Deduplication Efforts:** While some near-duplicates might persist due to the conservative strategy, extensive deduplication aims to reduce redundancy and associated risks.

In conclusion, GPIC is designed to catalyze progress in visual generative modeling by providing an essential, modern, and legally transparent resource. Its structured approach to data collection, annotation, and evaluation aims to elevate the standards for open and reproducible research in the field.
