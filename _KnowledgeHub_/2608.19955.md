# MILD: Tractable Terrain Modeling for Learning Improved Bipedal Locomotion on Deformable Surfaces

> [!summary] Summary
> MILD introduces a discrete-element granular-media contact solver that captures spatially varying, eccentric foot-terrain forces for large bipedal footplates, paired with a VAE-modulation RL controller for terrain-adaptive walking. Trained in Isaac Gym and validated on a real 12-DOF biped, it beats prior contact models and learned baselines on stability, energy efficiency, and cross-stiffness adaptation.

## Problem

- Mainstream rigid-body simulators cannot capture spatiotemporal heterogeneity of deformable terrain, limiting realistic training data for controller design.
- Prior granular-media contact models assume axisymmetric single-point penetration and uniform force distribution, invalid for large bipedal footplates with eccentric contact.
- RL methods generalize to soft terrain via geometry randomization or simplified spring-damper contact without explicit mechanics, degrading real-world performance.

## Method

- ==Discrete Element-based Granular Media Model==: discretizes the footplate into 154 elements, each forming independent developing cones for non-uniform, eccentric force prediction.
- ==Kinematic-aware multi-contact solver==: enforces continuity and friction-cone constraints across elements via ==floating-base Newton-Euler dynamics== and iterative impulse updates.
- ==Terrain Estimator and Modulation Net==: a multi-head ==VAE== estimates privileged states into a latent terrain code, adaptively rescaled by a ==FiLM-style modulation network== conditioned on foot velocity.

## Results

- Model achieves **38%** deeper penetration than Ecc-Spring and broader contact/slip diversity than concentric single-point baselines during training data generation.
- On hardware, MILD attains lower Cost of Transport than HT-2 and Clock baselines (e.g., **0.82** vs 1.05 vs 1.38 at 0.3 m/s) across all commanded velocities.
- Robot completes **10/10** forward and backward walking cycles at **1.2 m/s** across seven terrain types with zero failures, while baseline contact models fail via joint-limit or stability violations on 45d foam.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - For large-footprint contact (bipeds vs quadrupeds/hoppers), uniform-force assumptions break down; per-element discretization is needed to capture eccentric penetration.
> - Encoding terrain compliance into a modulated latent space lets a policy generalize to abrupt, never-seen stiffness transitions rather than memorizing training conditions.
> - Physics-grounded contact fidelity in simulation transfers directly to real-world energy efficiency and stability gains, beyond what geometry-randomization alone achieves.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of bipedal robotics has demonstrated substantial advancements, particularly in locomotion on rigid surfaces. However, a significant portion of natural and human-made environments consists of deformable, unstructured terrain, such as sand, mud, or rubble. Enabling bipedal robots to traverse such yielding substrates is crucial for applications like disaster response, planetary exploration, and outdoor navigation.

Existing bipedal locomotion controllers are predominantly optimized for rigid ground. Mainstream robotic simulators, such as MuJoCo, PyBullet, and Isaac Gym, primarily rely on rigid-body dynamics, which inherently limit their capacity to accurately model the spatiotemporal heterogeneity of deformable terrains. This fundamental limitation constrains the generation of realistic training data for controller design, leading to a performance gap when controllers are deployed on real-world soft surfaces.

Within the robotics physics community, accurately and efficiently simulating soft substrate deformation and stress upon robot contact is an active area of research. Early efforts focused on granular media (GM) simulation using particle-based methods, which, while detailed, are computationally intensive. To address efficiency, researchers proposed simplified contact mechanics like linear viscoelastic or rate-dependent plastic models. However, these models often oversimplify the complex nature of GM interactions. Resistive Force Theory (RFT) emerged as an empirical model for quasistatic reaction forces, applicable to bipedal quasistatic locomotion, and later extended to 3D intrusion scenarios. A limitation of RFT is its applicability primarily to slow interactions, failing to capture high-dynamic penetration forces. Impulsive force models were proposed for high-speed penetration, but these were found to lack considerations for the "added mass effect" caused by compacted grains, which significantly influences contact forces. Previous work, particularly for quadrupedal robots, also often assumed a uniform distribution of reaction force across the contact area, which is not accurate for the larger, arbitrarily shaped footplates of bipedal robots where contact forces vary significantly across regions.

Recent advances in deep reinforcement learning (RL) have enabled legged robots to traverse various challenging terrains. These methods often achieve generalization through implicit system identification combined with domain randomization over rigid terrain geometries. Some studies simulate terrain compliance by randomizing parameters of built-in contact models (e.g., mass-spring-damper systems). However, these approximations are limited in capturing realistic penetration dynamics. Prior work on realistic deformable terrain models for quadrupeds has been limited by assumptions of uniform force distribution across the contact area, which neglects the influence of insertion posture and the non-uniform nature of contact forces.

This work, MILD, addresses these limitations by proposing a physics-grounded discrete-element contact solver that models spatially varying foot-terrain interactions more accurately. It extends existing models by explicitly accounting for eccentric insertion and spatiotemporal heterogeneous penetration depths across substantial footplate areas. This enhanced simulation fidelity allows for the training of a terrain-aware locomotion controller via deep reinforcement learning, aiming to bridge the reality gap and enable bipedal robots to adapt naturally to real deformable surfaces. The research positions itself by providing a more tractable and accurate model for soft surface interaction in simulations, which is then leveraged within an RL framework to develop controllers that are robust and adaptive to varying terrain compliance, particularly for bipedal systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the limitations of current bipedal robot locomotion capabilities on deformable and unstructured terrains. While bipedal robots offer potential for navigating complex environments, their performance is hampered by the inability of existing simulation tools and control strategies to accurately model and manage dynamic interactions with yielding substrates. Mainstream simulators, relying on rigid-body dynamics, fail to capture the spatiotemporal heterogeneity inherent in soft-surface interactions. This deficiency restricts the generation of realistic data, which is crucial for developing robust locomotion controllers, especially those based on reinforcement learning. Specifically, existing terrain models often either oversimplify granular media mechanics (e.g., simple viscoelastic models), are computationally prohibitive (e.g., particle-based simulations), or do not adequately account for complex, high-dynamic interactions and non-uniform force distributions critical for bipedal footplates.

In response to these challenges, the work presents MILD, aiming to achieve improved bipedal locomotion on deformable surfaces through a two-pronged approach: enhanced physics modeling and sophisticated learning-based control. The key objectives of this research are articulated in three main aspects:

1. **Develop a Tractable Foot-Terrain Interaction Model:** The first objective is to propose a physics-grounded discrete-element contact solver that accurately simulates spatially varying foot-terrain interactions for substantial contact areas. This model aims to overcome the prevailing assumption of uniform force distribution found in prior work (e.g., for quadrupeds or 1-D hoppers). It explicitly accounts for eccentric insertion and spatiotemporal heterogeneous penetration depths, which are critical for accurate force prediction for biped-scale footplates. The goal is to provide a more realistic and computationally efficient simulation of deformable terrain mechanics that can be integrated into reinforcement learning frameworks.

2. **Design a Terrain-Aware Reinforcement Learning Control Framework:** The second objective is to train a locomotion controller using deep reinforcement learning that can implicitly adapt to terrain compliance. This involves an encoder-modulation architecture that dynamically encodes terrain properties into latent representations, allowing for online adaptation. The framework incorporates proprioceptive estimation to enhance robustness and adaptability, leveraging the high-fidelity contact model for generating diverse and realistic training scenarios.

3. **Validate Performance Through Extensive Experiments:** The third objective is to quantitatively demonstrate the superior performance of the proposed approach. This involves extensive simulations comparing MILD against state-of-the-art deformable terrain modeling methods in terms of contact richness and interaction characteristics. Furthermore, hardware experiments are conducted to validate the controller's capability for natural adaptation on real deformable surfaces, including online terrain identification and adaptation across a wide range of surface stiffnesses, demonstrating robustness and energy efficiency.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The MILD framework integrates a physics-grounded discrete-element contact solver with a reinforcement learning (RL) pipeline to enable bipedal robots to learn robust locomotion on deformable surfaces.

**A. Generalized Contact Solver for Robot-Deformable Terrain Interactions**

The core of the simulation environment is a generalized contact solver designed to model granular media (GM) interactions under arbitrary-shaped robot feet, extending existing physics-grounded models.

1. **Discrete Element-based Granular Media Model:**
 - **Foot Discretization:** To accurately capture contact force distribution, the robot's foot envelope surface is discretized into 154 elements, each approximately 0.0195 m in size. This resolution balances contact detail with computational efficiency.
 - **Independent Penetration:** Each discrete element (DE) undergoes independent penetration, forming distinct "jamming cones" within the granular medium. This allows for non-uniform force distributions across the footplate.
 - **Contact Area Calculation:** For each penetrating element, the model calculates the flat contact area ($S_{flat}$) and the conical contact area ($S_{cone}$) as functions of penetration depth ($h$), shear band angle ($\alpha$), recruitment rate ($\nu$), and initial intersection depth ($h_0$).
 - **Added Mass Effect:** The "added mass" ($m_A$) describes the accumulated effect of compacted grains inside the jammed cone. It is calculated based on volume fraction ($\phi$), grain density ($\rho$), and a surrounding mass scaling factor ($c_g$), integrated over the penetration depth.
 - **Granular Reaction Force ($F_{GM}$):** This force comprises a quasistatic force ($F_g$) and a force induced by the momentum change from colliding with the virtual added mass. $F_g$ depends on penetration stiffness ($k_{pen}$) and depth-dependent conical stress ($\sigma_{cone}$). The momentum change term involves the time derivative of added mass and the vertical velocity/acceleration, incorporating an inertial drag scaling factor ($c_d$). Both integral components have closed-form expressions.

2. **Whole-body Dynamics and Kinematic-aware Contact Solver:**
 - **System Modeling:** The bipedal robot is modeled as a multi-articulated floating-base system governed by the Newton-Euler equation. The contact solver aims to drive contact velocity to zero by iteratively adjusting tangential forces based on predicted velocity deviation.
 - **Velocity Changes at Contact Points:** The velocity change at each contact point is derived from the generalized robot dynamics, incorporating the mass matrix, generalized forces, and the Jacobian of contact points. For multiple contacts, these changes are superposed. Translational ($J_{c,L}$) and rotational ($J_{c,R}$) Jacobians map generalized velocities to contact motion. The Jacobian at each discrete element is derived from the ankle point Jacobian using rigid body transformation.
 - **Physical Constraint:** To ensure continuous motion and avoid unphysical artifacts (e.g., motion tearing), kinematic constraints are applied. The relative velocity error ($\delta^{rb}$) between any two elements on the foot is computed. An average constraint violation for each element is then used to quantify kinematic inconsistency.
 - **Friction Cone Condition:** The tangential impulse for the next step is adjusted to satisfy the friction cone condition. This involves checking if the impulse, combined with corrective terms (one to drive velocity to zero, another to maintain kinematic coherency), remains within the friction cone defined by the normal impulse and friction coefficient ($\mu$). If violated, the tangential impulse is projected onto the cone boundary. This process is iterative (Algorithm 1) until convergence.
 - **Drifting Resistance:** A drift-resistive force counteracts horizontal motion. This force scales with both the penetration depth and the accumulated horizontal travel distance ($d_D$) from the initial intrusion point, rather than just Euclidean distance. It is defined by stiffness ($k_D$) and damping ($b_D$) factors.
 - **Zero Moment Point (ZMP) Calculation:** The resultant force and torque from all active elements are used to calculate the ZMP, which is a key metric for bipedal stability.

**B. Terrain-aware Reinforcement Learning Pipeline**

The control problem is formulated as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) and solved using an asymmetric actor-critic framework.

1. **Observation-Action Space:**
 - **Actor Input:** The actor receives an H-step historical proprioceptive state ($o^H_t$), including base angular velocity, commanded velocity, projected gravity, joint angles, joint velocities, and the action from the previous step. Additionally, it receives estimates of key robot states and a latent representation of the terrain.
 - **Critic Input:** The critic, in addition to the actor's input, receives ground-truth states (available only in simulation) and real terrain parameters.
 - **Action Space:** The action is defined as the desired deviation of joint angles from a time-invariant nominal pose.

2. **Reward Engineering:** The controller learns a velocity-tracking periodic gait defined by a sinusoidal function. The reward function (Table I) includes terms for:
 - Linear and angular velocity tracking.
 - Feet clearance and gait alignment with a periodic schedule.
 - Penalties for feet slippery speed, high base acceleration, and lack of action smoothness.

3. **Terrain Estimator and Modulation Net:**
 - **Multi-head Variational Autoencoder (VAE):** A VAE serves as the first adaptive module. It estimates base velocity, feet velocities, and feet heights from proprioceptive states and outputs a latent variable ($z_t$) encoding implicit robot-environment information. The VAE is jointly optimized by a regression loss (MSE for state estimation and VAE reconstruction/KL divergence) and the PPO policy loss.
 - **Modulation Network:** A second adaptive module, the modulation network, dynamically adjusts the latent variable $z_t$. It processes the estimated feet velocity ($v_{f,t}$) to generate two adaptive scaling factors ($\theta_1, \theta_2$), which then perform an affine transformation on $z_t$ to produce a modulated latent state ($\tilde{z}_t = \theta_1 \cdot z_t + \theta_2$). This mechanism allows the policy to implicitly adapt to changes in terrain compliance, as variations in compliant terrain affect foot velocity patterns.

The entire training is performed on the Isaac Gym simulator using 4096 parallel agents over 20000 episodes, with extensive parameter randomization (Table II) to enhance generalization. Hardware validation uses an EngineAI SA01 bipedal robot with a PD controller for joint-level tracking.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents findings from both simulation and real-world experiments, evaluating the MILD framework's performance in modeling terrain interactions and enabling robust bipedal locomotion.

**A. Simulation Experiments**

The MILD approach was compared against five state-of-the-art deformable terrain modeling methods: Con-Spring, Con-Cone, Rigid, Ecc-Quasi (RFT), and Ecc-Spring. The comparison focused on how well these models capture the complexities of foot-terrain interaction.
- **Contact Interaction Characteristics:** MILD demonstrated the ability to generate more diverse and realistic contact scenarios. Specifically, it achieved deeper penetration (38% deeper than Ecc-Spring) and longer slip distances (Figure 5). This enhanced realism is attributed to MILD's granular media model, which accounts for substrate mass variation during penetration at different compression points, leading to a broader range of inertial drag. The ability to simulate a wider range of contact characteristics is beneficial for reinforcement learning agents, providing more varied training data and thus enhancing policy adaptability.
- **Stability during Dynamic Walking:** MILD was also compared against a passivity-based whole-body controller designed for compliant terrain. MILD, utilizing pre-optimized RL policies, exhibited significantly smaller Center of Mass (CoM) oscillations and maintained greater stability during dynamic walking compared to the passivity-based controller, which relies on computationally expensive online optimization (Figure 6).

**B. Real-World Experiments**

Hardware validation was conducted on an EngineAI SA01 bipedal robot across various real-world deformable terrains.

1. **Implicit Terrain Adaptability:**
 - **Diverse Terrain Evaluation:** The system's performance was evaluated on six distinct terrain types: rubber tiles, 60d, 45d, and 28d polyurethane (PU) foam (with decreasing density indicating softer surfaces), as well as natural grass and sand.
 - **Adaptive Gait:** The MILD controller demonstrated adaptive behavior, automatically increasing foot clearance on softer surfaces to compensate for ground deformation (Figure 7). This adaptation contributed to a high success rate and improved energy efficiency across different stiffness grades.
 - **Terrain Perception:** To assess the controller's implicit terrain perception, the affine-transformed latent state ($\tilde{z}_t$) was visualized using t-SNE. The visualization revealed distinct clusters corresponding to terrains with different stiffness-deformability properties. Softer terrains formed central clusters, while stiffer terrains were distributed peripherally (Figure 8). This indicates that the model effectively encodes terrain information, which facilitates adaptive behavior.

2. **Comparative Analysis with Learning-Based Methods:**
 - **Robustness on Varied Terrains:** The robot successfully completed 10 forward/backward walking cycles at 1.2 m/s on seven different terrain types (including rigid planes) without failure. In contrast, controllers trained with baseline contact models (as in Section III-B) exhibited limited performance on 45d foam, often failing due to conservative gaits, insufficient foot clearance, joint limit violations, or stability loss.
 - **Performance Metrics:** MILD was quantitatively compared against two state-of-the-art learning-based methods, HT-2 (transformer-based) and Clock (clock-signal guided), which rely on terrain geometry randomization over rigid surfaces without explicit terrain mechanics modeling. Table III presents the performance across various commanded velocities (0.3 to 1.2 m/s) in terms of:
 - **Cost of Transport (COT):** MILD consistently achieved lower COT values (e.g., 0.82 at 0.3 m/s, 0.83 at 1.2 m/s) compared to HT-2 (1.05 to 1.08) and Clock (1.38 to 1.42), indicating superior energy efficiency.
 - **Maximum Torque:** MILD exhibited lower maximum joint torques (e.g., 58.2 Nm at 0.3 m/s, 88.4 Nm at 1.2 m/s) compared to HT-2 (62.9 to 96.0 Nm) and Clock (67.6 to 115.2 Nm), suggesting less strenuous motor demands.
 - **Measured Velocity (Simulation):** MILD maintained good velocity tracking (e.g., 1.15 m/s measured for 1.2 m/s commanded), comparable to or slightly lower than HT-2, but superior to Clock.
 - These results suggest that the integration of a high-fidelity compliant contact model significantly enhances the controller's overall performance.

3. **Online Identification and Transition:**
 - **Emergent Adaptation:** The system demonstrated emergent terrain adaptation during unexpected transitions between surfaces of varying stiffness (e.g., from high-stiffness rubber to low-stiffness foam and back). The controller automatically adjusted its gait, such as increasing stride length, to maintain dynamic stability without prior knowledge of the change.
 - **Latent Space Trajectories:** The t-SNE visualization of the modulated latent space confirmed this process, showing distinct latent space trajectories during the complete rubber $\Rightarrow$ foam $\Rightarrow$ rubber transition cycle (Figure 9). This indicates that the latent representation effectively learns fundamental physical properties of ground stiffness, enabling real-time adaptation even to abrupt, previously unseen terrain changes.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework, MILD, that addresses critical challenges in enabling bipedal robots to walk on deformable surfaces. Its significance lies in the development of a high-fidelity compliant contact model specifically tailored for substantial bipedal footplates. This model advances beyond previous simplifications by accurately capturing eccentric penetration, high-speed impacts, and spatially varying forces, which are crucial for realistic foot-terrain interactions. When integrated with an advanced reinforcement learning controller that features online estimation and latent modulation for compliance adaptation, MILD enables bipedal robots to learn and execute robust locomotion strategies on challenging terrains.

The potential impact of this work is substantial for several key applications:
- **Robotics in Unstructured Environments:** By facilitating stable and energy-efficient locomotion on yielding substrates, MILD can significantly expand the operational capabilities of bipedal robots in real-world unstructured environments, such as disaster zones, uneven natural landscapes, or construction sites. This could enhance their utility in tasks ranging from exploration and surveillance to manipulation and assistance.
- **Disaster Response:** Robots capable of navigating rubble, mud, or soft ground can be vital for search and rescue operations in areas affected by natural disasters, where rigid-terrain optimized robots would struggle.
- **Planetary Exploration:** For missions to celestial bodies with unknown or variable surface mechanics, such as Martian or lunar regolith, robots equipped with MILD's capabilities could move more effectively and safely, expanding exploration reach.
- **Simulation Fidelity:** The physics-grounded discrete-element contact solver itself represents a significant contribution to the field of robotic simulation, providing a more accurate and tractable model for deformable terrain that can benefit a wider range of legged robot research.
- **Advancement in Reinforcement Learning for Robotics:** The integration of a VAE-based estimator and a modulation network within an asymmetric actor-critic framework demonstrates a robust approach to learning terrain-aware policies. The ability to implicitly identify terrain properties and adapt online, even to abrupt and previously unseen transitions, indicates a step forward in creating truly adaptive robotic systems.

Future work outlined by the authors, such as incorporating visual terrain deformation perception and exploring multi-gait generalization (e.g., jumping and running maneuvers) or implementation on model-based control architectures, indicates pathways for further enhancing robot autonomy and versatility on complex terrains. The MILD framework provides a foundation for developing bipedal robots that are more robust, adaptable, and energy-efficient in real-world dynamic and deformable environments.
