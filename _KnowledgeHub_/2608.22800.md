# Triplet2Track: A Hierarchical System with Object-Centric Representations for Reliable Long-Horizon Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Triplet-to-Track System (TTS) is a hierarchical closed-loop imitation system for long-horizon manipulation. It represents subgoals as instance-grounded object-relation triplets, constrains next-subgoal proposals with a temporal graph built from human videos, compiles each triplet transition into continuous point tracks, and feeds those tracks to a track-guided policy. Online relation checking triggers replanning. It reaches 74.8% average real-world success versus 30.4% for the best baseline.

## Problem

- End-to-end VLA policies demand large action-labeled datasets over extended episodes and internalize reasoning, making long-horizon behavior opaque, hard to diagnose, and unreliable at execution.
- Hierarchical planners emit free-form language or semantic subgoals weakly grounded in object instances and scene state, so hallucinated objects and relations arise frequently.
- Prior pipelines lack visually checkable predicates and closed-loop verification, so plans run open-loop, deviations go undetected, and errors compound across many subgoals.

## Method

- **Task Planner**: a lightweight Transformer relation classifier over ==SAM2== tokens plus event-triggered ==LoRA==-tuned ==InternVL2.5== verifier extracts triplets; a ==temporal graph== masks the zero-shot VLM's next-triplet proposals.
- **Track Predictor**: an ==ATM==-style multimodal masked track model conditioned on triplet-transition priors (instance masks plus relation-delta embedding), with ==masked-patch reconstruction== and ==relation-change recognition== auxiliary losses.
- **Action Policy**: a Transformer with a learnable action token fuses RGB, triplet, and predicted track features, decoding end-effector pose and gripper width via an MLP head under ==L2 regression==.

## Results

- Across **seven** real-world settings TTS averages **74.8%** success versus **30.4%** for the best baseline SeeDo; it scores **78.6%** on scale balancing and **83.3%** on seen stacking.
- Under perturbation TTS holds **60.0%** and **80.0%** success while SeeDo, PALO, and fine-tuned Pi0.5 collapse to **0.0%** on **two of three** perturbed settings.
- Ablations: removing the temporal graph drops balancing to **0.0%**; removing the Task Planner or using policy-only yields near-zero success; cascaded LTP+VLM gives **98.4%** accuracy at **32.2s** versus **202.6s** VLM-only.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Binding subgoals to concrete object instances and spatial relations makes plans checkable from pixels, converting open-ended VLM generation into constrained selection that suppresses hallucination.
> - A cascade of a cheap high-recall detector with an event-triggered high-precision verifier buys most of the accuracy at a fraction of per-frame VLM cost.
> - Discrete symbolic subgoals need a continuous interface; point tracks pretrained on action-free video bridge planning and control with only dozens of robot demos.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses the challenge of achieving reliable long-horizon robotic manipulation in uncertain environments. Long-horizon tasks, common in everyday scenarios, involve goals that are often only partially specified initially and become verifiable through a sequence of interactions. The core difficulty lies in maintaining correct and consistent plans that adapt to evolving observations over extended periods.

Existing approaches to long-horizon manipulation generally fall into two categories:

1. **End-to-end Visual-Language-Action (VLA) Systems:** These systems learn a direct mapping from raw images and language instructions to low-level actions, implicitly coupling perception, language understanding, planning, and control in a latent space. While they avoid hand-crafted abstractions, they typically suffer from high data demands, especially in long-horizon settings where extended episodes increase supervision costs. Their opaque reasoning processes also make diagnosis and interpretation difficult, which can lead to unreliable execution. Examples include models like Pi0.5 and RT-1.

2. **Hierarchical Pipelines:** These methods combine a high-level planner (often based on Large Language Models (LLMs) or Vision-Language Models (VLMs)) with a low-level controller. They aim to reduce data requirements by leveraging web-scale knowledge and human demonstrations, offering improved interpretability through semantic sketches. However, these approaches often have limitations:
 - **Weak Grounding:** Plans generated in free-form language or symbolic semantic space can lack explicit structural or spatial constraints, leading to hallucinations of objects or relations (e.g., QA-conditioned policies, semantic decompositions).
 - **Planner-Controller Mismatch:** A disconnect can exist between the high-level semantic plans and low-level physical actions, especially in open-loop settings or under imperfect synchronization.
 - **Limited Closed-Loop Mechanisms:** Many systems lack robust mechanisms to verify subgoals against visual observations or revise inconsistent plans online, making them vulnerable to error accumulation and unrecovered failures. Some recent systems incorporate periodic replanning or feedback but may still lack predicates that are directly verifiable from perception.

A complementary area of research involves policy learning from action-free videos, which offer rich cues about behavior and scene dynamics without costly action supervision. Prior methods include inverse-dynamics pseudo labeling (data-efficient but brittle for continuous actions), self-supervised feature pretraining (good for perception, weak for temporal control), and video prediction (prone to hallucinated geometry). Predicting point tracks as physically grounded motion priors has shown promise for closed-loop guidance, but without global task context, it can lead to myopic execution.

The Triplet-to-Track System (TTS) positions itself within this landscape by attempting to address the data inefficiency and limited verifiability of end-to-end VLAs, as well as the weak grounding and lack of automatic error detection and recovery in hierarchical schemes. It seeks to combine the benefits of hierarchical planning with robust, visually verifiable, and physically grounded execution by explicitly connecting planning via instance-grounded triplets to control via continuous tracks, while leveraging human video data for efficiency.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations behind the Triplet-to-Track System (TTS) are:

1. **Enhance Reliability in Long-Horizon Robotic Manipulation:** The core motivation is to develop a system that can consistently and correctly execute complex, multi-step manipulation tasks over extended time horizons, even in environments with inherent uncertainties. Existing methods struggle with maintaining plan correctness and consistency as observations evolve.

2. **Address Data Inefficiency:** End-to-end VLA models require substantial amounts of robot-collected action-labeled data, which is costly to acquire, particularly for diverse long-horizon tasks. TTS aims to reduce this reliance by leveraging human demonstration videos, which are easier to obtain and contain rich behavioral information, for pre-training and task planning.

3. **Improve Interpretability and Verifiability:** Unlike opaque end-to-end systems, hierarchical pipelines offer better interpretability. However, many hierarchical planners generate high-level goals that are weakly grounded in concrete observations, leading to "planning hallucinations" (e.g., proposing impossible or contextually irrelevant actions). A key objective is to ensure that high-level plans are explicit, object-centric, and directly verifiable against visual observations, allowing for easier diagnosis and error detection.

4. **Bridge the Gap Between Discrete Planning and Continuous Control:** Hierarchical systems often suffer from a mismatch between abstract, discrete subgoals and the continuous actions required for robot execution. TTS aims to create a structured and physically grounded interface that translates discrete high-level plans into continuous motion primitives that low-level controllers can effectively consume.

5. **Enable Closed-Loop Execution with Online Feedback:** Many hierarchical planners operate in an open-loop manner, making them susceptible to accumulating errors without online correction. TTS seeks to incorporate mechanisms for continuous monitoring of task progress from observations and enable online replanning when deviations or guard violations are detected, thereby enhancing robustness to disturbances.

6. **Support Generalization:** The system is motivated to achieve generalization to unseen object instances and novel task compositions, which is crucial for real-world applicability. This involves learning representations that are robust to variations in object appearance and task sequences.

In summary, the authors are motivated to develop a robotic manipulation system that is reliable, data-efficient, interpretable, verifiable, and capable of closed-loop adaptive behavior for long-horizon tasks by explicitly structuring the planning-to-control pipeline with object-centric representations.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Triplet-to-Track System (TTS) is a hierarchical closed-loop imitation learning framework designed for long-horizon robotic manipulation. It employs discrete, instance-grounded triplets for high-level planning and continuous point tracks for low-level execution. The system consists of three main layers: a Task Planner, a Track Predictor, and an Action Policy, operating in a closed-loop fashion with online monitoring and replanning.

**A. Task Planner for Discrete, Abstract Triplets**
The Task Planner generates high-level subgoals represented as object-centric triplets, `c = (x, r, y)`, where `x` and `y` are object instances and `r` is a spatial or gripper-object relation (e.g., "on," "grasped"). This planner operates in two stages:

1. **Relation Predictor:** This module is responsible for reliably detecting spatial relations between objects in the current scene. It uses a low-overhead, two-part design:
 - **Lightweight Transformer-based Predictor (LTP):** This is a pairwise relation classifier built with Transformer layers. It takes per-instance tokens from a SAM2 (Segment Anything Model 2) decoder as input, after object instances are detected by Grounding-DINO and SAM2. The LTP outputs relation logits for each ordered object pair, predicting the most probable relation `r`.
 - **LoRA-tuned VLM Branch:** To mitigate spurious flips (transient incorrect relation predictions) that the LTP can exhibit, a fine-tuned InternVL2.5 VLM is used as a high-precision verifier. This VLM is invoked only when the LTP signals a suspected change in a relation. It classifies relations within a masked set by conditioning prompts on instance masks and restricting the label space to feasible relations for the given object pair. Training for both LTP and VLM uses a small number of human video sequences with annotations only at change points. This cascaded approach aims for high recall in change detection with high precision in verification, reducing false positives and maintaining responsiveness.

2. **Graph-Guarded Next Triplet Generator:** This component uses a zero-shot VLM (e.g., GPT-4o) to propose the next subgoal triplet, but with constraints to reduce planning hallucinations.
 - **Temporal Graph Construction:** A directed graph `G` is constructed from human demonstration videos. Each node in `G` represents a task-relevant triplet `(x, r, y)`, and edges represent feasible transitions between triplets observed in the demonstrations. This graph is built by analyzing relation changes between consecutive frames in human videos, merging duplicates and branching for different execution orders.
 - **Constrained VLM Planning:** During inference, the VLM receives the current observation (`o_t`), language instruction (`ℓ`), current active triplet (`c_k`), and a binary mask (`M_k`) derived from the adjacency matrix of `G`. `M_k` restricts the VLM's proposals to only those next triplets that are feasible according to the learned temporal graph, preventing it from hallucinating unobserved or invalid transitions.
 - **Online Replanning and Guard Evaluation:** Inference starts from an initial node `c_0`. During execution, a "guard set" maintains the latest validated relation for each object pair from completed subgoals. Every `k` steps (e.g., 5 steps), the Relation Predictor evaluates whether all guards (i.e., expected relations for completed subgoals) still hold. If a guard violation is detected, the planner "relocalizes" to `c_0` and replans, ensuring closed-loop error recovery. If all guards hold, the Next-Triplet Generator proposes `c_{k+1}` within the masked neighborhood of `c_k`.

**B. Track Predictor for Continuous Tracks**
This module translates the discrete triplet transition `c_k → c_{k+1}` into continuous point tracks, providing a physically grounded motion prior for the low-level policy. It is pre-trained on action-free human videos for data efficiency.

1. **Data Preparation:** For each human video segment corresponding to a triplet transition, pixel-level masks for `x` and `y` are obtained using Grounding-DINO and SAM2. These masks are used to seed points, which are then tracked across frames using CoTracker to generate entity-aligned trajectories `V_{t:t+H}`. The relation change `r → r'` is encoded as a discrete token. The training samples consist of visual observations, instance masks, tracks, and the relation-delta embedding.

2. **Multimodal Masked Track Prediction:** Inspired by ATM, track forecasting is framed as a masked prediction task. The model predicts future point positions (`v_{t+1}`) given the current observation (`o_t`), current point positions (`v_t`), instance masks (`M_x`, `M_y`), and the relation-delta embedding `e(r → r')`. Mask encoders process `M_x` and `M_y` into instance-anchored tokens, and `e(r → r')` is embedded into the shared token space. A Transformer backbone processes these fused tokens, and a trajectory decoder outputs the continuous, entity-aligned track `p_{t:t+H}`.

3. **Auxiliary Objectives:** To enhance stability, two auxiliary objectives are used:
 - **Masked-Patch Reconstruction:** A decoder reconstructs masked image patches from visual features, which helps the Transformer backbone learn rich visual representations.
 - **Relation-Change Recognition:** The model predicts the relation-change class `δr` using object/subject mask decoder features and intermediate text decoder tokens, strengthening its ability to model relation dynamics.

**C. Policy Learning for Action**
A track-guided behavior cloning policy maps the predicted track prior to robot actions, trained on a small dataset of action-labeled robot data.

1. **Input and Architecture:** Given observations, the triplet-transition prior, and the track prior `p_{t:t+H}` from the Track Predictor, the low-level policy fuses these features. A Transformer backbone with a learnable action token processes the fused features. The resulting action token is then fused again with the track prior and decoded by an MLP-based action head to predict the robot action `a_t`. Actions consist of a 7-DoF end-effector pose and a scalar gripper width.

2. **Training and Inference:** The policy is trained with an L2 action-regression loss. During inference, TTS employs a receding-horizon scheme. The Task Planner sets `c_{k+1}` as the active goal. Every few steps (e.g., 5 steps), the Relation Predictor checks for `c_{k+1}` achievement. If achieved, the Next-Triplet Generator issues `c_{k+2}`. The Track Predictor computes a short-horizon track `p_{t:t+H}` conditioned on `c_k → c_{k+1}`. The Action Policy then uses this track and the current observation to predict `a_t`, which is executed. If a guard violation occurs, the planner re-localizes and replans.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates TTS on real-world long-horizon manipulation tasks using a 7-DoF Franka robot, comparing its performance against representative baselines.

**A. Experimental Setup**
- **Tasks:** Three families of long-horizon tasks were used:
 - **Dynamic Scale Balancing (DSB):** Evaluates closed-loop reasoning and online replanning for a two-pan balancing problem with toy frogs and weights. Task solvability is guaranteed, but specific configurations are not enumerated during training.
 - **Sequential Stacking (SS):** Evaluates multi-step ordering under rigid-body constraints (e.g., stacking blocks in a specific sequence). Training uses one order; testing requires generalization to unseen orders.
 - **Diverse Pick-and-Place (DPP):** Evaluates goal understanding and object-level generalization, involving placing specific objects into target boxes.
- **Generalization Settings:** Tasks were categorized as "Seen" (object set and execution order in robot training data) or "Unseen" (compositional or cross-category generalization).
- **Perturbations:** Robustness was tested by injecting disturbances during execution (e.g., adding/removing weights in DSB, removing target objects in DPP).
- **Data:** 30 robot demonstrations (action-labeled) and 50 human demonstrations (action-free, used for planning and track prediction) were collected per task. Human demonstrations included object variations and execution orders for generalization, unlike robot demos.
- **Hardware and Implementation:** Franka robot with two Intel RealSense D435i cameras. Models implemented in PyTorch, trained on NVIDIA A100 GPUs. Action Policy runs at 15 Hz.

**B. Baselines**
- **FT-Pi0.5:** An end-to-end VLA model, fine-tuned, mapping images and language directly to actions.
- **SeeDo:** A hierarchical baseline where a VLM generates QA-style text plans, executed with code-as-policy.
- **PALO:** A semantic planning baseline where a VLM recursively decomposes a task embedding into atomic actions.

**C. Main Results**

1. **Performance and Generalization (Q1):**
 - Across seven experimental settings (Seen, Unseen, Perturbed for different tasks), TTS achieved an average success rate of 74.8%.
 - The best-performing baseline, SeeDo, achieved 30.4%. FT-Pi0.5 and PALO performed lower.
 - **Quantitative Results (Table I):**
 - **Dynamic Scale Balancing:** TTS: 78.6% (Seen), 60.0% (Perturbed). Baselines were significantly lower (e.g., SeeDo: 26.7% Seen, 0.0% Perturbed).
 - **Sequential Stacking:** TTS: 83.3% (Seen), 66.7% (Unseen). Baselines were considerably lower (e.g., SeeDo: 41.7% Seen, 40.0% Unseen).
 - **Diverse Pick-and-Place:** TTS: 84.6% (Seen), 70.0% (Unseen), 80.0% (Perturbed). Baselines ranged from 16.7% to 54.5% in these categories.
 - **Analysis:**
 - Baselines (SeeDo, PALO) primarily failed in DPP due to weak grounding of free-form goal generation, leading to spatial hallucinations (e.g., wrong object selection).
 - In SS, baselines struggled with logical misordering and goal tracking drift, frequently grasping incorrect objects or failing to execute the prescribed sequence.
 - In DSB, baselines showed less stability due to a lack of explicit closed-loop planning that adapts to evolving physical states. FT-Pi0.5 struggled with object indecision or misselection under the low-data setting.
 - These results suggest that explicit instance-grounded subgoals and track-based execution priors contribute to higher reliability on both familiar and novel long-horizon tasks.

2. **Reliability and Stability (Q2):**
 - **Closed-Loop Robustness under Perturbations:** TTS demonstrated robustness in perturbed scenarios (Table I). For DSB, it achieved 60.0% success when weights were added/removed, and for DPP, 80.0% success when the target was removed post-grasp, indicating effective re-localization and replanning. Baselines often achieved 0% in perturbed settings.
 - **Interpretability and Hallucination Suppression:** The use of explicit triplet plans and track trajectories allows for traceability of execution failures to specific planning or control stages (Fig. 5). The temporal graph constraint, by limiting VLM proposals to feasible and observation-consistent updates, reduced planning hallucinations (e.g., incorrect relations, object mislocalization, redundant picks) compared to unconstrained VLM planning (as shown in ablations). Tracks serve as a visually interpretable interface for stable closed-loop behavior.

**D. Design Justification and Ablation Analysis (Q3)**

1. **Effect of Task Planner Components (Table II & III):**
 - **Relation Predictor:** Combining the Lightweight Transformer-based Predictor (LTP) with the LoRA-tuned VLM verifier improved overall accuracy (98.4%) with acceptable latency (32.2s for LTP+VLM vs. 202.6s for VLM-only). LTP alone had high speed (3.4ms) but lower accuracy (84.3%).
 - **Temporal Graph:** Removing the temporal graph constraint significantly increased next-triplet errors, making the VLM more prone to hallucinations and dependency-tracking failures (e.g., SS dropped from 83.3% to 25.0%, DSB from 78.6% to 0.0% in Table III). This indicates the graph's role in guiding feasible transitions.
 - **Relation Predictor (full removal):** Without a dedicated relation predictor, TTS struggled significantly, particularly with DSB (14.3%) and SS (37.5%), suggesting that zero-shot VLMs alone cannot infer fine-grained distinctions required for task switching reliably.

2. **Effect of Hierarchical Architecture (Table III):**
 - **w/o Task Planner (ATM):** Removing the entire Task Planner led to near-total failure across all tasks (7.1% DPP, 0.0% SS/DSB), indicating that long-horizon tasks collapse without high-level task progress maintenance.
 - **w/o Track Predictor:** Performance degraded substantially (DPP: 45.5%, SS: 46.1%, DSB: 43.8%), highlighting the importance of bridging the gap between discrete subgoals and continuous control with physically grounded motion priors.
 - **Only Action Policy (BC):** A behavior cloning policy without the higher-level components consistently failed (0.0% across all tasks), demonstrating the necessity of explicit task-level structure for maintaining global progress.

3. **Effect of Action-Labeled Data Size (Fig. 6):**
 - TTS achieved reasonable performance with only a few dozen action-labeled robot demonstrations (e.g., around 60-70% success with 10-20 demos), supporting its claimed data efficiency. Performance generally improved with more data, but the initial data efficiency was demonstrated.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Triplet-to-Track System (TTS) presents a hierarchical, closed-loop approach for long-horizon robotic manipulation that addresses several limitations of existing methods. Its significance and potential impact stem from several key aspects:

1. **Enhanced Reliability and Robustness:** By employing instance-grounded triplets for planning and continuous tracks for execution, TTS achieves a substantially higher average success rate (74.8%) on complex, multi-step real-world tasks compared to evaluated baselines. Its ability to maintain performance under significant perturbations and generalize to unseen object instances and task compositions demonstrates improved reliability in uncertain environments. The closed-loop design with online progress monitoring and replanning contributes to this robustness, allowing for recovery from deviations.

2. **Data Efficiency through Human Demonstrations:** TTS effectively leverages action-free human videos for pre-training the Track Predictor and constructing the temporal task graph for the Task Planner. This reduces the reliance on costly, robot-collected action-labeled data, making it more practical for real-world deployment. The system demonstrated reasonable performance with only dozens of robot demonstrations.

3. **Improved Interpretability and Verifiability:** The explicit, object-centric triplet representations for subgoals, combined with visualizable continuous tracks for motion priors, offer a level of interpretability and traceability often absent in opaque end-to-end systems. This structured representation allows for easier diagnosis of failures (identifying whether the issue is with planning or control) and helps suppress planning hallucinations by grounding abstract goals in concrete physical instances and relations.

4. **Effective Bridging of Planning and Control:** TTS provides a structured interface that explicitly connects discrete, high-level task plans (triplets) with continuous, low-level robot actions (via track priors). This bridge mitigates the common "planner-controller mismatch" problem in hierarchical systems, enabling more stable and coherent execution.

5. **Addressing Planning Hallucinations:** The novel Graph-Guarded Next Triplet Generator, which uses a temporal graph derived from human demonstrations to constrain VLM proposals, is significant. It limits the VLM's generative capabilities to observed and feasible transitions, thereby reducing the occurrence of planning hallucinations and ensuring that proposed subgoals are consistent with the robot's physical capabilities and scene state.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Wider Adoption of Long-Horizon Robotics:** By improving reliability and reducing data requirements, TTS could contribute to the broader adoption of robots for complex, multi-step tasks in manufacturing, logistics, healthcare, and domestic environments.
- **Foundation for More Capable Robotic Systems:** The explicit, verifiable representations and closed-loop nature could serve as a foundation for developing more robust and trustworthy autonomous systems, where safety and diagnostic capabilities are paramount.
- **Advancement in Learning from Human Data:** The approach of effectively extracting task structure (temporal graph) and motion priors (tracks) from action-free human videos could inspire further research in leveraging diverse, readily available human demonstration data for robot learning.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The authors acknowledge limitations including potential failures outside the defined temporal graph, susceptibility to perception errors, and applicability mainly to triplet-structured tasks (e.g., not deformable object manipulation). Future work is suggested to explore state relocalization beyond `c_0` and richer object representations. Despite these, TTS represents a contribution towards building more reliable, interpretable, and data-efficient robotic manipulation systems for long-horizon tasks.
