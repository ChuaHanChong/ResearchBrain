# Robust Fall Recovery for Armless Bipedal-Wheeled Robots Via Force-Guided Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> The Force-guided Teacher-student framework with Stage-wise Rewards (FTSR) enables robust fall recovery for armless bipedal-wheeled robots from diverse, unpredictable postures and terrains. The method achieved a 100% success rate in real-world outdoor trials for the JiaRan robot and effectively generalized to a high-DOF humanoid.

## Problem

- Armless bipedal-wheeled robots lack external support from arms or additional legs, making fall recovery from various prone positions challenging.
- Existing data-driven fall recovery methods often converge prematurely to suboptimal "dead points" when applied to these robots, leading to reduced success rates.
- The limited joint actuation in the lower body without upper-body support hinders efficient policy acquisition and real-world robustness for fall recovery tasks.

## Method

- The problem is formalized as a Constrained Markov Decision Process (CMDP) where external auxiliary forces and torques are treated as optimizable constraints, guiding the robot without explicit trajectories.
- A teacher-student architecture is employed, allowing the student policy to distill privileged information from a teacher model to enhance learning stability and robustness using only proprioceptive observations.
- Height-progressive stage-wise rewards are implemented to dynamically update target base heights, guiding the robot through distinct recovery phases from upper body erection to standing-up and subsequent walking.

## Results

- The FTSR framework achieved high simulation success rates, including **99.8%** on flat ground and **99.5%** on slopes, demonstrating superior performance over ablated methods.
- In real-world outdoor experiments, the JiaRan bipedal-wheeled robot demonstrated a **100%** success rate across 15 trials on diverse terrains, consistently recovering from varied initial poses and resuming locomotion.
- The framework successfully generalized to a 23-DOF Unitree humanoid, learning stable and smooth recovery motions from arbitrary poses, outperforming comparative methods in foot control and motion smoothness.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Formulating external auxiliary forces and torques as optimizable constraints within a CMDP effectively prevents premature convergence to local optima, enabling the policy to intrinsically develop recovery strategies.
> - Dynamically updated height-progressive stage-wise rewards provide a stable and efficient learning curriculum, guiding the robot through complex, multi-phase recovery processes.
> - A teacher-student architecture effectively distills complex recovery dynamics and privileged information, leading to robust and adaptable policies deployable with limited sensory inputs.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Bipedal-wheeled robots represent a class of robotic systems that integrate the terrain adaptability of legged locomotion with the efficiency of wheeled mobility. Despite advances in legged navigation across complex terrains, these robots remain susceptible to falls in unpredictable real-world environments, often leading to arbitrary postures. The ability to autonomously recover from such falls is a critical aspect of their operational autonomy and reliability.

Historically, fall recovery for legged robots has been addressed through model-based strategies. Early methods involved executing precomputed trajectories, often derived from motion capture or trajectory optimization. While deterministic, these approaches were limited by their sensitivity to initial poses and lacked the robustness required for diverse falling configurations.

In response to these limitations, research has shifted towards data-driven frameworks, predominantly utilizing reinforcement learning (RL) and imitation learning. Initial data-driven methods focused on training from simplified initial poses, replacing model-based optimization with end-to-end policy learning to improve success rates and motion robustness. Subsequent work extended these capabilities to more complex scenarios, including recovery from multiple poses and across varied terrains. Many of these contemporary approaches often employ staged training frameworks or curriculum learning, decomposing the complex recovery task into progressively difficult phases. For instance, some methods incorporate force curricula that apply and gradually phase out external upward assistance to enhance training and accelerate convergence. Imitation learning also contributes by generating human-like recovery motions from expert demonstrations.

However, a significant challenge persists for armless bipedal-wheeled robots. Unlike humanoids or quadrupeds that can leverage arms or multiple legs for support during recovery, armless bipedal-wheeled robots must rely exclusively on the actuation of their lower body and legs. Existing reward-based methods, when applied to these robots, have been observed to converge prematurely to local optima, sometimes referred to as "dead points," especially in complex terrains or under demanding constraints. This premature convergence can result in reduced recovery success rates or complete failure, primarily because the limited joint actuation without upper-body support exacerbates the problem, undermining both learning efficiency and real-world robustness. This paper directly addresses this specific gap by proposing a framework tailored for robust fall recovery in armless bipedal-wheeled robots.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable robust and adaptive fall recovery for armless bipedal-wheeled robots from diverse, unpredictable postures and terrains, without relying on supportive assistance from arms or additional legs.

The motivation for this work stems from the inherent difficulties faced by armless bipedal-wheeled robots in fall recovery scenarios. These robots, by design, lack upper-body appendages or the multi-leg coordination capabilities available to humanoids or quadrupeds. Consequently, they must generate all necessary forces and torques for recovery solely through the actuation of their legs, which limits their ability to reorient and stabilize from various prone positions.

Existing data-driven fall recovery methods, primarily reward-based, often struggle with this specific robotic embodiment. When applied to armless bipedal-wheeled robots, these methods are prone to premature convergence to suboptimal local equilibria, termed "dead points." These dead points represent stable but undesirable postures from which the robot cannot successfully complete the recovery process. This issue is particularly pronounced when training constraints are too severe or convergence conditions too demanding, leading to inefficient learning and poor generalization in real-world applications. The concentration of limited joint actuation in the lower body, without the balancing and supporting assistance of an upper body, exacerbates this problem, making policy acquisition difficult and unreliable.

To address these challenges, the authors propose the FTSR (Force-guided Teacher-student framework with Stage-wise Rewards) framework. The core motivations behind its design are:
- To prevent premature convergence to dead points by introducing a mechanism that provides guidance during the initial phases of learning without explicitly prescribing recovery trajectories.
- To allow the policy to autonomously discover effective recovery strategies by gradually reducing external assistance.
- To ensure stable and progressive posture refinement throughout the recovery process and a seamless transition to sustained locomotion.
- To validate the developed recovery capabilities on a physical armless bipedal-wheeled robot under varied real-world conditions.
- To demonstrate the potential generalizability of the proposed framework to other complex robotic platforms, such as high-DOF humanoids.

By explicitly formulating auxiliary forces as optimizable constraints and structuring the learning process with progressive rewards and privileged information, the research aims to overcome the unique limitations of armless bipedal-wheeled robots in fall recovery.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology is the Force-guided Teacher-student framework with Stage-wise Rewards (FTSR). This framework integrates three key components: a Constrained Markov Decision Process (CMDP) formulation for force-guided learning, a teacher-student architecture for efficient policy acquisition, and height-progressive stage-wise rewards for structured recovery.

**A. Constrained Markov Decision Process (CMDP)**
The problem is formalized as a CMDP, an extension of the Markov Decision Process that includes additional constraints, such as safety or resource limitations, alongside the objective of maximizing cumulative reward. A CMDP is defined by the tuple ⟨S, A, P, R, γ, C, d⟩, where S is the state space, A is the action space, P is the state transition probability, R is the immediate reward function, γ is the discount factor, C represents *m* constraint cost functions, and d specifies the allowable upper limits for each constraint cost. The goal is to learn an optimal policy π\* that maximizes the expected discounted return J(π) while ensuring that the expected return of each constraint J_C_i(π) remains below its limit d_i.

To solve this optimization problem, the authors adapt the Constrained Policy Optimization (CPO) algorithm. CPO integrates constraints into a trust-region framework, enabling larger, more stable update steps. Rather than solving constrained quadratic programs as in the original CPO, the authors employ a penalty function method to transform the optimization into a simpler form that includes only a KL-divergence term. This modified objective function is then optimized using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO).

**B. Force-Guided Fall Recovery Training**
The core innovation, "force-guided learning," addresses the issue of premature convergence in armless robots by formulating external auxiliary forces (F) and torques (T) as optimizable constraints within the CMDP, rather than as heuristically decaying curricula. This approach allows the policy to gradually reduce its dependency on external assistance as it develops internal, physically feasible recovery strategies.

The external force F and torque T are dynamically calculated based on the robot's real-time height (h) relative to a target height (h_cmd_i) and its current orientation (R) relative to a target orientation (R_tag). Specifically, F is proportional to `(1 - e^(-μ(h_cmd_i - h)))`, where μ is a height coefficient, ensuring that the force diminishes as the robot approaches the target height. A time coefficient `t_coeff = 1 - t/t_step_tag` is also incorporated, which phases out the force assistance by a specific time step `t_step_tag`. The torque T is derived from the logarithmic map of the relative rotation matrix `R_tag R^-1`, guiding the robot towards an upright orientation.

These computed forces and torques are then integrated into the CMDP as two constraint conditions: C1 = F and C2 = T. The objective of the training process is to drive these external forces and torques to zero, thus achieving autonomous recovery. This is implemented by setting the constraint limits d_i to zero at the specified `t_step_tag`. The advantage functions for both rewards and constraints are computed using Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) and standardized to enhance training stability.

To accelerate convergence and improve stability, the framework incorporates a teacher-student architecture. A teacher encoder (E_t) processes "privileged" information, which includes contact forces, base height, and full body state. A student encoder (E_s) learns to replicate the teacher's representations using only "proprioceptive" observations, consisting of angular velocity, gravity projection, user commands, joint positions and velocities, and previous actions over a history window (H steps). An "or" module directs either the teacher's or student's latent representations to shared Actor-Critic networks, which then output actions and value estimates. The student encoder is trained to minimize the Mean Squared Error (MSE) between its output and the teacher encoder's output, utilizing all generated trajectories. The Actor-Critic networks are trained using a PPO-like objective function, which incorporates the mixed advantage function combining reward and constraint terms for both teacher and student groups.

**C. Height-Progressive Stage-Wise Rewards**
To address training instability associated with fixed target heights in prior staged reward designs, FTSR employs height-progressive stage-wise rewards. These rewards dynamically update target base heights based on batch statistics of agent heights, guiding the robot through distinct recovery phases. The reward functions (Table II) are structured into three progressive stages:
1. **Upper Body Erection (r_u):** The initial phase focuses on raising the robot's upper body from a prone position to an upright posture. This is primarily driven by a gravity projection penalty and targets a base height h_cmd1. This stage is active until a significant proportion of agents (two-thirds) surpass h_cmd1.
2. **Standing-up (r_s):** Activated when two-thirds of the agents exceed h_cmd1, this phase encourages the robot to retract its lower body, bringing leg joints to angles resembling an initial standing configuration for a stable pose. The target base height is updated to h_cmd2.
3. **Walking (r_w):** This final phase begins when two-thirds of the agents surpass h_cmd2. It promotes normal locomotion after standing up, preventing the robot from remaining stationary, with the target base height further updated to h_cmd3.

In addition to standard reward terms, specific novel terms (highlighted in red in Table II) were designed for this problem:
- **Termination Penalty:** Agents remaining idle (contacting the ground for over 10 seconds) are terminated with a penalty.
- **Posture Penalties:** Penalties are applied for unnatural postures, such as aerial motion (both wheels off the ground), excessive changes in leg spacing, and asymmetric leg poses.
- **Leg-Usage Reward:** A reward encourages leg utilization during standing by penalizing deviations of wheel contact force from the robot's total weight.

Training is initialized from a basic model pre-trained with the walking reward (r_w) for approximately 200 iterations to establish elementary walking, preventing convergence issues in long-horizon tasks. Domain randomization (Table III) is applied to parameters like PD gains, CoM offset, and friction, along with randomizing initial base poses and applying additional perturbations, to enhance robustness and facilitate sim-to-real transfer. Training is conducted across five diverse terrain types, including flat ground, slopes, pyramid steps, uneven terrain, and rough surfaces.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of the FTSR framework was evaluated through extensive simulation experiments and real-world deployments on a physical armless bipedal-wheeled robot, as well as generalization tests on a high-DOF humanoid.

**Simulation Results:**
Training was conducted in IsaacGym using an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4070 Super GPU, involving 4,000 parallel environments (3,000 teacher and 1,000 student agents) over 8,000 iterations. The policy operated at 200 Hz and was updated at 50 Hz. Performance was assessed using metrics from prior research, including recovery success rate (E_succ), foot travel distance (E_feet), motion smoothness (E_smth), and energy consumption (E_engy).

**Ablation Studies (Table IV):**
- **Height-Progressive Stage-Wise Rewards (SWR):** The absence of SWR ("Ours w/o SWR") resulted in a 0.0% success rate across all terrains. This indicates that without the phased guidance and adaptive target height updates, the policy was unable to achieve stable standing, often collapsing after initial assistance. In contrast, the full FTSR framework ("Ours") achieved high success rates (99.8% on ground, 99.5% on slope, 99.8% on step) with comparatively low foot travel, high smoothness, and reasonable energy consumption. Figure 3 illustrates that FTSR's height reward curve reached its extremum more rapidly, signifying faster and more stable convergence. Figure 4 further supports this, showing FTSR achieving stable locomotion and high recovery success by 4,000 iterations.
- **Force-Guided Learning:** The conventional force curriculum approach ("Ours w/ Force Curr.") failed completely (0.0% success rate). A variant without explicit force-guided constraints ("Ours w/o Force Guid.") achieved reasonable success rates (97.4% on ground) but exhibited poorer motion quality with higher foot travel and lower smoothness (e.g., E_smth of 12.4 vs 5.80 on ground) and higher energy consumption. An excessive penalty multiplier in the force-guided mechanism ("Ours-Force0.02") also led to policy degradation (87.8% success on ground). The proposed force-guided method ("Ours") demonstrated rapid convergence, with external assistance removed after 3,000 iterations and a stable, rapid decay of auxiliary forces (Figure 3). The policy achieved force-free standing by 3,000 iterations and maintained high success rates with minimal foot adjustments by 4,000 iterations (Figure 4).
- **Teacher-Student (T-S) Architecture:** Training with proprioception alone ("Ours w/o T-S") yielded limited success (59.0% on ground) and failed on more complex terrains (slope, step). The full FTSR ("Ours") achieved comparable performance to a strong teacher model ("Ours w/ RMA Tea."), demonstrating that the teacher-student architecture effectively distills privileged knowledge of force effects and recovery dynamics, thereby enhancing robustness and adaptability.

**Generalization to Humanoid:** The FTSR framework was successfully applied to a 23-DOF Unitree humanoid (Figure 5). After 8,000 iterations, the method learned a policy for standing up from arbitrary poses, producing stable and smooth recovery motions. Comparative evaluation indicated superior foot control and motion smoothness compared to the HoST method.

**Real-World Experiments:**
The trained policy was deployed on the self-developed JiaRan bipedal-wheeled robot.
- **Diverse Poses and Terrains:** The policy was evaluated for standing recovery across various initial poses (front, back, side, and a "dead-point" pose, which caused failure in force-curriculum methods) and on diverse terrains, including indoor flat ground, slopes, steps (Figure 6), and outdoor environments such as grass, slopes, and gravel paths (Figure 7). The robot consistently demonstrated robust recovery and strong environmental adaptability.
- **Quantitative Success Rates (Outdoor):** On outdoor terrains, the method achieved a 100% success rate across 15 trials (5/5 on grass, 5/5 on slope, 5/5 on stairs) (Table V), confirming its real-world reliability.
- **Post-Recovery Locomotion:** To verify the retention of locomotor capability, the robot was subjected to kicking disturbances on grass. After losing balance and falling due to the external force, the policy autonomously initiated recovery to a standing pose and subsequently continued executing motion commands (Figure 8). This demonstrated the policy's ability to seamlessly transition from recovery to sustained locomotion.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant advancement in the field of legged robot autonomy, particularly for armless bipedal-wheeled robots. The proposed Force-guided Teacher-student framework with Stage-wise Rewards (FTSR) addresses a critical gap by enabling robust fall recovery in a challenging robotic embodiment that lacks external support from arms or additional legs.

The primary significance of this work lies in its novel methodological contributions:
- **Force-Guided Learning as Optimizable Constraints:** By formulating external auxiliary forces and torques as optimizable constraints within a Constrained Policy Optimization framework, the method prevents the common issue of premature convergence to suboptimal "dead points." This approach allows the policy to intrinsically develop effective recovery strategies by gradually reducing its dependence on external assistance, contrasting with heuristic force curricula.
- **Height-Progressive Stage-Wise Rewards:** The introduction of dynamically updated target base heights based on agent-level height statistics ensures stable and progressive training. This structured reward system effectively guides the robot through distinct phases of recovery, from initial body erection to stable standing and subsequent locomotion.
- **Teacher-Student Architecture for Efficient Learning:** The integration of a teacher-student model enables the student policy to effectively distill privileged information from the teacher, accelerating convergence and enhancing the stability and robustness of the learned recovery dynamics, even when operating with only proprioceptive sensors in deployment.

The comprehensive empirical validation, encompassing extensive simulation and real-world experiments, underscores the practical applicability and reliability of FTSR. The successful deployment on a physical armless bipedal-wheeled robot, the JiaRan, across diverse initial poses and challenging indoor and outdoor terrains, demonstrates the method's strong environmental adaptability and robustness. Furthermore, the ability of the robot to resume locomotion commands immediately after recovery highlights the policy's capacity to maintain full post-recovery motion capability.

The demonstrated generalizability of the FTSR framework to a high-DOF humanoid robot suggests its broader potential utility across various legged platforms, indicating that the underlying principles of force-guided learning and structured reward shaping are not confined to the specific bipedal-wheeled morphology.

The potential impact of this research is substantial. By endowing armless bipedal-wheeled robots with robust fall recovery capabilities, FTSR enhances their operational autonomy and reliability in real-world environments where falls are inevitable. This could lead to the deployment of such robots in more complex and unstructured settings for tasks such as inspection, delivery, or assistance, where stability and resilience are paramount. The framework provides a foundational approach for developing more advanced and resilient behaviors for legged robots, paving the way for future research into dynamic and adaptive locomotion in challenging conditions. The authors indicate future work will focus on testing the utility of the framework on broader platforms.
