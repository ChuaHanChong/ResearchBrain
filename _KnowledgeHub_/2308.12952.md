# BridgeData V2: A Dataset for Robot Learning at Scale

> [!summary] Summary
> BridgeData V2 is a large-scale, diverse dataset for robot learning, comprising over 60,000 real-world robot trajectories collected across 24 environments and 13 skills using a low-cost robot. Policies trained on this dataset generalize to unseen objects and environments and can transfer to independent institutions with varied setups, demonstrating the benefits of data scale and diversity.

## Problem

- Existing robot learning datasets are often small, lack diversity across tasks and environments, and are limited to specific scenarios or hardware, hindering generalization.
- Many large robot datasets rely on proprietary or expensive robots, creating a barrier to entry for most academic research labs.
- Prior datasets often lack compatibility with a wide range of learning methods or are not designed to explicitly facilitate generalization across multiple environments or unseen objects.

## Method

- Introduces BridgeData V2, a large-scale dataset comprising over 60,096 robot trajectories covering 13 manipulation skills across 24 distinct environments and over 100 objects.
- Utilizes a low-cost, publicly available WidowX 250 robot arm (approx. $4,000) for data collection to maximize accessibility for other research institutions.
- Employs a multi-task data collection protocol with systematic randomization of camera poses, objects, and workspace, alongside post-hoc natural language labeling, to enhance generalization and support open-vocabulary task specification.

## Results

- Evaluated methods learned a variety of tasks, with RT-1 achieving 49% average success on seen tasks compared to LCBC's 23%, due to its larger input images and action discretization.
- Policies demonstrated generalization to unseen objects and environments, for example, GCBC achieved 60% average success on unseen tasks, while RT-1 achieved 50%.
- Policies trained on BridgeData V2 showed successful zero-shot transfer to an independent institution, with RT-1 maintaining 40% average success compared to 47% in the original lab, demonstrating cross-institutional usability.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Performance scales with model capacity and dataset size, and importantly, increased skill diversity leads to positive transfer and improved generalization for fundamental tasks.
> - Policies trained on the dataset can achieve non-zero success when transferred zero-shot to entirely different institutions, demonstrating robustness to domain shifts.
> - Designing environments to allow many feasible tasks, rather than requiring pre-defined ones, forces policies to rely on explicit task specifications, enabling open-vocabulary learning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot learning has seen significant advancements, yet it often lags behind other AI domains like computer vision (CV) and natural language processing (NLP) in terms of generalization capabilities. The success of large models trained on massive, diverse datasets in CV (e.g., ImageNet) and NLP (e.g., GPT) has inspired a similar "data-driven" paradigm for robotics. However, applying this recipe to robotics is fraught with challenges.

**Current Limitations in Robot Learning Datasets:**

- **Scale and Diversity:** Many existing robot datasets are relatively small, focusing on a single task or a limited number of environments (e.g., MIME, RoboTurk, BC-Z). This limits the ability of trained policies to generalize to novel conditions, which is crucial for real-world deployment.
- **Single-Behavior Focus:** Some large datasets specialize in a single behavior like grasping or pushing, providing depth but lacking the breadth for multi-skill generalization.
- **Proprietary Hardware:** Datasets collected on proprietary or custom-built robots (e.g., RT-1 from Google DeepMind, BC-Z which uses a less accessible Google robot) are difficult for independent researchers to replicate and utilize without significant investment. This creates a barrier to entry for many academic labs.
- **Single-Environment Limitation:** Even multi-task datasets often operate within a single environment (e.g., RoboTurk, MT-Opt), requiring other researchers to perfectly replicate the scene to use the data, hindering cross-environment generalization.
- **Simulation vs. Real-World:** While simulated environments and datasets (e.g., Meta-World, ManiSkill) offer scale and control, they struggle to capture the full complexity, variability, and nuances of the real world (e.g., lighting, object physics, textures), making robust evaluation of generalization difficult.
- **Limited Method Compatibility:** Most prior robotic manipulation datasets have been evaluated with only one or a few specific learning methods (e.g., text-conditional behavioral cloning), limiting their versatility for the broader research community.

**BridgeData V2's Position:**

BridgeData V2 directly addresses these critical limitations by building upon and significantly expanding the original Bridge Dataset. It positions itself as a crucial stepping stone towards scalable, generalizable robot learning by:

- **Scale and Diversity:** Providing a substantially larger and more diverse dataset in terms of tasks, environments, and objects than its predecessor and many contemporaries.
- **Accessibility:** Leveraging a publicly available, low-cost robot (WidowX 250, ~$4,000) that is readily acquirable by a wide range of academic labs, significantly lowering the barrier to entry for real-world robot learning research.
- **Multi-Environment Generalization:** Intentionally collecting data across 24 distinct environments to explicitly facilitate generalization beyond the training setup, enabling researchers to use the data in their own labs with different scene configurations.
- **Open-Vocabulary Task Specification:** Designing the dataset to support flexible task conditioning via both goal images and natural language instructions, enabling a broader range of applications and more intuitive human-robot interaction.
- **Broad Method Compatibility:** Uniquely validating its utility by evaluating six different state-of-the-art offline learning methods (including both imitation learning and offline reinforcement learning, goal-conditioned and language-conditioned policies), demonstrating its versatility.
- **Scaling Analysis:** Providing empirical evidence of how performance scales with dataset size, diversity (skill diversity in particular), and model capacity, guiding future research directions.

In essence, BridgeData V2 carves out a unique niche by offering a highly accessible, large-scale, and diverse real-world dataset designed for *generalization* and *broad applicability* across learning methods and research institutions. It aims to be the ImageNet or GPT-like foundational dataset that the robot manipulation community needs to unlock the next generation of general-purpose robotic skills.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of BridgeData V2 is to **facilitate and accelerate research in scalable robot learning methods**, particularly those aimed at achieving broad generalization in robotic manipulation. The authors are motivated by the success of large, diverse datasets in fields like computer vision and natural language processing, and they aim to apply a similar paradigm to robotics.

More specifically, the key objectives and motivations driving the creation and release of BridgeData V2 can be broken down as follows:

1. **Achieve Broad Generalization:** The core motivation is to enable robotic systems to learn skills that generalize across a wide variety of real-world conditions, including novel tasks, objects, environments, and even different institutional setups (other research labs). This contrasts with many existing datasets that lead to policies brittle to minor changes.
2. **Enable Dataset Reusability and Accessibility:** Recognizing the time and cost associated with collecting large robot datasets, the authors aimed to create a resource that is reusable outside their own institutions. This meant:
 - **Public Availability:** Making the dataset publicly accessible.
 - **Low-Cost Hardware:** Using a widely available, affordable robot arm (WidowX 250, ~$4,000) to ensure that any researcher can acquire the necessary hardware to utilize the dataset for their experiments. This directly addresses the limitation of datasets relying on proprietary or expensive robots.
3. **Support Flexible Task Specification (Open-Vocabulary Learning):** The dataset is designed to allow policies to be commanded for new tasks using either goal images or natural language instructions. This "open-vocabulary" approach is crucial for practical, user-friendly robotic systems that can adapt to novel, un-preprogrammed requests. To support this, data was collected in scenes where many tasks were possible, forcing policies to learn to pay attention to the task specification rather than inferring the task from the initial scene.
4. **Demonstrate Compatibility with Diverse Learning Methods:** Unlike most prior datasets that are often validated with only one or a few specific algorithms, a key objective was to show that BridgeData V2 is compatible with, and beneficial for, a wide range of state-of-the-art offline robot learning methods. This includes various forms of imitation learning (behavioral cloning, diffusion-based, action chunking) and offline reinforcement learning, covering different policy architectures and learning paradigms. This versatility makes the dataset a more broadly useful resource.
5. **Provide Empirical Insights into Scaling Laws:** The authors aimed to analyze how the performance of learned policies scales with variations in model capacity, dataset size, and, crucially, **dataset diversity**. This includes exploring whether increased diversity in skills (beyond just objects) leads to improved generalization. Such insights are vital for guiding future research in scalable robot learning.
6. **Accelerate Research:** Ultimately, by providing a high-quality, accessible, and versatile dataset, along with pre-trained models, the authors aspire to accelerate the pace of innovation in the field of scalable robot learning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

BridgeData V2 is designed as a large and diverse dataset for robotic manipulation, specifically focusing on generalizable skills for a low-cost, publicly available robot.

**Dataset Scope and Composition:**
- **Scale:** The dataset comprises 60,096 trajectories in total. This includes 50,365 human-teleoperated expert demonstrations and 9,731 trajectories collected autonomously from a heavily randomized pick-and-place scripted policy. The scripted data, though often suboptimal, is included to boost robustness, particularly for methods like offline RL that can leverage noisy data.
- **Skills:** BridgeData V2 covers 13 distinct manipulation skills, ranging from foundational object repositioning (pick-and-place, pushing, reorienting) to more complex interactions with environments and tools (sweeping, folding, opening/closing doors/drawers/boxes, twisting knobs, flipping switches, zipping, turning levers, wiping surfaces). This broad range aims to foster generalizable behaviors.
- **Environments:** The data was collected across 24 distinct environments, including various toy kitchens (7 distinct ones), tabletops, standalone toy sinks, a toy laundry machine, and more. This environmental variability is critical for training policies that generalize beyond specific scene configurations.
- **Objects:** The dataset features manipulations involving over 100 different objects.
- **Data Types:** All trajectories include RGB images from multiple camera views and natural language labels. Portions of the data also include depth information.

**System Setup (Hardware):**
- **Robot Arm:** The **WidowX 250 robot arm**, a low-cost and publicly available system (approximately $4,000 total setup cost), was chosen to maximize accessibility for other research labs.
- **Sensors:**
 - One **Intel RealSense D435 RGBD camera** for a fixed over-the-shoulder view (primary camera).
 - Two **Logitech C920 webcams** for additional RGB views, with their poses randomized during collection.
 - One **Raspberry Pi camera module** attached to the robot's wrist for a gripper-centric view.
- **Control:** The robot operates at a 5 Hz control frequency.
- **Image Resolution:** Images are saved at 640x480 resolution, though experiments often downsample to 128x128 (except RT-1 which uses 320x256).

**Data Collection Protocol:**
- **Teleoperation:** Data was collected by teleoperating the robot using a Meta Quest 2 VR headset.
- **Multi-Task in Environment:** A key design principle was to set up environments with *many possible feasible tasks* (e.g., a kitchen with various food items and a drawer). Data collectors were then free to perform any task, without requiring pre-defined task names or resets between trajectories. This ensures policies must rely on task specification rather than inferring the task from initial observations.
- **Randomization:** To enhance generalization, randomization was systematically introduced:
 - Camera poses were randomized approximately every 50 trajectories.
 - Objects in the scene were swapped out regularly.
 - The workspace position relative to the robot was randomized.
- **Automated Data Collection:** A heavily randomized scripted pick-and-place policy was used to autonomously collect a significant portion of the pick-and-place data. While this data is often suboptimal and contains failures, it provides large-scale coverage of object repositioning, which is valuable for robust learning, particularly for offline RL methods.
- **Post-hoc Labeling:** Task names were not provided during collection. Instead, crowdsourcing was used to label trajectories with natural language descriptions of the performed tasks after data collection.

**Evaluation Methodology (Offline Learning Methods):**
To demonstrate the dataset's versatility, the authors evaluated six state-of-the-art offline robot learning methods:
- **Goal-Conditioned Methods (using goal images):**
 - **Behavioral Cloning with Goals (GCBC):** A standard baseline using a ResNet-34 encoder and fully connected layers.
 - **Diffusion Behavioral Cloning (D-GCBC):** Modifies GCBC to use a diffusion process for policy representation, based on DDPM.
 - **Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT):** Predicts sequences of actions using transformers and a conditional VAE, adapted from its original single-task design to be goal-conditioned.
 - **Contrastive RL (CRL):** A reinforcement learning method that casts goal-conditioned RL as a representation learning problem, using log-linear value functions.
- **Language-Conditioned Methods (using natural language instructions):**
 - **Behavioral Cloning with Language (LCBC):** A baseline using a ResNet-34 with FiLM conditioning on MUSE sentence embeddings for language.
 - **RT-1 (Robotics Transformer):** A large transformer model developed by Google DeepMind, trained via behavioral cloning, which uses tokenized images (EfficientNet) and language (pre-trained LM) and predicts discretized robot actions. Notably, RT-1 uses observation histories and a higher image resolution.

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Action Space:** 7D continuous (6D Cartesian end-effector relative pose changes + discrete gripper open/close).
- **Observation Space:** 128x128 RGB images (over-the-shoulder view), except for RT-1 at 320x256.
- **Metrics:** Success rates averaged over 10 trials, with object and distractor positions varied.
- **Experiments Conducted:**
 - Performance evaluation on tasks *seen* in the training data (testing skill acquisition and generalization to novel instances).
 - Performance evaluation on tasks requiring generalization to *unseen* objects, environments, or both.
 - Cross-institution evaluation: Testing policies trained on BridgeData V2 at a second, independent institution (Lab 2) to assess generalization under domain shift (differences in robot setup, camera placement, lighting, object instances).
 - Scaling analysis: Investigating the impact of model size, dataset size, and dataset diversity (specifically skill diversity) on policy performance.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted on BridgeData V2 yielded several key findings, demonstrating the dataset's utility for scalable robot learning and providing valuable insights into generalization:

1. **Learning a Broad Range of Tasks (Seen Tasks):**
 - All evaluated methods were able to learn a variety of tasks seen in the training data.
 - The goal-conditioned methods (GCBC, D-GCBC, ACT, CRL) showed comparable average success rates (around 41-49%) across the seen tasks.
 - Among the language-conditioned methods, **RT-1 significantly outperformed the LCBC baseline** (49% vs. 23% average success). This superior performance is attributed to RT-1's design choices, including larger input images, action discretization, and the use of observation histories.
 - Qualitatively, diffusion-based (D-GCBC), chunking (ACT), and transformer-based (RT-1) policies exhibited more natural, less jerky behaviors, often including realistic pauses observed in demonstrations. D-GCBC, lacking observation histories, occasionally showed oscillations.

2. **Generalization to New Objects and Environments (Unseen Tasks):**
 - The large and diverse training set enabled a considerable degree of generalization, with most methods achieving non-zero success rates on tasks involving unseen objects, unseen environments, or both. This is a strong validation of BridgeData V2's design for broad generalization.
 - For example, GCBC achieved 60% average success on unseen tasks, and RT-1 achieved 50%, while LCBC struggled with 8% average success.
 - **Language-conditioned methods (especially LCBC) struggled on tasks involving unseen objects**, as the object names were not grounded in the training data. This highlights a current limitation for language-conditioned policies when encountering entirely novel entities. RT-1 still fared much better than LCBC in these scenarios.

3. **Cross-Institution Usability:**
 - A critical finding was that policies trained on BridgeData V2 successfully transferred to a second, independent institution (Lab 2) with different robot setup, camera placement, lighting conditions, and object instances.
 - While performance generally degraded somewhat compared to the original lab (e.g., GCBC average dropped from 30% to 13%), **all methods still achieved non-zero success rates in Lab 2**.
 - **RT-1 demonstrated remarkable robustness**, showing only a small degradation in performance (47% in Lab 1 to 40% in Lab 2). This demonstrates that BridgeData V2 is indeed a useful resource for researchers outside the collecting institution, enabling zero-shot transfer under significant domain shift. The authors note that fine-tuning with a small amount of new data in the new lab would likely improve performance further.

4. **Scaling Analysis (Model Size, Dataset Size, and Dataset Diversity):**
 - **Model Size:** Experiments with GCBC showed that higher capacity models (e.g., ResNet-50 vs. ResNet-18 image encoders) consistently lead to strictly better performance, suggesting that BridgeData V2 can effectively leverage larger models.
 - **Dataset Size:** Performance for GCBC improved with increasing dataset size for both seen and unseen tasks, confirming the intuition that more data generally leads to better performance.
 - **Dataset Diversity (Skills):** A significant finding was that training GCBC on a dataset with greater **skill diversity** (13 skills) led to significantly improved performance on an unseen pick-and-place task, even when the total number of trajectories was approximately the same as a dataset with only 3 skills. This indicates **positive transfer** between different skills, where learning a broader repertoire of actions enhances the robustness of fundamental skills like pick-and-place. This corroborates prior findings on object diversity (Brohan et al.) and extends them to skill diversity.

These results collectively validate BridgeData V2 as a robust and versatile dataset for advancing scalable robot learning, particularly emphasizing the benefits of data scale and diversity for generalization.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

BridgeData V2 represents a significant contribution to the field of robot learning, particularly in accelerating research towards more general-purpose robotic systems. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Democratizing Robot Learning Research:** By collecting a large-scale, diverse dataset using a **low-cost and publicly available robot arm**, BridgeData V2 significantly lowers the barrier to entry for real-world robot learning research. This accessibility enables a much broader range of academic institutions and researchers, who may not have access to expensive proprietary hardware, to participate in and contribute to the development of scalable robot learning methods. This is a critical step towards democratizing access to cutting-edge robotics research.

2. **Enabling Generalization Across Domains and Institutions:** The dataset's extensive coverage of 24 environments, 13 skills, and over 100 objects, combined with its rigorous randomization during collection, is explicitly designed to foster generalization. The empirical demonstration that policies trained on BridgeData V2 can achieve non-zero success **across different institutions (zero-shot)**, despite variations in setup, lighting, and object instances, is a strong validation of this design. This capability is paramount for practical robotic deployment, where environments are rarely identical to training scenes.

3. **Versatile Benchmark for State-of-the-Art Methods:** Unlike many prior datasets tailored for specific algorithms, BridgeData V2 is shown to be compatible with a diverse array of state-of-the-art offline learning methods, including different forms of imitation learning and offline reinforcement learning, as well as goal-conditioned and language-conditioned policies. This versatility makes BridgeData V2 a valuable **standardized benchmark**, allowing researchers to compare different algorithmic approaches fairly on a common, challenging, and realistic dataset. This will undoubtedly accelerate algorithmic development.

4. **Guiding Future Research on Scaling Laws:** The empirical scaling analysis, which demonstrates performance improvements with larger models, more data, and crucially, **greater skill diversity**, provides crucial guidance for future research. The finding that diverse skills positively transfer and enhance the robustness of even foundational skills like pick-and-place suggests that collecting data on a broad range of behaviors, even if seemingly unrelated, can lead to more capable and generalized robots. This encourages a shift towards holistic data collection strategies.

5. **A Step Towards Foundation Models for Robotics:** Drawing inspiration from the success of large-scale datasets and models in CV and NLP, BridgeData V2 serves as a vital stepping stone towards the development of "foundation models" or "general-purpose policies" for robotics. By providing the raw material for training high-capacity models that can learn broadly generalizable manipulation skills, it paves the way for robots that can adapt to novel tasks and environments with minimal or no additional training.

6. **Catalyst for Open-Vocabulary Robot Learning:** The dataset's support for both goal images and natural language instructions for task specification is key to enabling more intuitive and flexible human-robot interaction. This "open-vocabulary" capability moves beyond pre-programmed tasks and allows users to command robots with natural language, akin to how we interact with large language models.

While the paper acknowledges limitations (e.g., focus on low-precision tasks, single-institution collection, single robot morphology), BridgeData V2's strengths overwhelmingly position it as a foundational resource. By publicly sharing the dataset and pre-trained models, the authors have made a significant contribution that is expected to stimulate a new wave of research and development in scalable, generalizable, and accessible robot learning.
