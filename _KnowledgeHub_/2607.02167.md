# Influence of Radial Basis Activation Functions on Intelligent Controller for Robotic Manipulators

> [!summary] Summary
> A study from the University of Turku systematically investigated the influence of Radial Basis Function (RBF) activation functions on an intelligent controller for robotic manipulators. Researchers found that the Inverse Multiquadratic (IMQ) kernel achieved up to a 51.9% reduction in RMS tracking error for continuous trajectories, while all RBF kernels introduced increased overshoot and settling times for discontinuous step-like commands.

## Problem

- Model-based nonlinear control techniques for robotic manipulators suffer from performance degradation due to parametric uncertainty, friction, unmodeled dynamics, and external disturbances.
- While RBF neural networks are used to enhance robustness, the choice of activation function is often a default (e.g., Gaussian) without systematic investigation into its impact on controller dynamics.
- There is a lack of understanding regarding how specific RBF kernel types influence the transient behavior, steady-state accuracy, and control smoothness of adaptive controllers.

## Method

- An intelligent control framework integrates ==feedback linearization== with an ==adaptive RBF neural network== to compensate for estimated dynamics and approximate lumped uncertainties in robotic manipulators.
- A ==Lyapunov-based adaptation law== with a ==projection algorithm== updates the RBF network weights, where the network approximates disturbances using **Gaussian**, **Inverse Multiquadratic (IMQ)**, and **Laplacian** activation functions.
- A rigorous comparison protocol was established, maintaining identical controller gains, neuron count, and experimental conditions to isolate the activation function as the sole variable influencing performance.

## Results

- For continuous sinusoidal trajectories, the Inverse Multiquadratic (IMQ) kernel achieved the most substantial improvement with a **51.9%** reduction in RMS error (**0.223 deg**) and the lowest Integral of Absolute Error (**12.65 deg·s**).
- For ramp-like triangular trajectories, the IMQ kernel again demonstrated superior performance, achieving a **49.0%** reduction in RMS error (**0.203 deg**) and the lowest IAE (**11.89 deg·s**).
- For step-like square trajectories, all neural intelligent controllers introduced increased overshoot (IMQ highest at **6.86%**) and longer settling times (Laplacian longest at **2.919 seconds**), despite generally reducing IAE compared to the baseline.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The selection of an RBF activation function is a consequential design parameter, as its properties (e.g., support, smoothness) directly influence the trade-off between tracking precision and transient response in intelligent control systems.
> - Kernels with global support, such as the Inverse Multiquadratic (IMQ) function, provide a more holistic and accurate approximation of disturbance manifolds, leading to superior steady-state tracking for smooth and ramp-like reference trajectories.
> - Localized kernels or neural adaptation in general can introduce increased transient aggression, manifesting as higher overshoot and longer settling times, when handling discontinuous commands like square waves.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Trajectory tracking for robotic manipulators is a fundamental problem often addressed using model-based nonlinear control techniques, such as feedback linearization. These methods leverage known system dynamics to cancel nonlinearities and achieve precise tracking under ideal conditions. However, practical applications often suffer from performance degradation due to factors like parametric uncertainty, friction, unmodeled dynamics, and external disturbances.

To enhance robustness against these real-world imperfections, the research community commonly integrates adaptive and learning-based extensions with model-based control. Adaptive control typically focuses on structured parametric uncertainties, using online estimation mechanisms and Lyapunov stability analysis to ensure boundedness of closed-loop signals. In parallel, learning-based approaches, particularly those employing neural networks, are utilized for approximating unmodeled or unstructured dynamics, such as complex friction models or residual coupling effects, owing to their universal approximation capabilities.

This work adopts a hybrid approach, combining the structured benefits of feedback linearization with the data-driven approximation power of Radial Basis Function (RBF) neural networks. While RBF networks are established tools, and Gaussian kernels are a default choice in many applications, a systematic investigation into how different RBF activation functions impact the dynamic characteristics of adaptive controllers has not been extensively documented. This study aims to fill this gap by exploring the direct influence of various kernel types on controller performance, thus contributing design-oriented insights to the field of intelligent control for robotics.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to systematically investigate how the choice of activation function within an RBF neural network influences the transient behavior, steady-state accuracy, and control smoothness of an intelligent controller designed for robotic manipulators. The underlying motivation stems from the observation that, while Lyapunov stability can be guaranteed for a broad class of bounded activation functions in RBF-based adaptive control, practical performance metrics such as speed of response, precision, and control effort can vary significantly depending on the specific kernel shape employed.

The authors note that Gaussian kernels are frequently adopted by default in RBF-based controllers without a thorough comparative analysis against alternatives. This study is motivated by the need to understand if the inherent properties of different kernels—such as their locality, smoothness, and support—directly affect the adaptation dynamics of the neural network and, consequently, the overall closed-loop performance of the robotic system. By isolating the activation function as the sole variable under identical controller tuning and experimental conditions, the research aims to demonstrate that kernel selection functions as a structural design parameter. The insights gained are intended to provide guidance for engineers in selecting activation functions to optimize for specific performance criteria in neural-network-assisted feedback linearization.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Intelligent Control Framework

The proposed intelligent control framework integrates a model-based nonlinear control strategy with an adaptive RBF neural network. The robotic manipulator's dynamics are represented by a standard n-DOF equation of motion, which is reformulated to highlight the estimated dynamics and a lumped uncertainty term.
The control law is based on feedback linearization, designed to compensate for estimated dynamics and the approximated disturbance. Specifically, the control input $u$ is formulated to drive a combined error $s = \dot{\tilde{q}} + \Lambda \tilde{q}$ to zero, where $\tilde{q}$ is the tracking error and $\Lambda$ is a positive definite diagonal matrix. The core of the intelligent aspect lies in the RBF neural network, which provides an online estimate $\hat{d}$ for the total uncertainty term $d$, encompassing parametric uncertainties, friction, and unmodeled dynamics.

#### 3.2. RBF Neural Network and Adaptation Law

The RBF neural network approximates the components of the disturbance term, with each component $\hat{d}_i$ expressed as a weighted sum of activation functions, $\hat{d}_i = w_i^\top \phi_i(s_i)$. Here, $w_i$ is a vector of adjustable weights, and $\phi_i$ is a vector of radial basis activation functions whose input is the combined error $s_i$.
The neural network weights are updated using a Lyapunov-based adaptation law. A positive-definite Lyapunov function is constructed, allowing the derivation of a weight update rule $\dot{w}_i = \eta_i s_i \phi_i$, where $\eta_i$ is a positive learning rate. To guarantee the boundedness of the adaptive weights and ensure stability, a projection algorithm is incorporated. This algorithm constrains the weights within a predefined convex region, ensuring that all closed-loop signals remain bounded and the tracking error ultimately converges to a compact region around the origin.

#### 3.3. Activation Functions Investigated

The study systematically compares three distinct radial basis activation functions:
- **Gaussian Kernel:** $\kappa_G(\rho) = \exp(-\rho^2)$. This is a commonly used kernel, characterized by its localized nature and smooth, bell-shaped response.
- **Inverse Multiquadratic (IMQ) Kernel:** $\kappa_{IMQ}(\rho) = 1 / \sqrt{1 + \rho^2}$. This kernel exhibits global support, meaning its influence extends across the entire input space, and it is also smooth.
- **Laplacian Kernel:** $\kappa_L(\rho) = \exp(-|\rho|)$. Similar to the Gaussian, this kernel is localized but features a sharper peak at its center and decays exponentially.
For all kernels, the normalized distance $\rho_{i,j}$ from the input to a neuron center $c_{i,j}$ scaled by a width parameter $\sigma_i$ is used as the argument.

#### 3.4. Kernel Parameterization and Comparison Protocol

To ensure a fair comparison, a rigorous protocol was established. All controller gains ($\lambda_i = 30$, $\eta_i = 10$), projection bounds ($\mu_i = 100$), and the number of neurons ($n_i = 6$) were kept identical across all experiments. The centers of the radial basis functions were uniformly distributed over a normalized interval, and a common width parameter $\sigma_i$ was applied using a tuning factor $\gamma$ that remained constant. The desired trajectories and all simulation/experimental conditions were also maintained consistently. This approach ensured that any observed performance differences could be directly attributed to the choice of the activation function.

#### 3.5. Experimental Setup

The experimental validation was conducted using a digital twin of the Quanser QArm robotic manipulator within the Quanser Interactive Labs environment. This setup allows for emulation of the physical arm's dynamics, including joint friction and actuator constraints. A single-degree-of-freedom (1-DOF) configuration of the manipulator was utilized, with specific physical parameters defined for mass, center of mass, length, and moment of inertia. Each trial lasted 62 seconds at a sampling frequency of 500 Hz. Standardized initial conditions were enforced by driving the manipulator to a home configuration and allowing it to settle before each experiment. Three distinct reference trajectories were employed to evaluate performance under different dynamic conditions: sinusoidal (smooth), square (step-like), and triangular (ramp-like) waveforms.

#### 3.6. Performance Metrics

The performance of the controllers was quantified using several metrics:
- **Root-Mean-Square (RMS) Tracking Error (RMS$_e$):** Measures the overall deviation from the desired trajectory, reported in degrees.
- **Integral of Absolute Error (IAE):** Quantifies the cumulative absolute tracking error over time, reported in degree-seconds.
- **RMS of Commanded PWM Signal (RMS$_{PWM}$):** Represents the control effort, normalized by actuator saturation limits.
For the step-like (square) reference, additional transient metrics were collected:
- **Overshoot:** The maximum deviation beyond the target value, expressed as a percentage of the step amplitude.
- **Settling Time:** The time required for the tracking error to remain within a specified $\pm2\%$ band around the commanded step.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation demonstrated that the choice of RBF activation function systematically influences the closed-loop performance, even though Lyapunov stability was preserved for all kernels tested.

#### 4.1. Sinusoidal Tracking

For smooth, continuous sinusoidal trajectories, all neural intelligent controllers significantly improved tracking accuracy compared to the baseline conventional feedback linearization controller (which had no neural disturbance estimator, i.e., $\eta=0$). The Inverse Multiquadratic (IMQ) kernel exhibited the most substantial improvement, achieving a 51.9% reduction in RMS error (0.223 deg) and the lowest IAE (12.65 deg·s) relative to the baseline (RMS$_e$ of 0.464 deg, IAE of 25.18 deg·s). The Laplacian kernel followed with a 39.2% RMS error reduction (0.282 deg), and the Gaussian kernel provided a 26.5% reduction (0.341 deg). The control effort, as measured by RMS$_{PWM}$, remained comparable across all intelligent controllers and was only slightly higher than the baseline (e.g., IMQ RMS$_{PWM}$ of 0.0391 vs. baseline 0.0381).

#### 4.2. Ramp-like Tracking (Triangular Reference)

Similar performance trends were observed for ramp-like triangular reference trajectories. The IMQ kernel again demonstrated superior performance, achieving a 49.0% reduction in RMS error (0.203 deg) and the lowest IAE (11.89 deg·s) compared to the baseline (RMS$_e$ of 0.398 deg, IAE of 20.86 deg·s). The Laplacian and Gaussian kernels also showed notable improvements, with RMS error reductions of 37.9% (0.247 deg) and 26.6% (0.292 deg), respectively. Control effort for the intelligent controllers was marginally higher than the baseline, consistent with the sinusoidal results.

#### 4.3. Step-like Tracking (Square Reference)

The square reference trajectory tests revealed a trade-off between steady-state accuracy and transient response. While the intelligent controllers generally improved the Integral of Absolute Error (IAE) compared to the baseline, they introduced increased overshoot and longer settling times. The Gaussian kernel provided the largest IAE reduction (49.09 deg·s, approximately 15.0% lower than baseline's 57.76 deg·s), yet it resulted in a 2.85% overshoot and a settling time of 2.567 seconds (compared to baseline's 0.00% overshoot and 1.277 seconds settling time). The IMQ kernel, despite its strong performance in continuous trajectories, exhibited the highest overshoot at 6.86% and a settling time of 2.624 seconds for the square reference. The Laplacian kernel also showed a considerable overshoot of 5.32% and the longest settling time of 2.919 seconds. Furthermore, the RMS tracking error (RMS$_e$) was slightly higher for all neural kernels (ranging from 3.106 to 3.142 deg) compared to the baseline (3.095 deg) for this discontinuous trajectory. Control effort also saw a minor increase for neural controllers.

#### 4.4. Discussion on Kernel Properties

The results indicate that kernels with global support, such as the Inverse Multiquadratic function, provided a more holistic and accurate approximation of the disturbance manifold, leading to superior steady-state tracking for smooth and ramp-like reference trajectories. This suggests improved generalization capability across the operating range. Conversely, localized kernels, while contributing to overall error reduction, tended to induce sharper adaptation dynamics that, under discontinuous commands (e.g., square waves), resulted in increased transient aggressiveness, manifesting as higher overshoot and longer settling times. This suggests that the adaptation mechanism around specific operating regions might become overactive or less damped when handling abrupt changes.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This study provides empirical evidence that the selection of an RBF activation function is a consequential design parameter in intelligent control systems for robotic manipulators, extending beyond a mere implementation detail. The work systematically demonstrates that kernel properties, specifically their support and smoothness characteristics, directly influence the trade-off between tracking precision and transient response.

The most significant finding is the consistent superior performance of the Inverse Multiquadratic (IMQ) kernel for continuous trajectories, achieving a substantial reduction in tracking error (up to 51.9% for sinusoidal and 49.0% for triangular references) while maintaining comparable control effort. This suggests that for applications demanding high steady-state accuracy on smooth paths, kernels with global support may be preferable due to their generalized disturbance approximation capability.

Conversely, the observation that all neural kernels introduced overshoot and increased settling times for discontinuous (step-like) commands highlights a critical design consideration. It implies that for applications where strictly monotonic step responses or minimal transient aggression are paramount, localized kernels or even the baseline controller without neural adaptation might be more suitable, or require careful re-tuning of the adaptive parameters.

The findings offer practical guidance for control engineers, enabling them to make informed decisions about activation function selection based on the specific performance requirements of a robotic system. It underscores that tuning for maximum steady-state precision might entail a compromise on transient robustness, and vice versa. This research contributes to a deeper understanding of how the internal structure of neural approximators shapes closed-loop dynamics, paving the way for more sophisticated and tailored intelligent controller designs. Future work, as indicated by the authors, will extend this analysis to multi-degree-of-freedom configurations and focus on developing systematic tuning strategies for kernels to balance competing performance objectives.
