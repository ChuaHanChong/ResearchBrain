# Manipulation by Feel: Touch-Based Control with Deep Predictive Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from UC Berkeley and Facebook AI developed Deep Tactile Model Predictive Control, a framework that uses high-resolution tactile sensors and deep predictive models to enable robots to perform complex manipulation tasks based solely on touch feedback. This method achieved an 86.6% success rate in die rolling and demonstrated superior performance over a hand-engineered baseline in ball repositioning and analog stick deflection tasks.

## Problem

- Existing robotic systems underutilize tactile sensing for continuous, non-prehensile manipulation, struggling with fine motor control due to sparse sensor data.
- Developing accurate physics models for complex contact dynamics involving friction and deformation is difficult and computationally intensive.
- Visual sensing is often insufficient for fine manipulation tasks due to occlusions at contact points by the robot's end-effector.

## Method

- A **Deep Tactile MPC** framework combines a high-resolution ==GelSight==-style tactile sensor with a ==deep recurrent convolutional network==.
- The deep network is autonomously trained on diverse exploratory interactions to predict ==future tactile observations==, implicitly learning complex contact dynamics.
- An ==MPC planner== uses the trained predictive model and ==Cross-Entropy Method (CEM)== to optimize action sequences, minimizing the pixel-wise ==Mean Squared Error (MSE)== to a user-specified goal tactile image.

## Results

- The deep predictive model accurately forecasted future GelSight sensor observations over multiple time steps, demonstrating effective learning of complex tactile dynamics.
- The Deep Tactile MPC framework successfully executed three distinct manipulation tasks (ball repositioning, analog stick deflection, 20-sided die rolling) relying solely on tactile feedback.
- Quantitatively, the method achieved a median L2 distance of **2.10 mm** for ball repositioning and **5.31 mm** for analog stick deflection, and an **86.6%** success rate in die rolling, consistently outperforming a hand-engineered centroid-based baseline across all tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - High-resolution tactile sensors, when combined with deep predictive models, allow robots to implicitly learn and leverage complex contact dynamics for fine manipulation.
> - Goal specification through desired tactile images simplifies interaction and offers a general, intuitive method for defining manipulation objectives without requiring explicit pose or force estimation.
> - Unsupervised learning from diverse exploratory interactions enables the system to acquire robust tactile control policies, reducing reliance on manual supervision or explicit reward functions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Dexterous robotic manipulation, particularly tasks requiring fine motor control and interaction with small objects, has long presented challenges in robotics. This research addresses a fundamental limitation in current robotic systems: the underutilization of tactile sensing for continuous, non-prehensile manipulation. Human dexterity, as evidenced by studies like Johansson et al. (2009) where anesthetized fingertips severely impaired fine motor tasks, relies heavily on touch to infer critical properties like friction, compliance, and mass, and to directly measure contact forces. Visual sensing alone is often insufficient for such tasks due to occlusions at contact points and its inherent physical remoteness.

Historically, exploiting tactile sensing in robots has been difficult for several reasons. First, available tactile sensing technologies have often been limited to sparse, pointwise force measurements, lacking the high spatial resolution and richness of biological skin. Second, developing accurate physics models for complex contacts and forces has remained largely elusive. Third, specifying desired manipulation behaviors in terms of tactile percepts has been ambiguous and challenging.

Prior work in touch-based control has explored various methods, from manual design of control laws and extraction of high-level features from touch sensors to reinforcement learning approaches that stabilize objects using low-dimensional tactile readings. Concurrently, advancements in deep learning have enabled progress in vision-based robotic control, where deep models predict future video frames to facilitate planning and control for tasks like object repositioning.

This paper positions itself at the intersection of these fields, leveraging two key recent advances to overcome the aforementioned difficulties: the development of high-resolution, deformable elastomer-based tactile sensors (such as GelSight) and the success of deep neural networks in modeling high-dimensional data for robotic control. The authors aim to bridge the gap between rich tactile data and effective control by proposing a data-driven approach that learns predictive models directly from raw tactile sensor inputs. Unlike previous works that used low-dimensional tactile data or focused solely on visual prediction, this research applies deep video prediction models to high-resolution tactile observations for repositioning freely-moving objects. It explores a novel method for specifying goals directly in the tactile observation space, a departure from traditional force-based or pose-based goal specifications. By integrating these components, the work seeks to provide a general-purpose control framework for complex, touch-driven manipulation tasks, particularly those where visual information is occluded or insufficient.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust and generalizable framework for touch-based robotic manipulation by combining high-resolution tactile sensing with deep predictive models. The authors aim to enable a robot to perform continuous, non-prehensile manipulation tasks by learning from unsupervised interaction, rather than relying on manual supervision or explicit reward functions.

The key motivations driving this work include:

- **Addressing limitations of existing tactile control:** Traditional tactile sensing often provides sparse, low-resolution data, making it difficult to achieve fine-grained control. The authors are motivated to utilize high-resolution tactile sensors (GelSight-style) to provide richer feedback, which is crucial for dexterous tasks.
- **Overcoming challenges with contact physics models:** Developing accurate physics models for complex, continuous contact dynamics, especially involving friction and deformation, is computationally intensive and often intractable. The motivation is to bypass this challenge by learning data-driven predictive models directly from raw sensor observations, allowing the system to implicitly capture intricate contact dynamics.
- **Enabling intuitive goal specification:** Defining desired manipulation outcomes in terms of forces or precise object poses can be complex and non-intuitive for users. The research is motivated by the idea of specifying goals directly as desired tactile sensor readings (images), which can be much more natural and informative for a user. This "tactile servoing" approach allows for general goal setting without requiring explicit object pose estimation.
- **Enabling manipulation under visual occlusion:** Many fine manipulation tasks involve the robot's end-effector occluding the object of interest, rendering visual feedback ineffective. The motivation is to develop a control system that can operate solely based on tactile information, making it suitable for such visually-occluded scenarios.
- **Reducing reliance on manual supervision:** The authors aim to demonstrate that effective tactile control policies can be learned from diverse, unsupervised exploratory interactions with the environment. This addresses the high cost and impracticality of providing manual supervision or reward functions for every manipulation task.
- **Demonstrating the potential of integrated technologies:** By combining state-of-the-art GelSight sensors with deep learning techniques for video prediction, the research seeks to illustrate the promise of these integrated technologies for solving previously intractable tactile manipulation problems.

In essence, the central objective is to demonstrate a generalizable, data-driven approach to high-precision tactile control that can maneuver objects into user-specified contact configurations, even in complex scenarios involving slipping, sliding, and re-establishing contact, purely from raw touch-based observations.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core methodology presented in this paper is **Deep Tactile Model Predictive Control (MPC)**, a framework designed to learn and execute touch-based manipulation policies directly from raw, high-resolution tactile sensor inputs.

#### 3.1. Hardware Setup

- **Tactile Sensor:** A custom-built GelSight-style elastomer-based sensor is utilized. This sensor has a diameter of 4 cm and incorporates a standard webcam to capture high-resolution images of surface deformations in the gel. This design offers high spatial resolution and a compliant contact surface.
- **Robot Platform:** The GelSight sensor is mounted on a modified miniature 3-axis CNC machine. This machine provides linear translation along the x, y, and z axes with a precision of approximately 0.04 mm, enabling accurate repositioning of the sensor.

#### 3.2. Tasks for Evaluation

The method is evaluated on three representative fine-grained tactile control tasks, each presenting distinct challenges:
- **Ball Repositioning:** Requires precise modulation of contact force to move a ball bearing to a target location on the sensor without slipping.
- **Analog Stick Deflection:** Involves deflecting an analog stick, which necessitates breaking and reestablishing contact with the stick by lifting and repositioning the sensor. This introduces partial observability and complex contact dynamics.
- **20-sided Die Rolling:** Demands rolling a 20-sided die to a specified face. This task involves complex dynamics, including slippage and undesired rolling, making it challenging for simple controllers.

#### 3.3. Autonomous Data Collection

To train the deep predictive model, a large dataset of robot interactions with the environment is collected autonomously:
- **Procedure:** Random movements are applied along the three axes, and tactile sensor readings are recorded. Environmental reset mechanisms (curved surface for ball, internal springs for analog stick, motor-driven thread for die) ensure that objects return to approximate starting conditions for new trajectories.
- **Dataset Size:** 7400 trajectories for the ball, 3000 for the analog stick, and 4500 for the die experiment. Each trajectory consists of 15 to 18 time steps.
- **Action Parameterization:** Actions are defined as changes in finger position (± 6.0 mm in x, y, and z directions).
- **Data Rate:** Data is collected at 1.5 Hz. Actions are repeated for 3 time-steps, but images are recorded at every time-step to aid in modeling discontinuous dynamics.

#### 3.4. Deep Predictive Model

- **Architecture:** A deep recurrent convolutional network architecture, similar to video prediction models from prior work, is employed.
- **Training Objective:** The model `g` is trained to predict future GelSight sensor observations (`I_hat_1:T`, represented as 48x64x3 RGB images) conditioned on the current observation (`I_0`) and a sequence of candidate actions (`a_1:T`). This effectively learns the tactile dynamics of object interaction.
- **Determinism:** A deterministic variant of the architecture proposed in is used.
- **Context Frames:** During the initial steps of RNN unrolling, ground truth observations are fed as context frames to enhance prediction accuracy.

#### 3.5. Goal Specification

- At test time, the user specifies a desired manipulation outcome by providing a **goal tactile image** (`I_g`). This image is a reading from the GelSight sensor corresponding to the desired object configuration. This method is noted for its simplicity, generality, and naturalness for users.

#### 3.6. Tactile MPC Control

Once the deep predictive model is trained, it is integrated into a Model Predictive Control (MPC) framework for planning and execution:
- **Planner:** A sampling-based planner using the **Cross-Entropy Method (CEM)** is utilized to optimize action sequences.
- **Optimization Process:** At each real-world time step:
 1. Multiple potential action sequences (`a_1:T`) are sampled.
 2. The deep predictive model (`g`) forecasts the resulting sequence of future tactile observations (`I_hat_t`) for each sampled action sequence.
 3. A cost function `c_t(I_g, I_hat_t)` quantifies the discrepancy between the predicted tactile observations and the user-specified goal image `I_g`. In this work, the **Mean Squared Error (MSE)** in pixel space is used as the cost function. The objective is to minimize the sum of costs over the prediction horizon: `a_1:T = arg min_{a_1:T} sum_{t=1...,T} c_t(I_g, I_hat_t)`.
 4. The first action of the optimal action sequence (the one yielding the lowest cost) is executed on the robot.
 5. The entire planning process is re-run at the next time step (MPC principle) to compensate for model inaccuracies and adapt to new observations.
- **Implementation Details:**
 - CEM uses 3 iterations with 100 samples per iteration.
 - The prediction horizon `T` ranges from 15 to 18 time steps, depending on the task.
 - Each commanded action is repeated three times, resulting in a planning horizon of 5 or 6 effective actions. This reduces the search space for the optimizer and improves control rate.
 - Tactile MPC runs at approximately 1 Hz during test time.

#### 3.7. Baseline Comparison

To evaluate the performance of Deep Tactile MPC, a hand-engineered baseline is designed:
- **Mechanism:** This baseline detects the pressure center of the object (ball, joystick, or die imprint) by computing the weighted centroid of pixels in the difference image (current image minus a blank background image). It then commands actions to move the sensor linearly towards the estimated pressure center in the goal image.
- **Tuning:** The step length is tuned for maximum control performance based on estimated object position. The authors note this is a strong baseline for the ball task but less effective for the joystick and die, which have more complex dynamics.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation demonstrates the efficacy of the Deep Tactile MPC framework across three distinct manipulation tasks, consistently outperforming a hand-engineered baseline.

#### 4.1. Predictive Model Accuracy

- The trained deep recurrent convolutional network demonstrates the ability to accurately predict future GelSight sensor observations. Figure 4 showcases successful predictions of contact patterns for sequences up to 13 time-steps, conditioned on initial images and action sequences from the test set. This indicates that the model effectively learns the complex tactile dynamics of object interaction.

#### 4.2. Overall Task Performance

- The Deep Tactile MPC method enables the robot to achieve all three manipulation tasks—ball repositioning, analog stick deflection, and 20-sided die rolling—using only tactile sensing, without relying on visual feedback. This includes maneuvering objects, manipulating a die to specific faces, and controlling a joystick solely by "feel."

#### 4.3. Quantitative Evaluation Metrics

Performance was assessed using three metrics:
1. **Mean Squared Error (MSE):** Pixel-wise MSE between the final observed tactile image and the user-specified goal tactile image (`I_g`). This metric directly reflects the objective optimized by tactile MPC.
2. **Human-Annotated Centroid Distance:** The Euclidean distance (in pixels) between the pressure centroid of the object at the end of the trajectory and the pressure centroid in the goal image, as manually annotated. This provides a more intuitive measure of object repositioning accuracy.
3. **Success Rate (for Die Rolling):** The fraction of trials where the desired face of the 20-sided die ended up facing upwards.

#### 4.4. Comparison with Hand-Engineered Baseline

- **Ball Repositioning Task:**
 - **Human-Annotated Centroid Distance (Figure 7, Left):** Deep Tactile MPC consistently achieves lower pixel distances to the goal centroid compared to the baseline. For example, a larger number of MPC trajectories fall below smaller distance thresholds.
 - **MSE Metric (Figure 7, Right):** Similarly, MPC demonstrates a significantly lower MSE between the final and goal images across a range of thresholds, indicating better overall image similarity to the goal.
 - **Table I:** Reports a median L2 distance of 2.10 mm for Tactile MPC, compared to 2.97 mm for the Centroid Baseline.

- **Analog Stick Deflection Task:**
 - **Human-Annotated Centroid Distance (Figure 8, Left):** Deep Tactile MPC again exhibits superior performance, with more trajectories achieving smaller centroid distances to the goal.
 - **MSE Metric (Figure 8, Right):** The MPC method shows a clear advantage in achieving lower MSE values compared to the baseline.
 - **Table I:** Reports a median L2 distance of 5.31 mm for Tactile MPC, compared to 8.86 mm for the Centroid Baseline.

- **20-sided Die Rolling Task:**
 - **Success Rate (Table I):** Deep Tactile MPC achieved a success rate of 86.6% (26 out of 30 trials), which is substantially higher than the 46.6% (14 out of 30 trials) achieved by the Centroid Baseline.
 - The authors attribute this larger performance gap to the complex slipping, sliding, and rolling motions involved, which are difficult for a simple, hand-tuned controller to manage but are implicitly learned by the deep dynamics model.

#### 4.5. Qualitative Examples

- Figures 5, 6, and 9 provide qualitative visual examples of successful task execution for the ball-bearing, analog stick, and die rolling tasks, respectively. These figures illustrate the goal image, the predicted sequence of tactile observations by the model for the optimal action sequence, and the actual trajectory executed by the robot, demonstrating alignment between predictions and real-world outcomes.

These results indicate that combining sampling-based planning with a deep dynamics model that operates on high-resolution tactile input is an effective method for solving a range of challenging manipulation tasks, particularly those involving complex contact dynamics and visual occlusion.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a notable advancement in the field of robotic manipulation by demonstrating a practical and effective method for integrating high-resolution tactile sensing into control systems. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Enabling Dexterous Manipulation:** The work addresses a long-standing challenge in robotics by providing a framework for fine-grained, continuous manipulation that heavily relies on touch, similar to human capabilities. This could lead to substantially more dexterous robots capable of handling delicate or small objects and performing complex in-hand manipulation.
- **Robustness to Visual Occlusion:** By demonstrating successful manipulation purely through tactile feedback, the method offers a solution for tasks where visual sensors are occluded by the robot's own body or the object itself. This expands the operational envelope of robots in confined spaces or during close-contact operations.
- **Data-Driven Approach to Contact Dynamics:** The use of deep predictive models bypasses the need for explicit, accurate physics models of contact and friction, which are notoriously difficult to formulate. This data-driven learning paradigm makes the approach more adaptable to diverse objects and contact scenarios without requiring extensive manual engineering or parameter tuning.
- **Intuitive Goal Specification:** Specifying manipulation goals directly as desired tactile images simplifies the user interface for robotic tasks. This allows non-experts to define goals by demonstrating a desired contact state, which is more intuitive and versatile than programming precise forces or kinematic trajectories.
- **Generalizability:** The learned dynamics model is general-purpose, enabling it to adapt to various tasks with different objects (ball, joystick, die) and complex dynamics (slipping, re-establishing contact). This generality is a key step towards widely applicable manipulation solutions.
- **Foundation for Future Work:** The method serves as a strong foundation for future research in several directions.
 - **Longer-Horizon Planning:** Improvements in video prediction models could extend the method to more complex, multi-step tasks requiring sequential manipulation or repeated contact operations.
 - **Multi-Fingered Manipulation:** Integrating multiple tactile sensors on a multi-fingered hand, combined with a joint predictive model, could unlock capabilities for complex in-hand object repositioning, assembly, and other intricate manipulation skills that closely mimic human dexterity. This represents a significant leap towards truly agile robotic hands.
 - **Reducing Supervision Requirements:** The emphasis on learning from unsupervised autonomous interaction is critical for developing scalable robotic systems, as it reduces the labor-intensive process of manual supervision or reward design.

In conclusion, this research is an important step toward realizing the full potential of tactile sensing in robotics. It demonstrates that by leveraging modern high-resolution sensors and deep learning, robots can acquire and utilize "feel" to perform complex manipulation tasks, opening avenues for more capable and adaptable robotic systems in various applications.
