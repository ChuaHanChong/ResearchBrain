# FluidGaussian: Propagating Simulation-Based Uncertainty Toward Functionally-Intelligent 3D Reconstruction

> [!summary] Summary
> FluidGaussian is a plug-and-play physics probe for active 3D Gaussian Splatting reconstruction. It flushes the reconstructed geometry with DFSPH fluid particles from five directions, aggregates near-surface velocity divergence into per-Gaussian scores, and re-ranks the top-K next-best-view candidates proposed by ActiveNeRF or FisherRF. Steering capture toward physically suspect regions yields up to +8.6% PSNR and 62.3% lower velocity divergence.

## Problem

- Radiance-field reconstruction optimizes photometric losses alone, so geometry that renders convincingly can still behave unphysically, because appearance cues ignore body contacts and mechanical couplings.
- Existing next-best-view planners score candidates by image-space uncertainty or Fisher information, treating cosmetic ornamentation identically to function-critical aerodynamic or hydrodynamic surfaces.
- Simulation-aware uncertainty work sits in scientific machine learning, not 3D reconstruction, leaving no metric that grades reconstructed assets by interaction fidelity.

## Method

- A ==simulation-induced uncertainty metric==: ==DFSPH== fluid is splashed at the voxelized 3DGS solid from five initial conditions, and ==SPH-kernel velocity divergence== is measured on near-surface particles.
- ==Per-Gaussian aggregation== averages particle divergence within three times each Gaussian's principal radius, then keeps the worst of the five initial conditions as that Gaussian's physics score.
- ==Physics-aware NBV selection== rescores the top-K poses from ActiveNeRF or FisherRF by visibility-weighted, screen-area-weighted divergence sums, acquiring the highest-divergence pose each round.

## Results

- Under a **10-view** budget, the selector raised FisherRF PSNR on Blender from **23.42** to **24.74**, and ActiveNeRF on DrivAerNet++ from **17.38** to **18.87**.
- Geometry divergence fell on every dataset, e.g. ActiveNeRF on MipNeRF360 from **0.0130** to **0.0049** (**-62.3%**), narrowing the gap to **20-view** perfect-geometry references.
- On function-critical DrivAerNet++ views, PSNR rose **15.68** to **16.89** (**+7.7%**) and divergence fell **0.0316** to **0.0231**; all **five** single-direction ablations beat FisherRF.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Pixel metrics systematically overestimate asset quality; a cheap simulation probe exposes geometric defects that photometric losses never penalize.
> - Fluids probe better than rigid contacts: they cover the whole exposed surface and give stable statistics aggregated over many particles.
> - The divergence advantage widens with Reynolds number, so appearance-only reconstruction errors amplify precisely in the turbulent regimes engineering simulation cares about.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

This research is situated within the intersection of 3D reconstruction, active learning, physics-informed machine learning, and the emerging field of functional intelligence in computer vision.

Traditional 3D reconstruction methods, particularly modern radiance field techniques like Neural Radiance Fields (NeRFs) and 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS), primarily optimize for visual fidelity. These methods aim to produce photorealistic renderings from novel viewpoints by minimizing photometric losses. While successful in visual tasks, this appearance-centric approach often overlooks the physical plausibility and functional behavior of the reconstructed geometry when objects interact with the physical world.

Active 3D reconstruction, a sub-field of active learning, addresses the cost of dense image capture by selecting "next-best-views" (NBVs). These NBV strategies typically use criteria such as uncertainty reduction or information gain, derived from visual cues (e.g., variance in radiance fields or Fisher Information in volumetric rendering), to guide camera pose selection. Current active reconstruction methods, such as ActiveNeRF and FisherRF, also largely focus on improving visual fidelity.

Physics-informed machine learning has gained traction in various scientific and engineering domains, with research exploring simulation-related uncertainty and incorporating physical laws into models. However, this focus has largely been on scientific machine learning rather than directly addressing the physical plausibility of 3D reconstructed geometries.

The concept of "functional intelligence" in 3D vision, which involves understanding how objects behave and interact based on their geometry and material properties, is another relevant area. This includes research on affordances, interaction-centric representations, and simulating object behavior under loads and environments (e.g., digital twins).

FluidGaussian contributes to this landscape by bridging the gap between visually-driven 3D reconstruction and physically-aware, functionally intelligent reconstruction. It introduces a mechanism to incorporate physical interaction signals (specifically, fluid-structure interactions) into the active view acquisition process, moving beyond purely photometric cues. This aims to produce 3D assets that are not only visually accurate but also physically consistent and "simulation-ready," particularly for applications involving fluid dynamics.

### 2. Key objectives and motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable 3D reconstruction methods to become aware of real-world interactions and underlying object functionality, beyond relying solely on visual cues. The authors pose the central question: "How can 3D reconstruction become aware of real-world interactions and underlying object function, beyond visual cues?"

The motivation for addressing this question stems from several identified limitations of current appearance-centric 3D reconstruction pipelines:
1. **Over-reliance on appearance cues:** Existing methods primarily optimize for visual fidelity using photometric losses, leading to reconstructions that may look correct visually but fail functionally in physical interactions. These methods tend to ignore body contacts, mechanical couplings, and the physical laws governing object behavior.
2. **Lack of functional preservation:** Current approaches often treat all geometric regions equally, including function-critical areas (e.g., aerodynamic surfaces) and purely ornamental features. This can result in reconstructed structures that are suboptimal for physical interactions.
3. **Missing fine-scale details:** Appearance-only optimization can miss fine-scale geometric details essential for stable and plausible physical behavior, particularly when evaluated in physical simulations. Such omissions can lead to "unphysical and implausible interactions."
4. **Inability to assess physical quality:** Despite progress in visual metrics (e.g., PSNR, SSIM), these do not reliably indicate the physical quality or plausibility of a reconstructed geometry. Reconstructions scoring high on pixel-space metrics can exhibit significant deficiencies when evaluated through interaction-based simulations, such as high velocity divergence in fluid flow (as illustrated in Figure 1).

To address these motivations, the authors propose to quantify physical quality by assessing how geometry behaves under interactions. They specifically choose fluid-structure interactions because:
- **Surface coverage and granularity:** Fluids interact with the entire exposed surface, providing a detailed probe for fine geometric defects (e.g., thin parts, gaps, self-intersections) with higher sensitivity than discrete rigid-body contacts.
- **Robust particle-wise metrics:** Aggregating measurements over large sets of fluid particles can provide stable and meaningful estimates of physical quality.
- **Ubiquity and practical relevance:** Fluid interactions (air or water) are pervasive in real-world settings and well-supported by standard simulators, making them broadly applicable and often more informative than rigid-body interactions for probing object integrity.

The overall motivation is to move towards "functional intelligence" in 3D vision, enabling the creation of 3D assets that are robust and stable for use in physical simulations, thereby improving their utility in scientific and engineering applications.

### 3. Methodology and approach

FluidGaussian is presented as a plug-and-play method that integrates geometry reconstruction with fluid-structure interactions to assess surface quality and guide active view acquisition. The approach combines existing vision-based next-best-view (NBV) planners with a novel simulation-based uncertainty metric.

#### 3.1. Overall Pipeline

The FluidGaussian pipeline operates in two main steps within an iterative active reconstruction loop:
1. **Vision-based Uncertainty Proposes Top-K Candidate Camera Poses:** Given a partially reconstructed 3D Gaussian Splatting model (G_t) and a set of collected views (D_t), an existing vision-based NBV planner (e.g., ActiveNeRF or FisherRF) proposes a pool of K candidate camera poses (T_K). These initial candidates are typically selected by maximizing a utility function derived from visual cues, such as variance reduction or Fisher Information.
2. **FluidGaussian Selects the Best Physics-Informed Camera Pose:** FluidGaussian then evaluates these K candidates. For each candidate, it performs fluid simulations on the current 3D Gaussian model to derive a physics-informed uncertainty metric. This metric quantifies the physical plausibility and interaction fidelity of the reconstructed geometry. The candidate pose that exhibits the highest "physical uncertainty" (indicating a region with geometric artifacts or poor simulation quality) is selected as the next best view (T*_t+1). This view is then acquired, added to the dataset (D_t+1), and the 3D Gaussian model (G_t+1) is updated through further training. This process iterates until a predefined view budget is exhausted.

#### 3.2. Geometry Representation: 3D Gaussian Splatting

The method uses 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) as the underlying 3D scene representation. A scene is parameterized by a collection of N anisotropic 3D Gaussians, each defined by its center (x_i), covariance (Σ_i), opacity (α_i), and view-dependent color coefficients (c_i). These parameters are learned by minimizing a photometric objective (e.g., SSIM) against multi-view 2D images.

#### 3.3. Measuring Physical Uncertainty via Fluid Simulation

FluidGaussian defines a simulation-induced uncertainty metric based on incompressible fluid simulations. The objective is not probabilistic uncertainty but an indicator of the reconstructed surface's physical reliability.
- **Setup:** To ensure robust and statistically meaningful measurements, fluid simulations are run from five initial conditions (ICs) for each uncertainty measurement. Fluid is "splashed" towards the object from four horizontal directions (e_x, -e_x, e_z, -e_z) and from the top (-e_y), each with a fixed initial speed. The current 3DGS representation is converted into a solid boundary (via on-the-fly voxel conversion) within the simulation.
- **DFSPH Solver:** The Divergence-Free Smoothed Particle Hydrodynamics (DFSPH) scheme is used. DFSPH iteratively enforces density consistency and, crucially, a zero velocity divergence (∇·v ≈ 0) constraint, ensuring the simulated flow is incompressible. While DFSPH aims for divergence-free flow, numerical inaccuracies at the fluid-structure interface can lead to higher local divergence, which the authors interpret as an indicator of geometric artifacts.
- **Divergence Estimator:** Velocity divergence at a fluid particle (i) is estimated as an SPH-weighted sum over its neighbors within a finite support (Eq. 1). The magnitude of this divergence, D_i = |(∇·v)_i|, serves as the per-particle measure.

#### 3.4. Physics-Aware NBV Selection

The per-particle divergence measures are then integrated into a per-Gaussian metric and subsequently into a view-dependent score:
- **Integration of Divergence into a Per-Gaussian Metric:** For each initial condition (IC), the per-particle divergence magnitudes are computed. To associate these with the 3D Gaussian primitives, for each Gaussian (g) centered at μ_g with radius r_g, the method identifies all fluid particles (P^(IC)_g) that pass through a sphere of radius n·r_g (where n=3) around μ_g during the simulation. The mean divergence D^(IC)_g for that Gaussian and IC is then computed by averaging the D^(IC)_i of these particles (Eq. 3). The final per-Gaussian physics score D_g is taken as the maximum D^(IC)_g across all five initial conditions (D_g = max_{IC} D^(IC)_g), to capture the most significant defect exposed by any flow direction.
- **View Scoring:** For a candidate camera pose (T), the per-Gaussian scores {D_g} are converted into a single scalar view score S(T) by a visibility-weighted accumulation. For each Gaussian visible in the rendered image of view T, a soft area weight w_g(T) is calculated based on its screen-space radius (Eq. 4). The view score S(T) is the sum of D_g weighted by w_g(T) for all visible Gaussians (Eq. 5). The NBV is selected as the pose T from the top-K candidates that yields the largest S(T), as this indicates the view observing the region with the highest physical uncertainty.

#### 3.5. Quantifying Function-Critical Region

To specifically evaluate the method's impact on functionally important areas, a measure for function-critical regions is introduced. For geometries like cars, simulated fluid particles are emitted towards the object's frontal surface (e.g., in the -e_x direction). For each Gaussian, the number of interacting particles (|P_g|) within its spatial influence region is counted (similar to Eq. 2 but for a single targeted simulation). A view score C(T) for evaluating test views is then calculated by summing these particle interaction counts, weighted by the Gaussian's screen-space area (Eq. 6), thereby prioritizing views that contain surfaces with strong frontal-flow interaction.

### 4. Main findings and results

The empirical evaluation of FluidGaussian was conducted across three datasets: Blender (synthetic), Mip-NeRF 360 (real, unbounded scenes), and DrivAerNet++ (parametric car dataset for aerodynamics), using ActiveNeRF and FisherRF as baseline NBV planners. The evaluation focused on both visual quality and simulation-induced physics quality, with a specific analysis of function-critical regions.

#### 4.1. Improved Visual Quality

The integration of FluidGaussian (denoted as "+FluidGaussian") with existing NBV planners demonstrated improvements in visual reconstruction quality.
- **Overall Visual Metrics:** On Blender, Mip-NeRF 360, and DrivAerNet++ datasets, FluidGaussian generally led to increased PSNR and SSIM, and decreased LPIPS values compared to the baselines (Table 1).
 - For example, with ActiveNeRF on DrivAerNet++, PSNR improved from 17.3840 to 18.8727 (+8.6%), SSIM from 0.9079 to 0.9297, and LPIPS decreased from 0.1595 to 0.1269.
 - With FisherRF on Blender, PSNR improved from 23.4230 to 24.7433, SSIM from 0.8761 to 0.8907, and LPIPS decreased from 0.1070 to 0.0923.
- **Geometric Accuracy:** Chamfer Distance metrics on the Blender dataset indicated that FluidGaussian achieved lower distances to the perfect geometry compared to both ActiveNeRF (0.0055 vs 0.0103) and FisherRF (0.0046 vs 0.0165), suggesting improved geometric accuracy (Table 8, Appendix B.3).
- **Generalizability:** Evaluations on 7 ShapeNet objects also showed consistent improvements in PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS when FluidGaussian was applied (Table 7, Appendix B.2).
- **Comparison to Random Selection:** FluidGaussian consistently outperformed random view selection under the same reconstruction baselines across all datasets, further supporting its effectiveness beyond simple sampling strategies (Table 6, Appendix B.1).

#### 4.2. Refined Simulation-Induced Physics Quality

A core finding is FluidGaussian's ability to reduce "geometry divergence" (¯D), a measure of physical implausibility in fluid simulations.
- **Reduced Divergence:** Across all datasets and both ActiveNeRF and FisherRF baselines, adding FluidGaussian consistently reduced the mean fluid divergence (¯D) (Table 3).
 - For instance, on Blender, ActiveNeRF's ¯D decreased from 0.0262 to 0.0145, and FisherRF's from 0.0144 to 0.0124.
 - On Mip-NeRF 360, ActiveNeRF's ¯D decreased from 0.0130 to 0.0049 (-62.3%), and FisherRF's from 0.0025 to 0.0022.
- **Divergence versus Turbulence (Velocity):** The benefits of FluidGaussian in reducing divergence were more pronounced in higher turbulence regimes (higher Reynolds numbers). As inflow speed (U) and Reynolds number increased, appearance-only reconstructions exhibited markedly amplified physical errors (higher divergence), while FluidGaussian maintained greater stability and a larger divergence gap (∆_gap) compared to baselines (Figure 7, bottom). This indicates that FluidGaussian mitigates physical defects more effectively as fluid conditions become more challenging. Visual quality (PSNR) remained largely consistent across these varying turbulence levels (Figure 7, top).

#### 4.3. Targeted Functional Intelligence

FluidGaussian effectively guided view selection toward function-critical regions, demonstrating improvements in both visual and physical metrics in these areas.
- **DrivAerNet++ Function-Critical Regions:** On the DrivAerNet++ dataset, evaluating specific regions identified as functionally critical (e.g., vehicle frontal surfaces interacting with flow), FluidGaussian improved PSNR (+7.7% for ActiveNeRF), SSIM, and LPIPS, while also reducing divergence (Table 4). This indicates that the simulation-based uncertainty metric successfully biases view selection towards areas crucial for fluid-structure interaction.

#### 4.4. Ablation Study: Influence of Fluid Initial Position

An ablation study on Blender showed that while individual fluid directions for uncertainty measurement yielded varying results, all single-direction FluidGaussian configurations still outperformed the FisherRF baseline in terms of PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS. The comprehensive approach, combining all five canonical directions (max pooling of divergence), achieved the best overall performance (Table 5).

#### 4.5. Computational Cost

FluidGaussian introduces an additional computational cost. On average, each fluid simulation takes 1.23 minutes. The total cost per geometry for FluidGaussian is approximately 45 minutes, compared to about 17-18 minutes for the baseline methods, representing roughly a 2.5x increase in overall computation time.

### 5. Significance and potential impact

FluidGaussian contributes to the field of 3D reconstruction by addressing a recognized limitation of existing methods: their primary focus on visual fidelity often neglects the physical plausibility and functional behavior of reconstructed geometries. This work proposes a novel approach to integrate physical awareness into the active 3D reconstruction pipeline, particularly for 3D Gaussian Splatting.

The key significance and potential impacts include:

1. **Creation of Simulation-Ready Assets:** By prioritizing physical plausibility, FluidGaussian facilitates the creation of 3D assets that are more suitable for downstream physical simulations, such as those in engineering design, robotics, or scientific modeling. Reconstructions exhibiting lower velocity divergence in fluid simulations are indicative of more geometrically sound and physically consistent structures, reducing the likelihood of simulation instability or erroneous behavior.
2. **Advancement Towards Functionally Intelligent Reconstruction:** This research represents a step towards "functionally intelligent" 3D reconstruction. Instead of merely reproducing appearance, the method introduces a mechanism to infer and prioritize regions critical for an object's function (e.g., aerodynamic surfaces), thereby enabling the reconstruction to be guided by interaction-centric understanding. This extends the utility of 3D models beyond visual rendering to practical applications where physical interaction is paramount.
3. **Enhanced Geometric Accuracy and Robustness:** The method's ability to reduce velocity divergence, particularly in turbulent flow regimes, suggests that it can identify and prompt the refinement of subtle geometric defects (like thin parts, gaps, or occlusions) that purely photometric losses might overlook. This can lead to more robust and accurate geometries, even if the primary metric is physical plausibility.
4. **Complementary Active Learning Strategy:** FluidGaussian acts as a plug-and-play module that complements existing vision-based active learning strategies. It introduces a new dimension of "uncertainty" (simulation-propagated physical quality) that can be integrated with visual uncertainty metrics, offering a more holistic approach to next-best-view selection. This expanded criteria for view selection can lead to improvements in both visual fidelity and physical fidelity concurrently.
5. **Applicability in Scientific and Engineering Domains:** The specific focus on fluid-structure interactions makes this method particularly relevant for domains such as aerospace, automotive design (as demonstrated with DrivAerNet++), marine engineering, and environmental modeling, where fluid dynamics play a critical role. The method’s ability to improve reconstructions of function-critical surfaces has direct implications for engineering analysis and design.

The proposed future work, including considering diverse fluid properties and curating more science-related datasets, suggests further avenues for broadening the applicability and impact of physics-aware 3D reconstruction.
