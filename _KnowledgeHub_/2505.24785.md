# EXP-Bench: Can AI Conduct AI Research Experiments?

> [!summary] Summary
> EXP-Bench is a benchmark for testing whether AI agents can run complete AI research experiments. A semi-automated pipeline extracts 461 tasks from 51 NeurIPS and ICLR 2024 papers and their code. Agents must design, implement, execute and draw conclusions. Leading agents score 20-35% on individual aspects, but only 0.5% of experiments are fully correct and executable.

## Problem

- Existing benchmarks test isolated skills such as literature synthesis, code snippets, Kaggle-style ML tasks or post-hoc analysis. They do not test full end-to-end research experimentation from question to conclusion.
- Papers present polished narratives and scatter experimental details across text, appendices and sprawling codebases. Manual curation of realistic experiment tasks therefore needs deep expertise and does not scale.
- Prior research-agent benchmarks are small (RE-Bench has 7 hand-curated tasks) or use simplified metrics. They give no scalable recipe for building high-fidelity tasks or fine-grained step-by-step supervision.

## Method

- ==Semi-automated pipeline==: filter papers by citations and repository popularity, then extract each task from the paper with ==multi-pass, multimodal retrieval-augmented prompting== that yields a question, high-level method and expected outcome.
- An ==implementation-extraction agent== searches the codebase for the script chain that solves each task. ==Containerized execution== validates it, and ==AST tracing== turns it into natural-language implementation requirements as ground truth.
- ==Multi-metric judge==: an LLM-based ==Monitor== checks integrity, then scores design (D), implementation (I) and conclusion (C). A ==Code Execution Validator== checks executability (E). ==Conjunctive scores== such as I·E and All·E combine these.

## Results

- Across **461** tasks (**12,737** gradable subtasks), agents score only **20-35%** on individual aspects. Full correctness is near zero: OpenHands with o3-mini reaches **1.4%** All and **0.5%** All·E, and Claude-3.7 Sonnet reaches **0.4%**.
- Stacking metrics collapses scores. The monitor check alone gives **20.6%**. Adding design and conclusion drops it to **3.7%**, adding implementation to **0.4%**, and adding execution to **0.2%**. Conjunctive metrics also lower judge variance.
- Failure analysis over **361** distilled failure types finds **39.7%** of implementation failures are missing components. Execution failures are **29.4%** environment or dependency errors and **23.8%** script errors. Design failures are **16.1%** misclassified variables.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Agents produce plausible-looking partial answers (design, code, conclusions) that fall apart under conjunctive, execution-verified scoring. Evaluate end-to-end correctness, not per-phase averages, to avoid overstating research-agent ability.
> - The main bottlenecks are turning abstract methods into complete code and keeping complex software stacks reproducible. They are not high-level ideation, so agent improvements should target implementation completeness and environment robustness.
> - Anchoring tasks to published papers and their working code lets ground truth be extracted at scale with light human review. The same data could supply verifiable rewards for training research agents.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The vision of automating AI research holds immense potential to accelerate scientific progress, particularly given the inherently digital nature of AI development. However, current AI agents, despite demonstrating nascent capabilities in isolated tasks such as literature synthesis, hypothesis generation, or code generation, profoundly struggle with the complexities of rigorous, end-to-end experimentation. This paper identifies a critical gap in the existing research landscape: the absence of a comprehensive benchmark capable of evaluating and guiding AI agents through the *entire* scientific experimentation pipeline, from hypothesis formulation to result analysis.

Existing benchmarks, while valuable, often address only fragmented aspects of the scientific process. "Scientific Reasoning Benchmarks" like BoxingGym and Lab-Bench focus on abstract reasoning or experimental design based on static artifacts, but crucially, they do not assess an agent’s ability to perform *actual* experiments. Similarly, "Scientific Coding Benchmarks" such as Scicode, BLADE, and ScienceAgentBench are designed for code generation or post-hoc data analysis, isolating these components from the broader, iterative experimental context.

Even benchmarks specifically targeting machine learning (ML) tasks fall short. DSBench, ML-Agent-Bench, and MLE-Bench evaluate ML problem-solving skills, often within constrained environments like Kaggle challenges, focusing on sub-components like script editing or hyperparameter tuning. Other benchmarks, including RE-Bench, ML-Gym, and PaperBench, attempt to compare agent performance against humans on research tasks, but are typically limited in scale, feature only a small number of hand-curated tasks, or employ simplified evaluation metrics. PaperBench, for instance, focuses on agents' proficiency in executing specific, well-defined sub-components like running documented code scripts.

EXP-Bench distinguishes itself by addressing the *holistic challenge* of end-to-end and step-by-step AI research experimentation. It uniquely leverages tasks derived directly from influential, peer-reviewed AI publications and their associated open-source implementations. This approach ensures that the benchmark reflects authentic, completed, and peer-validated research workflows, enabling a rigorous assessment of an AI agent's capacity to conduct established scientific procedures grounded in real-world AI experimentation. Furthermore, it introduces a scalable methodology for constructing such comprehensive benchmark tasks, which has been a significant limitation in prior work.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching motivation behind EXP-Bench is to accelerate scientific progress in AI by enabling the automation of AI research itself. The authors posit that because AI research is fundamentally digital, it is uniquely amenable to automation by Large Language Model (LLM)-driven AI agents. However, realizing this vision requires agents that can perform beyond isolated tasks and engage in the rigorous, end-to-end experimentation that is central to empirical AI research.

The primary objective of EXP-Bench is to serve as a novel and comprehensive benchmark specifically designed to systematically evaluate AI agents on their ability to conduct *complete* research experiments. This involves a series of complex, interconnected sub-tasks that current AI agents largely struggle with.

More specifically, the key objectives include:
- **Comprehensive Evaluation:** To challenge AI agents with realistic tasks where they must formulate viable hypotheses, design and implement experimental procedures (including aspects like data handling, model selection, and hyperparameter optimization), execute these procedures correctly, and derive valid conclusions from the generated results. This goes beyond mere code generation or problem-solving in constrained environments.
- **High-Fidelity Task Curation:** To address the significant challenge of curating such intricate and authentic research tasks at scale. Academic papers often present a polished narrative, omitting intermediate experimental details which are fragmented across various sources like dense papers, supplementary materials, and sprawling codebases. EXP-Bench aims to overcome this by introducing a semi-autonomous pipeline that extracts and structures these crucial experimental details with high fidelity.
- **Bottleneck Identification:** By evaluating leading AI agents on these comprehensive tasks, the benchmark aims to identify specific bottlenecks and persistent failure patterns in their current capabilities. This diagnostic insight is crucial for guiding future research and development efforts in AI agent design.
- **Tool for Advancement:** Ultimately, EXP-Bench is designed to serve as a vital tool and a rich dataset that will enable future AI agents to significantly improve their ability to conduct complex, real-world AI research experiments step-by-step, thereby accelerating the pace of AI innovation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

EXP-Bench is meticulously structured to evaluate an AI agent's ability to conduct end-to-end AI research experiments, grounding each task in influential, peer-reviewed AI papers and their corresponding open-source codebases. This approach ensures that tasks reflect real-world scientific workflows.

**Dataset Specification:**
The dataset comprises a collection of AI research tasks, each emulating a complete experimental process to address a specific research question.
- **Problem Statement (Agent Input):** For each task, an AI agent receives:
 1. **Research Question:** A specific goal derived from the source paper's experiments.
 2. **High-Level Method:** A description guiding the required experimental approach.
 3. **Code Repository:** Access to the relevant open-source code, with specific components or scripts masked to prevent direct answers.
- **Expected Outcome (Ground Truth):** Each task instance includes a curated ground-truth solution derived from the original research artifacts for evaluation. This comprises:
 1. **Experimental Design:** Specifies key variables (independent, dependent, control), constants, and procedures.
 2. **Code Modifications:** The necessary changes, assessed via a `git diff` against the provided repository.
 3. **Final Conclusion:** A direct answer to the research question based on expected experimental results.
The benchmark currently features 461 research tasks (resulting in 12,737 individually gradable subtasks) sourced from 51 papers published at top-tier conferences (NeurIPS 2024 and ICLR 2024), spanning diverse AI subfields like Computer Vision, NLP, and Reinforcement Learning.

**Semi-Automated Dataset Construction Pipeline:**
To overcome the challenges of curating high-fidelity, fragmented experimental details from papers and codebases, a three-stage semi-automated pipeline was developed:

1. **Stage 1: Source Selection and Filtering:** Identifies high-quality, impactful research papers from top-tier AI conferences with publicly available code repositories. Initial filtering prioritizes reproducibility and influence, considering citation counts and GitHub activity (stars, forks).

2. **Stage 2: Experiment Procedure Extraction:** Decomposes high-level research goals into explicit, executable sub-steps.
 - **Stage 2.1: Extract Research Task:** Extracts the core problem (research question, high-level methodology, expected outcome) from the paper. This involves:
 - Indexing PDF content using OCR and multimodal techniques to capture structured elements (tables, figures, headers).
 - A multi-pass retrieval-augmented querying process using LLMs: first, identifying broad research takeaways, then semantically extracting details from subsections (especially evaluation sections), classifying them as context or candidate research questions.
 - Refining tasks through targeted re-querying of the full paper and appendices to capture missed setup constraints or methodological details.
 - **Stage 2.2: Extract Experiment Implementation:** An LLM-driven agent (tool-augmented with PDF reading, terminal access, web browsing) identifies the specific chain of scripts in the codebase needed to address the extracted research task. The agent explores the repository, consulting documentation and auxiliary scripts to uncover domain-specific requirements. If an initial run of the extracted implementation fails, the pipeline iterates, allowing the agent to refine it. The validated script chain is then parsed via AST tracing to extract a step-by-step list of implementation requirements in natural language, forming the ground truth for evaluation. Additional contextual details (e.g., hyperparameters) are incorporated from raw code or documentation.

3. **Stage 3: Verification and Refinement:** All curated tasks undergo rigorous validation. For tasks with a matched implementation, associated scripts are executed in a clean, containerized environment, and their outputs are checked against the original paper's results. Failed validations return the task to Stage 2 for refinement. Tasks lacking a matched implementation undergo manual validation to ensure alignment with the source paper. A final lightweight human review cross-checks structured task content. Crucially, during agent evaluation, repositories are cloned freshly, and original solution files are masked using scripted Git operations to ensure agents must reason over the task input rather than relying on shortcuts.

**Evaluation Methodology:**
Agent performance is assessed across four core phases of experimentation: design, implementation, execution, and conclusion, using a multi-metric evaluation pipeline.
- **LLM-based Judge (o3-mini):**
 - **Monitor (M):** An integrity check that flags disallowed behaviors (e.g., accessing the research paper PDF, performing Git operations like checking out commits, using hardcoded or faked data).
 - **Design (D):** Assesses the correctness and completeness of the agent's experimental design against ground truth (proportion of design criteria met).
 - **Implementation (I):** Evaluates the correctness and completeness of the agent's code modifications against ground truth (proportion of implementation components satisfied).
 - **Conclusion (C):** Checks the semantic correctness and validity of the agent's derived conclusion.
- **Code Execution Validator (E):** Runs the agent-generated code modifications in a clean, containerized environment to verify executability and production of expected outputs.

Metrics include individual scores (D, I, C, E) and conjunctive metrics like `I·E` (implementation is correct and executable) and `All✓` (D, I, C are all correct), `All·E✓` (D, I, C, and E are all correct).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of leading AI agents, including OpenHands (OH) and IterativeAgent (IA), on EXP-Bench reveals significant limitations in their current ability to conduct end-to-end AI research experiments.

**Overall Performance:**
Initial evaluations demonstrate that while agents exhibit partial capabilities in individual experimental aspects, their success rate for completing entire, executable experiments is remarkably low.
- Scores on individual experimental aspects (Design (D), Implementation (I), Conclusion (C)) typically range from 20% to 35%. For instance, OpenHands with Claude-3.7 Sonnet achieved 16.0% for Design, 35.0% for Implementation, and 33.2% for Conclusion correctness.
- However, when all criteria are combined, the performance drops drastically. The success rate for fully correct and executable experiments (`All·E✓`) was a mere 0.5% for the best-performing configuration (OpenHands + o3-mini), with most other models achieving 0.0%. This highlights a significant brittleness in their ability to integrate all experimental phases.

**Detailed Analysis:**
- **Cost-Time Analysis:** Performance did not correlate directly with higher costs or longer execution times. OpenHands with o3-mini, despite being the best overall performer, showed a relatively low average cost ($0.55 USD) and moderate time consumption (24.89 minutes per task). Conversely, OpenHands with Claude-3.7 Sonnet, the second-best, was the slowest and most expensive (33.53 minutes, $10.15 USD). IterativeAgent models often consumed the full allotted time but were less effective.
- **Impact of Conjunctive Metrics:** Progressively applying stricter evaluation criteria (Monitor, Design, Conclusion, Implementation, Execution) revealed a sharp decline in agent scores. For OpenHands + o3-mini, the average score after initial monitoring was 20.6%. This dropped to 3.7% when design and conclusion correctness were added, further plummeted to 0.4% with implementation correctness, and reached a final accuracy of just 0.2% when execution verification was included. This vividly demonstrates that surface-level correctness in isolated steps does not translate to robust end-to-end experimental capability.
- **Metric Stability:** Individual metrics like Conclusion (C) and Execution (E) showed high variance. Agents could produce plausible conclusions without a sound experimental basis, or their implementations might execute successfully despite being fundamentally incorrect or mock. The use of compositional scoring (e.g., `C·D`, `I·E`) was crucial in mitigating this over-crediting, providing more stable and discriminative evaluations by ensuring correctness across multiple inter-dependent dimensions.

**Prevalent Agent Failure Patterns (Table 2):**
An in-depth analysis of error traces identified consistent failure patterns across different experimental phases:
- **Implementation (39.71% missing essential components):** This was the most prevalent issue. Agents frequently omitted critical elements specified in the ground truth, such as specific semantic retrieval strategies (e.g., UniXcoder-H2L), data validation functions (e.g., using GPT-3.5), or robustness-enhancing techniques (e.g., Mixup, CutMix, Label Smoothing). Incomplete data preprocessing (1.83%) was also common, with agents failing to include necessary dataset loading, transformation, or normalization steps.
- **Execution:** Failures here often stemmed from environmental and software stack issues.
 - **Environment/Dependency Misconfigurations (29.38%):** Agents frequently missed critical environments (e.g., STORM not registered in jaxmarl) or core libraries (e.g., PyTorch, Flax), leading to model loading failures.
 - **Script-Level Errors (23.84%):** These included unrecognized model names (e.g., moganet_tiny not found in timm) or missing checkpoint files, resulting in runtime or I/O errors. These highlight persistent challenges in reproducibility.
- **Design-Related Failures:** Agents struggled with conceptualizing experiments.
 - **Incomplete or Misclassified Design Variables (16.05%):** Agents failed to accurately identify or categorize independent, dependent, and control variables.
 - **Extraneous Procedural Additions (7.62%):** Agents included irrelevant components (e.g., a ResNet-50 backbone) or arbitrary hyperparameters not specified in the ground truth, suggesting a lack of discernment between essential factors and implementation noise.
- **Conclusion-Phase Errors:** Agents demonstrated limitations in interpretive reasoning.
 - **Missing or Underdeveloped Conclusions (26.18%):** Agents often omitted detailed comparisons (e.g., between PPO and Q-Learning on training time) or specific numerical gains (e.g., 1.25% improvement).
 - **Incorrect Interpretation (19.66%):** Agents made unsubstantiated claims, such as stating performance improvements without providing comparative evidence.

These findings underscore that while AI agents can perform isolated coding or analysis, they lack the sophisticated reasoning, environmental awareness, and iterative refinement capabilities needed for robust, end-to-end scientific experimentation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

EXP-Bench marks a significant advancement in the evaluation of AI agents for scientific research. Its core significance lies in its rigorous, end-to-end approach to assessing an agent's ability to conduct complete AI research experiments, moving beyond isolated tasks. By sourcing its 461 tasks from 51 influential, peer-reviewed AI publications and their open-source codebases, EXP-Bench provides an unprecedented level of realism and fine-grained challenge that reflects the complexities of real-world AI research workflows.

**Significance:**
- **First of its kind:** It fills a critical void in the benchmarking landscape by focusing on the holistic experimentation procedure—from design conceptualization to conclusion derivation—rather than fragmented sub-components.
- **Realistic and High-Fidelity:** The semi-automated curation pipeline ensures that tasks are authentic, derived directly from validated scientific literature, and accompanied by ground truths for robust evaluation. This addresses the traditional labor-intensive and error-prone nature of manual benchmark creation.
- **Diagnostic Tool:** The initial evaluations clearly expose significant bottlenecks in current leading AI agents. These include persistent struggles with conceptualizing complex experimental designs, translating abstract methodologies into robust and executable code implementations, and ensuring the stable execution of complex software stacks. These identified failure patterns provide crucial, actionable insights for developers aiming to improve next-generation AI agents.
- **Valuable Dataset:** Beyond its role as an evaluation tool, EXP-Bench contributes a substantial, structured dataset that can be used for training AI agents. By exposing agents to realistic, step-by-step experimental procedures, it offers a rich source of supervision to develop more capable and autonomous research agents.

**Potential Impact:**
The potential impact of EXP-Bench is transformative for the field of AI research itself:
- **Accelerated Scientific Discovery:** By enabling AI agents to automate core aspects of the experimental lifecycle, EXP-Bench can significantly shorten innovation cycles within AI, leading to more rapid advancements in machine learning capabilities and, consequently, broader scientific discoveries across various domains.
- **Advancing Autonomous AI Research:** The benchmark serves as a crucial guide for developing AI agents that can act step-by-step, reason, and adapt through the complex, iterative process of scientific inquiry. Future work can leverage this benchmark for reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards, pushing agents towards truly autonomous navigation of the research lifecycle.
- **Democratization of Research:** As AI agents become more proficient in conducting experiments, they could potentially democratize access to cutting-edge research tools, lowering barriers to entry for smaller labs or individual researchers.

**Limitations (as acknowledged by the authors):**
While comprehensive in its focus on experimentation, EXP-Bench does not yet capture the *entire* AI research lifecycle. This includes critical upstream stages like identifying research gaps through literature review, the initial unstructured ideation of novel research questions, and navigating the inherently complex, iterative, and often unpredictable path of real-world scientific discovery that precedes the formal experimental phase. These broader aspects remain open challenges for future AI research automation.

**Societal Implications:**
As discussed by the authors, the rapid evolution of AI agents capable of conducting research presents both significant benefits and challenges. While it promises to accelerate innovation and improve scientific efficiency, it also heightens the importance of addressing potential negative societal consequences, such as the ethical implications of autonomous research, the risk of algorithmic bias propagating through automated processes, and the evolving role of human researchers in an increasingly automated scientific landscape. These considerations underscore the need for robust governance and careful integration as AI takes on more complex research roles.
