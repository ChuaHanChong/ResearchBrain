# Reasoning as an Adaptive Defense for Safety

> [!summary] Summary
> Carnegie Mellon University researchers developed TARS (Training Adaptive Reasoners for Safety), a reinforcement learning approach that enables large language models to adaptively reason about safety using chain-of-thought traces, leading to superior safety-refusal trade-offs and enhanced robustness against adversarial attacks.

## Problem

- Large Language Models (LLMs) often struggle with nuanced safety judgments, frequently resorting to overly conservative refusals or generating harmful content when circumvented.
- There is a lack of systematic, open research into training LLMs to adaptively reason about safety using test-time computation.
- Existing safety methods like Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) risk teaching models degenerate refusal strategies or failing to generalize to complex safety scenarios.

## Method

- The **TARS** (==Training Adaptive Reasoners== for Safety) framework, a three-stage reinforcement learning (RL) methodology using GRPO (Generalized Reinforcement Learning for Policy Optimization).
- Stage I involves lightweight Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) to instill a foundational reasoning format and diverse, exploratory safety-related thought processes.
- Stage III employs RL with a mixed prompt set (harmful, harmless, ambiguous) and distinct reward functions for safety, task completion, and format adherence, preventing reward hacking and preserving reasoning capabilities.

## Results

- TARS-trained models achieve a superior safety-refusal trade-off compared to SFT, DPO, RL without reasoning, other reasoning-based defenses, and larger open-weight instruction-tuned models.
- The **1.5B** TARS model outperforms several 7-**8B** baseline models in safety, demonstrating enhanced robustness against both white-box (GCG) and black-box (PAIR) adversarial attacks.
- TARS-trained models maintain better generalization to out-of-distribution tasks (e.g., math, science, general reasoning benchmarks) compared to SFT/DPO, and in some cases, even improve upon the base model's performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Lightweight SFT is crucial for inducing diverse and exploratory reasoning traces, which are more conducive to effective refinement through subsequent reinforcement learning.
> - Mixing harmful, harmless, and ambiguous prompts with separate reward functions is essential to prevent models from learning degenerate refusal strategies and to maintain their reasoning abilities.
> - TARS-trained models develop distinct internal representations, enabling better separation of harmful and harmless prompts, and adaptively allocate more computational effort (longer reasoning) to ambiguous safety queries.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of large language models (LLMs) has led to significant advancements across various domains, including problem-solving in areas such as mathematics and code. A key characteristic of these advanced LLMs is their ability to leverage additional test-time computation for reasoning, often adaptively allocating resources based on perceived problem complexity. This approach has demonstrated improvements in performance on tasks requiring intricate thought processes.

Within the broader landscape of LLM research, a critical and evolving area is the alignment of these models with safety standards, preventing them from generating harmful or undesirable content. Existing efforts to enhance LLM safety largely fall into several categories:
- **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT):** Many methods employ SFT on curated datasets of safe examples, sometimes incorporating explicit guidelines or reasoning traces to guide model behavior. However, systematic studies on optimal SFT practices for safety reasoning are limited.
- **Reinforcement Learning (RL) and Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF):** RL-based approaches, including those leveraging human preferences, have been utilized to fine-tune LLMs for helpfulness and harmlessness. Some proprietary work has begun exploring RL with explicit reasoning for safety, but detailed methodologies remain largely undisclosed. A common challenge with RL for safety is the risk of models learning "shortcut" behaviors, such as overly conservative refusals, which can degrade overall utility.
- **Input/Output Layer Defenses:** Other defense strategies operate on the input or output layers, such as prompt filtering or output moderation, but these often lack adaptability to complex or ambiguous safety scenarios.
- **Adversarial Attacks:** Concurrently, research on adversarial attacks (e.g., jailbreaks) aims to circumvent LLM safety mechanisms. These include white-box attacks (e.g., GCG, AutoDAN) that exploit model internals and black-box attacks (e.g., PAIR, PAP) that probe models without internal access. Understanding how these attacks interact with advanced reasoning capabilities is an emerging area.

This work positions itself at the intersection of adaptive reasoning and LLM safety. It addresses a gap in systematic, open research into training LLMs to adaptively reason about safety using test-time compute. Specifically, the paper investigates how to integrate long chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning with RL for safety, aiming to prevent both harmful generations and degenerate refusal strategies. By proposing a structured recipe, TARS, the authors contribute to establishing best practices for training LLMs to robustly handle safety vulnerabilities while maintaining general utility, offering insights into the mechanisms underlying such defenses and their resilience against adversarial attacks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and systematically evaluate a training paradigm that enables Large Language Models (LLMs) to adaptively reason about the safety of their responses on a per-prompt basis. The goal is to train models that exhibit robustness to safety vulnerabilities by judiciously allocating test-time computational resources for reasoning.

This objective is driven by several key motivations:

- **Leveraging Adaptive Reasoning for Safety:** Previous work has shown that allowing LLMs to use more test-time computation for reasoning significantly enhances their performance in domains like mathematics and code. The authors hypothesize that this paradigm could naturally extend to safety, enabling models to better navigate complex or ambiguously harmful requests without resorting to blanket refusals. The motivation is to explore if such reasoning can lead to a more nuanced and effective defense against safety threats.
- **Addressing the Lack of Systematic Study:** While some proprietary research has explored reasoning for safety through Reinforcement Learning (RL), the specifics of these procedures are not publicly available. Furthermore, existing supervised fine-tuning (SFT) approaches for safety reasoning lack a systematic study of effective recipes or best practices. This paper is motivated to fill this gap by providing a comprehensive, open framework for training LLMs to reason about safety.
- **Mitigating Degenerate Refusal Strategies:** A common challenge in training LLMs for safety, particularly with RL, is the risk of models learning overly conservative refusal strategies. Naïvely optimizing for safety rewards can lead to models refusing to answer even harmless prompts, thereby degrading their overall helpfulness. This is a significant concern for reasoning models, as such shortcuts could also lead to a loss of their valuable reasoning capabilities. The research aims to develop training methods that prevent these failure modes.
- **Complexity of Safety as a Reward Signal:** Unlike domains such as math and code where correctness is often binary, safety involves nuanced judgments. There can be multiple acceptable "safe" responses, and the reward signal is not always straightforward. This motivates the need for carefully designed reward functions and training data mixtures to guide the model effectively without inducing undesirable behaviors.
- **Understanding Interaction with Adversarial Attacks:** As reasoning-capable models become more prevalent, it is crucial to understand how existing adversarial attacks (e.g., jailbreaks like GCG and PAIR), originally designed for non-reasoning models, interact with the explicit reasoning process. The authors are motivated to analyze these interactions to identify new vulnerabilities and inform the development of more robust defenses.
- **Improving Safety-Refusal Trade-offs:** Ultimately, the motivation is to achieve a superior trade-off between model safety (avoiding harmful content) and helpfulness (avoiding unnecessary refusals). The authors seek to demonstrate that adaptive reasoning can lead to models that are both safer and more useful to users.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology, termed **TARS (Training Adaptive Reasoners for Safety)**, is a reinforcement learning (RL) approach designed to train LLMs to reason about safety using chain-of-thought (CoT) traces. The overarching goal is to enable LLMs to adaptively decide the required computational effort per prompt, balancing safety with task completion.

#### 3.1. Problem Setup and Notation

The research formalizes the problem by defining a base LLM, `π_base(y|x)`, that maps an input `x` to an output `y`. The objective is to train `π_base` into `π_TARS`, which generates a reasoning/response trace `τ = (z, y)`. Here, `z` represents the reasoning block, enclosed by `<think>` and `</think>` tokens, and `y` is the final response. For harmful prompts (`x ∈ X_harmful`), `π_TARS` should produce a refusal (`y ∈ Y_R`), and for harmless prompts (`x ∈ X_harmless`), it should generate a compliant answer (`y ∈ Y_C`).

#### 3.2. Desiderata for Safety Training

The authors identify two key challenges unique to safety training compared to domains like math or code:
1. **Preventing Overly Conservative Refusal:** With numerous safe responses possible, models might find it easier to learn a blanket refusal strategy for all harmful or ambiguous prompts, rather than learning context-specific safe responses.
2. **Maintaining Reasoning Capabilities:** If models consistently default to refusals, they may not engage in specific reasoning, potentially degenerating their existing reasoning abilities.
TARS is designed with three core stages to address these issues.

#### 3.3. Stage I: Lightweight Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)

The initial stage involves a "lightweight" SFT of the base model (`π_base`) to equip it with foundational safety behaviors and the expected reasoning format.
- **Purpose:** To instill the structure of reasoning (`<think>` and `</think>` delimiters) and initial exploratory safety-related thought processes, thereby structuring the subsequent RL exploration.
- **Lightweight Training:** This stage employs a low learning rate (3 × 10^-5) and a small number of epochs (3 epochs). This is a critical design choice, as empirical results indicate that "lightweight" training, which stops early, fosters higher generation diversity. The rationale is that imperfect, exploratory reasoning traces from this stage are more conducive to further refinement through RL.
- **Data Collection:** A dataset of 1000 harmful prompts is collected from diverse sources: WildJailbreak, Aegis AI Content Safety Dataset 2.0, and SafeEdit. For each prompt, multiple (four) reasoning and answer traces are distilled from DeepSeek-R1. These traces are not required to be perfectly safe but must mimic the structure of reasoning.
- **Outcome:** The model after this stage, `π_SFT`, is capable of generating long, exploratory, and diverse reasoning traces.

#### 3.4. Stage II: Designing the Prompt Set for Reinforcement Learning

This stage focuses on curating a diverse set of prompts for RL to prevent reward hacking and maintain reasoning capabilities.
- **Motivation:** Training solely on harmful prompts with a safety reward can lead to degenerate refusal strategies and a collapse of reasoning length.
- **Prompt Mixture:** TARS uses a mix of three types of prompts:
 - **Harmful Prompts:** Collected from WildJailbreak, Aegis, and critically, additional adversarial prompts generated by "rainbow teaming" (`π_SFT`) to target its specific vulnerabilities. This ensures the model learns to defend against targeted attacks.
 - **Harmless Prompts:** Regular, non-toxic requests from UltraFeedback, intended to encourage helpfulness and reasoning in safe contexts.
 - **Ambiguous Prompts:** Prompts from OR-Bench that are often misclassified as harmful but are benign. These require genuine thought to avoid unnecessary refusals.
- **Data Ratios:** The harmful and harmless/ambiguous prompts are mixed in varying ratios (λ, where λ is the proportion of harmful prompts) to total 2000 datapoints for RL training.

#### 3.5. Stage III: Reinforcement Learning

The final stage employs a specific RL algorithm to refine `π_SFT` into `π_TARS`.
- **Algorithm:** GRPO (Generalized Reinforcement Learning for Policy Optimization).
- **Reward Design:** A key innovation is the use of separate and distinct reward functions:
 - **Safety Reward (`r_s`):** Applied to harmful prompts. It utilizes the Moderation API to obtain a harmfulness score (0-1) across various topics (e.g., harassment, hate). `r_s` is 1 if the maximum score is below 0.1, and `1 - max(s_i)` otherwise. This is based solely on the answer.
 - **Task Completion Reward (`r_n`):** Applied to harmless and ambiguous prompts. It uses GRM (General Preference Reward Model), an open-source model, to measure task success and non-refusal. The GRM output is passed through a sigmoid function to yield a score between 0 and 1.
 - **Format Reward (`r_f`):** A binary reward (1 or 0) that enforces the correct reasoning format (presence of `<think>` and `</think>` in the correct order).
 - **Total Reward (`r_total`):** Calculated as `r_f * (r_s` for harmful prompts or `r_n` for harmless prompts). This multiplicative structure ensures that correct formatting is a prerequisite for any positive reward.
- **Practical Implementation:** Qwen-2.5-1.5B-Instruct is used as the base model (`π_base`). SFT uses a learning rate of 3 × 10^-5 for 3 epochs. RL uses GRPO for 3 epochs with a learning rate of 1 × 10^-6, batch size of 32, KL coefficient of 1 × 10^-3, and 8 rollout generations. A 7B model (Qwen-2.5-7B-Instruct) is also trained at λ=0.5.

#### 3.6. Experimental Setup

- **Benchmarks:** Safety is evaluated using Harmbench (Defense Success Rate - DSR% against GCG, AutoDAN, PAIR, PAP). Compliance/non-refusal is evaluated on XSTest ("safe" subset) and WildChat (non-toxic subset), with StrongReject for refusal scoring.
- **Evaluation Protocol:** If an end-of-thinking (EOT) token (`</think>`) is present, only the subsequent portion is evaluated; otherwise, the full output is. This allows consistent comparison across reasoning and non-reasoning models.
- **Attack Implementations:** GCG is optimized to target generation immediately after the begin-of-thinking (BOT) token (`<think>`) to manipulate reasoning. PAIR uses only the model's final answer for the judge/attacker.
- **Baselines and Comparisons:** TARS is compared against existing defenses (circuit-breakers like Llama-RR, Mistral-RR), prior reasoning-based safety work (RealSafe-R1, SafeChain, Deliberative Alignment), and open-weight models (Llama-3.1/3.2). Controlled studies compare TARS with SFT (using distilled traces or guidelines), DPO (RPO with GRM ranking), and RL *without reasoning* to isolate the impact of different components.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several key findings regarding the effectiveness, mechanisms, and robustness of TARS.

#### 4.1. Effectiveness of TARS

- **Adaptive Safety Behavior:** Models trained with TARS exhibit adaptive behavior by dedicating more computational effort (longer reasoning and response outputs) to ambiguous queries, particularly in areas like "Potentially Unqualified Advice" compared to clearly harmful ones like "Hate Speech Generation." This indicates a nuanced understanding of prompt complexity.
- **Superior Safety-Refusal Trade-off:** TARS-trained models consistently achieve a better safety-refusal trade-off compared to various baselines. This includes other reasoning-based defenses (SafeChain, Deliberative Alignment), representation re-routing (Llama-RR, Mistral-RR), and larger open-weight instruction-tuned models (Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct). Notably, the 1.5B TARS model outperforms several 7-8B models, and the 7B TARS model further extends this lead.
- **Impact of Harmful/Harmless Prompt Ratios (λ):** Increasing the proportion of harmful prompts (higher λ) generally improves safety but also leads to increased refusals and shorter reasoning lengths, highlighting the inherent trade-off.

#### 4.2. Comparison to SFT, DPO, and RL (without reasoning)

- **TARS's Optimal Performance:** In a controlled study, TARS (RL with reasoning) demonstrates the most optimal safety-refusal trade-off across varying λ ratios.
- **Essential Role of RL and Reasoning:** The comparison reveals that both reinforcement learning and explicit reasoning capabilities are crucial for simultaneously improving safety and minimizing unnecessary refusals.
- **Lightweight SFT Benefits:** The initial lightweight SFT stage is shown to significantly reduce refusal rates and slightly improve safety, attributed to its ability to induce diverse and exploratory reasoning traces.
- **Limitations of SFT/DPO:** SFT and DPO-based methods struggle to generalize and are prone to overfitting on in-distribution prompts. They also show less sensitivity to changes in λ, with limited impact on safety, even with increased compute or various SFT configurations (e.g., best-of-n generations, guideline-curated traces). This suggests that dynamic exploration via a reward system (as in TARS/RL) is more effective for developing adaptivity than relying on static reasoning traces.
- **Generalization Capabilities:** TARS-trained models maintain better generalization to out-of-distribution tasks (e.g., math, science, general reasoning benchmarks like GSM8K, MATH-500, GPQA, MMLU-Pro, SQuAD) compared to SFT/DPO, and in some cases, even improve upon the base model.

#### 4.3. Mechanisms of TARS's Effectiveness

- **Internal Representation Separation:** Analysis of final-layer embeddings using UMAP reveals that TARS-trained models achieve a larger margin of separation between harmful (GCG attack) and harmless (XSTest "safe") prompts compared to models trained with SFT, DPO, or RL without reasoning. This suggests that TARS fosters internal representations that better distinguish between prompt types, contributing to its adaptive refusal decisions even before generating a full chain-of-thought.
- **Compute Allocation on Complex Prompts:** TARS adaptively allocates reasoning resources based on prompt complexity. For instance, on the Sorry-Bench dataset, clearly harmful "Hate Speech Generation" prompts elicit shorter reasoning traces, while more ambiguous "Potentially Unqualified Advice" prompts lead to significantly longer and more elaborate reasoning.

#### 4.4. Robustness to Adversarial Attacks (GCG and PAIR)

- **Enhanced Robustness:** TARS-trained models exhibit stronger robustness against both white-box (GCG) and black-box (PAIR) attacks compared to non-reasoning models or those trained to reason supervisely.
- **Attack Manifestations in Reasoning Models:** The study reveals distinct ways attacks manifest in reasoning models:
 - Under GCG, generations can include a harmful answer in place of reasoning, followed by a safe answer after the EOT token.
 - Other GCG attacks lead to an answer in place of reasoning that is a refusal, or an unfinished reasoning trace that is also safe.
- **Format Integrity and Safety:** When reasoning traces are properly formatted (containing an EOT token), TARS is substantially safer than SFT. Even when the reasoning format is broken by an attack, TARS still outperforms SFT in generating safe content. This indicates that TARS's robustness extends beyond merely maintaining formatting, influencing the safety of the generated content itself.

#### 4.5. Ablation Studies (Supporting Design Choices)

- **Lightweight SFT:** Ablations confirm that lightweight training during the SFT stage, characterized by lower learning rates and fewer epochs, is crucial. This approach encourages higher diversity and exploration in generations, leading to a wider and more optimal safety-refusal trade-off in the subsequent RL stage. Heavy SFT leads to overfitting and limits exploration.
- **Prompt Mixing and Separate Rewards:** The strategy of mixing in harmless and ambiguous prompts with separate helpfulness rewards (GRM) for these, alongside the safety reward for harmful prompts, is vital. This prevents "reward hacking," where models trained solely on harmful prompts learn to universally refuse and degenerate their reasoning capabilities. This mixing strategy leads to longer reasoning lengths and a broader safety-refusal trade-off frontier.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents TARS (Training Adaptive Reasoners for Safety), a systematic and open recipe for enhancing the safety of Large Language Models (LLMs) through adaptive, per-prompt reasoning. Its significance and potential impact span several key areas:

- **Advancement in LLM Safety Alignment:** TARS offers a novel and effective method for improving LLM safety by integrating explicit reasoning with reinforcement learning. It demonstrates that enabling models to allocate test-time compute for deliberative thought processes can lead to superior safety outcomes without compromising helpfulness. This moves beyond simpler input/output layer defenses by addressing the model's internal decision-making process.
- **Improved Safety-Refusal Trade-offs:** The work empirically shows that TARS achieves a more favorable balance between refusing harmful requests and providing compliant answers to benign ones. This capability is critical for deploying LLMs in real-world applications, where both reliability and utility are paramount. The ability of TARS models to adaptively spend more compute on ambiguous queries is a direct step toward more intelligent and context-aware safety mechanisms.
- **Insights into Training Paradigms:** The detailed ablation studies provide critical insights into the design choices for training adaptive reasoners for safety. The findings regarding the benefits of lightweight Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) for diversity, the importance of mixing harmful, harmless, and ambiguous prompts, and the efficacy of separate reward functions for safety and helpfulness offer practical guidelines for future research and development in LLM alignment. These insights help to counteract issues like reward hacking and degeneration of reasoning abilities.
- **Enhanced Robustness to Adversarial Attacks:** TARS-trained models demonstrate increased robustness against both white-box (GCG) and black-box (PAIR) jailbreaking attacks. This is a crucial contribution as adversarial attacks continue to evolve. The analysis of how these attacks interact with reasoning models reveals new vulnerabilities and defense strategies, highlighting the need for attack methodologies specifically tailored to reasoning processes rather than solely targeting final outputs.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** By providing an "open recipe" along with released models and code, the authors facilitate reproducibility and encourage further exploration in this domain. This includes investigating alternative data curation techniques, exploring different base models (e.g., uncensored models for increased exploration), and studying novel asymmetries in LLM competence for safety training. The identified distinct patterns of attack manifestation in reasoning models also open new avenues for research into more sophisticated attack and defense mechanisms.
- **Ethical Implications and Responsible AI:** The work directly contributes to the field of Responsible AI by developing tools that make LLMs safer and more reliable. By addressing safety vulnerabilities and promoting ethical behavior, TARS helps in building AI systems that are less prone to misuse and more aligned with human values. The emphasis on transparency and reproducibility underscores a commitment to open science in a sensitive area.

Overall, this research represents a significant step forward in developing robust and intelligent safety mechanisms for LLMs, paving the way for more reliable and trustworthy AI systems.
