# CARoL: Context-aware Adaptation for Robot Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> The CARoL framework enhances robot learning by quantifying task similarity through state transitions, enabling adaptive integration of relevant prior knowledge for new tasks. This approach leads to faster policy convergence and higher rewards in simulated environments and facilitates successful sim-to-real transfer for physical robot navigation.

## Problem

- Reinforcement Learning (RL) for robotics is computationally inefficient for new tasks, often requiring extensive trial-and-error.
- Existing knowledge fusion methods in multi-task learning frequently combine prior knowledge indiscriminately, leading to suboptimal performance or negative gradient interference.
- A mechanism was needed to adaptively integrate only the most relevant prior knowledge into diverse RL algorithms for accelerated and effective learning on novel tasks.

## Method

- Develops **CARoL**, a ==three-step framework==: context representation via a learned ==state transition function== that predicts the next state from a current state and action for each source task, contextual similarity evaluation, and adaptive knowledge integration.
- Quantifies ==contextual similarity== by evaluating how accurately source transition functions predict dynamics from target task trajectories, then normalizes these evaluations into ==prioritized similarity weights==.
- Adaptively integrates relevant prior knowledge into target task learning using ==tailored loss functions== designed for policy-based, value-based, and actor-critic reinforcement learning algorithms.

## Results

- In simulated CarRacing and LunarLander tasks, CARoL consistently achieved faster convergence and higher accumulated rewards compared to baseline methods like Policy Distillation and Learning from Scratch.
- The framework's contextual similarity weights accurately identified task relationships, correlating inversely with friction differences in CarRacing and non-linearly with geometric distances in LunarLander.
- CARoL demonstrated successful sim-to-real transfer on a physical ground vehicle, achieving a **100%** success rate and improved navigation stability on off-road terrain using policies adapted from simulation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - State transition functions inherently capture combined robot-environment dynamics, proving to be direct and interpretable contextual indicators for task similarity.
> - Explicitly quantifying task relevance by evaluating source transition functions on target trajectories enables targeted prioritization of prior knowledge, bypassing issues with indiscriminate knowledge fusion.
> - Tailoring the adaptation of prioritized knowledge to specific RL paradigms (e.g., policy-based, value-based, actor-critic) significantly improves learning efficiency and stability on new tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has demonstrated capabilities in complex robotic control and task learning across various domains, including autonomous navigation and manipulation. However, RL methods typically require extensive trial-and-error exploration, rendering them computationally inefficient, particularly when learning new tasks from scratch. This inefficiency is a significant barrier to the widespread application of RL in robotics.

The broader research landscape addresses this challenge through approaches such as knowledge fusion and learning adaptation. These methods aim to leverage knowledge acquired from previously solved tasks to accelerate training on new, related tasks.

**Multi-task Reinforcement Learning** is one area that seeks to improve sample efficiency and generalization by enabling simultaneous learning of multiple tasks. Techniques in this field include hierarchical Bayesian frameworks, shared embedding spaces, and policy distillation. While these methods can improve data efficiency, they often suffer from negative gradient interference between tasks and can struggle when faced with unknown task structures, as they tend to fuse knowledge indiscriminately without considering the specific context of a new task.

**Learning Adaptation for Robotics** focuses on guaranteeing performance on new tasks through various mechanisms. **Lifelong learning** aims to continuously acquire, adapt, and transfer knowledge over a robot's operational lifespan, maintaining a single model that evolves over time. Examples include using Gradient Episodic Memory, federated learning, dynamic network structures, and Large Language Models (LLMs) for planning. However, lifelong learning can be susceptible to catastrophic forgetting and task order bias, and contradicting gradients may negatively impact the acquisition of new knowledge. **Meta-learning** offers another adaptation mechanism by training a meta-model with initial parameters that can quickly adapt to new tasks. Methods like Model-Agnostic Meta-Learning (MAML) learn initializations for fast policy convergence. A limitation of meta-learning, in the context of this work, is its tendency to treat all prior knowledge equally, lacking a mechanism for dynamic prioritization based on the relevance to a new task.

**Context-awareness for Reinforcement Learning** aims to make learning models adaptive by augmenting inputs with contextual information. Context can be represented as latent encodings of environments or robots, or through contextual Markov Decision Processes (MDPs). Existing approaches typically rely on data-driven mechanisms to infer context and map its impact on model outputs.

This work, CARoL, positions itself within this landscape by addressing the limitations of indiscriminate knowledge fusion and equal treatment of prior knowledge. It proposes a novel approach to context-awareness that directly uses state transitions as contextual indicators, distinguishing itself from methods that rely on latent representations of environments or robots. By explicitly quantifying task similarity based on state transitions, CARoL aims to enable more targeted and efficient adaptation of prior knowledge, bridging the gap between existing multi-task learning and adaptation methods.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind this research is to enhance the efficiency of Reinforcement Learning (RL) for robotics, particularly when learning new tasks that are similar to, but distinct from, previously mastered ones. Learning robotic tasks from scratch using RL is computationally intensive and time-consuming, making it impractical for many real-world applications where robots frequently encounter novel scenarios.

To address this, the authors aim to leverage prior knowledge from existing tasks to accelerate the learning process for new tasks. This approach, however, introduces two critical challenges that form the core objectives of the paper:

1. **Quantifying the relevance of existing knowledge to a new task:** Prior knowledge comprises various skills or models learned in different contexts. For efficient adaptation, it is crucial to determine which pieces of existing knowledge are most pertinent to the specific demands of a new task. Indiscriminate fusion of all available knowledge, as seen in some multi-task learning approaches, can lead to suboptimal performance due to the inclusion of irrelevant or conflicting information. The motivation is to move beyond "unaware" knowledge fusion by developing a mechanism to selectively prioritize knowledge based on the environment's context.

2. **Adaptively integrating relevant knowledge into the learning process:** Once the relevance of prior knowledge is quantified, an effective mechanism is required to integrate this prioritized information into the learning algorithm for the new task. This integration should be flexible enough to apply across different types of RL algorithms (e.g., policy-based, value-based, actor-critic) and should enable the new task's learning to converge faster and achieve higher performance.

CARoL's central motivation stems from the observation that many robotic skills are context-specific. Instead of relying on environmental or robot representations, the authors motivate the use of **state transition representations as contextual indicators**. The rationale is that state transitions are fundamental to Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) and inherently capture the combined effects of both the environment and the robot's interaction with it. For instance, different physical terrains might exhibit similar state transitions (e.g., wheel traction loss on a steep incline or an icy surface), suggesting a common underlying dynamic that can be exploited for adaptation. This approach aims to provide a more direct, comprehensive, and interpretable measure of task similarity, circumventing the need to manually select features from environments or robots.

Ultimately, the key objective is to propose a framework that can efficiently learn a new task by intelligently prioritizing and adapting specific pieces of prior knowledge based on their contextual similarity, thereby significantly enhancing the learning efficiency and generalization capabilities of robotic systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Context-aware Adaptation for Robot Learning (CARoL) framework is designed to facilitate efficient learning for new tasks by leveraging prior knowledge. It operates in three main steps, as illustrated in its architecture (Fig. 2): acquiring context representation and prior knowledge, evaluating contextual similarities, and performing contextual knowledge adaptation.

**Step 1: Acquiring Context Representation and Prior Knowledge for Each Source Task**
For each source task $T_i$, CARoL first acquires two components:
- **Prior Knowledge ($K_i$):** This represents the learned solution for the source task. Depending on the RL algorithm, $K_i$ can be a pre-trained policy $\pi_i(s)$, a state-action value function $Q_i(s,a)$, or an actor-critic pair comprising both.
- **Context Representation:** Alongside $K_i$, an approximation of the transition function $f_i(s_i, a_i | \phi_i) : S \times A \rightarrow S$ is trained for each source task. This function predicts the next state $s^+$ given a current state $s_i$ and action $a_i$. The parameters $\phi_i$ are updated via gradient descent by minimizing the squared difference between the predicted next state and the true next state observed from collected trajectories (Equations 1 and 2). This $f_i(\cdot)$ serves as the explicit contextual marker for the task.

**Step 2: Evaluating Contextual Similarity**
This step quantifies how relevant each piece of source knowledge is to the new target task $T_g$.
- Instead of learning a new transition function $f_g(\cdot)$ for the target task, which can be computationally expensive, CARoL utilizes sampled trajectories $\{(s_{g,k}, a_{g,k}, s^+_{g,k})\}$ collected from the target task.
- These target trajectories are then fed into each pre-trained source transition function $f_i(\cdot)$. For each source task $i$, a measure $Y_i$ is computed, representing the sum of squared prediction errors of $f_i(\cdot)$ on the target trajectories (Equation 3). A smaller $Y_i$ value indicates a higher contextual similarity between the $i$-th source task and the target task, meaning $f_i(\cdot)$ is better at predicting the dynamics of the target task.
- These $Y_i$ values are then normalized into similarity weights $w_i$ using a softmax function (Equation 4). This ensures that the weights sum to one and provides a prioritized distribution over the source tasks, where higher weights correspond to more relevant prior knowledge.

**Step 3: Contextual Knowledge Adaptation**
The calculated similarity weights $w_i$ are used to adapt the relevant prior knowledge to the target task. CARoL provides specific adaptation mechanisms for different RL algorithm types:

- **CARoL for Policy-based RL (Algorithm 1):**
 - Assumes prior knowledge $K_i$ consists of pre-trained policies $\pi_i(s)$.
 - The target policy $\pi_g(s|\theta_g)$ is initialized and interacts with the target environment to collect trajectories.
 - A learning loss $L_P$ is defined as a weighted sum of Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergences between the target policy's action distribution and the action distributions of each source policy, with weights $w_i$ (Equation 5). This encourages the target policy to mimic relevant source policies.
 - The parameters $\theta_g$ of the target policy are updated iteratively through gradient-based optimization to minimize $L_P$.

- **CARoL for Value-based RL (Algorithm 2):**
 - Assumes prior knowledge $K_i$ consists of pre-trained state-action value functions $Q_i(s,a)$.
 - The target value function $Q_g(s,a|\psi_g)$ is initialized. Actions are selected using an epsilon-greedy strategy, and collected data is stored in a replay buffer.
 - For each data sample, the "next Q-value" ($Q_{next}$) is computed as a weighted average of the maximum Q-values from each source $Q_i(s,a)$ for the next state $s^+$ (Equation 7).
 - The learning loss $L_Q$ is defined as the mean squared error between the target $Q_g(s,a|\psi_g)$ and the Bellman target $(r + \gamma Q_{next})$, where $r$ is the reward and $\gamma$ is the discount factor (Equation 8).
 - The parameters $\psi_g$ of the target value function are updated iteratively.

- **CARoL for Actor-Critic RL (Algorithm 3):**
 - Assumes prior knowledge $K_i$ consists of pre-trained actor-critic pairs ($\pi_i(s)$, $Q_i(s,a)$).
 - The focus is on learning a target actor $\pi_g(s|\theta_g)$, while the source critic functions $Q_i(s,a)$ are leveraged through a static fusion method to avoid instability issues associated with simultaneous actor-critic updates.
 - The total learning loss $L_{AC}$ is a combination of two terms (Equation 10):
 - $L_P$ (from policy-based CARoL): Measures the weighted divergence from source actors (Equation 5).
 - $L_C$: Measures the negative weighted sum of $Q_i(s, \pi_g(s|\theta_g))$, where actions are chosen by the current target actor. This term aims to maximize the value estimated by the fused source critics (Equation 9).
 - A parameter $\beta$ balances the importance of $L_P$ and $L_C$. The target actor parameters $\theta_g$ are updated to minimize $L_{AC}$.

**CARoL+ Extension:** The paper also describes CARoL+ (Remark 1), an extension where standard RL loss terms for the target task can be added to the CARoL loss functions ($L_P$, $L_Q$, or $L_{AC}$). This allows the model to not only adapt prior knowledge but also learn directly from the target environment through interaction-based rewards, potentially leading to higher final performance when prior knowledge is insufficient.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The effectiveness and generalizability of CARoL were evaluated through experiments on two simulated robotic platforms (CarRacing and LunarLander) and a physical ground vehicle.

**A. Simulated Experiment: CarRacing**
- **Contextual Similarity:** Table I indicates that the contextual similarity weights derived by CARoL are inversely related to the friction difference between source and target tasks. Source tasks with friction levels closer to the target task consistently received higher similarity weights. For target tasks identical to a source task (e.g., $\mu=0.5$), the corresponding source task was assigned the highest weight, confirming the mechanism's ability to identify task similarity.
- **Convergence and Reward:** Figure 4 illustrates that CARoL consistently achieved faster convergence speeds and generally higher final rewards compared to baseline methods.
 - When the target environment matched a source environment (subplots b, d, g), CARoL's performance was comparable to that of the directly applied well-trained Source Knowledge (SK) policies.
 - Compared to Policy Distillation (PD), which indiscriminately fuses knowledge, CARoL demonstrated better performance and more stable training processes, exhibiting fewer oscillations in reward curves.
 - Learning from Scratch (LfS) struggled to converge in most tasks within the allotted training steps, particularly in challenging, low-friction environments, highlighting its inefficiency.
 - Model-Agnostic Meta-Learning (MAML) performed poorly, suggesting potential issues with generalization or insufficient meta-training, and was noted as computationally expensive.

**B. Simulated Experiment: LunarLander**
- **Contextual Similarity:** Table II showed that contextual similarity weights were generally negatively correlated with the geometric distance between source and target tasks in the gravity-wind power configuration space. The relationship was observed to be non-linear, indicating that state transitions capture complex interactions beyond simple parameter proximity. Similar to CarRacing, target tasks identical to a source task (subplots a-d) were correctly identified with high weights for the corresponding source.
- **Convergence and Reward:** Figure 6 demonstrates CARoL's superior performance in terms of faster convergence and higher accumulated rewards.
 - For target tasks identical to source tasks (a-d), CARoL achieved rewards comparable to the best SK, though a slight performance gap was noted in one instance where the weight assigned to the most relevant source was not exceptionally high.
 - For novel target tasks, CARoL consistently outperformed any single SK policy by effectively combining knowledge based on contextual similarity.
 - PD again showed lower reward convergence and oscillations compared to CARoL.
 - LfS exhibited significantly slower convergence and generally lower final rewards, especially in tasks requiring substantial learning.
 - CARoL+ (which integrates additional interaction-based learning from the target task) achieved the highest reward across all cases. It compensated for instances where CARoL alone might not reach the absolute maximum reward (e.g., tasks (i) and (j), where LfS occasionally outperformed CARoL due to the target task being distant from source knowledge), while maintaining CARoL's fast and stable convergence properties.

**C. Real-World Experiment: Ground Vehicle Off-road Navigation**
- **Adaptation Performance:** CARoL was applied to adapt policies learned in simulation to a physical ground vehicle navigating real-world off-road terrain. The adaptation involved collecting limited real-world trajectories to compute contextual similarity, followed by on-policy adaptation using CARoL-Algorithm 1.
- **Success Rate and Stability:** As detailed in Table III, CARoL achieved a 100% success rate (5/5 trials) in autonomously navigating from start to goal. The adapted policy also demonstrated the most stable navigation, exhibiting lower average roll (4.08° $\pm$ 4.65°) and pitch (8.55° $\pm$ 5.13°) angles compared to directly deployed source knowledge policies (SK1 and SK2).
- **Comparison to Source Knowledge:** SK1 (trained on concrete terrain) and SK2 (trained on grass terrain) showed lower success rates (3/5 and 2/5, respectively) and higher roll/pitch angles, indicating poorer generalization and stability when directly applied to the novel rocky terrain. While CARoL took a longer average time to reach the goal (16.88s $\pm$ 1.97s), this was expected given no time penalty was imposed, suggesting the robot prioritized stable and successful traversal.

In summary, the experiments collectively demonstrated that CARoL effectively quantifies contextual similarity using state transitions and leverages this to prioritize and adapt prior knowledge. This resulted in faster convergence, higher rewards, and improved stability across diverse simulated and real-world robotic tasks compared to various baselines. The CARoL+ extension further showcased the benefits of combining adaptation with explicit learning from the target environment.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces CARoL, a framework addressing a fundamental challenge in robotic reinforcement learning: the inefficiency of learning new tasks from scratch and the difficulties in effectively leveraging existing prior knowledge. The significance of this work lies in its novel approach to context-aware adaptation, its broad applicability, and its demonstrated effectiveness in both simulated and real-world scenarios.

**Novel Approach to Context Awareness:** CARoL's explicit use of state transitions as contextual indicators represents a significant contribution. Unlike existing context-aware methods that often rely on latent encodings of environments or robots, state transitions inherently capture the combined dynamics of the robot and its interaction with the environment. This offers a more direct, interpretable, and potentially robust measure of task similarity, which is crucial for targeted knowledge adaptation. By quantifying relevance based on state dynamics, CARoL moves beyond indiscriminate knowledge fusion, enabling more precise and effective transfer learning.

**Broad Applicability and Generality:** The framework's design allows for integration with various types of RL algorithms, including policy-based, value-based, and actor-critic methods. This broad applicability increases CARoL's utility across diverse robotic learning problems and underscores its potential as a general mechanism for knowledge adaptation. The ability to enhance different RL paradigms with context-aware knowledge transfer strengthens its foundational impact on the field.

**Demonstrated Effectiveness and Efficiency:** The empirical results consistently show that CARoL enables faster convergence and higher reward attainment compared to several baseline methods across multiple tasks. In simulated environments like CarRacing and LunarLander, CARoL consistently achieved superior learning efficiency and performance. This accelerated learning can drastically reduce the computational resources and time required to deploy robots in new, similar tasks.

**Real-world Applicability:** Perhaps most notably, CARoL demonstrated its capability to successfully adapt policies learned in simulation to a physical ground vehicle navigating complex off-road terrain. This successful sim-to-real transfer, particularly in a data-scarce real-world setting, highlights the practical utility and robustness of the framework. The ability to rapidly and stably adapt policies to challenging real-world conditions holds substantial promise for advancing autonomous robotics.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Reduced Training Costs:** By leveraging prior knowledge effectively, CARoL has the potential to significantly reduce the extensive data collection and computational resources typically required for training RL agents in new robotic tasks, thereby lowering the barrier to entry for many applications.
- **Faster Deployment and Adaptability:** Robots equipped with CARoL could be deployed more rapidly into novel or dynamic environments, adapting quickly to unforeseen variations without needing to relearn from scratch. This enhances the autonomy and operational flexibility of robotic systems.
- **Improved Robustness and Generalization:** The targeted adaptation provided by CARoL can lead to more robust policies that generalize better to new conditions, as the system intelligently prioritizes and integrates relevant experiences, avoiding interference from irrelevant or conflicting knowledge.
- **Advancement of Transfer Learning in Robotics:** CARoL contributes a novel and effective mechanism to the field of transfer learning in robotics, particularly for scenarios where task dynamics vary but share underlying principles.
- **Future Research Directions:** The paper also outlines future work, such as integrating context similarity evaluation synchronously into the adaptation process and extending CARoL to multi-agent environments. These directions suggest a clear path for further developing and expanding the framework's capabilities and impact.

In conclusion, CARoL offers a method for efficiently adapting robotic policies by leveraging context-aware knowledge transfer, addressing a critical bottleneck in the deployment of RL-driven robots and paving the way for more autonomous and adaptable robotic systems.
