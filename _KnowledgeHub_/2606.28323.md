# DexCompose: Reusing Dexterous Policies for Multi-Task Manipulation with a Single Hand

> [!summary] Summary
> DexCompose is a framework developed at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill for composing pretrained dexterous manipulation policies, allowing a single robotic hand to execute multiple tasks sequentially. It effectively mitigates destructive interference and scales by assigning finger-level action ownership and using dual residual stabilization, achieving a 77.4% average composite success rate across 16 tasks, an improvement of 15.8 percentage points over existing methods.

## Problem

- Directly chaining independently trained single-task policies often leads to unreliable performance due to conflicting demands on the shared action space of a dexterous hand.
- Training a distinct, specialized policy for every possible composite task combination is computationally and data-inefficient, limiting scalability for complex multi-skill scenarios.
- Existing methods for multi-task dexterous manipulation often rely on predefined finger allocations or require task-specific integration during initial grasp generation, limiting flexibility and generalizability across diverse task combinations.

## Method

- **DexCompose** is a ==two-stage pipeline== that identifies and exploits redundant hand degrees of freedom within already trained policies to facilitate new behaviors.
- A ==Finger Attribution== stage uses data-driven ==release tests== and an ==LLM-based agent== to identify which fingers are essential for preserving the initial task and which can be released for a downstream task, assigning ==finger-level action ownership==.
- A ==Dual Residual Stabilizer== stage applies ==PPO==-trained residual policies to generate targeted corrections within structurally assigned action subspaces, enabling stable grasp preservation (Task A) and adaptive execution of the downstream interaction (Task B) without retraining base policies.

## Results

- DexCompose achieved an average composite success rate of **77.4%** across **16** diverse dexterous multi-task combinations, demonstrating robust performance.
- The framework consistently outperformed all baselines, showing a **15.8 percentage point** improvement over the strongest comparison method ('FullRes' residual learning).
- Ablation studies confirmed that the Task A residual, finger allocation, and action masking were critical components for success, with the Task A residual being essential for maintaining the initial task outcome.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicit finger-level action ownership and structural enforcement of action masks are crucial for mitigating destructive interference when composing pretrained policies on a high-DoF dexterous hand.
> - Treating 'idle' or redundant degrees of freedom within a trained policy as a reusable resource can significantly enhance the efficiency and versatility of dexterous manipulation systems for multi-task scenarios.
> - A dual residual stabilization approach, which separately stabilizes the initial task and adapts the downstream task, provides robust composition without requiring modification or retraining of the original base policies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Dexterous manipulation is a complex field in robotics, focusing on enabling robotic hands to perform intricate tasks. A recurring challenge is the ability to compose multiple manipulation skills sequentially or concurrently using a single hand, especially when one skill's outcome must be maintained while another is executed. For instance, grasping an object and then manipulating it with the same hand without dropping it.

Existing approaches to dexterous policy composition often face limitations. Directly chaining independently trained single-task policies typically leads to unreliable performance due to conflicting demands on the shared action space of the robot hand. The downstream policy may inadvertently interfere with the established state of the first task, such as overwriting the finger motions essential for maintaining a grasp. An alternative, training a distinct policy for every possible composite task, becomes computationally and data-inefficient, scaling poorly with the number of skills involved.

Previous work has explored leveraging selective hand degrees-of-freedom (DoF) allocation to enable multi-object or multi-functional interactions. Examples include MultiGrasp for multi-object grasping, DexMulti for concurrent prehensile and non-prehensile tasks, and HANDFUL for resource-aware grasping that reserves fingers for subsequent subtasks. While these methods demonstrate the potential of resource allocation, they often rely on predefined finger allocations or integrate downstream task considerations during initial grasp generation or policy training. This limits their flexibility and scalability across diverse, unforeseen task combinations.

Simultaneously, the development of large-scale dexterous grasp datasets, such as DexGraspNet, UniDexGrasp, and UniDexGrasp++, has led to the availability of robust, reusable full-hand grasp controllers. This growing accessibility of powerful single-task policies creates a need for methods that can effectively *reuse* these pretrained full-hand controllers in composite scenarios without requiring extensive retraining for each new task combination.

This paper addresses the gap by focusing on a post-hoc composition framework for *pretrained* full-hand dexterous manipulation policies. It reframes the problem as identifying and exploiting redundant hand DoFs within already trained policies to facilitate new behaviors, rather than relying on prior knowledge of finger allocation or end-to-end retraining. This approach aims to reduce destructive interference between tasks that share the same high-DoF multi-fingered hand, offering a more scalable and flexible path for composite dexterous manipulation. The work builds upon general principles of policy composition and residual learning, but specifically adapts them to the challenges of dexterous hands with explicit finger-level action ownership.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust and scalable framework for composing pretrained dexterous manipulation policies to perform multiple tasks with a single robotic hand. This framework specifically aims to address the challenge where the outcome of an initial manipulation skill must be preserved while a new, distinct interaction task is executed concurrently or sequentially using the same hand.

The motivation for this work stems from several key observations and limitations in current dexterous manipulation research:

1. **Destructive Interference in Shared Action Space:** When chaining two single-task policies, the downstream policy, designed to control the entire hand, often overrides the finger motions essential for maintaining the outcome of the first task (e.g., a stable grasp). This leads to conflicting demands on overlapping fingers and contact modes, resulting in destructive interference and task failure. The research seeks to mitigate this by managing how finger actions are allocated across objectives.

2. **Scalability Limitations of Task-Specific Training:** Training a new, specialized policy for every possible composite task combination (e.g., *n* grasp-maintenance skills combined with *m* downstream interactions, requiring *n* × *m* policies) is impractical and resource-intensive. This motivates a compositional approach that leverages and reuses existing pretrained single-task policies, thereby improving efficiency and generalizability.

3. **Exploiting Redundancy in High-DoF Hands:** Modern dexterous hands possess many degrees of freedom. While a full-hand policy might be initially trained for a specific task, certain fingers or joint DoFs may be "idle" or redundant for maintaining the task outcome once it is established. The motivation is to identify and utilize these redundant DoFs as a reusable resource for executing new, secondary behaviors, thus reframing task combinations as a resource-allocation problem at the embodiment level.

4. **Advancements in Single-Task Policy Learning:** The increasing availability of large-scale dexterous grasp datasets and robust single-task controllers (e.g., diffusion policies for grasping) provides a foundation of powerful individual skills. The motivation is to build upon these advancements by enabling their flexible composition, rather than requiring their retraining or redesign for composite tasks.

To achieve its objective, the research decomposes the problem into two sub-problems:
- **Discovery:** Identifying which specific action dimensions (e.g., individual fingers or joints) are truly necessary for preserving the outcome of the first task and which are available for reassignment.
- **Composition:** Chaining different policies by ensuring each policy operates only within its allocated DoFs, thereby enabling concurrent realization of their objectives without destructive interference.

Ultimately, the goal is to enable robust multi-skill manipulation by structurally assigning action ownership and adaptively stabilizing the manipulation outcome without necessitating the retraining of the original base policies for every composite task.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework, DexCompose, is a two-stage pipeline designed to compose pretrained dexterous manipulation policies. It addresses the sub-problems of finger-level resource discovery and action composition through a dual residual stabilization approach. The framework operates on two frozen, pretrained diffusion policies, $\pi_A$ for an initial skill (e.g., grasping) and $\pi_B$ for a downstream interaction skill.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The action space is defined by wrist position ($p_t$), wrist rotation ($r_t$), and hand joint values ($q_t$), resulting in a $d$-dimensional action vector. The objective is to produce a composed action $a_{comp,t} = F_{\Theta}(o_t, a_{A,t}, a_{B,t})$ that maximizes the probability of both Task A preservation ($S_A(\tau)$) and Task B success ($S_B(\tau)$) throughout a rollout $\tau$.

#### 3.2. Stage 1: Finger Attribution (Discovery)

This stage identifies which fingers are essential for preserving the Task A outcome and which can be released for Task B, thereby assigning finger-level action ownership.
1. **Held-State Collection:** The framework first collects a dataset of successful post-grasp states ($D_{hold}$) from the execution of the pretrained Task A policy. Each state includes the hand configuration ($q_{ref}$) and object pose.
2. **Release Tests:** For each candidate binary finger mask $m \in \{0, 1\}^F$ (where $F=5$ for five fingers), a release test is performed.
 - In each test, a held state is restored.
 - Fingers designated by $m_f=1$ (preserved) replay their reference grasp configuration ($q_{ref}$).
 - Fingers designated by $m_f=0$ (released) are gradually opened towards a fully open pose over $T_{test}$ steps.
3. **Metric Evaluation:** Each mask is evaluated based on two metrics:
 - **Retention Rate ($P_{ret}(m)$):** The proportion of tests where Task A (object retention) remains successful throughout the release.
 - **Clean-Release Rate ($P_{clean}(m)$):** The proportion of tests where released fingers disengage without sustained contact, indicating they are free for Task B.
4. **Mask Selection:** A task-aware allocation agent (implemented using an LLM, e.g., OpenAI GPT-5.4) selects the optimal mask $m^*$ from the candidates. The agent considers $P_{ret}(m)$, $P_{clean}(m)$, and $\eta_B(m)$ (residual dexterity available for Task B) to balance grasp stability, clean release, and functional dexterity for the downstream task. This selection process aims to preserve functionally important fingers, such as the index finger, even if it means a slightly lower immediate retention rate compared to a purely retention-maximizing heuristic. The selected finger mask $m^*$ is then expanded into a joint-level mask $\bar{m}^*$ for hand joints.

#### 3.3. Stage 2: Dual Residual Stabilizer (Composition)

Once $m^*$ is determined, it defines action ownership for execution: Task A controls preserved fingers, and Task B controls the wrist and released fingers.
1. **Task A Residual Stabilizer:**
 - At the beginning of a composite rollout, after Task A success, the Task A hand reference configuration $q_{ref}$ is stored.
 - A bounded Task A residual policy ($\pi_{A_{res}}$) is trained via Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). This policy predicts a joint-space correction $\Delta q_{A,t}$.
 - The Task A preservation command is $q_{A,pres,t} = q_{ref} + \bar{m}^* \odot \Delta q_{A,t}$. This ensures that only preserved fingers receive corrections to stabilize the grasp, with the wrist actions explicitly set to zero as they are managed by Task B.
 - The Task A residual module is initialized to produce near-zero corrections, allowing it to learn to compensate for disturbances caused by downstream motion and redundant finger release.

2. **Task B Residual:**
 - The frozen downstream policy $\pi_B$ produces a nominal action $a_{B,t}$.
 - A separate bounded Task B residual policy ($\pi_{B_{res}}$) is trained via PPO. This policy predicts a correction $\Delta a_{B,t}$.
 - This correction is applied *only* within the Task B-owned action subspace (wrist and released fingers), enforced by mask $M_B^*$: $\Delta a_{B,t} = M_B^* \odot (\beta_B \odot \tanh(\pi_{B_{res}}(o_t, a_{B,t}, m^*)))$.
 - The Task B execution command is $a_{B,exec,t} = a_{B,t} + \Delta a_{B,t}$. This adapts the frozen $\pi_B$ policy to the constraints imposed by the maintained grasp and the newly assigned action space.

3. **Final Composed Action:** The final action $a_{comp,t}$ is assembled by combining the masked outputs of the Task A preservation command and the Task B execution command: $a_{comp,t} = M_A^* \odot a_{A,pres,t} + M_B^* \odot a_{B,exec,t}$. The masks $M_A^*$ and $M_B^*$ enforce the structural action ownership.

#### 3.4. Training Details

Both residual modules ($\pi_{A_{res}}$ and $\pi_{B_{res}}$) are trained using PPO. The $\pi_{A_{res}}$ is trained first to stabilize the held object against general perturbations. Subsequently, $\pi_{B_{res}}$ is trained with $\pi_A$ and $\pi_B$ fixed, optionally fine-tuning $\pi_{A_{res}}$ with a reduced learning rate to adapt to Task B-specific disturbances. The base policies $\pi_A$ and $\pi_B$ are never updated.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of DexCompose was conducted on 16 composite dexterous manipulation tasks, comprising four object-retention skills (GraspBall, PourMug, PickCan, PickStick) and four downstream interaction skills (OpenDoor, PushButton, OpenMicrowave, TurnOnSwitch). Performance was primarily measured by the Composite Success Rate, which requires both Task A preservation and Task B completion.

1. **Overall Performance:** DexCompose achieved an average composite success rate of 77.4% across all 16 task combinations. This indicates the framework's capability in enabling robust multi-skill manipulation with a single hand.

2. **Comparison with Baselines (Table 1):**
 - **"Frozen grasp" (Direct Chaining):** This baseline, which directly links Task A and Task B policies while freezing Task A fingers, largely failed, achieving only 3.09% average success. This highlights the severe interference caused by direct policy chaining.
 - **"Decomposed Action Space":** This method, which uses predefined action space decomposition, showed improvement over direct chaining but remained unstable across tasks, achieving 45.61% success.
 - **"Residual Learning" ("FullRes"):** This baseline, employing a learned residual correction without explicit action masking, achieved 61.60% average success.
 - **"Ours-ZS" (DexCompose without Task B Residual):** This variant, which includes finger allocation and the Task A stabilizer but applies the frozen Task B policy directly, achieved 69.23% average success.
 - **DexCompose ("Ours"):** The full DexCompose framework consistently outperformed all baselines, demonstrating a 15.8 percentage point improvement over the strongest baseline ("FullRes"). Significant gains were observed particularly on challenging tasks like OpenMicrowave and TurnOnSwitch.

3. **Base-Policy Preservation (Figure 3):** DexCompose exhibited high preservation ratios for both Task A (0.811) and Task B (0.893). In contrast, "Frozen grasp" had very low Task A preservation (0.102), indicating frequent failure of the initial skill. This suggests that DexCompose not only effectively composes policies but also maintains the original capabilities of the base policies, residing in the upper-right region of the preservation-versus-composition plot.

4. **Failure-Mode Analysis (Figure 4):**
 - Transition failures were identified as the dominant source of failure across task combinations, underscoring the importance of the Task A stabilizer.
 - Tasks involving more significant interaction dynamics, such as TurnOnSwitch, led to more preservation failures (e.g., object drops), while tasks like OpenMicrowave showed more Task B failures even when the object was retained, indicating inherent difficulty in actuating under grasp constraints.

5. **Component Ablations (Table 2):**
 - **Removal of Task A Residual:** This caused the most severe performance degradation (average 9.13% success), confirming its essential role in preserving the first-task outcome.
 - **Removal of Finger Allocation and Action Masking:** Both components substantially reduced success rates (54.52% for -Finger, 59.13% for -Mask). This indicates that explicit ownership assignment and structural enforcement of action subspaces are critical for mitigating cross-task interference. The "−Mask" result, specifically, showed that training an unmasked residual (as in conventional residual learning) performed worse than the masked variant, validating the benefit of structural ownership.
 - **Removal of Task B Residual and Transition Stage:** These components had smaller but meaningful effects, contributing to execution refinement and stage-switching stability. The overall ablation study revealed a hierarchical contribution, with Task A preservation being foundational, followed by interference reduction through finger allocation and action masking, and then further robustness improvements from the Task B residual and transition stage.

6. **Agent-Based Mask Selection (Table 3, Figure 5):**
 - The LLM-based mask selection outperformed a heuristic baseline (73.0% vs. 66.5% mean success).
 - The LLM's ability to incorporate semantic reasoning about finger utility for downstream tasks (e.g., preserving the index finger for button pressing), rather than merely maximizing immediate object retention, was shown to be advantageous. For example, in GraspBall+OpenDoor, the LLM selected a grasp that kept the index finger free for door manipulation, even if it had slightly lower immediate retention than a thumb-index grasp chosen by the heuristic.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents DexCompose as a framework that offers several significant contributions to the field of dexterous manipulation and robotics:

1. **Scalable Policy Composition:** DexCompose provides a method for composing *pretrained* single-task dexterous manipulation policies. This significantly enhances scalability by enabling the reuse of existing learned skills for new composite tasks, bypassing the need to train specialized policies for every *n* × *m* task combination. This reduces data requirements, computational costs, and development time for deploying robots in multi-task scenarios.

2. **Effective Conflict Resolution:** The framework introduces a mechanism for managing the inherent conflicts that arise when multiple tasks share the same high-DoF action space of a dexterous hand. By explicitly assigning finger-level action ownership through a data-driven attribution process and enforcing these assignments with structural action masks and dual residual stabilizers, DexCompose effectively reduces destructive interference between grasp preservation and downstream interaction.

3. **Exploiting Embodiment Redundancy:** The work highlights the concept of treating "idle" or redundant degrees of freedom within a trained policy as a reusable resource. This perspective shift allows for a more efficient utilization of the robot's physical capabilities, enabling a single hand to perform complex sequences of actions that would otherwise require multiple specialized policies or human intervention.

4. **Robustness through Dual Residual Stabilization:** The dual residual stabilizer, comprising a Task A preservation residual and a Task B execution residual, is shown to be critical for the framework's robustness. The Task A residual maintains grasp stability against disturbances, while the Task B residual adapts the downstream policy to the constrained hand state. This modular approach allows for targeted corrections without altering the core behaviors of the frozen base policies.

5. **Improved Task-Aware Resource Allocation:** The use of an LLM-based agent for finger mask selection demonstrates the potential for incorporating higher-level, semantic reasoning into resource allocation decisions. This allows for a more functional trade-off between immediate task stability and downstream dexterity, leading to more successful overall task completion compared to purely numerical heuristics.

**Potential Impact:**
The implications of DexCompose extend to developing more autonomous, versatile, and adaptable robotic systems. Robots equipped with this framework could:
- Perform intricate multi-stage assembly tasks where objects must be manipulated and held simultaneously.
- Operate in unstructured environments where objects need to be grasped and then interacted with using tools or environmental features.
- Reduce the engineering effort in developing new manipulation capabilities, by facilitating the combination of existing skills rather than requiring ground-up development for each new composite task.
- Serve as a foundational component for more complex, long-horizon manipulation planning that involves sequencing and composing multiple dexterous skills.

While the current framework focuses on composing two sequential skills, its success points toward a promising direction for creating truly generalizable dexterous manipulation systems that can flexibly combine learned skills, fostering advancements in robotic intelligence and dexterity.
