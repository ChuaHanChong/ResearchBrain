# Proprioceptive Invariant State Estimation for Humanoid Robots on Non-Inertial Ground

> [!summary] Summary
> Purdue University researchers developed an Invariant Extended Kalman Filter (InEKF) for humanoid robots, enabling accurate real-time state estimation relative to a non-inertial ground frame using only onboard proprioceptive sensors. This method eliminates the need for external ground motion measurements, achieving faster convergence and higher accuracy for relative base position and velocity compared to prior approaches.

## Problem

- Most state estimation methods for legged robots assume a static ground, limiting their use in dynamic environments like moving ships or trains.
- Existing solutions for non-inertial ground typically require direct ground motion knowledge or external ground-mounted sensors, which are often impractical due to instrumentation and communication challenges.
- Control and planning in dynamic environments benefit from robot state estimates relative to the moving ground, rather than absolute states in an inertial frame.

## Method

- An ==Invariant Extended Kalman Filter (InEKF)== is designed to estimate the robot's state (orientation, position, velocity) relative to a dynamic ground frame using only onboard joint encoders, a base IMU, and foot-mounted IMUs.
- The approach infers ground motion by exploiting kinematic constraints at the stance foot, effectively eliminating the dependency on direct external ground motion measurements.
- The system models robot and stance-foot dynamics within a ==matrix Lie group==, ensuring robust and accurate covariance propagation through ==right-invariant errors== and observations.

## Results

- The proposed FRS filter achieved significantly faster convergence for base position (within **0.1 seconds**) compared to the DRS baseline (**3-5 seconds**) and successfully converged where the static ground baseline (SRS) failed.
- FRS demonstrated superior steady-state position accuracy, achieving errors below **4 cm** compared to approximately **10 cm** for the DRS baseline in several test cases.
- The filter operates in real-time with average computation times under **3 ms** per cycle, and base orientation was accurately reconstructed with mean absolute errors of **0.3-0.4 degrees** when using augmented IMU sensing.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Accurate inference of dynamic ground motion and relative robot state estimation is achievable using only proprioceptive sensors, by leveraging the kinematic constraints of the stance foot.
> - Observability analysis reveals that relative base velocity is consistently observable, while relative base position observability depends on the angular motion complexity of the stance-foot frame.
> - Full state observability, including base orientation, can be practically recovered by augmenting the robot's base sensing with multiple non-collinear IMUs.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Accurate and real-time state estimation is a fundamental requirement for the model-based planning and control of legged robots. The broader research landscape for robot state estimation has traditionally relied on fusing proprioceptive sensors (e.g., joint encoders, Inertial Measurement Units - IMUs) with exteroceptive sensors (e.g., LiDAR, cameras, GPS) using frameworks such as Extended Kalman Filters (EKFs) or optimization-based methods. A common assumption underpinning many of these approaches, including invariant EKFs (InEKFs) that offer improved accuracy and convergence rates, is that the ground on which the robot operates is static and inertial. This implies a zero-velocity constraint for the stance foot in the inertial frame.

Recent advancements in proprioceptive estimation have explored the use of multiple onboard IMUs to enhance drift mitigation and vertical position estimation. However, these methods fundamentally retain the static-ground assumption, which limits their applicability in dynamic, real-world scenarios.

The static-ground assumption becomes problematic in non-inertial environments, such as moving ships, trains, or aircraft, where the supporting surface undergoes translational and rotational motion relative to an inertial frame. In such settings, ground-induced accelerations and rotations significantly alter the system dynamics and the structure of the estimation problem, leading to substantial modeling errors if ignored.

Prior attempts to address state estimation on non-inertial ground include:
- An InEKF approach that explicitly models ground motion to estimate a robot’s absolute state in the inertial world frame. However, this method requires direct and accurate knowledge of the moving ground's absolute pose and velocity, an assumption often not met in practice.
- Another InEKF approach that formulates state estimation in a non-inertial frame rigidly attached to the moving ground. While this enables full observability of the robot’s relative pose and velocity, it relies on an external IMU mounted on the ground to measure its motion, which can be impractical due to instrumentation constraints, communication delays, or signal loss.
- Learning-based and hybrid model-learning estimators have shown promise in handling contact uncertainty and complex terrains but typically estimate states in the inertial frame. Their generalization to non-inertial environments without substantial retraining or specific adaptations is unclear.

This work addresses the identified gap by developing a proprioceptive state estimation method for humanoid robots operating on non-inertial ground that does not require direct measurements of ground motion or external sensors. It aims to provide state estimates directly relevant for tasks defined relative to the moving environment, thus expanding the operational envelope for legged robots in dynamic settings.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable accurate, real-time state estimation for humanoid robots operating in non-inertial environments solely using onboard proprioceptive sensors. This involves estimating the robot’s base position and velocity relative to a dynamically moving ground frame without relying on external instrumentation or direct ground motion measurements.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges and limitations of existing state estimation techniques:
1. **Limitation of Static-Ground Assumption:** Most contemporary state estimation methods for legged robots assume a static ground, which simplifies the problem by enforcing zero stance-foot velocity in the inertial frame. This assumption restricts robot operation to stationary environments and renders these methods inaccurate or unstable when the ground itself is moving (e.g., on a ship, train, or aircraft).
2. **Impracticality of External Ground Sensing:** Solutions that explicitly model ground motion often depend on knowing the ground’s absolute pose and velocity, or require an external IMU mounted on the dynamic ground. Such external sensors introduce practical deployment challenges, including instrumentation difficulties, communication latency, and potential signal loss, making them unsuitable for many real-world applications.
3. **Need for Relative State Information:** For control and planning objectives in dynamic environments, robot tasks (e.g., obstacle avoidance, goal reaching) are naturally defined relative to the moving support surface. Therefore, estimates of the robot's state *relative to the dynamic ground* are more directly useful than absolute states in an inertial frame.
4. **Robustness to Non-Linearities and Initial Errors:** Ground-induced accelerations and rotations introduce significant non-linearities into both the system dynamics and measurement models. An estimation framework is needed that can robustly handle these non-linear effects and maintain accuracy even under large initial uncertainties, which InEKFs are designed to provide due to their favorable error dynamics.
5. **Exploiting Proprioceptive Sensing:** Leveraging only onboard proprioceptive sensors (IMUs, joint encoders) is crucial for practical deployability, as it minimizes hardware complexity and external dependencies. The authors motivate the use of foot-mounted IMUs to exploit kinematic constraints at the stance foot, effectively inferring ground motion without direct measurement.

In summary, the key motivation is to bridge the gap between advanced state estimation techniques and the practical requirements of legged robots operating in unpredictable, dynamic environments, by providing a robust, self-contained, and relative state estimation solution.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology centers on an Invariant Extended Kalman Filter (InEKF) designed to estimate the humanoid robot’s state relative to a non-inertial ground frame. The approach distinguishes itself by relying exclusively on onboard proprioceptive sensors, avoiding the need for external ground motion measurements.

**Sensing Configuration:**
The filter utilizes standard onboard proprioceptive sensors:
- **Joint encoders:** Measure all joint angles.
- **Base (torso) IMU:** Provides linear acceleration and angular velocity of the robot’s torso.
- **Foot-mounted IMUs:** One on each footpad, providing linear acceleration and angular velocity of the foot.
These IMUs are assumed to coincide with the base frame {B} and stance-foot frame {F} for simplicity. Sensor measurements are modeled as true values corrupted by additive white Gaussian noise.

**State Representation:**
The filter estimates the robot’s motion state relative to the dynamic ground frame {D}. The state variables are defined as:
- **Relative base orientation:** $R_B := {}^D R_B$, orientation of base {B} relative to ground {D}.
- **Relative base position:** $p := {}^D p_{DB}$, position of base {B} relative to ground {D}.
- **Relative base velocity:** $v := ({}^W R_D)^T ({}^W v_{WB} - {}^W v_{WD})$, velocity of base {B} relative to ground {D}, expressed in {D}. This is not simply $\frac{d}{dt} p$ due to ground rotation.
- **Relative stance-foot orientation:** $R_F := {}^D R_F$, orientation of stance foot {F} relative to ground {D}.
- **Relative stance-foot position:** $d := {}^D p_{DF}$, position of stance foot {F} relative to ground {D}.

These state variables are embedded into a matrix Lie group G (a double spatial isometry SE3(3) and SO(3)) to leverage the InEKF framework. The estimation process uses right-invariant errors $\eta_t = \bar{X}_t X_t^{-1}$ and their associated logarithmic errors $\xi_t$.

**System Modeling:**

1. **Process Model (Continuous Phase):**
 - **Relative IMU Motion Dynamics:** The authors first formulate the continuous-time evolution of $R_B$, $v$, and $p$ in the dynamic ground frame {D}. This formulation initially depends on the dynamic ground’s acceleration (${}^D a_t$) and angular velocity (${}^D \omega_t$).
 - **Eliminating Ground Motion Dependency:** To achieve proprioceptive estimation, the dependency on direct ground motion sensing is eliminated. This is done by exploiting the kinematic constraint that the stance foot (when in contact) has approximately zero relative velocity and angular velocity with respect to the ground. By differentiating the definitions of $d_t$ and $R_F_t$ (Eq. 2), expressions for ${}^D a_t$ and ${}^D \omega_t$ are derived in terms of foot-IMU measurements. These expressions are then substituted into the relative base dynamics (Eq. 4) to yield a process model (Eq. 7) that only relies on onboard IMU and encoder data.
 - **Stance-foot Contact Dynamics:** Foot slippage and rolling are modeled as white Gaussian noise, causing $d_t$ and $R_F_t$ to evolve slightly (Eq. 8).
 - **Overall Process Model:** Equations (7) and (8) are combined into a comprehensive process model (Eq. 9) that includes IMU measurements and various noise sources (sensor noise, contact uncertainties).

2. **Measurement Model (Continuous Phase):**
 - **Leg Odometry:** The measurement model is based on leg odometry, specifically the relative position between the base and the stance-foot frames. This is expressed as $h_p(q_t) = (R_B_t)^T (d_t - p_t)$.
 - **Right-Invariant Observation Form:** By left-multiplying by $h_R^T(q_t)$, the measurement is transformed into the stance-foot frame: $h_R^T(q_t) h_p(q_t) = (R_F_t)^T (d_t - p_t)$. This is then cast into a right-invariant observation form $Y_t = X_t^{-1} b + n_t$ (Eq. 11), a property crucial for InEKF robustness and accurate covariance propagation.

3. **Process Model (Discrete Contact-Switch Event):**
 - At foot-landing events, the stance foot changes. The new stance-foot orientation and position are computed using forward kinematics between the outgoing and incoming stance feet (Eq. 12). The robot’s base pose and velocity relative to the ground are assumed to remain continuous across these events.

**Filter Design (InEKF):**

- **Propagation Step (Continuous Phase):** The state estimate $\bar{X}_t$ is propagated using the deterministic component of the process models. The error covariance $P_t$ is propagated using the linearized log-error dynamics (Eq. 13) via the Riccati equation (Eq. 14), which ensures consistent covariance propagation.
- **Measurement Update Step (Continuous Phase):** The predicted state and covariance are corrected using leg-odometry measurements. The updated state $\bar{X}_t^+$ is computed using the right-invariant update rule (Eq. 15). The covariance is updated using a linearized update equation for the logarithmic error (Eq. 16) and standard Kalman gain (Eq. 18).
- **Propagation Step (Discrete Contact Switch):** The state $\bar{X}_K^+$ undergoes a discrete jump approximated by forward kinematics (Eq. 19). The covariance is propagated through a linear transformation, accounting for foot impact and slippage.

**Observability Analysis:**
A local observability analysis is performed on the linearized log-error dynamics in discrete time. The observability matrix (Eq. 21) is analyzed to determine which state components are observable under different ground and robot motions, particularly the role of stance-foot gyroscope measurements. The analysis also identifies the conditions under which base orientation can be recovered through sensor augmentation (e.g., using multiple non-collinear IMUs on the torso).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides both theoretical observability analysis and experimental validation on a Digit humanoid robot, comparing the proposed "FRS" filter against two baseline InEKF methods: "SRS" (static ground) and "DRS" (dynamic ground with external ground IMU).

**Observability of Base Position and Velocity:**
- **Theoretical Predictions:** The observability analysis (Table I) indicates that the relative base velocity ($v_t$) is consistently observable under all ground motion conditions. The relative base position ($p_t$) exhibits conditional observability:
 - Partially observable (e.g., along certain axes) when only one principal axis of the stance-foot frame shows non-trivial angular motion (Cases T1, T3, T5).
 - Fully observable when at least two axes exhibit non-zero angular motion (Cases T2, T4).
- **Experimental Validation:** The theoretical predictions were largely confirmed. While some position components were theoretically predicted to be unobservable in specific cases (e.g., $p_x$ in T1, $p_y$ in T3 and T5), experimentally these components still converged to small neighborhoods around the ground truth. This practical observability is attributed to non-ideal experimental conditions (e.g., minor treadmill motion errors, slight robot misalignment) that introduced incidental foot IMU excitation. The SRS baseline, as expected, failed to converge for base position estimates due to its inherent observability limitations on non-inertial ground. The DRS baseline showed observability behavior similar to FRS.

**Reconstruction of Base Orientation via Base IMU Augmentation:**
- The nominal sensing configuration (single torso IMU) did not yield observable base orientation ($R_B_t$).
- An augmented sensing configuration with three non-collinear IMUs rigidly mounted on the robot's torso allowed for the reconstruction of base orientation. Experimental results on Digit standing on a pitching treadmill showed that the reconstructed base orientation closely matched motion-capture ground truth, with mean absolute errors of 0.3904°, 0.2709°, and 0.3241° for roll, pitch, and yaw, respectively. This confirms that full state observability, including base orientation, can be achieved in practice through this augmentation.

**Real-time Computation:**
- All three filters (FRS, DRS, SRS) demonstrated real-time performance, with average computation times under 3 ms per cycle on a laptop with a 13th-Gen Intel Core i7-13620H processor and 16 GB RAM.

**Error Convergence Rate:**
- **Base Velocity:** All three filters converged within approximately 1 second to a small neighborhood of the ground truth across all test cases, with the proposed FRS filter being consistently the fastest.
- **Base Position:** The proposed FRS filter exhibited significantly faster convergence for base position estimation, typically within 0.1 seconds. In contrast, the DRS baseline required 3-5 seconds on average. This faster convergence for FRS is attributed to its measurement model incorporating both positional and rotational forward kinematics between the base and the stance foot.

**Steady-State Accuracy (Table III):**
- **Base Velocity:** For standing and squatting cases (T1-T4), all three filters achieved comparable steady-state accuracy in base velocity estimation. In the walking case (T5), FRS showed larger steady-state velocity errors, primarily due to impact-induced noise in the foot-mounted IMUs, amplified by the moving ground. The authors mitigated this through mechanical impact absorption, stable contact switching, and IMU data low-pass filtering.
- **Base Position:** The proposed FRS filter consistently outperformed the SRS baseline, which did not converge for position estimates. FRS also achieved superior overall performance compared to the DRS baseline in cases T1-T4, demonstrating steady-state position errors below 4 cm, whereas DRS exhibited errors of approximately 10 cm. In the walking case (T5), FRS achieved better x- and y-position estimates than DRS, despite the challenges of contact noise.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant advancement in state estimation for legged robots, particularly in expanding their operational capabilities to dynamic, non-inertial environments. The primary significance lies in its ability to perform accurate, real-time state estimation of a humanoid robot's motion relative to a moving ground frame using **only onboard proprioceptive sensors**, thereby eliminating the need for external ground-mounted IMUs or direct knowledge of ground motion.

**Key Impacts:**

1. **Expanded Deployment Scenarios:** By removing the static-ground assumption and the reliance on external ground sensing, the proposed InEKF (FRS) enables humanoid robots to operate robustly on moving platforms such as ships, trains, aircraft, or large industrial vehicles. This broadens the potential applications of legged robots in logistics, exploration, and disaster response scenarios where stable, dynamic platforms are common.
2. **Enhanced Autonomy and Practicality:** The proprioceptive-only nature of the filter contributes to higher autonomy and practical deployability. It reduces instrumentation complexity, eliminates issues related to external sensor communication delays, signal loss, or infrastructure requirements, making the system more self-contained and robust in unstructured or unknown dynamic environments.
3. **Improved Estimation Performance:** The FRS filter demonstrates notable performance improvements over existing InEKF baselines. It achieves significantly faster convergence rates (e.g., 96% speedup in convergence rate for base position compared to DRS) and lower steady-state position errors (e.g., 80% reduction in position estimate errors over existing InEKFs in certain cases). This enhanced accuracy and rapid convergence are crucial for real-time control and planning algorithms.
4. **Robustness to Non-Linearities and Initial Errors:** The InEKF formulation, with its right-invariant measurement model, promotes robust and accurate estimation. The experimental validation under large initial uncertainties confirms its ability to converge reliably even from significant initial estimation errors. The explicit modeling of ground-induced nonlinearities further contributes to its robustness.
5. **Theoretical Contributions and Practical Observability:** The detailed observability analysis clarifies the conditions under which relative base position and velocity are observable with respect to the non-inertial ground frame. The practical demonstration of recovering base orientation observability through simple augmentation with multiple torso-mounted IMUs provides a concrete solution for achieving full-state observability, contributing both theoretically and practically to the field.

The direct relevance of the estimated relative states for planning and control tasks formulated in the dynamic ground frame is a crucial aspect, indicating a clear pathway for integrating this filter into future locomotion control systems for robots operating in complex, dynamic environments.
