# Real-Time Out-of-Distribution Failure Prevention via Multi-Modal Reasoning

> [!summary] Summary
> FORTRESS, developed by researchers at Stanford University's Autonomous Systems Laboratory, introduces a multi-tiered framework that enables autonomous robots to prevent out-of-distribution (OOD) failures by integrating foundation models with control-theoretic planning. It achieved over 0.90 balanced accuracy in OOD detection and over 90% success in real-time, semantically safe fallback planning in simulations, and demonstrated real-time performance on a quadrotor drone.

## Problem

- Autonomous robots face out-of-distribution (OOD) failure scenarios in open-world environments, which traditional safety methods struggle to handle due to reliance on precise models.
- Foundation models (LLMs, VLMs) offer semantic understanding but are dynamics-agnostic and too slow for real-time, safety-critical decision-making in robotics.
- Existing recovery planning often relies on static fallbacks or lacks the ability to dynamically generate new, semantically safe alternative goals when the nominal mission becomes unsafe.

## Method

- **FORTRESS** employs a '==slow-fast== hierarchical architecture': a low-frequency tier proactively generates semantic fallback goals and calibrates ==OOD-aware safety cost functions== using foundation models.
- A rapid online tier, triggered by anomaly detection, uses a ==Reach-Avoid== problem formulation and ==RRT== planning to generate dynamically feasible, semantically safe fallback trajectories, guided by the pre-calibrated cost functions.
- It leverages ==Vision-Language Models== for identifying physical fallback locations and text embedding models, calibrated via ==conformal prediction==, to quantify semantic hazards relative to known safe states.

## Results

- FORTRESS achieved a balanced accuracy consistently above **0.90** for OOD failure detection on synthetic datasets and **0.87** on real-world quadruped robot data, outperforming on-the-fly LLM prompting.
- Foundation models successfully anticipated relevant failure modes, and increasing these modes improved semantic safety classification accuracy, with AUROC curves often exceeding **0.95** for top embedding models.
- In CARLA simulations, FORTRESS yielded over **90%** success rate for semantically safe fallback landing plans, accurately avoiding dynamic hazards and significantly improving over baseline methods, with critical 'fast' components executing in milliseconds to seconds on hardware.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A multi-tiered 'slow-fast' architecture effectively mitigates the high latency of foundation models, allowing their generalist knowledge to be utilized for real-time safety-critical robot operation.
> - Semantic safety can be effectively quantified by calibrating text embedding similarities to anticipated failure modes against known safe state observations, enabling OOD-aware hazard detection.
> - Integrating high-level semantic reasoning from foundation models with low-level control-theoretic planning allows for adaptive, context-aware fallback strategies beyond static or pre-defined emergency procedures.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The rapid advancement and deployment of autonomous robots, from self-driving cars to aerial drones and legged robots, are pushing these systems beyond structured, controlled environments into unstructured, open-world settings. This expansion inevitably leads to encounters with **out-of-distribution (OOD) failure scenarios**—situations not encountered during design or training, which can lead to degraded performance or catastrophic failures. The paper directly addresses this critical challenge, positioning itself at the intersection of robust robotics, AI safety, and the emerging field of foundation models.

**Traditional approaches** to robot safety, such as formal methods like Hamilton-Jacobi reachability (HJ reachability) and Control Barrier Functions (CBFs), provide strong theoretical guarantees. However, they typically rely on precise models of the environment and explicitly defined failure regions. In open-world OOD scenarios, these prerequisites are often unmet, rendering traditional methods insufficient.

The advent of **foundation models (FMs)**, including Large Language Models (LLMs) and Vision-Language Models (VLMs), has opened new avenues for semantic understanding and planning in robotics. These models can perform zero-shot reasoning about high-level, semantically appropriate responses, as seen in works on semantic anomaly detection and real-time anomaly detection with LLMs. However, current integrations of FMs into robotics face significant hurdles:
1. **Dynamics Agnosticism:** FMs provide high-level semantic advice but are inherently unaware of robot dynamics, potentially suggesting physically infeasible or unsafe actions.
2. **High Latency:** The inference time of large foundation models is typically too high for real-time decision-making in safety-critical situations.
3. **Static Fallbacks:** Existing methods often resort to rigid, hard-coded fallback regions or rely on constant human intervention, which are impractical for the sheer variety of OOD scenarios in open worlds.

**Real-time recovery planning** in general robotics has seen developments in areas like game-theoretic trajectory repair or LLM-based semantic fixes. However, these mostly focus on localized repairs to existing plans and lack the capability to dynamically generate entirely new fallback goals and paths when the nominal mission becomes unsafe in a novel, OOD environment. Similarly, research in OOD detection combined with fallback controllers often still relies on pre-defined policies or static fallback sets.

**FORTRESS** bridges these gaps by proposing a novel framework that effectively harnesses the generalist knowledge of foundation models for *real-time, dynamically feasible, and semantically safe fallback plan generation*. It introduces a "slow-fast" hierarchical architecture to overcome the latency problem of FMs while leveraging their semantic understanding to adaptively identify goals and infer unsafe regions in an OOD-aware manner. This work represents a significant step towards enabling truly robust and autonomous robot operation in complex, unpredictable real-world environments by systematically integrating cutting-edge AI reasoning with established control-theoretic planning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching motivation for FORTRESS is to enhance the safety and reliability of autonomous robots by enabling them to effectively respond to unforeseen hazards in safety-critical, out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios. As robots venture into increasingly unstructured and dynamic open-world environments, the likelihood of encountering situations outside their training distribution grows significantly. Current robotic systems struggle with these OOD events, often relying on pre-defined emergency procedures, human intervention, or simplified models, which are inadequate for the complexity and variability of real-world deployments.

The paper identifies a crucial gap: how to leverage the powerful, generalist semantic knowledge embedded in foundation models (LLMs, VLMs) for real-time, dynamically feasible, and semantically safe responses when traditional methods fail. While foundation models can understand complex contexts, their high latency and lack of dynamics awareness make direct application to real-time control problematic.

To address this, FORTRESS sets out to achieve three key objectives:

- **M1: Be prepared with relevant concrete fallback strategy goal locations aligned with semantic strategy idea set Σ, when the original nominal path is rendered infeasible or unsafe.**
 - **Motivation:** In open-world environments, a robot's original goal or path can suddenly become unsafe. Instead of halting or relying on vague instructions, the robot needs concrete, physically achievable alternative goals that align with high-level semantic fallback strategies (e.g., "land in a parking lot," "find an empty rooftop"). This objective aims to automate the translation of abstract semantic strategies into specific, actionable physical locations.

- **M2: Bootstrap its understanding of semantically unsafe states that would result in OOD failures, i.e., states x where nearby(x, l) ∈ Ωd, using our plentiful safe state descriptions Ωs.**
 - **Motivation:** OOD failures are, by definition, novel and difficult to predict. Robots lack specific training data for these failures. The goal here is to enable the robot to anticipate potential failure modes and, crucially, to identify novel semantically unsafe regions in real-time without prior direct experience. This requires a mechanism to generalize from known safe states and abstract failure modes to discern truly hazardous conditions.

- **M3: Rapidly generate safe plans that implement a fallback strategy while satisfying the semantic safety constraints and physical constraints.**
 - **Motivation:** Once a potential OOD failure is detected and a fallback goal is identified, the robot must execute a plan immediately. This plan must not only be dynamically feasible (respecting the robot's physical limits) but also *semantically safe*, meaning it actively avoids regions identified as hazardous based on multi-modal reasoning. The challenge is to combine semantic understanding with low-level motion planning in a real-time, robust manner, moving beyond static avoidance zones or human-defined safety interventions.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

FORTRESS proposes a **multi-tiered, slow-fast hierarchical framework** to integrate the generalist knowledge of foundation models with dynamics-aware planning for real-time OOD failure prevention. This architecture is key to overcoming the inherent latency of large models during safety-critical moments.

**A. Low-Frequency Reasoning (Nominal Operation or Offline):**
This "slow" tier performs computationally intensive reasoning proactively, caching results for rapid retrieval when needed.

1. **Generating Potential Fallback Strategy Sets (M1):**
 - FORTRESS uses **Vision-Language Models (VLMs)**, specifically Molmo (trained on PixMo dataset), to interpret abstract semantic fallback strategies (e.g., "empty, horizontal building roofs") from robot scene images.
 - The VLM outputs pixel coordinates, which are then converted into 3D global coordinates using depth information and camera intrinsics.
 - This process, which can take several seconds, is executed *preemptively* at a low frequency during nominal operations (e.g., as the robot enters a new environment) or offline. The resulting set of potential fallback goal locations is cached, ready for immediate use.

2. **Reasoning About Semantic Safety Constraints (M2):**
 - **Anticipating Failure Modes:** To identify semantically unsafe regions, FORTRESS leverages pre-trained foundation models (LLMs/VLMs) offline or at low frequency. These models are prompted with a general description of the environment and, optionally, an image, to enumerate a set of high-level abstract **failure modes (Φ)** (e.g., "Near Human," "Turbulence," "High Temperature"). This step functions as an automated Failure Mode and Effects Analysis.
 - **Calibrating Semantic Safety Cost Functions:** The core idea is to determine if a new state description is anomalously similar to a failure mode compared to a wealth of known safe observations (Ω_s).
 - **Text Embedding Models:** A text embedding model (e.g., Voyage AI, OpenAI Text Embedding Large) is used to convert safe state descriptions (Ω_s) and failure mode descriptions (Φ) into numerical embedding vectors (E_s, e_ϕ).
 - **Similarity Measure:** Cosine similarity is used to measure the semantic affinity between a state description embedding and a failure mode embedding.
 - **Threshold Calibration:** For each failure mode ϕ, a similarity threshold (∆_ϕ) is calibrated using conformal prediction on the safe data (Ω_s). This threshold defines what constitutes an "anomalously close" resemblance to a failure mode, making the classification OOD-aware.
 - **Semantic Safety Cost Functions (θ_ϕ(x)):** These functions are constructed to quantify the hazard. They take a robot state `x` as input, use `nearby(x, l_ϕ)` to get a semantic description of concepts within a radius `l_ϕ`, embed this description, and then compare its similarity to `e_ϕ` against the calibrated threshold `∆_ϕ`. If `θ_ϕ(x) > 0`, the region is deemed semantically unsafe. Collision hazards (`θ_c(x)`) are also included. These calibrated cost functions are cached.

**B. Rapid Online Fallback Planning (Runtime Triggered):**
This "fast" tier is activated instantaneously when a runtime monitor detects an anomaly or potential hazard, prompting a fallback response.

1. **Synthesizing Semantically Safe Fallback Plans (M3):**
 - **Control-Theoretic Optimization:** The problem is formulated as a **Reach-Avoid problem**. The goal is to find an optimal trajectory (τ*) that starts from the robot's current location (`b`), reaches a specified fallback goal region (`B_ρ(g)`) within a horizon `T`, and minimizes the maximum value of all semantic safety and collision cost functions (max_h∈Φ∪{c} max_x∈τ θ_h(x)). This ensures dynamic feasibility and avoids both physical and semantically unsafe regions.
 - **Motion Planning:** A **Rapidly-exploring Random Tree (RRT)** algorithm is employed for motion planning. The semantic safety cost functions `θ_h(x)` are directly used to guide the RRT search, invalidating unsafe states and pruning branches that venture into hazardous regions.
 - **Path Tracking:** The generated RRT plan is tracked using **Model Predictive Control (MPC)** or a **Linear Quadratic Regulator (LQR)** to ensure dynamic feasibility and accurate execution. A formal proof is provided to guarantee that by carefully inflating the distance thresholds (`l_h`) for the cost functions and ensuring a sufficiently small RRT step size, the executed trajectory will adhere to safety and reachability requirements, accounting for path tracking errors.
 - **Adaptive Strategy Switching:** If the planner fails to find a safe trajectory (`Θ* > 0`) for the current fallback goal, FORTRESS iteratively attempts other cached fallback goals associated with various strategies from the `Σ` set until a safe plan (`Θ* ≤ 0`) is found. This adaptive capability provides robustness in dynamic environments.
 - **Interpretable Explanations:** The system can log which specific hazards prevented a fallback strategy, offering insights into failure modes.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

FORTRESS's effectiveness is validated through extensive experiments on synthetic benchmarks and real-world robot hardware, addressing three main hypotheses:

**H1: Superior OOD Failure Detection via Embedding Classification**
- **Finding:** FORTRESS's approach of using text embedding models calibrated with a limited set of failure modes (proactively identified) significantly outperforms on-the-fly prompting of slow reasoning models in classifying semantically unsafe OOD descriptions.
- **Details:** On synthetic datasets for drone taxis, maritime vessels, and autonomous vehicles, FORTRESS achieved a balanced accuracy consistently higher than **0.90**. In contrast, on-the-fly prompting of models like GPT-4o, o3-mini, and o4-mini yielded much lower balanced accuracies (e.g., 0.76 for GPT-4o on synthetic boat data). This highlights that querying slow models in real-time is both inefficient and less accurate due to their abstract reasoning.
- **Real-world Validation:** Similar strong performance (balanced accuracy of 0.87) was observed on real-world data collected from an ANYmal quadruped robot navigating a construction zone, demonstrating its practical applicability.

**H2: Accuracy of Proactively Anticipating Failure Modes**
- **Finding:** Foundation models can effectively and automatically anticipate failure modes, and increasing the number of these anticipated modes generally improves semantic safety classification accuracy.
- **Details:** Experiments querying various reasoning models (e.g., Gemini 2.0 Flash, Claude 3.7 Sonnet, DeepSeek-R1, OpenAI models, Llama 4 Scout, Mercury) for up to 50 failure modes showed that the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic (AUROC) curves for the embedding-based cost functions generally **improved and stabilized**, often reaching values above 0.95 for top embedding models (e.g., OpenAI Text Embedding Large, Multilingual-E5, Voyage AI). This confirms that FMs can generate relevant failure modes that enhance coverage of OOD scenarios without introducing spurious suggestions that increase false positives.

**H3: Real-Time Safe Fallback Planning**
- **Finding:** The "slow-fast" architecture of FORTRESS enables rapid, semantically safe, and dynamically feasible fallback plan generation in real time, outperforming baselines that rely on hard-coded or naively avoided regions.
- **Simulation (CARLA):** For a drone agent in the CARLA simulator tasked with landing on building roofs, FORTRESS significantly improved both the **success rate and safety** of generated fallback plans. It accurately demarcated semantically safe and unsafe regions, avoiding hazards like firetrucks and people on rooftops, which baselines (modified AESOP and Safe-Lang) either ignored or treated too broadly, leading to lower success rates or entry into unsafe areas. FORTRESS achieved over 90% success rate for "landing on building roofs." The ability to replan with dynamic concepts (e.g., a falling skydiver and box) was also demonstrated, showing adaptability.
- **Hardware (Quadrotor Drone):** Deployment on a quadrotor drone with Jetson Nano hardware validated the real-time capability.
 - The "fast" components—semantic safety reasoning inference (0.011s) and the Reach-Avoid Planner (1.28s)—executed rapidly, making them suitable for safety-critical moments.
 - The "slow" components—Molmo querying for goals (5.82s), FM querying for failure modes (e.g., Claude 3.7 Sonnet at 15.65s), and embedding calibration (e.g., Voyage AI at 3.91s)—were confirmed to be feasible for preemptive execution during nominal operations, thus mitigating their latency. The drone successfully identified a safe rooftop landing spot (a garden roof) while avoiding unsafe ones (construction zones, fires, parking lots with vehicles).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

FORTRESS makes several significant contributions with far-reaching potential impacts on the field of autonomous robotics and AI safety:

- **Pioneering OOD Failure Prevention:** This work offers a robust and adaptive framework specifically designed to prevent catastrophic failures in *safety-critical, out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios*. This is a crucial step for moving autonomous systems beyond controlled environments into the unpredictable open world.
- **Effective Integration of Foundation Models and Control Theory:** A key innovation is its "slow-fast" hierarchical architecture, which ingeniously addresses the inherent latency and dynamics-agnostic nature of foundation models. By performing computationally intensive semantic reasoning proactively and caching the results, FORTRESS successfully bridges the gap between high-level, open-world reasoning from FMs and low-level, real-time, dynamics-aware control-theoretic planning. This integration is a significant advancement for robust robot autonomy.
- **Adaptive and Semantically-Aware Safety:** Unlike rigid, hard-coded fallbacks or simple object avoidance, FORTRESS enables robots to understand and avoid *semantically unsafe regions* based on anticipated failure modes. This allows for nuanced, context-aware decision-making, differentiating between individually safe objects (e.g., a person, a ladder) and dangerous combinations (e.g., "person on a ladder" implying "Worker Injury").
- **Reduced Reliance on Human Intervention:** By automating the identification of fallback goals, anticipation of failure modes, and generation of safe fallback plans, FORTRESS significantly reduces the need for human safety interventions in novel OOD situations. This enables more scalable and truly autonomous robot deployments.
- **Improved Safety and Success Rates:** The empirical results demonstrate a tangible improvement in planning success rates and overall system safety in complex simulated and real-world environments compared to existing baseline approaches.
- **Interpretable Safety Mechanisms:** The framework provides interpretable explanations of why certain fallback strategies might be infeasible (e.g., logging specific hazards that prevent a plan), which is valuable for debugging, system improvement, and regulatory compliance.
- **Broad Applicability Across Robotic Platforms:** The methodology is demonstrated across diverse robotic platforms (drones, legged robots, ground vehicles, maritime vessels) and scenarios, suggesting its generalizability and potential impact across various safety-critical domains, including autonomous driving, aerial logistics, exploration, and industrial automation.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The identified limitations (e.g., automatic semantic description extraction, non-static fallback goals, dynamic/context-dependent constraints, active failure diagnosis) lay out clear and promising avenues for future work, ensuring the impact of FORTRESS extends beyond this initial contribution.
