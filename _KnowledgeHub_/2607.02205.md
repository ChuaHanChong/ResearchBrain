# Actuator Reality Shaping for Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Robot Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Yamamori et al. developed "actuator reality shaping," a method that modifies physical actuators to emulate idealized simulator dynamics, enabling zero-shot sim-to-real transfer of reinforcement learning policies. This approach reduces mean position tracking error by over 96% and facilitates policy reusability across diverse robotic platforms without hardware-specific tuning.

## Problem

- The "sim-to-real gap" in robot learning arises from significant discrepancies between idealized simulator joint dynamics and the complex, nonlinear behaviors of real-world actuators (e.g., friction, stiction, backlash).
- Existing sim-to-real methods often require extensive hardware-specific data collection, system identification, or policy adaptation, which limits the reusability and generality of trained policies.
- Unmodeled actuator phenomena cause physical robots to deviate from trajectories optimized in simulation, leading to degraded task performance and instability, especially for high-gear-ratio joints.

## Method

- Introduces "actuator reality shaping" to modify the physical actuator's closed-loop behavior to precisely match the idealized second-order reference dynamics used during policy training in simulation.
- Employs a cascaded two-degree-of-freedom (2-DoF) controller, where the feedforward path explicitly tracks the simulator's reference dynamics, and the feedback path provides robust stabilization.
- Augments the 2-DoF controller with a disturbance observer (DOB) at each joint to estimate and actively cancel lumped disturbance torques, such as friction and external loads, using a low-pass filter.

## Results

- The 2-DoF+DOB controller reduced mean position deviation by 96.3% and mean velocity deviation by 99.1% on a single-axis testbed compared to a factory PID cascade controller.
- On a 7-DOF robotic arm, the method achieved a 96.5% reduction in mean end-effector position deviation relative to the factory PID cascade, outperforming a learned actuator model baseline (ASAP).
- Policies trained with actuator reality shaping demonstrated successful zero-shot transfer for complex tasks on diverse platforms, including slope climbing with a wheeled-legged robot and stable walking with a 22-DOF humanoid robot.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decoupling policy training from physical hardware specifics by standardizing the actuator interface significantly enhances policy reusability and simplifies the policy optimization process.
> - Well-established control-theoretic concepts (2-DoF control, disturbance observers) can be effectively applied as a robust, low-level interface to bridge the sim-to-real gap for reinforcement learning.
> - Actively shaping the real actuator's response to match an ideal model, rather than adapting the simulator or policy to reality, offers a modular and transparent approach to zero-shot transfer.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has demonstrated success in developing control policies for robots in simulated environments. However, a significant challenge known as the "sim-to-real gap" arises when these policies are transferred to physical hardware. This gap refers to the performance degradation observed in real-world deployment compared to simulation. A primary contributor to this discrepancy originates at the actuator level. Simulators often simplify joint dynamics, modeling them as ideal torque sources or basic servo systems, whereas real-world actuators exhibit complex, nonlinear behaviors such as friction, stiction, backlash, transmission compliance, inertia mismatch, saturation, and electromechanical delays. These unmodeled phenomena cause the physical robot to deviate from trajectories intended by policies optimized in simulation, potentially leading to increased tracking errors, degraded task performance, and instability, particularly in dynamic tasks.

The conventional approaches to mitigate the sim-to-real gap generally focus on adapting the simulator or the policy to better match the real world. These methods broadly fall into three categories:
1. **Domain Randomization (DR)**: This involves training RL policies across a wide range of randomized physical parameters within the simulator. The aim is to make the policy robust enough to generalize to the real system, which is assumed to lie within this randomized distribution. While effective, DR can lead to conservative policies that do not fully exploit precise actuator models, and often requires extensive simulation budgets. Adaptive variants aim to refine the randomization distribution using real-world data.
2. **Actuator Modeling**: This approach augments the simulator with physics-based or learned actuator models. These models are typically identified from hardware input-output data. While successful in some contexts, particularly with quasi-direct-drive actuators, this method assumes that the residual gap after modeling is small. For high-gear-ratio joints, where nonlinearities like friction and backlash are dominant, simple parametric models may be insufficient.
3. **Data-driven System Identification and Online Adaptation**: These methods involve estimating environment or actuator parameters online during deployment or refining a bootstrap controller against a data-driven model. Examples include inferring latent parameters from proprioceptive history or training environment-aware policies.

A common limitation across these dominant strategies is their dependency on hardware-specific data collection or identification. This process must be repeated when actuators are replaced, degrade over time, or are transferred to different robot platforms, thereby limiting the reusability of policies trained in simulation. This dependency motivates the current work, which proposes an alternative interface to standardize the actuator behavior observed by the policy, rather than continuously adapting the simulator or policy to each hardware instance.

This paper introduces "actuator reality shaping" as a dual perspective to existing sim-to-real methods. Instead of making the simulator more realistic to match physical actuators, this approach modifies the physical actuators' closed-loop behavior to match an idealized reference model used during simulation. This paradigm draws on established control-theoretic concepts such as Two-Degree-of-Freedom (2-DoF) control and Model Reference Control, which allow for independent design of reference tracking and disturbance rejection. While these control strategies are well-known in individual joint control, their application as a standardized sim-to-real interface layer for robotic learning has not been extensively explored. The research positions itself as a reusable interface that enables zero-shot transfer by making the physical actuator emulate the simulated ideal, thereby decoupling policy development from hardware idiosyncrasies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to enable **zero-shot sim-to-real transfer** of reinforcement learning policies for robotic control. This means allowing policies trained exclusively in simulation to be deployed directly on physical hardware without requiring any further task-level fine-tuning, hardware-specific data collection, or learned actuator models in the real world.

The motivation for this objective stems from addressing the **actuator gap**, which is identified as a major bottleneck in bridging the sim-to-real divide. Specifically, the paper highlights the significant discrepancies between:
- **Idealized Simulator Dynamics**: In simulation, joints are often modeled as simple second-order systems, such as PD controllers driving an inertial load (e.g., $I_{sim}\ddot{\theta}_j = K_p(\bar{\theta}_j - \theta_j) - D\dot{\theta}_j + f_j$). These models implicitly assume instantaneous torque production and omit complex physical phenomena.
- **Complex Real-World Actuator Behavior**: Physical actuators exhibit numerous nonlinearities and hardware-dependent characteristics. These include friction (Coulomb and viscous), stiction, backlash in gearboxes, transmission compliance, variations in rotor inertia, current saturation limits, and electromechanical delays. These unmodeled effects cause the actual joint motion to deviate from the commanded motion, leading to a mismatch between the simulated and real-world system dynamics.

The limitations of existing sim-to-real strategies further motivate this research:
- **Hardware-Specific Dependence**: Current methods, such as domain randomization or learned actuator models, typically require hardware-specific data collection or intensive system identification. This process is time-consuming, resource-intensive, and must be repeated if actuators are replaced, degrade, or are used on a different robot platform. This dependency significantly restricts the reusability and generality of trained policies.
- **Performance vs. Robustness Trade-off**: Domain randomization, while providing robustness, can lead to conservative policies that may not achieve optimal task-specific performance because they are designed to succeed across a broad range of dynamics rather than precisely exploiting a particular actuator model.
- **Complexity for Policy Optimization**: When the policy directly interacts with complex, nonlinear hardware dynamics, the learning process can be exposed to these intricacies, potentially making policy optimization more challenging or unstable.

In response to these challenges, the authors propose "actuator reality shaping" to create a **standardized actuator interface**. The motivation is to:
1. **Decouple Policy Training from Hardware Specifics**: By making the real actuator behave like the idealized simulator model, the policy can be trained against a consistent and predictable dynamics model, regardless of the underlying physical hardware.
2. **Enhance Policy Reusability**: A policy trained for a specific idealized dynamics can then be deployed on any compatible hardware platform, provided that platform's actuators can be "shaped" to match that same ideal dynamics. This would eliminate the need for retraining or significant re-tuning.
3. **Simplify Learning**: Presenting an idealized, "clean" actuator response to the RL policy could lead to more stable and efficient policy optimization, as the policy is less susceptible to hardware-specific nonlinearities like friction or backlash.
4. **Improve Modularity**: The proposed low-level driver can be designed and tuned based on a compact, minimal description of the actuator (e.g., torque constant, rotor inertia, bandwidth limits), without requiring extensive task-level real-world rollouts or complex learned actuator models. This promotes a more modular and transparent control architecture.

By achieving these objectives, the research aims to provide a fundamental improvement in the efficiency and generality of robot learning systems, particularly in scenarios involving diverse and evolving hardware platforms.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core methodology introduced by the authors is **actuator reality shaping**. This paradigm proposes to modify the low-level controller of physical actuators such that their closed-loop behavior precisely matches the idealized second-order reference dynamics assumed during policy training in simulation. This contrasts with conventional approaches that adapt the simulator or the policy to the real hardware.

The technical implementation of actuator reality shaping involves a **cascaded two-degree-of-freedom (2-DoF) controller** augmented with a **disturbance observer (DOB)** at each robot joint. This architecture aims to drive the real motor to reproduce the simulator's reference dynamics.

**Problem Formulation (Actuator Gap):**
The research first formally defines the discrepancy.
- **Simulated Dynamics**: In simulation, a joint $j$ is governed by a PD controller acting on a goal joint angle $\bar{\theta}_j$, represented as $I_{sim}\ddot{\theta}_j = K_p(\bar{\theta}_j - \theta_j) - D\dot{\theta}_j + f_j$. This assumes ideal, instantaneous torque production.
- **Real Actuator Dynamics**: On physical hardware, the actual motor dynamics are more complex: $J_{real}\ddot{\theta}_j = K_{\tau i}(i_j - i_{d,j}) - d_{real}\dot{\theta}_j - \tau_{f,j}(\dot{\theta}_j) - \tau_{ext,j}$. This equation accounts for actual rotor inertia ($J_{real}$), torque constant ($K_{\tau i}$), current command ($i_j$), disturbance current ($i_{d,j}$), viscous damping ($d_{real}$), velocity-dependent friction ($\tau_{f,j}$), and external load torque ($\tau_{ext,j}$).
- **Actuator Gap**: The gap is characterized by a multiplicative uncertainty $\Delta P_j(s) = \frac{P_{real}(s)}{P_{sim}(s)} - 1$, capturing inertia and damping mismatches, and additive disturbance torques (friction, cogging, external loads). The objective of the controller is to suppress this gap and disturbance influence so that $\theta_j(t) \approx \theta_{ref,j}(t)$, where $\theta_{ref,j}$ is the output of the simulator's reference model.

**Two-Degree-of-Freedom (2-DoF) Controller:**
The 2-DoF control structure is employed to independently design the reference tracking and disturbance rejection characteristics. The control input $u$ is calculated as $u = \frac{F(s)}{\hat{P}(s)}r + K(s)e$, where $r$ is the reference (policy goal joint angle $\bar{\theta}$), $y$ is the measured output ($\theta$), $F(s)$ is the desired system response block (feedforward path), and $K(s)$ is the feedback block.
- **Feedforward Path ($F(s)$)**: This block is explicitly designed to match the simulator's desired reference dynamics. For the outer position loop, $F_1(s) = \frac{K_p}{I_{sim}s^2 + Ds + K_p}$, which corresponds to the second-order dynamics of the simulated PD controller. For the inner velocity loop, a first-order response $F_2(s) = \frac{\alpha}{s+\alpha}$ is chosen. The feedforward path ensures that, in the absence of disturbances or model error, the output perfectly tracks the reference dynamics.
- **Feedback Path ($K(s)$)**: This path is responsible for robust stabilization and disturbance rejection. A standard Proportional-Integral-Derivative (PID) controller, $K(s) = k_p + \frac{k_i}{s} + k_d s$, is utilized. The integral term ($k_i$) is crucial for guaranteeing zero steady-state tracking error, even in the presence of constant disturbances, aligning with the internal model principle.

**Disturbance Observer (DOB):**
The 2-DoF controller is augmented with a DOB to estimate and cancel lumped disturbance torques, such as friction and external loads.
- **Estimation**: The DOB estimates the disturbance torque $\hat{\tau}_d$ by comparing the commanded torque and the measured velocity against a nominal motor model ($J\ddot{\theta} = \tau - d\dot{\theta} - \tau_d$). To avoid noise amplification from velocity differentiation, a low-pass filter with cutoff frequency $\alpha_{dob}$ is applied to the estimate: $\hat{\tau}_d = \frac{\alpha_{dob}}{s+\alpha_{dob}} (\tau - (sJ+d)\dot{\theta})$.
- **Cancellation**: The estimated disturbance $\hat{\tau}_d$ is converted into a compensation current $i_{comp} = \hat{\tau}_d/K_{\tau i}$ and subtracted from the commanded current before being sent to the motor driver. This actively attenuates residual model errors and disturbances below the DOB's cutoff frequency.

**Cascaded Position-Velocity Control:**
For position-controlled revolute joints (e.g., robotic arm joints), a cascaded architecture is implemented:
- **Inner Loop**: A velocity control loop utilizing a 2-DoF controller and DOB. Its plant model is $\hat{P}_2 = 1/(Js+d)$, and its reference model is $F_2(s)$.
- **Outer Loop**: A position control loop, also with a 2-DoF controller. Its reference model is $F_1(s)$ (the full second-order response of the simulator), and its plant is approximately $\hat{P}_1 = F_2/s$ (as the inner loop closely tracks $F_2$). The output of the outer loop is a goal joint velocity $\bar{\dot{\theta}}$, which serves as the reference for the inner loop.

**Implementation Details:**
The 2-DoF controller runs at a frequency of 500 Hz. The process involves reading encoder data, computing feedforward and feedback torques, calculating the DOB estimate, summing these terms, saturating the current command, and sending it to the motor driver. A low-pass filter is also applied to the velocity signal used by the DOB to further reduce noise. Controller parameters ($K_{\tau i}$, J, d, $\alpha$, $\alpha_{dob}$, PID gains, simulator $K_p$, $D$, $I_{sim}$) are provided, with hardware parameters identified from a pendulum free-response fit. Policies are trained in Isaac Sim using PPO within the IsaacLab framework.

**Comparison Methods:**
The proposed method (2-DoF and 2-DoF+DOB) is compared against:
- **PID Cascade**: The factory default multi-stage position–velocity–current PID controller in the servo firmware, representing a feedback-only, 1-DoF baseline.
- **Simple PD**: A manually tuned single-loop PD controller outputting motor current, also a feedback-only approach.
- **ASAP**: A learned actuator-modeling baseline that trains a "Delta Action" model on real-world rollouts to compensate for sim-to-real dynamics mismatch. This requires hardware-specific data.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The validation of actuator reality shaping was conducted across a range of platforms, progressing from single-joint experiments to complex multi-DOF robot tasks.

**Single-Axis Tracking Experiments:**
- **Setup**: A single Dynamixel YM070 high-gear-ratio servo was used. The task involved tracking a sinusoidal reference trajectory (20° amplitude, 2.5 s period) over 20 seconds, with controlled disturbance injection.
- **Metrics**: Position error ($\Delta\theta = \theta - \theta_{ref}$) and velocity error ($\Delta\dot{\theta} = \dot{\theta} - \dot{\theta}_{ref}$) relative to the simulated reference dynamics.
- **Comparison**: PID Cascade, Simple PD, 2-DoF (feedforward-feedback only), and 2-DoF+DOB (with disturbance observer).
- **Results**:
 - The PID Cascade and Simple PD controllers exhibited a substantial sim-to-real gap, characterized by larger position and velocity errors. Their position loops tracked the goal angle rather than the reference dynamics, leading to a systematic bias in velocity error.
 - The 2-DoF and 2-DoF+DOB controllers demonstrated significantly superior tracking performance, with error clouds concentrated near the origin in phase portraits. The feedforward filter F reduced phase lag without causing overshoot.
 - Quantitatively, the 2-DoF+DOB controller reduced the mean position deviation by 96.3% compared to the PID Cascade and 94.5% compared to the Simple PD. Mean velocity deviation was reduced by 99.1% relative to the PID Cascade.
 - While 2-DoF alone performed well, the DOB provided additional benefits, further suppressing disturbance torques like friction and reducing a slight position offset observed with 2-DoF without DOB.

**7-DOF Robotic Arm Reaching Task:**
- **Setup**: A 7-DOF robotic arm was tasked with tracking a lemniscate (figure-eight) end-effector trajectory. Policies were trained against the ideal reference model in IsaacSim and deployed zero-shot.
- **Metrics**: End-effector position error (∥∆p∥) and velocity error (∥∆ ˙p∥).
- **Comparison**: PID Cascade, Simple PD, 2-DoF+DOB, and ASAP (a learned Delta Action model).
- **Results**:
 - Controllers other than 2-DoF+DOB exhibited poor tracking of the target trajectory.
 - The 2-DoF+DOB controller achieved the lowest end-effector deviation. It resulted in a 96.5% reduction in mean end-effector position deviation relative to the factory PID cascade and a 72% reduction compared to the tuned Simple PD baseline.
 - The DOB's contribution was more pronounced in this multi-joint task, accounting for a further 50% reduction in error over 2-DoF without DOB. Uncompensated friction in the PD and cascaded controllers introduced systematic offsets that the policy could not anticipate.
 - The ASAP baseline, which requires hardware-specific data collection for its Delta Action model, showed larger actions compared to the base policy, which destabilized training in simulation. Its real-robot performance was not substantially better than the untuned factory default, despite its aim to correct the sim-to-real gap. In contrast, actuator reality shaping attained good zero-shot performance without any fine-tuning.

**Zero-Shot Demonstrations on Other Embodiments:**
The method's generality was evaluated on two additional platforms:
- **Wheeled-Legged Robot**: For a 31-DOF wheeled-legged robot with velocity-controlled wheel actuators, a policy trained in simulation against the reference model was transferred zero-shot. The robot successfully climbed a slope, demonstrating that the 2-DoF controller could reproduce the simulator's compliant spring-damper behavior even in challenging terrain where height inference from joint states and IMU is required.
- **Humanoid Robot**: On a 22-DOF humanoid robot (using Dynamixel PH54 servos, different from the YM070), a walking policy was trained using the BeyondMimic framework. The only modification was replacing the actuator model with the ideal reference model. The policy transferred without hardware-specific tuning, enabling the robot to perform stable forward walking. This indicated the approach's applicability to high-DOF, dynamically balancing whole-body platforms.

These results collectively demonstrate that actuator reality shaping substantially reduces sim-to-real tracking error and improves zero-shot task performance across diverse robot hardware and tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work "Actuator Reality Shaping for Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Robot Learning" presents a notable contribution to the field of robot learning and control by addressing the pervasive sim-to-real gap, particularly at the actuator level. Its significance and potential impact stem from several key aspects:

1. **Enabling Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer**: The primary impact is its ability to facilitate zero-shot transfer of RL policies from simulation to physical robots. By shaping the physical actuator's behavior to match the idealized simulator dynamics, the method eliminates the need for hardware-specific fine-tuning or data collection. This directly reduces the often-prohibitive cost and time associated with deploying learned policies on real hardware.

2. **Increased Policy Reusability**: The paradigm of actuator reality shaping promotes hardware abstraction. Policies trained against a standardized, idealized actuator model become largely independent of the specific physical actuator hardware. This means the same trained policy can potentially be reused across different robot platforms, or even with replaced/degraded actuators, simply by designing or retuning the low-level 2-DoF driver. This capability enhances the modularity and economic viability of robot learning solutions.

3. **Simplified Policy Development and Learning**: By presenting a consistent, idealized, and predictable dynamic interface to the RL agent, the method simplifies the policy optimization process. Policies are less exposed to the complex, nonlinear, and often unpredictable characteristics of real-world actuators (e.g., friction, stiction, backlash). This can lead to more stable, faster, and more efficient training of RL policies, as the learning algorithm operates in a "cleaner" dynamic environment.

4. **Modular and Transparent Control Architecture**: The proposed 2-DoF controller with a disturbance observer can be designed from a minimal description of the actuator (e.g., torque constant, inertia, damping limits). This modular approach separates the high-level policy from low-level hardware intricacies, analogous to a software device driver. This enhances the transparency and maintainability of the overall control system compared to end-to-end learned approaches or extensive system identification.

5. **Broad Applicability Across Diverse Robot Platforms**: The demonstrations on a single-joint testbench, a 7-DOF robotic arm, a wheeled-legged robot, and a humanoid robot indicate the method's potential generality. Its success with both position-controlled joints and velocity-controlled wheels, as well as on dynamically balancing platforms, suggests it can serve as a reusable interface across a wide range of robotic embodiments and tasks, including complex locomotion and manipulation.

6. **Addressing High-Gear-Ratio Actuator Challenges**: The research specifically highlights the challenge posed by high-gear-ratio servos, where reflected friction, stiction, and backlash dominate joint torque and are difficult for simple parametric models to capture. The 2-DoF+DOB approach effectively compensates for these effects, making it particularly impactful for robots utilizing such actuators.

7. **Contribution to Control Theory and Practice**: The work demonstrates a practical application of well-established control-theoretic concepts (2-DoF control, disturbance observers) to solve a contemporary problem in robot learning. It bridges the gap between low-level robust control and high-level reinforcement learning, suggesting a synergistic approach.

While the method currently assumes constant joint-axis inertia (negligible for high-gear-ratio actuators but a limitation for quasi-direct-drive systems) and requires coarse estimates of nominal parameters, its foundational principle offers a new direction for mitigating the sim-to-real gap. Future work may explore extensions to dynamically varying inertia and highly nonlinear hardware, further expanding its impact. Overall, actuator reality shaping has the potential to accelerate the development and deployment of robust, capable robots by fundamentally altering how RL policies interact with physical hardware.
