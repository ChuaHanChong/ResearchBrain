# Macro-Operator Generation and Predicate Selection for TAMP Operator Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> A TAMP operator-learning system that automatically discovers macro-operators via effect-complement analysis, pairing actions where one consistently adds a predicate the next deletes, then compresses each pair into one planning step, and prunes predicates unused by any learned operator. It yields roughly 4.6x planning speedup on Blocks and solves a 7-ingredient Kitchen domain the baseline cannot solve at all.

## Problem

- Hand-authoring symbolic operators for task and motion planning demands heavy domain expertise, and is the main bottleneck blocking deployment of such planners.
- Prior operator learning treats each action in isolation, so the planner rediscovers recurring two-step causal routines at every node, inflating horizon and branching factor.
- Every user-supplied predicate is parsed at every search node even when no learned operator ever references it, wasting parsing effort and enlarging grounded symbolic state.

## Method

- ==Effect-complement analysis== mines consistent per-action-type adds and deletes from demonstrations, forms ordered causal pairs where one action's add is another's delete, then validates by consecutive-occurrence frequency and resolves symmetric conflicts.
- **Macro-operator** integration reuses the unchanged operator learner: argument unification with index vectors, synthetic self-loop negative transitions for precondition learning, and a sequential sampler conditioning the second sub-action's continuous parameters on the simulated intermediate state.
- **Iterative Predicate Selection** is a post-learning feature-selection filter retaining only predicates appearing in some learned precondition or effect, shrinking the state parsed at each ==A*== node.

## Results

- Blocks planning time drops from **0.185 s** to **0.040 s**, about **4.6x** speedup, with plan length unchanged after macro expansion (**10.0** versus **10.2** steps) and success rising to **100%**.
- The **7-ingredient** Kitchen domain, needing **55.6-step** plans, is solved on all **100 instances** in **0.051 s**, while the baseline finds no plan even with the **30-minute** timeout lifted.
- Subtractive ablation: removing macros costs **4.4x** slowdown on Blocks and total **0%** success on Kitchen; removing predicate pruning changes little, and Cover regresses from **0.001 s** to **0.093 s**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Macro benefit is not a fixed speedup but a shortened effective horizon, so gains scale with plan length and can invert on very short tasks.
> - Causal structure needed for useful macros is already latent in demonstration data as add-delete complementarity, requiring no manual guidance or extra supervision.
> - Sub-actions inside a macro have coupled continuous parameters, so independent sampling breaks feasibility; ordered conditional sampling through the intermediate state is required.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Autonomous robotic systems often encounter complex planning tasks that necessitate reasoning across both high-level discrete choices and low-level continuous parameters, such as object interactions, grasp poses, and motion trajectories. Task and Motion Planning (TAMP) is a prominent approach designed to address this challenge. TAMP employs a bilevel structure where a symbolic planner generates abstract action sequences, and a low-level component refines these sequences by assigning continuous values. A core characteristic of many TAMP systems is their reliance on manually specified symbolic operators, typically defined in formalisms like the Planning Domain Definition Language (PDDL). The manual creation of these operators requires significant domain knowledge and represents a considerable bottleneck in deploying TAMP systems.

Recent research has focused on reducing this dependency by applying machine learning techniques to various aspects of the TAMP pipeline. Efforts include learning continuous samplers for grasp poses and placement locations, as well as developing guidance models to steer symbolic search. A distinct line of inquiry, and one most pertinent to this work, involves learning the symbolic operators themselves directly from demonstration or interaction data. Previous works in action-model learning for classical planning, such as noisy deictic rules, LOCM, and STRIPS-style learning, have explored recovering action models. More recent advancements include methods like DeepSym that discover predicates from continuous observations and other neuro-symbolic approaches.

Learning Operators for Task and Motion Planning (LOFT) represents a direct antecedent to the current paper. LOFT demonstrated that lifted, object-parameterized TAMP operators can be learned from limited demonstrations and integrated into sampling-based TAMP planners. However, LOFT and similar operator learning methods exhibit two primary limitations that this work seeks to address. First, they typically learn each action in isolation, failing to recognize and leverage the recurring multi-step action sequences that are common in manipulation tasks. This isolated treatment can lead to an inflated effective planning horizon and an unmanageable branching factor in search for long sequential tasks. Second, these methods often evaluate all provided symbolic predicates at every search node, even if a predicate does not appear in any learned operator's preconditions or effects, thereby incurring unnecessary computational overhead and expanding the symbolic search space.

Macro-operators, or composite actions, have a historical basis in classical AI planning, dating back to systems like MACROPS, where chaining individual actions into single steps was shown to reduce search depth. In robotics, macro-actions are used to group low-level behaviors into reusable skills. Applied to TAMP, macros can condense multi-step sub-routines (e.g., picking and placing an object) into a single planning step, thus shortening the effective planning horizon. While the concept of macro-operators is established, their automatic discovery and validation specifically for learned probabilistic TAMP operators, particularly in a manner that accounts for the sequential and causal structure within demonstration data, has remained an underexplored area. This research contributes to the TAMP landscape by proposing a system that automates the generation of such macro-operators and optimizes predicate usage, thereby enhancing the efficiency and practical solvability of TAMP problems, especially for long-horizon tasks, building upon and extending the capabilities of existing operator learning frameworks like LOFT.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from two identified practical limitations in current approaches to learning symbolic operators for Task and Motion Planning (TAMP) systems, particularly those building upon methods like LOFT. While operator learning offers a significant advantage over manual operator creation, these limitations hinder the scalability and efficiency of TAMP in complex, real-world robotic environments.

The first key objective is to address the issue of **isolated action learning**. Existing operator learning methods for TAMP typically treat each action as an independent entity, learning its preconditions and effects without explicitly considering the frequent sequential dependencies between actions. In many manipulation tasks, certain actions almost invariably occur in fixed causal orders; for instance, picking an object is often immediately followed by placing or stacking it, where the first action creates a condition (e.g., holding the object) that the second action consumes. When a TAMP planner uses only individual actions, it must repeatedly discover these recurring multi-step patterns from scratch at every search node. This practice significantly inflates the effective planning horizon, as a task requiring many interdependent actions translates into a long sequence of individual steps in the symbolic search space. The resulting large search depth and high branching factor can render the planning problem intractable, especially for long-horizon tasks. The motivation here is to compress these frequent multi-step sub-routines into single planning steps, thereby shrinking the effective planning horizon and making long-duration tasks more tractable.

The second key objective is to mitigate the **inefficiency of symbolic state representation**. In current TAMP operator learning systems, the symbolic state at each search node is derived by evaluating every predicate from a pre-defined, user-provided set. This comprehensive evaluation occurs repetitively throughout the search process. A significant inefficiency arises when many of these provided predicates never appear in the preconditions or effects of any of the learned operators. Such unused predicates still contribute to the computational effort of state parsing at every node and enlarge the grounded symbolic state, even though the planner cannot leverage or alter the facts they represent. The motivation behind this objective is to optimize the symbolic state representation by pruning irrelevant predicates, thereby reducing the computational overhead associated with state parsing and shrinking the effective symbolic search space.

In summary, the research aims to enhance the practical deployment of learned TAMP systems by:
1. **Automatically generating macro-operators:** These composite actions compress recurring sequences of individual actions, addressing the limitations of isolated action learning by recognizing and formalizing causal links between actions. This is intended to shorten the effective planning horizon and manage the branching factor in planning for complex tasks.
2. **Implementing Iterative Predicate Selection (IPS):** This mechanism prunes predicates that are not referenced by any learned operator. This is intended to reduce the size of the symbolic state representation and the computational cost of evaluating it during planning.

Together, these objectives aim to improve planning speed, reduce computational burden, and, critically, enable the solvability of long sequential tasks that current baseline methods cannot effectively address.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed system extends an existing operator learning pipeline for TAMP, specifically building upon the Learning Operators for Task and Motion Planning (LOFT) framework. The overall architecture integrates two main contributions: automatic macro-operator generation and iterative predicate selection, into the operator learning and TAMP planning process.

**Background (LOFT baseline):**
The underlying TAMP approach involves a bilevel planning scheme. At the high level, a symbolic planner, utilizing A* search with a standard heuristic, generates plan skeletons (sequences of high-level actions with fixed discrete arguments but open continuous parameters). At the low level, a backtracking search mechanism fills in these continuous parameters by calling domain-specific samplers and simulates the actions to check for feasibility.
The operator learning module in LOFT takes as input a dataset of low-level transitions (oracle demonstrations `D` and random exploration `R`) and a fixed set of user-provided typed predicates `P`. Each continuous state `x` is converted into a symbolic state `s` using a `Parse` function, which identifies the predicates holding in `x`. Operator learning proceeds in three stages:
1. **Lifted effect clustering:** Identifies consistent added and deleted predicates for each action type. Transitions with similar effects, after lifting object instances to typed variables, are grouped into candidate operators.
2. **Precondition learning:** For each candidate operator, a set of preconditions is learned by scoring observed predicates preceding the action. Positive scores are given for predicates consistently observed in the operator’s own transitions, while negative scores are assigned for predicates observed in transitions where the same action produced different effects (often from `R`).
3. **Parameter estimation:** Probabilities for each effect are computed. Effects falling below a probability threshold are discarded, and the remaining outcomes are converted into deterministic operators via all-outcome determinization.

**Our Method:**

**1. Macro-Operator Generation (Algorithm 1):**
This component automatically discovers and constructs macro-operators from the training data, designed to collapse causally linked action pairs into single planning steps.
- **Phase 1: Consistent effects of each action type:** This phase analyzes the oracle demonstrations `D`. For each transition `(x_i, a_i, x_{i+1})`, the initial and resulting continuous states (`x_i`, `x_{i+1}`) are converted into symbolic states (`s_i`, `s_{i+1}`). The predicates added (`s_{i+1} \ s_i`) and deleted (`s_i \ s_{i+1}`) by action `a_i` are recorded. A predicate is identified as a consistent add (or delete) of an action type `a` if it is added (or deleted) in more than 50% of `a`’s transitions within `D`. This yields sets `Adds[a]` and `Dels[a]` for each action type. Random exploration data `R` is excluded here to avoid diluting the consistency of successful effects.
- **Phase 2: Causal pairs:** This phase identifies potential causal links between distinct action types `(a_i, a_{i+1})`. A causal-link set `L(a_i, a_{i+1})` is computed as the intersection of `Adds[a_i]` and `Dels[a_{i+1}]`. A non-empty `L(a_i, a_{i+1})` indicates that `a_i` consistently produces a condition that `a_{i+1}` consistently consumes. Only pairs where `L` contains at least one predicate with arity greater than zero (i.e., object-specific predicates) are retained, forming the causal pair set `C`. This filters out global state predicates that do not represent specific causal links between objects.
- **Phase 3: Validation and conflict resolution:** The causal pairs in `C` are validated by counting their consecutive occurrences `f(a_i, a_{i+1})` in the combined training data `D ∪ R`. Only pairs occurring at least once are kept, forming the validated set `V`. If both `(a_i, a_{i+1})` and `(a_{i+1}, a_i)` are in `V`, the pair with the lower frequency `f` is discarded to prevent redundant macro generation for the same underlying pattern.
- **Phase 4: Building macro operators:** For each validated pair `(a_i, a_{i+1})` in `V`, occurrences of the consecutive sequence `(x_i, a_i, x_{i+1}, a_{i+1}, x_{i+2})` are found in `D ∪ R`. A new macro action `M` is created by unifying the arguments of `a_i` and `a_{i+1}` into a single argument list, along with index vectors to map back to the original sub-action arguments. This `M` is added to the set of macro action types `A_M`. A macro training transition `(x_i, M, x_{i+2})` is generated, effectively skipping the intermediate state `x_{i+1}`. These macro transitions form the set `T_+`. It is noted that a single causal pair can lead to multiple macro types if different argument-sharing patterns are observed.

**2. Integrating Macros into the Operator Learning Module:**
- **Negative Example Generation:** Since macros were not part of the original action set during `R` collection, synthetic negative examples are generated. For each macro type `M`, a random state `x` from `R` is chosen, and `M`’s parameters are bound to matching objects `o`. The transition `(x, M(o), x)` is recorded as a negative example in `T_-`, implying `M`’s preconditions did not hold if the state remained unchanged.
- **Learning Macro Operators:** The operator learning module is augmented: the action types become `A' = A ∪ A_M`, positive transitions `D' = D ∪ T_+`, and random exploration transitions `R' = R ∪ T_-`. Each macro is treated as a regular action, and its preconditions and effects are learned using the same three stages as individual actions.
- **Sequential Continuous Sampling:** To handle continuous parameters for macros, a sequential sampler is introduced. It samples parameters for the first sub-action, simulates it to an intermediate state, and then samples parameters for the second sub-action conditioned on this intermediate state, leveraging the index vectors from Phase 4 for argument mapping. After planning, macros in a returned plan are expanded back into their original two sub-actions for execution.

**3. Iterative Predicate Selection (IPS):**
After all operators (individual and macro) have been learned, IPS is applied as a post-learning step. This component filters the initial user-provided predicate set `P`. Only predicates that appear in at least one precondition or effect of any learned operator `o` (`o ∈ O`) are retained, forming a reduced set `P'`. The planner then uses `P'` instead of `P` to parse symbolic states, thereby reducing the computational cost of state evaluation at each A* search node and shrinking the grounded state representation. This filtering process incurs a negligible one-time cost of scanning the learned operators.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of the proposed system was conducted across four TAMP domains: Cover (short plans, 2-4 steps), Blocks (medium plans, ~10 steps), Painting (longer plans, ~32 steps), and Kitchen (long-horizon tasks, >50 steps). The full pipeline (including macro-operator generation and Iterative Predicate Selection, referred to as "Ours") was compared against the baseline operator learning method (LOFT).

**Macro Discovery and Learned Operators:**
In the Blocks domain, effect-complement analysis identified five causal candidates, which resolved into two validated macro-operators: Pick→PutOnTable and Pick→Stack. Both macros were found to be based on the causal link where the `Pick` action adds the `Holding(b)` predicate, which is subsequently deleted by `PutOnTable` or `Stack` as a precondition. An example `MacroPickStack` operator learned automatically demonstrated the successful unification of arguments and absorption of the intermediate `Holding` state, correctly representing the combined action of picking an object off one support and stacking it on another. The addition of these macros increased the total action set from 4 individual operators to 7 (including one `Pick→PutOnTable` and two `Pick→Stack` variants due to different argument patterns). In the Kitchen domain, three causal pairs were validated (Pick followed by PlaceOnBoard, PlaceOnBurner, or PlaceOnDish), resulting in 6 macro operators, bringing the total to 16 operators (10 individual + 6 macro).

**Planning Performance and Solvability:**
- **Success Rates:** Both the baseline and "Ours" achieved 100% success on Painting. In Blocks, the baseline had a 99.0% success rate, while "Ours" achieved 100%, indicating that macros can reduce instances of low-level sampling failures by shortening plan skeletons. In Cover, "Ours" experienced a slight reduction to 98.0% success compared to the baseline's 100%. The most significant difference was in the Kitchen domain, where the baseline achieved 0.0% success, unable to find any plans, while "Ours" successfully solved all 100 test instances with a 100% success rate. This demonstrates that macro-operators can enable solvability for long-horizon tasks.
- **Planning Time:** The impact on planning time varied with task length.
 - **Blocks:** "Ours" achieved a planning speedup of approximately 4.6 times (0.040 ± 0.059 s) compared to the baseline (0.185 ± 0.912 s), attributed to the reduced number of A* nodes expanded.
 - **Kitchen:** While the baseline failed to solve any problem, "Ours" completed planning for the complex Kitchen tasks in 0.051 ± 0.019 s. The baseline was tested with disabled timeouts and still failed to find a plan after 30 minutes for a single problem instance.
 - **Painting:** Both approaches solved the tasks quickly, with "Ours" showing a small gain (0.024 ± 0.007 s vs. 0.028 ± 0.026 s for baseline).
 - **Cover:** "Ours" exhibited a net planning slowdown (0.093 ± 0.190 s) compared to the baseline (0.001 ± 0.001 s). For these very short tasks (2-4 steps), the added branching factor from macro operators outweighed any benefit from reduced plan depth.
The observed pattern indicated that the benefit of macro-operators scaled with plan length: a minor detriment for very short tasks, a significant speedup for medium-length tasks, and the decisive factor for solvability in long-horizon tasks.

**Structural Properties and Overhead:**
- **Operator Count:** Macro generation added 1 operator in Cover and Painting, 3 in Blocks, and 6 in Kitchen, consistent with the number of validated causal pairs and argument-sharing patterns.
- **Predicate Count (after IPS):** Iterative Predicate Selection (IPS) reduced the active predicate count in Cover (from 5 to 3) and Blocks (from 6 to 5), by removing predicates not referenced by any learned operator. In Painting and Kitchen, IPS had no effect, as all provided predicates were utilized by the learned operators.
- **Training Time:** The addition of macro generation introduced a modest overhead to the overall training time (e.g., from 0.069 s to 0.196 s in Blocks). Training times remained below 4 seconds across all domains, indicating that these costs are paid once upfront and are small relative to the planning benefits.

**Ablation Study:**
An ablation study on the Blocks and Kitchen domains quantified the contributions of each component:
- **Blocks:** Removing macro-operators from the full pipeline significantly degraded performance (success rate from 100% to 99.0%, planning time increased from 0.041 s to 0.180 s), reverting it close to baseline levels. Removing IPS resulted in a negligible increase in planning time (from 0.041 s to 0.042 s) and no change in success rate, due to one additional predicate being considered. This suggests macros are the primary driver of planning efficiency, while IPS provides a secondary, node-level optimization.
- **Kitchen:** Removing IPS had no impact on performance (100% success, 0.051 s), as all predicates were deemed useful. However, removing macro-operators led to complete failure (0.0% success), confirming their critical role in the solvability of long-horizon tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research addresses two fundamental challenges in deploying learned Task and Motion Planning (TAMP) systems: the inefficiency caused by extraneous symbolic state information and the intractability of planning for long-horizon tasks when using only individual actions. The proposed system, through automated macro-operator generation and Iterative Predicate Selection (IPS), offers a method to mitigate these issues.

The primary significance of this work lies in demonstrating that the benefit of macro-operators in TAMP is not limited to a fixed speedup, but rather fundamentally alters the effective planning horizon. For very short tasks (e.g., two steps), the overhead of considering additional macro branches can outweigh the benefits of reduced plan depth. However, for tasks of moderate length (e.g., ten steps), the system yielded substantial planning speedups (up to approximately 4.6 times). Critically, for long-horizon manipulation tasks involving over 50 steps, the presence of macro-operators shifted the outcome from complete planning failure (the baseline could not solve these tasks even with extended computation time) to successful, efficient plan generation (solving tasks in approximately 0.05 seconds). This indicates that, for certain complex domains, the automated discovery and utilization of macro-operators can be a prerequisite for planning success rather than a mere optimization.

The Iterative Predicate Selection (IPS) component contributes to reducing computational overhead by pruning unreferenced predicates. While its impact on overall planning time was modest in some domains and negligible in others (where all predicates were utilized), it provides a systematic way to optimize the symbolic state representation, thereby reducing the parsing effort at each search node and potentially improving the efficiency of the underlying symbolic planner.

The potential impact of this research is multifaceted:
- **Enhanced TAMP Deployability:** By automating the generation of complex operators and optimizing symbolic state representations, the proposed system reduces the domain engineering burden, making TAMP more accessible and practical for real-world robotic applications.
- **Scalability to Complex Tasks:** The ability to solve long-horizon tasks that were previously intractable significantly expands the scope of problems that learned TAMP systems can address. This is particularly relevant for intricate manipulation sequences in unstructured or semi-structured environments, such as household chores or manufacturing assembly.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The work opens several avenues for further investigation. Extending macro-operator generation to discover longer or nested action chains would enable deeper plan abstraction. Research into automated predicate discovery from raw sensor data, rather than relying on user-provided sets, could further reduce manual intervention. Learning continuous samplers for macro-operators from interaction data would also represent a step towards a more fully autonomous system. Finally, evaluating the robustness of these learned operators and macro-operators on physical robots, considering challenges like noisy perception and sim-to-real transfer, would be a critical next step towards practical deployment. The current offline learning approach also suggests a future direction for online, incremental operator learning for adaptive systems.
