# RL Token: Bootstrapping Online RL with Vision-Language-Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> A method named RLT is introduced, which enables general-purpose Vision-Language-Action models to achieve precision and speed for real-world manipulation tasks. By employing a compact "RL token" and lightweight online reinforcement learning, RLT policies demonstrated up to a 3x speedup and substantial success rate increases on precision-critical tasks with only a few hours of real-robot practice, discovering emergent strategies superior to demonstrations.

## Problem

- General-purpose Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models frequently lack the precision and speed needed for "last millimeter" real-world manipulation, constrained by demonstration data quality.
- Real-world robotic reinforcement learning (RL) faces tight sample budgets, making direct fine-tuning of large, billion-parameter VLA models computationally and sample-inefficient.
- Existing RL fine-tuning approaches either require extensive computation by updating entire VLAs or sacrifice the broad generalization capabilities of VLAs by training smaller models from scratch.

## Method

- A pretrained **VLA** is adapted to expose a compact "==RL token==," a learned compressed embedding summarizing internal **VLA** features as an efficient, low-dimensional state for RL.
- A lightweight ==actor-critic== network is trained online on the RL token combined with proprioceptive robot state, operating on short action chunks for increased reactivity.
- The RL actor is directly conditioned on the VLA's sampled reference action chunks and regularized towards them with an L2 penalty, incorporating a dropout mechanism to prevent simple copying.

## Results

- RLT improved critical phases across four manipulation tasks, achieving up to a **3x** speedup on easier tasks and boosting success rates from **20%** to **65%** for more challenging ones, like screw installation.
- On Ethernet insertion, RLT matched base VLA success while cutting mean steps by **2x** (vs DAgger, DSRL), hitting a median 66 timesteps vs 146 for expert teleoperation and 228 for the base VLA.
- Ablation studies confirmed that the RL token, action chunking, and behavioral cloning regularization towards VLA reference actions were each essential components contributing to RLT's performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The compact "RL token" effectively encodes manipulation-relevant structure from the VLA, serving as an efficient low-dimensional state for online RL without full VLA fine-tuning.
> - Acting on action chunks, not single steps, aids credit assignment and sample efficiency in sparse-reward, long-horizon tasks; conditioning the RL actor on VLA-predicted reference chunks stabilizes training and refines robust initial behaviors.
> - The RLT method allows for the emergence of novel and more efficient manipulation strategies, surpassing initial demonstrations and even expert human teleoperation speeds.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in "RL Token: Bootstrapping Online RL with Vision-Language-Action Models" addresses a significant challenge in contemporary robotics: enabling general-purpose Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models to achieve the precision and speed required for real-world manipulation tasks.

**Vision-Language-Action Models:** Recent advancements in robotics have seen VLA models emerge as a dominant paradigm for learning diverse manipulation skills from large demonstration datasets. These models, often leveraging large pretrained vision-language models (e.g., RT-2, OpenVLA, π0, π0.5, π0.6), excel at generalization and can perform a wide range of tasks "out of the box" by integrating web-scale prior knowledge. Key enablers for their success include action chunking (predicting sequences of actions for open-loop execution) and expressive output distributions (e.g., diffusion models) to capture multimodal demonstration data. However, a common limitation is their performance on precision-critical tasks, where they may exhibit slow motions, require retries, or fail due to small errors in "last millimeter" execution. Their performance is ultimately bounded by the quality and coverage of their training data, which can be noisy or inconsistent for highly precise behaviors.

**Real-World Reinforcement Learning:** Reinforcement Learning (RL) offers a method to overcome the limitations of demonstration data by allowing agents to discover faster, more precise, or more robust strategies through practice. However, real-world robotic RL operates under strict constraints, primarily due to tight sample budgets (each robot rollout consumes time, effort, and wear). Sample-efficient off-policy actor-critic methods (e.g., SAC, TD3) combined with replay buffers and demonstration data have shown promise in solving contact-rich tasks within hours. These methods often employ smaller models, which can adapt quickly but lack the broad generalization capabilities of large VLA models. Existing efforts in this area, such as HIL-SERL and RL-100, typically train policies from scratch on standard visual encoders, thus not fully leveraging the rich behavioral priors of modern VLAs.

**RL Fine-tuning of VLA Models:** A growing research direction focuses on improving pretrained VLAs through RL. Approaches vary in the extent of model updates and how the RL signal is incorporated. Some methods update the entire VLA model (e.g., RECAP, SimpleVLA-RL, PPO variants), which can be computationally and sample-inefficient for rapid online adaptation on physical robots. Others adopt lightweight approaches, training small auxiliary modules on top of frozen VLA backbones (e.g., ConRFT, Policy Decorator, PLD, GR-RL, DSRL). These methods often involve predicting residuals, modulating latent spaces, or using consistency-based objectives.

This work, RLT, positions itself within this landscape by seeking to bridge the gap between the generalization power of large VLAs and the sample efficiency of lightweight online RL. It aims to develop a practical method for rapid online adaptation of VLAs for precision tasks, directly on real robots, by leveraging VLA representations without incurring the costs of full VLA model fine-tuning. RLT distinguishes itself through several design choices: introducing a compact "RL token" as a state observation, operating over chunked actions aligned with the VLA's native interface, and directly conditioning the RL actor on and regularizing it towards the VLA's sampled reference action chunk.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the observed limitations of current general-purpose Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models in real-world robotic manipulation. While these models can perform a wide array of tasks from demonstration data, they frequently exhibit deficiencies in achieving the required precision and speed for delicate operations. Specifically:
- **Precision and Speed Deficiencies:** VLAs often struggle with the "last millimeter of execution," leading to slow motions, requiring multiple retries, and accumulating small errors that can result in task failure, particularly in contact-rich or sub-millimeter precision tasks. This performance ceiling is imposed by the inherent noise and inconsistencies in large-scale demonstration datasets.
- **Resource Constraints in Real-World Robotics:** Fine-tuning VLAs with RL is a natural approach to overcome these limitations. However, real-world robotic learning operates under severe constraints: every training episode consumes robot time, incurs wear and tear, and adaptation must occur within a practical timeframe, often a few hours.
- **Inefficiency of Existing RL Fine-tuning Approaches:**
 - Directly fine-tuning the entire VLA model with online RL is computationally expensive and sample-inefficient due to the models' large parameter counts (billions). This makes rapid adaptation infeasible.
 - Training much smaller models from scratch, while sample-efficient, sacrifices the extensive generalization capabilities and rich behavioral priors embedded within the pretrained VLA.

Given these challenges, the central question addressed by this work is: **How can one leverage the generalization capabilities and rich representations of a large pretrained VLA model while simultaneously achieving the speed and sample efficiency characteristic of lightweight online reinforcement learning for real-world adaptation?**

The key objectives of this research are:
1. **Develop a Lightweight Adaptation Method:** Introduce a practical recipe, RLT, that enables sample-efficient online RL fine-tuning of pretrained VLAs using only a few hours of real-world practice.
2. **Expose an "RL Token" Interface:** Adapt the VLA to expose a compact "RL token"—a compressed readout representation of the VLA's internal features. This token is designed to preserve task-relevant pretrained knowledge while serving as an efficient and low-dimensional interface for online RL.
3. **Train a Lightweight Actor-Critic Head:** Train a small actor-critic network on this exposed RL token. This actor-critic system is intended to refine the actions proposed by the VLA, anchoring its learning to the VLA's prior behaviors rather than learning from scratch.
4. **Demonstrate Performance Improvement:** Validate that RLT can improve both the success rate and execution speed of precision-critical manipulation tasks (e.g., screw installation, zip tie fastening, Ethernet/charger insertion) within minutes to a few hours of real-robot practice, potentially surpassing human teleoperation speed on certain task phases.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The RLT method is designed to combine the broad perceptual understanding and action recommendations of a pretrained Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model with the sample efficiency of lightweight online Reinforcement Learning (RL). The core idea involves adapting the VLA to expose a compact, task-relevant "RL token" and then training a small actor-critic network on this representation, guided by the VLA's predictions.

**A. Adapting the VLA to Expose an RL Interface (RL Token Extraction)**
The initial step focuses on extracting a suitable state representation for efficient online RL from the high-dimensional internal features of a large VLA (specifically, the π0.6 model).
1. **Problem:** Directly using the full VLA representation (e.g., billions of parameters, high-dimensional transformer embeddings) for online RL is computationally expensive and sample-inefficient.
2. **Solution: RL Token:** An "RL token" is introduced as a learned, compressed embedding that summarizes the VLA's internal knowledge into a small vector, serving as the RL state.
3. **Architecture:** A small additional transformer network is appended to the pretrained VLA. This transformer operates in an encoder-decoder fashion.
 - **Encoder (g_ϕ):** Takes the VLA's final-layer token embeddings (z_1:M) for the current state (s) and language instruction (ℓ), appends a learned embedding (e_rl), and processes the augmented sequence. The output at the special token position (z_rl) becomes the RL token (1x2048 dimension).
 - **Decoder (d_ϕ):** With a linear output projection (h_ϕ), the decoder is trained to autoregressively reconstruct the original VLA embeddings (sg(z_i)) from the extracted z_rl. This reconstruction objective (L_ro) forces the z_rl to act as a bottleneck, ensuring it retains sufficient task-relevant information.
4. **Training:** The parameters (ϕ) of this encoder-decoder transformer are trained on a small, task-specific demonstration dataset. During this process, the VLA's parameters (θ_vla) are generally frozen with respect to L_ro, though optional supervised fine-tuning of the VLA itself can occur. After this adaptation, both the VLA (θ_vla) and the RL token module (ϕ) are frozen for subsequent online RL.

**B. Online RL to Refine VLA Action Chunks**
After the RL token is established, lightweight actor-critic networks are trained online to refine the VLA's actions.
1. **Actor-Critic Networks:** A stochastic actor (π_θ) and a critic (Q_ψ) are initialized. Their inputs (x) combine the RL token (z_rl) with additional proprioceptive robot state (s_p).
2. **Chunked Actions:** Both the actor and critic operate over action chunks, a_t:t+C-1, which are sequences of C actions. The RL chunk length (C) is chosen to be shorter than the VLA's prediction horizon (H) to allow for greater reactivity (C=10 steps for 0.2s, compared to H=50 steps for 1s). The VLA (π_vla) provides a reference action chunk, ˜a_t:t+C-1.
3. **Critic Training:**
 - The critic Q_ψ(x, a_1:C) estimates the value of a given state and action chunk.
 - It is trained using standard off-policy temporal-difference (TD) learning (following TD3 principles) on transitions sampled from a replay buffer (B).
 - The target Q-value (Q̂) is computed using a C-step lookahead, incorporating discounted future rewards and the estimated value of the next state-action chunk.
 - A sparse binary reward (1 for success, 0 for failure) is provided by a human supervisor at the end of each episode.
4. **Actor Training:**
 - The actor network π_θ(·|x, ˜a_1:C) produces a Gaussian action distribution over action chunks.
 - **VLA Reference Action Conditioning:** The actor is directly conditioned on the reference action chunk (˜a_1:C) sampled from the VLA's policy. This allows the RL to refine a strong initial proposal rather than learning from scratch and helps preserve mode information from the VLA's multimodal action distribution.
 - **Regularization:** The actor's objective function (L_π) is designed to maximize the critic's value while simultaneously regularizing the learned action (a_1:C) towards the VLA's reference action (˜a_1:C) using an L2 penalty (β ||a - ˜a||^2). This promotes local refinement around promising VLA behaviors.
 - **Reference Action Dropout:** To prevent the actor from merely copying ˜a, a dropout mechanism is applied. For a random subset of training transitions, the reference chunk (˜a) is replaced with zeros. This forces the actor to maintain an independent action-generation capability, allowing it to deviate from the VLA proposal when the critic signal indicates higher value.

**C. The Complete System and Training Loop**
Algorithm 1 outlines the full training process:
1. **Initial Adaptation:** The RL token representation is trained, and the base VLA is optionally fine-tuned on task-specific demonstration data.
2. **Warmup Phase:** The replay buffer (B) is pre-filled by rolling out the base VLA policy for a set number of steps (N_warm), providing initial data for the critic.
3. **Online Rollout and Data Collection:** The robot alternates between collecting experience and performing updates.
 - At each action chunk boundary, the frozen VLA provides a reference chunk, the RL token module extracts z_rl, and the RL actor outputs an action chunk.
 - Human operators can intervene by providing teleoperated commands (a_h) which overwrite the actor's output and replace the VLA reference in the replay buffer.
 - Transitions (x_t, a_t:t+C-1, ˜a, r_t, x_t+1) are stored in B.
 - **Subsampling Action Chunks:** To increase data efficiency, intermediate steps are also stored in the replay buffer, taking transitions at a stride of 2 (e.g., <x_0, a_0:C>, <x_2, a_2:C+2>).
4. **Off-Policy Updates:** Asynchronous actor-critic updates are performed from the replay buffer. A high update-to-data ratio (G=5) is used for sample efficiency.
5. **Targeted Improvement of Critical Phases:** RLT is primarily applied to the most difficult, high-precision "critical phases" of tasks. A human operator decides when to switch control from the base VLA to the RL policy, focusing data collection and credit assignment. For autonomous testing, a VLA can be trained to predict this handover point.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The RLT method was evaluated on four real-world manipulation tasks: screw installation, zip tie fastening, Ethernet insertion, and charger insertion. These tasks require dexterous control and sub-millimeter precision, each featuring a critical phase that demands high accuracy. Experiments were conducted in both a controlled setting (isolating the critical phase) and a full-task setting (starting from home position, involving full task variability). Performance was measured by success rate and throughput (successful task completions per 10 minutes), with training lasting between 15 minutes and 5 hours of actual robot data.

**Q1. RLT Performance vs. Base VLA Model:**
- **Consistent Improvement:** RLT consistently improved the critical phase of all four tasks.
- **Speed-Up:** For relatively easier tasks (charger, Ethernet insertion) where the base VLA already achieved good reliability, RLT policies were approximately 3x faster in the critical phase.
- **Success Rate Boost:** For harder tasks (zip tie fastening, screw installation) where the base VLA struggled, RLT significantly boosted success rates. For example, screw installation success improved from 20% to 65%.
- **Full-Task Evaluation:** In the more challenging full-task setting (where errors from earlier phases compound), RLT still improved success rates by 40% for the screw task and 60% for the zip tie task, demonstrating robustness to broader state distributions.

**Q2. RLT Comparison to Alternative RL Approaches:**
- RLT demonstrated a significant boost in throughput compared to several baselines, evaluated on the Ethernet insertion task.
- **Single-Step Online RL (HIL-SERL, PLD):** These methods, which operate on single-step actions, performed poorly. The long effective horizon of tasks with hundreds of steps and sparse rewards made value function updates ineffective for credit assignment without action chunking.
- **Imitation Learning (DAgger):** DAgger achieved comparable success rates to RLT for the Ethernet task but provided less improvement in speed, as it is inherently limited by the speed of human demonstrations and interventions.
- **Latent Space RL (DSRL):** DSRL also achieved high success rates but significantly lagged in throughput. It strongly constrains the policy to remain close to the base VLA, leading to stable training but less potential for speed improvement compared to RLT.
- **RLT's Advantage:** RLT matched the high success rate of the base policy while reducing the mean steps to completion by 2x over the base policy, indicating a superior balance of reliability and efficiency.

**Q3. Contribution of Each Component (Ablation Study):**
An ablation study on the Ethernet task demonstrated that each core design choice contributed meaningfully to RLT's performance (Fig. 7 & 8).
- **Without RL token:** Replacing the RL token with a frozen, ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-10 encoder reduced throughput by 50%. This confirmed that the RL token encodes manipulation-relevant structure derived from the VLA that a generic vision encoder does not provide.
- **Without Chunk:** When the RL policy outputted single-step actions (C=1) instead of chunks (C=10), it failed to reliably match the base policy's performance. The effective horizon of the task became too long for efficient credit assignment.
- **Without BC Regularizer (β=0):** Removing the behavioral cloning regularizer (β=0) resulted in the largest performance drop. This indicated that without regularization, the actor was forced to explore the full action space relying solely on Q-function gradients, leading to less stable training and more failures.
- **Without Pass-Through:** Removing the VLA's reference action (˜a) from the RL actor's input slowed down learning, led to early exploration drift, and occasionally resulted in degenerate behaviors. While this variant eventually matched RLT's final performance on the simpler Ethernet task, it experienced more failures and slower convergence during the training process.

**Q4. Emergent Strategies:**
- RLT enabled the robot to discover more effective strategies that were qualitatively different from the initial demonstrations.
- **Ethernet Task Example:** On the Ethernet task's critical phase, the base VLA often exhibited "probing" behavior (approaching, retreating, readjusting). In contrast, the RLT-trained policy approached the port and inserted the connector in a fluid motion. When facing initial resistance, it applied pressure and wiggled the connector slightly to exploit compliance, leading to faster insertion.
- **Beyond Imitation:** This emergent behavior was not present in the original demonstration data, illustrating that RLT can leverage online exploration to go beyond merely imitating human strategies and discover more efficient solutions. The final RLT policy achieved a median episode length of 66 timesteps, significantly faster than expert teleoperation (median 146 timesteps) and the base policy (median 228 timesteps) (Fig. 9).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The RLT method represents a notable advancement in the field of robotic manipulation, particularly for tasks requiring high precision and efficiency. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Bridging the Generalization-Precision Gap:** RLT effectively bridges the gap between the broad generalization capabilities of large Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models and the need for precision and speed in real-world robotic tasks. By enabling lightweight online RL fine-tuning on top of a frozen VLA, it offers a practical solution to enhance performance where VLAs typically struggle (the "last millimeter" of execution).
- **Sample-Efficient Real-World Adaptation:** The method demonstrates that significant performance improvements (e.g., up to 3x speedup, substantial success rate increases) can be achieved within minutes to a few hours of real-robot practice. This is crucial for practical robot deployment, where interaction time and resources are limited, enabling rapid on-the-job improvement.
- **Leveraging Pretrained Knowledge Effectively:** RLT efficiently leverages the rich, pre-trained knowledge within VLAs by extracting a compact "RL token." This token serves as a meaningful, low-dimensional state representation for a lightweight actor-critic, avoiding the computational and sample inefficiencies of fine-tuning large, billion-parameter models directly.
- **Discovery of Superior Strategies:** The research demonstrates that RLT can lead to the emergence of novel and more effective manipulation strategies that surpass those present in human demonstrations or the initial VLA policy. The example of fluid, compliant insertion motions for the Ethernet task, even outperforming expert human teleoperation in speed, highlights its ability to find optimal behaviors through targeted online exploration. This suggests that VLAs can serve as excellent initializers, providing a "good enough" starting point from which RL can discover truly expert-level performance.
- **Reduced Engineering Effort:** By providing a structured way to fine-tune generalist VLA policies, RLT has the potential to reduce the extensive task-specific engineering often required to achieve high-performance manipulation. This moves towards more adaptable and versatile robotic systems.
- **Future Autonomy in Robotics:** The work lays a foundation for future fully autonomous RL improvement pipelines. While RLT currently requires some human intervention (reward signals, corrections, policy switching), the authors identify avenues for automating these components (e.g., using reward models), which could lead to robots that continuously learn and improve without constant human oversight.

In conclusion, RLT offers a practical and efficient paradigm for enhancing the performance of VLA models on challenging real-world tasks. By marrying the generalization power of large foundation models with the adaptive capabilities of online reinforcement learning, it paves the way for more dexterous, precise, and autonomously improving robotic systems.
