# Bridging the Sim-to-Real Gap from the Information Bottleneck Perspective

> [!summary] Summary
> Historical Information Bottleneck (HIB) is a single-stage framework that distills privileged simulation knowledge into real-world robot policies by learning a compact, informative historical representation. HIB enables robust zero-shot transfer to a physical Unitree A1 robot, demonstrating 100% success descending 0.6m stairs and outperforming baselines in complex locomotion tasks.

## Problem

- Reinforcement Learning (RL) policies trained in simulation often fail to transfer effectively to real-world robotic control due to the 'sim-to-real gap.'
- Simulated environments provide 'privileged knowledge' (e.g., ground truth dynamics) that is typically unavailable to real-world agents relying on local observations.
- Existing privileged knowledge distillation methods often lack comprehensive theoretical understanding, rely on computationally intensive two-stage training, or do not fully exploit available privileged information.

## Method

- A single-stage Historical Information Bottleneck (HIB) framework is proposed, leveraging the Information Bottleneck principle to learn a compressed, relevant representation of privileged knowledge from historical local states and actions.
- The method maximizes mutual information between the historical representation and the privileged state while minimizing information redundancy with the raw history.
- It uses a two-stream neural network architecture with a Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN) as the history encoder, optimized with a simplified contrastive learning objective (cosine similarity) and integrated with the standard RL objective.

## Results

- HIB consistently achieved superior episodic returns and stronger generalization on DeepMind Control Suite tasks with randomized dynamic parameters and morphology shifts, often matching the 'Teacher' policy's performance.
- The framework outperformed leading baselines (Student (RMA), DreamWaQ) on the Isaac Gym legged robot benchmark, showing higher success rates and average distances on challenging terrains.
- Policies trained with HIB demonstrated robust zero-shot transfer to a physical Unitree A1 quadruped robot, successfully descending 0.6m stairs with a **100%** success rate and maintaining balance when a leg was pulled with a **75%** success rate, without any real-world fine-tuning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The performance discrepancy between policies with privileged knowledge and those with only local states can be theoretically bounded by the error in modeling the privileged state from historical information.
> - Minimizing the privileged state modeling error is directly linked to maximizing mutual information between the historical representation and the privileged state, providing a theoretical foundation for the HIB approach.
> - A single-stage training approach, directly incorporating the learned historical representation into the RL policy, effectively distills privileged knowledge without requiring a separate teacher policy or explicit imitation learning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has demonstrated considerable success in simulated environments, particularly in domains such as games and financial trading. However, the application of RL to real-world robotic control remains a significant challenge. This discrepancy is commonly referred to as the "sim-to-real gap." A primary factor contributing to this gap is the differential access to information: simulated environments often provide "privileged knowledge" (e.g., ground truth dynamics, surrounding terrain, precise morphology parameters) that is generally inaccessible to robot agents operating in real-world scenarios, which typically rely on local states like proprioceptive feedback.

Previous research efforts to bridge the sim-to-real gap have explored several avenues. One category involves minimizing the environmental mismatch itself through methods like developing more realistic simulators, employing adversarial training, or utilizing domain randomization, where policies are trained under a wide range of simulated parameters to enhance robustness. However, these approaches often face limitations such as the difficulty and cost of creating high-fidelity simulators, or the potential for training overly conservative policies that sacrifice optimality for robustness.

A more recent direction focuses on leveraging privileged knowledge during the simulation training phase. Existing methods in this category include:
- **Two-stage policy distillation**: A common paradigm involves first training an "oracle" or "teacher" policy in the simulator with full access to privileged information. Subsequently, a "student" policy, constrained to local states, is trained to imitate the teacher's behavior. This approach can be computationally intensive and demands careful design for the imitation learning process, often leading to sample inefficiency.
- **Gradual information reduction**: Some methods progressively reduce the policy's reliance on privileged information during training, aiming for a smooth transition to local-state-only operation.
- **Privileged critic**: In this setup, the critic network, responsible for value estimation, has access to privileged information, while the actor, which dictates actions, only sees local states.

The authors of this paper identify that these existing privileged knowledge methods often lack a comprehensive theoretical understanding of the sim-to-real problem. Furthermore, they may not fully exploit the available privileged information during the training phase, potentially leading to sub-optimal performance in real-world deployments. This work positions itself within this landscape by proposing a representation-based, single-stage learning approach that seeks to more effectively and theoretically ground the distillation of privileged knowledge.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to bridge the sim-to-real gap in reinforcement learning by developing a novel and theoretically grounded method for distilling privileged knowledge from simulation into policies deployable in real-world robotic control, where only local observations are available.

The motivations for this work are detailed through a theoretical analysis of performance discrepancies:

- **Motivation from Policy Generalization (Theorem 1)**: The paper first examines traditional policy imitation algorithms. Theorem 1, "Policy imitation discrepancy," establishes a bound on the value discrepancy between an optimal policy trained with privileged knowledge (oracle policy, $\pi^*$) and a policy learned only with local states ($\hat{\pi}$). This bound is proportional to the total variation (TV) distance between $\pi^*(\cdot|s_l, s_p)$ and $\hat{\pi}(\cdot|s_l)$. While minimizing this TV distance is desirable, it is inherently challenging because the two policies operate on different state spaces (one has access to $s_p$, the other does not). Previous attempts to mitigate this by using historical trajectories ($h_t$) to inform $\hat{\pi}(\cdot|s_l, h_t)$ still face difficulties if historical information is not utilized appropriately, potentially leading to sub-optimal performance and sample inefficiency due to their two-stage nature. The authors argue that these methods struggle to fully leverage privileged knowledge for improved generalization.

- **Motivation from Privilege Modeling Discrepancy (Theorem 2)**: To address the limitations of direct policy imitation, the paper introduces an alternative theoretical motivation that shifts focus from imitating an oracle policy to effectively modeling the privileged state. Theorem 2, "Privilege modeling discrepancy," quantifies the performance discrepancy between the optimal value function $Q^*(s_l, s_p, a)$ and a learned value function $\hat{Q}_t(s_l, \hat{s}_p, a)$ where $\hat{s}_p$ is a *predicted* privileged state derived from historical information $h_t$ using a density model $\hat{P}(s_p|h_t)$. This theorem states that the value discrepancy is bounded by two main components:
 1. **$\Delta_E$**: Represents the inherent difficulty of learning without direct privileged information, reflecting the difference between the optimal Q-value and its expectation given the history. The authors suggest this term can be made small with a sufficiently long and informative history.
 2. **$\epsilon_{\hat{P}}$**: Measures the model discrepancy as the total variation distance between the true distribution of privileged states $P(s_p|h_{t+1})$ and the learned model $\hat{P}(s_p|h_{t+1})$.

 The key insight from Theorem 2 is that minimizing $\epsilon_{\hat{P}}$ (the error in modeling the privileged state from history) is crucial for reducing the overall performance discrepancy. This formulation allows for a single-stage distillation algorithm, removing the reliance on a separately trained oracle policy. The paper then demonstrates that minimizing $\epsilon_{\hat{P}}$ is closely related to maximizing the mutual information between the historical representation and the privileged state. This theoretical foundation directly motivates the proposed Historical Information Bottleneck (HIB) method, which aims to learn a maximally compressed representation of privileged knowledge from history while preserving sufficient information for decision-making.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, Historical Information Bottleneck (HIB), is a single-stage privileged knowledge distillation approach inspired by the Information Bottleneck (IB) principle. Its core objective is to learn a compact and informative representation of privileged knowledge from historical local states and actions, which is then used by the reinforcement learning policy alongside current local observations.

The methodology involves the following components and steps:

- **Information Bottleneck Formulation**: HIB frames the problem as learning a historical representation $Z_t = f_{\psi}(H_t)$ that maximizes mutual information with the privileged state $S_p_t$ (i.e., $I(Z_t; S_p_t)$) while minimizing mutual information with the raw history $H_t$ (i.e., $I(H_t; Z_t)$). This objective is formulated as $\min -I(Z_t; S_p_t) + \alpha I(H_t; Z_t)$, where $\alpha$ is a Lagrange multiplier controlling the trade-off between relevance and compression.

- **Approximation of Mutual Information Terms**:
 - **Compression Term ($I(H_t; Z_t)$)**: This term is minimized by minimizing a tractable upper bound, $D_{KL}[p(z_t|h_t) \| q(z_t)]$. A spherical Gaussian $N(0, I)$ is used as an approximation for the marginal distribution $q(z_t)$.
 - **Relevance Term ($I(Z_t; S_p_t)$)**: This term is maximized using a contrastive learning objective. The authors simplify the standard contrastive loss by omitting negative sampling and instead employ a cosine similarity loss between positive pairs. This simplification was empirically found to maintain performance and avoid complexities associated with negative sampling.

- **Two-Stream Architecture**: HIB employs a two-stream neural network architecture for learning the representations:
 - **Online Network**:
 - **History Encoder ($f_\psi$)**: A Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN) processes the fixed-length historical trajectory ($h_t = \{s_l_t, a_{t-1}, s_l_{t-1}, \ldots, a_{t-k}, s_l_{t-k}\}$) to produce the latent representation $z_t$.
 - **Online Projector ($g_\theta$)**: Transforms $z_t$ into $y_t = g_\theta(z_t)$.
 - **Target Network**:
 - **Privilege Encoder ($f_\omega$)**: This encoder processes the privileged state $s_p_t$ to produce a representation $\tilde{e}_t$. In scenarios where $s_p_t$ is high-dimensional (e.g., in the Legged Robot benchmark), $f_\omega$ is implemented as an AutoEncoder, trained with a reconstruction loss $L_{rec} = \|s_p_t - D_{\omega'}(f_\omega(s_p_t))\|^2$, to project $s_p_t$ into a lower-dimensional space matching $z_t$. For compact privileged states (e.g., in the DMC benchmark), $f_\omega$ can be an identity operator.
 - **Target Projector ($g_{\theta-}$)**: Transforms $\tilde{e}_t$ into $\tilde{y}_t = g_{\theta-}(\tilde{e}_t)$.

- **HIB Loss and Optimization**:
 - The primary HIB loss ($L_{sim}$) is defined as the negative cosine similarity between $y_t$ and $\tilde{y}_t$: $L_{sim} = - \frac{y_t}{\|y_t\|_2}^\top \frac{\text{sg}[\tilde{y}_t]}{\|\text{sg}[\tilde{y}_t]\|_2}$, where $\text{sg}[\cdot]$ denotes the stop-gradient operation applied to the target $\tilde{y}_t$. This prevents collapse and promotes stable representation learning.
 - A **momentum update** mechanism is used for the target network parameters $\theta^-$: $\theta^- \leftarrow \tau \theta^- + (1-\tau) \theta$, where $\tau$ is a momentum factor.
 - The overall training process for the HIB parameters (using an Adam optimizer) involves a composite loss:
 - The online projector $\theta$ is updated based on $L_{sim}$.
 - The history encoder $\psi$ is updated with a combination of $L_{sim}$ (weighted by $\lambda_1$), $L_{KL}$ (weighted by $\lambda_2$), and the standard RL objective $L_{RL}(s_l_t, z_t)$. The RL objective directly leverages the learned historical representation $z_t$ concatenated with the local state $s_l_t$ as input to the policy $\pi(a|s_l_t, z_t)$, facilitating single-stage training.
 - The privilege encoder $\omega$ (and its decoder $D_{\omega'}$) is updated with $L_{rec}$ if an AutoEncoder is used.
 - For practical implementation, a separate IB buffer $B_{ib}$ is maintained to store normal trajectories for HIB loss computation, and zero-padding is used for initial historical trajectories.

- **Evaluation**: During evaluation, in real-world environments, the agent relies solely on local states and the learned history encoder $f_\psi$. The history encoder infers the privileged knowledge representation $z_t$ from the historical trajectory $h_t$, and this $z_t$ is then fed into the policy $\hat{\pi}(a|s_l_t, z_t)$ to select actions. The true privileged state $s_p_t$ is not directly accessed.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical evaluation of HIB was conducted across both simulated and real-world robotic tasks, comparing its performance against several baselines.

- **Simulated DeepMind Control Suite (DMC) Benchmark**:
 - **Dynamic Parameter Shifts (Table 1)**: Experiments on tasks like Pendulum, Finger Spin, and Quadruped Walk, with randomized dynamic parameters (e.g., gravity, friction, actuator strength), demonstrated HIB's superior performance. HIB achieved the highest episodic returns across almost all testing difficulties, including "ordinary," "o.o.d." (out-of-distribution), and "far o.o.d." environments. Its performance closely matched that of the "Teacher" policy, which had full access to privileged knowledge. This indicated effective distillation of privileged knowledge and strong generalization capabilities.
 - **Morphology Shifts (Table 2)**: HIB also showed robust generalization to environments with morphology changes (e.g., varying leg lengths in Quadruped Walk). HIB consistently outperformed baselines, maintaining high returns even in "far o.o.d." morphology conditions. While the Student (RMA) baseline occasionally exhibited slightly higher mean returns, HIB consistently displayed lower variance, suggesting greater stability and reliability.

- **Simulated Legged Robot Benchmark (Isaac Gym - Table 3)**:
 - On a quadrupedal robot locomotion task trained in the Isaac Gym simulator, HIB outperformed leading baselines like Student (RMA) and DreamWaQ. It achieved higher success rates and average distances on challenging terrains such as stairs, discrete obstacles, and slopes. This indicated HIB's effectiveness and scalability for more complex robotic control problems.

- **Visualization of Latent Representations (Figure 3)**:
 - A t-SNE visualization of the learned latent representations in the Finger Spin task provided insights into HIB's mechanism. The distribution of HIB's learned representation closely mirrored the distribution of the true privileged information. In contrast, the Student agent's representation only captured specific subsets of the privileged knowledge distribution. This visualization supported the conclusion that HIB learns a more comprehensive and faithful representation of privileged knowledge.

- **Ablation Study (Figure 4)**:
 - An ablation study on the Quadruped Walk task investigated the contribution of HIB's components:
 - **`HIB-w/o-ib`** (without the Information Bottleneck loss components, i.e., only RL loss updating the history encoder) performed poorly, demonstrating the critical role of the HIB-specific loss in learning generalizable policies.
 - **`HIB-w/o-rl`** (without the RL objective updating the history encoder) achieved relatively high scores, underscoring the significant impact of the HIB loss in driving robust representation learning.
 - **`HIB-w/o-proj`** (without the projector networks) showed reduced performance, indicating that the two-stream architecture with projector layers and momentum updates is important for learning effective representations.
 - **`HIB-contra`** (using a traditional contrastive loss with negative sampling) performed well initially but subsequently failed, suggesting that the simplified cosine similarity loss with only positive pairs, adopted by HIB, provided a more robust solution in this context.

- **Real-world Application (Unitree A1 Robot - Figures 2, 5)**:
 - Policies trained with HIB in the Isaac Gym simulator were successfully deployed on a physical Unitree A1 quadruped robot without any fine-tuning (zero-shot transfer).
 - The HIB-trained agent exhibited stable control behavior and successfully traversed various challenging real-world terrains, including plains, grass fields, slopes, and stairs.
 - In specific challenging tests, HIB enabled the robot to descend high stairs (0.6m height) with a 100% success rate over 10 trials, a task where the strongest baseline (DreamWaQ) reportedly failed completely. HIB also achieved a 75% success rate in maintaining balance when a leg was unexpectedly pulled during dashing, outperforming DreamWaQ's 25% success rate. These results provided empirical validation for HIB's ability to bridge the sim-to-real gap without requiring real-world tuning.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of robotic reinforcement learning by addressing the critical sim-to-real gap with a principled and empirically effective method. The significance and potential impact of HIB are multifaceted:

- **Theoretical Grounding for Sim-to-Real**: By formulating the privileged knowledge distillation problem through the lens of Information Bottleneck theory, the work provides a stronger theoretical foundation compared to many prior heuristic-based approaches. The theoretical analyses (Theorems 1 and 2) motivate the learning objective and explain why effectively modeling privileged information from history is crucial for policy generalization.

- **Enhanced Policy Generalizability and Robustness**: The core strength of HIB lies in its ability to learn robust historical representations that enable RL agents to adapt to varied and unseen environmental dynamics and robot morphologies. The consistent outperformance in out-of-distribution simulated environments and the successful zero-shot transfer to a real robot demonstrate that HIB can produce policies that are more generally applicable and robust in dynamic real-world settings.

- **Efficient Single-Stage Learning**: Unlike conventional two-stage policy distillation methods that require training a separate teacher policy, HIB integrates privileged knowledge distillation directly into the reinforcement learning pipeline. This single-stage approach has the potential to be more sample-efficient and less computationally burdensome, simplifying the overall training process.

- **Reduced Reliance on High-Fidelity Simulators**: While simulators are still necessary for initial training, HIB's ability to infer and adapt to underlying environmental properties from historical local observations suggests a pathway to reduce the absolute requirement for perfectly matched simulators. This can be particularly beneficial when creating high-fidelity simulations for complex real-world phenomena is impractical or impossible.

- **Broad Applicability Across RL Algorithms**: The methodology demonstrates compatibility with various RL algorithms, as shown by its combination with SAC, suggesting its potential for broad adoption across different RL frameworks and tasks. The successful deployment on a quadrupedal robot highlights its practical relevance for complex locomotion and manipulation tasks.

- **Practical Impact on Robotics**: The real-world experiments on the Unitree A1 robot represent a significant step towards practical, autonomous robotic systems. The ability to perform challenging maneuvers, such as descending high stairs, without any fine-tuning in the real environment, indicates that HIB can enhance the deployability of learned policies in diverse and demanding real-world scenarios.

- **Future Research Directions**: The identified limitation regarding the recovery of multi-modal and high-dimensional privileged knowledge (e.g., RGB images) suggests a clear avenue for future research. Extending HIB to effectively handle such complex information could further broaden its applicability and impact.
