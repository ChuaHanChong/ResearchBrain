# Attend Before Attention: Efficient and Scalable Video Understanding via Autoregressive Gazing

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from UC Berkeley, NVIDIA, MIT, and Clarifai developed AutoGaze, a lightweight module that enables Multi-modal Large Language Models to efficiently process long-form, high-resolution videos by adaptively selecting multi-scale informative visual patches before attention. This approach achieved up to 19x Vision Transformer and 10x MLLM latency reduction, scaling MLLMs to 1K frames at 4K resolution and improving performance by 10.1% on the new HLVid benchmark compared to baselines.

## Problem

- Multi-modal Large Language Models (MLLMs) face significant computational and memory challenges when processing long-form, high-resolution videos.
- Existing MLLM architectures process every pixel uniformly across frames, leading to high computational costs due to spatiotemporal redundancy in video data.
- Current token reduction techniques primarily prune tokens *after* the Vision Transformer (ViT), leaving the ViT as a bottleneck unable to scale efficiently for high-resolution, long-duration content.

## Method

- Introduces **AutoGaze**, a compact (3M parameters) module that employs ==autoregressive gazing== to identify and select a minimal set of multi-scale visual patches *before* ViT processing.
- AutoGaze utilizes an autoregressive transformer decoder with a multi-scale patch vocabulary and an automatic gazing length decision head to select patches that reconstruct the original video within a user-defined error.
- The model is trained with a two-stage pipeline: Next-Token Prediction (NTP) pre-training on ground-truth gazing sequences, followed by Reinforcement Learning (RL) post-training to refine gazing quality.

## Results

- Achieved significant visual token reduction (**4
x** to **100
x**) and substantial latency reductions (up to **19
x** for ViT, **10
x** for MLLM), enabling processing of **1K** frames at **4K** resolution without Out-Of-Memory errors.
- Enabled an MLLM (NVILA-**8B**-Video) to achieve a **10.1%** performance gain on the new HLVid benchmark (from **42.5%** to **52.6%**), outperforming previous state-of-the-art MLLMs like GPT-4o (**49.3%**).
- Outperformed heuristic gazing methods and existing MLLM token reduction techniques by achieving higher reconstruction quality with fewer patches and providing more comprehensive efficiency improvements.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - AutoGaze effectively mimics human visual perception by prioritizing dynamic (high optical flow) and detailed regions, adaptively using coarser scales for broad areas and finer scales for intricate content.
> - The system demonstrates robust generalization to out-of-distribution video content, maintaining consistent and effective gazing patterns across varied styles and semantics.
> - Pre-ViT token reduction is critical for comprehensive efficiency gains, as it alleviates the primary computational bottleneck in video MLLMs beyond what post-ViT pruning can achieve.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Multi-modal large language models (MLLMs) have significantly advanced general-purpose video understanding capabilities. However, their application to long-form, high-resolution videos faces substantial computational challenges. Existing MLLMs typically process every pixel uniformly across frames within their Vision Transformers (ViTs) or Language Models (LLMs), leading to high computational costs due to the inherent spatiotemporal redundancy in video data. This approach limits their scalability, preventing effective processing of videos crucial for many real-world applications, such as surveillance, autonomous driving, and detailed event analysis.

The current research landscape for addressing video redundancy in MLLMs primarily focuses on token reduction or compression techniques. Many approaches prune tokens within the LLM component or between the ViT and LLM, but they often leave the ViT to process all pixels, thereby retaining a significant computational bottleneck. Examples include methods that rely on attention scores for token selection or involve complex search and reasoning mechanisms, which can introduce overhead and further impede scalability. While classical video understanding models, including 3D-ConvNets, early transformers, and various pre-training algorithms (e.g., masked auto-encoding, predictive coding), have made progress, they too struggle with the spatiotemporal resolution demands of modern video data. Recent efforts to develop long-video benchmarks and models emphasize extended temporal understanding but typically remain constrained to low resolutions. This demonstrates a clear gap in the field: a need for methods and evaluation benchmarks that efficiently support both extensive temporal context and high-resolution detail under realistic computational constraints.

This work, "Attend Before Attention," proposes a method to address this specific gap by drawing inspiration from human visual processing. Humans selectively attend to informative regions, such as moving objects or fine details, while largely disregarding static backgrounds, enabling efficient real-time scene understanding. By introducing a mechanism for pre-ViT token reduction, this research aims to overcome the limitations of existing MLLMs and contribute a more efficient paradigm for processing complex video data.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an efficient and scalable framework, named AutoGaze, that enables Multi-modal Large Language Models (MLLMs) to effectively process and understand long-form, high-resolution videos. This objective is motivated by several critical limitations observed in the current state of video understanding models.

Firstly, contemporary MLLMs, despite their advancements in general-purpose video understanding, encounter significant computational and memory hurdles when applied to videos characterized by both extended duration and high spatial resolution. These models often process every pixel in every frame uniformly, leading to redundant computations given the substantial spatiotemporal redundancy typically present in video content. For instance, static backgrounds or slowly changing regions contribute heavily to computational load without necessarily providing proportional new information.

Secondly, existing attempts to mitigate this redundancy primarily focus on pruning tokens within the LLM component or between the Vision Transformer (ViT) and the LLM. This design choice leaves the ViT, which is responsible for initial visual encoding, to process all pixels from the original video. Consequently, the ViT becomes a computational bottleneck, preventing MLLMs from scaling efficiently to scenarios involving longer video durations or higher resolutions. This inefficiency means that MLLMs are currently unable to exploit the rich visual details available in high-resolution video streams, which are often crucial for real-world applications such as fine-grained object recognition, detailed event analysis, or understanding subtle temporal changes.

The motivation for AutoGaze is rooted in an analogy to human visual perception. Humans do not process every visual detail equally; instead, their eyes selectively "gaze" at salient or informative regions, adapting their attention to moving objects, fine details, and novel content, while largely ignoring static or redundant areas. This selective attention mechanism allows for efficient scene understanding. The researchers sought to imbue MLLMs with a similar capability to proactively identify and prioritize informative visual patches.

Therefore, the specific objectives for AutoGaze were to:
- Develop a lightweight module capable of identifying and removing redundant visual patches *before* they are processed by a ViT or MLLM.
- Design this module to autoregressively select a minimal set of multi-scale patches that can accurately reconstruct the original video content within a user-defined error tolerance, thereby preserving critical information.
- Achieve substantial acceleration for both the ViT encoding phase and the subsequent MLLM processing, specifically aiming for speedups that enable processing of videos with frame counts up to 1K and resolutions up to 4K.
- Ensure that this efficiency gain does not compromise, and ideally enhances, the downstream MLLM's performance on various video understanding benchmarks.

Additionally, recognizing a gap in existing evaluation methods, a secondary objective was to introduce a new benchmark, HLVid. This benchmark specifically targets long-form, high-resolution video Question-Answering (QA) tasks to rigorously test the capabilities of models like AutoGaze in scenarios where both extended temporal context and fine spatial detail are essential for accurate responses.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of this research is AutoGaze, a lightweight module designed to efficiently select informative visual patches from videos for downstream MLLM processing. AutoGaze operates on the principle of autoregressive gazing, aiming to identify a minimal set of multi-scale patches that can reconstruct a video within a specified error threshold.

**AutoGaze Model Design:**
AutoGaze is a compact model with 3 million parameters, featuring a convolutional vision encoder and an autoregressive transformer decoder.
- **Autoregressive Gazing**: AutoGaze processes video frames sequentially. It begins by encoding the first frame using the convolutional encoder, and the features are then passed to the transformer decoder. The decoder autoregressively decodes patch indices, akin to how LLMs predict next tokens, but with a vocabulary consisting of patch indices (1 to V, where V is the total number of possible patches). For subsequent frames, AutoGaze encodes the current frame and decodes its patch indices by considering the features of all preceding frames and their corresponding gazed patches. This historical context allows the model to avoid selecting redundant patches that contain information already captured.
- **Automatic Gazing Length Decision**: A dedicated head on the decoder predicts the reconstruction loss of the current frame based on the patches gazed up to that point. The gazing process for a given frame is terminated once the predicted loss falls below a user-defined threshold, optimizing for efficiency while maintaining reconstruction quality.
- **Multi-scale Gazing**: To adapt to varying levels of detail within a scene, AutoGaze supports multi-scale patch selection. The decoder's vocabulary includes patches extracted at different scales (e.g., 32x32, 64x64, 112x112, and 224x224 pixels, assuming a 16x16 patch size, resulting in a total of 265 possible patch indices). This allows AutoGaze to select coarser scales for broad, low-detail areas and finer scales for intricate regions.
- **Multi-token Prediction**: To accelerate the gazing process, AutoGaze employs multiple decoding heads to predict several patch indices and their associated reconstruction losses concurrently.

**Reconstruction Objective and Model Instantiation:**
AutoGaze's learning objective is to select patch indices that minimize reconstruction loss for any given gazing length, and to predict the smallest gazing length that satisfies a predefined reconstruction loss threshold.
- The reconstruction model, `Recon(·)`, is a modified VideoMAE (Masked Autoencoder) pre-trained on images and fine-tuned on videos. It uses block-causal attention, meaning it reconstructs each frame based only on the current and previous frames, aligning with the causal nature of the gazing process.
- The distance function, `L(·, ·)`, is a weighted sum of pixel-wise $\ell_1$ loss (weight 1) and perceptual losses ( $\ell_2$ loss on DINOv2 and SigLIP2 embeddings, both with weight 0.3) between the original and reconstructed videos.

**Two-Stage Training Pipeline:**
AutoGaze is trained in two stages, inspired by modern LLM training methodologies:
- **Stage 1: Next-Token Prediction (NTP) Pre-training**:
 - AutoGaze is pre-trained on a dataset of 250,000 raw videos paired with "ground-truth" gazing sequences. These sequences are generated via a greedy search algorithm that approximates the minimal reconstruction loss for randomly sampled gazing lengths.
 - Training involves a cross-entropy loss for next-token prediction and an $\ell_2$ loss for supervising reconstruction loss prediction at each decoding step.
 - The gazing ratios are sampled from an exponential distribution (0.02 to 0.2) for the average video, then distributed among frames using a Dirichlet distribution biased towards the first frame to reflect initial information gain.
- **Stage 2: Reinforcement Learning (RL) Post-training**:
 - To refine gazing quality beyond the sub-optimal pre-training data, AutoGaze is further fine-tuned using a simplified, on-policy GRPO (Generalized Policy Optimization) algorithm.
 - The reward signal is the negative reconstruction loss of current and future frames, discounted over time.
 - Reconstruction loss prediction is also supervised at the final patch of each frame using the actual reconstruction loss. This stage helps AutoGaze discover more optimal gazing sequences.

**Training Data Curation:**
The training dataset comprises approximately 800,000 videos from diverse sources including Ego4D, 100DoH, and InternVid (egocentric and exocentric natural videos). Additionally, artificial videos are generated from high-resolution image datasets (SA-1B, IDL) by simulating camera movements, capturing natural and text-rich content.

**Downstream Usage of AutoGaze:**
- **Any Resolution and Duration Inference**: AutoGaze can process videos of arbitrary resolution and duration by splitting them into 16-frame, 224x224 spatiotemporal tiles. AutoGaze processes each tile, and the resulting gazed patch positions are merged.
- **Integration with ViTs and MLLMs**: Standard MLLMs typically use image ViTs to encode full frames. To integrate AutoGaze:
 - ViTs are adapted to accept multi-scale patch inputs by interpolating frames and positional embeddings to different scales, performing patch embedding separately for each scale, and then feeding these embedded tokens to the ViT.
 - Image ViTs are repurposed into video ViTs by processing tokens from all 16 frames within the same sequence. The tokens encoded by the ViT are then passed to the MLLM.

**HLVid Benchmark:**
To evaluate the method's ability to handle high-resolution, long-form videos, the authors introduce HLVid. This benchmark consists of 268 multiple-choice Question-Answering (QA) pairs based on videos up to 5 minutes long with 4K resolution. The questions are specifically designed to necessitate high-resolution perception (1K-2K resolution) and long-context understanding.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments focused on evaluating AutoGaze's behavior, efficiency, and its impact on MLLM performance, including generalization to out-of-distribution (OOD) content.

**AutoGaze Behavior:**
- **Motion Prioritization**: AutoGaze demonstrated a tendency to gaze more frequently at patches exhibiting higher optical flow. This behavior was observed across all scales, indicating that the model prioritizes dynamic regions, which often contain new information. The use of coarser scales for regions with higher optical flow was also noted.
- **Scale-Detail Correlation**: The model effectively utilized its multi-scale gazing capability, selecting finer scales for patches with more detail (quantified by higher Laplacian variance) and coarser scales for regions with less detail. This adaptive selection helps preserve crucial information while reducing overall patch count.
- **Generalizability**: AutoGaze exhibited robust generalization to OOD videos. It maintained consistent gazing patterns and effectively tracked changing regions in videos with unfamiliar semantics (e.g., CCTV footage, robot grasping demonstrations, object-swapping videos) and varied visual styles (e.g., style-transferred videos), regardless of texture or global illumination changes.

**Efficiency of ViT and MLLM with AutoGaze:**
- **Patch Reduction**: AutoGaze achieved significant reduction in visual tokens, ranging from 4× to 100×, depending on the video's frames per second (FPS) and resolution. For instance, only approximately 1% of patches were required for 30-FPS, 4K-resolution videos to meet a reconstruction loss threshold of 0.7. This threshold was determined to cause less than 0.5% performance degradation in downstream MLLMs.
- **Latency Reduction**: When processing one second of video, AutoGaze reduced Vision Transformer (ViT) latency by up to 19× and MLLM latency by up to 10×. This efficiency enabled processing of 4K-resolution videos, which would otherwise lead to Out-Of-Memory (OOM) errors for baseline models.
- **Streaming Efficiency**: In streaming scenarios, AutoGaze-enabled ViTs and MLLMs achieved up to approximately 16× higher frames per second (FPS) compared to baselines, demonstrating potential for real-time video understanding applications.

**Scaling MLLMs with AutoGaze and Performance Improvements:**
- **Extended Scalability**: AutoGaze allowed an MLLM (NVILA-8B-Video) to scale to 1K frames and 4K resolution, a capability beyond the memory limits of baseline MLLMs.
- **Benchmark Performance**:
 - On the VideoMME benchmark, NVILA-8B-Video with AutoGaze achieved 67.0%, an improvement of 2.8% over the baseline NVILA (64.2%).
 - On the newly introduced HLVid benchmark, specifically designed for high-resolution, long-form video QA, the performance of NVILA-8B-Video with AutoGaze improved significantly from 42.5% to 52.6%. This represents a 10.1% gain and surpassed previous state-of-the-art MLLMs (e.g., Qwen2.5-VL-7B at 48.1% and GPT-4o at 49.3%) by 4.5%. This outcome validated the necessity of high-resolution processing for HLVid.
 - Consistent improvements were also observed on other benchmarks such as LongVideoBench, MVBench, NExT-QA, EgoSchema, and MLVU.

**Comparison to Token Pruning Baselines:**
- **Gazing Methods**: AutoGaze outperformed heuristic-based gazing approaches (e.g., Random Gaze, RGB-Diff Gaze, Optical-Flow Gaze) by achieving the same reconstruction quality with a significantly smaller gazing ratio (e.g., 5% patches for a reconstruction loss of 1.0, compared to 15% for Random Gaze).
- **MLLM Token Reduction Methods**: Unlike existing methods that primarily reduce tokens *after* the ViT, AutoGaze also achieved a 4× reduction in ViT latency, in addition to improving LLM latency. This demonstrated a more comprehensive efficiency gain while maintaining comparable performance to no-reduction baselines.

**Ablation Studies:**
- **Training Pipeline**: Both Next-Token Prediction (NTP) pre-training and Reinforcement Learning (RL) post-training were found to contribute to AutoGaze's efficiency. Combining both stages yielded the lowest gazing ratio for a given reconstruction loss, with NTP being the larger contributor.
- **Model Designs**: Multi-scale gazing reduced the gazing ratio and improved efficiency by 2.3×. Using multi-token prediction (decoding multiple tokens simultaneously) offered a trade-off: decoding 10 tokens at once provided a balance between lower latency and acceptable gazing ratio.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces AutoGaze, a framework designed to enhance the efficiency and scalability of Multi-modal Large Language Models (MLLMs) for video understanding. Its significance lies in addressing a fundamental bottleneck in current MLLMs: their inability to efficiently process long-form, high-resolution videos due to computational costs arising from spatiotemporal redundancy.

The primary impact of AutoGaze is its capacity to significantly extend the operational limits of MLLMs. By achieving substantial reductions in visual tokens (4× to 100×) and accelerating both Vision Transformers (ViTs) and MLLMs (up to 19× and 10× respectively), AutoGaze enables these models to process videos with unprecedented scales—up to 1K frames at 4K resolution. This breakthrough is crucial for practical applications where detailed visual information over extended periods is essential, such as in comprehensive surveillance systems, advanced autonomous driving platforms, detailed sports analytics, or complex medical imaging analysis.

The inspiration drawn from human visual attention, which selectively focuses on salient information, provides a biologically plausible and intuitive approach to resource allocation in AI perception systems. This not only makes the models more efficient but also potentially aligns their information processing strategies closer to human cognitive mechanisms.

Furthermore, the introduction of HLVid, a novel benchmark specifically designed for high-resolution, long-form video Question-Answering, represents a significant contribution to the research community. HLVid addresses a critical gap in existing evaluation metrics, which often prioritize either long duration or high resolution but rarely both. By providing a challenging testbed, HLVid will drive future research and development in this area, fostering the creation of MLLMs that can genuinely understand complex real-world video scenarios requiring fine-grained detail and extensive temporal context. The reported performance gains on HLVid, where an AutoGaze-enabled MLLM outperformed prior state-of-the-art and proprietary models by a notable margin, underscores the practical utility and effectiveness of the proposed method.

The lightweight nature of AutoGaze (3 million parameters) and its demonstrated generalizability to out-of-distribution video styles and semantics suggest broad applicability across diverse domains without requiring extensive re-training. This adaptability, coupled with the potential for real-time processing in streaming environments, opens avenues for deployment in dynamic, time-sensitive applications. In summary, AutoGaze represents a step towards more efficient, scalable, and capable MLLMs for video understanding, with direct implications for advancing artificial intelligence in complex visual environments.
