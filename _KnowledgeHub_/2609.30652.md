# Recursive Self-Improvement via On-Policy Distillation for Reasoning

> [!summary] Summary
> Dynamic Co-Evolution (DCE) makes on-policy self-distillation recursive. After each update, the new checkpoint becomes both the next student and a detached, gold-conditioned privileged teacher, so revision behavior learned in one round improves the next round's supervision. Self-Refined Concise Learning (SRCL) curbs verbosity by training on shorter, answer-verified self-rewrites. On Qwen3-8B, DCE+SRCL reaches 65.97% Average@12 across four competition math benchmarks, 35.62 points above frozen-teacher OPSD.

## Problem

- Outcome-reward RLVR methods such as GRPO give only a coarse, sequence-level signal: they show whether the final answer is correct but not where reasoning failed or how to revise it.
- Standard on-policy distillation needs a capable external teacher whose guidance is not conditioned on a verified solution, so the student can inherit that teacher's errors along with its strengths.
- OPSD freezes its gold-conditioned teacher at the initial checkpoint. That teacher puts 94.5% probability on EOS at wrong endpoints and cannot supply the revision behaviors the student develops.

## Method

- **Dynamic Co-Evolution** (**DCE**): after each update, the new checkpoint initializes both the student and a ==stop-gradient== privileged teacher, trained with ==token-level forward KL== along the student's on-policy rollouts.
- Assistant-side solution prefill: the teacher's context places the verified solution in the assistant turn, followed by a transition instruction and the student's prefix, rather than presenting it as user-side reference.
- **Self-Refined Concise Learning** (**SRCL**): the current model rewrites its own rollout without seeing the solution, and ==cross-entropy== trains only on rewrites that are shorter, naturally terminated, structurally valid and answer-correct.

## Results

- Qwen3-8B DCE+SRCL reaches **65.97%** Average@12, versus **30.35%** for OPSD and **20.28%** for GRPO. Qwen3-4B reaches **61.88%** versus **22.85%**, beating each model's thinking-mode base.
- Freezing the teacher drops DCE from **65.76% to 47.99%** at 8B and from **60.00% to 25.28%** at 4B. Forward KL beats reverse KL by **5.55 points**, and JSD collapses to **17.99%**.
- Forcing OPSD to 16K tokens gives only **30.76%**, so longer output alone does not explain the gains. SRCL cuts 8B output length **7.80%** versus DCE, and Gemma-4-12B reaches **63.61%** versus **51.04%** for OPSD.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A frozen supervisor falls out of step as the student learns new behaviors. Refreshing the teacher from the current checkpoint keeps dense token-level guidance matched to the evolving policy.
> - Where privileged information sits in the prompt matters: framing the solution as the model's own prior reasoning gives much stronger distillation signals than presenting it as external reference material.
> - Concision objectives help only on top of a capability-building signal. SRCL alone collapses to 2.36% accuracy, while paired with DCE it shortens outputs without losing accuracy.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position in the Broader Research Landscape

This work addresses the problem of improving reasoning in large language models through post-training. Recent reasoning research has primarily used reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR), in which a model receives a reward based on whether its final answer passes an automatic checker. RLVR has produced behaviors such as longer derivations, self-verification, backtracking, and apparent reflection. Its principal limitation is that the reward is usually sequence-level: it indicates whether a completed response is correct, but does not identify which intermediate step was erroneous or what continuation would repair the error.

Process supervision provides more local feedback, but it generally requires step-level labels or a separately trained process verifier. On-policy distillation (OPD) represents an intermediate approach. In OPD, the model generates its own response, while a stronger external teacher supplies next-token distributions on the generated prefixes. This produces dense token-level supervision, including on mistakes made by the student, but requires an external teacher and may transfer the teacher’s own errors.

On-policy self-distillation (OPSD) removes the external teacher. A student model receives only the problem, while a second copy of the same model receives the problem together with a verified solution. This gold-conditioned copy acts as a privileged teacher. The teacher can use the solution to produce token-level guidance, while the student learns to approximate that guidance without seeing the solution at inference time. Earlier OPSD work kept the teacher frozen to improve stability.

The present paper focuses on the limitation created by a frozen teacher. As the student learns new behaviors, especially reflection and recovery from incorrect reasoning, a teacher fixed at the initial checkpoint cannot incorporate those improvements. The authors therefore propose **Dynamic Co-Evolution (DCE)**, which updates the student and then uses the updated checkpoint to initialize the teacher for the next training round. They also introduce **Self-Refined Concise Learning (SRCL)** to limit the increase in reasoning length that may result from stronger revision behavior.

The paper therefore sits at the intersection of on-policy distillation, privileged-information training, self-training, recursive self-improvement, reasoning post-training, and efficient inference. It differs from test-time scaling approaches because its main intervention occurs during training rather than by simply forcing the model to generate more tokens at inference.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective is to improve a reasoning model’s ability to recognize and recover from incorrect intermediate reasoning. The authors argue that a model’s ability to reflect is not sufficient by itself. A model may generate a phrase such as “Wait” or “Actually,” yet fail to revise the underlying error. Effective training should therefore provide guidance at the specific prefixes where the model must decide whether to terminate, continue checking, backtrack, or pursue a correction.

The motivation for DCE comes from an empirical probe of incorrect Qwen3-8B trajectories from AIME 2024, AIME 2025, and AIME 2026. At incorrect response endpoints, the frozen gold-conditioned teacher assigned approximately 94.5% probability to the end-of-sequence token, while assigning only about 22.0% probability to observed reflection cues. This indicated that the initial teacher favored stopping after an incorrect solution rather than continuing toward a correction. Meanwhile, the evolving student became more likely to reflect during training, creating a growing mismatch between student behavior and teacher supervision.

DCE is intended to address this mismatch recursively. If the student learns stronger revision behavior in one round, the next teacher should also possess that behavior and provide more useful guidance on subsequent student trajectories. The second objective concerns efficiency. More checking and backtracking can improve accuracy but may also produce unnecessarily long responses, repeated calculations, or extended self-criticism. SRCL is designed to preserve correct reasoning while removing redundant detours.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Dynamic Co-Evolution

At training round \(k\), the current model checkpoint, with parameters \(\theta_k\), generates an on-policy response to a problem \(x\). A retained prefix of this response is then scored by two branches that share the same current checkpoint.

The student receives the problem and the generated prefix. The privileged teacher receives the problem, a verified solution \(g\), a transition instruction, and the same generated prefix. The verified solution is placed in an assistant-side context, so the teacher treats it as part of preceding reasoning rather than as ordinary user-provided reference text. The paper’s main implementation uses the first 512 reference tokens.

At each token position, the teacher produces a next-token distribution \(q\), and the student produces a distribution \(p\). Gradients are stopped through the teacher distribution. The main loss is the forward Kullback–Leibler divergence, \(D_{KL}(q\|p)\), averaged over retained response tokens. This loss emphasizes tokens that receive probability under the teacher, including alternatives that the student may currently assign low probability.

After optimization, the resulting checkpoint \(\theta_{k+1}\) initializes both the next student and the next detached privileged teacher. Thus, the teacher is refreshed every training step or round rather than remaining at the initial checkpoint. The authors compare this dynamic schedule with frozen teachers, exponential moving average teachers, and periodic hard-copy updates.

#### 3.2. Self-Refined Concise Learning

For SRCL, the current model generates an on-policy response and then receives a separate prompt asking it to rewrite that response into the shortest direct, self-contained, correct solution. The model does not see the verified solution during rewriting.

A rewrite is accepted only if it is shorter than the original response, terminates naturally, remains structurally valid and self-contained, contains no explicit reflection or correction language, and produces the correct answer according to an automatic verifier. Accepted rewrites become ordinary supervised targets trained with autoregressive cross-entropy. The verified solution is used only for filtering; it is not included in the rewrite prompt.

The joint objective is a weighted sum of the DCE guidance loss and the SRCL supervised loss. If no rewrite passes the acceptance filters in a minibatch, the update uses DCE alone.

#### 3.3. Experimental Design

The main experiments use Qwen3 models at 1.7B, 4B, 8B, and 14B parameters. A cross-family experiment uses Gemma-4-12B-IT. Training uses 14,717 mathematical-reasoning problems from the OpenThoughts data used by prior OPSD work. The models operate in non-thinking mode. Optimization uses AdamW, bfloat16, LoRA with rank 128, and a learning rate of \(5\times10^{-6}\) for the principal Qwen3 experiments.

Evaluation uses 30 problems each from AIME 2024, AIME 2025, AIME 2026, and HMMT February 2025. Twelve responses are sampled per problem, and results are reported as Average@12 accuracy, based on final-answer verification, together with mean generated tokens. The standard evaluation cap is 32,000 tokens. Baselines include the base model, supervised fine-tuning, GRPO, and OPSD. An OPSD test-time-scaling control forces early-terminating responses to continue to 8K or 16K tokens.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Accuracy Across Model Scales

DCE substantially outperforms OPSD and conventional post-training baselines on the main mathematics benchmarks. For Qwen3-8B, OPSD obtains 30.35% Average@12 accuracy, while DCE reaches 65.76% and DCE+SRCL reaches 65.97%. For Qwen3-4B, the corresponding values are 22.85%, 60.00%, and 61.88%. For Qwen3-1.7B, OPSD reaches 10.35%, DCE reaches 23.33%, and DCE+SRCL reaches 26.88%.

The largest gains come from DCE. SRCL generally preserves or improves accuracy while reducing output length at medium and larger model scales. At 8B, SRCL reduces mean output from 19,046 to 17,561 tokens while producing nearly identical accuracy. At 4B, it increases accuracy from 60.00% to 61.88% and reduces length by approximately 10.8%.

The method also transfers to Gemma-4-12B-IT. DCE achieves 62.01% Average@12, and DCE+SRCL achieves 63.61%, compared with 51.04% for OPSD. SRCL reduces mean output length from 8,812 to 8,526 tokens in this experiment.

#### 4.2. Evidence for Improved Revision Behavior

Fixed-trace probes show that recursive training changes both the student and teacher. Across three AIME cohorts, the student’s probability of producing EOS at incorrect response endpoints falls from 92.6% at initialization to 26.3% at step 200. The teacher’s corresponding probability falls from 90.4% to 41.3%.

At observed reflection points, the student’s probability of producing the actual reflection token increases from 30.1% to 77.0%, while the teacher’s probability increases from 32.8% to 77.4%. These results are consistent with the claim that teacher refresh transfers newly learned revision behavior into subsequent supervision.

Teacher-refresh ablations support this interpretation. At 8B, frozen-teacher DCE reaches 47.99% in the selected comparison, while dynamically refreshed DCE reaches 65.76%. With SRCL, the corresponding values are 41.04% and 65.97%. Exponential moving average teachers also improve over frozen teachers, but the every-step dynamic schedule performs best in the reported comparison.

#### 4.3. Length and Objective Ablations

Forcing OPSD responses to use more tokens does not reproduce DCE’s gains. At 16K, OPSD test-time scaling reaches only 30.76% at 8B, compared with 65.97% for DCE+SRCL. Under an 8K budget, DCE+SRCL with a continuation cue reaches 35.07%, exceeding the OPSD control by 6.60 percentage points while using approximately 7,500 tokens per response.

Forward KL is substantially more effective than reverse KL or Jensen–Shannon divergence. At 8B, forward KL produces 65.97% accuracy with 17,561 tokens, reverse KL produces 60.42% with 23,748 tokens, and JSD produces 17.99% with 3,767 tokens. The JSD result suggests that aggressive distribution matching can cause premature shortening without transferring useful revision behavior.

SRCL alone performs poorly, reaching only 2.36% accuracy at 8B and 0.76% at 4B. This indicates that concise rewrites are useful primarily when DCE has first developed the underlying ability to generate correct revised reasoning. A direct EOS penalty produces shorter responses but lower accuracy than SRCL, suggesting that concision must be tied to verified successful solutions rather than termination pressure alone.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The paper’s main significance is methodological. It shows that a privileged self-distillation teacher need not remain fixed and that updating the teacher can make supervision more aligned with the evolving student’s behavior. The results provide evidence that recursive training can amplify a latent capacity for revision into a more reliable behavioral pattern.

If the approach generalizes beyond mathematical reasoning, it could reduce dependence on externally trained teachers and step-level annotations. It may also provide a way to combine outcome verification with dense token-level learning: the verifier identifies correct solutions, while the privileged branch supplies local guidance along the student’s own trajectories.

The work also addresses the practical cost of reasoning. SRCL demonstrates one mechanism for trading some redundant reflection for shorter verified solutions, although this benefit is not consistent at the smallest model scale. The main limitations are that the evaluation is concentrated on competition-level mathematics, depends on answer verifiers and verified training solutions, and uses substantial inference during training. Performance is also sensitive to model scale, training duration, prompt serialization, and stopping time; Qwen3-1.7B becomes unstable later in training. Further research is needed to establish whether dynamic co-evolution remains reliable for open-ended tasks, imperfect verifiers, non-mathematical reasoning, and settings where correctness cannot be checked automatically.
