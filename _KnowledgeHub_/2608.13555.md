# HumanTracker: Towards Comprehensive and Human-Aligned Motion Tracking Benchmark

> [!summary] Summary
> HumanTracker introduces a human-aligned benchmark for humanoid motion tracking, arguing that kinematic metrics like MPJPE miss physical artifacts, foot skating, mistimed touch-downs, that determine perceived correctness. It contributes 153 hours of motion-capture across four diagnostic families with a standardized MuJoCo protocol, plus HumanScore, a Transformer reward model trained via Bradley-Terry preference learning that aligns with human judgment far better than any kinematic diagnostic.

## Problem

- Kinematic metrics like MPJPE average per-frame pose differences and miss physical artifacts that matter most for perceived quality, particularly unstable support and incorrect contacts.
- Two rollouts can achieve similar pose error yet differ substantially in perceived stability, and humans reliably prefer the rollout with clean contacts and steady support.
- The most common evaluation suite is still an AMASS test set of only 140 sequences, under-representing the long tail of human movement and contact transitions.

## Method

- Collected **153** hours of optical motion-capture from **24** professional performers, retargeted to a 29-DoF humanoid via ==General Motion Retargeting==, artifacts manually removed.
- Introduced **HumanScore**, a masked bidirectional Transformer reward model ingesting a 539-dimensional per-frame feature over 250-frame windows to regress a scalar reward.
- Trained HumanScore with a ==Bradley-Terry== pairwise preference loss on 12K human-annotated motion pairs labeled by six doctoral annotators via a randomized interface.

## Results

- **Humanoid-GPT** is the strongest overall tracker, leading Daily (**94.4%** Succ) and Highly Dynamic, while **SONIC** leads Interaction completion (**97.6%**) and Ground HumanScore (**26.5**).
- HumanScore achieves the highest Align Rate with human judgments at **0.9083**, clearly outperforming MPJVE (**0.8404**), keypoint MAE (**0.8405**), and MPJPE (**0.8049**).
- Alignment improves as temporal context grows from 1 to 5 seconds (**0.857** to **0.908**), showing HumanScore needs multi-second context to capture drift and recovery.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Per-frame kinematic averaging is the wrong unit of analysis, since artifacts that determine perceived stability are trajectory-level events unfolding over seconds.
> - A preference-trained reward model conditioned on contact and force state generalizes zero-shot better than any hand-crafted diagnostic, though it relies on privileged simulator state.
> - The authors caution against directly optimizing a policy against HumanScore as an RL reward without additional regularization and independent human evaluation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Humanoid motion tracking is a foundational capability for applications such as robotic teleoperation and whole-body imitation. Current research in this area often relies on physics-based character control, where learned feedback policies in simulation track a reference motion. However, the evaluation of these tracking systems presents two primary challenges that limit progress.

Firstly, a disparity exists between quantitative kinematic metrics and human perception of motion quality. Traditional metrics, such as mean joint position error (MPJPE), quantify per-frame pose differences but often fail to capture critical physical artifacts. These artifacts include unstable support, incorrect contacts like foot skating, and mistimed touch-downs, which significantly influence human judgments of stability, smoothness, and naturalness. Observers may perceive a rollout as poor despite low kinematic error, particularly in contact-rich scenarios. This suggests that existing metrics do not adequately account for contact dynamics, balance, and the accumulation of errors in closed-loop control, which are crucial for human-like motion. Related work in contact-aware global motion reconstruction and refinement also highlights the importance of addressing these physical artifacts.

Secondly, the evaluation data suites commonly used in humanoid tracking are often small and lack sufficient diversity. The AMASS test set, for instance, with its 140 sequences, is widely used but does not represent the full spectrum of human movement, especially challenging contact transitions, asymmetric balancing, and complex recovery motions. This limitation hinders the ability to thoroughly stress-test and diagnose the failure modes of tracking controllers across varied scenarios. Furthermore, aggregating results into a single score obscures specific weaknesses in different motion categories.

This paper addresses these limitations by introducing a new benchmark and a perceptually aligned metric. By focusing on a large, diverse dataset and a human-preference-trained evaluation metric, HumanTracker aims to bridge the gap between algorithmic performance and human judgment, thereby providing a more relevant and scalable framework for evaluating humanoid motion tracking systems. It builds upon previous efforts to create large motion repositories and physically grounded datasets, but distinguishes itself by explicit categorization and human-aligned evaluation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this research are to improve the evaluation of humanoid motion tracking systems by making it both perceptually aligned with human preferences and scalable to a wide variety of motions. These objectives are motivated by the two main issues identified in the current state of the field:

1. **Metric Misalignment:**
 - **Motivation:** Existing kinematic metrics (e.g., mean joint error, keypoint error) often do not correlate with human perception of motion quality. Rollouts with low kinematic errors can still appear unstable, unnatural, or exhibit physical artifacts like foot sliding, incorrect contact timing, or loss of balance. These issues are particularly prevalent in contact-rich motions and are critical for the perceived realism and stability of humanoid control. By averaging per-frame pose differences, kinematic metrics fail to account for the physical plausibility, stability, and smoothness that human observers prioritize. This discrepancy makes it difficult for researchers to truly gauge progress in developing robust and human-like motion tracking systems.
 - **Objective:** To develop a human-aligned evaluation metric, termed **HumanScore**, that explicitly captures human preferences regarding stability, physical contacts, and overall naturalness. This metric is designed to penalize physical artifacts that are visually apparent and contribute to the perception of poor tracking quality, providing a more reliable measure of success than traditional kinematic metrics.

2. **Limited Diversity and Scale of Evaluation Data:**
 - **Motivation:** Current evaluation benchmarks, such as small subsets of AMASS, are insufficient in scale and diversity to thoroughly test the capabilities and limitations of advanced humanoid tracking policies. They often fail to include challenging scenarios like highly dynamic movements, complex interaction motions, or ground-level transitions that involve multiple contacts. The lack of varied motion categories and fine-grained diagnostics means that researchers struggle to identify specific failure modes or assess performance across different types of movements, often relying on a single aggregate score.
 - **Objective:** To establish a **massive and diverse motion tracking benchmark** that provides approximately 153 hours of high-fidelity optical motion trajectories. This benchmark is explicitly organized into distinct motion families with text labels to enable detailed, category-level diagnosis of tracker performance, moving beyond single aggregate scores. The goal is to provide a comprehensive test suite that can stress controllers under a wider range of contact regimes and movement complexities.

In summary, the paper aims to provide both a robust dataset and a refined evaluation methodology to overcome the limitations of current humanoid motion tracking assessment, thereby ensuring that future advancements are more accurately measured against human perception and real-world challenges.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The HumanTracker project introduces a novel benchmark and a preference-aligned metric to address the limitations of current humanoid motion tracking evaluation.

#### 3.1. HumanTracker Benchmark

The benchmark comprises a large dataset of optical motion trajectories and a taxonomy for diagnostic evaluation.

- **Motion Collection and Processing:** Approximately 153 hours of optical motion data were collected from 24 professional performers, including dance teachers, fitness coaches, and full-time motion-capture actors. This diverse group was selected to cover a wide range of routine and challenging movements. Each human motion sequence was retargeted to a standard 29-DoF benchmark humanoid using General Motion Retargeting (GMR). A crucial step involved inspecting and cleaning the retargeted sequences to remove artifacts such as floating, ground penetration, or discontinuous contacts, ensuring physical plausibility for robot references. Each released clip includes a motion-family label, a natural-language description, a fitted SMPL sequence, and the robot-space reference trajectory in qpos format. The complete dataset is split 9:1 into training and test partitions, preserving family distribution and preventing duplicate motions from crossing partitions.

- **Taxonomy for Diagnostic Evaluation:** The dataset is organized into four distinct motion families to enable fine-grained diagnosis of failure modes:
 - **Daily:** Contains routine movements like walking, turning, and gestures, focusing on steady-state stability and residual drift.
 - **Highly Dynamic:** Includes jumps, kicks, acrobatics, and fast dance footwork, designed to expose errors related to impacts, rapid support switching, and timing.
 - **Interaction:** Features human body motions involving objects or the environment, evaluating hand, arm, and whole-body coordination and providing kinematic priors for manipulation.
 - **Ground:** Covers kneeling, sitting, rolling, and recovery, where low center of mass and multiple simultaneous contacts challenge controller sensitivity to contact geometry and friction.
 This categorization allows for per-family metrics, providing more detailed insights than a single aggregate score.

#### 3.2. Standardized Tracker Evaluation Protocol

To ensure fair comparison across different tracking methods, a standardized evaluation protocol was established:

- **Common Evaluation Entry Point:** Four state-of-the-art trackers (GMT, TWIST2, SONIC, and Humanoid-GPT) were evaluated. While each tracker retains its native policy observations and action decoder, all methods receive the same retargeted reference motions and are executed through a common MuJoCo evaluation environment.
- **Standardized Metrics:** The evaluator standardizes the motion list, robot model, reference indexing, rollout accounting, and metric implementation. The control trajectory is recorded at 50 Hz, storing simulated generalized position and velocity, policy action, motor target, foot contacts and forces, foot and pelvis velocities, and 14 keypoint poses and spatial velocities.
- **Success Criteria:** The evaluation uses the same tracking metric and success criterion as SONIC. An episode fails if vertical position error at the pelvis, ankles, or wrists exceeds 0.25 m, pelvis rotation error exceeds 1 rad, or if generalized position or velocity becomes nonfinite.
- **Reported Metrics:**
 - **Succ:** Fraction of completed episodes.
 - **MPJPE:** Mean absolute error over 29 actuated joint angles (in radians) over the executed portion of each rollout.
 - **HumanScore:** The preference-aligned metric (detailed below).
 - **Additional Diagnostics:** Joint-velocity error, keypoint-position error, foot-contact agreement, and finite-difference joint acceleration and jerk.
All benchmark values are computed on the HumanTracker test split and reported by family and in aggregate.

#### 3.3. HumanScore: Preference-Aligned Evaluation

HumanScore is a reward model trained on human preference data to predict perceptual quality.

- **Preference Data Construction:**
 - **Rollout Segmentation:** Rollouts from the four evaluated trackers (GMT, Humanoid-GPT, SONIC, TWIST2) on the HumanTracker *training* split motions were divided into consecutive 250-frame (5-second) windows.
 - **Uniform Rollout Pair Sampling:** A uniformly spaced set of unique window indices was selected from the concatenated, ordered catalogue of all rollouts. Each selected window yielded one comparison between two of the four trackers, with all six unordered tracker combinations allocated equally. Display order was randomized.
 - **Human Annotation:** Six doctoral researchers specializing in humanoid robotics acted as annotators. They viewed synchronized videos of two rollouts and provided judgments: "Left is better," "Right is better," "Similar," or "Cannot compare." They prioritized failures (loss of balance), then compared jitter, foot sliding, locomotion consistency, and naturalness. "Cannot compare" pairs were excluded from model training.
 - **Data Split:** The collected labels (12,000 preference records from 6,000 original pairs and their mirrored variants) were split 80/20 into training/test sets based on `motion_id` to prevent clips from the same source motion from crossing partitions. The split was balanced across families, tracker pairings, label types, and annotators.

- **HumanScore Reward Model:**
 - **Trajectory Representation:** Each frame of a rollout segment is represented by a 539-dimensional vector, combining 70 dimensions for the current reference state and 469 dimensions for simulated state, control, measured contact dynamics, root motion, and keypoint kinematics.
 - **Model Architecture:** Frame vectors are linearly projected, normalized, and augmented with sinusoidal positional encoding. A Transformer encoder processes these tokens, applying a padding mask in attention layers. Masked mean pooling of valid output tokens forms a trajectory representation, which an MLP maps to an unbounded scalar reward, $r_{\theta}(\tau) \in \mathbb{R}$.
 - **Preference Objective:** The model is optimized using a modified Bradley-Terry loss. For strict preferences, the loss is $L^{(i)}_{diff} = -\log \sigma(\Delta_i)$, where $\Delta_i = r^{(i)}_{chosen} - r^{(i)}_{rejected}$. For "Similar" pairs, a symmetric loss is used: $L^{(j)}_{similar} = -\frac{1}{2}\log \sigma(\Delta_j) - \frac{1}{2}\log \sigma(-\Delta_j)$.
 - **HumanScore Computation:** At evaluation time, a rollout is divided into 5-second windows. Each window's unbounded reward is mapped to a bounded reward $\rho_{\theta}(s_i) \in (0, 1)$ using a sigmoid function. The final HumanScore for a rollout is the frame-weighted average of these window rewards, scaled by 100.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several key findings from the evaluation using the HumanTracker benchmark and the HumanScore metric.

#### 4.1. Zero-Shot Evaluation on HumanTracker

Table 3 in the paper details the performance of representative state-of-the-art trackers (GMT, TWIST2, SONIC, Humanoid-GPT) on the HumanTracker test set across the four motion families.

- **Overall Performance:** Humanoid-GPT demonstrated strong overall performance, leading most comparisons and all three metrics (Succ, MPJPE, HumanScore) on the "Daily" and "Highly Dynamic" motion families. SONIC was its closest competitor.
- **Family-Specific Strengths:**
 - Humanoid-GPT achieved the highest HumanScore (54.7) and lowest MPJPE (0.046 rad) on "Daily" motions, with a high success rate (94.4%). It also performed best on "Highly Dynamic" motions, with a HumanScore of 49.2 and MPJPE of 0.047 rad, and a high success rate (86.9%).
 - SONIC achieved the highest completion rate (97.6%) and HumanScore (54.6) on "Interaction" motions, although Humanoid-GPT had a slightly lower MPJPE (0.070 rad vs. SONIC's 0.128 rad).
 - On "Ground" motions, SONIC recorded the highest HumanScore (26.5), while Humanoid-GPT achieved the highest success rate (32.9%) and lowest MPJPE (0.216 rad). This indicates a qualitative difference where SONIC's rollouts for "Ground" motions were perceived as more natural and stable despite Humanoid-GPT's slightly better kinematic fidelity and completion rate.
- **Discrepancy between Metrics:** The results highlighted that traditional kinematic metrics (MPJPE) do not always align with HumanScore. For instance, on "Ground" motions, Humanoid-GPT had a lower MPJPE and higher success rate than SONIC, but SONIC received a higher HumanScore, suggesting that human perception prioritized aspects not fully captured by MPJPE, such as contact quality and stability in complex ground interactions. This supports the paper's motivation for a perceptually aligned metric.
- **Persistent Weaknesses:** All trackers showed significantly lower success rates and HumanScores on "Ground" and "Highly Dynamic" motions compared to "Daily" and "Interaction" motions, indicating persistent challenges in these contact-rich and dynamic regimes.

#### 4.2. Human Preference Comparison

Table 4 in the paper compares the alignment rate of HumanScore and various individual analytic diagnostics with human preferences.

- **Superior Alignment of HumanScore:** HumanScore achieved an average alignment rate of 90.83% with human preferences, which was consistently higher than any single conventional diagnostic metric.
- **Limitations of Traditional Metrics:**
 - MPJPE (mean absolute joint-angle error) had an alignment rate of 80.49%.
 - MPJVE (mean joint-velocity error) and KPT Position MAE (keypoint position mean absolute error) showed similar alignment rates around 84.04-84.05%.
 - Foot Contact Accuracy showed 78.82% alignment.
 - Metrics related to smoothness, such as Average Joint Acceleration (69.33%) and Average Joint Jerk (72.32%), exhibited lower alignment rates.
- **Holistic Assessment:** The lower agreement of individual diagnostics indicates that human annotators consider a holistic set of qualities, including stability, smoothness, contact fidelity, and the temporal evolution of errors, which no single diagnostic fully captures. HumanScore, by being trained on direct human comparisons, better integrates these nuanced perceptual qualities.

#### 4.3. Sensitivity Analysis

Figure 5 illustrates the sensitivity of HumanScore to different input features and temporal context.

- **Importance of Contact Features:** Removing measured contact features from the HumanScore model led to a notable degradation in performance, particularly on "Ground" motions. This suggests that explicit contact information is crucial for accurately evaluating motions involving complex contact transitions, reinforcing the argument that kinematic metrics alone are insufficient.
- **Limited Benefit of Future Reference Information:** Adding future reference information to the model input performed slightly worse than the baseline. This indicates that the current and historical states are largely sufficient for evaluating the quality of actions, and exploiting future information may be challenging or offer limited additional benefit for human preference prediction in this context.
- **Impact of Temporal Context:** The alignment rate of HumanScore steadily improved as the available temporal context for the model increased from one to five seconds. This finding suggests that human perception of motion quality relies on observing evolving artifacts (e.g., foot sliding, repeated jitter, progressive drift, recovery from instability) over a longer duration, rather than assessing motion purely frame-by-frame. Integrating complementary evidence over time is essential for capturing perceived trajectory quality.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The HumanTracker project presents a significant contribution to the field of humanoid motion tracking through its introduction of a large-scale, diverse benchmark and a human-aligned evaluation metric.

- **Addressing Evaluation Gaps:** The primary significance lies in directly addressing the two critical limitations of current evaluation practices: the misalignment between kinematic metrics and human perception, and the inadequacy of small, undiversified datasets. By providing a benchmark of approximately 153 hours of optical motion trajectories across four distinct categories ("Daily," "Highly Dynamic," "Interaction," "Ground"), HumanTracker offers a comprehensive and challenging testbed for controllers. This scale and categorization enable researchers to diagnose specific failure modes and assess performance across different movement complexities and contact regimes, rather than relying on a single, often misleading, aggregate score.

- **Improved Metric Fidelity:** The proposed HumanScore metric, trained on 12,000 pairwise human preference comparisons, demonstrates a substantially higher alignment with human judgments compared to traditional analytical diagnostics like MPJPE, joint velocity error, or foot contact accuracy. HumanScore's ability to capture nuanced perceptual qualities, especially concerning contact, stability, and smoothness over time, is crucial. This means that advancements measured by HumanScore are more likely to correspond to motions that are perceived as natural and stable by humans, fostering the development of truly human-aligned humanoid control policies.

- **Standardized and Reproducible Evaluation:** HumanTracker establishes a rigorous and standardized evaluation protocol, ensuring that comparisons between different tracking methods are fair and reproducible. By standardizing the motion list, robot model, reference indexing, rollout accounting, and metric implementation, the benchmark facilitates objective assessment and allows for clear identification of tracker strengths and weaknesses, fostering healthy competition and focused research.

- **Impact on Future Research:** The availability of this benchmark and metric is anticipated to accelerate progress in humanoid motion tracking by:
 - **Guiding Policy Development:** Researchers can use HumanScore as a more accurate feedback signal for training and optimizing humanoid control policies, particularly in reinforcement learning settings, to produce motions that are not only kinematically accurate but also perceptually plausible.
 - **Facilitating Failure Analysis:** The categorized nature of the benchmark allows for targeted analysis of controller failures in specific motion regimes (e.g., highly dynamic impacts, multi-contact ground transitions), enabling researchers to develop specialized solutions for these challenging areas.
 - **Cross-Embodiment and Real-World Applications:** While currently focused on a simulated 29-DoF humanoid, the framework lays groundwork for future extensions to cross-embodiment settings and real-world hardware. The development of observable feature sets or validated state estimators will be necessary for applying HumanScore to physical robots.
 - **Benchmarking Foundation:** HumanTracker provides a foundation for future work, including the integration with reward optimization techniques and exploration of generalization to unseen robots or simulators.

In conclusion, HumanTracker moves beyond traditional evaluation paradigms by prioritizing human perception and comprehensive testing, offering a toolset that can significantly advance the capabilities and human-likeness of humanoid motion tracking systems.
