# Aegis: Automated Error Generation and Attribution for Multi-Agent Systems

> [!summary] Summary
> The Aegis framework automates error generation and attribution for multi-agent systems, constructing a dataset of nearly 10,000 annotated trajectories, and enabling fine-tuned models to significantly outperform baselines in identifying faulty agents and error modes. This approach addresses the data scarcity bottleneck in MAS reliability research, providing a scalable method for creating diagnostic data.

## Problem

- Debugging LLM-based Multi-Agent Systems (MAS) is challenging due to complex error propagation and difficulty in tracing root causes to individual agents.
- A severe scarcity of large-scale, diverse datasets for MAS error attribution exists, with current benchmarks relying on costly and unscalable manual expert annotation.
- State-of-the-art LLMs demonstrate limited capability in accurately attributing errors within MAS contexts due to the lack of sufficient training data.

## Method

- The **Aegis** framework employs an LLM-based ==adaptive manipulator== to programmatically inject context-aware errors (based on a 14-mode taxonomy) into successful MAS trajectories via prompt injection or response corruption.
- Generated faulty trajectories are automatically validated, and ground-truth attributions are recorded, creating a dataset of 9,533 annotated error trajectories across six MAS frameworks and six task domains.
- The dataset supports training diagnostic models using Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), Reinforcement Learning (RL) with GRPO, and Contrastive Learning (CL) with DCL.

## Results

- Aegis-SFT, fine-tuned on Qwen2.5-**14B**, achieved the highest average performance score of 26.51, nearly doubling the base model's score (13.99) and outperforming all zero-shot baselines.
- Models demonstrated higher accuracy in identifying faulty agents than specific error modes and struggled more with rarer error categories compared to frequently occurring ones.
- A human Inter-Annotator Agreement (IAA) of **0.85** and programmatic-human agreement of **0.81** on Aegis-Bench validated the high quality and realistic characteristics of the synthetically generated data.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Automated synthetic data generation effectively overcomes the data scarcity bottleneck for MAS error attribution, providing realistic and verifiable training examples.
> - Fine-tuning open-source LLMs on the domain-specific Aegis synthetic dataset yields superior error attribution performance, surpassing proprietary models an order of magnitude larger.
> - The effectiveness of Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting for open-source models is enhanced after GRPO training, suggesting it depends on cultivated underlying reasoning capabilities rather than being a standalone technique.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work on Aegis is situated within the rapidly evolving field of Large Language Model (LLM)-based Multi-Agent Systems (MAS). These systems are recognized for their capability to address complex problems by decomposing tasks among specialized, collaborative agents, demonstrating advancements in areas such as mathematical reasoning, scientific discovery, and software engineering.

However, the decomposition inherent in MAS also introduces a structural fragility. Errors originating from a single agent can propagate and cascade through interactions, leading to system-level failures that are difficult to trace back to their root cause. This complexity creates a significant challenge for debugging and improving the reliability of MAS.

Prior research on MAS error attribution has been constrained by the scarcity of large-scale, diverse datasets. Existing benchmarks, such as Who&When (Zhang et al., 2025c), MASFT (Cemri et al., 2025), and TRAIL (Deshpande et al., 2025), rely on labor-intensive and costly manual annotation by experts, resulting in relatively small datasets. This data scarcity has been identified as a bottleneck, limiting the ability of even state-of-the-art LLMs to perform effective error attribution.

A broader trend in the AI community involves the adoption of synthetic data generation techniques, where models themselves create training data, verifiable tasks, and interactive environments. This approach has proven effective in overcoming data scarcity in domains such as reasoning and software engineering. Aegis extends this methodology by applying automated synthesis to the problem of MAS error attribution, aiming to resolve the scalability deadlock associated with manual annotation.

The research also relates to the established field of anomaly detection in distributed systems, which has developed methods for tracing and root-cause analysis. Aegis specifically adapts these diagnostic principles to the unique context of LLM-based MAS. Furthermore, it addresses concerns related to the structural safety and cascading risks in MAS interaction topologies, and vulnerabilities such as persuasion or adversarial attacks, highlighting the importance of robust error attribution for MAS integrity.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the inherent difficulties in debugging and improving the reliability of LLM-based Multi-Agent Systems (MAS). While MAS offer advancements in solving complex problems, their distributed nature introduces a structural fragility where a single agent's error can trigger a cascade of failures, making root-cause analysis challenging.

A significant bottleneck hindering progress in MAS reliability is the severe scarcity of large-scale and diverse datasets specifically designed for error attribution. Existing datasets are comparatively small and depend on expensive, expert-driven manual annotation of complex execution logs, which is not scalable. Consequently, current state-of-the-art LLMs demonstrate limited capability in accurately attributing errors within MAS contexts. The authors observe that in other AI domains, automated synthetic data generation has successfully mitigated data scarcity issues, suggesting a similar opportunity for MAS error attribution.

Against this background, the key objectives of the Aegis project are:

- **To develop a novel framework for automated error generation and attribution:** The core objective is to introduce "Aegis" (Automated error generation and attribution for multi-agent systems), a systematic approach to programmatically create realistic error trajectories.
- **To overcome the data scarcity bottleneck:** By implementing an LLM-based manipulator, Aegis aims to adaptively inject context-aware errors into successful multi-agent execution trajectories. This process is designed to automatically validate and record the faulty agents and corresponding error modes, thereby transforming the human-annotation dependency into a scalable engineering problem.
- **To construct a large and diverse dataset:** The project aims to generate a comprehensive dataset of nearly 10,000 annotated error trajectories. This dataset is designed to encompass a variety of MAS architectures (e.g., MacNet, DyLAN, Debate) and task domains (e.g., mathematical reasoning, code generation, scientific problem-solving), alongside diverse error modes based on an empirically grounded taxonomy.
- **To support multiple learning paradigms:** A further objective is to ensure the generated dataset is structured to facilitate training across different machine learning paradigms, specifically Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), Reinforcement Learning (RL), and Contrastive Learning (CL).
- **To develop and evaluate learning methods:** The research seeks to develop specific learning methods tailored for each of the supported paradigms and to conduct comprehensive experiments to validate their effectiveness. This includes assessing performance on the internally generated Aegis-Bench and evaluating generalization capabilities on external, human-annotated datasets like Who&When.
- **To contribute to open-source resources:** A final objective is the open-source release of all code, data, and models developed, providing a foundation for future research aimed at building more robust and interpretable multi-agent systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Aegis framework implements a multi-stage pipeline for automated error generation and attribution in Multi-Agent Systems (MAS), coupled with the development and evaluation of learning methods across three paradigms.

**Problem Formulation:**
The research defines MAS as comprising *k* agents interacting within a state space *S* and a joint action space *A*. An agent scheduling policy determines the active agent at each step, whose action causes system evolution. A trajectory *τ* represents a complete interaction, with a binary function *Z(τ)* indicating system failure. For failed trajectories, the objective is to attribute the failure to responsible agents (*n***) and their error modes (*Y***) from a predefined taxonomy *Y* of *M* distinct error modes. The goal is to train a diagnostic model *f_θ* that maps a failed trajectory *τ* to a predicted attribution map *G_hat(τ)* approximating the ground truth *G(τ)*.

**Aegis Dataset Construction:**
The dataset creation process follows a principled three-stage pipeline:
1. **Collection of Deterministic Successful Trajectories:** The process begins by gathering tasks that a given MAS can solve correctly. For each task, a "baseline" successful trajectory (*τ_corr*) is generated under deterministic conditions (e.g., LLM temperature set to 0, fixed random seed). This ensures that any subsequent system failure directly results from intentional interventions.
2. **LLM-based Adaptive Manipulator:** An adaptive manipulator, *M_manip*, is employed to inject context-aware failures. *M_manip* uses a Large Language Model (GPT-4o-mini with temperature 0.7) to generate plausible error modes aligned with a taxonomy of 14 empirically-grounded error modes from the MAST taxonomy (Cemri et al., 2025). These errors fall into three high-level categories: Specification Issues, Inter-Agent Misalignment, and Task Verification Failures. The manipulator can apply two strategies:
 - **Prompt Injection:** Altering an agent's input state to induce an error.
 - **Response Corruption:** Tampering with an agent's output by substituting a faulty action for a correct one.
 For each *τ_corr*, multiple faulty counterparts are generated based on unique "Injection Plans," which specify target agents and error modes. Non-invasive wrappers are used to intercept and modify agent behavior without altering the underlying MAS codebase. All agents within the MAS typically utilize GPT-4o-mini with temperature 0 for deterministic execution.
3. **Validation and Ground Truth Labeling:** Each generated trajectory (*τ_inj*) is validated. Only trajectories where the intervention successfully induces a system failure (*Z(τ_inj) = 1*) are included in the dataset. For these validated failures, the ground-truth attribution map *G(τ_inj)* is known by construction and equals the intentionally injected errors.

This process was applied to six MAS frameworks (MacNet, DyLAN, Debate, AgentVerse, Magnetic-One, SmolAgents) and six task domains (MATH, GSM8K, HumanEval, SciBench, MMLU-Pro, GAIA), resulting in a dataset of 9,533 annotated error trajectories with 24,843 individual error instances.

**Learning Methodologies:**
The structure of the Aegis dataset supports three distinct learning paradigms:

1. **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT):** The error attribution task is framed as a sequence-to-sequence problem. For each sample, an input prompt (*x*) is constructed, containing the model's role, definitions of error modes, and the serialized conversation log. The target output (*o*) is a JSON-formatted string specifying faulty agents and their error modes. A diagnostic LLM is fine-tuned to minimize the negative log-likelihood of generating *o* given *x*.
2. **Reinforcement Learning (RL) using GRPO:** Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) is employed with a hierarchical reward function (*R*) designed to provide dense, structured feedback. The reward function assigns positive credits for correctly identified agent-error pairs, individual faulty agents, and correct error modes, with partial, non-repeatable credits. Penalties are applied for malformed, duplicate, or excessive predictions. The raw score is normalized by the maximum attainable score, and the policy is optimized using GRPO.
3. **Contrastive Learning (CL) using Disentangled Contrastive Learning (DCL):** DCL formulates error attribution as weakly supervised representation learning. Each trajectory is treated as a "bag" of turns, utilizing Multiple Instance Learning (MIL) attention to identify salient "evidence turns." Turn representations are disentangled by aligning them with two prototype banks for agents and error modes, initialized from textual definitions. The model is trained with a composite loss function:
 - *L_cls*: A standard multi-label classification loss (Binary Cross-Entropy) applied at the agent, error, and agent-error pair levels.
 - *L_con*: A supervised contrastive loss that pulls embeddings of salient error turns closer to their positive counterparts (corresponding "clean" turns from successful baselines and ground-truth agent/error prototypes) and pushes them away from negative samples.
 - *L_hier*: A hierarchical consistency regularizer (squared hinge loss) that enforces logical constraints, penalizing instances where a predicted agent-error pair's probability exceeds the minimum of its constituent agent and error probabilities.

**Experimental Setup:**
The dataset is partitioned into training, validation, and a test set called Aegis-Bench (600 trajectories). Generalization is assessed on the Who&When benchmark, an out-of-distribution (OOD) human-annotated dataset, which is preprocessed to map free-text error descriptions to the 14 defined error modes. Performance is evaluated using Micro-F1 and Macro-F1 scores at three granularity levels: Pair (agent-error pairs), Agent (faulty agents), and Error (error modes). Fine-tuning efforts for SFT and RL concentrate on 7B to 14B parameter models (e.g., Qwen2.5, Qwen3) using FSDP on 4 NVIDIA A800 GPUs. The DCL model utilizes an all-MiniLM-L6-V2 encoder and is trained on 4 NVIDIA 4090 GPUs. Various open-source and proprietary LLMs are included as comparison baselines, evaluated in a zero-shot setting.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of the Aegis framework and the generated dataset in enhancing error attribution capabilities for Multi-Agent Systems (MAS).

**Overall Performance:**
Aegis-SFT, particularly when fine-tuned on the Qwen2.5-14B model, achieved the highest average performance, with a score of 26.51. This performance significantly surpassed all baseline models. Aegis-GRPO (Qwen2.5-14B) also performed competitively, scoring 18.41, aligning with the performance of larger, proprietary foundation models. Among general-purpose proprietary LLMs, o3 (20.24), Gemini-2.5-Flash (19.55), and Claude-Sonnet-4 (18.16) showed the highest scores. While model scale generally correlated with improved performance, task-aligned fine-tuning on the Aegis dataset provided the most substantial gains, nearly doubling the score of the base Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct model (from 13.99 to 26.51). The lightweight, contrastive-learning-based DCL model, built on a smaller encoder, achieved a score of 12.61, demonstrating a proof of concept by outperforming the random baseline (4.08).

**Granularity of Attribution:**
A consistent finding across models was that Micro-F1 scores were substantially higher than Macro-F1 scores. This indicates that models performed well on frequently occurring error modes but struggled with rarer, more specific categories, highlighting a "long-tail" distribution of error types and underscoring the importance of Macro-F1 for assessing true generalization. Furthermore, models generally achieved higher accuracy in Agent-level attribution compared to Error-level attribution, suggesting that identifying "who" is responsible for a fault is a more tractable task than diagnosing the specific "why" or nature of the error, which demands a deeper semantic understanding of the interaction log.

**Influence of Task and MAS Architecture:**
Performance varied across task domains and agentic architectures. Aegis-SFT provided broad and significant performance improvements across most task domains, including coding and science, with its advantage being less pronounced only in the highly generalist GAIA task. Aegis-GRPO's benefits were more concentrated, particularly excelling in mathematical reasoning tasks such as MATH. Regarding MAS architectures, models generally performed better in structured frameworks like Debate and AgentVerse. In contrast, they demonstrated greater difficulty with complex topologies such as DyLAN and MacNet, where fine-tuning on Aegis yielded the most substantial performance boosts. For smaller-scale MAS like Magnetic-One and SmolAgents, Aegis-SFT exhibited a slight performance drop, possibly indicating over-specialization, whereas the GRPO model maintained more stable performance, suggesting stronger robustness across underrepresented systems.

**Analysis of Fine-Tuning Methods:**
- **GRPO Training:** The reward curves for GRPO training demonstrated the stability and effectiveness of the hierarchical reward function, showing steady improvements across models. Larger 14B models consistently achieved higher reward levels than their 7B counterparts. The Qwen3-8B-Thinking model, despite initial output truncation due to verbose reasoning, learned to produce parsable answers within token limits, indicating the robustness of the RL approach.
- **Chain-of-Thought (CoT) Prompting:** For base open-source models, simply adding a CoT prompt was detrimental to performance, often disrupting the strict JSON output format required for evaluation. However, after GRPO training, CoT provided a significant performance boost. This suggests that CoT's benefits are not solely a prompting technique but depend on an underlying reasoning capability, which GRPO training appears to cultivate in open-source models. Proprietary models, in contrast, benefited from CoT out-of-the-box.
- **SFT Overfitting:** An analysis of SFT training epochs revealed a clear pattern of overfitting. While in-domain performance on Aegis-Bench continued to improve with extended training, performance on the out-of-distribution (OOD) Who&When benchmark peaked at two epochs before declining. This indicates that after two epochs, models began to overfit to the stylistic patterns of the synthetic data rather than learning more generalizable features. Consequently, two epochs were selected for the final SFT models to balance in-domain performance with OOD generalization.

**Detailed Assessment of Contrastive Learning (DCL):**
- **Learned Representations:** A t-SNE visualization of learned representations indicated that at the "bag-level" (trajectory), the DCL model produced highly discriminative and compact representations, showing clear separation between different clusters. In contrast, "turn-level" embeddings exhibited higher variance and overlapping clusters. This highlighted the effectiveness of the attention-based aggregation in distilling sparse signals from individual turns into robust trajectory representations.
- **Ablation Study:** Ablation experiments confirmed the criticality of both semantic guidance from text-based prototypes ("w/o intent") and compositional consistency ("w/o consistency") for DCL's performance, with the latter causing a significant decrease in Pair-level accuracy. Simpler architectural choices, such as "only-bilinear" head structures, were less effective than the proposed compositional design. These trends were also observed on the OOD Who&When benchmark, confirming the framework's robustness.

**Dataset Quality Validation (Appendix B.3):**
The quality of the Aegis dataset was validated through several measures. A human Inter-Annotator Agreement (IAA) study on 100 trajectories from Aegis-Bench yielded a Fleiss’ Kappa (κ) of 0.85 for human-human agreement and 0.81 for programmatic-human agreement. These scores are comparable to those reported in the MAST paper, indicating high label fidelity. A distributional analysis using UMAP and t-SNE visualizations showed that the synthetic Aegis-Bench data was heavily interspersed with real-world MAST-Data and Who&When data in a semantic embedding space. A low Silhouette score (0.11) and comparable centroid distances between synthetic and real datasets further confirmed that the synthetic data is not semantically separable from real-world failure data, validating its realistic characteristics.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Aegis framework introduces a novel approach with several significant implications for the field of Multi-Agent Systems (MAS) and broader AI reliability.

Firstly, Aegis directly addresses the critical challenge of **data scarcity** for error attribution in LLM-based MAS. By providing a scalable, automated pipeline for generating nearly 10,000 verifiable error trajectories, it removes the reliance on costly and unscalable manual annotation. This methodological shift from human-driven annotation to programmatic data generation represents a foundational step for advancing MAS reliability research.

Secondly, the research demonstrates the **efficacy of synthetic data** for complex diagnostic tasks. The strong performance of models fine-tuned on the Aegis dataset, particularly their generalization capabilities to the human-annotated Who&When benchmark, validates the hypothesis that automatically synthesized data can be realistic and valuable for training sophisticated error attribution models. This finding suggests a viable pathway for improving AI reliability in domains where real-world failure data is inherently difficult and expensive to acquire.

Thirdly, the trained models represent a **significant improvement in error attribution performance**. Aegis-SFT models achieve state-of-the-art performance, outperforming proprietary models an order of magnitude larger. This indicates that domain-specific, high-quality synthetic data, even with smaller open-source models, can yield superior results compared to generalist large models without specialized training. The ability to accurately attribute errors to specific agents and identify their modes is crucial for debugging, monitoring, and enhancing the trustworthiness of complex MAS.

Fourthly, the **open-source release** of all code, data, and models generated by Aegis provides a valuable resource for the research community. This contribution lowers the barrier to entry for researchers to investigate MAS reliability, fostering further innovation in debugging tools, diagnostic methods, and system design.

The potential impact of this work extends to the development of more **robust and interpretable MAS**. By enabling precise identification of failure origins, Aegis contributes to building systems that are not only more reliable but also more transparent in their operations, which is essential for deploying MAS in sensitive or high-stakes applications. Furthermore, this research paves the way for future advancements towards **self-repairing agentic systems**. By systematically understanding and attributing emergent failures, subsequent research can focus on developing mechanisms for autonomous error detection, diagnosis, and recovery, ultimately leading to more resilient and intelligent AI systems. The broader implication is a methodological paradigm shift that advocates for programmatic data generation as a cornerstone for enhancing the reliability and safety of advanced AI.
