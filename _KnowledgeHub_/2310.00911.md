# Accurate Simulation and Parameter Identification of Deformable Linear Objects using Discrete Elastic Rods in Generalized Coordinates

> [!summary] Summary
> This work integrates Discrete Elastic Rods theory into MuJoCo for deformable linear objects like ropes and cables. Cartesian stiffness forces are converted to generalized joint torques via force-lever analysis, avoiding per-node Jacobian queries. A two-test parameter identification pipeline recovers bending and twisting stiffness from a 3-D printed apparatus and depth camera. The adapted model beats MuJoCo's native cable on analytical validation and real robot poses, with under 3% computational overhead.

## Problem

- Accurate rod models live in standalone numerical simulators that cannot handle external objects or require tedious work to couple with robot manipulators.
- MuJoCo's native cable assumes torque responses linear in deformation, inconsistent with Kirchhoff rod theory, so accuracy degrades at large deformations.
- Existing stiffness identification methods often omit twist, need costly sensing or large real datasets, and rarely recover twisting stiffness accurately.

## Method

- ==Force-lever analysis== converts ==Discrete Elastic Rods== Cartesian centerline forces into ball-joint torques by moments about each joint, removing n+1 ==Jacobian queries== per simulation step.
- Stiffness moduli are formally tied to physical parameters as bending equals ==Young's modulus== times second moment of area, twisting equals ==shear modulus== times torsion constant.
- Two-stage identification pipeline: ==golden-section search== fits bending modulus to depth-camera node positions; ==bisection== matches simulated critical buckling twist to the real twist ratio.

## Results

- Computation overhead over a plain kinematic chain is **-1%** to **3%** for the adapted model versus **2%** to **15%** for direct Cartesian force application.
- On Michell's instability, average 2-norm error per data point is **0.0483** for the adapted model against **0.7725** for native, nearly an order of magnitude better.
- Across **4** robot poses and **3** real wires, per-node position error stays below **5%** of total length; parameter cloud sizes reach **2.182%** bending, **0.155%** twisting.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Converting Cartesian elastic forces into generalized coordinates buys physical fidelity almost for free when the simulator already exposes joint-space passive forces.
> - Quasistatic treatment of material-frame twist removes unnatural twist wave oscillations, sidestepping the impossible task of tuning coupled bend-twist damping on single-value ball joints.
> - Identification consistency tracks material elasticity; plastically deforming samples yield larger parameter spread, so such pipelines best serve as warm starts for learning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Deformable Linear Objects (DLOs), such as ropes, wires, and cables, are prevalent across diverse scientific and industrial domains, including polymer physics, musculoskeletal modeling, hair simulation, DNA mechanics, medical suturing, and wire harness assembly. The robotic manipulation of DLOs is a field of active research, characterized by challenges arising from the complex, nonlinear dynamics of these objects, their sensitivity to various parameters, and their often underactuated nature.

Traditional control techniques for DLO manipulation encounter limitations due to these complexities. Machine learning approaches have emerged as potential solutions, but their implementation is hindered by the substantial data requirements for training, especially for intricate tasks. Collecting such data in real-world environments is often laborious or impractical. Consequently, accurate and easily integrable simulations are critical for generating diverse training data to develop generalizable control policies.

Existing DLO simulation models vary in their approach and accuracy. Mass-spring systems offer simplicity but often lack a clear interpretation of parameters as physical properties, necessitating extensive tuning. Position-based models are common in animation for visual fidelity but may not prioritize physical accuracy. More advanced models, such as those based on Cosserat rod theory, provide comprehensive representations by including stretching and shearing, relevant for a wider range of applications. The Discrete Elastic Rods (DER) model offers an explicit centerline representation, facilitates collision handling, and uses a quasistatic treatment of the material frame twist, which can enhance simulation stability. However, many accurate DLO models are implemented in standalone numerical simulators, which makes their integration with robot physics simulators and external objects difficult or time-consuming. MuJoCo, an established physics simulator, includes a native cable model, but its linear torque response to deformations limits its accuracy, particularly for large deformations, and is inconsistent with Kirchhoff rod theory.

Parameter identification for DLOs is another area of ongoing research. Static methods often compare real and simulated equilibrium data to infer physical parameters, but some simplify DLO modeling by omitting twist, thereby limiting twist stiffness estimation. Optimal control techniques derive parameters from real trajectories, while machine learning approaches enable concurrent parameter updates during shape control. However, these methods can be complex or require extensive real-world data.

This paper addresses these gaps by:
1. Integrating an accurate DER-based DLO model into the established MuJoCo simulator, improving upon MuJoCo's native cable model by adopting a generalized coordinate representation to reduce computational load and enhance stability.
2. Developing a novel parameter identification pipeline that emphasizes simplicity and accuracy for determining both bending and twisting stiffness, distinct from existing complex or data-intensive methods.
3. Formally defining the stiffness moduli used in the DER formulation in terms of more widely recognized physical and material parameters.
4. Evaluating the proposed model and parameter identification method against theoretical validation tests and real-world robot manipulation experiments. This work aims to reduce the reliance on real experiments for DLO manipulation by providing a robust simulation environment and a practical method for parameter estimation, thereby offering a "warm start" for data-driven learning approaches.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and validate a fast and accurate model of a deformable linear object (DLO) that can be seamlessly integrated into an established robot physics simulator, MuJoCo. This overarching goal is driven by several motivations and broken down into specific contributions:

**Motivation:**

- **Demand for Accurate DLO Manipulation:** Robotic manipulation of DLOs is a critical and active research area in various fields, from medical suturing to industrial wire assembly. These applications require precise control strategies that can account for the complex, nonlinear, and underactuated dynamics of DLOs.
- **Limitations of Real-World Data Collection:** Machine learning techniques are increasingly applied to DLO manipulation problems. However, training these models necessitates vast amounts of diverse data. Collecting such data from real robotic setups can be time-consuming, resource-intensive, and sometimes infeasible.
- **Need for High-Fidelity Simulations:** To overcome data collection challenges, simulations are indispensable. For learned policies to be effective and generalizable to real-world scenarios, these simulations must accurately replicate real DLO dynamics and integrate easily with robotic manipulators.
- **Deficiencies in Existing Simulation Solutions:**
 - Many accurate DLO models exist in standalone numerical simulators, which are not designed for easy interaction with external objects or robot systems, thus requiring significant effort for integration.
 - MuJoCo, a widely used physics simulator, possesses a native cable model. However, this model relies on a linear torque response to bending and twisting deformations, which is inconsistent with Kirchhoff rod theory and leads to inaccuracies, particularly under large deformations.
 - Direct implementation of complex DLO theories into simulators like MuJoCo can be computationally expensive due to frequent queries for Jacobians.

**Key Objectives (Contributions):**

1. **Formal Definition of Stiffness Moduli:** To bridge the gap between theoretical DLO models and practical applications, a key objective is to formally define the bending (α) and twisting (β) stiffness moduli used in the Discrete Elastic Rods (DER) formulation in terms of widely recognized physical and material parameters (Young's modulus E, shear modulus G, second moment of area I, and torsion constant J_T). This clarifies the physical meaning of the simulation parameters.
2. **Development of an Adapted DER Model in Generalized Coordinates:** The central objective is to present a generalized coordinate representation of the Cartesian stiffness forces derived from the DER theory. This adaptation is specifically designed for integration into MuJoCo, aiming to reduce computational load compared to direct force application. The model also aims to improve stability and minimize unnatural oscillations by adopting the DER theory’s quasistatic treatment of the rod's material frame twist.
3. **Introduction of a Novel Parameter Identification Pipeline:** A significant objective is to develop a new, simple, and accurate pipeline for identifying the bending and twisting stiffness parameters of real-world DLOs. This pipeline is intended to be straightforward to implement, requiring minimal and accessible equipment, and capable of independently determining bending stiffness and the twisting-to-bending stiffness ratio.
4. **Comprehensive Validation and Evaluation:** The research aims to evaluate the performance of the developed DLO model (adapted DER) against MuJoCo's native cable model. This evaluation includes:
 - Assessing computational speed for different numbers of discrete sections.
 - Validating accuracy against theoretically proven tests (Localized Helical Buckling and Michell's Buckling Instability).
 - Evaluating the consistency and suitability of the parameter identification pipeline on different DLO materials.
 - Comparing the shape prediction performance of both simulation models against real experiments involving robot manipulation of DLOs, using the identified stiffness parameters.

By achieving these objectives, the authors aim to provide a robust and practical tool for researchers working on DLO manipulation, ultimately facilitating the development of more capable and generalizable robotic systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed a multi-faceted approach encompassing DLO modeling, simulation integration, computational performance analysis, theoretical validation, and a novel parameter identification pipeline, culminating in real-world experimental comparisons.

**A. DLO Model and Implementation**

1. **Discrete Elastic Rods (DER) Theory:** The DLO model is based on the Discrete Elastic Rods (DER) theory, which discretizes the DLO into sections with nodes at their joining points. The model calculates Cartesian forces on each node as the negative derivative of the total potential energy, ensuring movement towards a stable configuration. The paper assumes inextensibility, considered a reasonable assumption for wire manipulation and medical suturing.
2. **Stiffness Moduli Definition:** The paper formally defines the DER's bending (α) and twisting (β) stiffness moduli in terms of physical material parameters: α = EI (Young's modulus E, second moment of area I) and β = GJ_T (shear modulus G, torsion constant J_T).
3. **MuJoCo Integration:**
 - **DLO Representation:** Within MuJoCo, the DLO is modeled as a chain of discrete capsules connected by ball joints, allowing rotational but no translational motion between segments.
 - **Constraints Handling:** MuJoCo's native constraint solver is utilized to ensure inequality constraints are respected, solving a convex optimization problem at each step.
 - **Program Integration:** The model is integrated using MuJoCo's Python bindings (with a C++ plugin also provided). At each time step, an `update_torque` function calculates centerline forces based on updated twist angles and node positions. These forces are then converted into equivalent joint torques and applied to the DLO's ball joints via the `qfrc_passive` variable.

**B. Adapted Model in Generalized Coordinates (Key Innovation)**

- **Force-Lever Analysis:** To minimize computational load, the Cartesian stiffness forces from the DER theory are converted into joint torques through moment-based force-lever analysis. This conversion is feasible because the net stiffness forces on the DER system are zero, and discrete sections are connected by ball joints.
- **Torque Calculation:** The torque contribution $\vec{\tau}_{i,j}$ at joint *i* due due to a stiffness force $\vec{F}_j$ applied at joint *j* is calculated as $\vec{F}_j \times \vec{X}_{i,j}$, where $\vec{X}_{i,j}$ is the vector from joint *i* to *j*. The total torque $\vec{T}_i$ on joint *i* is the sum of these contributions from all forces.
- **Kinematic Tree Adaptation:** MuJoCo's kinematic tree structure (parent to child) means torques are applied from piece *i* to *i+1*. The conversion for torques acting in the opposite direction is handled with a negative sign.
- **Quasistatic Twist Treatment:** The model adopts the DER theory's quasistatic treatment of the material frame twist. This approach aims to improve stability and prevent unnatural twist wave oscillations, which can occur with dynamic twist models.

**C. Performance Evaluation**

1. **Computational Speed:** The computational time for three DLO models—the adapted model, MuJoCo's native cable model, and a direct application of DER Cartesian forces—were compared against a plain kinematic chain model. Tests were conducted on an Intel i5-12400 CPU, measuring the real computation time to simulate one second, with varying numbers of discrete rod sections (n) over 10,000 time steps at 0.0015 s per step.
2. **Accuracy Validation (Theoretical):**
 - **Localized Helical Buckling:** This test involved a straight isotropic rod where one end is twisted and the ends are brought closer. The helix envelope of the deformed rod was compared against an analytical solution, evaluating the adapted and native models for different *n*.
 - **Michell’s Buckling Instability:** This test involved an elastic rod loop subjected to increasing twist along its tangent axis until buckling occurs. The critical twist angle was compared against an analytical solution, evaluating both models for varying β/α ratios with fixed α and *n*=50.

**D. Parameter Identification Pipeline**

A novel, two-step parameter identification pipeline was developed using a simple experimental setup:

1. **Experimental Setup:**
 - A Custom Twist Apparatus (CTA) held the DLO ends, affixed to a tripod.
 - An Azure Kinect depth camera captured 3D DLO positions.
 - Green markers were placed along the DLO, and a white sheet provided background contrast.
 - Three distinct DLOs were used: white (silicone rubber), black (copper internals, PVC body), and red (copper internals, PVC body, nylon braided cover).

2. **Bending Stiffness (α) Identification:**
 - **Procedure:** The CTA was set to 0° twist, allowing the DLO to hang in a loop. The depth camera captured N=50 nodes of the DLO. A golden-section search algorithm minimized the 2-norm error between real and simulated node positions to estimate the bending stiffness modulus α*.
 - **Evaluation:** The experiment was repeated 5 times. The suitability of the method for each DLO was assessed by calculating the normalized parameter cloud size, S_bend, which measures the consistency of the identified α values across repeats.

3. **Twisting Stiffness (β/α Ratio) Identification:**
 - **Procedure:** Twist was introduced to the CTA in 5° increments until buckling occurred (defined as the first instance of self-collision after the loop folds into itself), determining the real critical twist angle (θ_c,real). A bi-section algorithm then found the β/α ratio in simulation that matched θ_c,real, using the previously identified average α.
 - **Evaluation:** This experiment was also repeated 5 times, with the twist returned to 0° between repeats. The consistency of the identified β/α ratios was evaluated using the normalized parameter cloud size, S_twist.

**E. Real Experiments for Model Evaluation**

- **Setup:** A 0.40 m DLO had one end fixed by the CTA (with adjustable axial rotation) and the other manipulated into 4 distinct poses by a Denso VS-060 robot arm.
- **Comparison:** Real DLO shapes (detected using the Azure Kinect and green markers) were compared with simulated shapes from both the adapted and native MuJoCo models, using the stiffness parameters identified in the previous step. The DLOs were discretized into 10 sections for this comparison.
- **Metric:** Performance was quantified using the normalized position error, calculated as the average 2-norm Euclidean error across 11 ordered points along the DLO.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study yielded several findings regarding computational performance, model accuracy, and the effectiveness of the proposed parameter identification pipeline.

**A. Simulation Speeds**

- **Adapted Model Efficiency:** The adapted model exhibited a small increase in computational time compared to the plain kinematic chain model, ranging from -1% to 3%. This indicates its computational efficiency and suitability for integration into MuJoCo.
- **Comparison with Direct DER:** The adapted model significantly reduced computational load compared to the direct application of DER Cartesian forces, which showed a 2% to 15% increase in computational time. This difference was attributed to the adapted model bypassing the need for *n+1* distinct Jacobian queries at each simulation step.
- **Native Model Speed:** MuJoCo's native cable model was marginally faster than the adapted model, with a computational time increase ranging from -3% to 2%. However, this speed advantage was observed to come at the expense of accuracy.

**B. Accuracy Validation**

1. **Localized Helical Buckling Test:**
 - **Adapted Model Performance:** The adapted model consistently converged towards the analytical solution as the number of discrete rod sections (*n*) increased. It generally exhibited smaller average errors compared to the native model. The improved stability, attributed to its quasistatic treatment of centerline twist, contributed to more consistent convergence and reduced unnatural twist wave oscillations.
 - **Native Model Performance:** The native model showed larger and less consistent average errors, particularly at lower *n* values, and did not converge as reliably to the analytical solution.

2. **Michell’s Buckling Instability Test:**
 - **Adapted Model Performance:** The adapted model's average error (0.0483) was approximately an order of magnitude smaller than that of the native model (0.7725). Its results were significantly closer to the analytical solution for critical buckling angles across different β/α ratios.
 - **Native Model Performance:** The native model's data suggested a linear relationship between β/α and the critical twist angle, which is inconsistent with the theoretical behavior of an elastic rod. This indicated a fundamental limitation in its ability to accurately represent twist-induced buckling.

**C. Parameter Identification Pipeline**

1. **Consistency Evaluation (Parameter Cloud Size):**
 - **Bending Stiffness:** The white DLO (silicone rubber) demonstrated the highest consistency in bending stiffness identification, with the smallest normalized parameter cloud size (S_bend = 2.182%). The black DLO (6.064%) and red DLO (7.553%) showed progressively larger cloud sizes, indicating less consistency.
 - **Twisting Stiffness:** Similarly, the white DLO exhibited the highest consistency for twisting stiffness identification (S_twist = 0.155%), followed by the black (1.625%) and red (2.297%) DLOs.
 - **Suitability:** The results suggest the pipeline is most suitable for DLOs exhibiting more elastic behaviors, with the white DLO showing the least plastic deformation and the highest suitability.
2. **Pipeline Characteristics:** The proposed pipeline was described as being more cost-effective and simpler to implement than existing engineering or learning-based approaches, requiring minimal equipment. It provides a "warm start" estimate for learning techniques, potentially reducing the need for extensive real training data.

**D. Real Experiments**

- **Overall Performance:** The adapted model generally demonstrated superior shape prediction performance across all three DLOs and four different robot poses when compared to MuJoCo's native model.
- **Error Magnitudes:** For the adapted model, the normalized position error (average 2-norm Euclidean error per node) was consistently below 5% of the total DLO length, indicating a high degree of accuracy. The white DLO, characterized by greater elasticity, showed the smallest position errors across all poses.
- **Native Model Limitations in Real Experiments:** The native model exhibited instances of "spiked position error" and significant shape discrepancies in certain poses (e.g., black DLO in pose 2, red DLO in pose 4). These inaccuracies were attributed to unnatural twist wave oscillations inherent in the native simulation, leading to unrealistic deformations (e.g., loops forming in incorrect locations or exaggerated protrusions). The adapted model successfully mitigated these issues.

In summary, the adapted DER model provides a balance of computational efficiency and accuracy, outperforming the native MuJoCo cable model in theoretical validation and real-world shape prediction. The parameter identification pipeline offers a practical and consistent method for determining DLO stiffness properties.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents an accurate and computationally efficient simulation model for Deformable Linear Objects (DLOs) integrated into a widely used robot physics simulator, MuJoCo, alongside a novel parameter identification pipeline. These contributions have several significant implications and potential impacts:

1. **Enhanced Robotic Manipulation of DLOs:** The primary significance lies in providing a more reliable simulation environment for research in robotic manipulation of DLOs. Accurate simulations are crucial for developing and testing control strategies, particularly for complex tasks like knot tying, suturing, or cable routing. By accurately modeling DLO dynamics, this work can accelerate the development of robust robotic systems capable of handling such objects.

2. **Facilitation of Machine Learning Approaches:** The availability of a high-fidelity DLO simulator directly addresses a key challenge in machine learning for robotics: the need for large, diverse datasets. The adapted model can generate synthetic data that closely mimics real-world DLO behavior, reducing the reliance on tedious and sometimes infeasible real-world data collection. This can lead to faster training times, more generalizable policies, and a reduction in the "sim-to-real" gap, where policies learned in simulation fail to transfer effectively to physical robots.

3. **Accessible and Practical Parameter Identification:** The introduction of a simple, accurate, and low-cost parameter identification pipeline is a practical contribution. Researchers can easily set up the necessary apparatus (using 3D-printable designs provided by the authors) and utilize standard depth cameras to determine the bending and twisting stiffness of various DLOs. This democratizes access to accurate DLO characterization, which was previously a more complex or resource-intensive process. The pipeline's ability to provide a "warm start" estimate for stiffness parameters can further optimize more advanced, data-driven identification methods.

4. **Addressing Limitations of Existing Simulators:** The work highlights and overcomes specific limitations of existing general-purpose physics engines, such as MuJoCo's native cable model. By demonstrating that the native model can exhibit inaccuracies like unnatural twist wave oscillations and inconsistent stiffness representation, the paper contributes to improving the realism and physical fidelity of robotic simulations. The adapted model's use of generalized coordinates and quasistatic twist treatment provides a robust solution to these issues, leading to more stable and accurate DLO behavior in simulation.

5. **Foundation for Future Research and Applications:** This research lays a foundation for future advancements. The open-source nature of the DLO model and apparatus designs encourages further development and adoption within the robotics community. Potential future work includes demonstrating the model's effectiveness in end-to-end robotic manipulation tasks, refining the parameter identification pipeline with automated perception, and extending the model to simulate DLOs experiencing plastic deformation, which is relevant for many industrial processes.

In summary, this research advances the state of the art in DLO simulation and characterization, providing essential tools that can significantly impact the progress of robotic manipulation in various domains, from manufacturing to healthcare.
