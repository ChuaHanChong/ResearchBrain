# Where to Touch, How to Contact: Hierarchical RL-MPC Framework for Geometry-Aware Long-Horizon Dexterous Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> A hierarchical reinforcement learning (RL) and model predictive control (MPC) framework addresses contact-rich robotic manipulation by decoupling geometric reasoning from contact dynamics through a "contact intention" interface. This approach enables robust zero-shot sim-to-real transfer and demonstrates significant data efficiency improvements, achieving 100% success on seen pushing tasks and 95% on unseen ones in real-world experiments.

## Problem

- Existing robotic manipulation systems struggle to jointly reason about global geometry and non-smooth contact dynamics, particularly in contact-rich tasks.
- End-to-end learning policies are data-inefficient and face challenges in zero-shot sim-to-real transfer due to implicitly learning complex contact dynamics.
- Model-based optimization methods, like MPC, often suffer from short planning horizons, leading to local optima and poor generalization for long-horizon or geometrically diverse tasks.

## Method

- A ==hierarchical framework== integrates a high-level ==Reinforcement Learning (RL)== policy with a low-level ==Model Predictive Control (MPC)== operating at different frequencies.
- The high-level RL policy, trained in an ==abstract simulation==, predicts an object-centric ==contact intention== comprising desired contact locations and a post-contact object subgoal pose.
- A low-level ==contact-implicit MPC (ComFree-MPC)== then executes this contact intention by planning precise robot actions to realize the specified contacts and achieve the subgoal.

## Results

- The framework achieved **100%** success for seen letter shapes and **99.69%** for unseen letter shapes in abstract simulation after approximately **40,000** RL steps, indicating high data efficiency.
- Policies trained in abstract simulation transferred zero-shot to real-world hardware, achieving **100%** success for seen pushing tasks, **95%** for unseen pushing, and **100%** for 3D reorientation on a Franka robot arm.
- The hierarchical approach demonstrated a **40x** reduction in RL decision steps and higher success rates compared to end-to-end RL baselines, while MPC-only baselines consistently failed in long-horizon tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decoupling geometric planning from physical contact dynamics into a hierarchical structure simplifies the overall manipulation problem, making each sub-problem more tractable.
> - An object-centric "contact intention" interface serves as an effective abstraction, enabling high data efficiency for the RL policy and robust zero-shot sim-to-real transfer by focusing on geometry and kinematics.
> - Explicitly guiding the low-level MPC with high-level object subgoals helps overcome the short-sightedness of purely model-based planners and prevents them from getting stuck in local optima.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation, particularly dexterous and contact-rich manipulation, presents a significant challenge in requiring robots to jointly reason about global geometry and non-smooth contact dynamics. Recent advancements have largely focused on two main paradigms: end-to-end policies and model-based optimization.

End-to-end policies, often trained using reinforcement learning (RL) or imitation learning, infer low-level motor commands directly from robot observations. While these methods have demonstrated performance in specific tasks, they typically suffer from data inefficiency, requiring extensive training data, and exhibit difficulties in transferring learned policies from simulation to real-world environments (the sim-to-real gap). This is partly attributed to their monolithic nature, attempting to learn complex contact dynamics implicitly.

In contrast, model-based optimization approaches, such as Model Predictive Control (MPC) and trajectory optimization, are designed to explicitly reason over contact dynamics. These methods can discover contact modes through optimization. However, they are often characterized by a short planning horizon, which can lead to local optima, and may not generalize effectively across diverse geometric scenarios or long-horizon tasks without high-level guidance.

The presented work positions itself by drawing inspiration from the hierarchical nature of human manipulation, where high-level geometric decisions (e.g., "where to touch") are decoupled from low-level physical execution (e.g., "how to move through contact"). This research aims to bridge the gap between data-intensive end-to-end learning and potentially short-sighted model-based planning by proposing a hierarchical framework.

Previous hybrid RL-MPC architectures in robotics have utilized RL for tasks such as inferring latent dynamics of deformable objects, providing warm-starts for MPC solvers, or predicting intermediate end-effector goals for tracking controllers. The distinguishing feature of the current work is its specific allocation of reasoning: RL is dedicated to geometric reasoning, while MPC is dedicated to contact-dynamics reasoning, mediated by a "contact intention" interface.

In the domain of non-prehensile manipulation, which involves manipulating objects without grasping, earlier work primarily focused on motion primitives, facing scalability issues. More recent methods involve contact-implicit planning or learning-based approaches. This work differentiates itself from other hierarchical non-prehensile methods, such as HACMan, by explicitly offloading the complex, high-variance contact dynamics reasoning to a model-based MPC, allowing the RL policy to focus solely on higher-level geometric decision-making. The approach also relates to affordance-based manipulation by dynamically predicting contact locations, but extends beyond simple affordance prediction by coupling these decisions with a physics-based planner for adaptive, physically grounded motions.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the inherent difficulties in contact-rich dexterous manipulation, which necessitates simultaneous consideration of global geometric understanding and the intricacies of non-smooth contact dynamics. The paper identifies limitations in existing robotic manipulation paradigms:

1. **Data Inefficiency and Sim-to-Real Gap in End-to-End Policies:** End-to-end approaches, while powerful, often require substantial amounts of data for training and frequently encounter challenges in transferring learned policies from simulated environments to real-world deployment. This is hypothesized to be due to the implicit learning of complex, non-smooth contact dynamics, which may not generalize well.
2. **Short-Sightedness and Local Optima in Model-Based Optimization:** Pure model-based optimization methods, such as MPC and trajectory optimization, can explicitly reason about contact dynamics. However, their planning horizons are often limited, making them susceptible to local optima and less effective for long-horizon tasks that require strategic, geometry-aware global planning.

Building upon an analogy to human manipulation, the authors propose that dexterous manipulation is intrinsically hierarchical. Humans first make high-level decisions regarding *where* to contact an object (geometric reasoning) and *what* object-level outcome to achieve, followed by low-level decisions on *how* to execute these plans through anticipated contact dynamics (e.g., rolling, sliding, sticking).

The key objectives of this work are:

- **To propose a hierarchical RL–MPC framework:** This framework aims to explicitly decouple geometric reasoning from contact dynamics planning and control. The high-level component (Reinforcement Learning) is designed to make strategic decisions about interaction locations and desired intermediate object states, while the low-level component (Model Predictive Control) is responsible for the precise execution of these decisions through contact dynamics.
- **To develop a "contact intention" interface:** This novel, object-centric interface serves as the output of the high-level RL policy, specifying (i) precise contact locations on the object's surface and (ii) a post-contact object subgoal pose. This abstraction is intended to make the RL problem more tractable by focusing on geometry and kinematics rather than directly inferring low-level motor commands.
- **To achieve high data efficiency:** By delegating the computationally intensive and complex contact dynamics reasoning to the MPC, the RL policy can focus on learning geometry-aware, long-horizon contact plans more efficiently.
- **To enable robust zero-shot sim-to-real transfer:** The contact intention interface is designed to decouple geometric reasoning (which transfers more reliably) from low-level contact dynamics (which are sensitive to simulation inaccuracies). This decoupling aims to make policies trained in simulation directly deployable in the real world without further fine-tuning.
- **To facilitate geometry-aware exploration across diverse object shapes:** The framework aims to be applicable to various objects and tasks, moving beyond single-object or fixed-geometry scenarios.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology involves a hierarchical RL–MPC framework that partitions the manipulation problem into distinct high-level geometric reasoning and low-level contact dynamics reasoning components.

#### 3.1. High-Level Reinforcement Learning (RL) for Geometry Reasoning

The high-level policy, trained using RL, is responsible for predicting a "Contact Intention," which is an object-centric interface.

- **Contact Intention Definition:** A contact intention $I$ is defined as a tuple $(\bar{c}_1, \dots, \bar{c}_n, \bar{q}_{\text{obj}})$, where $(\bar{c}_1, \dots, \bar{c}_n)$ are specific contact locations on the object surface for $n$ active robot contact points, and $\bar{q}_{\text{obj}}$ is a post-contact object subgoal pose. For the tasks considered in this paper, $n=1$, meaning a single stick-like end-effector. The subgoal $\bar{q}_{\text{obj}}$ represents an intermediate object state intended to facilitate progress towards the final task objective.

- **RL Observation Space Design:** The high-level RL policy receives a tri-component observation $o_k = \langle P, \Delta P_k, D_k \rangle$, represented in an object-canonical frame to promote geometry-aware reasoning independent of the object's world pose.
 - **Geometry Component ($P$):** A set of $N$ keypoints uniformly sampled from the object surface. These keypoints approximate the object's geometry and serve as discrete candidates for contact location selection.
 - **Target Component ($\Delta P_k$):** The object-frame keypoint goal flow, defined as the displacement of each keypoint from the current object pose towards the target pose. This component encodes task progress relative to the object's geometry.
 - **Collision Component ($D_k$):** Per-keypoint clearance distances to the environment, indicating the feasibility of making contact at specific keypoints by identifying regions blocked by environmental constraints.

- **RL Action Space Design for Object Subgoal:** To manage the complexity of predicting subgoals in the continuous SE(3) space, two options are proposed:
 - **Option 1: Subgoal Re-parameterization using MPC Weights:** The RL policy predicts the contact locations and a pair of weights $(w_{\text{pos}}, w_{\text{ori}})$ that implicitly define the object subgoal within the low-level MPC's terminal cost function. This guides the MPC to prioritize positional or orientational accuracy towards the final target.
 - **Option 2: Selecting from a Pre-defined Discrete Subgoal Candidate Set:** For tasks with well-defined intermediate states, the RL policy selects a specific object pose from a pre-defined discrete set of subgoal candidates.

- **Policy Architecture:**
 - **PointNet++ Backbone:** Utilized for tasks employing Option 1, this architecture features a dual-branch design: a geometric processing branch for per-keypoint feature extraction (segmentation-style) and a global kinematic processing branch for object-level embedding (classification-style).
 - **MLP Backbone:** A lightweight alternative used for tasks employing Option 2, processing flattened input features through shared fully connected layers with separate heads for contact point and subgoal selection.

- **Training Details:**
 - The high-level policy is trained in an abstract-robot simulation, where the robot's end-effector is simplified to a mass–spring–damper point. This abstraction is intended to accelerate learning by reducing the computational cost of low-level MPC solving.
 - The training algorithm used is Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO).
 - The reward function is composite, combining negative dense keypoint flow, a sparse target-reaching reward, and a negative penalty for infeasible contact locations.
 - Domain randomization (object mass, friction, actuation gains) is applied during training to enhance robustness and sim-to-real transfer.

#### 3.2. Low-Level Contact-Implicit Model Predictive Control (MPC)

The low-level component is responsible for executing the contact intention provided by the high-level policy.

- **Controller:** A complementarity-free model predictive control (ComFree-MPC) is employed. This controller enables high-speed receding-horizon planning and control (approximately 100Hz) by reasoning over various contact modes (e.g., sliding, sticking, breaking).
- **Optimization Problem:** Conditioned on the contact intention $I_k$, the ComFree-MPC minimizes an objective function over a prediction horizon $H$.
 - **Running Cost ($L$):** Encourages the robot's active contact point(s) to approach and maintain proximity to the contact location(s) specified in $I_k$.
 - **Terminal Cost ($V$):** Guides the object towards the subgoal pose $\bar{q}_{\text{obj},k}$ (either directly from $I_k$ or implicitly defined by MPC weights).
 - **Constraints:** The optimization is subject to complementarity-free contact dynamics, which model the system's state evolution under contact.
- **Execution:** At each control step, ComFree-MPC plans $H$ steps ahead, and only the first computed robot action is executed.

#### 3.3. Multi-Rate RL–MPC Coupling

The two levels operate at different frequencies. The contact intention generated by the high-level RL policy remains fixed for $T$ environment steps, during which the low-level ComFree-MPC continuously executes and re-plans at a higher rate. When switching contact intentions, a heuristic is used for free-space robot movement to reposition the end-effector.

#### 3.4. Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer

A key aspect of the methodology is the claim of zero-shot sim-to-real transfer. The high-level RL policy, trained exclusively in the abstract-robot simulation, is directly deployed on real-world hardware. This process only requires integrating a robot-specific ComFree-MPC and minimal tuning of physical parameters (e.g., mass, stiffness) to match the hardware characteristics, without any additional training or fine-tuning of the RL policy itself.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The framework was evaluated on three non-prehensile manipulation tasks: geometry-generalized pushing, pivoting/flipping-based object reorientation, and environment-assisted object repositioning. Evaluations were conducted in abstract-robot simulation, full-robot simulation, and real-world hardware.

#### 4.1. Training Performance (Abstract-Robot Simulation)

The high-level RL policy demonstrated high success rates with relatively few RL steps:
- **Geometry-generalized pushing:** Achieved 100% success for seen letter shapes and 99.69% for unseen letter shapes after approximately 40,000 RL steps.
- **Pivoting-flipping based object reorientation:** Reached 96.56% success after about 100,000 RL steps.
- **Environment-assisted object repositioning:** Achieved 98.44% success after approximately 80,000 RL steps.
The results indicate that the method learned efficient long-horizon contact plans using a comparatively small amount of training data.

#### 4.2. Zero-Shot Transfer to Full Robotic Systems (Simulation)

Upon transferring the trained RL policies to a full robot-arm simulation environment, performance was largely maintained:
- Success rates remained at or above 95% across all tasks (100% for pushing, 95% for 3D reorientation and environment-assisted repositioning).
- Tasks were completed with a low number of high-level RL decision steps, averaging approximately 10 steps per task.

#### 4.3. Baseline Comparisons (Abstract End-Effector Simulation)

Comparisons were conducted against end-to-end RL, HACMan-style policies, and an MPC-only baseline.
- **End-to-end RL:** For a T-pushing task, the end-to-end policy converged slowly, reaching 83.44% success after approximately 600,000 RL steps. The proposed hierarchical method achieved 100% success in 15,000 RL steps, demonstrating a 40x improvement in RL decision steps and approximately 2x improvement in total control steps.
- **HACMan-style policy:** In the pivoting-flipping object reorientation task, the HACMan-style policy required around 300,000 RL steps to achieve 96.25% success. The proposed method reached 96.56% success within 200,000 steps, indicating faster convergence and smaller performance variance.
- **MPC-only baseline:** This baseline consistently failed (0% success rate) in the simulated T-pushing task. This outcome suggests that short-sighted model-based planning alone is insufficient for long-horizon, complex manipulation without high-level guidance.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies

Ablation studies were conducted to evaluate the contribution of specific components:
- **Removing Object Subgoal from Contact Intention:** When the high-level policy predicted only contact locations (without subgoals), the success rate in the geometry-generalized pushing task dropped from 100% to 20.62%. This indicates the critical role of subgoal selection for efficient manipulation and for guiding the MPC away from local minima.
- **World-Frame Observation:** Expressing the target component ($\Delta P$) in the world frame instead of the object frame for the geometry-generalized pushing task resulted in learning failure. This highlights the importance of a consistent object-centric representation for stable and geometry-aware policy learning.
- **Removing Collision Component from Observation:** Excluding the per-keypoint clearance distances from the observation in the pivoting-flipping object reorientation task led to a decrease in both learning speed and final success rate (from 98.75% to 75.00%). This component is demonstrated to be important for identifying feasible contact locations and leveraging environment-object interactions.

#### 4.5. Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer to Hardware

The high-level RL policy trained in abstract simulation was directly transferred to a Franka robot arm without fine-tuning, demonstrating robust performance:
- **Pushing (seen):** Achieved 100% success (e.g., letter E, H, L, N, T, X, Y, Z).
- **Pushing (unseen):** Achieved 95% success. Failures were attributed to occasional tracking issues (e.g., letter I).
- **3D Reorientation:** Achieved 100% success, although it required more RL decision steps on average compared to pushing.
- **Environment-assisted object repositioning:** Achieved 88% success, with the lower rate partially attributed to vision tracking errors in the real environment.
- These results demonstrate the framework's capability for robust zero-shot sim-to-real transfer with high success rates across diverse tasks.

#### 4.6. Qualitative Observations (Real-World Deployment)

The real-world experiments showed the emergence of diverse and task-appropriate contact strategies:
- **Object reorientation:** The robot demonstrated frictional sliding on the object's top face, pivoting at edges, and pivoting at corners to reorient the cube.
- **Environment-assisted repositioning:** The robot effectively utilized environmental features, such as stair edges and table surfaces, by sliding, pivoting, nudging, and toppling the object to achieve the target flip pose.

#### 4.7. Real-World Baseline Comparison

In real-world experiments on the pushing-T task, the end-to-end baseline achieved a 35% success rate, with common failures including excessive pushing or failure to establish contact. The MPC-only baseline achieved 0% success, often getting stuck mid-execution due to myopic planning. These real-world results corroborate the simulated findings regarding the advantages of the hierarchical approach.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a hierarchical RL–MPC framework that offers a method for addressing critical challenges in contact-rich dexterous manipulation. The key contributions and potential impacts are:

1. **Effective Decoupling of Reasoning:** The framework explicitly separates high-level geometric reasoning (deciding *where* and *what subgoal*) from low-level contact dynamics planning (deciding *how* to execute). This decomposition aligns with observations of human manipulation and facilitates a more tractable learning problem for robots.

2. **Increased Data Efficiency:** By delegating the complex, non-smooth contact dynamics to a model-based MPC, the high-level RL policy can focus on learning geometry-aware, long-horizon plans with significantly less data. The reported 10x reduction in data compared to end-to-end baselines, and a 40x reduction in RL decision steps for specific tasks, represents a substantial improvement in training efficiency. This can accelerate the development and deployment of manipulation skills.

3. **Robust Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The "contact intention" interface, by decoupling geometry and kinematics (which transfer reliably) from intricate contact dynamics (which are sensitive to sim-to-real discrepancies), enables policies trained entirely in simulation to perform robustly on real hardware without any fine-tuning. This capability can drastically reduce the cost and complexity of real-world robot training and deployment.

4. **Enhanced Global Planning and Robustness:** The high-level RL policy provides geometry-aware contact locations and object subgoals, guiding the low-level MPC towards effective long-horizon plans and preventing it from getting trapped in local optima, a common limitation of purely model-based approaches. This leads to more robust performance and lower variance compared to RL-only hierarchical baselines.

5. **Generalizability Across Diverse Geometries and Tasks:** The object-centric observation space and the abstract nature of contact intentions enable the policy to generalize across diverse object shapes (e.g., various letters) and different types of non-prehensile tasks (pushing, reorientation, environment-assisted repositioning).

6. **Emergence of Diverse Contact Strategies:** The framework allows for the emergence of varied and effective contact strategies in real-world scenarios, demonstrating its ability to exploit complex physical interactions to achieve task goals.

**Potential Limitations Acknowledged by the Authors:**

- **Reliance on Accurate Object Pose Estimation:** The framework's performance is dependent on the accuracy and stability of real-time object pose estimation. Errors in perception could degrade the effectiveness of both the high-level geometric reasoning and the low-level MPC.
- **Scalability with Multiple End-Effectors:** The use of a discrete keypoint set for contact location selection might face scalability challenges when dealing with multiple end-effectors due to the combinatorial growth of the action space. This suggests potential areas for future research in action space design for multi-fingered or multi-arm manipulation.

In conclusion, this research presents a method for efficient and robust robot manipulation by leveraging a hierarchical architecture that capitalizes on the strengths of both reinforcement learning for strategic planning and model predictive control for dynamic execution, with demonstrated benefits in data efficiency, sim-to-real transfer, and task generalizability.
