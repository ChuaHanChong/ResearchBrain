# TacPAC: Tactile Prediction and Real-Time Action Correction in World-Action Models for Contact-Rich Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> TacPAC is a world-action model for contact-rich manipulation that turns tactile prediction into real-time action correction. After the base Mixture-of-Transformers model plans an action chunk, it caches the predicted contact and plan representation; a single-pass tactile expert reads each new tactile image against that cache and emits delta actions for the unexecuted suffix. Average real-robot success rises from 22% to 64%, with corrections 20.7x cheaper than replanning.

## Problem

- Vision-centric world-action models predict future frames but miss local contact cues like incipient slip, deformation, and small misalignments that decide contact-rich tasks.
- Naively predicting future tactile frames as extra views recovers only about a third of achievable gain, since informative tactile transitions are temporally sparse.
- Predictions are fixed before a chunk executes while tactile feedback arrives during it, so prior models cannot revise actions already in flight.

## Method

- Base model uses a ==Mixture-of-Transformers== with video and action experts under ==conditional flow matching==, with an ==asymmetric mask== so action queries attend to predicted visual and tactile tokens.
- A ==per-chunk cache== stores per-layer ==attention keys and values== for tactile views and action tokens at flow time zero, encoding the predicted contact and the committed plan.
- A ==tactile expert==, initialized from the action expert and conditioned on execution offset, reads each new tactile image against the cache and outputs ==delta actions== applied to unexecuted steps.

## Results

- TacPAC averages **64%** success on five real-robot tasks, beating the strongest baseline T-Rex at **48%**, Dream-Tac at **45%**, and vision-only or reactive visuo-tactile methods at **22-36%**.
- Ablations: vision-only **22%**, prediction-only **37%**, reactive correction without prediction **33%**, correction without the cache **47%**, confirming the cache contributes a **17-point** gain.
- One correction pass takes **30.4 ms** (**32.90 Hz**), **20.7x** faster than the **628.6 ms** chunk regeneration; only **1.6%** of calls lagged a control step.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Sensory prediction pays off mainly as a reference for interpreting live feedback, not as an extra output head; expectation-relative comparison beats absolute sensing.
> - Reactive tactile correction without a predicted expectation can hurt, scoring below vision-only on damage-sensitive tasks because uncontextualized feedback is ambiguous.
> - Caching a frozen plan's attention keys makes closed-loop correction cheap enough for sensor-rate control, and delta-on-original updates avoid compounding correction error.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation has seen advancements with the emergence of vision-language-action (VLA) policies and world-action models (WAMs). VLA policies typically map multimodal context (e.g., vision, language) directly to action sequences, while WAMs additionally predict future observations to inform action generation. However, a limitation of many existing WAMs and VLA policies is their predominant reliance on visual observations. While visual information provides essential global context, it often fails to resolve local contact cues critical for contact-rich manipulation tasks, such as precise alignment during insertion, incipient slip, or delicate force regulation. These local cues, including object deformation or subtle misalignments, can be obscured in visual streams or are inherently non-visual.

Tactile sensing directly measures these physical interactions and has demonstrated value in various manipulation policies. Prior work has explored using tactile sensors as a present-time signal for reactive control, such as tactile servoing or visuo-tactile policies, or conditioning VLAs on current tactile feedback. More recently, attempts have been made to integrate tactile information into predictive models, treating tactile images as additional views to be predicted alongside future video. While this approach has shown some improvements, the present paper identifies a significant gap: predicting future tactile observations alone captures only a fraction of the potential gains offered by tactile sensing.

The identified shortfall stems from two main issues: the temporal sparsity of informative tactile changes (meaning critical contact events are infrequent but crucial) and a fundamental timing mismatch. Predictions are made *before* an action chunk is executed, but vital tactile feedback arrives *during* the execution. This means a pre-planned action sequence, conditioned on a fixed prediction, cannot dynamically adapt to real-time discrepancies between anticipated and actual contact. Previous tactile world-action models have either focused on tactile prediction without real-time correction (e.g., Dream-Tac, committing to a chunk in a single pass) or on real-time tactile refinement without a predictive expectation to compare against (e.g., T-Rex, refining an already-generated chunk from live tactile).

TacPAC addresses this specific gap by integrating real-time action correction that is *grounded in prior tactile predictions*. It proposes a mechanism where the robot's plan, which is conditioned on predicted contact, also serves as a reference against which actual tactile feedback is interpreted. This approach positions TacPAC as an advancement in WAMs, moving beyond purely visual predictions and reactive tactile control to a system that leverages predicted contact as an explicit expectation for dynamic, closed-loop adjustment during contact-rich tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research is to enhance the capabilities of robotic systems in performing contact-rich manipulation tasks, which are often challenging for existing world-action models (WAMs) and vision-language-action (VLA) policies. The paper identifies a fundamental limitation in these models: their reliance on vision, which fails to capture critical local contact cues such as deformation, incipient slip, or small misalignments, all of which are crucial for successful physical interaction.

While integrating tactile sensing and predicting future tactile observations within WAMs is a logical step, the authors found that this approach only yields a limited improvement in task success, recovering approximately one-third of the potential benefit. This observation motivated a deeper investigation into the underlying causes of this inefficiency, leading to two key insights:
1. **Temporal Sparsity of Informative Tactile Changes:** Critical tactile events are often sparse and transient, occurring only at specific moments of interaction. The general objective of predicting an entire future tactile stream may not adequately prioritize or sensitize the action generation process to these highly informative, yet rare, contact transitions. Much of the prediction objective is dominated by frames where tactile input remains largely static.
2. **Timing Mismatch between Prediction and Execution:** WAMs generate predictions of future observations *before* an action chunk is executed, and the planned action chunk is conditioned on these predictions. However, real-time tactile feedback, which contains the most relevant information for correcting errors, arrives *during* the execution of that action chunk. A prediction that is fixed once the action chunk is planned cannot be revised or used to dynamically correct actions that are already in flight but not yet completed. This temporal decoupling prevents the model from acting upon observed discrepancies as they arise.

Based on these motivations, the key objectives of this work are articulated as follows:
- **To identify and articulate the practical limitation** that tactile-predictive WAMs face: specifically, that fixing predicted future tactile observations once an action chunk is planned leaves a significant portion of the achievable gain from tactile sensing unrealized.
- **To introduce TacPAC, a novel framework** that transforms tactile prediction into real-time action correction. This involves caching the predicted contact (that the planned action chunk was conditioned upon) alongside the plan itself. This enables current tactile feedback to be interpreted not in isolation, but in relation to what the plan had anticipated.
- **To develop and evaluate a single-pass tactile corrector.** This corrector is designed to be efficient enough to operate in real-time, trained on the base model's own plans at densely sampled execution offsets. The objective is to demonstrate its effectiveness across a suite of contact-rich manipulation tasks on a real robot.

The overarching goal is to achieve more robust and adaptive contact-rich manipulation by establishing a direct and dynamic link between predicted contact expectations and real-time tactile observations, allowing for immediate and context-aware action adjustments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

TacPAC is developed upon a tactile-predictive world-action model, which incorporates a Mixture-of-Transformers (MoT) architecture comprising a video expert and an action expert, augmented with a specialized tactile expert for real-time correction.

**Problem Formulation:**
The policy takes a task instruction, current visual observation, proprioceptive state, and raw tactile images `I_t` as input. Tactile sensor images are treated as additional views, integrated into a single video stream `z`. The policy generates an action chunk `ˆa` of horizon `H` along with future visual and tactile observations. Conditional flow matching is utilized for both observation and action generation, where future observation latents and the action chunk are generated from noise, while the first frame is kept clean.

**Base Model Architecture (Video Expert and Action Expert):**
The base model uses an MoT architecture (Liang et al., 2025). The video expert predicts future visual and tactile observations, while the action expert attends to these predictions to generate action chunks. Layer-wise attention interactions occur over a concatenated token sequence. An asymmetric mask ensures that action queries attend to all video and action tokens, but video queries only attend to video tokens. This setup conditions the planned action chunk on the contact the model predicts its actions will produce, as tactile tokens are part of both the generated observations and the context for action generation. This initial model, without the tactile expert, is termed the base model, yielding a planned action chunk `ˆa`.

**Prediction-Grounded Tactile Correction (TacPAC Extension):**
The core innovation of TacPAC is to leverage the base model's predicted contact for real-time action correction.
- **Motivation for Caching:** The correction mechanism needs to understand not only what is currently being sensed but also what *should have been sensed* according to the original plan. The base model's joint denoising of the action chunk and predicted future tactile stream provides this expectation.
- **Caching the Prediction and the Plan:** After the base model completes its denoising schedule and generates `ˆa`, an additional pass is executed at flow time `τ=0` over the denoised video latent and `ˆa`. The per-layer attention keys and values generated for the tactile views (encoding predicted contact) and action tokens (encoding the planned chunk) are stored in a cache `C`. This cache, representing a clean prediction and plan representation, is built once per chunk and reused for subsequent corrections. Visual keys are not stored, as their changes are slower and already implicitly carried by the tactile and action tokens.
- **Tactile Expert:** This component is a single-pass corrector. When a new tactile image `I_m` arrives (after `m` actions of the chunk have been executed), the tactile expert's query sequence consists of `I_m` and an updated version of `ˆa`. The first `m` entries of `ˆa` are replaced with the commands actually sent to the robot, while the remaining entries retain their planned values, augmented with a learned status embedding indicating execution status. The expert attends to these queries, the cache `C`, and its own tokens. It outputs a per-step delta action `∆a`, which is added to `ˆa` to produce the corrected chunk `ˆa + ∆a`. The expert shares the depth and attention geometry of the base experts to consume their cache and is conditioned on the execution offset `m`.
- **Technical Advantages:** This cache-based correction is efficient, requiring only one forward pass of the tactile expert, making it suitable for high-frequency corrections at the tactile frame rate. It is 20.7 times faster than regenerating an entire chunk. Furthermore, each correction is applied as a delta to the original `ˆa`, preventing error propagation from previous corrections.

**Training and Inference:**
- **Stage 1: Base Model Training:** The video and action experts are trained over the full flow interval. The objective is a weighted sum of video and action flow-matching losses, where the video term covers both future visual and tactile latents.
- **Stage 2: Tactile Expert Training:** All components from Stage 1 are frozen. The tactile expert is initialized from the action expert. During training, the frozen base model plans a chunk (`ˆa`), and `C` is built. For each episode, multiple execution offsets `m` are sampled. The executed prefix of `ˆa` is filled with ground-truth actions, and the tactile expert is supervised to predict the residual `∆a` that brings `ˆa` closer to the ground-truth `a` on the unexecuted suffix.
- **Inference:**
 1. The base model generates `ˆa`, and the robot begins executing it.
 2. The cache `C` is computed (prefill) concurrently.
 3. Upon arrival of each tactile frame at offset `m`, the tactile expert computes `∆a`.
 4. The correction `∆a` is applied to `ˆa` starting from `m+d` (where `d` is a small delay margin, usually 0 or 1 action step), ensuring that already-executed actions are immutable.

**Experimental Setup:**
Experiments are conducted on a Flexiv Rizon 4 robot arm equipped with two InTac S1 tactile sensors, a wrist-mounted Intel RealSense D405, and a third-person Intel RealSense D435i camera. The action space is a seven-dimensional joint command plus a one-dimensional gripper command, issued at 30 Hz. The evaluation involves five real-robot tasks: charger-plug insertion, multi-object fruit transfer, fragile potato-chip transfer, empty-bottle uprighting, and expansion-card insertion. These tasks require precise alignment, regulated grip force, and multi-stage execution. TacPAC is compared against several baselines, including general VLAs (π₀.₅), action-chunking imitation (ACT), a visuo-tactile policy (VITaL), a vision-only WAM (LingBot-VA), and two tactile-aware WAMs (Dream-Tac, T-Rex). Evaluation consists of 20 real-world trials per method per task, with binary success criteria.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of TacPAC focused on its performance across five contact-rich real-robot tasks and an ablation study designed to isolate the contributions of its core components.

**Q1: Overall Contact-Rich Manipulation Capability**
TacPAC demonstrated superior performance across all five tasks. It achieved an average success rate of 64%, surpassing the strongest baseline, T-Rex, by 16 percentage points (T-Rex achieved 48%).
- **Task-Specific Performance:** TacPAC recorded an 80% success rate on charger-plug insertion and 65% on multi-object fruit transfer, marking the largest margins over baselines. On fragile potato-chip transfer, TacPAC achieved 90% success.
- **Comparison with Baselines:**
 - Vision-only methods (π₀.₅, ACT, LingBot-VA) and the reactive visuo-tactile policy (VITaL) exhibited lower success rates (22-36% average), struggling significantly with tight-tolerance tasks like expansion-card insertion and empty-bottle uprighting.
 - Tactile world-action models, Dream-Tac (45% average) and T-Rex (48% average), showed improved performance. Dream-Tac, which predicts contact, performed better on tasks where initial grip is critical (e.g., fragile object handling). T-Rex, which performs reactive correction, was stronger on tasks requiring dynamic adjustments during insertion. TacPAC outperformed both across various task types, indicating the benefit of integrating both prediction and informed correction.

**Q2: Insufficiency of Tactile Prediction Alone**
The research provides both data-level and evaluation-level evidence for why tactile prediction, without real-time correction, is insufficient for optimal performance.
- **Data Analysis:** An analysis of tactile activity showed that significant tactile changes (`c_t`) are concentrated during "contact transition" phases, which constitute only 4.9% of the frames but account for 18.6% of the total tactile change. Most of the prediction objective is therefore dominated by frames with minimal tactile activity. Furthermore, these critical contact changes typically occur *during* the execution of an action chunk, after the initial plan conditioned on future predictions has already been committed.
- **Evaluation:** The ablation variant "w/o tactile expert (prediction only)" (which includes tactile prediction but no real-time correction) achieved an average success rate of 37%. While this is an improvement over the "Vision only" baseline (22%), it still falls considerably short of TacPAC's 64%, indicating that tactile prediction alone captures only about one-third of the total potential gain.

**Q3: Components Enabling Tactile Action Correction**
An ablation study systematically examined the contribution of each component within the tactile pathway (Table 1).
- **Vision only:** 22% average success.
- **w/o tactile expert (prediction only):** 37% average success. (Tactile prediction offers moderate improvement).
- **w/o tactile prediction (reactive correction):** 33% average success. (Reactive correction without predicted context performed similarly to prediction-only, and even lower than vision-only on damage-sensitive tasks, suggesting uncontextualized tactile feedback can be detrimental).
- **w/o tactile cache:** 47% average success. This variant included tactile prediction and a tactile expert performing correction, but the expert was denied access to the cached predicted tactile keys. This configuration achieved a 14-point improvement over "w/o tactile prediction," demonstrating the value of predictive planning even without direct cache access for correction. However, it remained 17 points below TacPAC.
- **TacPAC (Ours):** 64% average success. This variant included all components: tactile prediction, real-time correction, and the tactile expert's access to the cached predicted contact. The 17-point gain from "w/o tactile cache" to "TacPAC" directly supports the central claim that interpreting current tactile feedback *against the anticipated contact* is crucial for effective correction.

**Q4: Real-Time Performance of the Tactile Expert**
The efficiency of the tactile expert was quantified to confirm its suitability for real-time operation.
- **Latency:** A single pass of the tactile expert for correction took an average of 30.4 ms.
- **Rate:** This corresponds to a correction rate of 32.90 Hz.
- **Efficiency Comparison:** This latency is 20.7 times faster than regenerating an entire action chunk (which takes 628.6 ms), making real-time, high-frequency adjustments feasible.
- **Execution Smoothness:** During execution, the robot advanced by one or more control steps while a correction was being computed in only 1.6% of tactile-expert calls. This indicates that most corrections are returned before the next action step. The asynchronous inference scheme and the application of corrections only to the unexecuted suffix of the chunk ensured that no correction-induced jerks were observed in the robot's motion.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The TacPAC research presents a notable advancement in robotic manipulation, particularly for tasks requiring nuanced physical interaction and precise contact control. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Addressing a Core Limitation in Robotic Control:** The work directly tackles the problem of effectively integrating real-time tactile feedback into predictive world-action models. By identifying and mitigating the timing mismatch between action planning (which uses future predictions) and action execution (which generates real-time feedback), TacPAC resolves a fundamental challenge in achieving adaptive, contact-rich manipulation. This is crucial for tasks where visual information alone is insufficient or misleading.

- **Enabling Robust and High-Precision Manipulation:** TacPAC's ability to interpret live tactile data against a plan's expectation allows for highly targeted and context-aware action correction. This leads to a significant increase in success rates across diverse and challenging real-robot tasks, including precision insertion and fragile object handling. The demonstrated performance suggests that TacPAC can make robots more reliable and capable in environments requiring delicate and forceful interactions.

- **Improving Efficiency in Robot Learning:** The design of the tactile expert as a single-pass corrector leveraging a cached prediction is computationally efficient. With an average correction latency of 30.4 ms (20.7 times faster than full replanning), TacPAC enables high-frequency adjustments without incurring substantial computational overhead. This efficiency is critical for deploying adaptive policies in real-world robotic systems, where timely responses are essential.

- **Advancing Multimodal World Modeling:** TacPAC underscores the complementary nature of tactile prediction and real-time tactile correction. It demonstrates that the most effective use of tactile information involves not just sensing or predicting, but actively comparing current observations with learned expectations. This principle of "prediction-grounded correction" could inform the development of more sophisticated multimodal world models, where various sensory predictions serve as dynamic references for continuous action refinement.

- **Broad Applicability and Future Directions:** The framework's success on diverse contact-rich tasks implies its potential applicability across a wider range of manipulation scenarios and even to other embodied AI domains. The modular nature of TacPAC, separating the base world-action model from the tactile expert, could facilitate its integration into different existing robotic control architectures. Future research might explore extending this prediction-grounded correction paradigm to other types of physical feedback (e.g., force, torque) or to more complex, long-horizon tasks.

- **Contribution to Reproducible Research:** The provision of open-source code for TacPAC facilitates further research and development in the robotics community, enabling other researchers to build upon and validate these findings.

In summary, TacPAC provides a practical and effective solution for a critical problem in robotic manipulation, offering a path towards more intelligent, adaptive, and reliable contact-rich robot behaviors by harmonizing predicted future states with real-time sensory feedback.
