# Shadow: Leveraging Segmentation Masks for Cross-Embodiment Policy Transfer

> [!summary] Summary
> A method named SHADOW enables zero-shot policy transfer between different robot embodiments by leveraging segmentation masks to align visual input distributions during training and evaluation. This approach achieves performance on target robots comparable to source-robot-trained policies, outperforming prior in-painting methods by avoiding visual artifacts in both simulated and real-world settings.

## Problem

- Vision-based policies fail to transfer between different robot embodiments due to visual discrepancies, causing significant performance degradation.
- Collecting large, specific datasets for every new robot hardware or gripper configuration is resource-intensive and impractical.
- Existing cross-embodiment transfer methods often rely on target robot data, extensive multi-embodiment pre-training, or introduce image artifacts.

## Method

- ==The SHADOW== method implements a data editing scheme that aligns visual input distributions for training and evaluation.
- During training, source robot pixels are masked and replaced with a fixed color, then a segmentation mask of the target robot (at the source robot's end-effector pose) is overlaid.
- During evaluation, the real target robot's pixels are masked, and a segmentation mask of the source robot (at the real target robot's current end-effector pose) is overlaid before feeding to the policy.

## Results

- SHADOW achieved success rates on target robots comparable to the upper-bound performance on the source robot in five out of six simulation tasks, showing no significant performance degradation.
- It consistently and statistically outperformed the Mirage baseline in both simulation and real-world experiments, with Mirage's failures attributed to image distortions from in-painting.
- Ablation studies confirmed that both masking the active robot's pixels and overlaying the other robot's segmentation mask are essential for robust zero-shot policy transfer.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The distinct RGB visual appearance of the robot itself is less critical for policy transfer than the consistent representation of the end-effector's pose and interaction with objects.
> - Aligning visual input distributions by replacing robot details with simple segmentation masks is more effective for zero-shot transfer than generative in-painting, which can introduce performance-degrading artifacts.
> - Precise knowledge of robot kinematics, geometry, and camera calibration enables the creation of highly accurate, physically consistent composite images for robust cross-embodiment adaptation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics is experiencing a proliferation of diverse hardware embodiments, with data collection efforts spread across various robot types. This trend necessitates the development of methods that can effectively utilize growing bodies of data, particularly when policies need to be transferred across different robot platforms. A significant challenge in this context is the visual dissimilarity between robots, which can severely degrade the performance of vision-based policies when transferred from one embodiment to another.

Existing research has explored several avenues for addressing cross-embodiment policy transfer:
- **Minimizing Visual Differences:** Some approaches, such as using wrist cameras, aim to reduce variations in the input observation space. However, these methods often constrain the range of tasks and cannot leverage third-person viewpoint data.
- **Video Prediction and Masking:** Other works propose masking robot pixels and training video prediction models to forecast non-robot pixels, which can be computationally intensive.
- **Architecture-Specific Adaptations:** Several studies introduce policies tailored to specific tasks or robot morphologies, or train separate heads for each robot. These typically assume knowledge of the robot distribution at evaluation time or require few-shot trajectories from the target robot for adaptation. Such approaches generally necessitate data collection on the target robot prior to deployment.
- **Large-Scale Multi-Embodiment Datasets:** A growing area involves pre-training policies on extensive and diverse robotic datasets (e.g., OXE, RT-1). While these datasets aim to foster generalization, policies derived from them, particularly those using Cartesian actions, often lack zero-shot deployability to new robots without leveraging explicit knowledge of the robot's dynamics or aligning action spaces. Some also still involve collection of target robot demonstrations.
- **Image Augmentation and In-painting:** Domain randomization techniques, including image editing and augmentation, are used to create more robust policies. This includes generating subgoals with diffusion models, simulating different viewpoints, and in-painting human hands with robot grippers. A particularly relevant prior work is Mirage, which utilizes in-painting to overlay a target robot onto a source robot's scene during evaluation. However, in-painting models can suffer from blurriness, distortion, and the generation of erroneous artifacts, which may limit generalizability in visually complex environments or when source and target robots are significantly different.

SHADOW addresses the challenge of generalizing policies to *unseen* robot embodiments without requiring any data collection on the target robot. It distinguishes itself from prior work by:
1. **Zero-Shot Transfer:** Operating strictly in a zero-shot setting where no data from the target robot is available for training or adaptation.
2. **Data Efficiency:** Avoiding the need for large, diverse multi-embodiment datasets for co-training, instead relying on data from a single source robot.
3. **Distribution Alignment:** Focusing on a data editing scheme that ensures a close match between the input data distribution at train time and test time, which is a common challenge for cross-embodiment transfer.
4. **Avoiding In-painting Artifacts:** Deliberately discarding the RGB details of the robots and using segmentation masks, thereby circumventing the distortions and blurriness that can arise from generative in-painting models like Mirage. This approach preserves crucial gripper/object interaction details and the RGB details of the surroundings.
5. **No "No-Robot" Scene Requirement:** Unlike some keypoint tracking or affordance prediction methods, SHADOW does not require an image of the scene without the robot present.

By focusing on a simple yet effective data editing technique, SHADOW contributes to the development of more generalizable and data-efficient robot learning systems, enabling policies trained on one robot to be readily deployed on novel hardware.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a method that enables the robust transfer of policies learned on a single robot embodiment (the "source") to an entirely different, previously unseen robot embodiment (the "target") without requiring any data collection on the target robot.

This objective is motivated by several critical challenges and trends in contemporary robotics:
- **Increasing Hardware Diversity:** The rapid development and adoption of new robot hardware lead to a fragmented landscape of data collection. As more researchers and institutions contribute to open-source robotics data, the variety of robot embodiments and their associated datasets is expanding. Effectively leveraging this growing but disparate body of data necessitates methods capable of learning and generalizing across these diverse platforms.
- **Impracticality of Extensive Data Collection:** It is often unrealistic and resource-intensive to collect large-scale, embodiment-specific datasets for every new robot or hardware iteration. The demand for new methods that can generalize to novel embodiments without requiring extensive, new, embodiment-specific data collection is therefore high.
- **Visual Discrepancies and Distribution Shift:** A fundamental obstacle to policy transfer across different robot embodiments is their inherent visual dissimilarity. Vision-based policies are highly sensitive to changes in visual observations. Even minor variations between robot types can cause significant distribution shifts in the input data, leading to a dramatic degradation in policy performance. Current multi-robot datasets, such as those found in Open-X Embodiment (OXE), offer a limited number of distinct robot embodiments (e.g., 18 in OXE), which is insufficient to provide the necessary visual diversity for existing algorithms to generalize effectively to unseen platforms.
- **Limitations of Current Generalization Techniques:** While various methods attempt to address cross-embodiment transfer, they often come with limitations. Some require target robot data, pre-training on vast and diverse datasets, or rely on computationally expensive generative models for image augmentation that can introduce artifacts. The goal is to overcome these limitations by proposing a more data-efficient and robust solution.

In summary, the motivation stems from the need for a practical, efficient, and robust method to bridge the gap between policies learned on specific robot hardware and their deployment on a continually evolving array of new, unseen robot embodiments, without incurring the prohibitive costs and time associated with new data acquisition.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the SHADOW methodology is a data editing scheme designed to align the input data distributions during both training and evaluation, thereby facilitating robust policy transfer across different robot embodiments. The approach leverages knowledge of robot kinematics, geometry, and camera calibration to generate segmentation masks.

#### 3.1. Problem Setup

The method operates under the following conditions:
- **Dataset:** A dataset D is available from a single "source" robot embodiment. This dataset consists of N trajectories, where each trajectory includes observations (stacks of RGB images from T previous steps), states (joint positions), and actions (absolute Cartesian pose of the end-effector).
- **Target Robot:** The policy is evaluated on a "target" robot embodiment for which no trajectories were collected.
- **Camera Calibration:** The pose of the camera is calibrated relative to the base frames of both the source and target robots (T_Camera^S0 and T_Camera^T0 are known).
- **Assumptions:**
 - The distribution of object locations relative to the camera remains consistent.
 - The background, lighting, and camera pose in the scene are fixed. Robustness to scene variations is outside the scope of this work.
 - Both source and target robot embodiments can complete the tasks using similar strategies.
 - Accurate robot geometry (CAD models), joint angles (proprioception), and kinematics are available.

#### 3.2. Shadow Data Editing

The SHADOW method involves distinct data editing procedures for the training and evaluation phases:

##### 3.2.1. Training Phase

During training, the policy is exposed to modified images derived from the source robot's dataset:
1. **Source Robot Masking:** For each image I_t in the training dataset, the pixels corresponding to the source robot are segmented out. This segmentation is performed using the robot's proprioception (joint angles), known kinematics, geometry, and camera parameters. The segmented robot pixels are then replaced with a fixed, solid color (e.g., black) to obtain a modified image, I_t,*.
2. **Target Robot Mask Overlay:** A virtual rendering of the target robot is generated. Crucially, the target robot's end-effector is positioned to match the exact Cartesian pose of the source robot's end-effector in the original image I_t. The joint positions required for this pose are calculated using an operational space controller. A simulated camera, configured with the same intrinsics and extrinsics (T_Camera^T0) as the real-world camera for the target robot, renders this virtual scene. A segmentation mask of the target robot is then extracted from this rendering.
3. **Composite Image Creation:** This target robot segmentation mask (also filled with the fixed color) is overlaid onto I_t,*. The resulting composite image, I_t,•, effectively shows the scene with the source robot's original visual information removed, and the target robot's silhouette superimposed at the same end-effector pose.
4. **Policy Training:** A policy (specifically, Diffusion Policy in this work) is then trained using imitation learning on this dataset of modified images (D_•), paired with the original actions from the source robot demonstrations.

##### 3.2.2. Evaluation Phase

When deploying the trained policy on the target robot, a symmetrical data editing process is applied to ensure consistency with the training distribution:
1. **Target Robot Masking:** The current real-world image I_t from the target robot is acquired. The pixels corresponding to the target robot are segmented out (using its proprioception, kinematics, geometry, and camera parameters) and replaced with the same fixed color, yielding I_t,⋆.
2. **Source Robot Mask Overlay:** A virtual rendering of the source robot is generated. Its end-effector is positioned to match the current Cartesian pose of the *real target robot*. The simulated camera uses the source robot's intrinsics and extrinsics (T_Camera^S0) for rendering. A segmentation mask of the source robot is extracted from this rendering.
3. **Composite Image Creation:** This source robot segmentation mask (fixed color) is overlaid onto I_t,⋆ to produce I_t,•.
4. **Action Execution:** This composite image o_t,• is fed as input to the trained policy, which outputs the next action. This action is then executed on the real target robot using a blocking controller for accurate trajectory tracking.

The fundamental principle behind SHADOW is that by replacing the "active" robot (source during training, target during evaluation) with a composite segmentation mask of both robots at the *same end-effector pose*, the visual input presented to the policy remains consistent. Since CAD models allow for high-fidelity segmentation masks, the distribution shift between training and testing images is minimized, thereby enabling robust cross-embodiment policy transfer.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates SHADOW's efficacy in both simulated environments and real-world hardware, comparing its performance against established baselines using Diffusion Policy for imitation learning.

#### 4.1. Baselines and Upper-bound

- **Naive Baseline:** Training on unmodified source robot data and evaluating on unmodified target robot images.
- **Mirage Baseline:** A cross-embodiment method leveraging in-painting. It was re-implemented with Diffusion Policy for a fair comparison. The paper notes that Mirage was previously reported to outperform Octo (a large model pre-trained on diverse embodiments) for cross-embodiment learning on similar tasks.
- **Upper-bound:** A policy trained and evaluated on raw source robot images (without any data editing or transfer). This serves as a performance benchmark.

#### 4.2. Simulation Experiments

Experiments were conducted in the Mujoco simulator using Mimicgen and Robomimic datasets. The source robot was a Panda with a Robotiq 2F-85 gripper. Target robots included Sawyer, IIWA, and UR5e, evaluated with both Franka and Robotiq 2F-85 grippers across six manipulation tasks (e.g., Stack, Can, Coffee).

- **Naive Baseline Performance:** The naive baseline consistently showed near 0% success rates across all tasks and embodiments, confirming that the visual distribution shift between different robots severely impedes direct policy transfer.
- **Mirage Performance:** Mirage performed adequately on simpler tasks like "Stack" and "Can." However, its performance significantly deteriorated on tasks requiring higher precision, such as "Stack Three" (balancing multiple objects) or "Coffee" (inserting a pod into a narrow slot). This degradation was attributed to the image distortions and erroneous artifacts generated by the in-painting process. Mirage also struggled even when only the gripper changed (Panda robot with Franka gripper) due to differences in end-effector control point to flange distances, which still necessitated in-painting.
- **SHADOW Performance:** SHADOW consistently achieved success rates on target robots comparable to the upper-bound performance on the source robot, demonstrating no significant performance degradation in five out of six tasks. In the "Mug Cleanup" task, while still significantly outperforming baselines, SHADOW showed some drop, which was explained by the Franka gripper (used on target) being prone to jamming, a physical constraint not faced by the source gripper. SHADOW demonstrated statistically superior performance compared to Mirage on all tasks.
- **Ablation Study (Target Mask Contribution):** An ablation study confirmed the importance of overlaying the target robot's segmentation mask. A "Black-only" approach (only blacking out the source robot without overlaying the target mask) resulted in near 0% success on target robots, highlighting that the target mask overlay is crucial for SHADOW's effectiveness.
- **Masking Robot Pixels:** An additional experiment showed that simply replacing the source robot with its segmentation mask (discarding its RGB information) had no significant effect on task performance, indicating that the specific RGB details of the robot itself are not critical for these tasks.

#### 4.3. Real-world Experiments

Real-world evaluations were performed on four tasks (Mug, Hexagon, Cups, Blocks). The source robot was a Panda with a Robotiq gripper (300 demonstrations per task). Two cross-embodiment scenarios were tested:
1. **Same Robot, Different Gripper:** Panda robot with a Franka gripper as the target.
2. **Different Robot, Same Gripper:** UR5e robot with a Robotiq 2F-85 gripper as the target.
The naive baseline was omitted for safety reasons due to its poor performance in simulation.

- **SHADOW vs. Mirage:** SHADOW significantly outperformed Mirage across all real-world tasks.
- **Failure Modes:** Mirage's primary failure mode was observed to be the execution of seemingly random actions, which the authors hypothesize is linked to the image distortion caused by in-painting. SHADOW's main failure mode was identified as a lack of precision, sometimes resulting in slight misses during grasping and an inability to recover. Performance was lower on tasks requiring grasping wider objects (Hexagon, Blocks) compared to narrower objects (Cups, Mug) due to the increased demand for precise positioning.
- **Performance on Source Robot (Appendix A.2):** SHADOW's policy generally performed equally well on both source and target robots for most tasks, suggesting that the data editing does not inherently degrade performance on the original embodiment.
- **Sensitivity to Camera Calibration (Appendix A.4):** Experiments showed that SHADOW's performance in real hardware deteriorated proportionally to the amount of noise introduced into the camera extrinsic parameters at evaluation time, indicating a reliance on accurate calibration.

In summary, SHADOW demonstrated robust and superior performance compared to the strongest baseline (Mirage) in both simulation and real-world experiments, effectively enabling policy transfer to unseen target robots with minimal performance degradation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The SHADOW method presents a significant contribution to the field of robotic learning by addressing the critical challenge of cross-embodiment policy transfer, particularly in data-scarce scenarios for new robot hardware. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Enabling Zero-Shot Generalization:** The primary impact of SHADOW is its capacity to facilitate policy transfer to unseen target robots without the need for any data collection on the new hardware. This addresses a major bottleneck in robot deployment, as collecting large, diverse datasets for every new robot embodiment is often impractical and resource-intensive.
- **Enhanced Data Efficiency:** SHADOW operates effectively by leveraging data from only a single source robot. This stands in contrast to approaches that require co-training on vast, multi-embodiment datasets or few-shot adaptation data from the target robot. Its data efficiency makes it a practical solution for research labs and industrial settings where extensive data collection is a constraint.
- **Robustness through Distribution Alignment:** The core insight of SHADOW—using composite segmentation masks to align the visual input distribution between training and evaluation—leads to policies that are robust to visual variations across robot types. By abstracting away the specific RGB details of the robots and maintaining consistent end-effector poses, the method minimizes distribution shift, a common cause of failure in transfer learning.
- **Avoidance of Generative Model Limitations:** Unlike methods relying on complex generative models for in-painting, SHADOW avoids introducing visual artifacts, blurriness, or distortions that can degrade policy performance, especially in tasks requiring high precision. This ensures that the crucial visual information for object interaction remains clear and consistent.
- **Leveraging Existing Data:** By demonstrating robust transfer from a single source, SHADOW enhances the utility of existing robot datasets. It allows a policy trained on a readily available dataset (e.g., from a common robot like a Panda) to be deployed on various other robots without further data acquisition, thus maximizing the value of current data collection efforts.
- **Simplifying Robot Deployment:** The method's relative simplicity, relying on known kinematics and camera calibration, makes it a more accessible tool for researchers and developers. It lowers the barrier to deploying robot learning policies on novel hardware, potentially accelerating the development and adoption of generalist robots capable of operating in diverse environments.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** While acknowledging limitations (e.g., reliance on camera calibration accuracy, per-target policy training, challenges with occlusions, limited generalization to novel scenes), SHADOW provides a strong foundation. These limitations present clear avenues for future work, such as incorporating noise injection for calibration robustness, developing a single policy for multiple target embodiments, integrating depth data, and combining with orthogonal methods for scene generalization. Addressing these could further expand its applicability and impact.

In conclusion, SHADOW contributes a practical, data-efficient, and robust solution for cross-embodiment policy transfer, moving closer to the goal of general-purpose robots that can learn from limited data and adapt seamlessly to a variety of physical forms.
