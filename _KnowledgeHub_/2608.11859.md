# Small-Scale Experiments: Are We There Yet?

> [!summary] Summary
> Scaling laws do emerge from models as small as 4M parameters, but only on the fully tuned frontier; the confounder is hyperparameter sensitivity, which fades with scale as the hyperparameter loss surface loses intrinsic dimension. Combining the noisy quadratic limit, scaling laws, and perplexity-capability correspondence into a diagnostic methodology, small-scale sweeps recover the known large-scale result that pre-norm beats post-norm.

## Problem

- The two most influential scaling-law studies reached contradictory parameter-versus-data conclusions, later traced to methodological choices that disproportionately distort small models.
- Surveys report small-model scaling laws are hard to reproduce and unreliable below 100M parameters, so the field concluded sizable models cannot be avoided.
- Downstream scaling laws fail broadly, with emergent, inverse and U-shaped behaviour meaning as little as 39% of tasks scale predictably from compute.

## Method

- Random search over hundreds of configurations per scale with a ==warmup-stable-decay (WSD) schedule==, branching decay phases from eight checkpoints so one sweep yields stable and decayed evaluations.
- Fitting the ==noisy quadratic limit (NQL)== to random-search tails to estimate best attainable loss and the ==effective hyperparameter count==, the surface's intrinsic dimension at the optimum.
- A four-diagnostic methodology chaining ==noisy quadratic limit==, ==joint parameter-data scaling law==, and ==perplexity-capability correspondence==, turning each assumption into a falsifiable check rather than pure extrapolation.

## Results

- Tuning dominates: with **4** or **16** configurations per scale no law is visible, **64** makes it emerge imprecisely, and only **256** yields accurate extrapolation.
- Ablations show per-budget hyperparameter tuning cuts test MSE by **50%** and learning-rate decay by **98%**, while parameter-counting conventions and tied exponents barely matter.
- Effective hyperparameter count drops toward **one** as parameters grow from **4M** to **268M**, and sweeps at **4M**, **34M**, **134M** recover pre-norm's advantage.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A null result at small scale may signal under-tuning rather than an absent effect; search hundreds of configurations before declaring a phenomenon missing.
> - Spend tuning budget where the surface is high-dimensional and unforgiving, at small scale; large models are low-dimensional, so simple transfer rules suffice.
> - Extrapolate loss cautiously, since far-field comparisons mostly compare noisy irreducible-error estimates, and the whole approach breaks once the pretraining data changes.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The emergence of "foundation models" has significantly influenced computing paradigms over the past five years, becoming foundational for discoveries across various scientific fields. A key factor in their development is "scale," which unfortunately leads to high experimental costs. The natural progression for addressing this cost is to conduct experiments at smaller scales and then transfer the insights to larger models. This approach was initially supported by the promise of "scaling laws," which suggested a predictable relationship between model size, data, and performance.

However, the practical application of scaling laws has not fully delivered on this promise. Pretraining research has experienced an exponential increase in costs, with experimentation often dominating the overall expense of model development. Scaling laws have proven elusive or unreliable at small scales, a challenge highlighted by contradictory conclusions in influential early works (e.g., Kaplan et al. vs. Hoffmann et al.) regarding the optimal balance of parameters and data. Subsequent research traced these discrepancies to methodological choices, particularly those disproportionately affecting small-scale models, leading to a "scaling gap" where small-scale insights often fail to transfer to larger systems.

This paper addresses this "scaling gap" by investigating the factors that hinder the reliable application of scaling laws at small scales. It aims to reconcile the theoretical promise of cost-effective small-scale experimentation with the observed practical difficulties, thereby re-establishing a foundation for efficient model-centric research. The work builds upon existing literature regarding scaling laws, the analysis of hyperparameter loss surfaces (e.g., the noisy quadratic limit), and the perplexity-capability correspondence, seeking to synthesize these elements into a robust methodology for developing large models from small-scale insights.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable reliable and cost-effective experimentation at small scales for foundation model development, fulfilling the original promise of scaling laws. The authors contend that the current unreliability of small-scale scaling laws is not inherent to small models but rather stems from a confounding factor: hyperparameter sensitivity.

The key motivations and specific objectives driving this work are:

- **Addressing Escalating Costs:** The cost of developing large language models has increased exponentially, with pretraining experiments consuming a significant portion of research and development budgets. This necessitates methods to gain reliable insights from cheaper, smaller-scale experiments.
- **Reconciling Contradictory Findings:** Previous research on scaling laws has yielded conflicting conclusions, particularly concerning optimal parameter-to-data ratios and the reproducibility of findings at smaller model sizes. The authors aim to resolve these discrepancies by identifying the overlooked critical ingredient for accurate scaling law estimation.
- **Demonstrating Small-Scale Scaling Laws:** A core objective is to show that scaling laws *do* exist and are predictable even in very small models (as small as 4 million parameters), provided that hyperparameters are rigorously tuned. This contrasts with the prevailing belief that such regularities only emerge at much larger scales.
- **Understanding Hyperparameter Sensitivity:** The research seeks to explain *why* small models are particularly sensitive to hyperparameters and why this sensitivity diminishes with scale. This involves analyzing the geometry of the hyperparameter loss surface, specifically its intrinsic dimension, as models grow.
- **Developing a New Methodology:** Based on the insights gained from hyperparameter behavior, the authors propose a novel, holistic methodology for model-centric research. This methodology integrates tools such as the noisy quadratic limit, scaling laws, and the perplexity-capability correspondence to provide a diagnostic framework for small-scale experiments.
- **Validating the Methodology:** The effectiveness of the proposed methodology is demonstrated through a case study: resolving the long-standing architectural question of optimal normalization layer placement (pre-norm vs. post-norm) in transformer models, using only small-scale experiments to recover known large-scale results.
- **Improving Research Rigor and Efficiency:** Ultimately, the motivation is to offer a practical approach that simultaneously reduces the computational cost of experiments and increases the rigor and reliability of conclusions drawn from them, particularly for model-centric improvements.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs an empirical approach primarily based on extensive random search for hyperparameter tuning across various model scales and architectural designs. This is coupled with the application of specific analytical tools to understand and model the loss surface.

#### 3.1. Experimental Setup

- **Models:** The study uses a decoder-only Llama variant as implemented in Meta Lingua. Model sizes range from approximately 4 million (2^22) to 268 million (2^28) effective parameters, increasing in powers of two.
- **Parameter Definition:** Model size is measured by "effective parameters," which is proportional to FLOPs per token. This count excludes the embedding layer (due to no FLOPs) but includes the unembedding layer and attention costs, consistent with prior work on compute-optimal scaling.
- **Training and Evaluation:**
 - **Schedule:** Models are trained using an AdamW optimizer with a Warmup-Stable-Decay (WSD) learning rate schedule. Training continues until the number of tokens reaches 32 times the effective parameters, ensuring coverage of both under- and over-trained regimes.
 - **Checkpointing:** Checkpoints are saved at 1/8, 2/8..., 8/8 intervals of training during the stable phase. Decay phases are branched off these checkpoints, extending training by 25% more steps with a linear cooldown to a minimum learning rate. This allows for evaluation of both stable and decayed learning rate regimes.
 - **Pretraining Metric:** Bits-Per-Character (BPC) on a validation set, derived from the negative log-likelihood per character.
 - **Downstream Capabilities:** Evaluated using the EleutherAI lm-evaluation-harness on six multiple-choice tasks: AI2 ARC (Easy, Challenge), BoolQ, MMLU, OpenBookQA, and PIQA.
- **Data:** Pretraining is performed on a 100 billion token subset of FineWeb-Edu. Random seeds are fixed across various components (data, model, evaluation harness) to ensure that run differences are primarily due to hyperparameters.
- **Data Partitioning:** Scales are partitioned into training (4M–34M effective parameters), validation (67M–134M), and test (268M) to assess the ability of scaling laws to extrapolate from small to larger, held-out models.

#### 3.2. Hyperparameter Search and Experimental Designs

- **Extensive Random Search:** The central approach is to perform extensive random searches over hyperparameters. The search space includes `batch_size`, `learning rate`, `beta1`, `beta2`, `warmup` proportion, `weight_decay`, and `rope_theta`. These are sampled from LogUniform, LogitUniform, or DiscreteUniform distributions as appropriate.
- **Model Ladder Design:** Hand-crafted architectural hyperparameters (hidden dimension, number of layers, attention heads) are used, increasing width and depth to scale parameter count while maintaining a consistent aspect ratio.
- **Random Architecture Design:** Architectural hyperparameters (number of layers, number of attention heads) are also sampled via random search, with the hidden dimension derived from the effective parameters. Constraints (e.g., head_dim divisibility, multiples of 8 for GPU efficiency) are accounted for in the sampling distribution.
- **Pre-Norm vs. Post-Norm Comparison:** For this case study, configurations from the random architecture experiment are reused, but the transformer architecture is explicitly changed between pre-normalization and post-normalization. A higher number of configurations is sampled for the post-normalization architecture due to its known tuning difficulty.

#### 3.3. Analytical Framework

The methodology synthesizes three tools from the literature:
- **The Noisy Quadratic Limit (NQL):** Used to assess the thoroughness of hyperparameter tuning and the difficulty of optimization. NQL reveals if a search has converged near the optimum, characterized by quadratic, low-rank, and normal loss surfaces. It provides parameters like the best attainable performance (α), concentration (β), effective number of hyperparameters (γ), and noise (σ). This is applied to the tail of the score distribution from random search.
- **Scaling Laws:** The Hoffmann et al. (2022) functional form (L(p, d) = ϵ + ζ/p^ι + η/d^κ) is used to model pretraining loss as a function of parameters (p) and data (d) at the "fully tuned frontier." The coefficients (ϵ, ζ, η, ι, κ) are fitted by minimizing mean squared error on the best-attained loss for each parameter-data budget pair.
- **Perplexity-Capability Correspondence:** This principle states that for a fixed pretraining dataset, models achieving the same pretraining loss tend to exhibit similar downstream capabilities, regardless of scale or architecture. This allows pretraining loss to serve as a proxy for evaluating model improvements without needing to predict specific task capabilities.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yields several findings regarding the nature of scaling laws at small scales and the underlying hyperparameter loss surface.

#### 4.1. Hyperparameter Tuning as the Critical Factor

- **Elusive Scaling Laws:** The study demonstrates that scaling laws, which relate model performance to compute, are present even at very small scales (down to 4M parameters). However, they are consistently observed only when hyperparameters are "fully tuned."
- **Tuning Effort:** Rigorous hyperparameter tuning is identified as the single most important ingredient for observing these laws. When the tuning effort is insufficient (e.g., 4 or 16 random configurations per scale), the scaling law is not discernible. An accurate law emerged only with 256 configurations in the ladder design.
- **Methodological Robustness:** In contrast to hyperparameter tuning, other methodological choices—such as different ways of defining parameter counts, fine-tuning hyperparameters per token budget, applying learning rate decay, or tying scaling exponents (ι = κ)—were found to primarily refine the precision of an already visible scaling law rather than enabling its initial observation.

#### 4.2. Hyperparameter Sensitivity and Scale

- **Small-Scale Sensitivity:** Small models are considerably more sensitive to their hyperparameters than large models. This sensitivity is evident in the wider spread of validation scores from random searches at smaller scales.
- **Decreased Sensitivity with Scale:** As model scale increases, good hyperparameter configurations become more common. This is reflected in the score distributions from random searches, which concentrate more tightly around the best achievable loss, forming a distinct peak.
- **Role of Parameters and Data:** The decrease in hyperparameter sensitivity is driven by both parameters and data. Scaling either dimension while the other is small reduces loss but only slightly narrows the gap between optimal and suboptimal performance. However, scaling both parameters and data significantly collapses this gap, indicating a substantial reduction in sensitivity.

#### 4.3. Dimensionality of the Hyperparameter Loss Surface

- **Lower Intrinsic Dimension:** The reduction in hyperparameter sensitivity with scale is explained by a change in the geometry of the hyperparameter loss surface. The "effective number of hyperparameters" (γ), representing the intrinsic dimension of the loss surface near the optimum, decreases as models scale. This intrinsic dimension was observed to drop to one for the largest models.
- **Parameters as Primary Driver:** Parameters are the main driver of this decrease in intrinsic dimension. Increasing the number of parameters consistently reduces γ, whereas increasing the amount of training data (tokens) has a comparatively smaller effect on γ.
- **Implications:** This suggests that while small scales are high-dimensional and require extensive hyperparameter exploration, large scales are lower-dimensional and thus easier to tune, allowing simpler rules to adapt hyperparameters.

#### 4.4. Limitations of Pure Extrapolation

- While scaling laws are observable at small scales and can extrapolate over an order of magnitude, quantitative extrapolation far beyond the observed data range faces statistical limitations. Extrapolating to very large scales can magnify small sampling errors, making comparisons of models based solely on extrapolated "irreducible error" unreliable. The scaling law is more trustworthy closer to the data used for fitting.

#### 4.5. Case Study: Pre-Norm vs. Post-Norm Architectures

The methodology was applied to compare pre-normalization (pre-norm) and post-normalization (post-norm) in transformer architectures, a question that historically took years to settle.
- **Tuning Difficulty:** The noisy quadratic limit diagnostic revealed that post-norm architectures exhibit greater hyperparameter sensitivity and a smaller "asymptotic regime" (region where the NQL holds) compared to pre-norm, indicating harder tuning.
- **Scaling Behavior:** Both architectures showed decreased sensitivity with scale. However, post-norm maintained a persistent peak at suboptimal values in its score distribution, further indicating ongoing tuning challenges, even at larger scales.
- **Perplexity-Capability Correspondence:** The study confirmed that pretraining loss corresponds to downstream capabilities for both architectures. Models with similar pretraining loss achieved similar performance across various downstream tasks, validating the use of pretraining loss as a reliable proxy.
- **Scaling Laws Comparison:** Pre-norm was found to scale better than post-norm within the observed data range. When assuming a shared irreducible error (i.e., both architectures ultimately reach the same minimum possible loss), the scaling advantage of pre-norm grew with compute, recovering the previously established large-scale superiority of pre-norm from small-scale experiments.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research offers significant implications for the efficiency and rigor of foundation model development.

- **Cost Reduction in AI Research:** By demonstrating that reliable scaling laws can be observed and utilized at very small scales, the paper provides a pathway to significantly reduce the computational cost of experiments. This can make advanced model research more accessible to a wider range of academic and industry labs.
- **Enhanced Rigor in Model-Centric Research:** The proposed methodology, which synthesizes the noisy quadratic limit, scaling laws, and perplexity-capability correspondence, provides a structured framework for diagnosing and understanding model behavior. This qualitative understanding, coupled with diagnostic checks for underlying assumptions, fosters more robust and reliable conclusions compared to simple quantitative extrapolation.
- **Practical Guidance for Hyperparameter Tuning:** The identification of hyperparameter tuning as the critical element for small-scale scaling laws offers concrete practical guidance. Researchers are advised to conduct extensive searches at smaller scales where models are most sensitive, and then apply simpler adaptation rules for scaling up, leveraging the decreased sensitivity of larger models.
- **Reconciling Past Discrepancies:** The work provides an explanation for the previously observed unreliability and conflicting results of small-scale scaling laws, attributing them to insufficient hyperparameter tuning. This reconciliation can rebuild confidence in the utility of scaling laws as a foundational tool.
- **Improved Model Design:** The ability to recover large-scale architectural insights (e.g., pre-norm vs. post-norm) from small-scale experiments demonstrates the practical utility of the methodology for efficiently evaluating and improving model architectures.
- **Future Research Directions:** The findings regarding the dimensionality of the hyperparameter loss surface open new avenues for theoretical and empirical work, such as investigating how this dimensionality changes under alternative parametrizations or architectural designs. Additionally, the explicit limitation to "model-centric research" due to the fixed-data requirement for perplexity-capability correspondence highlights a clear area for future work in adapting these insights for "data-centric research."
