# EgoRecovery: Acquiring Failure Recovery Ability Through Human Recovery Demonstration

> [!summary] Summary
> EgoRecovery is a co-training framework enabling robots to acquire failure recovery capabilities by leveraging scalable egocentric human demonstrations. It achieves this by extracting a compact "corrective intent" from human recovery actions and using a gated mechanism to modulate robot policy features, improving average recovery success rate to 85.0% and nominal success rate to 80.0% across four manipulation tasks.

## Problem

- Robot policies trained on successful demonstrations often lack robustness and fail to recover from off-nominal states in real-world environments.
- Collecting diverse and effective robot-specific recovery data through teleoperation is extremely costly and time-consuming, hindering scalability.
- Transferring recovery knowledge from human demonstrations to robots is challenging due to the significant embodiment gap between human and robot kinematics and control.

## Method

- A ==co-training framework==, **EgoRecovery**, uses both robot and efficiently collected egocentric human recovery demonstrations, along with success data, to train robot policies.
- It bridges the embodiment gap by learning a compact "==corrective intent==" (low-frequency DCT coefficients of future end-effector motion) from human data, rather than directly mapping human actions.
- A ==Recovery Gate Head== predicts the need for recovery, and a ==gated FiLM modulation== mechanism applies the learned corrective intent to the robot action decoder only when a failure state is detected, preventing interference with nominal task execution.

## Results

- EgoRecovery achieved an **85.0%** average Recovery Success Rate (SR) from off-nominal starts, outperforming direct human recovery mix (**71.2%** SR) and robot-only baselines (**0.0%** SR for nominal-only, **60.0%** SR for more robot data).
- Human recovery data collection was measured to be approximately **10.5x** faster than robot recovery data, averaging **516.5** episodes per operator hour.
- Ablation studies confirmed the necessity of corrective intent supervision (reducing Recovery SR from **85.0%** to **65.0%**), intent modulation (**65.0%** SR), and the recovery gate (reducing Initial SR from **80.0%** to **70.0%** if always on).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Egocentric human demonstrations are over 10 times more efficient for collecting recovery data compared to robot teleoperation, providing a scalable source for diverse failure modes.
> - Abstracting recovery behavior into a low-dimensional "corrective intent" allows for effective cross-embodiment transfer, guiding the robot's specific corrective actions without requiring direct motion imitation.
> - Contextual gating of recovery intent is crucial for maintaining high nominal task success rates while ensuring recovery capabilities are activated appropriately.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper addresses the challenge of enhancing the robustness of embodied robots, particularly their ability to recover from task failures. This work is situated within the field of robot learning, specifically imitation learning and cross-embodiment transfer, with a focus on improving policy resilience in unstructured and noisy real-world environments.

A significant hurdle in imitation learning is covariate shift, where policies trained on successful demonstrations struggle when rollouts drift into unseen or off-manifold states. Previous research has demonstrated that incorporating explicit recovery and correction data can substantially improve long-horizon robustness by training policies to handle perturbed states or by using intervention signals. However, existing methods for collecting such recovery data, typically through robot teleoperation or interventions, are often costly and difficult to scale. Inducing diverse failure states, performing corrective actions, and resetting the environment are time-consuming processes, further complicated by the high variability of potential failure modes.

Concurrently, learning from egocentric human data has gained prominence for its potential to improve the generalization capabilities of robot policies. Prior studies have utilized human videos and human play for nominal task execution, broad pretraining, or action alignment. These approaches leverage humans' ability to generate diverse interactions without requiring direct operation of robot hardware. However, most of this work has focused on learning successful task execution rather than addressing failure recovery explicitly.

A key challenge in transferring knowledge from human demonstrations to robots is the "embodiment gap," which encompasses differences in kinematics, contact mechanics, control frequency, and action representation between human hands and robot grippers. Researchers have explored transferring compact behavior descriptions, such as latent plans, skills, affordances, or intent variables, rather than raw trajectories, to bridge this gap.

This research builds upon these areas by specifically exploiting the efficiency of egocentric human data collection for acquiring *failure recovery* data. It proposes a novel approach to overcome the embodiment gap for recovery behaviors by transferring a compact "corrective intent" rather than direct human motion, and integrating a mechanism to activate this intent only when recovery is needed. This positions EgoRecovery as a contribution to scalable, robust robot learning, bridging the gap between efficient human data collection and effective robot recovery in complex scenarios.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the recognized need for embodied robots to be resilient and capable of recovering from errors to operate reliably in dynamic, real-world settings. Policies trained solely on successful demonstrations often lack this robustness, leading to performance degradation when unexpected events or execution inaccuracies cause the robot to deviate from the nominal task trajectory. Explicit recovery data is crucial for addressing this, but its acquisition presents significant challenges.

The key motivations are detailed as follows:
- **Cost and Scalability of Robot Recovery Data Collection:** Collecting recovery trajectories through robot teleoperation is inherently expensive and difficult to scale. Each recovery episode typically requires:
 - **Inducing a meaningful failure state:** This often involves manual staging or allowing the robot to fail, which can be time-consuming and unreliable for generating diverse failure modes.
 - **Performing corrective actions:** Teleoperating precise corrective movements can be complex and demanding for human operators.
 - **Resetting the environment:** After each recovery attempt, the environment must be reset to a new failure state or the initial state, adding to the overall time and labor cost.
- **Diversity of Failure Modes:** The vast and often unpredictable nature of failure modes necessitates a much larger and more diverse dataset for robust recovery policies compared to successful task demonstrations. Covering this wide distribution of off-manifold states significantly exacerbates data collection costs.
- **Leveraging Egocentric Human Data for Efficiency:** Human operators can set up task-level failure configurations and record short, effective recovery segments much more quickly than teleoperating a robot through similar scenarios. The authors empirically demonstrate this efficiency, showing that human operators can generate over 10 times more valid recovery data per hour compared to robot teleoperation under their protocol.
- **Addressing the Embodiment Gap for Recovery:** While egocentric human data offers scalability, direct transfer of human motions or actions to a robot is problematic due to differences in kinematics, contact mechanics, and control interfaces. The goal is not to copy human trajectories but to extract transferable "corrective intent" that can guide robot actions. This intent needs to capture the essence of correction—when and how much adjustment is needed—without being specific to human physical execution.
- **Developing a Co-training Framework:** The overarching objective is to develop a robust co-training framework, EgoRecovery, that can effectively utilize scalable human recovery demonstrations by aligning them with robot data through a shared corrective intent space. This framework aims to enable robots to learn diverse recovery behaviors while minimizing the need for extensive, costly robot-specific recovery data.

In summary, the research is motivated by the need for robust robot recovery, the high cost of collecting robot recovery data, and the potential of scalable human egocentric data, provided the embodiment gap and contextual application of recovery behavior can be effectively addressed.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

EgoRecovery is designed as a co-training framework to enable robots to learn recovery behaviors by leveraging egocentric human recovery demonstrations, while addressing the embodiment gap and ensuring corrective actions are applied only when necessary.

#### 3.1. Human and Robot Data Collection

The method utilizes four types of data:
- **Robot Success Data:** Provides nominal robot action labels for successful task execution.
- **Robot Recovery Data:** Contains failed robot start states and corresponding corrective actions.
- **Human Success Data:** Similar to robot success, but from human egocentric perspective.
- **Human Recovery Data:** Captures human operators performing recovery actions from staged task-level failure configurations. This data is collected by arranging failure setups, recording short recovery segments, and moving quickly to the next failure mode, resulting in over 10 times more valid recovery episodes per hour compared to robot teleoperation (Table 1).

**Recovery Labels:** Each recovery episode (human or robot) is annotated with a temporal boundary, $t_{rec}$, which marks the point where corrective motion concludes and ordinary task execution resumes. This boundary is used to define two frame-level labels:
- **Recovery State Label ($s_t$):** Set to `true` for frames before or at $t_{rec}$ and `false` thereafter. This supervises the **Recovery Gate Head**.
- **Intent Mask ($m_t$):** A stricter mask set to `true` for frames where the future motion window remains within the recovery portion and passes checks for padding, truncation, missing motion, or near-stationary motion. This masks the **corrective intent loss**. Success episodes set both $s_t$ and $m_t$ to `false`.

#### 3.2. Corrective Intent for Human to Robot Recovery Learning

To bridge the embodiment gap, EgoRecovery transfers a compact "corrective intent" rather than direct human motion. This intent is defined as a coarse envelope of the remaining recovery motion, describing the *timing* and *magnitude* of a short correction, while abstracting away embodiment-specific details like exact direction or contact mechanics.

The corrective intent target ($y_t$) is constructed from short future end-effector motion windows. For a frame $t$ with an active end-effector $a$ of embodiment $d$:
1. **Relative Future Motion ($\Delta P_{d,t,a}$):** The future active end-effector positions over a window of length $L$ are computed relative to the current position.
2. **Embodiment Scaling:** This relative motion is normalized by an embodiment-specific scale factor ($s_d$) to account for size differences.
3. **DCT Projection:** The normalized future motion is projected onto the first $K$ low-frequency Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT) basis vectors ($C_K$). This compresses the temporal profile of the motion.
4. **Magnitude Extraction:** The $l_2$ norm of these DCT coefficients is taken across all active effectors for each coefficient, forming the $K \times A$ dimensional target vector $y_t \in \mathbb{R}^{KA}$. In main experiments, $K=4$ coefficients and one active effector result in a 4D target.

The intent mask ($m_t$) determines when this target supervises the policy, ensuring that the intent loss is applied only to valid recovery windows and not to nominal or invalid segments. Human recovery data primarily trains the intent predictor on diverse failure states, while robot recovery data provides the same intent target alongside executable robot action labels.

#### 3.3. Gated Intent Modulation for Robot Recovery Execution

The predicted corrective intent ($c_t$) should influence robot actions only when recovery is needed, to avoid interfering with nominal task execution. This is achieved through a gated modulation mechanism:

1. **Policy Architecture:**
 - A **heterogeneous backbone** (HPT-style) is employed, featuring embodiment-specific observation adapters feeding into a shared visual and Transformer trunk.
 - A **shared Intent Head** predicts the continuous corrective intent $c_t \in \mathbb{R}^{KA}$.
 - A **shared Recovery Gate Head** predicts $p_t$, the probability that the current observation requires recovery behavior, based on visual features.
 - A **robot action decoder** maps robot features to robot actions. An auxiliary human action decoder maps human features to human motions, but its output does not supervise the robot decoder.

2. **Gated FiLM Modulation:** The predicted intent $c_t$ modulates the features of the robot action decoder ($H_t$) using a gated residual Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM) layer:
 - A scalar gate $\alpha_t = \text{sg}(p_t)$ (with stop-gradient) is computed from the predicted recovery probability $p_t$.
 - The robot decoder feature $H_t$ is modulated as $\tilde{H}_t = H_t + \alpha_t (H_t \odot \Gamma(c_t) + B(c_t))$, where $\Gamma$ and $B$ are functions mapping $c_t$ to scale and shift terms.
 - If $p_t$ is low, $\alpha_t$ is near zero, and $\tilde{H}_t$ approximates $H_t$, preserving nominal execution. If $p_t$ is high, $\alpha_t$ is near one, and $c_t$ actively modulates $H_t$ before action prediction.

3. **Training Objective:** The total loss is a sum of several components:
 - $\mathcal{L}_{rbc}$: Behavior cloning loss on robot success and robot recovery data for the robot decoder.
 - $\mathcal{L}_{hbc}$: Behavior cloning loss for the auxiliary human decoder.
 - $\mathcal{L}_c$: Intent loss (L1 norm) between predicted intent $c_t$ and target $y_t$, applied only when $m_t$ is `true`.
 - $\mathcal{L}_g$: Binary Cross-Entropy loss for the Recovery Gate Head, using $s_t$ as the target.
 - $\mathcal{L}_n$: Nominal regularizer that penalizes non-zero intent modulation ($\Gamma(c_t), B(c_t)$) when the robot is in a non-recovery state ($s_t = \text{false}$).

At deployment, the robot relies solely on its observations, the predicted intent, the predicted gate, and its action decoder to perform tasks and recover from failures, without access to human videos or recovery labels.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates EgoRecovery through real-world closed-loop rollouts on four tabletop manipulation tasks: cup brush insertion, table sweep, round disk placement, and cube stacking. Performance is measured by Initial Success Rate (Initial SR) from normal starts and Recovery Success Rate (Recovery SR) from off-nominal failure starts. Each method is tested with 20 rollouts per task for both start-state regimes.

#### 4.1. Main Closed-Loop Comparison (Figure 4)

- **Necessity of Recovery Supervision:** Methods trained solely on nominal demonstrations (e.g., Robot success (50), or Robot success (50) + Human success (300)) achieved 0.0% average Recovery SR. This indicates that explicit recovery supervision is essential for handling failure states, as nominal training alone is insufficient.
- **Role of Human Recovery Data:** Adding human recovery data alone (Robot success (50) + Human recovery (300)) provided limited benefit, achieving only 8.8% average Recovery SR. However, when human recovery data was combined with a foundational robot recovery anchor (Robot success (50) + Robot recovery (50)), performance improved substantially. The "Direct human recovery mix" (Robot success (50) + Robot recovery (50) + Human recovery (300)) achieved 71.2% average Recovery SR.
- **EgoRecovery's Performance Advantage:** EgoRecovery, which routes human recovery data through its corrective-intent bottleneck and Recovery Gate Head, further improved performance. It achieved 85.0% average Recovery SR, a 13.8 percentage point increase over the "Direct human recovery mix." Additionally, EgoRecovery achieved the highest average Initial SR of 80.0%. This highlights that the specific architecture for transferring human recovery through a gated, intent-specific pathway is crucial, not just the presence of human recovery data.

#### 4.2. Data Efficiency (Figures 5, 6, Table 1)

- **Collection Throughput:** A data collection audit (Table 1) revealed that human recovery demonstrations could be collected approximately 10.5 times faster (averaging 516.5 episodes per operator hour) than robot recovery demonstrations (averaging 49.0 episodes per operator hour). This directly supports the scalability claim of using human data.
- **Human Data Scaling:** Increasing the volume of human recovery data (from 200 to 800 episodes) under a fixed robot recovery grounding (Robot success (50) + Robot recovery (50)) consistently improved EgoRecovery's performance. Recovery SR rose from 75.0% (200 episodes) to 90.0% (800 episodes), demonstrating that additional human recovery data, when transferred through EgoRecovery, directly enhances recovery capabilities more effectively than adding nominal human success data.
- **Cost-Matched Substitution:** When the total "robot-equivalent recovery budget" was fixed ($B_{RobotEq} = 80$, considering 1 human recovery = 0.1 robot recovery), a moderate substitution of robot recovery with human recovery (e.g., 50 robot recovery + 300 human recovery) yielded 85.0% Recovery SR for EgoRecovery. This significantly outperformed the "Robot-Only Reference" (80 robot recovery) which achieved 60.0% Recovery SR. This suggests that human recovery can efficiently augment robot data to improve coverage.
- **Low Robot Grounding:** Even with a low robot recovery grounding budget (Robot recovery (20)), EgoRecovery showed steady improvements in Recovery SR as human recovery data increased (from 52.5% at 100 human recovery to 75.0% at 600 human recovery). However, this did not fully close the gap to settings with more robot recovery, indicating that human recovery augments rather than entirely replaces the need for some robot-specific grounding.

#### 4.3. Ablation Study (Table 2)

An ablation study systematically investigated the contribution of EgoRecovery's core components:

- **Corrective Intent Supervision:** Removing the intent loss reduced average Recovery SR from 85.0% to 65.0%. Further removing the corrective segment mask, which defines valid intent supervision regions, led to a drop to 55.0% Recovery SR. This confirms that both the low-frequency DCT magnitude target and its recovery-specific masking are vital for the corrective intent bottleneck.
- **Intent Modulation:** If the learned intent did not modulate the robot action decoder ("without intent modulation" or "$c_t$ zero at test time"), average Recovery SR dropped to 65.0%. This indicates that the corrective intent must actively influence the robot's action generation, rather than serving as a passive auxiliary representation.
- **Recovery Gate Head:** The "always on modulation" variant, which bypasses the Recovery Gate Head, resulted in the largest drop in average Initial SR (from 80.0% to 70.0%), while maintaining a competitive Recovery SR of 70.0%. This demonstrates the gate's role in protecting nominal task execution by activating corrective intent only when truly needed, preventing interference during non-recovery states.

Overall, the findings demonstrate EgoRecovery's effectiveness in improving robot recovery from failures through a scalable, gated, and intent-driven transfer of human recovery demonstrations, while maintaining strong nominal task execution.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant advancement in the development of robust embodied robots, particularly in addressing the critical challenge of failure recovery. The core significance and potential impact are multifaceted:

- **Scalable Acquisition of Recovery Capabilities:** The most direct impact of EgoRecovery is its demonstration of a scalable method for acquiring failure recovery behaviors. By leveraging egocentric human demonstrations, which are shown to be over 10 times more efficient to collect than robot teleoperation data, the framework mitigates a major bottleneck in developing robust robot policies. This reduced data collection cost can accelerate the deployment of robots in real-world, less structured environments where encountering failures is inevitable.
- **Bridging the Embodiment Gap for Recovery:** The proposed "corrective intent" mechanism offers a principled approach to transfer knowledge across physically distinct embodiments (human hands vs. robot grippers). Instead of attempting a direct, often problematic, mapping of human actions to robot actions, EgoRecovery extracts a compact, abstract representation of "how much correction is needed." This abstraction is more transferable and allows the robot to learn its specific executable actions from its own limited data, guided by the general corrective signal from humans. This approach has implications for broader cross-embodiment learning tasks beyond just recovery.
- **Context-Aware Policy Modulation:** The introduction of the "Recovery Gate Head" allows the robot policy to contextually apply corrective intent. This ensures that the recovery mechanism is activated only when a failure state is detected, preventing interference with nominal task execution. This selective activation contributes to both improved recovery performance and the preservation of high success rates during normal operation, which is crucial for overall system reliability.
- **Enhanced Robot Robustness and Reliability:** By enabling robots to effectively recover from common off-nominal states, EgoRecovery contributes to making embodied AI systems more robust and reliable. This improved resilience is vital for applications ranging from industrial automation and logistics to assistive robotics and household tasks, where autonomous agents must adapt to unforeseen circumstances and recover gracefully from errors to complete long-horizon tasks.
- **Potential for Broader Application in Embodied AI:** While the current work focuses on tabletop manipulation tasks and related failure modes, the underlying principles of using scalable human data for specific behavioral aspects (like recovery), combined with abstract, gated transfer mechanisms, could be extended to other complex robot skills, generalizable representations, and diverse environments. Future research might explore transfer to qualitatively new recovery skills or more abstract failure concepts.

**Limitations:**
The authors acknowledge several limitations. The findings are currently restricted to "related off-nominal states within the same task-level recovery family" in tabletop manipulation. This implies that the current framework might not directly transfer to entirely novel failure modes or require new skills that deviate significantly from the learned recovery patterns. Furthermore, while human recovery data significantly reduces the burden, it does not fully replace the need for robot recovery grounding. Robot-specific data remains essential for learning embodiment-specific intricacies, such as precise contact handling, regrasping, or completely new recovery strategies.

Despite these limitations, EgoRecovery represents a concrete step towards more intelligent and resilient robotic systems, demonstrating a practical pathway to endow robots with a crucial capability for real-world deployment.
