# JRDB-AVR: An Active Visual Reasoning Benchmark for Embodied Agents in Real-World Environments

> [!summary] Summary
> JRDB-AVR is a real-world embodied active visual reasoning benchmark built from JRDB panoramic robot videos. An agent requests observations by timestamp and viewing angle, and is scored on both its answer and its bounding-box evidence. A reference agent, JRDB-AVR-Agent, plans each question as a graph query and keeps an observation-grounded graph world model. It reaches a 14.54 combined score, versus 5.96 for the best VLM baseline.

## Problem

- Existing visual reasoning benchmarks such as GQA, STAR and JRDB-Reasoning supply pre-selected passive observations, so they never test whether an agent can decide where and when to look.
- Prior evaluation scores only final answers, so VLMs that answer correctly from language priors or partial context look the same as agents truly grounded in the right visual evidence.
- Real active embodied reasoning data is hard to collect because a live robot cannot watch every direction and moment at once, so no real-world testbed for evidence acquisition exists.

## Method

- Structured question-generation engine: parses JRDB, JRDB-Act, JRDB-Social and JRDB-Reasoning annotations into ==temporal scene graphs==, where seven generators search subgraphs for multi-hop, temporal, viewpoint and action-boundary questions, yielding 2,098 questions.
- Validation filters and active protocol: questions must pass evidence, temporal, hop and view necessity, and uniqueness checks. Agents call observe(sequence, frame, angle) for 480x480 crops and are scored on answer, evidence and combined correctness.
- **JRDB-AVR-Agent**: a training-free VLM agent that turns questions into schema-validated graph plans, writes only observation-grounded entities, attributes and relations into an explicit ==graph world model==, and answers through a ==graph solver==.

## Results

- JRDB-AVR-Agent on Qwen3.5 9B reaches **38.51** answer, **27.50** evidence and **14.54** combined, beating the best baselines across six VLM backbones (33.65, 13.01 and 5.96 respectively).
- The conditional hallucination rate, meaning correct answers without correct evidence, falls from the lowest baseline's **81.22%** to **62.25%**. Passive monolithic VLM and Chain-of-Thought baselines mostly exceed **88%**, and reach about **99.8%** on Gemma-4.
- Ablation: active observation alone lifts combined score from 2.72 to 9.20. Adding the world model raises evidence from 18.22 to 27.50 and combined to 14.54. The world model without active observation reaches only 3.62.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Answer accuracy is an unsafe proxy for visual reasoning: most correct VLM answers lack correct evidence, so embodied benchmarks should score grounding together with answers.
> - Letting agents choose when and where to look matters more than reasoning style: Chain-of-Thought over passive context barely helps grounding, while active observation more than triples the combined score.
> - Explicit, provenance-tracked memory that admits only observed facts mainly improves evidence binding rather than answers, suggesting structured world models reduce hallucination more than they raise accuracy.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A robot with a narrow view may need to identify someone in a crowd, look at another time or angle, and then explain which person and observation support its answer. In one illustrated case, the agent must find a person standing near another person who is sitting and working on a laptop behind them. It must then determine the first person’s action after leaving a counter. Identifying the right person and action may require looking across time and direction. A plausible answer alone does not show that the agent found the right evidence.

The authors’ benchmark, JRDB-AVR, lets the agent choose its observations and checks both its answer and its supporting evidence. Current baselines’ answer scores can substantially exceed their evidence and combined scores.

Bottom: in the passive setup, a preselected visual input leads to an answer. In the active setup, an agent decides whether to observe, then returns an answer and evidence for evaluation. This is a schematic, not an experimental result.

### 2. Choosing where and when to look

A robot’s limited field of view means useful evidence can be distributed across moments, directions, and people. JRDB-AVR repurposes JRDB panoramic footage as an observation interface. The agent receives a question. It then requests a timestamp and viewing angle, instead of being handed one complete, fixed view. It must submit an answer and a target localization. The benchmark evaluates decisions over recorded observations, not control of a live robot.

The benchmark reports four scores:

- **Answer score:** the fraction of questions whose answer meets its answer-mode rule.
- **Evidence score:** whether the target is correctly identified. When box annotations are available, it also checks whether the predicted box exceeds the required IoU threshold in a frame where the target appears.
- **Combined score:** counts a question only when both answer and evidence are correct on that question.
- **Hallucination rate:** among answer-correct predictions, the proportion lacking correct evidence.

The authors’ reference agent converts the question into an anchor-to-target graph plan. It requests observations as needed. It stores entities, attributes, and relations only when they are tied to an observed frame and view. A graph solver then produces the answer and evidence. This is an explicit, auditable graph memory, not a predictive video model.

### 3. The answer can outrun its evidence

The first comparison uses a matched backbone on the 2,098-question benchmark. The Qwen3.5 9B ReAct baseline scores 32.98% answer, 11.82% evidence, 5.96% combined, and 81.94% conditional hallucination. JRDB-AVR-Agent, also using Qwen3.5 9B, scores 38.51%, 27.50%, 14.54%, and 62.25%, respectively. The 62.25% figure is not the share of all answers that are hallucinations. It is the share of answer-correct predictions without correct evidence. Answer performance does not guarantee evidence grounding. The proposed agent’s absolute combined score remains low, so the benchmark is still difficult.

The second comparison is an ablation, which is the strongest test of the mechanism. Active observation stays on in both conditions. Enabling the world-model/planning package raises evidence from 18.22% to 27.50% and combined correctness from 9.20% to 14.54%. Answer rises from 37.66% to 38.51%, and conditional hallucination falls from 75.57% to 62.25%. The toggle switches the paper’s bundled package, including graph planning and persistent world-model state. It does not identify which internal piece caused the difference.

Ablation of active observation and the world model. All scores are percentages, and × and ✓ mark component presence. Comparing the last two rows shows the world-model-package contrast while active observation is held on.

### 4. What counts as seeing

In an embodied visual question, a plausible or correct answer is not enough to show grounding; the system must also identify visual support for it. JRDB-AVR makes that distinction measurable by scoring the answer and the evidence together. Its reference agent improves evidence and combined scores in this setup. Even so, the absolute combined score remains low, and the ablation does not isolate which part of the bundled package drives the gain.
