# FailureSpot: Label-Efficient Timestamp-Level Failure Detection for Vision-Language-Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> FailureSpot is a label-efficient framework for timestamp-level failure detection in vision-language-action policies. It pretrains a lightweight MLP or LSTM detector on the policy's internal representations using weak pseudo-labels derived from action-chunk consistency and magnitude, then fine-tunes on densely annotated trajectories chosen by uncertainty-based active learning. With a 15% annotation budget it improves timestamp-level and trajectory-level detection across three VLA policies.

## Problem

- Vision-based failure detectors are reactive: they flag errors only after the bad action executes and becomes visible in camera observations, precluding early intervention.
- Proactive detectors supervise per-timestamp scores with trajectory-level labels, so normal pre-failure timestamps inside failed runs are mislabeled as failure, injecting substantial label noise.
- That mismatch blurs the normal/failure boundary, degrading both trajectory accuracy and onset localization, while the dense annotation that would fix it needs costly frame-by-frame inspection.

## Method

- ==Action-derived weak supervision== builds soft pseudo-labels from three signals: ==short-term chunk consistency==, ==full-chunk magnitude==, ==executed-action magnitude==, each blended with an ==exponential moving average== and percentile-normalized.
- ==Uncertainty-based active learning== ranks unlabeled trajectories by mean per-timestamp prediction entropy, requests dense timestamp labels only for the top-ranked 15%, then fine-tunes the detector with binary classification loss.
- A fine-grained annotation protocol marks failure-onset and optional recovery times per trajectory; ==functional conformal prediction== converts detector scores into a time-varying alarm threshold and onset estimate.

## Results

- FailureSpot-MLP reaches **95.3**/**90.5** timestamp-level AUROC on seen/unseen pi-0 tasks versus **82.8**/**76.6** for SAFE-MLP, and also leads trajectory-level AUROC on pi-0 and pi-0-FAST.
- Ablation on pi-0: weak supervision alone collapses to **33.7** average timestamp AUROC, random fine-tuning reaches **79.5**, and active selection lifts the full method to **92.9**.
- On OpenVLA, whose single non-overlapping actions void the consistency signal, trajectory-level AUROC falls behind SAFE; data sweeps show timestamp labels help far more than extra pretraining trajectories.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Label granularity beats label volume: a small densely annotated subset localizes failure onset better than abundant trajectory-level labels that mislabel normal pre-failure behavior.
> - Cheap action-space statistics work as a pretraining prior but fail as a standalone detector; a modest amount of ground-truth supervision is needed to calibrate the boundary.
> - Consistency cues presuppose overlapping action chunks, so autoregressive single-action policies lose a key signal; weak-supervision design must match the policy's action interface.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-language-action (VLA) policies represent a paradigm in robotics for general-purpose manipulation, enabling robots to interpret language instructions and translate visual observations into action sequences. Examples of such policies include RT-1, RT-2, Octo, OpenVLA, ACT, $\pi_0$, and $\pi_0$-FAST. Despite their generalization capabilities, VLA policies exhibit unpredictable failures during long-horizon task execution, necessitating reliable failure detection for safe and robust deployment.

Existing failure detection methods for VLAs generally fall into two categories: reactive and proactive. Reactive approaches typically rely on visual models to analyze camera observations after actions have been executed, detecting failures only once their behavioral consequences are visible (e.g., Du et al. 2023, Duan et al. 2024). While effective, these methods are inherently delayed and computationally intensive, limiting their utility for real-time intervention.

Proactive approaches, conversely, aim to detect failures before actions are executed, often utilizing internal representations of the VLA policy. Some methods involve large Vision-Language Models (VLMs) for proactive failure prediction (e.g., Yang et al. 2026, Ma et al. 2026a), but these can be computationally expensive for real-time monitoring. A separate line of work develops lightweight detectors operating on VLA output action chunks (e.g., STAC by Agia et al. 2024, ActProbe by Huang et al. 2026) or learned internal representations (e.g., FAIL-Detect by Xu et al. 2025, SAFE by Gu et al. 2026, Foresight by Zhang et al. 2026b). These lightweight detectors offer proactive alarms with lower inference costs.

A key limitation of many existing proactive methods, particularly approaches like SAFE, is their reliance on trajectory-level failure labels (success or failure for an entire robot run) to supervise timestamp-level detection. This introduces significant label noise, as initial normal behavior within an unsuccessful trajectory is erroneously labeled as a failure. This supervision mismatch degrades both the accuracy of trajectory-level detection and the precision of timestamp-level failure localization, often leading to alarms that are either too early or too late relative to the actual failure onset. While concurrent work such as Hide-and-Seek (Park et al. 2026) attempts to address this through contrastive learning, its primary evaluation remains at the trajectory level.

This paper addresses the limitation of imprecise failure localization and label noise in proactive VLA failure detection by focusing on fine-grained timestamp-level detection. It acknowledges the high cost of dense, manual timestamp-level annotation and proposes a label-efficient framework to mitigate this challenge, thereby advancing the field towards more accurate and practical real-time failure monitoring for VLA systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to advance failure detection for Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies by enabling precise timestamp-level failure localization. This involves identifying not only *if* a robot execution will ultimately fail, but critically, *when* the failure begins during the task.

The motivation for this objective stems from several factors:
- **Unpredictable Failures in VLA Policies:** VLA policies, despite their capabilities, are prone to diverse and unpredictable failures during long-horizon tasks, such as missing target objects, getting stuck, becoming idle, or executing unstable actions. For safe and reliable deployment of robotic systems, particularly in real-world scenarios, detecting these failures is essential for timely intervention, recovery, or safe termination of the task.
- **Limitations of Reactive Detection:** Existing reactive failure detection methods rely on visual observations, meaning they can only identify a failure after the erroneous action has been executed and its consequences are visibly manifest. This delay makes them unsuitable for proactive intervention that could prevent further errors or unsafe conditions.
- **Inadequacy of Trajectory-Level Supervision for Timestamp Detection:** Current proactive methods, while addressing the timeliness issue, frequently use coarse trajectory-level success/failure labels to train detectors that output timestamp-level failure scores. As illustrated in the paper, this creates a significant supervision mismatch. Normal pre-failure timestamps within an ultimately unsuccessful trajectory are incorrectly labeled as failure instances. This noise hinders the detector's ability to learn a distinct boundary between normal and anomalous behavior, leading to less accurate trajectory-level classification and imprecise identification of the failure onset time (alarms being too early or too late).
- **High Cost of Dense Annotation:** Directly training a timestamp-level failure detector requires fine-grained, per-timestamp labels for robot trajectories. Manually inspecting frames and annotating the exact onset of failure is labor-intensive and costly, posing a significant barrier to applying such methods on a large scale.

Therefore, the key motivation is to develop a method that can achieve accurate timestamp-level failure detection to facilitate early intervention, while simultaneously overcoming the prohibitive cost associated with obtaining dense, fine-grained human annotations.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors propose FailureSpot, a data-efficient, two-stage framework designed for timestamp-level VLA failure detection. The problem is formulated as a timestamp-level binary classification task, where a lightweight detector (either an MLP or an LSTM) takes internal VLA policy representations $h_{i,t}$ as input and outputs a failure probability $p_{i,t}$.

**Timestamp-Level Failure Annotation:**
For ground truth and targeted active learning, a process for timestamp-level annotation is introduced. Each timestamp is manually labeled binary ($y_{i,t} \in \{0, 1\}$), with $y_{i,t}=1$ indicating a failure state (e.g., robot deviating from task, getting stuck, idle, or moving unpredictably). For failed trajectories, timestamps from the deviation onset ($t_{dev}$) onwards are labeled 1. For trajectories that recover after a deviation, only timestamps between $t_{dev}$ and recovery time ($t_{rec}$) are labeled 1. Normal trajectories have all timestamps labeled 0. This fine-grained annotation enables precise training and evaluation.

**Framework Overview (Two Stages):**

**Stage 1: Pretraining with Weak Supervision (Action-Derived)**
This stage aims to pretrain the detector without requiring human timestamp-level labels, by leveraging observable abnormal patterns in the VLA's predicted action chunks.
1. **Action Chunk Representation:** At each timestamp $t$ in trajectory $i$, the VLA outputs an action chunk $A_{i,t} = [a^1_{i,t}, \dots, a^H_{i,t}]$, where $a^h_{i,t} \in \mathbb{R}^D$ is the $h$-th action in the chunk of length $H$. The robot executes the first $K$ actions before replanning ($K \le H$). For autoregressive policies (e.g., OpenVLA), $H=K=1$.
2. **Weak Supervision Signals:** Three signals are defined:
 - **Short-term action consistency ($c_{i,t}$):** This measures the average $\ell_2$ distance between overlapping actions of consecutive chunks. For chunked models (e.g., $\pi_0$, $\pi_0$-FAST), $c_{i,t} = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M} \|a^{K+j}_{i,t-1} - a^j_{i,t}\|_2$, where $M = \min(K, H-K)$. For autoregressive policies, it is the $\ell_2$ distance between consecutive single actions $c_{i,t} = \|a_{i,t-1} - a_{i,t}\|_2$. Higher values indicate instability.
 - **Full-chunk action magnitude ($r_{i,t}$):** The average $\ell_2$ norm of all $H$ actions in the chunk, $r_{i,t} = \frac{1}{H}\sum_{j=1}^{H} \|a^j_{i,t}\|_2$.
 - **Executed action magnitude ($r^{exec}_{i,t}$):** The average $\ell_2$ norm of the first $K$ executed actions, $r^{exec}_{i,t} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=1}^{K} \|a^j_{i,t}\|_2$. Abnormally small magnitudes may indicate stagnation (frozen/idle), while large/unstable magnitudes suggest aggressive random motions.
3. **Exponential Moving Average (EMA):** An EMA, $E(u)_{i,t} = \beta E(u)_{i,t-1} + (1-\beta)u_{i,t}$ (with $\beta=0.8$), is computed for each signal to capture temporal trends and reduce noise.
4. **Combined Signal:** The instantaneous and EMA values of all three signals are combined into a single scalar score: $s_{i,t} = (c_{i,t} + E(c)_{i,t}) + \eta_1(r_{i,t} + E(r)_{i,t}) + \eta_2(r^{exec}_{i,t} + E(r^{exec})_{i,t})$, where $\eta_1, \eta_2$ are hyperparameters.
5. **Normalization:** The combined score $s_{i,t}$ is normalized using the 1st and 99th percentiles ($s_{low}, s_{high}$) from the training set, and clipped to $[0, 1]$ to produce $\tilde{s}_{i,t}$, which serves as the soft timestamp-level pseudo-label for pretraining the detector.

**Stage 2: Fine-tuning with Active Learning**
To refine the detector using high-quality labels while minimizing annotation costs, active learning is employed.
1. **Uncertainty Estimation:** The pretrained detector's uncertainty for each timestamp $p_{i,t}$ is quantified using entropy: $H(p_{i,t}) = -p_{i,t} \log p_{i,t} - (1-p_{i,t}) \log(1-p_{i,t})$.
2. **Trajectory Selection:** Since timestamp-level annotation is more practical on complete trajectories, the uncertainty for a trajectory $U_i$ is calculated as the average entropy across its timestamps: $U_i = \frac{1}{T_i}\sum_{t=1}^{T_i} H(p_{i,t})$. The most uncertain (top-ranked) unlabeled trajectories are then selected for human timestamp-level annotation.
3. **Fine-tuning:** The detector is fine-tuned on this small, densely annotated subset of trajectories using a supervised binary classification loss. This stage leverages the accuracy of human labels to refine the failure boundaries learned during pretraining.

**Conformal Prediction for Decision Thresholds:**
To convert the continuous failure scores $p_{i,t}$ into binary failure alarms, functional conformal prediction is used. This method establishes a one-sided, time-varying conformal band $b_\alpha(t)$ using successful trajectories, allowing the decision threshold to adapt to natural temporal variations in scores during normal execution. A timestamp $t$ is classified as a failure if $p_{i,t} \geq b_\alpha(t)$, and the failure-onset time $\tau_\alpha$ is the first such timestamp.

**Experimental Settings:**
- **Benchmarks:** LIBERO-10 (LIBERO-Long), a suite of 10 long-horizon manipulation tasks.
- **VLA Policies:** $\pi_0$, $\pi_0$-FAST (chunked policies), and OpenVLA (autoregressive policy) are evaluated.
- **Data:** 50 trajectories per task (500 total) for each policy.
- **Data Splits:** Three tasks designated as "seen" (60% training, 40% seen-task evaluation), and seven tasks as "unseen" (all for evaluation).
- **Annotation Budget:** A fixed 15% of the seen-task training trajectories are selected for timestamp-level annotation.
- **Baselines:** Include training-free detectors (STAC-Single), detectors trained with trajectory-level labels (LogpZO, SAFE-MLP/LSTM, ActProbe), and detectors trained directly with timestamp-level labels (MLP/LSTM with 15% budget).
- **Metrics:** Trajectory-level and timestamp-level AUROC for ranking quality. For threshold-based evaluation, timestamp-level balanced accuracy (BalACC_ts) and average normalized detected failure-onset time, analyzed across different conformal significance levels ($\alpha$).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted across three VLA policies ($\pi_0$, $\pi_0$-FAST, and OpenVLA) on the LIBERO-10 benchmark yielded several findings:

- **Overall Performance (Table 1):** FailureSpot achieved the highest overall timestamp-level AUROC across all three VLA policies. This indicates that the proposed framework is capable of learning fine-grained failure signals effectively, even with a limited human annotation budget (15% of training trajectories). For trajectory-level detection, FailureSpot achieved the best AUROC on $\pi_0$ and $\pi_0$-FAST. However, its trajectory-level performance on OpenVLA was slightly lower, which the authors suggest might be due to the autoregressive nature of OpenVLA (H=K=1) limiting the effectiveness of the short-term action consistency signal that relies on chunk overlaps.

- **Accuracy and Failure-Onset Time (Figure 2):** When evaluating balanced accuracy against detected failure-onset time, FailureSpot demonstrated superior overall performance. It maintained high balanced accuracy across a range of conformal prediction significance levels ($\alpha$). Crucially, at the $\alpha$ value yielding the highest balanced accuracy, the detector triggered failure alarms *before* the average ground-truth failure-onset time, which suggests it enables timely intervention without compromising detection accuracy.

- **Ablation Study (Table 2):** The ablation studies confirmed the individual contributions of the framework's components:
 - Pretraining solely with action-derived weak supervision was insufficient for high performance.
 - Adding timestamp-level supervision significantly improved performance, even with a limited budget.
 - Active learning further enhanced the effectiveness of this limited annotation budget compared to random selection of trajectories for fine-tuning.
 - The full FailureSpot framework, combining action-derived weak supervision pretraining with active-learning-based fine-tuning, consistently achieved the best timestamp-level AUROC across all policies and strong trajectory-level performance, highlighting the complementary benefits of both stages.

- **Impact of Training Data Amount (Figure 3):** Varying the amount of pretraining data (trajectory-level) and fine-tuning data (timestamp-level) showed that increasing either source improved performance. However, the gains from timestamp-level annotations were observed to be substantially larger. The chosen configuration of 60% trajectories for weak-supervision pretraining and 15% timestamp-level annotated trajectories for fine-tuning was identified as a favorable trade-off between labeling cost and detection performance.

- **Visualization of Timestamp-Level Failure Detection (Figure 4):** Qualitative examples illustrated that FailureSpot's detected failure scores remained low during normal robot operation and then rose sharply above the decision threshold just before the ground-truth failure onset. This visual evidence supports the claim that the detector can distinguish normal actions from emerging failures early, enabling proactive measures.

- **Failure Mode Discovery (Figure 5):** The analysis of failed trajectories identified three recurring failure modes across the VLA policies:
 1. **Repeated ineffective actions:** Characterized by strong temporal similarity in action chunks despite task failure (e.g., $\pi_0$ making repeated, unsuccessful grasp attempts).
 2. **Stagnation:** Where the gripper remains in a nearly fixed pose, and generated action magnitudes approach zero (common for OpenVLA).
 3. **Ineffective large-amplitude motion:** The robot moves significantly but without task progress, with large action magnitudes but decreasing consistency in consecutive predictions (e.g., $\pi_0$-FAST exhibiting excessive swinging motions). These observations provide insights into VLA failure behaviors and validate the use of both action magnitude and temporal consistency as complementary weak-supervision signals.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of robotic manipulation by introducing a label-efficient method for precise, timestamp-level failure detection in Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. The significance and potential impact are multifaceted:

- **Enhanced Safety and Reliability in Robotics:** By enabling the detection of failures *as they begin* rather than after they manifest or at the trajectory's end, FailureSpot facilitates timely intervention. This capability is crucial for enhancing the safety and reliability of VLA-controlled robots, particularly in long-horizon, complex, or safety-critical applications. Early warnings can prevent further damage, allow for recovery attempts, or initiate a safe shutdown, which is a substantial improvement over reactive detection.
- **Addressing the Annotation Bottleneck:** The proposed framework offers a practical solution to the high cost of data annotation for fine-grained failure detection. By combining action-derived weak supervision with uncertainty-based active learning, it significantly reduces the amount of manual, timestamp-level labeling required. This makes the development of robust failure detection systems more scalable and accessible for large robotic datasets.
- **Improved VLA Policy Deployment:** The ability to precisely localize failure onset can be used in several ways during robot deployment. For developers, it provides a tool to diagnose when and how VLA policies begin to deviate, aiding in debugging and improving policy robustness. For operators, it allows for more informed decision-making about when to intervene or switch to a different recovery strategy.
- **Generalizability Across VLA Architectures:** The evaluation across different VLA policies ($\pi_0$, $\pi_0$-FAST, OpenVLA), which utilize different action chunking strategies, suggests the method's robustness and potential for broad applicability across diverse VLA architectures. This indicates its utility as a general diagnostic tool.
- **Foundation for Advanced Intervention and Recovery:** By providing accurate timestamp-level failure signals, FailureSpot lays a foundation for future research in automated failure intervention and recovery systems. Knowing *when* a failure starts is a prerequisite for developing intelligent agents that can self-correct or invoke recovery protocols autonomously.
- **Insights into VLA Failure Modes:** The empirical discovery of distinct failure modes for different VLA policies (e.g., repeated ineffective actions for $\pi_0$, stagnation for OpenVLA, excessive swinging for $\pi_0$-FAST) offers valuable insights. This understanding can inform the design of more resilient VLA policies and guide the development of failure detection methods tailored to specific policy behaviors.

In summary, FailureSpot represents a step towards more intelligent and reliable robotic systems by providing an efficient and accurate mechanism for understanding and responding to VLA policy failures in a timely manner.
