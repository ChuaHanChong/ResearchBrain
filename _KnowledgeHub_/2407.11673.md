# Safe Learning of Locomotion Skills from MPC

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the Munich Institute of Robotics and Machine Intelligence developed LocoSafeDAGGER, an iterative learning algorithm that enables safer and more sample-efficient acquisition of locomotion policies for quadrupedal robots from a Model Predictive Control (MPC) expert. The algorithm reduces failure rates during training and improves push recovery capabilities by actively intervening with the MPC expert during unsafe states, while matching the robustness and tracking performance of policies trained via Behavioral Cloning.

## Problem

- Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) for legged robots is sample-inefficient and lacks inherent safety mechanisms, making it prone to failures in real-world deployment.
- Behavioral Cloning (BC) from expert demonstrations suffers from distribution shift, leading to compounding errors as policies encounter states not represented in the initial training data.
- Model Predictive Control (MPC) offers robust behaviors but is computationally intensive at runtime and can struggle with handling uncertainties, limiting its direct high-frequency application.

## Method

- Proposed LocoSafeDAGGER, an iterative online learning algorithm that adapts Data Aggregation (DAGGER) and SafeDAGGER for legged robots, combining a neural network policy with an **==MPC==** expert.
- Implemented a safety policy (πsafety) to actively monitor robot states during policy rollouts, enabling the MPC expert to immediately take over and stabilize the robot upon detecting unsafe conditions.
- Targeted data collection for the training dataset by only recording state-action pairs when the MPC expert intervened due to a safety violation or during an additional stabilization phase at the end of rollouts, focusing on critical states.

## Results

- LocoSafeDAGGER demonstrated a consistently low failure rate during data collection (below **5%** after the first iteration), significantly outperforming LocoDAGGER which had higher failure rates as expert influence decayed.
- The algorithm achieved comparable robustness and velocity tracking performance to Behavioral Cloning policies, while also exhibiting superior sample efficiency, requiring four times fewer policy rollouts than LocoDAGGER for similar database sizes.
- Policies trained with LocoSafeDAGGER showed superior push recovery capabilities across most impulse angles compared to both Behavioral Cloning and LocoDAGGER, attributed to the collection of data in specific unsafe or recovery scenarios.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Active safety monitoring with immediate expert intervention during online learning significantly reduces catastrophic failures, enabling safer policy training for physical robot deployment.
> - Targeted data collection in critical or unsafe states, rather than random expert influence, leads to more sample-efficient learning and policies that are more robust to external disturbances.
> - Iterative learning algorithms can effectively mitigate the distribution shift problem inherent in Behavioral Cloning by continuously expanding the training dataset with relevant out-of-distribution states encountered by the policy.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The control of legged robots in dynamic environments presents significant challenges, particularly in multi-contact scenarios. The current research landscape for legged locomotion control is largely dominated by two primary approaches: optimal control, often implemented through Model Predictive Control (MPC), and (deep) Reinforcement Learning (DRL).

MPC-based methods, such as those demonstrated in recent works for multi-gait quadrupedal locomotion, are known for producing robust behaviors. However, they typically suffer from intensive run-time computation and face difficulties in handling uncertainties, especially during contact events.

Conversely, DRL offers advantages by shifting the computational burden to an offline training phase, thus avoiding run-time computation issues. DRL can also address various uncertainties through techniques like domain randomization. Nevertheless, DRL is notably sample-inefficient, can suffer from policy instability in continuous control settings, and often requires extensive, task-specific reward shaping. A significant limitation of current DRL algorithms is their insufficient consideration of safety, which complicates their direct application for fine-tuning policies on real robot hardware.

Given these complementary strengths and weaknesses, a promising avenue in robotics research involves combining the benefits of optimal control and learning. Previous work has explored using MPC to guide the learning of optimal control policies. Some approaches, such as, utilize trajectory optimization to guide DRL exploration, which can simplify reward shaping but remains sample-inefficient and lacks inherent safety mechanisms for real-world learning. Other efforts, like, leverage MPC as an expert to learn control policies. For instance, learned a control Hamiltonian function from MPC data, which still required substantial run-time computation. focused on learning the value function of MPC to solve a one-step optimal control problem with reduced run-time computation via quadratic programming. demonstrated that by properly representing the action space, a policy could directly mimic MPC actions through offline dataset generation (behavioral cloning, BC), enabling a range of gaits and robust policies in sensor space.

However, behavioral cloning is widely recognized for its susceptibility to compounding errors, where small mistakes can lead the robot into states not represented in the training data, potentially causing failures. This distribution shift problem is a critical limitation for deploying BC-trained policies, especially on physical hardware where safety is paramount. Iterative supervised learning algorithms, such as Data Aggregation (DAGGER) and its variants like SafeDAGGER, have been proposed to mitigate distribution shift by actively expanding the training dataset with states encountered during policy rollouts. While SafeDAGGER, which allows an expert to intervene in unsafe situations, shows promise for real-world application, its use in legged robot locomotion has been limited due to the difficulty of generating expert data. Advances in optimal control solvers, allowing efficient Non-linear Model Predictive Control (NMPC) for locomotion, now enable MPC to serve as a viable expert. This paper aims to bridge this gap by adapting iterative online learning frameworks, specifically DAGGER and SafeDAGGER, to safely learn locomotion skills from an MPC expert, directly addressing the challenges of safety, sample efficiency, and distribution shift in legged robot control.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the acknowledged challenges in safely and robustly learning locomotion skills for quadrupedal robots. Current methods, while effective in certain aspects, exhibit limitations that hinder their deployment in real-world scenarios:

- **Safety Concerns in Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL):** DRL policies, though capable of handling uncertainties and operating offline, lack inherent safety mechanisms. This makes them prone to catastrophic failures, especially during fine-tuning on real hardware, where physical damage is a significant risk.
- **Sample Inefficiency in DRL:** DRL algorithms are notoriously sample-inefficient, requiring vast amounts of data to learn effective policies.
- **Distribution Shift in Behavioral Cloning (BC):** While BC offers simplicity and can learn from MPC data, it suffers from compounding errors. As a BC-trained policy executes, it can drift into states not present in its initial training dataset, leading to unpredictable behavior and potential failures. This limitation necessitates an approach that can actively adapt to the policy's encountered state distribution.
- **Computational Intensity of MPC:** Pure MPC approaches, despite their robustness, demand significant run-time computation, which can be restrictive for high-frequency control or onboard deployment.

To address these motivations, the paper sets forth the following key objectives:

1. **Enhance Safety During Training:** Develop an online learning framework that minimizes the number of failures during the policy training or fine-tuning process. This is crucial for enabling the direct learning and deployment of policies on physical robot hardware without incurring damage.
2. **Improve Data Efficiency:** Design a method that collects more relevant data, thereby covering a broader and more pertinent part of the state space. This objective aims to reduce the overall data requirements for learning robust locomotion policies.
3. **Mitigate Distribution Shift:** Create a framework that actively addresses the problem of distribution shift, ensuring that the policy is trained on data representative of the states it actually encounters during execution. This is expected to lead to more robust and reliable policies.

The main contributions of this paper are:
- The proposal of a new algorithm, LocoSafeDAGGER, specifically designed for safely learning locomotion policies from an expert controller (MPC).
- An extensive set of simulation experiments comparing LocoSafeDAGGER with existing methods (BC and LocoDAGGER, an adapted DAGGER version for locomotion). These experiments aim to demonstrate that LocoSafeDAGGER substantially reduces the number of failures during training and enhances robustness against external disturbances, all without compromising performance.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

This research proposes two iterative learning algorithms, LocoDAGGER and LocoSafeDAGGER, designed for learning legged locomotion policies from an MPC expert while considering the different operating frequencies of the policy and MPC.

**Background Algorithms:**
- **Behavioral Cloning (BC):** A form of imitation learning where a policy $\pi$ is trained offline on an expert dataset $\mathcal{D}$ comprising state-action pairs $(s, a)$. The objective is to maximize the likelihood of the policy's actions given observed states. While simple, BC is susceptible to compounding errors and distribution shift.
- **Data Aggregation (DAGGER):** An iterative online learning algorithm that addresses BC's distribution shift. At each iteration, the current policy is rolled out to collect a trajectory of states. An expert then provides actions for these states, which are added to the dataset. The policy is retrained on this expanded dataset. DAGGER uses a weighted combination of the expert and current policy, with the expert's influence decreasing over iterations.
- **SafeDAGGER:** A variation of DAGGER designed to prevent catastrophic failures. During policy rollouts, a safety strategy (`$\pi_{safe}$`) monitors the system. If an unsafe situation is detected, the expert takes over. Only states where the expert intervened are then relabeled with expert actions and added to the dataset, focusing data collection on critical states and preventing failures.

**Proposed Algorithms:**

1. **LocoDAGGER (Algorithm 3):** This algorithm adapts the original DAGGER for legged robots using an MPC controller.
 - **Pre-training:** An initial policy ($\pi_0$) is pre-trained using Behavioral Cloning on a dataset collected from a few expert rollouts to improve initial stability and learning efficiency.
 - **Main Algorithm Loop (N iterations):**
 - **Policy Rollout:** An expert influence probability ($p$) is calculated, decaying exponentially with iterations. At each `f_expert` step (e.g., 20Hz for MPC), a random choice is made based on $p$ to determine whether the expert or the current policy ($\pi_{i-1}$) controls the robot. Robot states are recorded.
 - **Data Collection:** From randomly sampled points along the recorded state trajectory, the expert is deployed for a fixed duration (`T_query`). The observed states, actions, and goals are added to the aggregated dataset ($\mathcal{D}$). This is repeated for `N_query` times. This reduces computational cost compared to querying every single state.
 - **Training:** The policy ($\pi_i$) is retrained on the expanded dataset $\mathcal{D}$ for a limited number of epochs to prevent overfitting.

2. **LocoSafeDAGGER (Algorithm 4):** This algorithm builds on SafeDAGGER to minimize failures during training, crucial for real-world deployment.
 - **Pre-training:** Similar to LocoDAGGER, an initial policy ($\pi_0$) is pre-trained on expert data.
 - **Main Algorithm Loop (N iterations):**
 - **Combined Policy Rollout + Data Collection:** During policy rollout, a safety policy (`$\pi_{safety}$`) actively monitors robot states (e.g., base position, angles, joint configurations, as specified in Table I).
 - **Safety Intervention:** If `$\pi_{safety}$` detects an unsafe condition, the expert immediately takes over for a fixed duration (`T_block`) to stabilize the robot. During this expert control period, the states, actions, and goals are recorded and added to the dataset ($\mathcal{D}$). Control returns to the policy only after `T_block` is complete and the robot state is deemed safe. Data is *only* collected when the expert is in control due to a safety violation or during the blocking period.
 - **Extra Data Collection:** To address scenarios where the robot remains safe but exhibits suboptimal behaviors (e.g., stagnant), an additional data collection phase is performed at the end of each policy rollout. The expert is deployed from the final recorded robot state for a fixed duration (`T_end`), and this data is also added to $\mathcal{D}$.
 - **Training:** The policy ($\pi_i$) is trained on the expanded dataset $\mathcal{D}$ for a limited number of epochs.

**Implementation Details:**
- **Simulation Environment:** Experiments were conducted using a simulation model of the Solo12 quadruped in a PyBullet environment.
- **Gait and Velocity:** The trot gait was used, with desired center of mass (CoM) velocities from 0.0 m/s to 0.3 m/s in the positive x-direction.
- **Policy Network:** A fully connected MLP with 3 hidden layers (512 nodes each), 1D batch normalization, and ReLU activation. Input includes normalized robot states (base orientation/velocity, joint positions/velocities, end-effector relative positions) and desired goals (gait phase, CoM x, y, and base yaw velocity). Output is desired joint position targets, passed to a low-impedance PD controller for torque computation.
- **Robot Initial Condition Generation:** Robot's initial configuration was randomized with contact-consistent perturbations along the nominal trajectory to increase complexity and collect recovery maneuver data.
- **The Expert:** The BiConMP NMPC framework was used, which solves kinematic and dynamic optimal control problems sequentially. It updates plans at 20 Hz and generates joint torques at 1 kHz via an unconstrained inverse dynamics module.
- **Iterative Algorithm Parameters:**
 - **LocoDAGGER:** Pre-trained with 112k data points (100 * 1.5s expert rollouts). 10 iterations, 15 epochs/iteration. 10 * 5s policy rollouts/iteration. Average of 5 samples per trajectory for NMPC rollout (1s/sample). Expert influence decay rates of 0.5 and 0.8 were investigated.
 - **LocoSafeDAGGER:** Also pre-trained with 112k data points. 10 iterations, 15 epochs/iteration. 40 * 10s policy rollouts/iteration. NMPC takes over for at least 2s if unsafe. 1s NMPC rollout for extra data collection at the end.
 - **Safety Policy (`$\pi_{safety}$`) Bounds:** Monitored CoM height, body roll/pitch, hip abduction/adduction, hip flexion/extension, and knee flexion/extension against predefined safe ranges (Table I).
- **Evaluation Procedure:**
 - **Safety during Data Collection:** Failure rate (NMPC divergence or robot fall) recorded for LocoDAGGER and LocoSafeDAGGER.
 - **Benchmark (BC):** Compared against BC policies trained on datasets of varying sizes (up to 736k samples from 20 velocities, 400 * 3s rollouts) for 150 epochs.
 - **Robustness and Performance:** 100 * 5s evaluation rollouts per policy across 10 velocities. Success defined as not falling and maintaining constant stepping for >= 2/3 of episode. Performance measured by Mean Squared Error (MSE) of actual vs. desired base x, y, and yaw velocity.
 - **Push Recovery Robustness:** Best-performing policy from each method tested for maximum impulse (N s) withstood from 45-degree intervals, maintaining >= 80% recovery success rate.
- **Runs:** 3 runs per learning technique to ensure statistical reliability.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments compared LocoSafeDAGGER, LocoDAGGER (with two different expert influence decay rates), and Behavioral Cloning (BC) across three key dimensions: safety during data collection, policy robustness and tracking performance, and push recovery capabilities.

**A. Safety during Data Collection**

- **Failure Rates:** LocoSafeDAGGER consistently maintained a low failure rate during data collection, exceeding 5% only in the first iteration (Figure 1). In contrast, LocoDAGGER, especially with a 0.5 expert influence decay rate, exhibited significantly higher failure rates (consistently above 5% after the second iteration) as the expert's influence diminished. This indicates that LocoSafeDAGGER's active safety check effectively enhances rollout safety.
- **Expert Usage:** LocoSafeDAGGER maintained a relatively constant NMPC (expert) usage of approximately 10% across all iterations. LocoDAGGER, on the other hand, showed a monotonic decay in NMPC influence from 100% (Figure 2). LocoSafeDAGGER's targeted expert intervention in unsafe states resulted in significantly fewer expert queries and improved computational efficiency without compromising safety, despite its rollouts being twice as long as LocoDAGGER's.

**B. Policy Robustness and Tracking Performance**

- **Robustness to Perturbations:** Policy robustness, measured by the success rate of evaluation rollouts under initial condition perturbations, generally increased with database size for all learning techniques (Figure 3). LocoSafeDAGGER, BC, and LocoDAGGER with a 0.5 decay rate converged to similar robustness levels, just below 90%. LocoDAGGER with a higher expert influence decay rate (0.8) showed poorer robustness. This suggests that iterative algorithms can achieve comparable robustness to BC.
- **Velocity Tracking Performance (MSE):**
 - **v_x tracking:** BC and LocoSafeDAGGER converged to similar low Mean Squared Error (MSE) values for desired x-velocity tracking. LocoDAGGER with a 0.5 decay rate showed slightly higher error, while LocoDAGGER with a 0.8 decay rate performed the worst (Figure 4).
 - **$\omega$ tracking:** BC demonstrated the best performance in yaw velocity tracking, followed closely by LocoSafeDAGGER, then LocoDAGGER (Figure 4).
 - **v_y tracking:** No single approach showed a clear best performance for y-velocity tracking (Figure 4).
- **Implications:** High expert influence in LocoDAGGER (0.8 decay rate) was associated with less robust policies and poorer tracking, potentially due to overly conservative data collection that limited genuine exploration. LocoSafeDAGGER demonstrated better sample efficiency than LocoDAGGER, achieving the same database size with four times fewer policy rollouts, while producing outcomes comparable to BC in terms of robustness and tracking performance.

**C. Push Recovery Robustness**

- **Maximum Impulse Tolerated:** The best-performing policies from each method were tested for their ability to recover from external pushes. Iterative algorithms (LocoSafeDAGGER and LocoDAGGER) exhibited superior push recovery performance compared to BC (Figure 5).
- **LocoSafeDAGGER vs. LocoDAGGER:** LocoSafeDAGGER generally outperformed LocoDAGGER across most impulse angles. This difference is attributed to LocoSafeDAGGER's strategy of specifically collecting data in dangerous or near-failure states through expert intervention, providing a more relevant dataset for critical recovery scenarios, leading to a more robust policy. BC's reliance solely on expert rollouts meant its dataset did not adequately represent the out-of-distribution states encountered by the policy during execution, resulting in inferior push recovery.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of legged robot control by presenting LocoSafeDAGGER, a novel algorithm that enhances the safety and efficiency of learning locomotion policies from Model Predictive Control (MPC) experts. The findings carry several significant implications and potential impacts:

1. **Enhanced Safety for Real-World Deployment:** The primary significance of LocoSafeDAGGER lies in its demonstrably low failure rate during data collection. By actively monitoring robot states and allowing the MPC expert to intervene during unsafe situations, the algorithm provides a mechanism for safely collecting training data. This feature is critical for the potential fine-tuning and deployment of learned policies directly on physical robot hardware, where preventing catastrophic failures and damage is paramount. This advancement reduces the risks associated with learning on real systems.

2. **Improved Sample Efficiency and Robustness:** LocoSafeDAGGER achieves comparable policy robustness and velocity tracking performance to traditional Behavioral Cloning (BC), while simultaneously demonstrating superior sample efficiency compared to LocoDAGGER. Its targeted data collection in critical states (when the expert intervenes) ensures that the training dataset is more representative of the challenging situations the policy will encounter. This approach mitigates the problem of distribution shift inherent in BC, leading to more robust policies, as evidenced by its superior push recovery capabilities compared to BC.

3. **Bridge Between Optimal Control and Learning:** The work effectively leverages the strengths of both optimal control (via MPC as a robust expert) and imitation learning, offering a practical pathway to transfer the capabilities of sophisticated, computationally intensive MPC controllers into fast, reactive, and robust neural network policies. This combination addresses the limitations of purely MPC-based or DRL-based approaches.

4. **Foundation for Future Advanced Control Systems:** The methodologies introduced, particularly the safety policy with blocking, establish a foundation for developing more advanced and secure learning architectures for legged robots. The concept of an intelligent safety mechanism that can detect and react to impending failures opens avenues for more autonomous and reliable robot operation in unstructured environments.

**Potential Future Impact:**

- **Accelerated Real-Robot Learning:** The safety and sample efficiency improvements could significantly reduce the time and resources required to train and validate locomotion policies on real robots, potentially leading to faster development cycles for new robotic platforms and skills.
- **Robustness in Dynamic Environments:** Policies trained with LocoSafeDAGGER are shown to be more robust to external disturbances and initial condition perturbations. This robustness is crucial for robots operating in dynamic, unpredictable environments, enabling them to maintain stability and complete tasks under unforeseen conditions.
- **Complex Skill Acquisition:** While this study focused on a limited gait and velocity range, the principles could be extended to learning a wider array of gaits, more complex maneuvers, and adaptability to challenging terrains. The ability to safely learn from an expert could facilitate the acquisition of highly dynamic and complex skills that are difficult to program manually.
- **Human-Robot Interaction:** Increased safety and reliability of robot locomotion can indirectly impact the development of more trustworthy and collaborative human-robot interaction scenarios, as robots become less prone to dangerous failures.

Future research directions, such as implementing predictive neural networks for earlier failure detection, gradually relaxing the safety policy to encourage exploration, and validating these algorithms on real robots across diverse conditions (e.g., sensor noise, external forces, wider gait ranges), indicate the broad applicability and ongoing potential of this work.
