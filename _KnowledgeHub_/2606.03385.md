# Grasp-Then-Plan with Failure Attribution: A Closed Two-Stage Framework for Precise and Generalizable Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Grasp-Then-Plan with Failure Attribution (GTP-FA) is a closed-loop framework for robotic manipulation that diagnoses failures in tightly coupled grasp-then-plan systems, attributing them to either grasp selection or downstream policy. This diagnosis guides targeted optimization, achieving average terminal success rate improvements of up to 54.0 percentage points in simulation and 65.6 percentage points on a real Franka robot across various long-horizon tasks.

## Problem

- Difficulty in attributing failures in robotic manipulation to either grasp selection or downstream planning, leading to inefficient trial-and-error learning.
- Task-incompatible grasps, which are geometrically stable but hinder subsequent manipulation, often get misdiagnosed as planning failures.
- Contaminated learning signals when using failure data, as end-to-end fine-tuning may incorrectly assign grasp-induced errors to the downstream policy.

## Method

![[2606.03385-method.png]]
*The GTP-FA framework operates in a loop: executing a task, diagnosing the failure mode, and routing updates to either the grasp selection or the planning policy.*

- A closed-loop execute-diagnose-update framework (**GTP-FA**) that explicitly links grasp selection to downstream task outcomes.
- A Failure Attribution Discriminator trained with weak pseudo-labels, predicting whether a failure is due to task-irrelevant grasp, unstable grasp, or planning insufficiency.
- A Grasp-Conditioned Diagnostic Representation Space combined with neighborhood priors to generalize failure attribution to unseen grasps and fuse with the discriminator's prediction for robust diagnosis.
- A diagnosis-driven bidirectional optimization strategy that routes targeted updates: augmenting grasp scores with task priors and risk penalties, and reshaping training data and reweighting policy objectives for planning-side improvement.

## Results

- GTP-FA consistently improved terminal success rates across five base learners in ManiSkill3 simulations, with an absolute gain of up to 54.0 percentage points (Behavior Cloning) and 31.3 percentage points (PPO).
- On a real Franka Research 3 robot, GTP-FA-π_0.5 achieved an average task-level success rate of **76.8%** across five long-horizon tasks, an absolute increase of 65.6 percentage points compared to the original π_0.5 baseline (**11.2%**).
- Ablation studies confirmed that the full attribution-guided bidirectional optimization is critical, as planning-only, grasp-only, or naive joint optimization approaches generally performed worse than the full framework or the baselines.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly attributing failures to either grasping or planning is crucial for effective and stable optimization in coupled robotic manipulation systems.
> - Naive joint optimization without specific failure attribution can be unstable and ineffective, potentially misassigning optimization pressure.
> - Integrating task-level priors and diagnostic risk assessment into grasp selection significantly improves task compatibility and downstream execution reliability.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robotic manipulation, particularly long-horizon tasks, frequently encounters challenges due to the inherent coupling between grasping and subsequent motion planning. Prior work in robotics has explored various facets of this problem, and this paper contributes to several interconnected areas:

**Six-Degree-of-Freedom (6-DoF) Grasp Synthesis and Task-Oriented Grasping:** Advances in 6-DoF grasping have led to methods for estimating and generating grasps based on RGB-D or point cloud data, addressing challenges like occlusion and clutter. However, the paper notes that geometric grasp stability alone is often insufficient for task success. Task-oriented grasping aims to integrate task context, sometimes using language or vision-language inputs or semantic/geometric priors from foundation models. This work aligns with task-oriented grasping by explicitly linking grasp selection to downstream task outcomes and failure modes, rather than relying solely on geometric stability. It contrasts with methods like VLM-based grasp correction by providing explicit diagnostic signals for optimization.

**Failure Detection, Diagnosis, Reasoning, and Recovery:** Existing literature addresses failure handling in long-horizon manipulation through detection, diagnosis, reasoning, and recovery mechanisms. While these approaches enhance observability and recovery, the paper argues that they often lack system-level credit assignment within grasp-plan pipelines, making it unclear whether a failure originates from grasping or planning. This research introduces a grasp-planning-aware failure attribution mechanism that explicitly distinguishes between these sources, using attribution for both supervision and optimization routing.

**Coupling between Grasp Selection and Downstream Policy Execution:** Many robotic manipulation systems operate as two-stage pipelines: grasp selection followed by motion planning conditioned on that grasp. This often results in mismatches, such as a geometrically feasible grasp leading to an infeasible plan, or vice versa. Efforts to mitigate this coupling include jointly optimizing grasp and motion through unified objectives or differentiable costs. Other approaches leverage reinforcement learning (RL), imitation learning (IL), diffusion policies, and vision-language-action (VLA) models to improve robustness and generalization. The paper differentiates itself by offering a base-learner-agnostic, diagnosis-driven interface that explicitly separates grasp-side and planning-side failures and routes optimization pressure accordingly, making it applicable across various policy learning paradigms. It moves beyond specific grasp-motion optimization formulations by introducing a generalized failure diagnosis and optimization-routing mechanism.

In summary, this work addresses a persistent challenge in robotic manipulation by providing a structured, closed-loop framework that resolves the ambiguity of failure attribution in tightly coupled grasp-then-plan systems. It integrates advancements from task-oriented grasping, failure diagnosis, and policy learning into a unified system capable of applying targeted improvements to specific modules.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from a fundamental challenge in long-horizon robotic manipulation: the tight coupling between grasping and subsequent motion planning or control. This coupling frequently obscures the true origin of a failure, making it difficult to determine if an unsuccessful task outcome resulted from a poor grasp selection or an insufficient downstream planning/control policy.

The authors identify several specific problems arising from this ambiguity:
- **Inefficient Trial-and-Error:** Without clear failure attribution, robotic systems resort to inefficient trial-and-error learning, as they cannot precisely target the failing component.
- **Contaminated Learning Signals:** When failure data in strongly coupled grasp-execution settings is used for training, end-to-end fine-tuning may incorrectly assign grasp-induced errors to the downstream policy. This contamination can weaken generalization and hinder effective policy improvement. For instance, a policy might learn compensatory behaviors for a consistently poor grasp, rather than the system addressing the grasp itself.
- **Task-Incompatible Grasps:** Standard grasp synthesis methods often prioritize geometric feasibility or stability. However, a geometrically stable grasp can still be task-incompatible if it occludes critical interaction regions, restricts motion, or conflicts with the overall task objective (e.g., a stable grasp on a tool's functional end preventing its proper use). Such grasp-induced failures are often observed only as "planning failures."
- **Fragile Grasp Stability:** Real-world factors like friction, material properties, and execution noise can make grasp stability fragile under distribution shifts, leading to unexpected failures that are hard to diagnose.

To address these issues, the core objective of this work is to develop a closed-loop framework that can reliably answer the question: "should a failure be attributed to grasping or to downstream planning/control?" This diagnostic capability is then used to guide targeted optimization.

Specifically, the key objectives are:
1. **Introduce a Task-Centric Grasp-Then-Plan Framework:** Design a closed-loop system that explicitly links grasp selection to downstream task outcomes. This framework aims to move beyond simple decomposition by considering the task's influence on optimal grasp choice.
2. **Develop a Robust Failure Attribution Mechanism:** Create a model capable of mapping an execution outcome to a structured distribution over predefined failure modes (FM-G1: task-irrelevant grasp contact/functionality, FM-G2: unstable grasping/slippage, FM-P: downstream planning/control insufficiency). This mechanism must generalize reliably to unseen grasps by incorporating local consistency priors.
3. **Propose a Diagnosis-Driven Bidirectional Optimization Strategy:** Based on the attributed failure source, route optimization pressure to the correct module. This involves:
 - **Grasp-side Optimization:** Injecting task-level priors and risk penalties into grasp candidate scoring to suppress unstable or task-incompatible grasps.
 - **Planning-side Optimization:** Targeting high-risk initial states through data collection and fine-tuning to address genuine planning bottlenecks, while reducing the impact of grasp-induced failures on policy training.

By achieving these objectives, the authors aim to enable more efficient, precise, and generalizable robotic manipulation, ultimately maximizing overall task success under varying task and environment conditions.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework, Grasp-Then-Plan with Failure Attribution (GTP-FA), operates as a closed-loop `execute–diagnose–update` process. It addresses the strong coupling between grasping and planning by explicitly attributing failures and directing optimization pressure to the appropriate module.

#### 3.1. Overall Framework (GTP-FA)

GTP-FA starts with task-aware grasp optimization, executes the selected grasp, diagnoses the outcome, and then routes optimization signals to either the grasp module or the planning module. The system aims to avoid misattributing grasp-side errors to the planning/policy side.

#### 3.2. Failure Attribution Discriminator (D)

The core of the diagnostic process is the Failure Attribution Discriminator (D), which performs post-hoc diagnosis for each execution.
- **Inputs:** D receives the task instruction (τ), visual observation (o), the selected grasp (g), and an execution summary (ξ), which includes events like slip/drop indicators, collision events, and end-effector errors.
- **Output:** D predicts a probability distribution over three predefined failure modes:
 - **FM-G1:** Task-irrelevant grasp contact or functional mismatch.
 - **FM-G2:** Unstable grasping or slippage during execution.
 - **FM-P:** Downstream planning/control or policy insufficiency.
- **Weak Supervision:** Because manual labeling is expensive, D is trained using weak pseudo-labels derived from controlled repeated trials. For a given grasp `g`, the downstream policy is run `K` times, and the empirical terminal success rate `q_end(g)` is calculated.
 - If `g` shows clear functional/contact mismatch, it's labeled FM-G1.
 - For non-G1 failures: if `q_end(g)` is high (task usually achievable), the failure is likely FM-P. If `q_end(g)` is low AND instability events (slip/drop) occur, it's labeled FM-G2. Ambiguous samples (where `q_end(g)` falls between low and high thresholds, `θ_low` and `θ_high`) are discarded or soft-labeled.
- **Architecture and Training:** D uses a lightweight encode-fuse-classify architecture, with separate encoders for task, observation, grasp, and execution summary features, concatenated into a fused representation. A classification head outputs the failure mode distribution. It's trained with a cross-entropy loss on high-confidence pseudo-labeled data.

#### 3.3. Failure Attribution Generalization to Unseen Grasps (E)

To ensure reliability when D encounters novel grasps (unseen contact regions, wrist poses, etc.), GTP-FA introduces a Grasp-Conditioned Diagnostic Representation Space (E).
- **Concept:** The premise is that grasp conditions inducing similar post-grasp initialization states should exhibit similar failure-mode distributions.
- **Grasp-Conditioned Embedding:** For each grasp `g`, a grasp-conditioned initialization feature `x_g` (e.g., object pose, relative gripper pose, contact region) is extracted and mapped to a diagnostic embedding `e = f_θ(x_g)`.
- **Training `E`:** The embedding model `f_θ` is trained using a supervised contrastive objective (`L_con`), encouraging diagnostically similar grasps to be close in the embedding space. Auxiliary classification heads for success/failure and failure mode (`L_aux`) are also used.
- **Neighborhood Diagnostic Prior:** At test time, for an unseen grasp `g` with embedding `e`, its `k` nearest neighbors `N_k(e)` are retrieved from an embedding bank. A weighted voting scheme over their failure-mode labels constructs a neighborhood diagnostic prior `˜p_E(m|e)`.
- **D/E Fusion:** The final fused attribution `p_fuse(m)` is a weighted combination of D's prediction `p_D(m)` and E's neighborhood prior `˜p_E(m)`. The weighting factor `α` depends on D's confidence: lower `α` if D is confident, higher `α` if D is uncertain, allowing the system to rely more on the prior for unseen or ambiguous cases.

#### 3.4. Diagnosis-driven Bidirectional Optimization

The `p_fuse(m)` distribution guides optimization routing.
- **Responsibility Weights:** `w_G = p_fuse(FM-G1) + p_fuse(FM-G2)` (grasp-side responsibility) and `w_P = p_fuse(FM-P)` (planning-side responsibility) are calculated.
- **Grasp-side Update (when `w_G` is high):**
 - A lightweight risk predictor `r_φ(z(g))` is trained to estimate the instability risk (`p_fuse(FM-G2)`).
 - The original grasp score `s_base(g;o)` is augmented: `S_GTP-FA(g) = s_base(g;o) + λs_prior(g;π_VLM(τ)) - βr_φ(z(g))`.
 - `s_prior`: A task-prior term, derived from a VLM, that incorporates coarse task-level constraints (e.g., preferred/forbidden grasp regions to avoid functional obstruction).
 - `r_φ`: A risk penalty to down-weight unstable grasps.
 - The system then selects the grasp `g*` that maximizes `S_GTP-FA(g)`. This optimizes for geometric stability, task feasibility, and downstream execution requirements.
- **Planning-side Update (when `w_P` is high and grasp-side risk is low):**
 - **Hard-P Subset Identification:** The system identifies a `G_hardP` subset of initial states where the grasp is relatively feasible (low FM-G1/FM-G2 risk) but downstream planning or policy execution is challenging (high FM-P risk). This is done by aggregating `p_fuse(FM-P|g)` for each grasp and applying thresholds (`δ_P`, `δ_G1`, `δ_G2`).
 - **Distribution Reshaping:** During training, the sampling distribution for initial states `g` is reshaped: `(1-ρ) Unif(G_full) + ρ Unif(G_hardP)`. This biases training towards genuinely difficult planning scenarios. `ρ` is 0.2 during training and 0 during evaluation.
 - **Responsibility-weighted Policy Objective:** The downstream policy's training objective `L_down` is reweighted by `w_P`: `min_θ E[w_P · L_down(θ;...)]`. This reduces the impact of grasp-induced failures on policy learning, preventing misattribution.

This iterative `execute–diagnose–update` loop allows GTP-FA to refine both its grasp selection and downstream policy based on targeted, attribution-guided feedback, reducing training contamination and enhancing overall performance.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of GTP-FA was evaluated through experiments in both simulated ManiSkill3 environments and on a real Franka Research 3 robot.

#### 4.1. Simulation Experiments

Eight long-horizon manipulation tasks from ManiSkill3 were used: StackCube, PokeCube, LiftPegUpright, PlaceSphere, PickCube, PushCube, PullCube, and PullCubeTool. GTP-FA was integrated with five different base learners: PPO, SAC (Reinforcement Learning), BC (Behavior Cloning), DP (Diffusion Policy), and a VLA-based `π_0.5` learner. Metrics reported include `success_once` (task reaches a successful state at any point) and `success_at_end` (task remains successful at episode termination).

- **Overall Performance Improvement:** Full GTP-FA consistently improved the terminal success rate (`success_at_end`) across all tested base learners compared to their original baselines (`00`):
 - **PPO:** +31.3 percentage points (pp)
 - **BC:** +54.0 pp
 - **DP:** +25.7 pp
 - **SAC:** +7.7 pp
 - **VLA-based `π_0.5`:** +17.8 pp
 These gains demonstrate that the framework is not tied to a specific policy-learning paradigm and is particularly pronounced for data-driven methods like BC, DP, and `π_0.5`.

- **Ablation Studies:**
 - **Planning-side-only optimization (`01`):** Generally resulted in negative or minimal changes. For PPO, BC, SAC, and `π_0.5`, terminal success rates decreased by -27.9 pp, -9.4 pp, -54.5 pp, and -31.4 pp, respectively. This indicates that solely updating the downstream policy can contaminate the learning signal if failures originate from the grasp side.
 - **Grasp-side-only optimization (`10`):** Also generally underperformed the baselines, with PPO, SAC, and `π_0.5` showing decreases of -38.6 pp, -54.5 pp, and -54.0 pp, respectively. This suggests that improving grasp candidates alone is insufficient without corresponding downstream policy adaptation.
 - **Naive joint optimization without attribution (`11`):** Showed mixed results, with gains for BC (+23.9 pp) and DP (+18.2 pp), but decreases for PPO (-6.0 pp) and SAC (-8.2 pp), and only a modest gain for `π_0.5` (+4.9 pp). This indicates that undifferentiated joint optimization without failure attribution can be unstable and may still misassign optimization pressure.
 - **Conclusion:** The consistent positive gains of full GTP-FA across all base learners highlight that the failure attribution mechanism is critical for enabling reliable and cooperative optimization of both grasp and planning modules.

#### 4.2. Real-Robot Experiments

GTP-FA was evaluated on a Franka Research 3 robot across five long-horizon tasks: placing an orange into a pink tray, stacking a blue cube, pushing a yellow cube with a stick, pulling a yellow cube with a hook, and pouring water from a cup. The `π_0.5` VLA learner was used as the base policy, with both the original `π_0.5` and GTP-FA-`π_0.5` adapted using the same 300 real-robot expert trajectories. Each task was evaluated over 50 physical trials.

- **Significant Real-World Improvement:** GTP-FA-`π_0.5` achieved substantial improvements in task-level success rates compared to the original `π_0.5` baseline:
 - **Average Success Rate:** Original `π_0.5` achieved 11.2%, while GTP-FA-`π_0.5` achieved 76.8%, an absolute gain of +65.6 pp.
 - **Task-Specific Gains:**
 - Orange-to-tray: from 10% to 92% (+82 pp)
 - Cube stacking: from 4% to 74% (+70 pp)
 - Poke cube with stick: from 24% to 86% (+62 pp)
 - Pull cube with hook: from 16% to 78% (+62 pp)
 - Pour water from cup: from 2% to 54% (+52 pp)
- **Interpretation:** The low success of the original `π_0.5` baseline in the real world is attributed to the need for precise, task-compatible grasping and stable post-grasp execution, which are amplified by real-world uncertainties (camera viewpoints, calibration errors, contact uncertainty, slippage). GTP-FA-`π_0.5` addresses this by explicitly selecting a grasp that is compatible with downstream objectives (considering task semantics, grasp candidates, and diagnostic risk), providing a more reliable initial state for the VLA policy. This is particularly beneficial for tasks requiring functional part interaction (tool use, pouring) and precise alignment (cube stacking).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents GTP-FA, a framework designed to enhance the reliability and efficiency of long-horizon robotic manipulation by addressing the problem of failure attribution in tightly coupled grasp-then-plan systems.

#### 5.1. Significance of the Research

- **Resolving Attribution Ambiguity:** The primary significance lies in its systematic approach to distinguishing between grasp-induced failures (functional mismatch, instability) and planning/policy-induced failures. This is a crucial step towards more effective robotic learning and control, as it directs optimization efforts to the true source of an issue.
- **Generalizable Framework:** GTP-FA demonstrates compatibility and provides performance improvements across a diverse range of policy learning paradigms, including reinforcement learning (PPO, SAC), imitation learning (BC, DP), and vision-language-action models (VLA-based `π_0.5`). This broad applicability indicates its potential as a foundational improvement for various robotic systems.
- **Diagnosis-Driven Optimization:** The diagnosis-driven bidirectional optimization strategy represents a methodological advancement. By incorporating task-level priors and risk penalties into grasp selection, and by reweighting planning-side objectives based on attribution, the framework ensures that learning signals are not contaminated by misattributed errors. This leads to more stable and robust policy improvements compared to naive end-to-end or module-specific optimization.
- **Empirical Validation:** The substantial gains observed in both high-fidelity simulation and real-robot experiments, particularly the significant improvements over strong VLA baselines in physical environments, underscore the practical utility and robustness of GTP-FA. The real-robot results demonstrate its ability to overcome common real-world challenges such as sensor noise, contact uncertainty, and calibration errors by providing more reliable initial conditions.

#### 5.2. Potential Impact

- **Enhanced Robotic Autonomy:** By improving the precision and generalizability of robotic manipulation, GTP-FA could contribute to the deployment of more autonomous robots in complex, unstructured environments, reducing the need for human intervention in diagnosing and correcting failures.
- **Accelerated Learning and Development:** The ability to efficiently attribute failures can significantly accelerate the development cycle for new manipulation tasks. Developers can identify and rectify bottlenecks in either the grasping or planning module more quickly, leading to faster iteration and policy refinement.
- **Safer Robot Operations:** More reliable manipulation reduces the likelihood of unintended actions, collisions, or object damage, which is critical for safe interaction in human environments or with delicate objects.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The framework's modularity and generalizability provide a basis for future research, such as integrating more sophisticated online feedback, expanding to multi-stage hierarchical tasks, or adapting to a wider variety of robot platforms and object categories.

#### 5.3. Limitations and Broader Impacts

The authors acknowledge limitations, including the current validation scope in terms of robot platforms, object categories, and open-world scenarios. The reliance on weak supervision for the attribution module suggests that richer online feedback could further enhance diagnostic robustness. Regarding broader impacts, the authors note the potential for unsafe deployment or misuse of automated manipulation systems without proper supervision, consistent with responsible AI development. The experiments were conducted in a controlled lab setting with human supervision, mitigating immediate safety risks.
