# World Models for Embodied Intelligence: From Plausible to Controllable to Actionable

> [!summary] Summary
> A survey that reorganizes embodied world-model research around what predictions do for behavior rather than visual fidelity. It defines a cumulative Plausible, Controllable, Actionable capability ladder, crosses geometry, physics, and action grounding with data, reward, policy, and model-self improvement loops in a 3×4 matrix, reviews manipulation, driving, navigation, and locomotion, and proposes capability-aligned evaluation protocols and six open problems.

## Problem

- Existing surveys organize world models by architecture, output modality, or application domain, leaving implicit which predictive capabilities actually improve an embodied agent's behavior.
- Progress is commonly measured by visual fidelity metrics such as SSIM, LPIPS, and FVD, which reward sharp futures but do not test causal correctness or decision value.
- Action-conditioned generators can accept a command yet ignore it, and recorded-action conditioning confounds intervention effects, so conditioning is routinely mistaken for controllability.

## Method

- **Plausible-Controllable-Actionable capability ladder**: cumulative levels requiring task-relevant state consistency, correct paired-intervention effects under do(a), and measured decision gain over a matched controller and compute budget.
- **Grounding-improvement matrix**: a 3×4 grid crossing geometry, physics, and action grounding with data, reward, policy, and model-self loops, each loop tagged with its self-reinforcing failure and settlement metric.
- ==Capability-aligned evaluation protocol==: drift-versus-horizon curves for plausibility, shared-start branched interventions with masked, shuffled, and wrong-action controls for controllability, and matched-budget closed-loop utility with latency for actionability.

## Results

- Surveys **275** references across manipulation, autonomous driving, navigation, and locomotion, placing each representative method once in a taxonomy of **3** rungs, **12** subsections, and thematic groups.
- Positions itself against **13** prior world-model, VLA, and WAM surveys, arguing none orders work by the progression from state consistency to intervention fidelity to decision utility.
- Identifies **six** open problems at ladder boundaries, each with a settling test, including hide-and-reveal persistence, regret under fixed compute budget, conflicting-axis grounding cases, and multi-cycle loop benchmarks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Representation choice does not confer capability: a pixel model can support planning while a 3D model stays inaccurate or unused, so evaluate what predictions enable, not their form.
> - A world model that trains a policy cannot also certify it, because the policy is optimized against the same prediction errors the audit must detect.
> - Closed improvement loops amplify shared errors: synthetic data teaches artifacts, critics reward them, policies seek them, so repeated updates need a held-out audit the generator cannot inspect.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

This paper is a survey and conceptual framework for world models in embodied intelligence. It is situated at the intersection of model-based reinforcement learning, robot learning, computer vision, generative video modeling, autonomous driving, navigation, and vision-language-action systems.

Earlier world-model research primarily focused on learning environment dynamics, predicting future observations, or supporting planning through imagined trajectories. Representative developments include pixel-based visual foresight, latent-dynamics models such as PlaNet and Dreamer, and value-equivalent models such as MuZero. More recent research has expanded toward 3D occupancy prediction, persistent spatial memory, physics-aware dynamics, action-conditioned video generation, and joint world-action models.

The authors argue that existing surveys generally organize the field by architecture, representation, output modality, or application domain. Those categories are useful for describing system design, but they do not directly answer the central practical question: whether a model’s predictions improve embodied behavior. The paper therefore proposes a decision-centered organization based on increasing functional capability:

1. **Plausible:** The model preserves task-relevant state and structure over time.
2. **Controllable:** The model predicts how interventions change the state.
3. **Actionable:** The model’s predictions produce measurable improvements in planning, learning, evaluation, verification, recovery, or data collection.

This perspective places visual generation within a broader continuum. A visually convincing video model is not necessarily a useful world model if it fails to preserve object identity, physical constraints, or the consequences of robot actions. Conversely, a latent model that does not reconstruct pixels may still qualify if it preserves the information required for decisions.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The main objective is to establish a common framework for evaluating and comparing world models according to their contribution to embodied decision-making rather than their visual realism alone.

The motivation arises from limitations of direct observation-to-action systems, including many vision-language-action models. A reactive policy can map observations and instructions to actions, but it may lack an explicit mechanism for maintaining hidden state, anticipating delayed consequences, comparing alternative actions, or recognizing execution failures. These capabilities are important in partially observed, contact-rich, and long-horizon environments.

The paper uses an everyday manipulation example to motivate this distinction. Before grasping a cup, a person anticipates its weight, resistance, and required hand configuration. This anticipation may not involve detailed visual reconstruction, but it supports successful action. The authors consequently ask what predictive information is behaviorally useful.

The survey has four stated goals. First, it defines the Plausible–Controllable–Actionable capability ladder. Second, it introduces a matrix that relates the content of prediction to the part of the agent that it improves. Third, it organizes representative methods across manipulation, navigation, locomotion, autonomous driving, and general embodied learning. Fourth, it reviews data resources and evaluation protocols and identifies unresolved problems involving long-horizon consistency, causal intervention testing, uncertainty, latency, verification, recovery, and cross-embodiment transfer.

### 3. Methodology and Analytical Approach

#### 3.1. Functional Definition of a World Model

The paper defines a world model as a learned or hybrid predictive model of agent–environment dynamics. Given an interaction history, and optionally a candidate action sequence, the model predicts how task-relevant aspects of the environment will evolve.

The interaction history is represented as:

\[
h_t=(o_{1:t},a_{1:t-1},r_{1:t-1},g),
\]

where observations, previous actions, task signals, and optional goals or language instructions are included. An action-conditioned model may predict future states and rewards according to:

\[
p_\theta(z_{t+1:t+H},r_{t:t+H-1}\mid h_t,a_{t:t+H-1}).
\]

The predicted state \(z\) can consist of pixels, visual tokens, latent features, object states, occupancy, point clouds, physical variables, symbolic predicates, rewards, or values. Reconstruction of observations is optional.

#### 3.2. Capability Ladder

The authors distinguish the three levels using progressively stronger tests.

A **Plausible** model must preserve task-relevant temporal, geometric, or physical structure. Evaluation may examine object identity, pose, depth, occupancy, occlusion, contact, collision, support, or material response. The paper emphasizes reporting error as a function of rollout horizon so that drift is visible.

A **Controllable** model must represent intervention effects. If two action sequences differ, the predicted futures should differ in the corresponding grounded variables. The authors stress that action conditioning alone is insufficient: a model may accept an action input while effectively ignoring it. Proper tests should use shared initial states, paired interventions, action-shuffled controls, and measurements of the requested versus realized change.

An **Actionable** model must alter a downstream decision or update and improve a measurable outcome relative to a matched baseline without the model. Relevant outcomes include task success, return, collision rate, safety, planning regret, policy-ranking accuracy, recovery success, data efficiency, and real-world interaction cost.

#### 3.3. Grounding–Improvement Matrix

The second analytical tool is a 3 × 4 matrix. The rows describe what the model grounds:

- **Geometry:** Structure, layout, occupancy, depth, pose, and persistent 3D or 4D state.
- **Physics:** Motion, contact, force, friction, deformation, material response, and feasibility.
- **Action:** Intervention identity, timing, magnitude, composition, coordinate frame, semantics, and embodiment.

The columns describe where predictions enter an improvement cycle:

- **Data loop:** Selecting, generating, or acquiring informative transitions.
- **Reward loop:** Producing rewards, values, critics, progress signals, or risk estimates.
- **Policy loop:** Selecting or learning actions from predicted futures.
- **Model-self loop:** Updating the model, memory, or uncertainty estimates from prediction errors.

The matrix is not intended to classify methods into exclusive categories. A single system may use several grounding dimensions and improvement loops.

#### 3.4. Literature Organization

The literature is reviewed in four major sections corresponding to the capability ladder. Plausible models are grouped by compact latent or object-centric state, metric scene representations, physics-grounded dynamics, and persistent memory. Controllable models are grouped by action conditioning, latent or language-based controls, geometry- and contact-grounded interventions, and interactive world-action models. Actionable models are grouped by planning and search, action decoding from imagination, learned simulators for policy training and evaluation, and runtime verification and recovery.

The survey then reinterprets these methods across embodiments and examines datasets, benchmarks, metrics, matched baselines, compute budgets, and latency requirements.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

Because this is a survey rather than an experimental research paper, its principal results are conceptual syntheses rather than new quantitative benchmark scores.

First, the authors find that **visual plausibility is an inadequate measure of world-model quality**. A model can produce sharp or realistic futures while losing object identity, violating spatial relationships, ignoring actions, or generating physically impossible interactions. State consistency, physical feasibility, and persistence should therefore be evaluated separately from image quality.

Second, the literature shows a progression from **compact predictive representations to grounded state representations**. Latent and object-centric models reduce the cost of prediction and can preserve decision-relevant information without reconstructing every pixel. Metric representations such as occupancy, point clouds, and 4D scene states make errors measurable in spatial units. Physics-aware models add constraints related to collision, support, contact, friction, and material response. Persistent-memory models address identity and spatial drift over longer horizons.

Third, the paper identifies a distinction between **conditioning and controllability**. Action-conditioned models are not necessarily intervention-faithful. Stronger evidence requires counterfactual or paired-action testing in which the initial state is held fixed and only one intervention variable changes. This issue is especially important because demonstration data confounds action choice with scene state and task intent.

Fourth, actionability depends on **where prediction is used**. World models may score externally proposed plans, generate actions through imagined futures, train policies inside learned simulators, evaluate policies, or monitor execution and trigger correction or recovery. Each use requires different forms of accuracy. A planner needs correct relative ranking of candidate outcomes; a verifier needs calibrated deviation signals with low latency; a policy-training simulator must remain reliable on states induced by the policy itself.

Fifth, the review identifies four improvement loops but notes that each can reinforce model errors. Synthetic data may become concentrated in scenarios the model already handles. A learned critic may reward artifacts rather than task success. A policy may perform well inside an imperfect simulator but fail in the real environment. Joint model-policy updates may obscure whether improvement comes from better prediction or policy adaptation. Independent audit sets and matched real-world evaluation are therefore required.

Sixth, the required grounding differs by embodiment. Manipulation depends heavily on contact, force, object state, and recovery. Autonomous driving requires multi-agent futures, road geometry, safety, and closed-loop route outcomes. Navigation depends on persistent spatial belief and loop closure. Locomotion requires compact, high-rate predictions of body dynamics and stability. The authors conclude that transfer requires separating transferable representations, such as object motion or geometry, from embodiment-specific dynamics and action semantics.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The paper’s main significance is methodological. It proposes that world-model research should be evaluated by the behavioral consequences of prediction, with increasingly demanding evidence for state consistency, intervention fidelity, and downstream utility.

The framework could improve research comparability by encouraging authors to report the specific capability they demonstrate, the grounding dimension involved, the downstream improvement loop, and the resources used. Its evaluation recommendations also address common sources of ambiguity, including visual metrics that do not test causality, unequal model-call budgets, unreported latency, and comparisons against policies that lack equivalent computational resources.

Potential practical impact includes more reliable planning, safer manipulation, improved simulator-based policy training, runtime failure detection, adaptive replanning, and better data selection. However, the paper also makes clear that these outcomes remain constrained by unresolved problems. Long-horizon drift, calibrated uncertainty, causal intervention testing, real-time inference, independent verification, and cross-embodiment transfer remain significant barriers.

Overall, the survey shifts the field’s emphasis from whether a model can generate a plausible future to whether it predicts the right state, responds to interventions for the right reasons, and improves closed-loop embodied behavior under explicit data, compute, and latency constraints.
