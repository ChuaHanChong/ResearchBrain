# Generalization in LLM Problem Solving: The Case of the Shortest Path

> [!summary] Summary
> Large language models demonstrate robust generalization to new environments but consistently fail at scaling to longer problem instances, as shown in a controlled study on shortest-path planning. Effective training involves prioritizing broad question coverage in supervised learning and introducing a small fraction of slightly longer paths to address length scaling, while reinforcement learning primarily stabilizes existing capabilities.

## Problem

- Ambiguity in understanding the root causes of Large Language Model (LLM) reasoning failures due to confounded factors in complex natural language benchmarks.
- Lack of precise control in existing benchmarks to differentiate between true systematic generalization and superficial pattern exploitation.
- Unclear fundamental nature of LLM generalization, specifically regarding their ability to generalize compositionally to novel problem structures or increased problem length.

## Method

- Developed a controlled synthetic environment using ==shortest-path== planning on grid maps, a verifiable sequential optimization problem (SOP), to disentangle factors affecting generalization.
- Defined and evaluated two orthogonal out-of-distribution generalization axes: 'spatial transfer' (applying rules to novel maps) and 'length scaling' (solving problems requiring longer paths than seen during training).
- Systematically analyzed the impact of training data properties (quantity, coverage, diversity), training paradigms (Supervised Fine-tuning vs. Reinforcement Learning), and inference-time strategies.

## Results

- Models achieved over **90%** success rates on unseen maps (spatial transfer) but universally failed when paths were longer than training examples (length scaling).
- Adding just **1%** of slightly longer paths to the training data substantially mitigated length scaling failures, rescuing performance to nearly **90%** success rates.
- Training with 'more questions' (distinct start-end pairs) led to better spatial transfer than 'more answers' (multiple solutions for fewer questions), with optimal performance achieved at mid-to-high coverage and modest diversity in training data.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - LLMs exhibit an asymmetric generalization capability, showing strong spatial transfer to new environments but consistent failure in length scaling due to 'recursive instability' in composing longer solutions.
> - For supervised fine-tuning, prioritizing broad coverage of distinct questions and primitives over multiple solutions or excessive diversity significantly improves spatial transfer.
> - Reinforcement learning acts primarily as a training stabilizer and overfitting mitigator, rather than fundamentally expanding LLM generalization capabilities beyond those established by supervised learning.
