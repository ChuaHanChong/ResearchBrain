# Generalization in LLM Problem Solving: The Case of the Shortest Path

> [!summary] Summary
> Large language models demonstrate robust generalization to new environments but consistently fail at scaling to longer problem instances, as shown in a controlled study on shortest-path planning. Effective training involves prioritizing broad question coverage in supervised learning and introducing a small fraction of slightly longer paths to address length scaling, while reinforcement learning primarily stabilizes existing capabilities.

## Problem

- Ambiguity in understanding the root causes of Large Language Model (LLM) reasoning failures due to confounded factors in complex natural language benchmarks.
- Lack of precise control in existing benchmarks to differentiate between true systematic generalization and superficial pattern exploitation.
- Unclear fundamental nature of LLM generalization, specifically regarding their ability to generalize compositionally to novel problem structures or increased problem length.

## Method

- Developed a controlled synthetic environment using ==shortest-path== planning on grid maps, a verifiable sequential optimization problem (SOP), to disentangle factors affecting generalization.
- Defined and evaluated two orthogonal out-of-distribution generalization axes: 'spatial transfer' (applying rules to novel maps) and 'length scaling' (solving problems requiring longer paths than seen during training).
- Systematically analyzed the impact of training data properties (quantity, coverage, diversity), training paradigms (Supervised Fine-tuning vs. Reinforcement Learning), and inference-time strategies.

## Results

- Models achieved over **90%** success rates on unseen maps (spatial transfer) but universally failed when paths were longer than training examples (length scaling).
- Adding just **1%** of slightly longer paths to the training data substantially mitigated length scaling failures, rescuing performance to nearly **90%** success rates.
- Training with 'more questions' (distinct start-end pairs) led to better spatial transfer than 'more answers' (multiple solutions for fewer questions), with optimal performance achieved at mid-to-high coverage and modest diversity in training data.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - LLMs exhibit an asymmetric generalization capability, showing strong spatial transfer to new environments but consistent failure in length scaling due to 'recursive instability' in composing longer solutions.
> - For supervised fine-tuning, prioritizing broad coverage of distinct questions and primitives over multiple solutions or excessive diversity significantly improves spatial transfer.
> - Reinforcement learning acts primarily as a training stabilizer and overfitting mitigator, rather than fundamentally expanding LLM generalization capabilities beyond those established by supervised learning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a central and ongoing debate regarding whether language models can systematically generalize. Despite substantial advancements in post-training methodologies, such as instruction tuning and reinforcement learning from human feedback, and the development of more complex reasoning benchmarks, the fundamental nature of LLM generalization remains unclear. A key challenge in interpreting LLM performance on reasoning tasks is that it is influenced by multiple interacting factors, including the properties of training and testing data, the specific training paradigms (e.g., supervised fine-tuning vs. reinforcement learning), and the strategies employed during inference. This complexity makes it difficult to ascertain whether observed failures stem from insufficient data, inadequacies in training, or limitations in how models express their capabilities. Furthermore, natural language benchmarks often lack the precise control necessary to cleanly separate training and evaluation settings, making it challenging to determine if models are truly generalizing or merely exploiting superficial patterns.

This research contributes to the landscape by introducing a controlled synthetic environment based on shortest-path planning, a canonical example of a composable sequential optimization problem (SOP). This controlled setup enables the clean separation and analysis of the aforementioned influencing factors, offering a direct operational test for systematic generalization. The study positions itself within the broader discussion of compositional generalization, specifically examining "spatial transfer" (analogous to systematic generalization) and "length scaling" (related to length generalization or productivity), which are critical dimensions of extrapolative problem-solving. By disentangling these factors in a verifiable domain, the paper aims to provide a more integrated understanding of how different stages of the learning pipeline shape LLM generalization, building upon existing work that has explored various facets of compositionality and length extrapolation in neural networks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the ambiguity surrounding LLM reasoning failures in natural benchmarks. These benchmarks often confound multiple factors—data properties, training dynamics, and inference strategies—making it challenging to pinpoint the root causes of generalization limitations. The absence of strict control over the differences between training and test distributions further obscures whether models genuinely generalize or merely rely on memorized patterns.

To address these limitations, the authors constructed a controlled synthetic environment centered on composable sequential optimization problems (SOPs), using shortest-path planning as a specific instantiation. This environment allows for the explicit definition of state spaces, action spaces, transition rules, goal sets, and global objectives, providing a verifiable and unambiguous optimal solution. Within this controlled setup, the research pursues three interconnected objectives:

1. **Systematic Generalization on Composable SOPs**: To determine if language models are capable of systematic generalization when applied to composable sequential optimization problems. This involves assessing their ability to apply learned rules in novel contexts beyond the specific configurations encountered during training.
2. **Compositional Scaling to Larger or Novel Problems**: To investigate whether a model that can successfully solve smaller instances of a problem can effectively compose these learned abilities to solve larger-scale problems or those with novel structural characteristics. This specifically targets the model's capacity for recursive application of learned rules, particularly in the context of path length.
3. **Influence of the Learning Pipeline Stages**: To analyze how generalization capabilities are shaped by distinct stages of the learning pipeline. This includes systematically evaluating the impact of:
 - **Training data properties**: Such as the quantity of distinct questions, the diversity of solutions per question, and the coverage and diversity of primitives within the training set.
 - **Training paradigms**: Specifically comparing supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and reinforcement learning (RL) in terms of their ability to foster generalization.
 - **Inference-time strategies**: Assessing whether advanced decoding and search methods can mitigate observed generalization failures.

By methodically varying these factors within a controlled environment, the researchers aimed to disentangle their individual and interactive effects on LLM generalization, offering insights that are difficult to obtain from more complex, naturalistic tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed a systematic approach within a controlled synthetic environment to analyze LLM generalization.

**Sequential Optimization Problem (SOP) Setup**:
The core of the environment is shortest-path planning on sparse grid maps, a canonical composable SOP.
- **Map Representation**: Grid maps `G = (V, A)` with nodes `V` and adjacency `A` (some edges blocked).
- **Problem Formulation**: Given start `i` and end `j` nodes, the model must generate the complete shortest path `f(i, j | G)` as a sequence of movement directions (E, W, N, S). This "direct-answer" setting avoids intermediate reasoning steps.
- **Composability**: Shortest paths are inherently composable, meaning `ShortestPath(i, j) = ShortestPath(i, k) ◦ ShortestPath(k, j)`.

**Generalization Axes**:
The study defines two orthogonal axes of out-of-distribution generalization:
- **Spatial Transfer**: Evaluates the model's ability to solve shortest-path tasks on entirely new maps `ˆG = (ˆV, ˆA)` where `ˆV` and `ˆA` are disjoint from the training map `G`. This tests the application of learned rules to novel primitive combinations.
- **Length Scaling**: Assesses the model's capacity to handle problems requiring strictly longer paths than those observed during training. This is formalized by `max l(D_train) ≤ min l(D_test)`.

**Metric**:
- **Success Rate (SR)**: `Pr( (i,j)∼D_test: ˆf_θ (i, j | G) = f (i, j | G) )`. A generated path is successful if it is one of the (potentially multiple) valid shortest paths.

**Model Architecture and Pretraining**:
- **Model**: 8-layer, 8-head Transformer models, based on the LLaMA architecture, utilizing Rotary Positional Embeddings (RoPE). The models were trained from scratch.
- **Pretraining**: Models were pretrained on a large corpus (10M trajectories, 1.3B tokens) of random-walk paths across all maps (training and test maps). This phase aimed to expose the model to node primitives and their adjacency relationships (map semantics) without imparting any shortest-path finding ability. Random walks were constrained to be substantially longer than fine-tuning paths to prevent interference.

**Fine-tuning and Evaluation Setup**:
- **Training Map (G)**: A 50x40 sparse grid map with 2000 nodes.
- **Node Split**: 80% of nodes from `G` were used to form the training dataset `D_train`. The remaining 20% were used for length scaling evaluation within the training map.
- **Test Maps (ˆG)**: Spatial transfer was evaluated on three distinct, disjoint novel maps of varying sizes (30x30, 40x40, 50x50), sparsity (25%–75%), and adjacency.

**Training Paradigms**:
- **Supervised Fine-tuning (SFT)**:
 - **Data Format**: Training samples formatted as `<s> i j : <path_directions> </s>`. `i` and `j` are start/end nodes, and `<path_directions>` is a sequence of cardinal directions. Using directions prevents trivial memorization of node n-grams.
 - **Loss**: The prompt prefix `<s> i j :` was excluded from the loss calculation.
- **Reinforcement Learning (RL)**:
 - **Algorithm**: Dr.GRPO, an unbiased variant of GRPO, which is a common algorithm for RL with LLMs.
 - **Reward**: Binary reward of 1 for a valid shortest path and 0 otherwise, leveraging the verifiability of shortest paths.
 - **Initialization**: RL models were warm-started from various SFT checkpoints.
 - **Rollouts**: Number of rollouts per prompt was varied (4, 8, 16) during training.

**Controlled Data Selection Experiments (for SFT)**:
- **More Questions vs. More Answers**: Explored how to allocate a fixed training budget by varying the number of unique start-end pairs (questions) and the average number of distinct shortest paths (answers) per question.
- **Coverage vs. Diversity in Questions**:
 - **Coverage (c)**: Fraction of unique nodes in the training map `G` that appear in `D_train`. Varied from 4% to 80%.
 - **Diversity (d)**: Measure of how richly observed nodes are combined, defined as `|supp(D_train)| / |V_train|`. Varied exponentially from `2^0` to `2^7`.
 - The total number of question-answer records was kept constant across conditions.
- **Length Augmentation**: Investigated the effect of adding a small fraction (approx. 1%) of slightly longer paths, shorter paths, or overly long paths to the training set to address length scaling failures.

**Inference-Time Strategies**:
- **Greedy Decoding**: Standard approach.
- **Majority-of-10**: Samples 10 trajectories and selects the most frequent output (akin to Self-Consistency).
- **Shortest-of-10**: Samples 10 trajectories and selects the one with the shortest path length (objective-guided).

**MathQA Case Study**:
- **Model**: Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (7B-scale).
- **Dataset**: MathQA, focusing on 'probability', 'gain', and 'physics' categories.
- **Primitive Extraction**: Operation programs from MathQA problems were converted into unordered multisets of operations to define 'operation-sets' (conceptual skills).
- **Definitions**: Coverage measured distinct operation-sets; Diversity measured distinct program structures (operator orderings) for the same operation-set.
- **Strategies**: Compared "More Questions" (with high coverage vs. high diversity variants) and "More Solutions" within a strict data budget.
- **Solution Generation**: DeepSeek-R1 was used to generate high-quality chain-of-thought solutions for supervision.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study yielded several key findings regarding LLM generalization in the context of shortest-path planning and a math reasoning case study.

#### 4.1. Generalization Asymmetry: Strong Spatial Transfer, Failure in Length Scaling.

- Models consistently demonstrated strong spatial transfer, achieving success rates above 90% when evaluated on entirely unseen maps. This indicates an ability to generalize structural rules to novel primitive combinations.
- However, models exhibited consistent failure under length scaling. Performance degraded sharply and universally when paths were longer than those observed during training, regardless of whether spatial transfer was also required.
- Analysis of length scaling failure revealed that it was primarily driven by "recursive instability," meaning the model struggled to stably compose individually solvable subproblems into longer sequences. Hardness accumulation from imperfect subpath solutions played a less dominant role.

#### 4.2. Effects of Data Selection on Spatial Transfer (SFT).

- **More Questions vs. More Answers**: Under a fixed training budget, allocating resources to cover more distinct questions (unique start-end pairs) consistently led to better spatial transfer generalization than providing multiple solutions for fewer questions. One high-quality solution per question appeared sufficient for SFT under this setting. The benefits of adding more questions, however, showed saturation at very high budgets.
- **Coverage vs. Diversity**:
 - **Coverage** (the fraction of unique nodes/primitives covered in training questions) was found to set the ceiling for spatial transfer performance. High coverage enabled models to achieve high success rates.
 - A **minimal level of diversity** (how richly primitives are combined in questions) was required to efficiently unlock the benefits of coverage. Without it, performance remained lower.
 - Beyond a modest threshold, increasing diversity yielded diminishing returns and could even negatively impact performance when coverage was low, potentially encouraging memorization over rule abstraction.
 - Insufficient coverage could not be compensated by increasing diversity.
 - The most resource-efficient strategy involved targeting mid-to-high coverage (≥32%) with modest diversity (8–32).

#### 4.3. Effects of Data Selection on Problem-Solving Scaling (SFT).

- Length generalization failures, observed when path lengths exceeded the training maximum, could be substantially mitigated by adding even a small fraction (approximately 1%) of *slightly longer* paths to the training data. This curriculum-like exposure rescued performance to nearly 90% success rates.
- Conversely, adding shorter paths yielded minimal improvements, even in large quantities, while including *overly long paths* could degrade performance, suggesting a sensitivity to the length distribution in the training data for scaling.

#### 4.4. Effects of Training Paradigm: RL Stabilizes but Does Not Expand Capability.

- Reinforcement Learning (RL), specifically Dr.GRPO, was observed to stabilize training and effectively prevent overfitting during prolonged optimization. RL curves remained consistent across epochs, unlike SFT, which showed initial improvement followed by degradation due to overfitting.
- However, RL did not enhance spatial transfer or length scaling capabilities beyond what was achievable by the best-performing Supervised Fine-tuning (SFT) model. The performance ceiling for RL was consistently bounded by the SFT baseline.
- Qualitative analysis indicated that SFT and RL exhibited nearly identical error types and distributions, suggesting that RL primarily functions to robustly optimize existing SFT capabilities rather than introduce new ones or correct fundamental errors intrinsic to the underlying SFT model.

#### 4.5. Effects of Inference-Time Strategies: Improve Performance, Not Scaling.

- Advanced inference-time strategies, such as Majority-of-10 and Shortest-of-10 sampling, improved empirical success rates for both SFT and RL models by shifting their performance curves upward. These strategies allowed models to surface latent capabilities by generating and selecting among multiple trajectories.
- However, these strategies did not fundamentally rescue length scaling failures. The overall trend of performance degradation for longer paths remained largely unchanged, indicating that such failures stem from intrinsic model limitations rather than insufficient search during inference.
- Notably, RL models consistently underperformed their SFT counterparts across all inference settings, even with objective-guided sampling. This suggested that RL training might restrict the effective solution space, limiting the diversity of trajectories available for inference-time selection.

#### 4.6. MathQA Case Study Reinforces Findings.

- A case study on the MathQA dataset with Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct corroborated the findings from the synthetic environment.
- Allocating the training budget to "More Questions" (distinct math problems/operation-sets) consistently outperformed "More Solutions" (multiple reasoning traces per problem).
- Within the "More Questions" strategy, maximizing "High Coverage" (broader range of conceptual skill combinations) yielded better results than "High Diversity" (more varied applications of seen skills). This reinforced the notion that breadth of conceptual exposure is more impactful than depth of practice for real-world generalization under limited budgets.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research offers several significant contributions to the understanding of LLM generalization and holds practical implications for the development and training of these models:

1. **Clarifying Generalization Mechanisms**: By establishing a controlled synthetic environment, the study successfully disentangles multiple factors influencing LLM performance. It provides clear evidence of an asymmetry in generalization, where structural transfer to unseen environments is robust, but length-based scaling remains a fundamental challenge due largely to recursive instability. This distinction offers a more nuanced understanding of "systematic generalization" in LLMs, highlighting that not all forms of extrapolation are equally attainable.
2. **Guiding Data Selection for Generalization**: The findings provide actionable insights for dataset design, particularly under resource constraints. The emphasis on maximizing distinct questions and broad primitive coverage (rather than redundant solutions or excessive diversity) for achieving strong spatial transfer offers a cost-efficient strategy for dataset curation. For length generalization, the discovery that exposure to *slightly longer* examples is critical for enabling scaling has direct implications for curriculum learning strategies in sequential tasks.
3. **Illuminating the Role of RL**: The research refines the understanding of Reinforcement Learning's contribution in LLM post-training. It indicates that RL primarily functions as a training stabilizer and overfitting mitigator, rather than a mechanism for unlocking novel reasoning capabilities or expanding the inherent generalization frontier beyond what a well-tuned SFT model can achieve. This suggests that while RL is valuable for robustness, the foundational capabilities are largely established through supervised learning with appropriately structured data.
4. **Assessing Inference-Time Strategies**: The analysis of inference-time strategies demonstrates that advanced decoding techniques can enhance empirical performance by better surfacing existing model capabilities. However, it also clarifies their limitations, showing that they cannot fundamentally resolve core generalization failures like length scaling. This informs the appropriate application of such strategies, indicating they are optimizers of existing capacity, not substitutes for intrinsic model improvements.
5. **Mechanistic Insights**: The observation of strong spatial transfer, coupled with probing results suggesting the encoding of distance-based heuristics, implies that LLMs develop structured latent operators that are flexibly reusable across novel domains. This aligns with emerging theoretical perspectives on how attention mechanisms might facilitate compositional behavior.
6. **Bridging Synthetic and Realistic Tasks**: The validation of key data selection principles in a realistic mathematical reasoning domain (MathQA) demonstrates that insights derived from the controlled synthetic environment can translate to more complex, heterogeneous, and practical LLM applications. This enhances the relevance and applicability of the findings beyond the academic testbed.

In summary, this work provides a unified, data-centric perspective on LLM generalization, delineating how training data, paradigms, and inference strategies interact to shape different dimensions of problem-solving. It offers both fundamental insights into LLM capabilities and limitations, and practical guidance for optimizing training pipelines to improve specific generalization behaviors.
