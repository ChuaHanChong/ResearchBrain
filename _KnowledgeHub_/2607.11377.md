# A Glimpse into Long-term Physical Coexistence with Intelligent Robots

> [!summary] Summary
> The Astribot Team developed PHILIA, an architecture enabling long-term physical coexistence with intelligent robots by decoupling high-level agent reasoning from low-level execution via capability-centric robot gateways. This system, validated on Astribot S1 robots, demonstrates multi-robot control, effective grounding of free-form language and long-term memory into physical actions, and achieves high precision in intent routing.

## Problem

- Developing robotic systems capable of sustained coexistence with humans in everyday environments, moving beyond isolated task execution.
- Integrating heterogeneous robot platforms, diverse user interfaces, and policy servers into a unified, scalable system.
- Addressing the fundamental disparity between low-frequency, high-level semantic reasoning and high-frequency, physically grounded robot execution.

## Method

- Proposed **PHILIA**, a three-layer ==agentic multi-robot architecture== that decouples a central ==agent control plane== from robot-local execution runtimes.
- Introduced ==robot gateways== as capability-centric abstractions to encapsulate platform-specific details and expose a stable, high-level interface to the agent.
- Implemented a ==five-stage cascaded routing== system to efficiently and robustly interpret free-form user instructions, balancing responsiveness and precision.

## Results

- Achieved high model-agnostic routing reliability (F1 **0.96**, recall **0.98**) for classifying robot-related utterances and up to **94%** top-1 skill matching accuracy with frontier models.
- Demonstrated successful grounding of high-level agent reasoning (e.g., semantic categories, user preferences) and long-term memory into low-level physical actions on Astribot S1 robots.
- Validated concurrent control of multiple Astribot S1 robots and plug-and-play integration of new or improved policies without requiring modifications to core system interfaces or the assistant logic.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decoupling high-level agent intelligence from robot-specific implementations through a capability-centric gateway enables scalable control over heterogeneous robot actors.
> - A compositional system design allows independent advancements in user interfaces, memory, robot embodiments, and policies to cumulatively improve overall system performance.
> - A hierarchical intent routing mechanism, from fast binary gates to full capability-grounded semantic routers, effectively manages latency and accuracy for diverse user requests.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Broader Research Landscape

The paper positions its work within the context of long-standing aspirations for robots capable of sustained coexistence with humans in everyday environments. The authors acknowledge recent progress in both generalist and specialized robot policies, which has expanded the operational capabilities of robotic systems. However, they identify a gap: the mere execution of policies is deemed insufficient for realizing intelligent robotic companions that can interact through diverse interfaces, maintain long-term memory, coordinate across multiple physical embodiments, and safely translate open-ended human intentions into physical actions.

Current robotic systems are often designed for isolated command execution, where users invoke specific skills through robot-specific interfaces. This paradigm, while effective for standalone tasks, does not scale to scenarios requiring long-term human-robot coexistence or multi-robot environments. Such environments necessitate seamless integration of heterogeneous robots, policy servers, navigation systems, user interfaces, and communication channels. Furthermore, the paper highlights a fundamental disparity in temporal and semantic scales: high-level tasks like language interaction, planning, and memory operate at a low frequency and high level of abstraction, whereas low-level functions such as perception, navigation, manipulation, and safety-critical control demand high-frequency, physically grounded execution.

Existing research on language model (LLM)-based robot agents has explored grounding instructions in various modalities, demonstrating the potential of large models for high-level reasoning in embodied tasks, including multi-robot task allocation and collaboration. Similarly, large-scale robot learning has produced generalist policies connecting language, vision, and action across diverse robot embodiments. However, the paper clarifies that its work is complementary to these advancements, aiming not to replace underlying policies or models, but rather to provide the architectural framework for deploying and orchestrating them.

The work also relates to efforts in long-term human-robot coexistence, memory, and personalization, such as the STRANDS project, which emphasized the need for combining various robot capabilities for persistent service. Personalized assistants and modern navigation systems form part of this landscape. In terms of runtime abstractions, projects like RoboClaw and ABot-Claw have investigated agentic frameworks for scalable and cooperative robotic tasks, often utilizing modular control abstractions such as behavior trees. While these systems explore various aspects of robot execution, PHILIA specifically focuses on a deployment-oriented control plane for heterogeneous robot actors. Finally, the paper distinguishes its approach from robotic middleware systems like ROS and ROS 2, stating that PHILIA operates at a higher semantic layer, assuming the existence of functional middleware stacks and exposing a more abstract interface through a gateway.

In summary, PHILIA aims to bridge the gap between advanced but often isolated robot policies and the systemic requirements for practical, scalable, and safe long-term human-robot coexistence in multi-robot, heterogeneous environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable long-term physical coexistence with intelligent robots. This overarching goal necessitates robotic systems that are capable of more than just executing individual policies. The authors identify several core requirements for such systems:
1. **Support for Diverse User-Facing Interfaces:** Robots must be able to interact with users through multiple modalities and channels.
2. **Maintenance of Long-Horizon Memory:** Robots need to retain knowledge of people, preferences, and past interactions over extended periods.
3. **Coordination Across Robot Embodiments:** The system should facilitate seamless control and collaboration among multiple, potentially heterogeneous, physical robot platforms.
4. **Translation of Open-Ended Human Intent into Safe Physical Execution:** Robots must interpret natural language commands, even ambiguous ones, and execute them safely in the physical world.

The motivation for developing PHILIA stems from the limitations of existing robotic paradigms. Current systems primarily focus on isolated command execution, which becomes impractical for long-term coexistence or in environments where multiple, diverse robots must operate synergistically. The integration of heterogeneous robots, various policy servers, navigation systems, user interfaces, and communication channels often presents significant architectural and engineering challenges.

A key motivation also arises from the difference in operational frequencies and semantic levels across robotic components. High-level reasoning tasks (e.g., language interaction, planning, memory) operate at a lower frequency and higher semantic abstraction, whereas low-level control, perception, navigation, and safety mechanisms require high-frequency, real-time execution grounded in the physical world. The authors argue that this disparity necessitates a clear architectural separation.

Based on these challenges, the work sets forth several specific objectives:
- To propose an agent–runtime decoupled architecture that separates low-frequency semantic reasoning from high-frequency robot execution, thereby enabling scalable, long-term operation across heterogeneous robot embodiments.
- To introduce robot gateways as a capability-centric abstraction. This aims to decouple agent intelligence from robot-specific implementations, allowing semantic reasoning, memory, and planning capabilities to generalize across diverse robots without requiring modifications to their local execution stacks.
- To formulate robotic assistants as a compositional system. This design principle allows advances in user interfaces, memory systems, robot embodiments, navigation modules, and policy backends to be integrated independently behind stable capability boundaries, leading to cumulative improvements in the overall user experience.
- To validate the proposed architecture through real-world deployments on the Astribot S1 platform, demonstrating that robot-local policies can be continuously improved while adhering to the same capability contracts and system interfaces.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

PHILIA employs a three-layer agentic multi-robot architecture designed for long-term physical coexistence, built around a robot gateway abstraction.

**System Architecture:**
The architecture comprises:
- **User Interfaces:** These provide interaction channels, including messaging, voice, and web applications, for human-robot communication.
- **Agent Control Plane:** This central component, which extends the OpenClaw assistant infrastructure, maintains persistent assistant sessions, manages multi-modal user interfaces, tool invocation, session management, and media handling. It operates at a semantic level, resolving target robot actors and their capabilities, enforcing authorization and safety policies, and dispatching requests to appropriate robot gateways. It is responsible for user intent understanding, memory retrieval, tool use, and high-level task composition.
- **Robot-Local Gateways:** These encapsulate platform-specific execution details, such as middleware, sensing, navigation, control bridges, and robot policies. They expose a compact set of high-level capabilities (e.g., scene observation, policy execution, semantic navigation, speech output, task interruption) to the agent control plane through a stable interface.

**Key Abstractions:**
- **Persistent User-Facing Assistant:** Represents the conversational identity, managing long-term memory, user context, and high-level task planning.
- **Robot Actors:** Physical robot embodiments that execute the assistant's intentions. They interact through a shared capability interface, allowing integration of heterogeneous platforms.
- **Robot-Local Capabilities:** High-level operations (e.g., policy execution, navigation) exposed by individual robots, abstracting robot-specific implementations.

**Interaction Model:**
Human-robot interaction follows an interleaved planning-execution paradigm for complex, long-horizon requests, where tasks are decomposed into plans spanning both robot and non-robot actions, with observations continuously informing decisions. Simpler requests, such as status queries or stop commands, are executed directly. Both modes utilize the same actor resolution, authorization, and gateway interface.

**Agent Control Plane Operations:**
The agent control plane interprets free-form user instructions via a capability-grounded interface, which consults a runtime capability manifest to produce structured dispatch decisions. To balance responsiveness and robustness, a five-stage cascaded routing system is implemented: regex/deterministic fast-pass, local fast classifier, deterministic semantic router, capability-grounded semantic router, and end-to-end agent execution. This hierarchy ensures that most simple intents are resolved quickly, while complex requests proceed to more computationally intensive routing stages. The control plane emits high-level capability requests, not low-level motor commands.

**Robot Gateway Abstraction:**
Each robot gateway publishes a dynamically discoverable runtime manifest detailing its supported capabilities and their input/output schemas. This allows new policies or capabilities to be immediately available to the agent without modifying the control plane. The gateway interface is designed to be independent of specific middleware, SDKs, or navigation stacks, focusing on abstract capabilities.

**Unified Multi-Robot Interface:**
Robots are represented as actor-scoped instances of a shared assistant identity. An actor registry maps user-facing names to robot actors, a backend registry associates actors with gateways, and a capability catalog advertises operations. Semantic place names are managed by the control plane, while individual robots maintain their own navigation stacks and local maps. This structure enables heterogeneous robots to operate within a common semantic environment.

**Capability Exposure and State Management:**
Manipulation policies are exposed as robot-local capabilities, with the agent handling semantic reasoning and policy selection, while the robot runtime manages image processing and high-frequency control. State is partitioned: the agent maintains persistent semantic state (preferences, plans, task history), while robot-local runtimes manage high-frequency execution state (sensor streams, maps). Gateways exchange lightweight semantic summaries.

**Safety Envelope:**
Physical actions are mediated through explicit execution gates. Before dispatch, the control plane enforces authorization, confirmation, and readiness checks. During execution, actor-scoped task arbitration ensures only one motion-changing capability is active per robot, with preemption or rejection handled via explicit policies. Stop and cancel operations are scoped to individual actors.

**Policies as Capabilities:**
The system integrates various learning strategies for robot capabilities:
- **Foundation Models:** PHILIA leverages zero-shot capabilities of generalist robot policies, exposing them through the stable gateway interface.
- **Task-Specific Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT):** Expert demonstrations are used to fine-tune generalist policies for dexterity and long-horizon tasks.
- **Deployment Loop Closure:** Offline reinforcement learning with advantage-conditioned post-training is used to improve policies, especially around failure and recovery states. This involves collecting autonomous rollouts augmented with human interventions. Chunk-aware binarization and temporal smoothing are applied to trajectory data.
- **Smooth Execution:** Training-time real-time chunking and runtime trajectory filtering are employed to address temporal inconsistencies between training and asynchronous execution, ensuring trajectory continuity.

**Memory System:**
- **Agentic Memory:** Based on OpenClaw's Markdown-based framework, it stores user facts, preferences, instructions, and interaction context in human-readable files. Retrieval tools enable on-demand access to relevant memory snippets.
- **Policy Memory:** History injection mechanisms (fixed-window historical action tokens, projecting historical action trajectories onto the primary camera view) provide temporal context to policies, addressing limitations of single-frame observations in long-horizon tasks.

**Navigation:**
Navigation is exposed as a robot-local capability via the gateway interface. The agent reasons about semantic places, while robot-local runtimes handle mapping, localization, and motion planning. Site mapping and semantic place annotation are performed via operator-guided SLAM. Per-session re-localization calibration aligns odometry frames with saved maps. Multi-robot composability is achieved by sharing persistent maps and place manifests across actors, while each robot maintains its own navigation runtime. Bounded local motions are also exposed as gateway capabilities, with language models not having direct access to continuous motion control.

The architecture was validated through real-world deployments on Astribot S1 robots.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The validation of PHILIA focused on the control-plane dispatch boundary and its ability to bridge free-form user language with robot capabilities, as well as demonstrating various use cases on the Astribot S1 platform.

**Capability-Grounded Dispatch:**
An evaluation was conducted on a test set of 483 utterances covering four intent classes: `robot_task`, `robot_query`, `robot_control`, and `non_robot`. Two router configurations were compared across seven language models of varying scales (from local Qwen3.5-4B to frontier models like GPT-5.5 and Claude-opus-4.8).
- **Model-Agnostic Routing Reliability:** The lightweight binary gate, designed to classify utterances as robot-related or not, achieved consistent performance across all models, with F1 scores of ≥0.96 and recall rates of ≥0.98. The local Qwen3.5-4B demonstrated fast inference, completing this lightweight classification in 414 ms, making it suitable for rapid pre-filtering.
- **High-Precision Full Dispatch on Frontier Models:** For the capability-grounded configuration, frontier models achieved 89–94% top-1 skill matching accuracy on robot-related requests and 83–89% accuracy on explicit actor resolution. Notably, Claude-series models exhibited a 100% rejection rate for non-robot utterances, effectively preventing erroneous physical actions.
- **Zero-Overhead Skill Extensibility:** The grounded router dynamically draws candidate skills from a runtime manifest catalog. This design allows new skills to become routable immediately upon manifest publication, without requiring any modifications to the routing prompt or the underlying language model.

**Latency Decomposition and Cascaded Routing:**
The evaluation of dispatch latency motivated a five-stage cascaded architecture to optimize for both accuracy and speed. While the full capability-grounded router offers higher precision, it incurs greater latency (ranging from 1.4x to 25x depending on the model).
- The hierarchical design effectively reduces average inference cost by resolving simple intents at low-latency early tiers.
- The deployed configuration, utilizing Qwen3.5-4B for initial pre-filtering and claude-opus-4.6 for full routing, achieved a balanced precision-latency trade-off. This setup resulted in 100% non-robot rejection with a p50 routing latency of 3.6 seconds.

**PHILIA Playbook (Representative Use Cases):**
The architecture was validated through several illustrative deployment scenarios on Astribot S1 robots, demonstrating core system capabilities:
- **Agent-to-Policy Reasoning Grounding:** PHILIA demonstrated the ability to ground high-level agent reasoning in low-level policy execution. This was achieved by constructing policy prompts that integrate user instructions, robot observations, and external knowledge. Examples included organizing items on a table based on inferred semantic categories (e.g., vegetables, fruit), user preferences (e.g., "food that Chinese people usually eat at breakfast"), or nutritional information (e.g., "high-calorie food"). This grounding mechanism allowed the robot to act on semantic constraints that were not explicitly hard-coded into the policies.
- **Agent-to-Policy Long-Term Memory Grounding:** The system successfully retrieved and utilized stored long-term user preferences to guide physical task execution. An example illustrated the robot recalling a user's breakfast preference (sandwiches and espresso) to correctly identify and move items in response to a request like "Put the food that I usually eat at breakfast into the large wooden tray."
- **One Agent, Multi-Robot Control:** PHILIA demonstrated the capability to control multiple Astribot S1 robots concurrently through independently registered robot gateways. A scenario showed one robot ("Alice") cleaning a table while another ("Bob") lifted a garbage bag, illustrating parallel task assignment and execution.
- **Plug-and-Play Policy Execution:** The architecture supported the seamless invocation of registered policies as modular components. For instance, PHILIA successfully utilized a "pack-the-backpack" policy (Lumo-2) on the robot, demonstrating that new or improved policies can be integrated and executed without altering the core system interfaces or the assistant logic.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The PHILIA architecture represents a significant contribution to the field of robotics by addressing critical challenges in enabling long-term physical coexistence with intelligent robots. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

**Enabling Long-term Coexistence and Scalability:**
By decoupling high-level agent reasoning from low-level robot execution, PHILIA provides a scalable and robust framework for deploying robotic assistants in complex, open-ended environments. This separation allows the system to support persistent assistant identities, maintain long-horizon memories, and coordinate diverse robot embodiments, moving beyond isolated task execution paradigms.

**Compositional System Advancement:**
The architecture’s modular design, characterized by stable boundaries between user interfaces, agent control plane, and robot-local gateways, fosters compositional improvements. This implies that advancements in any individual subsystem—be it user interfaces, robot embodiments, reasoning and memory capabilities, perception modules, navigation stacks, or robot-local policies—can be integrated independently, leading to cumulative enhancements in the overall user experience without requiring a full system redesign. This property is crucial for the continuous evolution and improvement of robotic systems.

**Facilitating Heterogeneous Robot Integration:**
The robot gateway abstraction is central to PHILIA's ability to support heterogeneous robot platforms. By exposing a unified capability interface while encapsulating platform-specific middleware and control logic, the architecture enables semantic reasoning and planning to generalize across different robot models. This reduces the complexity of integrating new robot hardware or diverse specialized robots into a single assistant ecosystem.

**Enhanced Human-Robot Interaction and Safety:**
PHILIA supports natural language interaction, allowing users to communicate open-ended intentions. This is combined with robust safety mechanisms, including explicit execution gates, authorization checks, and actor-scoped task arbitration, which prevent direct, unconstrained language model control over physical actions. This design promotes a balance between intuitive interaction and reliable, safe operation. The integration of long-term memory and user preferences also allows for personalized and context-aware assistance, making interactions more meaningful and efficient.

**Practical Deployment and Policy Evolution:**
The validation on Astribot S1 robots and the demonstration of agent-to-policy reasoning and memory grounding illustrate the practical applicability of the architecture. The framework supports continuous improvement of robot-local policies through strategies like fine-tuning and advantage-conditioned post-training, all while maintaining stable capability contracts. This means that robot capabilities can evolve and mature over time within the existing system structure.

**Potential for Broader Impact:**
PHILIA contributes to a future vision where intelligent robots can act as reliable and personalized partners in daily life. By distributing cognitive tasks across humans, machines, and their environments, the system aims to extend human capabilities rather than replace human agency. The architectural design, particularly the focus on stable interfaces and decoupling, sets a precedent for building complex, evolving robotic ecosystems that are more manageable, secure, and adaptable to future technological advancements. While current validation is on Astribot S1, the design's inherent support for heterogeneous platforms suggests a broader impact on how future multi-robot systems might be developed and deployed.
