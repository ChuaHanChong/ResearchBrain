# PACE: Phase-Progress-Aware Credit for Long-Horizon Embodied Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> PACE post-trains VLA policies for long-horizon manipulation via phase-progress-aware credit assignment: a GLC-Critic fuses local temporal/motion cues with phase-progress coordinates to correct a distributional remaining-cost critic, then Progressive Policy Distillation converts credit into positive/negative conditions with a coverage-adaptive curriculum. PACE lifts LIBERO-Long success rate from 73.8% to 83.3% and real-robot success from 66.5% to 81.8% over the strongest baseline.

## Problem

- Long-horizon manipulation gives only sparse terminal rewards, making it hard to attribute credit to individual steps within hundred-step, multi-phase episodes.
- Single-frame critics cannot disambiguate visually similar observations across different task phases, causing unstable value estimates near contact and phase transitions.
- Existing credit-conditioned distillation applies positive and negative labels symmetrically from the start, risking suppression of rare useful actions when early rollout data has few successes.

## Method

- **GLC-Critic**: a Global-Local Cooperative Value-Correction Critic aggregating a temporal-window transformer and adjacent-frame motion-difference features to infer phase and intra-phase progress per step.
- ==Phase-progress scalar residual correction==: applies a lightweight task-specific MLP correction to a discretized distributional base critic's expected remaining cost, preserving the base distribution shape.
- **Progressive Policy Distillation (PPD)**: routes training between ==PR-CFG== (positive-only classifier-free guidance) at low rollout coverage and ==FACD== (positive-and-negative conditioning) above a 50% success-rate threshold, via a reversible router.

## Results

- PACE reaches **83.3%** average success rate on LIBERO-Long, a **9.5-point** gain over ReCAP (73.8%) and over **20 points** above SFT variants of OpenVLA, Xiaomi-Robotics-0, and pi-0.5.
- GLC-Critic cuts held-out remaining-cost MAE from 133.03 to **97.78** and Boundary MAE near phase transitions from 136.02 to **77.01**, a **43.4%** reduction, with gains on all 10 tasks.
- Real dual-arm robot evaluation shows **81.8%** task-macro success rate versus ReCAP's 66.5%, with AvgT dropping from 76.3s to **58.7s** and Boundary MAE falling from 58.73 to **4.12**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Correcting only the scalar expectation of a distributional critic, rather than reshaping its full output, avoids added fitting burden while still capturing phase-transition-specific errors.
> - A single global success signal hides which sub-behaviors help or hurt; local motion plus phase-progress context is needed to disambiguate visually similar pre- and post-transition states.
> - Whether to exploit negative credit should depend on rollout coverage: early aggressive penalization can destabilize a pretrained policy, but the same negative signal later sharpens the quality boundary.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, such as RT-1, RT-2, OpenVLA, π0, π0.5, and Xiaomi-Robotics-0, has advanced the capability of robots to perform diverse manipulation tasks. These models are typically pretrained on large-scale, heterogeneous robot data and subsequently fine-tuned or "post-trained" using expert demonstrations and policy interaction trajectories. PACE operates within this post-training paradigm, specifically addressing challenges in long-horizon manipulation.

A significant challenge in the current landscape of VLA model post-training is the "credit assignment problem" for long-horizon tasks. In these tasks, a single episode can involve hundreds of control steps and multiple distinct phases, but feedback on success or failure is often only available at the episode's termination. This sparse reward structure makes it difficult to determine which specific actions or sequences of actions contributed positively, neutrally, or negatively to the task's progress.

Existing critic-based robot post-training methods, like ReCAP and ARM, estimate values or relative advantages to guide policy improvement. However, these methods are sensitive to the quality of credit signals, which remains difficult to obtain under long-horizon sparse rewards. Visual similarity across different task phases can lead to unstable value estimates from critics that rely solely on single-frame observations, as they may struggle to identify phase transitions or changes in contact states. Furthermore, errors in value estimation can propagate, generating misleading signals for downstream policies.

The related field of temporal progress modeling for long-horizon manipulation uses techniques such as stage structures or framewise temporal distances to learn denser rewards or to enable progress-aware skill learning. While these approaches model temporal progress as a reward or policy-conditioning signal, PACE differentiates itself by integrating phase-progress coordinates and local dynamic evidence directly into the correction mechanism of a distributional base critic.

PACE aims to complement advances in VLA model backbones by providing a solution to this credit assignment problem, focusing on both the estimation and utilization of fine-grained, step-level credit signals during the post-training phase. It addresses the limitations of current methods by proposing a mechanism to improve the reliability of value estimates, especially around critical task transitions, and by introducing an adaptive strategy for policy distillation that accounts for varying levels of data coverage and reliability of credit signals.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust credit-assignment framework, named PACE (Phase-Progress-Aware Credit), for post-training Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models in long-horizon embodied manipulation tasks. The motivation stems from several limitations observed in current post-training methodologies for such complex tasks:

Firstly, long-horizon manipulation tasks are characterized by **sparse terminal rewards**. A robot might execute hundreds of control steps over multiple distinct phases, yet the only feedback received is a binary signal of success or failure at the episode's conclusion. This terminal outcome alone is insufficient to provide detailed information about the efficacy of intermediate behaviors, making it difficult to discern which actions advanced the task, which led to stalls, or which even reversed progress. Effective policy improvement necessitates a finer-grained, step-level credit signal.

Secondly, a significant challenge in estimating value or progress in multi-phase tasks is that **different task phases can present visually similar observations**. For instance, a robot might be in a preparatory stage for grasping or might have just completed a grasp, with minimal visual differences between the two states. Critics that rely primarily on single-frame visual information struggle to identify crucial phase transitions or subtle changes in contact state. This can lead to unstable and inaccurate value estimates, as illustrated by the phase-dependent errors shown in Figure 1 of the paper.

Thirdly, **errors in value estimation can propagate and degrade the downstream policy**. If the critic's value signals are unreliable, any policy improvement method that uses these signals, such as computing advantages, risks learning from misleading information. This can result in policies that perform suboptimally or fail to generalize effectively.

Finally, the **utilization of credit signals requires careful management**, particularly during the early stages of post-training. Initial interaction data often contain a limited number of successful behaviors. Aggressively incorporating "negative credit" (signals indicating poor performance) from this scarce and potentially noisy data can destabilize the pretrained policy by steering the action distribution away from rare but potentially useful behaviors. As rollout coverage improves and more diverse data are collected, negative examples become more informative for identifying the boundary between effective and ineffective actions. Therefore, there is a need for a curriculum that adapts how credit is used.

In response to these motivations, PACE sets out to achieve two main goals:
1. **Improve Credit Estimation:** Develop a phase-progress-aware critic that can infer the phase and intra-phase progress of each step by aggregating visual and motion-difference features within local temporal windows. This critic aims to provide step-level credit assignment by applying residual correction to a discretized remaining-cost distribution, thereby enhancing the reliability of value estimates, especially around critical phase transitions.
2. **Optimize Credit Utilization:** Implement a Progressive Policy Distillation (PPD) mechanism that converts these step-level credit signals into positive and negative conditions. PPD employs a coverage-adaptive curriculum, initially prioritizing the protection of the pretrained policy using high-credit positive samples, and subsequently incorporating both positive and negative credits to learn the quality boundary as data coverage improves.

By addressing both the precision of credit assignment and the adaptive application of these signals, PACE aims to facilitate more effective and stable post-training of VLA models for long-horizon manipulation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

PACE operates as a credit-assignment framework designed for post-training VLA models in long-horizon manipulation. It integrates two main modules: the Global-Local Cooperative Value-Correction Critic (GLC-Critic) for credit estimation and Progressive Policy Distillation (PPD) for credit utilization.

**Remaining-Cost Modeling with Survival Cost:**
The framework formalizes each task as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) where a per-step cost `c_t = 1` is incurred. Successful task termination incurs zero terminal cost, while failure incurs a fixed penalty `C_fail`. The remaining-cost target `y_t` for a given timestep `t` is calculated as the sum of discounted per-step costs from `t` to the terminal step `T_τ`, plus the discounted terminal cost. This formulation provides a dense cost signal even for sparse reward tasks, explicitly accounting for accumulated cost and a terminal penalty for failures.

**GLC-Critic: Global-Local Cooperative Value-Correction Critic:**
The GLC-Critic is designed to produce step-level credit signals by correcting the expected remaining cost of a base distributional critic using phase-progress-aware local dynamics.
- **Multi-task Organization:** The architecture includes shared visual and temporal backbones across multiple tasks. However, the phase, progress, and fusion heads are task-specific, as are the credit thresholds, which helps prevent cross-task selection bias.
- **Local Temporal Evidence Encoding:**
 - A trainable visual encoder extracts features `f_t` from image observations, proprioception, and language instructions.
 - A Transformer-based Temporal Encoder aggregates features within a centered local temporal window `W_t = [f_{t-r}, ..., f_{t+r}]` to produce `h_temp_t`. This provides context regarding recent history and immediate future.
 - An additional MLP branch encodes adjacent-frame changes, specifically `[f_t; f_t - f_{t-1}; f_{t+1} - f_t]`, generating `h_motion_t`. This captures short-term dynamic differences.
 - The centered context `H_off` (including future observations) is only used by the offline labeler, ensuring that future observations do not influence the deployed policy.
- **Phase and Progress Prediction:**
 - The encoded temporal (`h_temp_t`) and motion (`h_motion_t`) features are combined and passed through an MLP to generate `h_t`.
 - Task-specific heads then predict the current phase `z_t` (as a softmax over `K_ph` phases) and the intra-phase progress `p_t` (a sigmoid output between 0 and 1). Motion differences contribute to value correction through these temporal coordinates.
- **Phase-Progress Supervision:** Phase boundaries in simulation are derived from privileged semantic predicates. For real-robot tasks, these boundaries are manually annotated. Intra-phase progress, particularly for the final incomplete phase of failed trajectories, is estimated by Qwen3-VL and then manually reviewed. This auxiliary supervision for phase and progress prediction uses privileged states offline and does not directly enter the critic or policy observations.
- **Remaining-Cost Value Correction:**
 - A base critic outputs `ℓ_raw`, a vector representing the logits for discretized remaining cost bins. The raw probability distribution `P_raw` and its expectation `C_raw` are computed.
 - The core of GLC-Critic is a scalar residual correction `∆_t`, which is a function of the raw logits `ℓ_raw` and the predicted phase `z_t` and progress `p_t`, learned by a lightweight MLP `G_ψ`.
 - The corrected expected cost `C(H_off)` is then calculated as `C_raw + λ ∆_t`. This approach directly corrects the expectation without modifying the underlying distributional shape, which would require higher-dimensional outputs.
 - The critic is trained in two stages: first, `L_critic = L_cost + α_z L_phase + α_p L_progress` (categorical cross-entropy for cost and phase, smooth-L1 for progress). Then, with phase and progress frozen, `G_ψ` is trained to minimize `L_corr = (C_t - C_t)^2`, where `C_t` is the normalized remaining-cost target.
- **Step-level Credit Score:** From the corrected cost, a step-level credit score `s_t` is derived: `s_t = C_t - (sum_{i=0}^{H-1} γ^i c_{t+i} + γ^H C_{t+H})`. A positive `s_t` indicates that the observed H-step behavior resulted in lower cost than expected, while a negative `s_t` implies additional cost. These scores are used for task-wise quantile ranking.

**PPD: Progressive Policy Distillation:**
PPD is a curriculum-based approach that adapts the use of credit signals during policy distillation based on the policy's rollout coverage (success rate). It automatically switches between two modes:
- **Unified Action Objective:** Both modes utilize a flow-matching policy. The objective `L_FM` aims to train a velocity field `v_θ` that guides a noisy action `a_τ` towards the expert action `a_i`, conditioned on observation `o_i` and credit `g_i`.
- **PR-CFG (Positive Residual Classifier-Free Guidance):** This mode is active when the average rollout success rate (SR) is approximately 50% or below. In this phase, only high-confidence positive-credit samples are used. These samples are identified by a task-wise upper quantile threshold `τ^(k)_pos` on `s_i`. The policy objective `L_PR` combines the unconditional flow matching loss `L_FM(unc)` with a term for positive-conditioned samples `L_FM(pos)`. During inference, positive residual guidance (`v_PR = v_unc + η(v_pos - v_unc)`) reinforces high-credit behaviors without leveraging negative credit, thus protecting the pretrained policy when successful behaviors are scarce.
- **FACD (Full Advantage-Conditioned Distillation):** Once the policy's average rollout SR surpasses a nominal transition boundary (e.g., above 50%), FACD is activated. In this mode, samples are conditioned on both positive and negative credit signals, determined by task-wise credit thresholds. A probability of 0.3 is used to drop conditions, allowing for more robust learning. The objective `L_FACD` incorporates both `L_FM(unc)` and `L_FM(g_i)` where `g_i` can be positive or negative. Inference uses a combination of attraction to positive and repulsion from negative credit (`v_FACD = v_unc + η_+(v_pos - v_unc) - η_-(v_neg - v_unc)`).
- **Positive-Sample Protection:** To further safeguard policy stability, credit filtering remains task-specific. While failed episode candidates can enter the positive set, they are capped at a fraction `ρ_fail`. Expert demonstrations, when available, are always labeled as positive.
- **Iterative Loop:** The training process is iterative. In each round `R`, the GLC-Critic is updated using the accumulated data buffer `D_R`. Policy distillation then updates the VLA backbone using only newly collected data `Δ_R` from the current round. The router automatically switches between PR-CFG and FACD. The updated policy then collects `Δ_{R+1}` for the next round.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of PACE was conducted on 10 LIBERO-Long simulation tasks and three real-world robotic-arm manipulation tasks, using strong VLA baselines and a shared `π_0.5`-Base policy backbone for fair comparison.

**Simulation Results (LIBERO-Long Tasks):**
- **Overall Performance:** PACE achieved an average success rate (SR) of 83.3 ± 2.5%, representing a 9.5 percentage point improvement over the best-performing baseline, ReCAP (73.8 ± 1.8%). It also significantly outperformed SFT variants (e.g., SFT (π_0.5) at 63.3 ± 1.3%). The average task execution time (AvgT) remained comparable to ReCAP (252.55 steps for PACE vs. 254.26 for ReCAP), indicating that PACE primarily converts previously incomplete rollouts into successful ones rather than prolonging existing successful executions. A paired two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test confirmed the statistical significance of PACE's improvement over ReCAP (W=0, exact p=0.0078).
- **Critic Accuracy:** The GLC-Critic demonstrated improved accuracy in remaining-cost estimation. Held-out Mean Absolute Error (MAE) was reduced by 26.5% (from 133.03 to 97.78). More importantly, the Boundary MAE, calculated near semantic task transitions (within ±10 steps), decreased by 43.4% (from 136.02 to 77.01). The return-ranking correlation at boundaries also increased from 0.745 to 0.871. This suggests that the phase-progress-aware correction mechanism is effective at disambiguating visually similar states around critical task transitions.
- **Critic Architecture Ablation:** Ablation studies on the GLC-Critic components showed that all correction variants provided substantial improvements over the single-frame Raw Critic. Removing temporal aggregation or motion differences led to higher MAEs and lower SRs, affirming their role in capturing local task evolution. Removing either the phase or progress prediction heads also caused performance degradation, with the full GLC-Critic model consistently yielding the lowest overall and boundary errors, indicating the complementary benefit of all its components.

**Progressive Distillation Curriculum Ablation:**
- A controlled ablation study on Progressive Policy Distillation (PPD) confirmed the effectiveness of its adaptive curriculum. In early training rounds with limited rollout coverage (low SR), PR-CFG (positive-only guidance) consistently outperformed FACD (full positive-negative guidance). For instance, in the first simulation round, PR-CFG achieved 54.5% SR compared to FACD's 49.0%.
- As rollout coverage improved, FACD became more effective. In later rounds (e.g., simulation round 2, and real-robot rounds 3-4), FACD surpassed PR-CFG, with the largest gap observed in real-robot round 4 (81.8% for FACD vs. 67.8% for PR-CFG). This validated the router's strategy: protecting pretrained behavior early on and leveraging negative credit for boundary learning as data coverage matures.

**Real-Robot Results:**
- **Overall Performance:** On three real-world dual-arm manipulation tasks, PACE achieved an 81.8% task-macro SR, which is a 15.3 percentage point improvement over ReCAP (66.5%). It also reduced the average successful-trial execution time (AvgT) from 76.3 to 58.7 seconds.
- **Task-specific Gains:** PACE demonstrated the highest SR and lowest AvgT across all three tested tasks (Table Wiping, Towel Folding, and Mug Placement and Knob Turning), including the longer, more complex Mug Placement and Knob Turning sequence.
- **Critic Accuracy Transfer:** The critic improvements observed in simulation transferred to the real robot. Held-out MAE decreased from 68.17 to 18.89, and Boundary MAE significantly dropped from 58.73 to 4.12, suggesting that phase-aware correction is robust to real-world sensing and actuation noise.

**Critic Behavior and Failure Analysis:**
- On successful trajectories, the Fused Critic more accurately tracked the remaining return and exhibited clearer adjustments around annotated phase transitions, leading to a substantial reduction in episode MAE and signed bias compared to the Raw Critic.
- On incomplete or failed rollouts, while the Fused Critic still reduced episode MAE, its predictions remained more variable and sometimes retained a positive bias. This variability was attributed to uncertainty in local progress when trajectories stalled or regressed. This behavior reinforced the decision to apply scalar correction to the expected cost rather than replacing the base distributional critic.
- The analysis indicated that while completed milestones provided stable phase anchors, unresolved failures left progress more dependent on local dynamics, leading to some residual errors. The PPD curriculum, by selectively using positive and negative credit and delaying full conditioning, mitigates the impact of these remaining critic errors on policy updates.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of PACE represents a significant contribution to the field of robotic manipulation, particularly for long-horizon, multi-phase tasks. Its impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Addressing a Core Challenge in Robot Learning:** PACE directly tackles the long-standing credit assignment problem in reinforcement learning for complex, long-horizon tasks characterized by sparse terminal rewards. By providing a method for generating reliable, fine-grained, step-level credit signals, it enables more effective and targeted policy improvement.
- **Enhanced Reliability of Value Estimation:** The GLC-Critic's ability to incorporate local temporal evidence and phase-progress coordinates leads to more accurate and stable remaining-cost estimates. The substantial reduction in Mean Absolute Error, particularly around semantic phase transitions in both simulation and real-world experiments, demonstrates its capacity to disambiguate visually similar states that occur at critical junctures of a task. This improved fidelity of value signals is crucial for robust learning.
- **Robust and Adaptive Policy Learning:** The Progressive Policy Distillation (PPD) framework provides a principled and adaptive curriculum for leveraging credit signals. Its ability to transition from protecting the pretrained policy with positive-only feedback to incorporating both positive and negative credit as rollout coverage improves offers a robust mechanism for policy refinement. This adaptive approach helps mitigate the risks of destabilizing policies with potentially noisy negative samples during early training, leading to more stable and effective learning.
- **Demonstrated Performance Improvements:** PACE consistently achieved significant performance gains over strong baselines in both simulation (9.5 percentage point increase in success rate over ReCAP) and real-world dual-arm manipulation (15.3 percentage point increase over ReCAP). The reduction in average task execution time on real robots also indicates improved efficiency in task completion. These empirical results underscore the practical utility and effectiveness of the proposed framework.
- **Generalizability Across Manipulation Domains:** By building upon general VLA backbones and being evaluated on diverse LIBERO-Long tasks and real-world dual-arm scenarios, PACE demonstrates potential for broad applicability across different manipulation tasks and robotic platforms. Its reliance on visual, proprioceptive, and language inputs makes it compatible with modern multimodal robot learning approaches.
- **Insights into Critic Behavior:** The detailed analysis of the critic's behavior, including its performance on successful vs. incomplete rollouts and around phase boundaries, offers valuable insights into the challenges of credit assignment in long-horizon tasks and the specific benefits of the phase-progress-aware correction mechanism. This understanding can inform future research into more robust and generalizable critic designs.

While the current work relies on task-specific phase annotation and manual review for some progress estimates, highlighting areas for future exploration in automated phase discovery, PACE establishes a foundation for more intelligent and autonomous robotic manipulation by addressing a fundamental challenge in robot learning. Its contributions could lead to more capable and adaptable robotic systems for complex real-world applications.
