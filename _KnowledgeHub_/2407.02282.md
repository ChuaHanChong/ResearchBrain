# Learning Bipedal Walking on a Quadruped Robot via Adversarial Motion Priors

> [!summary] Summary
> A framework is presented for teaching a quadruped robot to walk stably on its two rear legs, using an adversarial motion prior (AMP) and a teacher-student reinforcement learning architecture. This approach enables robust bipedal locomotion on diverse simulated terrains and allows the robot's front limbs to be available for other functions.

## Problem

- Quadruped robots are not mechanically designed for bipedal locomotion, possessing soft, point-contact feet and rear legs with limited range of motion, which inherently reduces stability.
- Traditional model-based control methods for legged robots often lack robustness and generalization in novel environments, while learning-based methods need effective ways to achieve natural, stable gaits and bridge the sim-to-real gap.
- Enabling bipedal locomotion on quadruped robots would free their front limbs for complex manipulation tasks, but this capability is challenging to achieve without external assistance.

## Method

- A teacher-student ==reinforcement learning== framework was developed, leveraging Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP) to learn bipedal gaits.
- Reference bipedal walking and running gaits were generated using Trajectory Optimization for Walking Robots (TOWR), integrating inverse kinematics to produce joint space data.
- The teacher policy was trained with privileged information and terrain data, while the student policy used a history of proprioceptive observations to infer this information, aiming to improve robustness and sim-to-real transfer.

## Results

- The robot achieved stable bipedal walking in simulation across various terrains (uniform, wave, sloped, stairs, obstacles) with acceptable tracking of commanded base linear velocities.
- The locomotion policy demonstrated robust recovery from external disturbances, such as a 100N push force, quickly regaining stability.
- An asymmetry in ground reaction forces was observed during bipedal gait, indicating an area for future refinement in achieving more balanced and symmetrical motion.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP) can effectively train quadruped robots to imitate dynamically feasible, natural bipedal gaits generated by trajectory optimization.
> - A teacher-student architecture, where the student infers privileged information from proprioceptive history, is effective for learning robust blind bipedal locomotion policies on challenging terrains.
> - Domain randomization during training, combined with carefully designed reward functions, contributes to the learned policy's robustness and its potential for sim-to-real deployment.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Legged robots are recognized for their superior adaptability to various terrains compared to wheeled and tracked robots. Within this domain, quadrupedal robots are known for stability and agility, while bipedal robots offer greater flexibility, particularly in freeing the upper body for complex manipulation tasks. This paper explores the intersection of these two robot types by investigating the feasibility of enabling a quadrupedal robot to perform bipedal locomotion. This capability would potentially allow quadruped robots to use their rear legs for walking while their front legs are engaged in manipulation, thereby enhancing their versatility.

A significant challenge in adapting quadruped robots for bipedal walking lies in their mechanical design. Quadruped robots typically have soft, point-contact feet, which inherently provide less stability than the firm, flat feet common in bipedal designs. Furthermore, the rear legs of quadruped robots are not optimized for bipedal gaits, often possessing limited range of motion and underactuation, contributing to potential instability.

Historically, two primary approaches have been used for legged robot locomotion control: model-based and learning-based methods. Model-based methods, while effective for highly dynamic motions, often lack robustness and generalization in novel environments due to difficulties in accurately modeling ground interactions and contact dynamics. In contrast, learning-based methods, particularly deep reinforcement learning (RL), offer a more adaptable solution by enabling robots to explore their full dynamics and environmental interactions. Early RL research often relied on simplified physical simulators. More recent developments include reference-free learning frameworks, which efficiently explore various gait patterns, and reference-based methods, which leverage prior knowledge—such as predefined reference trajectories from motion capture or trajectory optimization (TO)—to accelerate learning and achieve robust locomotion.

The Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP) framework, a specific type of reference-based learning, utilizes adversarial learning to predict state transitions and evaluate the similarity between reference and agent-generated data. AMP has been previously applied to bipedal robots, often in conjunction with periodic rewards to maintain stable gaits. Reference trajectories for these methods can be generated via motion capture, which is versatile but requires re-targeting for different robots, or through trajectory optimization, which can be more efficient by directly generating dynamically feasible motions for specific robot models, either using reduced-order models like Single Rigid Body Dynamics (SRBD) or full dynamics models.

Another challenge in robotics is the "sim-to-real" gap, where policies learned in simulation struggle to perform effectively on physical robots. Privileged learning paradigms have been introduced to address this by allowing the simulated agent access to information that is not available to the physical robot, with the aim of distilling this knowledge into a student policy that operates only on accessible sensor data.

This work integrates these concepts by adapting a learning framework, initially designed for quadrupedal robots, to enable bipedal locomotion in a quadruped. It leverages Adversarial Motion Priors within a teacher-student policy framework, where the teacher policy has access to privileged information, to imitate reference trajectories and navigate challenging terrains. The paper distinguishes itself by evaluating this framework specifically for blind bipedal locomotion on a quadruped robot, aiming for stable walking on both flat and complex surfaces, and aims to achieve this without the need for assistant devices used in some prior research.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust and agile bipedal locomotion policy for a quadrupedal robot, enabling it to walk solely on its two rear legs in a blind locomotion mode. This capability is intended to free the robot’s front legs, allowing them to be utilized for more complex manipulation tasks, thereby enhancing the robot's overall versatility and functionality in diverse applications.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges and opportunities:
- **Mechanical Design Constraints:** Quadruped robots are not inherently designed for bipedal locomotion. Their soft, point-contact feet offer less stability than those of dedicated bipedal robots, and their rear legs often have limited range of motion and are underactuated for bipedal gaits. These design mismatches contribute to the difficulty of achieving natural and stable bipedal walking. The research aims to overcome these inherent mechanical limitations through advanced control policies.
- **Enhancing Robot Versatility:** By enabling bipedal locomotion, the robot’s front legs become available for tasks such as grasping, carrying, or interacting with the environment, significantly expanding the robot's functional scope beyond pure locomotion. This aligns with the broader goal of developing multi-modal robots capable of performing a wider array of tasks.
- **Leveraging Advanced Learning Frameworks:** The study is motivated by the potential of combining Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP) with a teacher-student policy framework. AMP provides a mechanism to learn natural and stylized gaits by imitating reference data, while the teacher-student paradigm, incorporating privileged information, aims to improve policy robustness and facilitate sim-to-real transfer by allowing the student to infer otherwise inaccessible environmental details.
- **Addressing the "Blind Locomotion" Challenge:** The objective includes achieving "blind" bipedal locomotion, meaning the robot relies on proprioceptive feedback and inferred environmental information rather than direct visual input for navigation, which is crucial for operating in unknown or dynamically changing environments.
- **Advancing Beyond Previous Work:** The authors note that similar frameworks have been introduced but not validated on bipedal robots or quadruped robots specifically for bipedal mode without the aid of external devices. This research seeks to bridge that gap by demonstrating robust bipedal movement on a quadruped without such assistance.

In summary, the research aims to significantly advance the capabilities of quadrupedal robots by enabling them to perform stable, agile, and robust bipedal walking on various terrains, unlocking new possibilities for their application in complex real-world scenarios.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

This research proposes a novel framework for learning bipedal locomotion in a quadruped robot, integrating a teacher-student policy architecture with Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP) and trajectory optimization.

**A. Reinforcement Learning (RL) on Legged Robots**
The locomotion task is formulated as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP). The objective is to learn an optimal policy $\pi_\theta$ that maximizes the expected discounted return of rewards over time.

- **State Space:**
 - **Teacher Policy State ($s_{teacher_t}$):** Comprises proprioceptive observations ($o_{p_t} \in R^{48}$), privileged state information ($s_{p_t} \in R^{45}$), and terrain information ($o_{e_t} \in R^{187}$).
 - Proprioceptive observations include gravity vector orientation, base linear and angular velocities, joint positions and velocities, and the previous action.
 - Privileged state information includes ground friction/restitution coefficients, contact and external forces, and collision states—data not directly accessible by a physical robot in the real world.
 - Terrain information consists of 187 height measurements sampled from a grid around the robot's base.
 - **Student Policy State:** Utilizes a sequence history of proprioceptive observations ($H_t: [O_{t-N}..., O_t]$) to approximate the privileged information, compensating for its inaccessibility in a real-world setting.
- **Action Space:** Actions ($a_t \in R^{12}$) are 12-dimensional target joint position offsets, which are added to nominal joint positions and fed into a Proportional-Derivative (PD) controller to compute motor torque commands.

**B. Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP) and Rewards Design**
The AMP framework uses adversarial learning with a generator (the robot policy) and a discriminator to train realistic motion.
- **Discriminator ($D_\phi$):** A neural network is trained to distinguish between state transitions originating from a reference dataset (D) and those generated by the agent (A). The state $S^{AMP}_t \in R^{31}$ for the discriminator includes joint positions, point velocities, base linear velocity, base angular velocity, and base height. The discriminator's training objective incorporates a gradient penalty to ensure smoothness.
- **Style Reward ($r_{s_t}$):** This reward is derived from the discriminator's output, scaled to a range of. It encourages the robot to replicate the gait style observed in the reference dataset.
- **Overall Reward Function ($r_t$):** The total reward is a sum of three components:
 - **Task Reward ($r_{g_t}$):** Encourages tracking of commanded base linear and angular velocities.
 - **Style Reward ($r_{s_t}$):** Promotes imitation of the reference gait style.
 - **Regularization Reward ($r_{l_t}$):** Aims to ensure safe, smooth motion and minimize energy expenditure.

**C. Reference Generation**
Reference bipedal walking and running gaits for the A1 robot's rear legs are generated using Trajectory Optimization for Walking Robots (TOWR). TOWR produces dynamically feasible and energy-efficient motions without extensive manual tuning. To enhance imitation fidelity, inverse kinematics are integrated into TOWR to convert optimized trajectories into joint space data. This method is preferred over motion capture due to its precise matching of the state space between the simulated agent and the demonstrator, and its ability to avoid complexities associated with motion re-targeting.

**D. Framework and Training**
- **Teacher Policy Architecture:** The teacher policy ($\pi^{teacher}_\theta$) is trained using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). It consists of three Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) networks. Two MLPs encode low-dimensional latent representations for terrain data ($l^e_t \in R^{16}$) and privileged data ($l^p_t \in R^8$), respectively. The third MLP, a low-level network, uses proprioceptive observations and these two latent representations to generate the teacher's actions. The use of separate MLPs for encoding is designed to mitigate information loss during compression.
- **Student Policy Architecture:** The student policy is designed to mimic the teacher's actions without direct access to privileged state or terrain information. It is trained via supervised learning by minimizing two losses:
 - **Imitation Loss:** Ensures the student's actions closely match the teacher's actions ($a^{teacher}_t$).
 - **Reconstruction Loss:** Encourages the student's memory encoder to accurately reproduce the teacher's combined latent representation ($l^{teacher}_t$, consisting of terrain and privileged latent states). The student's memory encoder processes a sequence of 45 historical proprioceptive observations ($H_t$) as input to an MLP, while its low-level MLP maintains an identical structure to the teacher's.

**E. Training and Implementation Details**
- **Termination Conditions:** Episodes terminate if collisions involving the robot's trunk, upper limbs, thighs, or calves occur, focusing the training on stable bipedal locomotion with two-foot ground contact.
- **Domain Randomization:** To facilitate sim-to-real transfer, various simulation parameters are randomized during training. These include link mass, payload mass, ground friction, ground restitution, joint PD controller gains (K$_p$, K$_d$), and random perturbations to the robot's base velocity.
- **Simulation Setup:** Training was conducted using the Isaac Gym simulator with 500 parallel agents across various terrains of increasing difficulty. The process involved 26,000 iterations over 15.88 hours on a single NVIDIA RTX 4070 GPU. Each RL episode had a maximum duration of 1000 steps, with a control frequency of 50 Hz.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluated the trained policy for bipedal locomotion of an A1 quadruped robot within the Isaac Gym simulation environment across different terrain types and speeds.

**A. General Performance Trends**
Table II summarizes the performance, showing varying success rates and tracking accuracy. Generally, the robot demonstrated higher success rates and tracking accuracy at lower speeds (0.5 m/s) and on less challenging terrains (e.g., uniform, wave). As speed increased and terrain difficulty escalated (e.g., sloped, stairs, obstacles), both success rates and tracking accuracy tended to decrease. The authors attributed this reduction in performance on more challenging terrains, particularly slopes and obstacles, to the inherent mechanical design limitations of the quadruped robot performing bipedal gaits.

**B. Detailed Analysis on Specific Terrains**
For a more in-depth assessment, uniform terrain and discrete obstacle terrain were selected for detailed analysis, as they represent a range of uneven and challenging surfaces.

- **Base Linear and Angular Velocity Tracking:** Figure 3 illustrates the robot's base linear velocity in the x-direction and base angular velocity in yaw on uniform and discrete obstacle terrains.
 - **Linear Velocity:** The robot's base linear velocity tracking generally exhibited acceptable performance, with measured velocities closely following the commanded values.
 - **Angular Velocity:** Base angular velocity tracking in yaw, while less accurate than linear velocity, indicated the robot's capacity to follow the commanded trajectory to a reasonable extent.
 - **Disturbance Rejection:** A notable spike in both linear and angular velocities was observed around the 2-second mark, attributed to an external push force applied during testing. Despite this disturbance, the robot demonstrated rapid recovery of stability, which the authors cited as an indication of the locomotion policy's robust recovery capabilities.

- **Contact Force Data:** Figure 4 presents the vertical ground reaction forces ($F_z$) for the rear-left (RL) and rear-right (RR) legs on both discrete obstacles and uniform terrains. The data revealed an asymmetry in the contact forces, suggesting that the learned policy did not generate a perfectly symmetrical bipedal gait. The authors identified this as an area for future improvement, suggesting the integration of a symmetrical reward function or an orientation-based reward into the learning algorithm.

- **Push Test Results:** Figure 5 provides snapshots of the robot's response to a 100N push force applied along the x-axis for 0.1 seconds. The sequence demonstrated that despite a significant external perturbation, the robot was able to stabilize itself quickly. This outcome further supported the conclusion regarding the robustness and resilience of the developed locomotion policy.

**C. Summary of Experimental Outcomes**
The experimental results indicate that the developed legged locomotion policy effectively enabled the quadruped robot to perform bipedal walking in simulation. It exhibited satisfactory performance in tracking commanded base linear velocities and demonstrated robust recovery from external disturbances. The ability to maintain stability and follow trajectories across varying terrains, coupled with efficient disturbance rejection, supported the policy's overall robustness. While an asymmetry in gait was observed in the contact forces, the overall findings validated the effectiveness of the locomotion policy in achieving stable, efficient, and resilient bipedal movement for a quadrupedal robot in simulated environments.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework that contributes to the field of legged robotics by demonstrating the viability of enabling quadrupedal robots to perform robust and agile bipedal locomotion. The significance and potential impact of this work are multi-faceted:

- **Novelty in Bipedal Quadruped Locomotion:** The paper introduces a novel framework for achieving blind bipedal walking for quadrupedal robots using only their rear legs. This capability, which has not been extensively validated on quadruped robots in bipedal mode without assistance, represents an expansion of the operational paradigms for these platforms.
- **Enhanced Robot Versatility:** By successfully enabling bipedal gaits, the framework allows the quadruped robot's front legs to be freed for other tasks, such as manipulation, grasping, or complex interaction with the environment. This significantly increases the robot's versatility and potential for deployment in applications requiring both dynamic locomotion and dexterous manipulation, such as search and rescue, industrial inspection, or personal assistance.
- **Advancements in Learning-Based Control:** The integration of Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP) with a teacher-student learning framework, incorporating both privileged and terrain information, represents a methodological advancement. AMP facilitates the learning of natural and stylized gaits by imitating optimized reference data, while the teacher-student approach enhances policy robustness and helps bridge the sim-to-real gap by enabling the student policy to infer complex environmental dynamics from limited observations.
- **Robustness to Challenging Terrains and Disturbances:** The simulation results demonstrate the trained policy's ability to navigate various terrains, including flat, uneven, and obstacle-rich environments, and to recover stability from external disturbances. This indicates a level of robustness crucial for practical applications in unpredictable real-world settings.
- **Potential for Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The methodology, including the teacher-student architecture and extensive domain randomization, is designed to create policies that are more amenable to transfer from simulation to physical hardware. This is a critical step towards deploying advanced locomotion skills on actual robots.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The findings lay a foundation for future work, specifically in developing more robust bipedal motion capabilities on highly uneven terrain, successfully transferring these learned policies to physical robots, and refining the dynamic transition between quadrupedal and bipedal modes. These future directions could further expand the operational envelope and adaptive capabilities of legged robots.

Overall, this research contributes valuable knowledge towards the development of more capable and adaptable legged robots, demonstrating how advanced learning techniques can overcome mechanical design constraints to unlock new functional modes for existing robotic platforms.
