# Reinforcement Learning Fine-Tuning Enhances Activation Intensity and Diversity in the Internal Circuitry of LLMs

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Tsinghua University developed a framework to analyze how reinforcement learning (RL) fine-tuning alters the internal circuitry of large language models (LLMs). They found that online RL methods consistently increase activation intensity and diversity of internal pathways, while Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) shows weaker and inconsistent effects, a distinction supported by an intervention experiment establishing a causal link to performance.

## Problem

- Despite the empirical success of RL fine-tuning in enhancing LLM capabilities, the underlying internal mechanisms facilitating these improvements across diverse architectures remain underexplored.
- Existing mechanistic interpretability studies often do not correlate observed internal changes with the specific RL-based post-training methodologies used to obtain LLMs.
- It is challenging to transfer internal analysis strategies developed on simpler "toy problems" to the complex reasoning tasks where RL-induced improvements in LLMs are typically observed.

## Method

- The study adapted an efficient ==Edge Attribution Patching== (EAP) framework, conceptualizing LLMs as directed graphs of residual pathways to quantify the importance of internal information flow.
- A systematic procedure for filtering and truncating model-generated token sequences was developed, using self-entropy from partially truncated generations as the loss signal for internal analysis on mathematical problem-solving tasks.
- Three metrics	Activation Intensity, Information Complexity, and Distribution Kurtosis	were defined to quantify changes in overall engagement, heterogeneity, and concentration of internal pathway activations before and after RL fine-tuning.

## Results

- Online RL fine-tuning (PPO, GRPO) consistently increased "Activation Intensity" and "Information Complexity" while decreasing "Distribution Kurtosis" across multiple mathematical benchmarks, indicating broader and more diverse internal pathway engagement.
- DPO fine-tuning for Qwen2.5 showed inconsistent increases in activation intensity and weaker information complexity compared to online RL, though it also successfully reduced distribution kurtosis.
- An intervention experiment on Qwen2.5-**3B**-Instruct demonstrated that effective exploration (temperature **0.6**) during GRPO training causally led to significant internal circuit changes and a **51.2%** GSM8K accuracy, whereas noisy exploration (temperature 1.0) suppressed these internal evolutions and performance (**36.5%** accuracy).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Online RL fine-tuning systematically reshapes LLM internal circuitry by increasing both the redundancy (activation intensity) and flexibility (diversity in activation patterns) of information flow, which may explain its superior generalization ability.
> - A unified mechanistic view explains that online RL's on-policy sampling and dynamic gradient coefficients compel the network to activate and reinforce latent circuits, unlike DPO's reliance on a fixed support set.
> - Effective sampling in online RL causally drives the observed increases in internal activation intensity and diversity, establishing these internal changes as critical drivers, not just byproducts, of improved task performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of Large Language Models (LLMs) has increasingly shifted focus from pre-training to post-training methodologies, aimed at enhancing their capabilities for specific downstream tasks. Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and Reinforcement Learning (RL)-based post-training are prominent strategies in this domain, with empirical evidence suggesting that RL fine-tuning can improve LLM performance beyond what SFT alone achieves across tasks such as writing, reasoning, and coding.

Concurrently, a growing body of research has aimed at understanding the internal mechanisms and structures of LLMs. Initial investigations involved analyzing internal weights to understand problem-solving mechanisms, while subsequent studies utilized external neural probes to identify activation patterns. More recently, graph-theoretic approaches like Automated Circuit Discovery (ACDC) and Edge Attribution Patching (EAP) have been developed to assign importance scores to internal "edges" or sub-modules, thereby revealing functional circuits within LLMs. These mechanistic interpretability studies seek to reverse-engineer how models compute or what knowledge they represent.

Despite these advancements, a gap persists in the literature: most studies on RL-based post-training focus on external behavioral changes, while analyses of internal mechanisms rarely correlate these changes with the specific RL methodologies used to obtain the LLMs. This lack of integration between the two research lines is partly attributed to the challenge of transferring analytical strategies developed on simpler "toy problems" to the complex reasoning tasks where RL-induced improvements are observed. This paper positions itself at the intersection of these two areas, aiming to bridge the gap by systematically analyzing the internal effects of RL fine-tuning on LLMs, particularly in the context of mathematical reasoning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the observed empirical success of Reinforcement Learning (RL)-based fine-tuning in enhancing the capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs) beyond what supervised fine-tuning (SFT) can achieve. While external performance gains are evident across various downstream tasks, the underlying internal mechanisms that facilitate this enhancement, especially across diverse LLM architectures and intrinsic characteristics, remain underexplored.

Existing interpretability research has focused on understanding the internal structures of LLMs, but these studies have largely proceeded in parallel with the development and evaluation of RL post-training methods. Specifically, internal mechanistic analyses often do not correlate with the RL-based post-training methodologies by which LLMs are commonly obtained. This disconnect creates a knowledge gap, preventing a comprehensive understanding of *how* RL fine-tuning fundamentally alters an LLM's internal circuitry to improve its capabilities. The authors also identify the difficulty in directly transferring analytical strategies from simpler, toy problems to the complex reasoning tasks that RL post-training often addresses as a contributing factor to this gap.

To address these motivations, the key objectives of this study are:
1. **To construct a framework for systematically analyzing the mechanisms through which RL fine-tuning affects LLMs.** This involves adapting an efficient Edge Attribution Patching (EAP) framework to investigate internal differences in LLMs before and after RL fine-tuning.
2. **To interpret changes in internal neuron activations and derive general conclusions regarding the structural effects of RL.** The study focuses on mathematical problem-solving tasks, using cross-entropy from partially truncated generations to estimate the contribution weights of internal edges.
3. **To compare the internal effects of different RL paradigms.** A specific objective is to analyze models fine-tuned with Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) in contrast to online RL methods like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), to highlight methodological distinctions at the internal circuit level.
By achieving these objectives, the research aims to bridge empirical performance gains with interpretable shifts in internal information pathways, thereby offering insights for the future development of both LLMs and post-training methodologies.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a methodology centered on the Edge Attribution Patching (EAP) framework, which conceptualizes LLMs as directed graphs formed by their residual pathways. This approach allows for a granular analysis of internal information flow before and after reinforcement learning (RL) fine-tuning.

#### 3.1. Graph View of Transformer Residual Computation

The Transformer architecture, with its residual connections, is modeled as a directed acyclic graph (DAG). Each sub-module, specifically the multi-head self-attention mechanism (Aℓ) and the position-wise feed-forward network (Fℓ) within each layer, is treated as a node. Directed edges represent the flow of information from the outputs of these sub-modules to subsequent hidden states, formalizing the residual information pathways. This graph abstraction facilitates the analysis of the model from both a network flow and circuit-based interpretability perspective.

#### 3.2. Edge-Level Attribution

To quantify the importance of individual residual edges, the EAP framework is adopted. Unlike Automated Circuit Discovery (ACDC), which requires two forward passes per edge for ablation-based loss perturbation measurement, EAP utilizes a gradient-based linearization. It estimates the loss perturbation caused by removing an edge's contribution (e.g., setting O's contribution to zero in H → H-O) through a first-order Taylor expansion. This involves computing the Euclidean inner product of the loss gradient with respect to the hidden state (∇H L) and the sub-module output (O). This approach allows for simultaneous computation of attribution scores for all edges with a single forward and backward pass, making it computationally efficient for large-scale LLMs. The self-entropy (cross-entropy of the model with respect to its own output) from truncated generations on mathematical problem-solving tasks is used as the supervised loss signal for target 'y'.

#### 3.3. Sample Selection and Token-Level Truncation

To ensure a fair and manageable analysis, a systematic procedure for filtering and truncating model-generated token sequences is implemented:
- **Question Filtering**: Only questions correctly answered by both the base (SFT) and RL-tuned models are selected. To avoid biases from extreme answer lengths, a mean token length (¯T) is calculated, and questions falling outside a defined range ([T_min, T_max] = [β¯T, γ¯T]) are excluded. Additionally, a balance coefficient (δ) ensures comparable sequence lengths between the base and RL models.
- **Token Truncation and Self-Entropy Computation**: For the filtered questions, sequences are truncated to a length T_cut = α¯T. The self-entropy is then computed based on the model's logit outputs for these truncated sequences and the tokens it actually generated. This provides the loss signal (L_trunc) used in the EAP attribution.

#### 3.4. Experimental Settings

The experiments involved four pairs of open-source LLMs, each approximately 7 billion parameters, consisting of a base SFT model and its RL-tuned counterpart:
- **Deepseek-Math**: `deepseek-math-7b-instruct` (SFT) vs. `deepseek-math-7b-rl` (GRPO).
- **Mistral**: `mistral-7b-sft` vs. `math-shepherd-mistral-7b-rl` (PPO).
- **Distilled-Qwen**: `DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B` (SFT-distilled) vs. `AceReason-Nemotron-7B` (GRPO).
- **Qwen2.5**: `Qwen2.5-7B-SFT` vs. `Qwen2.5-7B-DPO` (DPO).
These models represent diverse architectures and training methodologies. The analysis was conducted on three public mathematical benchmarks: GSM8K, MATH, and College Math. Hyperparameters for sample filtering were set as β = 0.5, γ = 1.5, and δ = 0.5. The truncation length parameter α was varied (0.03, 0.1, 0.3, 0.5) to assess robustness. For each configuration, 100 pairs of contrastive samples were randomly selected.

#### 3.5. Metrics

Three metrics were defined to quantify the internal circuit differences based on the estimated edge-weight matrices W^(k) (where k is a sample, o is output neuron, i is input neuron):
- **Activation Intensity (Act.Intens.)**: The average magnitude of all edge weights across all samples, outputs, and inputs. This metric reflects the overall engagement and strength of internal pathways.
- **Information Complexity (Info.Complex.)**: Shannon entropy computed over the binned absolute values of all edge weights. Higher entropy indicates a more heterogeneous and less predictable distribution of activations, reflecting greater diversity in active information pathways.
- **Distribution Kurtosis (Dist.Kurt.)**: The average kurtosis of each sample's edge-weight matrix. This metric assesses the shape and tail behavior of the activation distributions, with lower kurtosis suggesting less reliance on a few concentrated high-activation edges.

#### 3.6. Intervention Experiment (Appendix B)

To explore the causal link between internal circuit changes and performance improvement, an intervention experiment was conducted using Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct. The sampling temperature during GRPO training on the GSM8K training set was varied (0.6 for effective exploration, 1.0 for noisy exploration). The evolution of internal metrics (Activation Intensity, Information Complexity, Distribution Kurtosis) and GSM8K test accuracy was tracked over training steps, correlating exploration quality with internal circuit development and downstream performance.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study's main findings detail systematic alterations in the internal circuitry of LLMs following reinforcement learning (RL) fine-tuning, with distinct patterns observed for online RL methods versus Direct Preference Optimization (DPO).

#### 4.1. Robust Effects of Online RL Fine-Tuning

For models fine-tuned with online RL algorithms such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) (Deepseek-Math, Mistral, and Distilled-Qwen series), two consistent internal effects were observed across various mathematical datasets and hyperparameter settings:

1. **Increased Activation Intensity**: The "Activation Intensity" metric consistently increased after online RL fine-tuning (Table 1). This indicates that a larger number of internal pathways are engaged, and the signals propagating through these pathways become stronger. Visualizations (Figure 2) corroborate this by showing that many neural connections strengthen post-RL. This suggests a broader utilization of the model's internal capacity.
2. **Greater Diversity in Activation Patterns**: The "Information Complexity" metric generally increased, while "Distribution Kurtosis" tended to decrease (Table 1).
 - **Increased Information Complexity**: Higher entropy values signify more heterogeneous and less predictable distributions of edge activations, suggesting a diversification of active information pathways.
 - **Decreased Distribution Kurtosis**: A reduction in kurtosis indicates that the activation distributions become less concentrated on a few high-intensity outlier edges and more spread out.
 Figure 3 further supports this, showing increased inter-sample diversity in activation structures (panel a) and higher entropy of output edge patterns (panel b) after RL. These changes imply that RL fine-tuning leads to more redundant and flexible information flow within the LLM's internal representations, which is posited to contribute to improved mathematical generalization.

#### 4.2. Deviations in DPO Fine-Tuning

Models fine-tuned with Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), specifically the Qwen2.5 series, exhibited different internal changes compared to the online RL methods:
- **Inconsistent Activation Intensity**: Activation strengths did not show a clear or consistent increasing trend.
- **Weaker Information Complexity**: On the College Math dataset, as the truncation length (α) increased, the "Information Complexity" metric for DPO-trained models remained lower than that of the initial SFT model.
- **Diversity Improvement with Nuance**: While DPO-trained models did show some improvement in activation pattern diversity (Figure 3a), the magnitude of this improvement was relatively lower than that observed with PPO or GRPO.
DPO successfully reduced Distribution Kurtosis, similar to online RL, suggesting it attenuates reliance on a few high-intensity edges. However, its overall impact on systematically increasing activation intensity and diversity was weaker or inconsistent.

#### 4.3. Unified View Interpretation of Mechanisms

The observed internal differences are interpreted through a unified framework (Equation 4) by analyzing the sampling distribution (D) and gradient coefficient (GC_A) of different post-training algorithms:
- **SFT (Supervised Fine-Tuning)**: Operates on a static, fixed human-annotated dataset (D_SFT) with a constant gradient coefficient (GC_SFT = 1). This leads to optimization on a narrow manifold of "correct solutions," resulting in low-entropy internal representations concentrated on a few outlier edges (high kurtosis) and limited engagement of redundant pathways (low activation intensity).
- **Online RL (PPO, GRPO)**: Introduces on-policy sampling (D_RL), where outputs are dynamically generated by the evolving policy. This mechanism expands the stochastic support set of the training distribution, compelling the network to activate and reinforce latent or "dormant" internal circuits to handle an expanded state space. The dynamic gradient coefficient (e.g., GC_GRPO) from reward models further drives the mobilization of these less active circuits, especially for harder problems. This explains the observed increase in Activation Intensity and Information Complexity, and the decrease in Distribution Kurtosis.
- **DPO (Direct Preference Optimization)**: Functions as an offline (or semi-offline) algorithm, relying on a fixed support set of preferred/dispreferred pairs (D_DPO). This limits the mechanistic pressure to expand the network's functional capacity through stochastic sampling, leading to weaker and inconsistent increases in Activation Intensity and Information Complexity. However, DPO's soft margin preference optimization, captured by GC_DPO, relaxes strict token-matching constraints, which reduces reliance on a few high-intensity edges, thus successfully decreasing Distribution Kurtosis.

#### 4.4. Causal Hypothesis Support

An intervention experiment detailed in Appendix B provides empirical support for the causality of these internal changes. By modulating the sampling temperature during GRPO training of Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct on GSM8K:
- **Effective Exploration (Temperature 0.6)**: Led to significant increases in Activation Intensity and Information Complexity, along with a notable decrease in Distribution Kurtosis during mid-training. These internal changes correlated with a rapid increase in GSM8K test accuracy, reaching 51.2%.
- **Noisy Exploration (Temperature 1.0)**: Resulted in significantly suppressed growth in Activation Intensity and Information Complexity, and a smaller decrease in Distribution Kurtosis. This suppression of internal circuit evolution correlated with slower performance growth, stagnating around 36.5% accuracy.
This intervention-suppression effect suggests that diverse and high-intensity internal pathway activation is a key mechanism bridging effective sampling and performance improvement, rather than being a mere byproduct.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the understanding of Large Language Models (LLMs) by systematically investigating the internal mechanistic effects of reinforcement learning (RL) fine-tuning. Its significance and potential impact are multifaceted:

1. **Bridging the Interpretability Gap**: The study effectively bridges the divide between the observed empirical performance gains of RL-based LLM post-training and the underlying internal structural and computational changes. By directly analyzing internal circuitry alterations, it moves beyond external behavioral evaluation to provide a mechanistic perspective on RL's effectiveness.

2. **Unified Mechanistic Perspective on RL Post-Training**: It offers a unified view of how online RL fine-tuning systematically reshapes the internal circuitry of LLMs. The findings suggest that online RL consistently enhances both the redundancy (increased activation intensity) and flexibility (greater diversity in activation patterns) of information flow. This provides a potential explanation for RL's superior generalization ability in complex tasks, such as mathematical reasoning.

3. **Highlighting Methodological Distinctions**: The research highlights fundamental internal differences between online RL paradigms (PPO, GRPO) and preference-based approaches like Direct Preference Optimization (DPO). The observation that DPO yields weaker or inconsistent internal changes compared to online RL points to the distinct operational mechanisms and resulting internal dynamics of these methods, despite their shared basis in RL objectives. This distinction is critical for understanding the strengths and limitations of various post-training strategies.

4. **Guidance for Future LLM and Post-Training Algorithm Design**: The insights gained—specifically, the importance of increased pathway engagement and diversified activation patterns—can inform the design of future LLMs and the development of more effective post-training methodologies. Engineers and researchers could potentially aim to encourage these internal characteristics through explicit architectural design or novel training objectives, rather than solely relying on external performance metrics.

5. **Empirical Support for Causal Link**: The intervention experiment provides empirical evidence supporting a causal link between effective sampling in online RL, the resultant changes in internal circuit dynamics (increased intensity and diversity), and improvements in downstream task performance. This moves beyond mere correlation, suggesting that these internal changes are not just byproducts but critical drivers of enhanced capabilities.

6. **Broader Implications for LLM Interpretability**: By demonstrating a scalable and efficient method for internal circuit analysis (EAP adapted for large-scale LLMs), the study offers a robust tool for future interpretability research. This could facilitate deeper investigations into how various training paradigms, data properties, or architectural modifications influence the internal workings of LLMs across different domains.

In summary, this work provides foundational insights into the "how" behind RL's impact on LLMs, moving interpretability closer to explaining real-world LLM capabilities and offering actionable guidance for the future development of more robust and capable artificial intelligence systems.
