# Seeing Less Is Not Seeing Safely: Privacy Leakage from Task-Scoped Robot Perception Exports

> [!summary] Summary
> Task-Functional Perception Distillation (TFPD) is a privacy framework that keeps rich robot perception local and exports only task-scoped representations: free-space structure for navigation, anonymous geometry for collision checking, functional target references for object-goal execution, then profiles each export by task utility, direct exposure, and five residual inference attacks. Across 120 AI2-THOR scenes, task-equivalent navigation exports showed linkability ranging from 0.532 to 0.970.

## Problem

- Prior privacy-preserving robot systems protect information at or before visual perception, leaving derived semantic, geometric and spatial representations that cross the local boundary unexamined.
- Representation defenses are designed for one function, such as localization or cloud detection, and are never compared across different downstream robot tasks consuming different information.
- Data minimization deletes explicit sensitive fields, yet removed attributes stay inferable from retained geometry, topology or layout, so field removal alone evidences nothing.

## Method

- **TFPD** partitions each candidate export into retained, locally transformed, and omitted information, so a locally resolved semantic target can be released as a spatial region rather than a label.
- Task-scoped candidate families: metric, normalized and topological ==free space== for navigation; anonymous exact versus 0.1-rounded ==coarsened cuboids== for collision checking; label, coordinate, region, anonymous ID or ==graph node== for object goals.
- A vector-valued ==multi-risk profile== pairs utility with room, private-object, object-category, scene-linkability and target-category attacks, plus an audit expanding 26 of 33 attacks to use all public fields.

## Results

- Three navigation exports were task-equivalent (success **1.000**, path ratio **0.898**), yet Top-1 linkability spanned **0.532** normalized, **0.782** topological, **0.970** metric, against a **0.050** random baseline.
- Replacing the explicit target label with a target region cut target-category macro-F1 from **1.000** to **0.077** while object-goal success stayed at **0.995**; graph nodes reached **0.053**.
- Geometric coarsening lowered object-category macro-F1 from **0.704** to **0.556** but cut collision F1 from **1.000** to **0.950** and raised private-object inference from **0.844** to **0.928**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Task-equivalent exports are not privacy-equivalent, and stronger abstraction gives no universal privacy ordering; rankings even flip across scene distributions.
> - Weak attackers understate leakage: fuller use of the same public export raised private-object inference from 0.405 to 0.928, so audit attacker feature scope.
> - Report leakage per channel, never as one score, since a transformation can improve one risk while worsening another and costing task utility.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Domestic robots, while providing increasing utility, pose significant privacy challenges due to their reliance on rich perception capabilities within private living spaces. Existing research on robot privacy has largely focused on mitigating risks at or before the visual perception stage. This includes techniques such as reducing visual fidelity in raw sensor data, concealing sensitive objects or regions in robot video, or processing images locally to extract necessary information for semantic mapping before discarding raw observations. These approaches primarily aim to protect information contained within raw sensor streams (e.g., RGB or RGB-D).

However, modern robot architectures routinely transform raw sensor observations into structured representations—such as semantic maps, object representations, scene graphs, and planning data—which are then consumed by various downstream components like planners, cloud services, logging systems, and learning pipelines. These derived representations can still encode a significant amount of sensitive information, including object identities, geometry, spatial relationships, and task goals. The leakage of information from these processed representations, even when raw sensor data remains local, represents a distinct and less-explored privacy vector. Prior work has noted that high-level structures like line clouds or navigation policies can retain exploitable information, challenging the assumption that abstracting or obfuscating geometric or semantic data inherently guarantees privacy.

This paper addresses this gap by focusing on the "post-perception representation privacy surface." It investigates the privacy risks carried by representations *derived* from a robot's sensing and interpretation of the physical home, after raw sensor data has been processed locally. While related work on privacy-preserving localization,–, multiparty perception, and cloud-based object detection demonstrates that representation changes can reduce disclosure for specific functions, this work extends the scope to multiple, foundational robot tasks. It also connects to the principle of data minimization, but distinguishes itself by analyzing the privacy implications of *structured semantic, geometric, and spatial representations* required by different robot tasks, rather than focusing solely on minimizing independent input attributes. The core contribution is a framework for evaluating whether alternative, task-functional representations, with equivalent utility, expose comparable privacy risks across multiple downstream robot functions, and whether increasing abstraction necessarily reduces residual leakage.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the increasing deployment of domestic robots in private environments. While these robots require rich perception capabilities for effective operation (e.g., navigation, manipulation, safety), their sensor data and the derived representations inherently carry sensitive information about the household. The core problem addressed is that traditional privacy-preserving methods, which focus on securing raw sensor data, are insufficient because processed, structured representations can still inadvertently disclose private information even after raw data is localized.

The key objectives of this work are detailed as follows:

1. **Identify and Characterize Post-Perception Privacy Risk:** To establish that representations derived from local perception, even when raw sensor data is kept private, constitute a distinct "privacy surface." These representations—encompassing semantics, geometry, spatial structure, and task targets—can leak sensitive household information to downstream planners, cloud services, logs, or learning pipelines.

2. **Introduce Task-Functional Perception Distillation (TFPD):** To propose a representation-export framework designed to manage this post-perception privacy risk. TFPD operates by placing a "representation-export boundary" after rich local perception. Within this framework, rich interpretation of the environment remains local, and only task-scoped representations, constructed specifically around the requirements of downstream functions, are released.

3. **Distinguish Direct Exposure from Residual Inference:** To develop a comprehensive privacy profiling approach that goes beyond merely removing explicit privacy-bearing fields. TFPD aims to characterize both the information directly exposed by a representation and the *residual inference risks*—information that can still be inferred from the complete public representation through its geometry, spatial organization, topology, or task context, even if explicit sensitive fields are removed.

4. **Jointly Evaluate Task Utility and Multiple Privacy Risks:** To empirically assess candidate representations not just by their privacy-preserving properties in isolation, but also by their ability to maintain essential task utility. The framework profiles candidate exports according to three properties: the task utility they preserve, the information they expose directly, and multiple residual inference risks (e.g., room inference, private-object inference, scene linkability, target-category inference). This allows for understanding privacy improvements, privacy-utility tradeoffs, and conflicting effects across different privacy channels.

5. **Investigate the Relationship between Abstraction and Privacy:** To empirically test the hypothesis that stronger abstraction or the removal of more explicit information necessarily leads to a more privacy-preserving representation. The research seeks to demonstrate that representation abstraction does not induce a simple monotonic privacy ordering and that task-equivalent representations can exhibit substantially different residual privacy risks.

By addressing these objectives, the paper aims to shift the paradigm of robot privacy design towards a more nuanced, task-specific, and multi-risk evaluation of the complete public representation rather than relying on generalized field removal or abstraction as sufficient privacy guarantees.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors introduce Task-Functional Perception Distillation (TFPD), a framework designed to manage privacy leakage from post-perception representations. TFPD positions a representation-export boundary between a robot's rich local perception stack and downstream consumers (e.g., planners, cloud services, logging systems). The core idea is to keep detailed, privacy-sensitive perception information local while releasing only task-scoped representations tailored to the specific requirements of downstream functions.

**A. Task-Scoped Export Construction:**
For a given private home scene `S` and its rich local perception state `P(S)`, TFPD defines a family of candidate exports `R_τ = {R_τ,1, ..., R_τ,m}` for each downstream task `τ`. Each candidate `R_τ,j` is constructed from `P(S)` via a transformation `D_τ,j(P)`. The framework distinguishes three information-handling roles:
- `K_τ,j`: Information directly retained and exposed in the export.
- `X_τ,j`: Information used locally to construct a transformed, task-functional representation but not necessarily exposed.
- `O_τ,j`: Information omitted entirely, neither exposed nor used for construction.
This formulation allows for transformations (e.g., coordinate normalization, geometric coarsening, replacing semantic labels with spatial references) rather than just simple field removal. The downstream consumer receives the *complete* task-facing representation, not just the privacy-transformed component.

**B. Task-Specific Instantiations:**
The paper evaluates TFPD across three representative foundational robot functions:
1. **Navigation:** Requires traversable-space structure and connectivity.
 - `R_metric`: Retains free space in a global metric frame, including absolute position, scale, and spatial extent.
 - `R_normalized`: Transforms metric free space into a local normalized frame, removing global origin and absolute metric scale while retaining relative geometry and connectivity.
 - `R_topological`: Replaces explicit metric geometry with a node-edge connectivity graph, omitting coordinates and metric edge lengths but retaining reachability.
 - All navigation exports omit semantic object labels and identities.

2. **Collision Checking:** Requires obstacle geometry for intersection and clearance reasoning.
 - `R_exact`: Anonymous exact geometry, removing semantic labels and identities, but retaining precise obstacle position and dimensions (represented as axis-aligned cuboids).
 - `R_coarsened`: Further transforms exact geometry into lower-precision obstacle proxies by rounding all x, y, z components of centers and sizes to one decimal place.

3. **Object-Goal Execution:** Requires a reference to a locally resolved target.
 - `R_label`: Exports the semantic target `y_t` directly (baseline).
 - `R_coordinate`: Exports the target's coordinate.
 - `R_region`: Exports a spatial region encompassing the target.
 - `R_ID`: Exports an anonymous identifier for the target.
 - `R_node`: Exports a planning-graph node associated with the target.
 - For the transformed candidates, `y_t` remains local and is used to construct the reference.

**C. Privacy and Utility Profiling:**
TFPD evaluates each candidate representation `R` using a multi-risk profile `Φ_τ(R) = (U_τ(R), E_τ(R), L_τ(R))`.
- **Direct Exposure (E_τ(R)):** Measures explicitly privacy-bearing fields contained in the export.
- **Residual Inference (L_τ,q(R)):** Measures the success of an attacker `A_τ,q` in inferring private attribute `Z_q` from the *complete public representation* `R`. Metrics include macro-F1 for classification attacks and Top-1 accuracy for linkability. Baselines are a most-frequent-class predictor (for classification) or random matching (for linkability).
 - Evaluated risks: Room inference, Private-object inference (binary presence), Object-category inference, Scene linkability (matching a perturbed export to a gallery scene), Target-category inference.
- **Task Utility (U_τ(R)):** Measured by task-specific performance: navigation success, path feasibility, mean path ratio; collision F1; object-goal execution success.
The framework emphasizes that `R_a ≡_τ R_b` (task equivalence) does not imply `L_τ(R_a) = L_τ(R_b)` (privacy equivalence).

**D. Evaluation Protocol:**
1. **Primary AI2-THOR Evaluation:** Uses 120 AI2-THOR scenes with a scene-disjoint 80/20/20 train/validation/test split. Attacker models and representation-specific preprocessing are fitted on training data, selected using validation data, and frozen for held-out test evaluation. Results are reported with 95% confidence intervals from 1,000 scene-cluster bootstrap replicates.
2. **Representation-Aware Attacker Audit:** The study distinguishes between "restricted-feature attacks" (using a subset or summary of information) and "representation-aware attacks" (exploiting *all* public fields and structural information in the export). An audit of 33 representation-attack combinations identified 26 cases where broader, representation-aware feature variants were added for validation-selection, to ensure attackers fully leverage available information.
3. **ProcTHOR Navigation Replication:** The navigation evaluation is repeated on a fixed set of 120 ProcTHOR houses (80/20/20 train/validation/test split) to test the robustness of findings across a different scene distribution, using the same representation constructions and evaluation protocol.
4. **Controlled Synthetic Diagnostics:** A separate synthetic environment is used to isolate specific leakage mechanisms under simplified, controlled representation changes.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation addressed four core questions, with findings indicating that direct field removal or stronger abstraction does not universally guarantee privacy, and representation-aware evaluation is critical.

**Navigation (AI2-THOR Primary Evaluation):**
- **Utility Equivalence:** All three navigation representations (metric, normalized, topological) achieved identical high task utility: 1.000 success, 1.000 path feasibility, and 0.898 mean path ratio across 1,000 held-out trials. They are thus task-equivalent.
- **Privacy Inequivalence:** Despite equivalent utility, these representations exhibited substantial differences in privacy risks:
 - **Representation-level linkability:** Metric free space was highly linkable at 0.970 Top-1 accuracy. Normalized free space significantly reduced linkability to 0.532, a 43.8 percentage point reduction. Topological free space had linkability of 0.782. (Random baseline: 0.050). This demonstrates a privacy improvement (normalized vs. metric) without utility loss.
 - **Attribute Inference:** For room inference and private-object inference, the point estimates consistently showed `R_normalized < R_topological < R_metric`. Specifically, metric free space supported strong private-object inference (macro-F1 0.928), while normalized free space did not exceed the baseline (0.412). Topological free space (0.567) was above the baseline, indicating that more abstract representations (topological vs. normalized) did not necessarily yield lower leakage.
- **Representation-Awareness Impact:** For metric navigation, private-object inference macro-F1 increased from 0.405 (restricted attack) to 0.928 (representation-aware attack), highlighting that fully exploiting public information significantly changes leakage estimates.

**ProcTHOR Navigation Replication:**
- **Utility Equivalence:** Replicated the AI2-THOR finding, with all three representations achieving 1.000 success and 0.879 mean path ratio.
- **Privacy Inequivalence:** Maintained strong representation-dependent linkability: Metric (1.000), Normalized (0.573), Topological (0.497). The key change was the relative ordering of normalized and topological linkability, which reversed compared to AI2-THOR. This suggests that while task-equivalence/privacy-inequivalence holds, the precise privacy ranking can be distribution-dependent.

**Collision Checking:**
- **Utility-Privacy Trade-off:** Anonymous exact geometry achieved a collision F1 of 1.000, while coarsened geometry resulted in a lower F1 of 0.950, indicating a measurable utility cost.
- **Channel-Specific Privacy:**
 - **Object-category inference:** Coarsened geometry reduced object-category macro-F1 from 0.704 (exact) to 0.556, demonstrating a reduction in semantic leakage from geometry.
 - **Other risks:** Room inference remained high (1.000 for both), and private-object inference increased from 0.844 (exact) to 0.928 (coarsened), showing conflicting effects across privacy channels.

**Object-Goal Execution:**
- **Utility Equivalence:** All five representations (target label, coordinate, region, anonymous ID, graph node) achieved identical object-goal success of 0.995.
- **Target-Semantic Reduction:** Replacing an explicit target label (macro-F1 1.000) with functional references (coordinate, region, ID, node) drastically reduced target-category macro-F1 to between 0.053 and 0.094.
- **Persistent Scene-Level Leakage:** Broader scene-level attacks (room inference and private-object inference) remained high and unchanged across all five target representations (room inference ~0.644, private-object inference ~0.913), highlighting that reducing one privacy channel does not uniformly affect others.

**Controlled Synthetic Diagnostics:**
- These diagnostics supported the primary findings by isolating mechanisms: task-required geometry can encode removed semantics, task-functional references reduce semantic disclosure but not necessarily all target information, and spatial linkability depends on the retained information.

In summary, the results demonstrate that removing explicit privacy-bearing fields does not eliminate residual inference, task-equivalent representations can be privacy-inequivalent, and stronger abstraction does not necessarily lead to universal privacy improvements. The precise ranking of privacy leakage can depend on the environment distribution.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research makes several significant contributions to the field of robot privacy, with broad implications for the design and deployment of domestic robots:

1. **Identification of a New Privacy Surface:** The paper rigorously identifies and characterizes the "post-perception representation privacy surface" as a distinct area of privacy risk. It demonstrates that even when raw sensor data is kept local, the structured representations derived from this data can leak sensitive household information, including semantics, geometry, and spatial organization. This finding shifts the focus of robot privacy from solely protecting raw sensor streams to also meticulously managing the derived data products.

2. **Introduction of Task-Functional Perception Distillation (TFPD):** TFPD provides a principled framework for designing and evaluating privacy-preserving robot perception systems. By placing a representation-export boundary and emphasizing task-scoped representations, it offers a concrete strategy for limiting privacy exposure to downstream components without compromising local robot intelligence. This framework is a conceptual tool for system architects to systematically consider privacy at a critical stage of the robot's data pipeline.

3. **Comprehensive Multi-Risk Privacy Profiling:** The work introduces a methodology for evaluating candidate exports based on task utility, direct exposure, and *multiple residual inference risks*. This multi-faceted approach acknowledges that privacy is not a monolithic concept and that transformations can have varying, even conflicting, impacts across different privacy channels (e.g., reducing object category inference but increasing private-object inference). This granular evaluation is crucial for informed decision-making in privacy-preserving robot design.

4. **Empirical Demonstration of Privacy Inequivalence and Non-Monotonicity:** The empirical results are impactful, demonstrating that representations achieving identical task utility can exhibit substantially different privacy risks. Furthermore, the findings challenge the intuitive assumption that stronger abstraction or the removal of more explicit information necessarily leads to greater privacy. For example, the topological representation for navigation, despite being more abstract, sometimes exhibited higher leakage than a less abstract, normalized representation. This provides a critical caution against oversimplifying privacy guarantees based on abstraction levels.

5. **Emphasis on Representation-Aware Evaluation:** The study highlights the necessity of using "representation-aware" adversaries in evaluations. By showing that restricted attacks can significantly underestimate actual leakage, the paper underscores that attackers may exploit all available structural and contextual information within an exported representation, not just isolated fields. This stresses the need for robust evaluation methodologies that consider the complete information content of exported data.

**Potential Impact:**
The findings of this paper will significantly influence the development of privacy-aware domestic robots. By moving beyond raw sensor data, it provides a foundation for designing more secure robot architectures where information flow is meticulously controlled post-perception. This research could lead to:
- **Improved Robot System Design:** Encouraging robot developers to implement TFPD-like frameworks, ensuring that only necessary and appropriately transformed information is shared with downstream components or cloud services.
- **Standardization of Privacy Evaluation:** Promoting the adoption of multi-risk, representation-aware privacy profiling methods, fostering a more rigorous and comprehensive assessment of robot privacy.
- **Enhanced User Trust:** By demonstrating a commitment to addressing nuanced privacy challenges, robot manufacturers can build greater trust with consumers, accelerating the adoption of domestic robotics.
- **Future Research Directions:** TFPD motivates further research into adaptive, runtime representation selection mechanisms, formal privacy guarantees for structured data, and the application of these principles to a broader range of robot tasks and physical robot deployments.

In conclusion, this paper fundamentally reframes the discourse on robot privacy, establishing that "seeing less is not seeing safely" and advocating for a task-specific, multi-risk approach to safeguarding sensitive household information from derived robot perceptions.
