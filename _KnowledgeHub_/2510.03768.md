# Model-Based Adaptive Precision Control for Tabletop Planar Pushing Under Uncertain Dynamics

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Koç University developed a model-based adaptive precision control framework for tabletop planar pushing, utilizing a single learned dynamics model to achieve diverse manipulation tasks. The framework demonstrated 100% success rates for precise posing within a 5 mm position and 2° orientation threshold in real-world experiments, effectively transferring from simulation and significantly reducing the number of control steps compared to baseline methods.

## Problem

- Traditional analytical planar pushing models require precise object and environment parameters, limiting their generalization and practical application.
- Data-driven model-free methods typically demand extensive, task-specific training, making them inefficient for scenarios involving multiple manipulation objectives.
- Previous model-based data-driven approaches for planar pushing often had limitations such as coarse goal thresholds, fixed push lengths, or insufficient real-world validation.

## Method

- A Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU)-based dynamics model was developed to implicitly capture object-environment dynamics, trained entirely in PyBullet simulation with extensive domain randomization.
- A modified ==Model Predictive Path Integral== (MPPI) controller was integrated with the learned dynamics model, enabling adaptive, variable-length pushes and side-switching capabilities.
- The framework supports diverse manipulation tasks (precise posing, trajectory following, obstacle avoidance) by simply modifying the controller's objective function, without retraining the core dynamics model.

## Results

- The learned dynamics model consistently outperformed baselines in one-step prediction accuracy, achieving average position errors of **0.3** mm and orientation errors of **0.003** radians for small pushes in simulation.
- The improved control framework achieved **100%** success rates for precise posing with a 3 mm position and 2° orientation threshold in simulation, and **100%** for a 5 mm position and 2° orientation threshold in real-world trials on a Franka Emika Panda robot.
- Real-world experiments demonstrated successful execution of precise posing, trajectory following (average errors 5-7 mm), and obstacle avoidance tasks, validating the sim-to-real transfer and multi-task capabilities of the framework.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A tailored state-action representation and recurrent architecture allow the dynamics model to generalize effectively across uncertain dynamics, variable push lengths, and diverse tasks, supporting robust sim-to-real transfer.
> - Separating the training of translational and rotational motion prediction models simplifies optimization and improves overall prediction accuracy without requiring a tuned loss weighting.
> - The 'improved' MPPI controller, featuring increased sample counts, balanced sampling across object sides, perturbed directional rollouts, and greedy action selection, substantially enhances control efficiency and precision.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robotic object manipulation is a fundamental challenge in robotics, with planar pushing being a critical form of non-prehensile manipulation that allows robots to reposition objects without grasping. The approaches to this problem generally fall into three categories: analytical methods, data-driven model-based methods, and data-driven model-free methods.

**Traditional analytical methods** model push dynamics using explicit physical parameters of objects (e.g., mass, center of mass, shape) and environmental factors (e.g., friction). These methods, exemplified by foundational work in quasi-static pushing, offer interpretability and task-specific optimality under idealized conditions. However, their practical application is constrained by the need for accurate parameter measurement, which is often difficult in real-world scenarios. Small deviations in these parameters can lead to performance degradation, and they typically lack generalization across varying objects or environments, often requiring significant engineering effort or re-tuning.

**Data-driven approaches** emerged to address the limitations of analytical methods by learning from experience. These methods leverage robot-object interactions to collect data, which is then used to infer dynamics or directly learn policies. Within data-driven methods, two main subcategories exist:

- **Model-based data-driven methods:** These approaches learn a forward dynamics model that predicts object motion given a current state and action. Typically, a neural network or probabilistic regressor is trained on interaction data. This learned model is then coupled with a controller or planner to compute action sequences that achieve a desired goal. A key advantage of this modularity is that a well-learned dynamics model can be reused across multiple tasks, allowing for flexibility in controller design. However, these learned models can still have limitations in terms of accuracy, stability, or generalization if the training data is not sufficiently diverse or the architecture lacks expressive power. Previous works in this area have explored Gaussian processes, LSTM networks, and hybrid models combining deep learning with analytical physics. Some methods have addressed side-switching or novel object generalization, but often with limitations such as coarse goal thresholds, fixed push lengths, or lack of evaluation in real-world settings.
- **Model-free data-driven methods:** These methods directly learn a pushing policy that maps sensory inputs to actions without explicitly modeling the underlying dynamics. Often based on reinforcement learning (RL), they learn through trial and error to generate actions that guide an object to a desired configuration. While offering fast action inference once trained, model-free methods typically require extensive training data and often necessitate separate training for each specific task, limiting their reusability and scalability. Recent RL-based methods have addressed complex pushing scenarios, but still face challenges related to training efficiency and task-specific policy learning.

This paper positions itself within the **model-based data-driven approach**, aiming to combine the benefits of learned dynamics (handling uncertainty, reducing engineering effort) with the modularity and reusability that model-free methods often lack. It addresses previous limitations by developing a framework that supports multiple tasks without retraining the core dynamics model, allows for variable push lengths and side-switching, and demonstrates robust performance in both simulation and real-world environments with stringent precision requirements. The work contributes to improving the generalization, precision, and multi-task capability of non-prehensile manipulation systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the limitations observed in existing planar pushing methods. Traditional analytical models, while interpretable, demand precise object and environment parameters that are often unavailable or difficult to measure, limiting their generalization and requiring substantial engineering. Model-free data-driven approaches, while capable of learning complex policies, typically require extensive, task-specific training, making them inefficient for scenarios involving multiple manipulation objectives.

Against this background, the authors articulate the following key objectives:

- **Develop a unified model-based framework:** The central objective is to create a robust model-based framework for non-prehensile tabletop pushing that utilizes a single learned dynamics model. This model is designed to be versatile, addressing multiple manipulation tasks without requiring retraining for each new objective. This directly addresses the "training economy" motivation.
- **Learn comprehensive object-environment dynamics:** The framework aims to implicitly capture potentially non-stationary object and environmental dynamics. This involves designing a recurrent GRU-based architecture with additional non-linear layers to ensure stability and accuracy in predicting object motion.
- **Achieve broad generalization through tailored representation:** A key objective is to enable the learned model to generalize effectively across various conditions. This is achieved through a specific state-action representation that includes the previous change of object pose, the current relative position of the robot to the object (contact position), and the object's relative push vector (magnitude and direction). This representation is intended to support generalization across uncertain dynamics, variable push lengths, and diverse tasks, including side-switching.
- **Integrate learned dynamics with an adaptive controller:** The framework seeks to couple the learned dynamics model with a sampling-based Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) controller. This integration is intended to generate adaptive, task-oriented actions that are responsive to the current state and goal, and to support functionalities such as side switching and variable-length pushes.
- **Enable diverse manipulation capabilities:** A significant objective is to demonstrate that the framework can support a range of complex manipulation tasks. This includes precise positioning of objects, following predefined trajectories, and avoiding obstacles in cluttered environments, all achievable by modifying only the controller's objective function, not the underlying dynamics model.
- **Facilitate sim-to-real transfer:** To ensure practical applicability, the methodology includes training the dynamics model entirely in simulation, incorporating extensive domain randomization to support robust transfer of the learned model to real-world robotic systems without requiring additional fine-tuning.
- **Empirically validate architecture and system performance:** The research aims to conduct detailed evaluations, starting with ablation studies to confirm the architectural choices contribute to improved prediction accuracy and stable rollouts. Subsequently, the full system's performance is validated through comprehensive simulation and real-world experiments, demonstrating high success rates in various tasks under strict performance thresholds.
- **Extend capabilities for longer-horizon goals:** A further objective is to enhance the framework to handle wider push lengths and design a more balanced controller that reduces the number of steps required for achieving longer-horizon manipulation goals.

In essence, the core motivation is to overcome the inflexibility of analytical models and the training overhead of model-free methods by creating a data-efficient, modular, and generalizable model-based learning and control framework for planar pushing.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology presented involves two main components: a learned push dynamics model and a sampling-based Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) controller, integrated into a unified framework.

#### 3.1. Push Dynamics Model

The push dynamics model is designed to predict the change in object pose given robot-object configuration and robot action, without requiring explicit physical parameters.

##### 3.1.1. Architecture

The model utilizes a Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU)-based architecture. It processes a history of previous state-action pairs, along with the most recent pair, to infer implicit dynamics. The input at time step $t$ is a history $H_t = \{(\Delta O_k, RO_k, \Delta RO_k)\}_{k=t-w+1}^t$, where $w$ is the window length.
- $\Delta O_k$: Object motion in the previous time step (change in 2D translation and orientation).
- $RO_k$: Relative position of the pusher with respect to the object at the start of the push (contact position).
- $\Delta RO_k$: Relative pusher motion during the push (direction and magnitude of the push).
The architecture separates the current state-action pair inputs ($RO_t, \Delta RO_t$) and the latest object motion ($\Delta O_t$) which are fed to a fully-connected (FC) layer, from the historical components (from $t-w+1$ to $t-1$) which are fed to a GRU layer. The outputs of these layers are concatenated and passed through two additional FC layers before the final output layer, which predicts $\Delta \hat{O}_{t+1}$ (predicted object motion for the next time-step). All FC layers employ ReLU activations. This design emphasizes recent interactions while encoding long-term dynamics through the GRU.

##### 3.1.2. Data Collection

Training data is collected in a PyBullet simulated environment over 10,000 episodes, each consisting of 100 pushes on a planar cubic object.
- **Object and Push Diversity:** The cubic object is incrementally rotated by one degree across episodes to ensure diverse initial poses. Push magnitudes range from 2 mm to 3 cm (later extended to 3 mm to 5 cm for the improved version), and directions are sampled uniformly within a symmetric $\pi/4$ cone oriented towards the object at the contact point. Pushes originate from points on the perimeter of a square surrounding the object, with a 1 cm margin.
- **Domain Randomization:** To facilitate sim-to-real transfer and enhance generalization, key environmental and object parameters are randomized at the beginning of each episode. These parameters include object mass (0.3-0.7 kg), dimensions (length 0.11-0.13 m, width 0.09-0.11 m), friction coefficient (0.5-0.7), and restitution of the supporting surface (0.4-0.6).

##### 3.1.3. Training

Two separate models are trained: one for predicting 2D translational motion and another for predicting rotational motion. This separation avoids the need for tuning relative weights between position and orientation losses.
- **Loss Functions:** Mean squared error (MSE) for translation prediction; mean non-oriented angular difference for orientation prediction.
- **Optimization:** Adam optimizer with a learning rate scheduler, trained for 40 epochs. The model with the best validation performance is selected.

##### 3.1.4. Model Evaluation

The model's one-step prediction performance is evaluated on a test set. Baselines include a 2-layer LSTM model from prior work (Cong et al., 2020) and a model that does not incorporate historical state-action information. The test set is divided into "small" pushes (2-8 mm) and "big" pushes (8 mm - 3 cm) to assess performance across different magnitudes.

#### 3.2. Control

The control strategy employs a modified Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) approach, a sampling-based method for action generation.

##### 3.2.1. Basic Method

- **Goal-Aware Sampling:** In each control step, $N$ actions ($a_i = [RO_i, \Delta RO_i]$) are sampled. For each sample, a random location on the object's periphery ($RO_t$) is selected, followed by a random push direction within a $\pi/4$ cone. Push length is chosen based on the current goal, enabling variable-length pushes.
- **Rollout Generation:** For each sampled action, the learned dynamics model ($f(\cdot)$) is used autoregressively to generate an $T$-step rollout ($\tau_i$).
- **Objective Calculation:** An objective function $c(\tau_i)$ (e.g., distance to target) is used to calculate costs ($c_i$) for each rollout.
- **Action Selection:** Action weights ($\omega_i$) are computed using a softmax-like function based on the costs (Eq. 3), and the next action ($a_t$) is a weighted combination of the sampled actions (Eq. 4). If the resulting $RO_t$ is in collision, sampling restarts (up to 20 times).
- **Two-Stage Control:** To balance speed and precision, a two-stage approach is used. Initially, longer pushes (8 mm - 3 cm) are sampled to rapidly move the object towards the target. Once the object is within 1 cm of the target, shorter pushes (1 mm - 8 mm) are sampled for fine adjustments.

##### 3.2.2. Improved Method

This version introduces several modifications for enhanced performance and robustness:
- **Increased Sample Count:** $N$ is increased for denser exploration.
- **Separated Sampling:** $RO_t$ values are sampled independently and balanced across all four sides of the cubic object. $\Delta RO_t$ push magnitudes are sampled from a wider range (3 mm - 5 cm).
- **Directional Variation in Rollouts:** Instead of repeating the exact same action, while push magnitude remains constant, the direction of subsequent actions within a rollout is perturbed by $\pm 5^\circ$ relative to the previous direction. The initial action's direction in a rollout is sampled from either a narrow ([-5°, 5°]) or wide ([-45°, 45°]) interval.
- **Greedy Action Selection:** Instead of a weighted average, the action corresponding to the minimum rollout cost is directly chosen ($a_t = a_{i^*}$ where $i^* = \arg \min_i c_i$). This aims to prevent dilution and accidental collision.

#### 3.3. Tasks and Objective Functions

The framework is evaluated on three main tasks, each with specific objective functions:
- **Precise Posing:** The objective function (Eq. 6) minimizes the pose difference ($D_{pose}$) between the predicted object pose at the end of the rollout and the target pose. An alternative objective (Eq. 7) includes a penalty for robot travel to reduce frequent side-switching. $D_{pose}$ combines Euclidean distance for position and non-oriented angular difference for orientation (Eq. 5).
- **Trajectory Following:** For dense waypoint trajectories, the objective (Eq. 8) minimizes the pose difference between the predicted object pose after the first step of the rollout and the next waypoint.
- **Obstacle Avoidance:** This task augments the precise posing objective with an exponential penalty term (Eq. 9) that penalizes close proximity (less than 1 cm) of the object or the pusher to any obstacles.

#### 3.4. Real-World Setup

Real-world experiments utilize a Franka Emika Panda robot with a stick and elastic spherical ball pusher. A Kinect 360 RGBD camera performs markerless object tracking. The robot's velocity is limited to 10 cm/s for quasi-static conditions. After each push, the robot briefly retracts to allow the perception system to reacquire the object's pose.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of the proposed framework was conducted through a series of experiments in both simulation and real-world environments, covering model performance, precise manipulation, trajectory following, and obstacle avoidance.

#### 4.1. Model Evaluation

The dynamics model's one-step prediction accuracy was evaluated against two baselines.
- The proposed GRU-based model consistently **outperformed both the no-history baseline and the 2-layer LSTM model** (Cong et al., 2020).
- For "small" pushes (2-8 mm), the model achieved an average position error of 0.3 mm and an average orientation error of 0.003 radians. For "big" pushes (8 mm - 3 cm), errors were 0.8 mm and 0.007 radians, respectively.
- The LSTM baseline exhibited significantly higher errors, particularly for position prediction (e.g., 6.2 mm for "big" pushes), indicating potential overfitting or an inability to generalize with randomized data.
- The prediction time per step was 2 ms.

#### 4.2. Simulation Experiments

##### 4.2.1. Precise Manipulation

Experiments were categorized by target distance (5-15 cm, 15-20 cm, and later 10-20 cm, 20-30 cm) and initial-to-target orientation difference (0-30°, 30-60°, 60-90°).
- **Basic Method:** Demonstrated that fewer than 20 steps were typically required for the object to reach within 1 cm of the target pose. The number of steps increased with stricter thresholds, greater target distances, and larger orientation differences. Incorporating a robot motion penalty (Eq. 7) increased the required steps but successfully reduced robot travel, indicating a feasible trade-off between task duration and robot movement efficiency. The average episode completion time was 5.96 seconds.
- **Improved Controller:** The enhanced controller significantly improved efficiency, **reducing the number of steps required to reach success thresholds by approximately half**. It also demonstrated a substantial reduction in robot travel distance. For combined 3 mm position and 2° orientation thresholds, the improved method achieved a 100% task completion rate.
- **Comparison with Model-Free Baseline:** Against a reinforcement learning model-free baseline (Ferrandis et al., 2023), our method achieved 100% success rates for both 3 mm/2° and 7.5 mm/5° thresholds, whereas the model-free baseline achieved 30.3% and 88.6% respectively. This highlighted the model-based approach's flexibility and potential for high precision.
- Average one-step prediction error during control for the basic method was 1 $\pm$ 0.84 mm for distance and 0.0075 $\pm$ 0.0082 radians for orientation.

##### 4.2.2. Trajectory Following

Evaluations on circular and "L"-shaped trajectories with dense waypoints.
- Average position errors ranged from 3-4 mm for circular trajectories and 2-3 mm for "L"-shaped trajectories. These errors varied with spatial separation between consecutive waypoints.

##### 4.2.3. Obstacle Avoidance

This task combined precise posing with an obstacle avoidance objective.
- The method successfully handled configurations with single objects or two objects with relatively wide separation, maintaining a minimum clearance.
- Success rates decreased with decreasing separation between obstacles and with an increasing number of obstacles.
- The approach struggled in scenarios requiring significant object rotation (above 60°) to pass through narrow passages, primarily due to the local nature of the MPC framework's planning horizon.

#### 4.3. Real-World Experiments

Real-world tests were performed on a Franka Emika Panda robot with markerless tracking on three different cubic objects.

##### 4.3.1. Precise Manipulation

- Results mirrored simulation findings: the number of required steps increased with object size, target distance, and orientation difference.
- High success rates were observed: for a 1 cm position threshold, success rates were 87.5% (long distance) and 86% (short distance) for a 5° orientation threshold, improving to 98% and 97% for a 10° orientation threshold. For a stricter 5 mm position and 2° orientation threshold, a 100% success rate was achieved over 10 trials.
- The average one-step prediction error in the real world was 3.8-4.8 mm for position and 2.5° for orientation, higher than in simulation due to perception noise and real-world complexities.

##### 4.3.2. Trajectory Following

- Average displacement errors for real-world trajectory following ranged from 5-7 mm for L-shaped paths and 6-7 mm for circular paths, showing a slight increase compared to simulation.

##### 4.3.3. Obstacle Avoidance

- Successful in simpler configurations (e.g., single obstacle, widely separated two obstacles), demonstrating avoidance while maintaining target progress.
- Similar to simulation, complex scenarios that demanded large rotations or intricate maneuvers presented challenges, exacerbated by perception noise.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a model-based framework for planar pushing that offers several significant contributions and potential impacts on robotic manipulation:

- **Enhanced Generalization and Modularity:** The core significance lies in demonstrating that a single, learned dynamics model, trained with extensive domain randomization in simulation, can generalize effectively to multiple real-world tasks (precise positioning, trajectory following, obstacle avoidance) and varying object sizes without requiring retraining. This modularity, where only the controller's objective function needs modification for new tasks, is a key advantage over task-specific model-free learning approaches, significantly reducing engineering and training effort.
- **Robustness to Uncertain Dynamics:** By relying on a tailored state-action representation and recurrent neural networks to implicitly capture object-environment dynamics, the framework reduces the need for explicit physical parameter knowledge. This makes the system more robust to real-world uncertainties such as unknown friction coefficients or object masses, enhancing its practical deployability in less-structured environments.
- **High Precision and Efficiency in Non-Prehensile Manipulation:** The framework achieves high precision, demonstrating sub-centimeter and sub-degree accuracy in precise positioning tasks. The improved controller further optimizes this by reducing the number of steps required for long-horizon goals, balancing speed with accuracy. Capabilities like adaptive side-switching and variable-length pushes contribute to finer control and optimality, moving beyond the limitations of fixed-action strategies.
- **Successful Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The demonstration of effective sim-to-real transfer without any real-world fine-tuning is a critical step towards practical robotic systems. The use of domain randomization in simulation proved sufficient to bridge the reality gap, which can accelerate development and deployment cycles for robotic manipulation applications.
- **Multi-Task Capability within a Unified Framework:** The ability to perform diverse tasks—from static precise posing to dynamic trajectory following and reactive obstacle avoidance—using the same underlying learned dynamics model showcases the versatility of the approach. This moves robotic manipulation closer to systems that can adapt to a wider range of requirements without extensive re-engineering.
- **Foundation for Future Advanced Manipulation:** This work establishes a robust foundation for future research in several directions. It opens avenues for exploring more sophisticated sampling strategies (e.g., learning sampling policies, inverse dynamics models) to further improve computational efficiency and handle more complex planning challenges. Integration with global planners could address the current local optima limitations in obstacle avoidance. Furthermore, extending the model to incorporate vision-based perceptual representations could broaden its applicability to objects of arbitrary shapes and unknown geometries.

In summary, this research advances the state of model-based robot learning for non-prehensile manipulation by providing a flexible, robust, and efficient framework that bridges the gap between simulated learning and real-world application, while offering significant improvements in precision, generalization, and multi-task performance.
