# ARMOR: Egocentric Perception for Humanoid Robot Collision Avoidance and Motion Planning

> [!summary] Summary
> ARMOR introduces an egocentric perception system for humanoid robots, utilizing distributed low-cost ToF lidar sensors on robot arms, combined with a transformer-based imitation learning policy. This system achieves real-time collision avoidance, reducing collisions by 63.7% and increasing task success rates by 78.7% compared to exocentric perception, enabling more agile and safer robot operation.

## Problem

- Humanoid robots struggle with collision avoidance, particularly for their arms and hands, due to significant self-occlusions from centralized head or torso-mounted cameras.
- Existing perception systems often provide inadequate spatial awareness, hindering safe and effective operation in cluttered, human-centric environments.
- Traditional motion planning methods are typically computationally expensive, making real-time dynamic collision avoidance challenging for high-degrees-of-freedom robots.

## Method

- A novel egocentric perception system incorporates 40 low-cost Time-of-Flight (ToF) lidar sensors strategically distributed on the robot's arms to provide comprehensive, occlusion-minimized spatial awareness.
- **ARMOR**-Policy, a transformer encoder-decoder based imitation learning policy, is trained on a vast dataset of human-realistic motions from the AMASS dataset to learn dynamic collision avoidance.
- An inference-time optimization strategy batches multiple candidate trajectories using a latent variable and selects the safest path by minimizing robot-to-point cloud distance via ==Signed Distance Function (SDF)==.

## Results

- ARMOR's egocentric perception reduced collisions by **63.7%** and increased success rates by **78.7%** compared to exocentric camera setups in simulated environments.
- The ARMOR-Policy achieved a 26-fold speedup in inference time (50 ms vs. 1300 ms) and a **31.6%** reduction in collisions compared to the cuRobo sampling-based baseline.
- The system was successfully deployed on a Fourier GR1 humanoid robot, demonstrating real-time collision avoidance with 15 Hz trajectory updates in a physical environment.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Distributed, egocentric depth sensing on robot limbs provides superior spatial awareness by minimizing self-occlusions, outperforming centralized camera systems in cluttered environments.
> - Transformer-based imitation learning policies can achieve effective, real-time collision avoidance and high task success rates, significantly improving computational efficiency over traditional sampling-based methods.
> - Leveraging large human motion datasets for expert trajectory generation enables robust and generalizable collision-free robot behaviors, leading to more natural and safe manipulation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The ARMOR project addresses critical gaps in the perception and motion planning capabilities of humanoid robots, a field experiencing renewed interest due to advancements in AI, particularly transformer architectures and large language models. The broader research landscape for humanoid robots is characterized by several key challenges:

- **High Degrees of Freedom (DoF):** Humanoids possess many joints, making control and motion planning computationally intensive and complex.
- **Collision Avoidance and Safety:** Operating in unstructured, human-centric environments requires robust collision avoidance to ensure safety for both the robot and its surroundings.
- **Sensing and Perception Limitations:** Unlike humans with highly developed tactile and proprioceptive senses, robots often lack comprehensive spatial awareness, especially around their own bodies (arms, hands).

**Current Paradigms and Their Limitations:**
- **Centralized Head/Torso Cameras/Lidar:** Many existing humanoid systems rely on high-resolution cameras or lidar mounted on the head or torso. While convenient for integration and offering a wide field of view, these setups suffer from significant self-occlusion, particularly for the robot's arms and hands during manipulation tasks. This "blind spot" is a major hindrance for agile movement in dense environments.
- **External Cameras/Third-Person Perception:** Relying on external, fixed cameras (common in stationary manipulators) is impractical for mobile humanoid robots, which need to operate autonomously in dynamic settings.
- **Tactile Sensing:** While promising for close-range interaction, tactile sensors are expensive, challenging to integrate in large quantities across a robot's body, and their input is not yet fully leveraged in policy learning.

**ARMOR's Position and Novelty:**
ARMOR directly tackles these limitations by proposing a **novel egocentric and distributed perception system**. Instead of centralized high-resolution sensors, it leverages a network of small, low-cost, low-power Time-of-Flight (ToF) lidar sensors distributed strategically across the robot's body, particularly the arms and hands. This "wearable-like" approach provides several advantages:
- **Reduced Occlusions:** By distributing sensors directly on the body, ARMOR significantly minimizes self-occlusions, providing a more comprehensive "skin-like" awareness of immediate surroundings.
- **Scalability and Cost-Effectiveness:** Using numerous low-cost ToF sensors makes the system more scalable and affordable compared to dense tactile skins or multiple high-resolution depth cameras.
- **Egocentric Perception for Mobile Robots:** It embraces the necessity of egocentric perception for mobile humanoids, where external sensors are not feasible.
- **Data-Driven Motion Planning:** ARMOR pairs this novel perception hardware with a transformer-based imitation learning policy (ARMOR-Policy). This approach aligns with the growing trend of leveraging large datasets and deep learning for robust robot control, moving beyond purely sampling-based or optimization-based methods that can be computationally expensive and struggle with generalization. The use of the AMASS human motion dataset for expert trajectory generation is a key differentiator, aiming for more generalizable and "human-realistic" manipulation rather than task-specific actions.

In the context of collision-free motion planning, ARMOR distinguishes itself from:
- **Traditional Sample/Optimization-based Methods (e.g., cuRobo):** These methods are often computationally intensive and can struggle with sparse or incomplete perception data. ARMOR-Policy offers a significantly faster and more efficient alternative.
- **Reinforcement Learning (RL) approaches (e.g., ATACOM, SAFER):** While RL can learn complex behaviors, ARMOR uses imitation learning (IL), which can be more stable and data-efficient for acquiring expert behaviors, especially when good demonstrations are available.
- **Other Imitation Learning methods (e.g., MπNets, MPNet):** While these also use IL, ARMOR specifically focuses on humanoid arms, leverages human motion data, and is designed for an egocentric, distributed sensor setup, which is distinct from systems relying on external cameras or task-specific synthetic data.

By integrating a hardware solution with a sophisticated learning-based software policy, ARMOR pushes the boundaries of perception and control for humanoid robots, enabling more agile, safer, and efficient operation in complex environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind ARMOR stems from the recognized inadequacies in current sensing and perception solutions for humanoid robots, particularly concerning collision avoidance during manipulation tasks in dense environments. The authors argue that existing approaches leave "significant gaps" in a robot's spatial awareness, especially around its limbs.

The key objectives of the ARMOR project are:

1. **Develop a novel egocentric perception system for humanoid robots:** This involves both hardware and software, focusing on "wearable-like" depth sensors. The goal is to provide comprehensive spatial awareness by distributing low-profile, low-cost sensors across the robot's body (specifically arms and hands).
2. **Minimize occlusions and enhance spatial awareness:** Address the inherent occlusion issues faced by centralized head-mounted or external cameras, ensuring that the robot has a clear, distributed view of its immediate surroundings, particularly its own body parts and potential obstacles.
3. **Facilitate more agile and effective motion planning:** The improved perception is intended to enable robots to plan and execute motions more effectively, reducing collisions and improving task success rates, especially in cluttered or confined spaces.
4. **Train a data-driven, computationally efficient motion planning policy:** Develop a transformer-based imitation learning (IL) policy (ARMOR-Policy) capable of dynamic collision avoidance. This policy should leverage the novel egocentric perception data and be significantly faster than traditional sampling-based planners.
5. **Leverage human-realistic motions for expert data generation:** Utilize a large dataset of human motions (AMASS) to generate "expert" collision-free trajectories, aiming for generalizable and natural-looking robot movements.
6. **Validate the proposed system in both simulation and real-world environments:** Demonstrate the superiority of ARMOR's perception and policy against existing methods through rigorous experimental evaluations, culminating in real-world deployment on a Fourier GR1 humanoid robot.

In essence, ARMOR aims to equip humanoid robots with a "sense of touch-like" perception that is both comprehensive and computationally manageable, thereby enabling them to perform complex, collision-free manipulation with human-like dexterity and safety.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

ARMOR presents a holistic system comprising both novel egocentric perception hardware and a sophisticated learning-based motion planning policy.

#### 3.1. A. Egocentric Perception Hardware

- **Sensor Choice:** The system uses SparkFun VL53L5CX Time-of-Flight (ToF) lidar sensors. These sensors were chosen for their compact size (6.4 × 3.0 × 1.5 mm), low cost, low power consumption, and scalability. Each sensor provides an 8x8 depth image at 15 Hz (up to 30 Hz) with a 63° diagonal field-of-view and a 4000 mm range. While their resolution is coarse, their distributed nature compensates for this.
- **Distributed Placement:** Forty ToF sensors are strategically placed on the Fourier GR1 humanoid robot's arms (20 on each arm). This "skin-like" distribution is crucial for minimizing occlusions that are prevalent with centralized camera systems, providing a "nearly occlusion-free view" of the surrounding environment, especially close to the robot's manipulators.
- **Data Acquisition Pipeline:** Groups of four sensors are connected to XIAO ESP microcontrollers via the I2C bus. These microcontrollers stream data over USB to the robot's onboard computer (Jetson Xavier NX). Finally, the sensor data is wirelessly streamed over a socket to a Linux machine with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPU for processing, ensuring a consistent 15 Hz update rate.

#### 3.2. B. ARMOR-Policy (Neural Motion Planner)

- **Architecture:** The ARMOR-Policy is based on a transformer encoder-decoder architecture, similar to Action Chunking Transformers (ACT). This design allows for sequence modeling, enabling the policy to predict a sequence of future actions ($a_{t+k}$) conditioned on the current robot state ($q_t$), goal joint positions ($g$), observations ($o_t$) from the ToF lidars, and a latent variable ($z$).
- **Behavior Encoder and Latent Variable:** To account for the multi-modal nature of collision-free planning (i.e., multiple valid paths), an additional encoder layer (Behavior Encoder) infers a latent variable `z`. This `z` represents different "styles" or types of motion and is used during inference to sample diverse trajectories, a key aspect for the inference-time optimization.
- **Input and Output:**
 - **Inputs:** The policy takes the latent variable `z`, a 28-dimensional vector representing current and goal arm joint positions (14 DoF per arm), and 40 grayscale 8x8 depth images from the ToF sensors (each in its ego-frame).
 - **Feature Extraction:** Each 8x8 depth image passes through a modified mono-channel ResNet18 backbone to extract 512 features.
 - **Output:** The transformer policy outputs a sequence of `k` action sequences, represented as a `k x 14` vector.
- **Parameters:** The entire architecture comprises approximately 84 million parameters.

#### 3.3. C. Inference Time Optimization

- **Batch Trajectory Computation:** Leveraging the latent variable `z`, the ARMOR-Policy can batch compute multiple (`N`) candidate trajectories by sampling from random posterior distributions of `z`. This parallel computation is performed on the GPU, adding negligible latency.
- **Optimal Path Selection:** From these `N` candidates, the optimal path is selected by minimizing the robot-to-point cloud (PCL) distance using a Signed Distance Function (SDF). The optimization objective is $\min \sum_{t=1}^{T} \sum_{k=1}^{K} \{1/SDF_{q_t}(PCL_k)\}$, where `T` is the action horizon and `K` is the number of points in the point cloud. This step enhances safety by explicitly evaluating potential collisions.

#### 3.4. D. Motion Planning Expert Data Generation

- **Imitation Learning Pipeline:** The core pipeline relies on imitation learning from "expert" trajectories.
- **AMASS Dataset:** The expert data consists of 311,922 synthetic motion trajectories (86.6 hours worth) generated from the Archive of Motion Capture as Surface Shapes (AMASS) dataset. AMASS provides diverse human poses (manipulation, dance, social actions), which are re-targeted to the Fourier GR1 humanoid's specific joint configurations.
- **Retargeting Human Poses:** Human arm-related joint angles (collar, shoulder, elbow, wrist, fingers) from AMASS are mapped to the GR1's motors. Heuristics are used to combine collar and shoulder angles for the GR1's shoulder motors.
- **Data Generation Strategies (Figure 5):**
 1. **Collision-Avoidance Motions:** For a 1-second human motion sequence, random obstacles are generated tightly around the trajectory, ensuring no actual collision with the path. This creates positive examples of collision-free movement in cluttered spaces.
 2. **Emergency-Stop Motions:** The goal pose is deliberately set inside an obstacle, forcing the arm to collide. This teaches the policy to react to imminent collisions and potentially stop.
 3. **Collision-Free Motions:** Similar to collision-avoidance, but all obstacles are removed, and the expert trajectory is linearly interpolated from initial to goal pose. This provides examples of basic, unimpeded motion.

By combining a novel sensing paradigm with a robust, data-driven learning approach, ARMOR aims to provide a comprehensive solution for dynamic collision avoidance in humanoid robots.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments comprehensively evaluate ARMOR's hardware (egocentric perception) and software (ARMOR-Policy) contributions using three key metrics: number of robot-to-obstacle contacts (collisions), number of successful sequences (reaching the goal pose), and inference computation time. The evaluations are conducted in simulation and a real-world deployment on a Fourier GR1 humanoid.

#### 4.1. A. Egocentric ARMOR vs. Exocentric Perception

This comparison highlights the advantages of ARMOR's distributed ToF sensor setup over traditional exocentric (four Intel RealSense D435 cameras mounted on the head, left, right, and front) or head-mounted dense depth cameras.

- **With cuRobo (Sampling-based planner):** ARMOR perception led to a **38.7% reduction in collisions** and an **11.9% increase in success rate**. While cuRobo is a sampling-based method, it still benefits significantly from the superior perception provided by ARMOR's egocentric setup.
- **With ACT-Depth (ARMOR-Policy without inference optimization):** ARMOR perception achieved a **55.0% reduction in collisions** and a **76.3% increase in success rate**.
- **With ARMOR-Policy (ACT-Depth with inference optimization):** This combination yielded the best results, with a **63.7% reduction in collisions** and a **78.7% improvement in success rate**.

**Key finding:** ARMOR's egocentric perception system consistently and significantly outperforms exocentric perception across different motion planning policies, particularly in cluttered environments where occlusions are prevalent (illustrated in Figure 7). The distributed, "skin-like" coverage provides crucial local awareness that centralized cameras cannot.

#### 4.2. B. ARMOR ACT Policies vs. Sampling-based Baseline Policy (cuRobo)

This comparison evaluates the efficiency and effectiveness of the learned ARMOR-Policy against a state-of-the-art sampling-based motion planner, cuRobo, both using ARMOR's egocentric perception.

- **Collision and Success:** The ACT-Depth policy (without inference optimization) showed a **31.6% reduction in collisions** and a **16.9% higher success rate** compared to cuRobo. Notably, cuRobo struggled significantly with the challenging dataset, failing to find solutions for 64% of the evaluation data, which required the authors to prune the comparison to only solvable instances for fairness.
- **Computational Efficiency:** A major advantage of the neural-based ACT-Depth policy was its computational speed, operating at **50 ms**, which is approximately **26 times faster** than cuRobo (1300 ms).

**Key finding:** The learned ARMOR ACT policy is not only more effective at collision avoidance and task success than traditional sampling-based methods but also dramatically more computationally efficient. This speed is critical for dynamic collision avoidance in real-time scenarios (illustrated in Figure 8).

#### 4.3. C. Benefits of Inference-time Optimization

This evaluates the impact of the lightweight inference-time optimization (selecting the safest trajectory from multiple sampled candidates) within the ARMOR-Policy.

- **Collision and Success:** ARMOR-Policy (with optimization) showed a **19.4% further reduction in collisions** and a **1.4% increase in success rate** compared to ACT-Depth (without optimization).
- **Computational Cost:** The optimization added a relatively small overhead, increasing computation time from 50 ms (ACT-Depth) to **240 ms** (ARMOR-Policy), still significantly faster than cuRobo.

**Key finding:** The inference-time optimization provides a substantial safety boost (reducing collisions) with an acceptable increase in computational cost, making the ARMOR-Policy a robust choice for safety-critical applications.

#### 4.4. D. Real-World Deployment

- The ARMOR perception system (with 28 ToF lidars) was successfully deployed on a real Fourier GR1 humanoid robot.
- The ARMOR-Policy demonstrated real-time rollout for collision avoidance, updating trajectories at 15 Hz, synchronized with the ToF lidar update frequency.

**Key finding:** The system is not only effective in simulation but also robust and performant enough for real-world application on a physical humanoid robot, validating the practical feasibility of the approach.

In summary, ARMOR achieves a superior blend of effective collision avoidance, high success rates, and real-time computational efficiency by combining its novel egocentric, distributed ToF perception hardware with a transformer-based, imitation learning policy that leverages human motion data and inference-time optimization.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The ARMOR project makes significant contributions that have broad implications for the future of humanoid robotics and mobile manipulation.

### 6. Enhanced Robot Autonomy and Safety in Complex Environments

By addressing the critical perception gaps around robot bodies, ARMOR enables humanoids to navigate and manipulate objects more safely and effectively in cluttered, human-centric environments. The drastic reduction in collisions and improved success rates mean that robots can perform tasks previously considered too risky or complex due such environments. This directly impacts the deployability of humanoids in real-world scenarios, from manufacturing and logistics to assistive robotics.

### 7. Paradigm Shift in Robot Perception

ARMOR challenges the traditional reliance on centralized, high-resolution cameras by demonstrating the efficacy of distributed, low-cost, and low-profile egocentric sensors. This "skin-like" perception paradigm could inspire new hardware designs for future robots, moving towards more inherent spatial awareness similar to biological systems. The use of ToF sensors as "wearable receptors" offers a scalable and cost-effective alternative to expensive, high-resolution depth cameras or complex tactile skins for broad coverage.

### 8. Advancement in Learning-based Motion Planning

The ARMOR-Policy showcases the power of imitation learning with transformer architectures for complex, high-DoF robot tasks. Its superior performance and remarkable computational efficiency (26x faster than cuRobo) highlight the potential for neural-based planners to replace or significantly augment traditional sampling-based methods, especially for dynamic, real-time applications. The method of leveraging the AMASS dataset to generate human-realistic expert trajectories also provides a robust and generalizable approach to training, moving beyond task-specific synthetic data.

### 9. Bridging Hardware and Software Innovation

The project exemplifies successful co-design and integration of novel hardware (distributed ToF sensors) with advanced software (transformer-based policy). This holistic approach is crucial for pushing the boundaries of robotics, as improvements in one domain often unlock potential in the other. The release of source code, hardware descriptions, and 3D CAD files further facilitates replication and future research by the wider robotics community.

### 10. Foundation for Future Humanoid Capabilities

The improved collision avoidance and motion planning capabilities provided by ARMOR lay a critical foundation for more advanced humanoid tasks. In the future, this system could be integrated into higher-level manipulation policies, enabling dexterous object handling, tool use, and complex human-robot interaction where safety and precision are paramount. The ability to "steer attention to specific regions" with the policy, as mentioned in the abstract, suggests adaptability and focus that could translate to highly nuanced interactions.

In conclusion, ARMOR represents a significant step forward in making humanoid robots more capable, safer, and ultimately more practical for widespread deployment. Its impact is likely to be felt across robot design, perception system development, and the methodologies for training intelligent robot control policies.
