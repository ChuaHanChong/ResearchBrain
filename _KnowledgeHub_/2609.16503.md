# Dense to MoE Adaptation for Compact Vision Language Action Policies

> [!summary] Summary
> AdaDE compresses vision-language-action policies by converting selected dense LLM-side feed-forward blocks into mixture-of-experts layers via a function-preserving channel partition (Dense2MoE), then learning layer-wise expert retention masks during fine-tuning from EMA-smoothed router usage, with Wanda-based expert protection and staged warmup/freeze. On π0.5, deactivating 40% of LLM parameters keeps 95.7% average LIBERO success and 42.0% across all 50 RobotWin2.0 tasks versus 46.7% dense.

## Problem

- Dense structured pruning methods like LLM-Pruner and FLAP physically remove layers, heads, or channels, altering the pretrained function and requiring separate recovery fine-tuning to restore performance.
- Most VLA policies use dense transformer backbones that cannot exploit MoE conditional computation, and naive dense-to-MoE replacement disrupts pretrained policy behavior, causing unstable control.
- Existing dense-to-MoE and VLA-MoE work targets scaling or specialization, while MoE compression methods often assume fixed sparse structure, ignoring layer-varying expert usage and compression tolerance.

## Method

- **Dense2MoE** conversion: slices each ==SwiGLU FFN=='s intermediate channels into contiguous expert partitions of gate, up, and down projections; a zero-initialized router plus expert-count rescaling exactly reproduces the dense output.
- **AdaDE** mask update: after 1,000 warmup steps, every 100 steps each layer deactivates experts whose EMA-smoothed router probability falls below threshold, under a per-update removal budget, minimum-active constraint, and no reactivation.
- ==Stability safeguards==: ==Wanda-based protection== locks the top 25% important experts per layer, and a minimum-probability auxiliary routing loss keeps the weakest expert from collapsing before deactivation; frozen masks export a physically pruned checkpoint.

## Results

- On LIBERO with π0.5, Dense2MoE alone matches the dense policy; **40%** LLM deactivation keeps **92.2/97.4/97.6** Goal/Spatial/Object while cutting TFLOPs **4.692** to **3.279** and parameters **3.618B** to **2.841B**.
- On all **50** RobotWin2.0 tasks, **40%** deactivation retains **42.0%** average success versus **46.7%** dense (TFLOPs **4.38** to **3.18**); on seven real-robot tasks it scores **61.4%** versus **63.2%**, cutting memory **9.16** to **7.48 GB**.
- At **50%** LLM deactivation on LIBERO Spatial, AdaDE reaches **95.0%** versus **88.0%** for fixed-schedule removal and **90.0%** without Wanda protection; updating every **50** steps collapses success to **68.0%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Function-preserving reparameterization before sparsification lets compression proceed within ordinary policy fine-tuning, removing the separate recovery stage that abrupt structured pruning otherwise needs.
> - Redundancy in VLAs is uneven: the language backbone tolerates heavy expert removal, while ViT and action-heavy modules degrade quickly, so sparsity budgets should be allocated per layer and module.
> - Parameter and FLOP savings do not imply speed: fragmented small expert GEMMs plus routing overhead made the 50% model slower (236 versus 212 ms) at batch size one.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

Robot policies of the kind studied here take an image and a language instruction and choose actions. Onboard deployment budgets for compute, memory, and power limit how large such a policy can be. AdaDE takes a two-step approach. It first divides selected dense feed-forward network (FFN) layers into smaller expert slices without changing their function at initialization. It then learns, during policy fine-tuning, which slices each layer can do without. The paper reports 95.7% average LIBERO success and 42.0% average success over all 50 RobotWin2.0 tasks with 40% of the LLM parameters deactivated.

### 2. Split without a behavior jump

The compression target is the FFNs of the language model and action expert. These account for a large part of the vision-language-action (VLA) policy. In preliminary experiments, the vision and action-generation components appear more sensitive to compression.

Each FFN is split along contiguous intermediate channels. The gate and up projections are sliced by rows, and the down projection is sliced by columns. A zero-initialized router gives every expert equal probability. With all experts active and the paper's scaling, the experts' combined output reproduces the original FFN at initialization. This makes the conversion function-preserving. It does not mean a pruned model stays identical.

Training then proceeds in stages:

- **Warmup.** All experts stay active.
- **Usage tracking.** AdaDE tracks each expert's raw router usage with an exponential moving average (EMA), a smoothed score that reduces the effect of short-term routing noise.
- **Deactivation.** At intervals, each layer can deactivate persistently low-scoring experts. A minimum-active constraint and a per-update removal limit bound this step.
- **Protection.** Wanda importance protects a subset of experts from deactivation. A weak auxiliary routing loss discourages the least-used raw probabilities from collapsing too early.
- **Export.** The masks are frozen, and inactive slices are physically removed from the exported checkpoint.

This differs from conventional token-level top-k dispatch. The router produces probabilities for all experts, and a layer-level mask determines which experts remain.

### 3. What the comparisons show

Success rate is the reported percentage of successful task evaluations.

**LIBERO with π0.5.** At 40% deactivation, AdaDE scores 92.2% Goal, 97.4% Spatial, and 97.6% Object. Dense π0.5 scores 93.6%, 98.6%, and 98.2%. The 95.7% figure is the unweighted average of AdaDE's three suite success rates; the dense average is 96.8%. Reported computation falls from 4.692 to 3.279 TFLOPs. Total parameters go from 3.618B to 2.841B. LLM parameters go from 2.043B to 1.266B, while ViT and Flow counts stay at 0.268B and 1.307B. "40%" therefore refers to deactivated LLM-side parameters, not 40% of the entire policy.

**RobotWin2.0.** Across all 50 DemoClean tasks, the average is 42.0% at 40% deactivation versus 46.7% for the dense base. Average computation is 3.18 versus 4.38 TFLOPs.

**Fixed versus adaptive removal.** The strongest skeptical comparison holds LLM deactivation at 50% on LIBERO Spatial. Results are reported after 30,000 training steps:

- Fixed removal: 88.0%
- Dynamic removal with Wanda protection: 95.0%
- Dynamic removal without Wanda protection: 90.0%

The fixed-versus-full-AdaDE comparison supports the claim that adaptive removal together with protection helps. It does not isolate routing statistics alone, because both factors change. The with-versus-without-Wanda comparison supports the authors' interpretation that protected experts matter.

Fig. 3(a). Spatial success rate for Fixed and Dynamic pruning at total LLM pruning ratios of 50%, 70%, 80%, and 90%. At 50%, labels are 88.0 and 95.0. At the higher ratios, dynamic labels are 84.2, 86.6, and 75.7, versus fixed 32.2, 22.2, and 23.6.

### 4. Smaller is not faster—yet

AdaDE reduces deployed parameter count, peak GPU memory, and reported FLOPs. The authors report no end-to-end inference-latency improvement yet. At batch size one, their dense baseline takes 212 ms per forward pass, and AdaDE at 50% LLM deactivation takes 236 ms. They attribute this overhead to routing, selection, and aggregation. They also point to smaller projections running against optimized dense matrix kernels.

The established contribution is the combination of a function-preserving conversion with learned, layer-specific removal. The fixed-removal ablation suggests that where experts are retained matters alongside how many are removed.
