# VLAFlow: A Unified Training Framework for Vision-Language-Action Models via Co-training and Future Latent Alignment

> [!summary] Summary
> VLAFlow, a unified framework from Li Auto Inc. and Beijing University of Posts and Telecommunications, systematically compares Vision-Language-Action (VLA) training paradigms under controlled conditions. The research demonstrates that combining language supervision and future latent alignment improves VLA model robustness and transferability across diverse robotic tasks compared to action-only pre-training.

## Problem

- Lack of a unified framework for controlled comparison of VLA pre-training paradigms, making it difficult to isolate the impact of different training objectives.
- Conventional action-only VLA pre-training can suffer from instability and negative transfer when trained on heterogeneous data with significant distribution shifts from downstream tasks.
- The need for VLA models to learn transferable intermediate representations that generalize effectively across various robot embodiments and task semantics, rather than simply scaling data.

## Method

- **VLAFlow** establishes a unified ==flow-matching framework== that controls for model backbone (==Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct==), a standardized ==14D action space==, heterogeneous data sources (==OXEMix corpus==), and consistent downstream evaluation protocols.
- The framework instantiates and compares four training paradigms: action-only (**MindPI**), ==language-supervised co-training== (**MindLPI**), ==future latent feature alignment== (**MindWPI**), and a joint approach combining both (**MindLWPI**).
- It utilizes a ==Diffusion Transformer== as the action expert, trained with a ==flow-matching loss== for continuous action generation, while auxiliary tasks like language description or ==future latent prediction== (via frozen ==V-JEPA 2==) enhance representation learning.

## Results

- MindLWPI achieved the highest overall transfer performance, including **99.1%** average success rate on LIBERO and **74.8%** total on LIBERO-Plus, outperforming action-only pre-training and competing baselines.
- Future latent alignment (MindWPI) demonstrated strong generalization for cross-platform control, achieving **86.7%** success on RT-1 VM and **71.1%** on RT-1 VA tasks in SimplerEnv.
- Ablation studies showed that allowing action loss to backpropagate into the VLM (improving MindLPI from **45.8%** to **72.3%** on LIBERO-Plus) and carefully balancing the latent loss ratio during fine-tuning are crucial for performance gains.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Action-only pre-training on heterogeneous robot data is inherently unstable and can lead to negative transfer, underscoring the limitations of direct action imitation for broad generalization.
> - Language supervision provides high-level action intent, while future latent alignment captures action-induced state changes, and their combination fosters more stable and transferable 'meta-action' representations.
> - Effective downstream adaptation of VLA models, especially with methods like LoRA, requires sufficient adaptation capacity not only for the action expert but also for the underlying vision-language representations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models has seen advancements in robotic manipulation and embodied intelligence. Recent VLA foundation models, such as LingBot-VLA, ABot-M0, RT-1, RT-2, OpenVLA, Octo, π0, and π0.5, leverage large-scale robot data, vision-language models (VLMs), and continuous action generation methods to achieve cross-task generalization. These models typically integrate visual perception, language understanding, and robot control within end-to-end frameworks.

A challenge within this domain is the difficulty in conducting controlled comparisons of different VLA pre-training paradigms. Existing models often vary in architecture, data sources, action spaces, and evaluation protocols, which complicates the isolation and analysis of the specific effects of training objectives. Furthermore, previous studies indicate that simply increasing data scale in VLA pre-training does not consistently yield stable performance gains, particularly with conventional action-only modeling paradigms (e.g., π0). These conventional methods, which directly predict action chunks from visual observations and language instructions, can be susceptible to negative transfer when pre-training data distributions significantly differ from downstream tasks in aspects such as robot embodiment, action space, sampling frequency, or task semantics. This phenomenon suggests that learning transferable intermediate representations from heterogeneous data is a key factor for effective VLA pre-training.

This work, VLAFlow, positions itself to address this gap by providing a unified evaluation framework for VLA training paradigms. It aims to enable a controlled comparison of different pre-training objectives under consistent architectural, data, and evaluation settings. The paper draws on related developments in VLM co-training, exemplified by models like π0.5, LAP, and JoyAI-RA 0.1, which integrate high-level action intent through language. It also incorporates concepts from future latent feature alignment paradigms, such as Being-H0.7, LDA-1B, and V-JEPA 2, which enable feature-level future imagination or state change prediction. By combining and systematically comparing these approaches, VLAFlow seeks to clarify the individual and synergistic contributions of language supervision and future latent prediction to the stability and transferability of VLA models. The reliance on large-scale, heterogeneous datasets like Open X-Embodiment, DROID, RoboCOIN, and RoboMIND underscores the need for robust pre-training objectives capable of handling data diversity. The adoption of flow-matching for continuous action generation, as seen in π0, reflects current trends in efficient and fine-grained robot control.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to investigate how different VLA training paradigms affect downstream transfer performance under controlled conditions. Specifically, the authors pose the question: "under similar data distributions and the same model architecture, how do different VLA training paradigms affect downstream transfer performance?"

This objective is motivated by several observations and challenges in the field of robotic learning:

1. **Lack of Controlled Comparison:** The rapid proliferation of VLA models has led to diverse architectures, data, action spaces, and evaluation protocols. This heterogeneity makes it difficult to isolate the impact of different training objectives, hindering a clear understanding of which paradigms are most effective and why. A unified framework for controlled comparison is needed to systematically analyze these factors.
2. **Instability of Current Pre-training Paradigms:** While large-scale robot data has enabled progress, simply scaling data does not guarantee stable gains. Conventional action-only modeling (e.g., π0) relies heavily on direct action prediction. When pre-training data significantly deviates from downstream tasks (e.g., in robot embodiment, action space, sampling frequency, or task semantics), this paradigm can lead to negative transfer, where pre-training degrades performance instead of improving it.
3. **Need for Transferable Intermediate Representations:** The sensitivity of action-only modeling to data distribution indicates that the key to effective VLA pre-training lies not merely in data scale, but in learning transferable intermediate representations. These representations should be robust to heterogeneity and generalize well across tasks.
4. **Exploring Complementary Supervision Signals:** The paper hypothesizes that auxiliary supervision signals, such as language and future latent features, can provide valuable intermediate constraints to stabilize representation learning from heterogeneous data.
 - **Language Supervision:** Drawing on VLM co-training paradigms, language can provide high-level action intent, helping the model encode task semantics and abstract goals.
 - **Future Latent Alignment:** Based on world model concepts, predicting or aligning future latent representations allows the model to learn action-induced state changes and environment dynamics without the computational burden of pixel-level reconstruction.
5. **Understanding "Meta-Action Space":** The research aims to explore whether these intermediate representations can help form a stable "meta-action" representation that smooths the optimization space between abstract intent and real-world control signals, making heterogeneous action supervision more effective and transferable.

In summary, the motivation is to move beyond mere scaling of data and models by systematically dissecting the role of pre-training objectives in fostering robust, generalizable VLA capabilities across diverse robot environments and tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

VLAFlow is designed as a unified flow-matching framework for controlled comparison of VLA training paradigms, rather than a single model variant. The methodology is built upon four control principles to minimize confounding factors:

1. **Same Model Backbone:** All four paradigms utilize the same vision-language model (VLM) as a multimodal context encoder, specifically Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct. This VLM is followed by a π0-style continuous action expert.
2. **Same Action Space:** All pre-training data are mapped to a standardized 14-dimensional action space. This space consists of 7 dimensions per arm (end-effector translation increments, rotation increments, gripper state), with single-arm data incorporated via zero-padding and an action-validity mask.
3. **Same Data Sources and Training Budget:** A medium-scale, heterogeneous robot pre-training corpus named OXEMix (approximately 5,000 hours) is used across all paradigms. OXEMix comprises data from DROID, OpenX-Embodiment, OpenX-Augmented, and RoboCOIN. The same data sampling strategy, number of training steps, optimizer, and learning-rate configuration are applied, except for data fields specific to auxiliary supervision.
4. **Same Downstream Evaluation Protocol:** All pre-trained checkpoints are fine-tuned and evaluated on a consistent set of downstream benchmarks: LIBERO, LIBERO-Plus, and SimplerEnv. Baselines without robot-data pre-training are also established to quantify positive or negative transfer.

**Shared VLAFlow Architecture:**
The core architecture consists of two parts: a VLM backbone and an action expert.
- **VLM Context Encoding:** The Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct VLM processes multi-view images and language instructions, generating multi-layer multimodal representations. The action expert reuses these VLM representations.
- **Continuous Action Expert:** This is a Diffusion Transformer (DiT) decoder, which predicts the flow-matching velocity field for a future action chunk (default length T=16). It uses layer-wise key-value cache sharing to access multimodal context from the VLM.
- **Flow-Matching Action Modeling:** The action expert optimizes a flow-matching loss (`L_act`) to predict the velocity field `v_θ(x_t, t, c)` (where `x_t` is the noised action, `t` is continuous time, `c` is VLM context) targeting `a - ϵ` (ground-truth action minus noise). Inference involves generating action chunks from Gaussian noise via Euler integration. Auxiliary training tasks do not affect control frequency during inference.

**Four Training Paradigms Instantiated:**

1. **MindPI (Action-Only Pre-training):**
 - **Objective:** `L_MindPI = L_act`.
 - **Role:** Serves as a baseline, relying solely on low-dimensional action labels to learn representations. It tests the stability of action-only modeling across heterogeneous data.

2. **MindLPI (Language-Supervised VLM Co-training):**
 - **Objective:** `L_MindLPI = L_act + λ_lang * L_lang`.
 - **Mechanism:** Introduces language supervision (`L_lang`) by converting action chunks into LAP-style action-description text. `L_lang` is an autoregressive cross-entropy loss with `λ_lang = 0.1`.
 - **Fine-tuning:** Only `L_act` is retained, avoiding additional latency from language generation during control.
 - **Ablation:** Explores whether backpropagating action loss into the VLM (no gradient truncation) is beneficial.

3. **MindWPI (Future Latent Feature Alignment):**
 - **Objective:** `L_MindWPI = L_act + λ_lat * L_lat`.
 - **Mechanism:** Uses a frozen V-JEPA 2 model as a latent feature extractor for current (`z_cur`) and future (`z_fut`) frames (default 8-frame offset). `z_cur` is added as context to the action expert, which then predicts `z_fut` via `L_lat` (MSE loss).
 - **Pre-training:** `λ_lat = 1.0` (for `L_lat` vs `L_act`).
 - **Attention Mask:** Latent tokens are prevented from attending to action tokens to avoid shortcut prediction, while action tokens can attend to latent tokens.
 - **Fine-tuning:** Both `L_act` and `L_lat` are retained, typically with a weaker `L_lat` ratio (e.g., `λ_ft_lat / λ_ft_act = 0.1`).

4. **MindLWPI (Joint Language Supervision and Future Latent Pre-training):**
 - **Objective:** `L_MindLWPI = L_act + λ_lat * L_lat + λ_lang * L_lang`.
 - **Mechanism:** Combines MindLPI and MindWPI, optimizing action modeling, language action description, and future latent prediction simultaneously.
 - **Pre-training:** `L_act` and `L_lat` have a 1:1 ratio, `λ_lang = 0.1`.
 - **Latent Compression:** AvgPool-k4 is used to compress 256 V-JEPA 2 latent tokens to 64 tokens, reducing overhead.
 - **Fine-tuning:** Language loss is disabled; `L_act` and `L_lat` are retained (e.g., `λ_ft_lat / λ_ft_act = 0.1`).

**Training and Adaptation Protocol:**
- **Data Processing:** OXEMix data converted to LeRobot format and 14D action space. Sampling strategy balances dataset scale and trajectory length.
- **Optimization:** AdamW with `β = (0.9, 0.95)`. Learning rates: `1×10^-5` (VLM), `1×10^-4` (action expert). Cosine decay LR schedule, `5,000` warmup steps. bfloat16 mixed precision, gradient checkpointing, gradient clipping. Max `2×10^5` pre-training steps. Distributed training with ZeRO-2.
- **Flow Matching:** Timestep `t` sampled from `u ∼ Beta(1.5, 1.0)`, then transformed. 4 Euler integration steps at inference.
- **Downstream Fine-tuning:** Full-parameter fine-tuning (default) or Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) for efficient adaptation. Max `1×10^5` steps, `2,000` warmup steps.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments provide a controlled comparison of VLA training paradigms on LIBERO, LIBERO-Plus, and SimplerEnv, alongside ablations and efficient adaptation studies.

**Controlled Comparison of Training Paradigms (Table 2):**
- **LIBERO:** This benchmark is largely saturated, showing minor differences (within 1-2 percentage points) across methods, serving primarily as a sanity check for basic capabilities. MindLWPI achieved the highest average success rate (99.1%).
- **LIBERO-Plus (Zero-shot robustness):** This benchmark reveals more distinct generalization capabilities.
 - **MindPI (Full PT):** Improved over the no-pretraining baseline on WidowX tasks in SimplerEnv but showed substantial degradation on RT-1 VM/VA, indicating instability and potential negative transfer of action-only pre-training with heterogeneous data.
 - **MindWPI:** Achieved strong performance on RT-1 VM/VA in SimplerEnv (86.7% VM, 71.1% VA) and remained competitive on LIBERO and WidowX. This suggests that future latent supervision is effective in modeling action outcomes and state transitions, particularly for cross-platform control.
 - **MindLWPI:** Obtained the highest results on LIBERO (99.1%), LIBERO-Plus (74.8% total), and WidowX (75.5% avg), while maintaining competitive performance on RT-1 VM/VA (84.4% VM, 69.8% VA). This indicates a complementarity between language supervision (improving overall robustness) and future latent supervision (contributing to cross-platform control transfer).

**Comparison with Public Baselines (Tables 3, 4, 5):**
- **LIBERO:** VLAFlow variants, particularly MindLWPI (99.1% Avg), performed comparably to or slightly exceeded recent strong baselines like OpenVLA-OFT and π0.5, with notable improvement on L-Long tasks, suggesting benefits for long-horizon action chains.
- **LIBERO-Plus:** MindLWPI (74.8% Total) showed competitive zero-shot robustness, outperforming most listed baselines and improving across several perturbation categories (Language, Light, Background). MindWPI was particularly effective on Robot Initial States and Objects Layout, aligning with its focus on state changes.
- **SimplerEnv:** This benchmark, with its larger distribution gap, highlighted transfer differences. MindWPI achieved the strongest RT-1 VM/VA results, reinforcing the value of future latent alignment for cross-platform shifts. MindLWPI demonstrated the best WidowX performance, indicating that combining language and latent supervision can lead to robust performance, although the benefits may vary by platform.

**Ablation Studies:**
- **Gradient Truncation in MindLPI:** Removing `stop-gradient` (allowing action loss to backpropagate into the VLM) significantly improved LIBERO-Plus performance (from 45.8% to 72.3%). This suggests that action-outcome alignment signals benefit vision-language representations.
- **Downstream Fine-tuning Loss Ratio in MindWPI:** A balanced pre-training ratio (1:1 for action and latent loss) was more effective than a weak latent loss (1:0.1). During fine-tuning, a weaker `λ_ft_lat` (0.1:1) for latent loss was more effective than a 1:1 ratio or removing the latent loss entirely, indicating the need for careful balancing to prevent conflicts with the action objective.
- **Low-Cost Ablations (w/o PT):** Without pre-training, a `0.1:1` latent-to-action loss ratio during fine-tuning was superior to `1:1`. A longer future-frame offset (32 vs. 8) for latent prediction was weaker, suggesting that excessively long intervals dilute the association with the current action.
- **MindLWPI Latent Compression:** AvgPool-k4 compression (reducing 256 to 64 tokens) maintained strong performance, offering a favorable trade-off between performance and computational cost compared to other compression methods.
- **Pre-training Data Composition (MindPI):** Action-only pre-training performance was highly sensitive to the specific data sources within OXEMix. Some subsets (e.g., RoboCOIN) led to severe degradation, while others offered only task-specific gains or regressions. This supports the argument that intermediate constraints are crucial for stable transfer from heterogeneous data.

**Efficient Adaptation with LoRA (Figure 3, Table 12):**
- LoRA injection only into the action expert was insufficient to match full-parameter fine-tuning performance.
- VLM-side LoRA, with around 100M trainable parameters, approached the performance of full fine-tuning.
- LoRA applied to both the VLM and action expert achieved the highest scores under larger parameter budgets. This indicates that effective downstream adaptation for VLM-based VLA models requires sufficient low-rank adaptation capacity for vision-language representations, in addition to updating the action expert.

**Meta-Action Space Analysis:**
The collective results suggest that MindPI directly fits fragmented, embodiment-specific action labels, making it vulnerable to heterogeneity. In contrast, MindLPI, MindWPI, and MindLWPI provide intermediate constraints. Language supervision (MindLPI) offers high-level action intent, while future latent features (MindWPI) capture action-induced state changes. The combination in MindLWPI effectively bridges intent and execution, leading to smoother and more transferable "meta-action" representations that generalize better across heterogeneous robot embodiments.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the understanding of VLA model pre-training by providing a unified and controlled framework for comparing diverse training paradigms. Its significance and potential impact include:

1. **Establishing a Standardized Comparison Framework:** VLAFlow offers a methodology for systematically evaluating VLA training objectives, minimizing confounding variables. This framework can facilitate more rigorous analysis and accelerate progress in the field by enabling clearer identification of effective strategies.
2. **Highlighting Limitations of Action-Only Pre-training:** The findings demonstrate that simple action-only pre-training on heterogeneous robot data can be unstable and lead to negative transfer, particularly when significant distribution gaps exist between pre-training and downstream tasks. This challenges the notion that increasing data scale alone suffices for robust VLA learning.
3. **Validating Complementary Roles of Language and Future Latent Supervision:** The study provides empirical evidence for the complementary benefits of language-supervised co-training (for high-level action intent) and future latent alignment (for state-transition and action-outcome modeling). This suggests that combining these intermediate constraints can lead to more stable and transferable action representations.
4. **Informing VLA Model Design:** The "meta-action space" interpretation offers a conceptual framework for understanding why these auxiliary supervision signals are effective. It suggests that effective VLA foundation models for heterogeneous robot data may require representations that bridge abstract intent and concrete control signals, rather than relying solely on low-dimensional action imitation. This insight can guide the development of future VLA architectures and training objectives.
5. **Improving Generalization and Robustness:** MindLWPI, by integrating language and future latent supervision, demonstrates superior overall transfer performance across diverse benchmarks, including improved zero-shot robustness to perturbations and better cross-platform control transfer. This indicates a pathway toward building more general-purpose and robust VLA models for real-world robotic applications.
6. **Guiding Efficient Adaptation:** The LoRA ablation studies provide practical guidance for resource-constrained adaptation of VLA models. They emphasize the importance of enabling adaptation capacity not only for the action expert but also for the underlying vision-language representations to achieve performance comparable to full fine-tuning.

Future work, as indicated by the authors, includes scaling pre-training, further optimizing pre-training loss ratios, and validating the framework on real robot platforms, which could further solidify the practical impact of these findings.
