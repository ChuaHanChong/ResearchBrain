# Behavior-Skill: A Fine-Grained Benchmark for Evaluating Vision-Language-Action Policies in Long-Horizon Tasks
> [!summary] Summary
> Behavior-Skill is a benchmark that reframes long-horizon mobile manipulation around executable constituent skills. Built on BEHAVIOR-1K, it annotates 235,492 skill instances across 34 semantic categories from 10,000 demonstrations, pairing each with a restorable simulator snapshot, a BDDL success goal, and an evaluation horizon so skills run independently under valid preconditions. Evaluated policies complete under half of constituent skills.

## Problem

- Existing embodied benchmarks organize data and evaluation at the task level, so constituent skills cannot be independently learned or systematically measured.
- Full-task rollouts stop at the first intermediate failure, leaving later skills unexecuted and unobserved, so aggregate success hides where capability actually breaks down.
- Prior fine-grained analyses annotate subgoals or stages but retain task-level rollout protocols, never restoring valid preconditions for isolated skill execution.

## Method

- ==Skill dataset annotation pipeline==: ==duration-adaptive sampling== of at most 64 multi-view frames plus aggregated 23-DoF motion summaries fed to ==Qwen3-VL== for per-skill instructions, verified against manual references.
- ==Independent evaluation unit== pairs a restored intermediate simulator snapshot, skill instruction, ==BDDL== success goal, and horizon; ==trajectory-based state reconstruction== replays transitions and rebuilds assisted-grasp constraints.
- Capability metrics: ==Task Skill Completion Rate== averages per-demonstration skill success, while ==Skill-Type Success Rate== aggregates outcomes per semantic skill category across the benchmark.

## Results

- On the full 50-task benchmark, skill-trained policies reach TSCR of only **48.4%** for one flow-based VLA and **42.5%** for the humanoid foundation model.
- Skill-instruction training beats task-instruction conditioning by **6.0** and **5.6** points respectively, and by **9.8** points on average across a 12-task subset.
- Task-level scores diverge sharply from skill completion: two tasks scoring **0%** QScore reach **80.0%** and **69.0%** TSCR; the same skill ranges **9%-78%** across tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Aggregate task success is a lossy signal: early failures truncate rollouts, so a near-zero task score can coexist with mostly competent skill execution.
> - Failures concentrate in contact-rich and articulated-object skills, and the capability profile is shared across architectures, implying a data or mechanism gap rather than backbone choice.
> - Semantic skill labels do not determine difficulty; object geometry, target relation, and scene context shift the same skill's reliability substantially across tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a critical challenge in embodied artificial intelligence: the reliable execution of long-horizon mobile manipulation tasks by robots. Recent advancements in Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, such as RT-2, OpenVLA, π-series, and GR00T, have demonstrated progress in learning unified policies that map visual observations and language instructions to robot actions. These models exhibit improved generalization across objects, environments, and robots, achieving competitive performance on various short-horizon benchmarks.

However, the paper notes that performance on more complex long-horizon benchmarks, such as BEHAVIOR-1K, remains considerably lower. This indicates that current VLA policies struggle with reliably completing multi-stage tasks that require sequential execution of multiple constituent skills. The broader landscape shows a trend towards developing more capable VLA policies and constructing increasingly challenging benchmarks. This work positions itself by observing that existing benchmarks and evaluation protocols predominantly operate at the task level, providing aggregate metrics that obscure failures at the intermediate skill level. While some research explores fine-grained representations (e.g., subgoals, executable programs) for policy learning and planning, a unified framework for both learning and *independent evaluation* of constituent skills has been largely absent. Behavior-Skill aims to fill this gap by providing a fine-grained, skill-centric benchmark that complements existing task-level evaluations, offering a tool to diagnose and understand the specific limitations of VLA policies in executing individual skills within complex tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for introducing Behavior-Skill stems from the observed limitations of current VLA models and evaluation methodologies in addressing long-horizon mobile manipulation tasks.

1. **Challenge of Long-Horizon Tasks:** Long-horizon tasks fundamentally depend on the successful, sequential execution of multiple constituent skills. A failure in any intermediate skill can prevent the completion of the overall task. Despite advancements in VLA models, their performance on long-horizon benchmarks is significantly lower than on short-horizon ones, indicating that reliable execution of complex multi-stage tasks remains largely unsolved.

2. **Limitations of Existing Benchmarks and Evaluation:** Current embodied datasets and benchmarks (e.g., CALVIN, LIBERO, BEHAVIOR-1K) are primarily organized at the task level. They offer only task-level language annotations and evaluate policies through continuous task rollouts, using metrics like final task success or overall progress. This approach presents several limitations:
 - **Opaque Failures:** Intermediate failures are difficult to observe and analyze because they prevent later skills from being executed, leading to a lack of information on policy capabilities for those subsequent skills.
 - **Lack of Independent Skill Evaluation:** Constituent skills cannot be independently learned or systematically evaluated under valid preconditions. It remains unclear which specific skills limit long-horizon task completion.
 - **Limited Information for Improvement:** Aggregate task metrics provide insufficient detail for understanding granular policy capabilities or for guiding targeted improvements, especially as task horizons increase.

To address these challenges, the key objectives of the Behavior-Skill benchmark are:

- **Establish Constituent Skills as the Basic Unit:** To reformulate the learning and evaluation of long-horizon tasks by focusing on executable constituent skills rather than solely on full tasks.
- **Provide a Fine-Grained Skill Dataset:** To create a dataset with detailed skill instances, each pairing a skill instruction with an aligned observation-action segment and associated with a restorable intermediate state and a skill success condition.
- **Enable Independent Skill Evaluation:** To establish a framework that allows for the independent evaluation of constituent skills under valid preconditions, isolating skill performance from failures in preceding stages.
- **Introduce Capability-Oriented Metrics:** To propose novel metrics that characterize policy capability beyond aggregate task success, specifically at the trajectory level (sequence of skills) and the semantic skill type level.
- **Expose Intermediate Capability Profiles:** Through systematic evaluation, to reveal which specific skills constitute bottlenecks for VLA policies in long-horizon tasks, thereby complementing full-task evaluation and guiding future research.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Behavior-Skill is designed as a fine-grained benchmark that builds upon the BEHAVIOR-1K dataset, reformulating long-horizon tasks around constituent skills. Its methodology encompasses three main components: a skill dataset, an independent skill evaluation framework, and capability-oriented metrics.

#### 3.1. A. Skill Dataset

The Behavior-Skill dataset is constructed by annotating each constituent skill from 10,000 demonstrations across 50 BEHAVIOR-1K household tasks, resulting in 235,492 skill instances across 34 semantic skill categories. Each skill instance combines multimodal context for comprehensive understanding:

1. **Textual Description Context:** This is derived from the original BEHAVIOR-1K skill records, including action labels, object identifiers, manipulated objects, and temporal intervals. To provide broader context, the global task description and previously completed skill descriptions are also incorporated.
2. **Synchronized Visual Observations:** Multi-view observations (head, left-wrist, right-wrist) synchronized with the skill interval are included. A duration-adaptive visual sampling strategy ensures up to 64 frames are retained per skill, capturing both global scene context and local end-effector interactions.
3. **Aligned Robot Actions:** The raw 23-DoF action sequences are aggregated into compact motion descriptions (e.g., "left arm moving, gripper closing") to explicitly summarize robot execution evidence.
4. **Language Model Annotation:** The constructed multimodal context is provided to the Qwen3-VL-235B-A22B-Instruct model to generate detailed annotations for each constituent skill. A manual review process is used, comparing generated annotations against a manually established reference for consistency and accuracy, especially regarding object identities, skill sequences, and wording.

#### 3.2. B. Independent Skill Evaluation

The benchmark provides an independent evaluation framework, allowing constituent skills to be measured irrespective of preceding trajectory outcomes. Each evaluation unit for the j-th skill of the i-th demonstration is defined as E_i,j = (S_i,j, L_i,j, G_i,j, H_i,j), where:

1. **Intermediate State Restoration (S_i,j):** This is the simulator state immediately preceding the execution of a skill, serving as the initialization for independent evaluation while preserving original task context.
 - **Challenge:** Original OmniGibson HDF5 demonstration recordings used an active-object filter, potentially omitting "sleeping" objects from isolated frame states.
 - **Solution:** A trajectory-based state reconstruction procedure is performed. Starting from the initial state of each demonstration, recorded transition events are applied sequentially, states are loaded, and actions are executed to advance the physics engine up to the target skill's start frame. The complete simulator state, including robot configuration, articulated-object states, object poses, interaction context, and assisted-grasp constraints, is then serialized.
2. **Skill Success Specification (G_i,j):** This is a symbolic goal expressed in Behavior Domain Definition Language (BDDL), serving as the success criterion for independent skill evaluation.
 - **Construction:** Original task BDDL definitions and skill annotations are jointly analyzed to identify manipulated objects, target objects, interaction types, and execution semantics. A symbolic goal is manually defined to capture only the completion criterion of the current skill.
 - **Predicate Extensions:** Most skills use existing BDDL predicates. However, for skills describing intermediate robot behaviors (e.g., navigation, grasping, handover) not fully captured by terminal environment states, skill-specific predicate extensions are introduced. These extensions evaluate conditions like robot-object proximity or grasp relations.
3. **Skill Instruction (L_i,j):** The corresponding skill instruction derived from the dataset is used to condition the VLA policy.
4. **Maximum Evaluation Horizon (H_i,j):** This is set to twice the duration of the skill as recorded in the original demonstration, providing ample time for policy execution.

**Evaluation Procedure:**
For each E_i,j, the simulator is initialized with S_i,j. The skill goal G_i,j is injected, and the policy executes actions conditioned on L_i,j. The BDDL goal engine evaluates the state after each step. Success is recorded if all goal predicates are satisfied before H_i,j is reached; otherwise, the skill is deemed unsuccessful.

#### 3.3. C. Evaluation Metrics

Two capability-oriented metrics are introduced to summarize policy performance:

1. **Task Skill Completion Rate (TSCR):** This measures how completely a policy executes the constituent skills within a single demonstration. For a trajectory τ_i with M_i skills, TSCR(τ_i) is the average of binary success outcomes (y_i,j ∈ {0, 1}) for each skill. The overall benchmark TSCR is the average across all evaluated demonstrations.
2. **Skill-Type Success Rate (STSR):** This characterizes execution performance across semantic skill categories. For a skill category k, STSR_k is the ratio of successful executions of skills belonging to category k to the total number of skills of category k.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were conducted on the complete 50-task Behavior-Skill benchmark using two representative VLA policies: π_0.5 and GR00T N1.7. Policies were evaluated under "Task" (trained with task instructions, conditioned on task prompt) and "Skill" (trained with skill instructions, conditioned on skill prompt) settings.

#### 4.1. A. Overall Skill Capability

- **Limited Skill Execution:** Under the "Skill" setting, π_0.5 achieved a TSCR of 48.4%, and GR00T N1.7 achieved 42.5%. This indicates that even with skill-specific training, the evaluated VLA policies successfully execute less than half of the constituent skills on average, suggesting skill execution remains a significant limitation for long-horizon tasks.
- **Non-Uniform Failure Distribution:** Analysis of STSR (Skill-Type Success Rate) revealed substantial variation in execution reliability across semantic skill types.
 - **High Success Skills:** Simple spatial reasoning or object-state transitions like "Sweep Surface," "Place Under," "Hold," "Spray," "Release," and "Move To" generally achieved high success rates.
 - **Moderate Performance Skills:** Manipulation skills such as "Place On," "Place Next To," and "Place In" showed moderate performance.
 - **Bottleneck Skills:** Articulated-object interaction ("Open Lid," "Close Door," "Close Lid"), precise object manipulation ("Pick Up From"), and tool-use behaviors ("Pour") consistently had the lowest success rates. Both π_0.5 and GR00T exhibited similar capability profiles, suggesting these challenging skill types are common bottlenecks.
- **Impact of Skill Training:** Skill training increased TSCR for π_0.5 by 6.0 percentage points (from 42.4% to 48.4%) and for GR00T N1.7 by 5.6 percentage points (from 36.9% to 42.5%). However, these gains did not eliminate the low success rates in bottleneck skill categories, indicating that even with skill training, overall constituent-skill capability remains limited.

#### 4.2. B. Comparison with Conventional Full-Task Evaluation

A comparison with conventional full-task evaluation (using BEHAVIOR-1K's original protocol) on three representative long-horizon tasks using the π_0.5-Task policy revealed distinct differences:

- **Masked Intermediate Failures:** In full-task evaluation, intermediate failures frequently prevented later constituent skills from being executed, leaving parts of the task unevaluated. For instance, in "Make Microwave Popcorn," only five of eight skills were reached. Behavior-Skill, by contrast, evaluates every constituent skill independently, allowing execution outcomes to be observed across the entire task sequence.
- **Disparity in Metrics:** Task-level outcomes (e.g., QScore) and constituent-skill completion (TSCR) can differ substantially. Tasks with a QScore of 0% (e.g., "Wash a Baseball Cap," "Cook Hot Dogs") might still achieve TSCRs of 80.0% and 69.0%, respectively, because many individual skills within them were successfully executed, even if the overall task goal was not met due to an earlier failure. This highlights that conventional metrics can obscure significant partial successes.

#### 4.3. C. Task-Dependent Skill Capability

An analysis on a 12-task subset further investigated how the reliability of a semantic skill varied across different task contexts:

- **Task-Wise TSCR Variation:** Task Skill Completion Rate varied considerably across different tasks, ranging from 36.3% to 65.3% under the "Task" setting and 43.8% to 77.2% under the "Skill" setting.
- **Contextual Skill Reliability:** The success rate of the same semantic skill differed across tasks. For example, "Open Door" ranged from 9.0% in "Make Microwave Popcorn" to 78.0% in "Cook Hot Dogs" under the "Task" setting. Similarly, "Place In" varied from 15.2% to 88.0%. This suggests that semantic skill labels alone do not fully determine difficulty; object geometry, target relations, and surrounding scene context also significantly influence execution reliability. These task-dependent variations were observed under both "Task" and "Skill" settings.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Behavior-Skill benchmark provides a novel and complementary perspective to the conventional evaluation of VLA policies in long-horizon mobile manipulation tasks. Its significance and potential impact include:

- **Enhanced Diagnostic Capabilities:** By establishing constituent skills as fundamental units and enabling their independent evaluation, Behavior-Skill offers granular diagnostic insights. It allows researchers to pinpoint specific skill categories that act as bottlenecks (e.g., contact-rich manipulation, articulated-object interaction), which are often obscured by aggregate task-level metrics. This detailed understanding of failure modes can guide more targeted research and development efforts.
- **Improved Policy Development:** The fine-grained skill dataset and independent evaluation framework provide a structured environment for learning and improving VLA policies. Researchers can focus on enhancing performance for specific challenging skills, potentially leading to more robust and capable embodied systems. The benchmark can facilitate the development of new architectures or training paradigms explicitly designed to address these identified weaknesses.
- **Standardized Skill Evaluation:** Behavior-Skill establishes a unified protocol for measuring constituent skill capabilities under valid preconditions. This standardization allows for direct comparison of different policies' performance on individual skills, fostering a more rigorous and transparent evaluation landscape in robotic learning.
- **Understanding Generalization:** The task-dependent skill capability analysis highlights the importance of contextual factors (object geometry, scene context) beyond just the semantic skill label. This insight can drive research into more context-aware and robust skill learning methods, pushing VLA models toward better generalization across varied task instantiations.
- **Future Research Directions:** The benchmark's current focus on independent skill evaluation naturally opens avenues for future work. The authors suggest extending Behavior-Skill toward planning-aware and sequential skill evaluation, which would incorporate task planning, automatic task decomposition, and the inherent sequential dependencies between skills during execution. This evolution could bridge the gap between isolated skill proficiency and overall intelligent task completion, ultimately contributing to the development of more capable long-horizon embodied AI systems.
