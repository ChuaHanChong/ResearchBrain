# Harness Robotic OS: A Unified Embodied-Agent Runtime for Closed-Loop Quadruped Inspection

> [!summary] Summary
> Harness Robotic OS (HROS) is a layered embodied-agent runtime unifying robot runtime, autonomy skills, a cognitive agent runtime, and operations planes through shared context, hierarchical working-episodic-semantic memory, grounded voice interaction, and a safety-gated self-evolution loop. Argos realizes it on a Vbot quadruped for residential inspection, reaching 100% waypoint reachability, sub-10 cm outdoor localization error, and 99% alarm-delivery success.

## Problem

- Quadruped inspection deployments wire sensing, navigation, vision-language services, and enterprise apps through task-specific adapters, so semantic task intent disconnects from pose, observations, and execution state.
- Prior systems do not systematically retain and retrieve mission experience, so repeated patrols of the same property gain no long-term adaptation from past hazards, corrections, or recovery outcomes.
- Changes to prompts, tool policies, task graphs, or skills lack evaluation, provenance, and rollback, making controlled adaptation of a long-running deployed robot unsafe and untraceable.

## Method

- **HROS** layers robot runtime, embodied autonomy skills, cognitive agent runtime, and interaction/operations planes over a ==shared context bus==; every skill publishes timestamps, execution state, and failure codes without entering the real-time control loop.
- ==Hierarchical agent memory== splits working (mission, dialogue, pending calls), episodic (time-indexed trajectories, hazards, recoveries), and semantic (map regions, assets, inspection rules) tiers, retrieved by intent, location, scene, and execution state.
- A ==safety-gated self-evolution loop== turns execution traces into candidate memory/prompt/tool/task-graph updates that must pass offline regression and safety evaluation, provenance recording, and versioned rollout before deployment.

## Results

- Residential field deployment reached **100%** waypoint reachability, outdoor localization error below **10 cm** via Fast-LIO2, and EGO-Planner obstacle-response latency below **200 ms**.
- Qwen3-VL hazard detection hit **95%** for garbage overflow and fire-passage obstruction, **90%** lane occupancy, **88%** standing water, **85%** facility damage, with false-positive and missed-detection rates each under **5%**.
- Alarm delivery through DingTalk/Feishu and structured-report generation both succeeded at **99%**; platform endurance exceeded **3 h** against a full-coverage mission of at most **60 min**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Placing agent orchestration strictly above deterministic localization, perception, and motion skills keeps real-time guarantees intact, because a reasoning failure cannot bypass the motion controller.
> - Binding every image, model response, and event to a shared map frame and mission converts raw media into auditable, replayable evidence that feeds enterprise workflows and later evaluation.
> - Self-improvement in deployed robots is safer as an offline experience-to-update pipeline with versioned rollout and rollback than as unconstrained online modification; memory benefits stay unquantified here.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position in the Broader Research Landscape

This work lies at the intersection of quadruped robotics, autonomous navigation, multimodal perception, embodied artificial intelligence, human–robot interaction, and enterprise workflow automation.

Research on legged robots has established that quadruped platforms can traverse terrain that is difficult for wheeled systems, including ramps, thresholds, uneven pavement, and cluttered passages. Existing systems such as ANYmal and Spot demonstrate the feasibility of legged platforms for inspection and industrial operations. In parallel, substantial progress has been made in the component technologies required for autonomous inspection. These include LiDAR–inertial simultaneous localization and mapping, three-dimensional global planning, local obstacle avoidance, stereo depth estimation, and vision-language models capable of open-vocabulary scene interpretation.

The paper argues that the main limitation is not the absence of individual algorithms, but the weak integration of these algorithms into a persistent operational system. Conventional robot deployments often connect navigation, perception, language models, operator interfaces, and enterprise applications through task-specific adapters. Such designs can be effective for individual demonstrations, but they may not maintain a unified representation of mission intent, robot state, observations, inspection rules, and downstream actions.

Harness Robotic OS (HROS) addresses this integration problem by proposing an embodied-agent runtime rather than another localization or planning algorithm. It provides a layered framework in which deterministic robot capabilities are exposed as reusable skills to a higher-level cognitive runtime. The framework also incorporates voice interaction, persistent memory, structured reporting, and a safety-controlled process for adapting prompts, task graphs, memory, and tool policies. The Argos system is a concrete implementation of this architecture for residential-property inspection.

Accordingly, the paper’s primary contribution is architectural and systems-oriented. Its novelty lies in organizing existing robotic and AI components into a governed closed loop that connects physical inspection with human and enterprise operations. The underlying navigation and perception algorithms are established methods rather than new algorithms introduced by the paper.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

#### 2.1. Operational Motivation

Residential-property inspection covers roads, fire passages, entrances, equipment rooms, waste-collection areas, and other shared spaces. These environments change over time and contain pedestrians, vehicles, temporary obstructions, varying illumination, standing water, clutter, and sanitation or safety hazards.

Manual patrols provide contextual judgment but are limited by labor availability, patrol frequency, inconsistency between personnel, and incomplete documentation. An autonomous system must therefore do more than move through an environment. It must repeatedly cover specified areas, identify relevant conditions, communicate findings, and preserve evidence that can be reviewed by property-management staff.

The paper defines this requirement as an operational closed loop: the robot should sense the environment, localize itself, execute a mission, interpret observations, produce structured events, notify responsible personnel, and retain the resulting experience for later use.

#### 2.2. Technical Objectives

The authors pursue four main objectives. First, they seek to unify robot resources, autonomy algorithms, multimodal reasoning, human interaction, and enterprise services within a traceable runtime. Second, they aim to maintain shared context so that language instructions and visual observations remain grounded in the robot’s pose, mission, current waypoint, and execution state.

Third, the system is designed to support long-term operational memory. The authors distinguish short-term mission context from time-indexed mission history and stable site knowledge. This is intended to allow the robot to reuse information about previous hazards, assets, recovery episodes, and operator corrections.

Fourth, the authors propose a controlled self-evolution process. Rather than allowing a language model or agent to modify itself directly during deployment, HROS records execution traces, generates candidate updates, evaluates them offline, and promotes only approved versions. The stated goal is to enable improvement while preserving safety, provenance, reproducibility, and rollback.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. HROS Architecture

HROS is organized into four major planes or layers.

The **robot runtime** abstracts hardware and low-level control. It includes the quadruped platform, sensors, onboard computing, connectivity, and actuation interfaces. Higher-level agents do not directly issue unvalidated actuator commands.

The **embodied autonomy skills layer** exposes navigation capabilities through stable interfaces. These capabilities include mapping and localization, global mission planning, local perception, motion generation, and recovery.

The **cognitive agent runtime** manages intent routing, task graphs, multimodal reasoning, memory retrieval, tool execution, monitoring, and recovery at the mission level. OpenClaw orchestrates interactions with tools and the Qwen3-VL vision-language model.

The **interaction and operations plane** handles streaming speech recognition, text-to-speech, text and image input, mission consoles, enterprise alerts, structured reports, and human confirmation.

A shared context bus connects these layers. Components report timestamps, execution states, confidence or failure codes, and references to outputs. This allows the cognitive layer to monitor and coordinate the system without entering the time-critical motion-control loop.

#### 3.2. Robotic Platform and Navigation Stack

Argos uses a Vbot quadruped equipped with stereo cameras, a 16-line LiDAR, an inertial measurement unit, GNSS, 4G/5G connectivity, and an RDK S100P edge computer. The computer contains six ARM Cortex-A78AE CPU cores and a 128-TOPS Nash processing unit.

Fast-LIO2 is used in three modes. During commissioning, it constructs a global point-cloud map from synchronized LiDAR and IMU data. During routine patrols, it estimates the robot’s pose relative to this prior map. It also supports relocalization after tracking degradation or system restart. A common map frame is used to associate waypoints, images, hazards, and reports with consistent spatial coordinates.

PCT-Planner generates a global route through policy-defined inspection viewpoints on the three-dimensional map. The mission layer orders these viewpoints and stores the route as a reusable, versioned task template.

For local obstacle avoidance, Hobot-Stereo supplies dense near-field depth. These data complement the wider-range but sparser LiDAR representation, particularly for low obstacles, thin objects, and partially occluded boundaries. The fused local geometry is supplied to EGO-Planner, which continuously optimizes a dynamically feasible trajectory. The system supports nominal tracking, local detours around temporary obstacles, and a recovery mode in which the robot stops and reports a typed failure when no feasible trajectory is available.

#### 3.3. Voice, Memory, and Agent Operation

Streaming automatic speech recognition converts spoken instructions into timestamped intent hypotheses. The system grounds these hypotheses against the active mission, robot location, visible scene, current state, and access policy. Ambiguous or safety-critical commands require confirmation. TTS communicates task acceptance, progress, hazards, recovery actions, and completion.

The memory subsystem has three levels. Working memory stores current mission details, recent dialogue, robot state, observations, and pending tool calls. Episodic memory stores time-indexed trajectories, decisions, hazards, failures, and recovery outcomes. Semantic memory stores relatively stable site information, including assets, map regions, inspection rules, historical defects, and management procedures. Retrieval is conditioned on mission intent, spatial location, scene semantics, and execution state.

The self-evolution loop records execution traces and human feedback in an experience buffer. A reflection and evaluation stage assigns outcomes and attributes failures. Candidate changes may affect memory, prompts, tool-selection policies, task graphs, or reusable skills. These candidates are evaluated against offline regression cases and safety rules, assigned provenance, and deployed through versioned rollout only after passing a safety gate.

#### 3.4. Hazard Analysis and Enterprise Integration

At designated viewpoints and during traversal, Argos captures images. OpenClaw attaches the image to the current pose, timestamp, waypoint, mission identifier, and inspection policy before sending it to Qwen3-VL.

The resulting interpretation is parsed into a constrained event schema containing hazard category, severity, evidence, location, and recommended review action. The reported categories include blocked fire passages, standing water, exposed wiring, overflowing waste containers, scattered refuse, lane occupancy, and abnormal clutter near equipment rooms.

Only schema-valid events enter the operational workflow. The original image, raw model response, parsed fields, spatial labels, and delivery status are retained. Reports and alerts are sent through DingTalk or Feishu for review and work-order processing. Human corrections are stored as feedback while preserving the original system output.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The field evaluation was conducted in a residential property environment. The mission contained eight inspection waypoints and followed a five-stage deployment process: map construction, mission configuration, daily autonomous inspection, image collection and analysis, and report delivery.

The reported navigation results were as follows:

- Waypoint reachability was 100% in the reported trials.
- Outdoor localization error was below 10 centimeters.
- Obstacle-response latency was below 200 milliseconds.
- Continuous operating endurance exceeded three hours.
- A full-coverage mission required no more than 60 minutes.

These results indicate that the integrated mapping, planning, local perception, and motion pipeline completed the tested missions under the stated deployment conditions.

Hazard-recognition performance varied by category. Garbage-overflow detection and fire-passage obstruction detection each achieved 95%. Lane-occupancy detection reached 90%, standing-water detection reached 88%, and public-facility damage detection reached 85%. The paper attributes the lower facility-damage rate to the visual diversity of that category. Aggregate false-positive and missed-detection rates were both reported as below 5%, although the paper does not provide the associated sample counts or confidence intervals in the presented results.

The operational handoff performed at a reported 99% success rate for alarm delivery through DingTalk and Feishu. Structured-report generation accuracy was also reported as 99%. These figures suggest that the system connected model outputs to downstream management processes with relatively few transmission or formatting failures in the evaluated setting.

However, the cognitive-runtime experiments described in the methodology were not accompanied by numerical results. The paper defines tests for voice interaction, hierarchical memory, and safety-gated self-evolution, including word error rate, intent accuracy, retrieval performance, adaptation regressions, and gate escapes. It explicitly states that these values would be reported after the controlled trials were completed. Consequently, the current evidence primarily validates the navigation, inspection, reporting, and delivery pipeline. It does not yet quantitatively establish the benefit of memory, voice interaction, or self-evolution.

The evaluation also provides limited information about the number of missions, images, hazards, commands, or independent repetitions. No ablation study compares HROS with a conventional navigation stack, removes the memory hierarchy, or measures the effect of stereo–LiDAR fusion. These omissions make it difficult to determine which architectural elements account for the reported outcomes or how well the results generalize.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The principal significance of HROS is that it frames robotic inspection as an operational system rather than an isolated navigation problem. By connecting physical state, task intent, multimodal interpretation, evidence retention, and enterprise response, the architecture offers a way to make inspection outcomes auditable and actionable.

The explicit separation between deterministic autonomy and cognitive reasoning is also important for safety. Language models and agent runtimes can coordinate tasks and interpret scenes, but they are not placed directly in control of low-level motion. This boundary can reduce the consequences of reasoning errors while allowing higher-level adaptation and interaction.

The memory design could support recurring inspections by retaining site-specific information and prior failures. The governed evolution process provides a mechanism for improving prompts, task graphs, and tool policies without permitting uncontrolled online changes. If validated through longer-term studies, this could be relevant to other inspection domains such as campuses, industrial facilities, utilities, and public infrastructure.

The limitations are substantial. The system depends on a prior map and may require maintenance as construction, vegetation, parking, and seasonal conditions change. Open-world hazard recognition remains incomplete, and the reported results come from a limited deployment context. Voice robustness in outdoor noise, operator workload, safety confirmation, memory staleness, and adaptation reliability require direct measurement.

Overall, the paper presents a coherent systems architecture and a fielded quadruped inspection prototype. Its current evidence supports the feasibility of the integrated navigation-to-reporting loop, while the claims concerning persistent memory, voice-aware interaction, and self-evolution remain architectural proposals pending the controlled evaluations described by the authors.
