# Enhancing Visual Domain Robustness in Behaviour Cloning via Saliency-Guided Augmentation

> [!summary] Summary
> RoboSaGA is a saliency-guided image augmentation for vision-based behaviour cloning. It back-propagates FullGrad saliency from visual encoders to set per-pixel blending weights, aggressively overlaying out-of-domain images onto task-trivial regions while preserving task-critical ones, with a global saliency buffer for efficiency. It drops the visual-domain-shift performance gap from 0.60 to 0.14 in simulation and 0.71 to 0.05 in the real world.

## Problem

- Conventional behaviour-cloning augmentations like Random Crop and Colour Jitter improve in-domain accuracy but collapse under shadows, distractors and background changes, leaving gaps near 0.6.
- Superimposition augmentations from computer vision blend out-of-domain images indiscriminately, risking destruction of task-critical semantics, spatial-temporal relationships and agent-target interactions that behaviour cloning depends on.
- Saliency-based variants such as KeepAugment use classification logits and fixed-size rectangular binary masks, which suit classification but poorly cover spatially separated, variable-sized manipulation objects.

## Method

- ==FullGrad encoder-level saliency extraction== back-propagates full gradients, including learned biases, from visual-feature outputs to full-resolution inputs, keeping augmentation modular across camera views and policy architectures.
- ==Per-pixel blending== with ==saliency clipping== at threshold 0.8 caps normalised saliency, so even task-critical pixels receive a subtle overlay, which proves crucial for background robustness.
- A ==global saliency buffer== stores 8-bit low-resolution maps, refreshing only the least-recently-updated 10% of each batch while augmenting 50% of trajectories, after a 10-epoch warm-up.

## Results

- Across BC-MLP, BC-RNN and Diffusion Policy on four Robomimic tasks, RoboSaGA cut the distractor and background gap to **0.14**, versus **0.24** for Random Overlay, **0.28** for SODA, **0.60** for Random Crop.
- On the real-world Toy pick-and-place task the gap fell from **0.71** with Random Crop to **0.18** with Random Overlay and **0.05** with RoboSaGA, a **72%** relative improvement.
- Ablation: Guided Erase alone leaves a **0.55** background gap, Random Overlay a **0.31** distractor gap; saliency blending gives **0.13** and **0.06**, while the buffer cuts augmentation time from **0.70s** to **0.22s**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Continuous per-pixel augmentation intensity beats binary masks or constant blending, erasing distractors and perturbing backgrounds simultaneously instead of trading one robustness for the other.
> - In behaviour cloning, direct training on aggressively augmented samples matches or slightly beats auxiliary representation-learning objectives, since action labels already provide the supervision signal.
> - Policy saliency need not match human intuition, history-dependent policies attend to non-obvious regions, yet it still works as an augmentation prior at roughly 1.5x compute overhead.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-based behaviour cloning (BC) is a field dedicated to transferring expert demonstrations into robot skills, often involving complex spatial-temporal interactions and multi-sensory integration. Despite progress in replicating behaviors, a significant challenge in BC is the generalization of learned policies to novel visual environments, referred to as visual domain shifts (VDS). These shifts include variations in lighting, shadows, distractors, and backgrounds, which frequently occur in real-world deployment scenarios. The high cost and difficulty of collecting diverse real-world demonstration data often lead to datasets that prioritize task-related variability over visual diversity, exacerbating this generalization issue.

Traditional image augmentation techniques, such as Random Crop and Colour Jitter, are commonly used in computer vision and robotics to enhance model generalization. While these methods can improve in-domain performance and some aspects of generalization (e.g., Random Crop for in-domain generalizability), they have demonstrated limitations in effectively addressing substantial visual domain shifts.

A distinct category of augmentation, superimposition-based techniques (e.g., methods that selectively remove parts of an image or overlay images), has shown promise in the broader computer vision community for improving model robustness. However, their direct application in vision-based BC is constrained by the necessity to preserve task-critical semantic information, spatial-temporal relationships inherent in demonstrations, and interactions between the agent and its target. Naive application of such methods risks corrupting the essential information required for correct task execution in robotic manipulation.

This research positions itself at the intersection of behaviour cloning and advanced data augmentation. It aims to bridge the gap by adapting superimposition-based augmentation for BC, specifically by integrating saliency-guided mechanisms. The work builds upon existing saliency-guided augmentation concepts from computer vision but tailors them for the unique requirements of robotic manipulation, where precise control and preservation of task-critical visual cues are paramount. By doing so, it attempts to improve visual robustness without sacrificing the fidelity of learned behaviors, which is a known limitation of prior general-purpose augmentation strategies when applied to sensitive BC tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the observed fragility of vision-based behaviour cloning (BC) policies when confronted with visual domain shifts (VDS) in deployment environments. Despite their ability to transfer complex skills from demonstrations, BC models trained on limited datasets often struggle with real-world variations such as changes in lighting, introduction of shadows, presence of distractors, or alterations in backgrounds. This lack of robustness significantly impedes the practical application of BC in dynamic and unstructured settings.

The authors identify several specific limitations and motivations:

- **Insufficient Robustness of Current Augmentations:** Traditional image augmentation techniques (e.g., Random Crop, Colour Jitter) are effective for improving in-domain performance and providing mild generalization but are generally insufficient for addressing the substantial visual domain shifts encountered in real-world robotic tasks.
- **Unsuitability of General Superimposition Methods for BC:** While superimposition-based augmentations have demonstrated efficacy in enhancing generalization in general computer vision tasks, their direct application to vision-based BC is problematic. BC tasks require the preservation of delicate task-critical semantics, precise spatial-temporal relationships, and specific agent-target interactions. Aggressive or indiscriminate superimposition could corrupt these vital visual cues, leading to policy degradation rather than improvement.
- **Cost of Data Collection:** Collecting large and visually diverse datasets for robotic manipulation is resource-intensive, particularly in real-world scenarios. This economic constraint often results in BC datasets that, while rich in task-related variability, are limited in visual diversity, thus necessitating robust data augmentation strategies.

Based on these motivations, the key objectives of this research are:

- **To develop a novel superimposition-based augmentation method, named RoboSaGA (Saliency-Guided Augmentation), specifically tailored for vision-based behaviour cloning.** This method aims to leverage the benefits of superimposition while addressing the unique constraints of BC.
- **To dynamically adjust augmentation intensity at a per-pixel level using policy-driven saliency maps.** The objective is to allow aggressive augmentation in areas deemed "task-trivial" by the policy, thereby enhancing visual diversity, while simultaneously preserving or subtly augmenting "task-critical" information to avoid corruption.
- **To ensure seamless integration of RoboSaGA into existing BC architectures.** A core objective is to avoid requiring structural modifications to the policy network or the introduction of additional learning objectives, thereby promoting its broad applicability and ease of adoption across various BC implementations.
- **To empirically evaluate RoboSaGA's effectiveness across a range of visual domain shift scenarios in both simulated and real-world environments.** This includes assessing its performance relative to traditional and other superimposition-based augmentation methods, and evaluating its compatibility with different BC policy types (BC-MLP, BC-RNN, and Diffusion Policy). The ultimate goal is to demonstrate significant enhancement in visual robustness while maintaining in-domain performance.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, RoboSaGA, is a Saliency-Guided Augmentation designed for vision-based behaviour cloning (BC). It integrates into existing architectures without requiring structural modifications or additional learning objectives.

#### 3.1. Core Mechanism

RoboSaGA operates by generating an augmented image $x^*$ through a weighted combination of an in-domain image $x$ and an out-of-domain (OOD) image $x_O$. This process is defined by the equation: $x^* = M \odot x + (1 − M) \odot x_O$, where $M$ is a pixel-wise blending matrix and $\odot$ denotes element-wise multiplication. The key distinction of RoboSaGA lies in how this blending matrix $M$ is determined.

#### 3.2. Saliency Extraction

RoboSaGA utilizes FullGrad to derive saliency maps. FullGrad back-propagates the full gradient from the output feature vector of a visual encoder ($f_θ_i(x_v_i)$) to the full-resolution input image. This method is noted for incorporating learned biases in addition to network weights during saliency computation, which is stated to result in sharper and more accurate saliency maps compared to approaches like Grad-CAM. The normalized saliency derived from Fullgrad is denoted as $g_v_i$.

#### 3.3. Saliency Clipping

A clipping mechanism is applied to the normalized saliency scores. The saliency map $s_v_i$ is formally defined as $s_v_i := \min(g_v_i, λ)$, where $λ$ is a pre-defined clipping threshold set to 0.8 across all experiments. This clipping allows for a subtle overlay on task-critical regions, aiming to improve robustness against background changes without fully masking important visual information. The clipped saliency map $s_v_i$ acts as the blending matrix $M$.

#### 3.4. Data Augmentation Strategy

Within each training batch, RoboSaGA selectively augments a proportion of trajectories. The hyper-parameter $\alpha$ (set to 50%) determines the number of trajectories targeted for augmentation. Each sample within a selected trajectory is augmented independently. The OOD images used for superimposition comprise approximately 6,000 images: 5,000 from a subset of MSCOCO and 1,000 synthetic images (plain colors, grids, gradients, chess, Perlin patterns). These OOD images are subjected to random rotations and brightness adjustments.

#### 3.5. Global Saliency Buffer

To mitigate the computational cost associated with FullGrad, RoboSaGA employs a global buffer. This buffer stores and monitors past saliency maps for each image. It is initialized with all-one maps. During training, the saliency for a subset of trajectories, specifically the least recently updated $\beta$ trajectories (where $\beta$ is 10% of the training batch), is recomputed and updated in the buffer. For augmentation, $\alpha$ saliency maps are retrieved from this buffer. To conserve memory, saliency maps are stored as 8-bit, single-channel images at a reduced resolution. A warm-up period of $\gamma$ epochs (default 10) is implemented, during which the buffer returns all-one saliency maps, allowing encoders to develop task-specific knowledge before aggressive augmentation.

#### 3.6. Experimental Setup

- **Manipulation Tasks:** Evaluations were conducted on four simulated tasks (Lift, Square, Can, Transport) from the Robomimic environment and one real-world pick-and-place task (Toy). These tasks encompass varying complexities, camera views (second-person, eye-in-hand), and action dimensions.
- **Visual Domain Shift (VDS) Scenarios:**
 - **Simulation:** Variations in lighting (intensity, color), shadows (on/off), distractors (category, quantity, location), and backgrounds (surface textures like textiles, wood, ceramics).
 - **Real-world:** Distractors (random items, clutter) and backgrounds (textile combinations).
- **Policies Evaluated:** Three seminal BC policies were tested: BC-MLP (Multi-Layer Perceptron), BC-RNN (Recurrent Neural Network), and Diffusion Policy. All policies incorporated robot proprioceptive states; BC-RNN and Diffusion Policy also utilized past observations.
- **Evaluation Protocol:** In simulation, performance was averaged over the top three checkpoints from 600 training epochs, each tested with 50 rollouts. In real-world settings, the final checkpoint after 500 epochs was used, with task success rates averaged over 20 trials.
- **Baseline Augmentation:** Random Crop (90% crop, resized to original resolution) was established as the default baseline augmentation for all methods, indicated by a "+" prefix when combined.
- **Comparison Methods:**
 - **Colour Jitter:** Implemented using PyTorch's ColorJitter with brightness, contrast, saturation, and hue set to 0.2.
 - **Guided Erase:** A variant of RoboSaGA where saliency maps are binarized at a threshold of 0.5, effectively erasing non-salient regions with OOD images.
 - **Random Overlay:** A variant of RoboSaGA where the context-aware per-pixel blending matrix is replaced by a global constant blending factor of 0.5.
 - **SODA:** A representation learning technique from visual reinforcement learning, which uses a student-teacher architecture with a consistency loss. This was included to compare direct augmentation with approaches involving additional learning objectives.
- **Evaluation Metric:** The performance gap ($\Delta P_{+\text{Aug}}$), defined as the difference between the in-domain mean success rate of the Random Crop baseline and the mean success rate under VDS with the applied augmentation method. A smaller gap indicates greater robustness.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluations were conducted in both simulated and real-world settings across various manipulation tasks and policy types, comparing RoboSaGA against traditional and other superimposition-based augmentation methods.

#### 4.1. Q1: How well do traditional augmentation methods handle visual domain shifts (VDS)?

- Simulated experiments indicated that Random Crop and Colour Jitter exhibited limitations when addressing distractors and background changes.
- Colour Jitter provided improvements under light intensity changes, reducing the average performance gap to 0.1, but its effectiveness was diminished under shadows, where the gap was 0.33. In contrast, superimposition-based methods achieved performance gaps below 0.1 under both lighting and shadow variations.
- For distractors and background variations, Colour Jitter offered an approximate 8% relative improvement, reducing the gap from 0.60 to 0.55, suggesting limited efficacy in these scenarios.

#### 4.2. Q2: Is representation learning more effective than direct training with augmented images?

- Comparison with SODA, a representation learning technique, showed that direct training with augmented samples from methods like Random Overlay and RoboSaGA delivered comparable, and in some instances marginally improved, performance.
- Direct training with augmented samples reduced the performance gap by an average of 0.04 for distractor and background variations, and by 0.02 for lighting and shadows, relative to SODA. This suggests that direct augmentation can be as effective without the added complexity of representation learning architectures in BC.

#### 4.3. Q3: Does RoboSaGA’s effectiveness against VDS extend to policies beyond BC-MLP?

- Across all tested policies (BC-MLP, BC-RNN, Diffusion Policy), RoboSaGA and Random Overlay consistently demonstrated translatable improvements in visual robustness under VDS.
- The Diffusion Policy generally exhibited the least performance degradation among the three policy types.
- Real-world experiments corroborated these findings, showing that both Random Overlay and RoboSaGA significantly enhanced performance against VDS compared to the Random Crop baseline.

#### 4.4. Q4: Does RoboSaGA maintain its performance lead against the computation-free Random Overlay?

- **Simulation:** Random Overlay proved to be an effective and efficient augmentation method, reducing the performance gap from Random Crop + Jitter’s 0.55 to 0.24 for distractor and background variations, and from 0.21 to 0.06 for lighting and shadow variations.
- RoboSaGA further improved these results, reducing the performance gaps to 0.14 and 0.03, respectively. This represents relative improvements of 41.6% for distractor/background and 50.0% for lighting/shadow variations over Random Overlay.
- **Real-world:** In real-world scenarios, Random Overlay reduced the average performance gap from 0.71 (Random Crop) to 0.18. RoboSaGA further reduced this gap to 0.05, demonstrating a 72% improvement over Random Overlay.

#### 4.5. Q5: How effective is RoboSaGA in the real world?

- Experiments on a 3-degrees-of-freedom pick-and-place task in real-world settings indicated that RoboSaGA facilitated successful task completion under severe visual domain shifts. These conditions included aggressive lighting variations (introducing strong shadows from distractors), significant occlusions in one camera view, and background changes with added clutter. This behavior was observed across BC-MLP, BC-RNN, and Diffusion Policy.

#### 4.6. Q6: How closely do saliency maps correspond to human expectations of task relevance?

- Observations of saliency maps suggested a misalignment between the robot's policy-driven focus and human intuition regarding task-critical regions. For instance, saliency might highlight general areas around the gripper and nut during reaching, or shift away from the object when lifting. During insertion, the front camera might overlook the target peg and nut while the eye-in-hand view focuses on the peg and hole.
- This misalignment is hypothesized to result from the multi-sensory integration (proprioception, observation history, multi-views) and task progression awareness inherent in vision-based BC policies.
- History-independent BC-MLP policies tended to produce more human-interpretable saliency maps compared to history-dependent BC-RNN and Diffusion Policy.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of vision-based behaviour cloning by addressing a persistent challenge: the lack of robustness to visual domain shifts. The introduction of RoboSaGA, a saliency-guided augmentation method, offers a practical approach to enhance the generalization capabilities of BC policies.

The significance and potential impact of this work include:

- **Enhanced Visual Robustness:** The primary impact is the demonstrated ability to significantly improve the robustness of behaviour cloning policies against substantial visual domain shifts, including variations in lighting, shadows, distractors, and backgrounds. This directly addresses a critical limitation that often hinders the real-world deployment of learned robotic skills.
- **Improved Generalization without Architectural Changes:** RoboSaGA’s design allows for seamless integration into existing BC architectures without requiring modifications to network structure or the addition of complex learning objectives. This modularity reduces implementation overhead and increases its adoptability across diverse BC frameworks (e.g., BC-MLP, BC-RNN, Diffusion Policy), making it a readily applicable tool for practitioners.
- **Efficient Augmentation Strategy:** The findings suggest that direct training with saliency-guided augmentation can be as, or more, effective than more complex representation learning approaches like SODA for enhancing robustness in BC. This indicates a potentially more efficient pathway to achieving robust policies. The global saliency buffer further mitigates computational costs associated with saliency map generation, balancing performance and efficiency.
- **Advancement in Data Augmentation for Robotics:** The work extends the utility of superimposition-based augmentation techniques, which have been primarily explored in general computer vision or reinforcement learning, to the nuanced domain of behaviour cloning. By explicitly accounting for the need to preserve task-critical semantics through saliency guidance, it offers a refined approach tailored for robotic manipulation tasks.
- **Insights into Policy Decision-Making:** The analysis of saliency maps, even when not perfectly aligning with human intuition, provides valuable insights into how different BC policies (especially history-dependent ones) prioritize visual information during task execution. This understanding can inform future research into interpretability and design of more effective visuomotor policies.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The identified limitations, such as computational overhead for saliency calculation and variations in policy performance across tasks, open avenues for future research. This includes exploring more efficient saliency extraction methods, investigating task-specific pixel density challenges, and expanding real-world evaluations to a broader array of complex manipulation tasks. Ultimately, leveraging the "task-specific insights" revealed by saliency could lead to further advancements in vision-based behaviour cloning.

In summary, this research presents a method that directly tackles a core practical challenge in behaviour cloning, offering a robust, adaptable, and interpretable augmentation strategy that could accelerate the deployment of learned robot skills in visually varied environments.
