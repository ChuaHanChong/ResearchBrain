# Sustainable Transfer Learning for Adaptive Robot Skills

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at RPTU University Kaiserslautern-Landau and German Institute of Artificial Intelligence investigated transferring learned manipulation skills between different robotic arms using reinforcement learning and hybrid control. Their method demonstrates that fine-tuning a pre-trained policy significantly improves success rates to up to 97.98% for a peg-in-hole task, achieving performance comparable to native training with substantially fewer learning interactions.

## Problem

- Traditional reinforcement learning (RL) approaches for robotics require extensive training time, high computational resources, and large datasets for each new robot and task.
- The inability to efficiently transfer learned skills across different robotic platforms limits practical applicability and adaptability in dynamic industrial or real-world settings.
- Current RL methods often suffer from poor sample efficiency, demanding millions of time-steps for convergence when learning complex manipulation skills independently.

## Method

- Employed a ==hybrid motion-force control scheme== for a precise peg-in-hole insertion task, with ==Soft Actor-Critic (SAC)== reinforcement learning autonomously adjusting controller parameters.
- Trained optimal policies on two distinct robotic arms (Universal Robots UR5e and Franka Emika Panda) in a MuJoCo simulation environment.
- Evaluated policy transfer scenarios, including zero-shot application and fine-tuning of pre-trained policies, between the source and target robotic platforms.

## Results

- Native training achieved high success rates: UR5e at **100%** (average 126 time-steps) and Panda at **97.98%** (average 117 time-steps).
- Zero-shot transfer reduced success rates: Panda using a UR5e policy achieved **78.79%** success, and UR5e using a Panda policy achieved **93.94%** success.
- Fine-tuning a UR5e policy on the Panda robot restored performance to **97.98%** success with an average of **135** time-steps, demonstrating a significant reduction compared to Panda's over **1 million** time-steps for native convergence.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Zero-shot policy transfer between different robotic arms, while offering a baseline, generally results in degraded performance (e.g., Panda using UR5e policy achieved 78.79% success) due to inherent hardware and control differences.
> - Fine-tuning a pre-trained policy on a new robot effectively bridges the domain gap, substantially improving success rates and sample efficiency (Panda reached 97.98% success with fine-tuning, matching native training).
> - Adaptive policy transfer is a viable strategy for developing more efficient, sustainable, and adaptable robotic systems, reducing the need for extensive retraining from scratch and contributing to sustainable robotic learning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in this paper addresses a prominent challenge within the fields of robotics and reinforcement learning (RL): the transferability of learned skills between different robotic platforms. Traditional RL approaches typically necessitate extensive training, requiring significant computational resources and large datasets for each new robot and task. This requirement limits their practical applicability, particularly in dynamic industrial or real-world settings where rapid adaptation is crucial.

The broader research landscape has seen increasing efforts to develop methods that enable robots to reuse learned skills rather than initiating training from scratch. This drive is motivated by the desire for computational efficiency, reduced development time, and enhanced sustainability in robotic systems. The concept of "sustainable learning" in robotics, where data and learned policies are leveraged across platforms, aligns with the principles of Industry 4.0, which emphasizes adaptable and intelligent manufacturing systems.

Prior work in this area has explored various facets of skill transfer, including:
- **Cross-embodiment transfer:** Learning from aggregated, heterogeneous datasets collected from multiple robot platforms to improve policy generalization, even for robots with different morphologies and control schemes. Examples include using vision-based multi-robot datasets or global policy models to adapt across simulations and hardware.
- **Meta-learning frameworks:** Employing probabilistic meta-learning to adapt policies to new robotic hardware, accounting for variations in control schemes, camera perspectives, kinematics, and end-effector designs.
- **Generalist policies:** Developing single policies capable of operating across multiple robots despite hardware differences, or using shared modular policies to improve cross-platform generalization.
- **Adaptive transfer methods:** Encoding state-action mappings across different domains and utilizing techniques like modular policy networks or cycle generative networks to facilitate skill adaptation between platforms.

This paper contributes to this landscape by specifically investigating policy transfer for a precise manipulation task (peg-in-hole) between two distinct robotic arms (UR5e and Panda) in a simulated environment. It evaluates zero-shot transfer and fine-tuning as adaptation techniques, providing empirical data on their effectiveness in terms of success rates, execution times, and sample efficiency. The study highlights the practical implications of such transfer mechanisms for reducing retraining efforts and supporting sustainable robotic learning, positioning itself as an empirical study within the broader effort to achieve robust and efficient multi-robot skill transfer.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate the feasibility and effectiveness of transferring learned robot skills, specifically control policies, between different robotic platforms. This investigation centers on a precise manipulation task, the peg-in-hole insertion, using reinforcement learning.

The key motivations behind this study are multifaceted:

1. **Addressing Computational Inefficiency of Traditional RL:** Conventional reinforcement learning methods often require substantial training time and vast amounts of data to achieve optimal performance for each new robot and task. This computational burden renders them resource-intensive and impractical for many real-world industrial and research applications. The paper aims to explore methods that mitigate this inefficiency.
2. **Promoting Sustainable Robotic Learning:** The ability to reuse learned skills and data across different systems, rather than continually retraining from scratch, contributes to the sustainability of robotic development. This approach reduces the carbon footprint associated with extensive computational training and optimizes resource utilization in robotics research and deployment.
3. **Enhancing Sample Efficiency:** A core motivation is to improve the sample efficiency of robotic learning. By transferring a pre-trained policy, it is hypothesized that the target robot can achieve high performance with significantly fewer training interactions (time-steps) compared to learning the skill independently. This is particularly relevant given that RL methods often demand millions of time-steps for convergence.
4. **Enabling Adaptability for Industry 4.0:** In the context of Industry 4.0, intelligent systems are expected to adapt seamlessly to new environments, tasks, and hardware configurations. Skill transfer is identified as a critical enabler for this adaptability, allowing robots to rapidly integrate into diverse manufacturing scenarios without extensive re-programming or re-training.
5. **Evaluating Policy Generalization Across Platforms:** The study seeks to empirically evaluate how well a policy trained on one robot (source domain) can generalize to another robot (target domain) with different kinematics, dynamics, and control characteristics. This includes assessing performance under zero-shot transfer conditions and with minimal adaptation through fine-tuning.
6. **Investigating Specific Adaptation Techniques:** The paper is specifically motivated to explore policy adaptation through fine-tuning of the neural network parameters in a few-shot learning manner. This approach is posited as a sample-efficient alternative to full retraining, aiming to identify its practical benefits in enhancing skill transfer.
7. **Determining Optimal Controller Parameters:** For the peg-in-hole task, the robot needs to determine an optimal path and apply precise forces. The motivation includes using RL to autonomously adjust hybrid motion-force controller parameters, thus improving robustness and reducing manual intervention required for traditional PID tuning.

In summary, the research aims to demonstrate that adaptive policy transfer is a viable and beneficial strategy for developing more efficient, sustainable, and adaptable robotic systems, particularly for complex manipulation tasks requiring precise force and motion control.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

This study employs a methodology that combines hybrid motion and force control with reinforcement learning to achieve adaptive robot skills for the peg-in-hole insertion task, followed by an evaluation of policy transfer scenarios.

#### 3.1. Task Definition: Peg-in-Hole Insertion

The chosen task is the peg-in-hole insertion, a manipulation task requiring precise position and force control. The objective for the robot is to determine an optimal path to locate and insert a peg into a hole, as illustrated in Figure 1 of the paper.

#### 3.2. Hybrid Motion and Force Control

The robots utilize a hybrid motion-force control scheme, which allows simultaneous regulation of motion and force. This is critical for tasks requiring interaction with the environment, such as peg-in-hole insertion. The controller is defined by Equation (1):
$u_{\text{hybrid}}(t) = S(K_{x_p} x_e + K_{x_d} \dot{x_e}) + (I - S)(K_{f_p} F_e + K_{f_i} \int F_e dt)$
- $x_e$, $\dot{x_e}$, and $F_e$ represent the position, velocity, and force errors, respectively.
- $S$ is a selection matrix (e.g., $diag([1\ 1\ 0])$), which dictates which degrees of freedom are controlled by motion (e.g., x and y axes) and which by force (e.g., z-axis). Rotational degrees of freedom are not implemented in this study.
- $K_{x_p}$, $K_{x_d}$, $K_{f_p}$, and $K_{f_i}$ are the proportional and derivative gains for motion control and proportional and integral gains for force control, respectively.
Reinforcement learning is employed to autonomously adjust these PID-like gains, moving beyond traditional manual or model-based tuning to enhance adaptability.

#### 3.3. Reinforcement Learning (RL) Framework

- **RL Algorithm:** Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) is utilized. SAC is a model-free, off-policy reinforcement learning algorithm designed for continuous control tasks. It operates within an actor-critic framework and incorporates the maximum entropy concept to encourage exploration and robust policy learning.
- **RL Agent's Output (Action Space):** The RL policy maps the current state to an action space of $\mathbb{R}^9$. Specifically, it predicts three components:
 1. Optimal position adjustment, $\hat{x}_a$.
 2. Proportional gain for motion control, $K_{x_p}$.
 3. Proportional gain for force control, $K_{f_p}$.
 The remaining controller parameters are fixed relative to these predictions: $K_{x_d} = 0.5 K_{x_p}$ and $K_{f_i} = 0.001 K_{f_p}$.
- **Resultant Control Command:** The predicted optimal position $\hat{x}_a$ is combined with the output of the hybrid controller ($u_{\text{hybrid}}(t)$) to generate the final position control command, $x_c(t)$, as defined by Equation (2):
 $x_c(t) = \hat{x}_a + u_{\text{hybrid}}(t)$
- **Reward Function:** The reward function is designed to incorporate both long-term and short-term feedback:
 - **Sparse (Long-term) Reward, $r_{\text{sparse}}(t)$ (Equation 3):**
 - $+100$ for task completion.
 - $-5$ for collision occurrence.
 - $-5$ for reaching maximum time-steps without completion.
 - **Dense (Short-term) Reward, $r_{\text{dense}}(t)$ (Equation 4):**
 $r_{\text{dense}}(t) = \alpha_1 \|x_g - x_m\|^2 + \alpha_2 \|F_g - F_m\|^2$
 - This component measures the Euclidean norm of the difference between the current position/force ($x_m$, $F_m$) and the goal position/force ($x_g$, $F_g$).
 - $x_g$ is defined as the hole position, and $F_g$ is defined as zero force.
 - $\alpha_1$ and $\alpha_2$ are weighting factors.

#### 3.4. Simulation Setup

- **Hardware and Software:** The experiments were conducted on a system running Ubuntu 20.04.6 with ROS 1 Noetic, equipped with an AMD Ryzen 7 3700X 8-core CPU and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 GPU.
- **Simulation Engine:** MuJoCo (Multi-Joint dynamics with Contact) was used as the physics engine for robot simulations.
- **Robotic Platforms:** Two distinct robotic arms were used as test platforms: the Universal Robots UR5e and the Franka Emika Panda. Both robots were simulated with wrist-mounted force/torque sensors.
- **RL Implementation:** Stable-Baselines3, a reliable reinforcement learning library, was used for the RL framework.
- **Policy Model Architecture:** The neural network for the policy (actor) consisted of two layers, each with 64 neurons. The critic network had two hidden layers with 300 and 400 neurons, respectively.
- **Control and Learning Frequencies:** The robot control frequency was up to 60 Hz, while the RL learning frequency was 20 Hz.
- **Training and Evaluation:** Each robot (UR5e and Panda) was trained from scratch until its mean reward per episode stabilized, and the optimal policy models were saved.
- **Experiment Scenarios for Policy Transfer (Table 1):** Five distinct scenarios were evaluated, each consisting of 100 episodes with fixed and predefined starting points to ensure fair comparison:
 1. **Testing UR5e model:** UR5e policy trained on UR5e.
 2. **Testing Panda model:** Panda policy trained on Panda.
 3. **Testing Panda using UR5e model (zero-shot):** UR5e pre-trained policy applied directly to Panda without further training.
 4. **Testing UR5e using Panda model (zero-shot):** Panda pre-trained policy applied directly to UR5e without further training.
 5. **Testing Panda using UR5e model (fine-tuning):** UR5e pre-trained policy fine-tuned on the Panda robot for a limited number of time-steps.
- **Evaluation Metrics:** The primary performance metrics were success rate (percentage of completed tasks) and average time-steps per episode.

This comprehensive methodology allows for a systematic evaluation of skill transfer under various conditions, assessing both the baseline performance of trained-from-scratch policies and the efficiency gains offered by zero-shot transfer and fine-tuning.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental results provide insights into the performance of individual robots and the effectiveness of policy transfer mechanisms for the peg-in-hole task. The key metrics analyzed were success rate and average time-steps per episode.

#### 4.1. Baseline Performance (Training from Scratch)

- **UR5e Model:** When trained and tested on the UR5e platform, the policy achieved a 100% success rate, completing the task with an average of 126 time-steps per episode. This indicates that the policy was effectively optimized for its native platform.
- **Panda Model:** The Panda robot, when trained from scratch on its own platform, achieved a 97.98% success rate. It completed the task with a slightly faster average of 117 time-steps per episode compared to the UR5e.

#### 4.2. Zero-Shot Policy Transfer

- **Panda using UR5e Model (Scenario 3):** When the pre-trained UR5e policy was directly applied to the Panda robot without any adaptation (zero-shot transfer), the success rate decreased to 78.79%. Concurrently, the average task execution time increased to 170 time-steps per episode. This suggests that while the policy retains some generalizability across platforms, significant configuration differences between the UR5e and Panda robots hinder optimal performance, leading to lower reliability and longer execution.
- **UR5e using Panda Model (Scenario 4):** Similarly, applying the pre-trained Panda policy to the UR5e robot in a zero-shot manner also resulted in a performance drop. The success rate for the UR5e decreased to 93.94%, and the average time-steps per episode increased to 134. This further supports the observation that direct zero-shot transfer without adaptation leads to reduced performance, although the drop was less severe than for Panda using UR5e.

#### 4.3. Policy Transfer with Fine-tuning

- **Panda using UR5e Model with Fine-tuning (Scenario 5):** The introduction of a fine-tuning process for the Panda robot, starting from the pre-trained UR5e policy, led to a substantial improvement in performance. The success rate increased to 97.98%, matching the performance of the Panda model trained from scratch. The average number of time-steps per episode was reduced to 135, which is notably lower than the 170 time-steps observed in the zero-shot transfer scenario and closer to the original 117 time-steps of the natively trained Panda. This result indicates that adaptive techniques like fine-tuning effectively bridge the domain gap between robots, enhancing both reliability and execution efficiency.

#### 4.4. Training Efficiency

- **UR5e Convergence:** The UR5e model converged to an average episode reward above 50 within approximately 200,000 time-steps.
- **Panda Convergence:** In contrast, the Panda model required over 1 million time-steps to reach a stable average reward when trained from scratch. This highlights a significant difference in sample efficiency between the two robots when learning the task independently.
- **Panda with Fine-tuning Convergence:** Although explicit convergence time for fine-tuning is not provided in detail for Panda, the rapid improvement in performance metrics after fine-tuning (as shown in Figure 5) and the implication from the text ("fine-tuning significantly improves performance with fewer training time-steps") suggest that it requires considerably fewer time-steps than training the Panda from scratch to achieve comparable performance. Figure 6, comparing mean episode reward during training, visually reinforces that fine-tuning enables the Panda to reach a higher reward plateau much faster than training from scratch.

In summary, the results confirm that direct zero-shot policy transfer without adaptation generally leads to degraded performance and increased task execution times due to inherent differences between robotic platforms. However, incorporating adaptation techniques, such as fine-tuning, significantly improves the success rate and sample efficiency, allowing a target robot to achieve performance comparable to a natively trained policy with fewer training iterations. This underscores the importance of adaptation mechanisms in practical policy transfer applications.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of robotic learning by empirically demonstrating the feasibility and benefits of sustainable transfer learning for adaptive robot skills. The findings have several significant implications and potential impacts:

1. **Enhanced Sample Efficiency and Reduced Training Costs:** The most direct impact is the demonstration that fine-tuning a pre-trained policy can significantly improve sample efficiency compared to training a robot skill from scratch. The observation that the Panda robot required over 1 million time-steps for native training but achieved comparable performance with fine-tuning (starting from a UR5e policy) implies substantial reductions in computational resources, energy consumption, and development time. This efficiency is critical for accelerating the deployment of robotic systems in industrial and research settings where extensive retraining is impractical.

2. **Promotion of Sustainable Robotics:** By enabling the reuse of learned policies across different robotic platforms, the work aligns with the principles of sustainable artificial intelligence and robotics. Reducing the need for repetitive, resource-intensive training processes contributes to a lower carbon footprint associated with robot development and operation, making robotic solutions more environmentally responsible.

3. **Improved Generalization and Adaptability for Industry 4.0:** The ability to transfer and adapt skills across diverse robotic embodiments is a key requirement for the flexible and intelligent manufacturing systems envisioned by Industry 4.0. This research demonstrates a method for robots to adapt to new tasks or hardware with minimal additional training, enhancing their versatility and operational flexibility in dynamic production environments. This can lead to faster setup times for new production lines or product variations.

4. **Foundation for Robust Multi-Robot Systems:** The findings suggest that a robust policy can be developed on one platform and then quickly adapted to other platforms. This forms a foundation for creating multi-robot systems where a common knowledge base of skills can be efficiently distributed and customized, rather than each robot operating in isolation.

5. **Guidance for Future Transfer Learning Strategies:** The comparison between zero-shot transfer and fine-tuning provides clear guidance: while zero-shot transfer can offer a baseline, practical deployment for tasks requiring high precision often necessitates adaptation. Fine-tuning emerges as an effective strategy for bridging performance gaps arising from differences in robot kinematics, dynamics, and control characteristics.

6. **Paving the Way for Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The paper explicitly states that future work will focus on transferring the UR5e policy from simulation to real-world execution with fine-tuning. This highlights the potential of the demonstrated methodology to facilitate the notoriously challenging sim-to-real gap, where policies learned in simulation are deployed on physical robots. Successful sim-to-real transfer with fine-tuning could unlock rapid development and deployment of complex robotic skills without the need for extensive, often hazardous, real-world training.

In conclusion, this research provides empirical evidence supporting the value of adaptive policy transfer in robotics. Its significance lies in its direct contributions to improving efficiency, promoting sustainability, and enhancing the adaptability of robotic systems, with a clear roadmap for extending these benefits to real-world applications.
