# A Minimalist Approach to LLM Reasoning: from Rejection Sampling to Reinforce

> [!summary] Summary
> The paper empirically investigates reinforcement learning algorithms for fine-tuning Large Language Models on mathematical reasoning, demonstrating that simple rejection sampling methods perform comparably to complex RL algorithms like GRPO, whose effectiveness is primarily attributed to implicit data filtering rather than its reward normalization.

## Problem

- Complex Reinforcement Learning (RL) algorithms like PPO introduce significant computational overhead and algorithmic complexity for LLM fine-tuning.
- The empirical success of Gradient Regularized Policy Optimization (GRPO) in LLM training, particularly for mathematical reasoning, lacked a clear understanding of its underlying mechanisms.
- Uncertainty existed regarding whether simpler, positive-only RL approaches like Reward-ranked Fine-tuning (RAFT) could effectively compete with or outperform more complex RL methods that utilize negative feedback.

## Method

- The authors formally analyzed and compared existing RL algorithms (RAFT, Reinforce, GRPO) and proposed ==RAFT++==, an improved rejection sampling method.
- They performed systematic ablation studies to disentangle the contributions of GRPO's components, specifically focusing on ==reward normalization== and ==sample filtering==.
- Based on these insights, a new lightweight algorithm, ==Reinforce-Rej==, was developed, which incorporates targeted data filtering for improved stability and performance.

## Results

- RAFT++ achieved **56.1%** average accuracy on mathematical reasoning benchmarks for Qwen2.5-Math-7B-base, closely matching GRPO's **56.3%** and outperforming iterative DPO (**48.8%**).
- Ablation studies revealed that filtering prompts with entirely incorrect responses was the most impactful component of GRPO, leading to significant performance gains, while reward normalization had minimal effect.
- The proposed Reinforce-Rej method, which filters both all-correct and all-incorrect samples, demonstrated comparable final performance to GRPO with improved KL efficiency and controlled entropy behavior.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Simple, positive-only fine-tuning methods (like RAFT and RAFT++) can achieve performance comparable to or better than more complex RL algorithms, challenging the necessity of negative signals.
> - The primary driver of GRPO's effectiveness is its implicit filtering of problematic samples (specifically, those where all generated responses are incorrect), rather than its sophisticated reward normalization.
> - Training on prompts where all sampled responses are incorrect ("harmful prompts") can actively degrade model performance, highlighting the importance of intelligent sample selection.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper is situated within the rapidly evolving landscape of fine-tuning large language models (LLMs) to enhance their performance on complex tasks, particularly mathematical reasoning. The dominant paradigm for LLM post-training has been Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), with Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) being the most widely adopted algorithm (e.g., in InstructGPT, ChatGPT). However, the authors highlight that PPO introduces significant computational overhead and algorithmic complexity, especially when applied to the deterministic transition nature of LLMs, where many of PPO's sophisticated components might be unnecessary.

The paper addresses a growing trend in the research community: the exploration of simpler yet effective RL algorithms for LLM fine-tuning. This includes a resurgence of interest in Reinforce-style approaches like ReMax, RLOO, GRPO, and Reinforce++, which aim to reduce the complexity associated with PPO's critic network. Concurrently, other methods like Reward-ranked Fine-tuning (RAFT) and Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) have emerged as alternatives, focusing on different mechanisms of leveraging feedback, from direct preference comparisons to simple rejection sampling.

Specifically, the paper zeroes in on GRPO (Gradient Regularized Policy Optimization), which has gained prominence due to its empirical success in training models like DeepSeek-R1 for mathematical reasoning. Despite its widespread adoption, the algorithmic details and fundamental sources of GRPO's effectiveness have remained "poorly understood." This lack of justification is a critical gap that the authors aim to fill, especially given that many projects adopt GRPO as a default without deep scrutiny.

In contrast, RAFT, a minimalist rejection sampling baseline that trains only on positively rewarded samples, has consistently shown strong empirical performance. This sets up a crucial comparison: is the complexity of RL methods leveraging negative signals truly superior to simpler, positive-only approaches like RAFT? The paper challenges the common belief that negative signals are universally beneficial, observing that the performance gap between positive-only (RAFT) and methods using negative signals (GRPO, PPO) is surprisingly small, and RAFT even exhibits faster early-stage convergence.

Furthermore, the work connects to the broader theme of data filtering in LLM post-training. While existing works have explored strategies like discarding noise in pairwise comparisons or removing overly easy/hard prompts, this paper uniquely investigates *online* filtering of samples during the RL training process itself. By dissecting GRPO, the authors uncover an implicit data filtering mechanism as a core driver of its success, proposing a principled extension of this insight. This work contributes to a deeper understanding of what truly matters in RL-based LLM fine-tuning, shifting emphasis from complex algorithmic structures to intelligent data selection.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this research are to conduct a thorough investigation into reinforcement learning algorithms for fine-tuning Large Language Models (LLMs) on verifiable reward-based tasks, with a particular focus on mathematical reasoning. The authors are motivated by several key observations and unanswered questions in the current LLM post-training landscape:

1. **Demystifying GRPO:** GRPO has achieved empirical success, notably in training DeepSeek-R1, but its underlying mechanisms and the reasons for its effectiveness are "poorly understood" and "largely undocumented." The authors aim to analyze GRPO's core components to ascertain whether its adoption stems from inherent algorithmic advantages or continuity with previous practices. This involves understanding what specific elements contribute to its performance gains over simpler policy gradient methods.

2. **Revisiting Minimalist Baselines:** The paper seeks to rigorously evaluate the performance of simple, interpretable baselines like Reward-ranked Fine-tuning (RAFT), also known as rejection sampling. Despite its minimalistic design, RAFT has shown competitive empirical performance. A key motivation is to challenge the prevailing belief that complex RL methods, especially those leveraging negative signals, inherently outperform simpler, SFT-like algorithms that rely only on positive samples. The authors observed a "surprisingly small" performance gap and even faster early convergence for RAFT, prompting deeper investigation into this phenomenon.

3. **Understanding the Role of Negative Samples and Reward Normalization:** A central motivation is to discern the precise impact of negative samples and reward normalization techniques (like mean and standard deviation adjustment, as used in GRPO) on LLM training. Is incorporating *all* negative signals always beneficial, or can certain types of negative feedback be detrimental? Does reward normalization contribute significantly to stability or performance, or are other factors more crucial? The preliminary observation that "certain types of negative signals... can significantly hurt model performance" fuels this inquiry.

4. **Developing Simpler and More Effective Algorithms:** Based on the insights gained from analyzing RAFT and GRPO, the authors aim to propose "minimalist extensions" or variants of existing algorithms that retain effectiveness while reducing complexity. This goal is exemplified by the development of Reinforce-Rej, a lightweight alternative designed to improve KL efficiency and stability.

In essence, the paper is driven by a desire to bring clarity and simplicity to the often complex and opaque world of RL for LLMs. By dissecting existing successful methods and rigorously testing minimalist alternatives, the authors seek to provide concrete guidance for future research, advocating for more principled designs in incorporating feedback signals, particularly negative ones.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors adopt a systematic and empirical approach to investigate and compare various RL algorithms for LLM post-training. Their methodology involves careful formalization of algorithms, comprehensive experimental setups, and detailed ablation studies to isolate the impact of different design choices.

**Algorithmic Formalization:**
The paper begins by formally defining the core algorithms under investigation, emphasizing their distinct features:
- **RAFT (Rejection Sampling Fine-Tuning):** Described as a three-step process: data collection (sampling `n` responses per prompt), data ranking (retaining only responses with highest reward, typically r=1), and model fine-tuning (maximizing log-likelihood on selected positive samples). It's positioned as the "most basic RL algorithm" for LLM post-training.
- **Policy Gradient and Reinforce:** The general policy gradient objective is introduced, followed by its practical implementation using importance sampling for off-policy updates from a replay buffer. The PPO-style clipping technique is also incorporated to stabilize training and manage variance, resulting in the Reinforce loss function. The autoregressive nature of LLMs is handled by extending the loss to the token level.
- **GRPO:** This variant builds on the Reinforce loss but introduces an advantage function for token-level updates. The key distinction is its use of within-prompt reward normalization: `(r_i - mean(r_1...,r_n)) / std(r_1...,r_n)`. This normalization is a central point of investigation for the paper.
- **Iterative DPO:** Briefly described as optimizing a contrastive loss based on pairwise comparisons, with the iterative variant leveraging self-generated on-policy data. While not a primary focus of ablation, it serves as a strong baseline.
- **RAFT++:** A novel extension proposed by the authors, applying importance sampling and clipping techniques (similar to Reinforce) to the original RAFT objective. The core idea is to train only on samples with the highest reward, but with the distributional correction and stability of policy gradient methods.

**Experiment Setup:**
- **Task Domain:** Mathematical reasoning, a challenging domain requiring multi-step logical inference and verifiable answers.
- **Implementation Framework:** `verl` (Sheng et al., 2024), an RLHF framework.
- **Dataset:** Numina-Math, a large collection (approx. 860k) of math problems ranging from high school exercises to Olympiad-level competitions, with ground-truth answers for reward verification.
- **Base Models:** Qwen2.5-Math-7B-base and LLaMA-3.2-3B-instruct. Using two different model families ensures the generality of findings.
- **Prompting Strategy:** Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting ("Let’s think step by step and output the final answer within \boxed{}") is used to encourage explicit reasoning.
- **Hyperparameters:** Consistent application of AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of 1e-6. For data collection, 1024 prompts are sampled per iteration, and `n=4` responses are generated per prompt for RAFT and GRPO. Mini-batch size is 512, and models can generate up to 4096 tokens. The authors state that all algorithms were tuned to their best performance.
- **Evaluation Metrics:** Performance is measured on three standard mathematical reasoning benchmarks: Math500, Minerva Math, and Olympiad Bench. The primary metric is `average@16` accuracy, where 16 responses are generated per prompt with a temperature of 1.0, and the average accuracy is reported. This robust metric accounts for the stochasticity of LLM generation.

**Ablation Studies:**
The core of the methodology lies in the detailed ablation studies designed to disentangle the effects of GRPO's components and understand the dynamics of RAFT.
- **RAFT++ vs. GRPO/Reinforce:** Policy entropy and KL divergence from the initial policy are tracked to understand the learning dynamics and potential for "entropy collapse" in positive-only training. The effect of "clip higher" (asymmetric clipping) is also tested to mitigate entropy decline.
- **Dissecting GRPO:** A series of controlled experiments are conducted using variants of Reinforce to isolate the impact of different components:
 - Vanilla Reinforce (baseline).
 - Reinforce + Mean Zero (subtracts mean reward, a common baseline technique).
 - Reinforce + Remove all correct (filters prompts where all `n` responses are correct).
 - Reinforce + Remove all wrong (filters prompts where all `n` responses are incorrect).
 - Reinforce + Remove both (filters prompts with *either* all correct *or* all incorrect responses, leading to "Reinforce-Rej").
 - Reinforce + Remove both + Normalized Std (adds standard deviation normalization on top of filtering, mimicking GRPO's full advantage function but with the proposed filtering).

This rigorous experimental design allows the authors to systematically evaluate hypotheses about the effectiveness of various RL components in the context of LLM fine-tuning.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several surprising and insightful findings regarding the effectiveness of various RL algorithms for LLM post-training on mathematical reasoning tasks:

1. **RAFT and RAFT++ Competitiveness:**
 - A significant finding is that RAFT, a simple rejection sampling baseline that only trains on positively rewarded samples, achieves performance competitive with, and in some cases surpasses, more complex state-of-the-art RL methods like iterative DPO and PPO. For Qwen2.5-Math-7B-base, vanilla RAFT achieved 52.3% average accuracy, outperforming iterative DPO (48.8%) and approaching PPO (52.5%).
 - The authors' proposed variant, RAFT++, which incorporates importance sampling and clipping (similar to policy gradient methods but still positive-only), further improves performance, reaching 56.1% average accuracy for Qwen2.5-Math-7B-base. This is remarkably close to GRPO's 56.3%, demonstrating RAFT++ as a near state-of-the-art method despite its conceptual simplicity. Similar trends were observed with the LLaMA model, confirming robustness.
 - The authors highlight that the common belief that RL methods leveraging negative signals "significantly outperform SFT-like algorithms that only use positive samples" is challenged by these results.

2. **Learning Dynamics of RAFT++:**
 - RAFT++ exhibits faster early-stage convergence compared to GRPO. However, its growth rate slows noticeably after approximately 100-200 iterations, and it is eventually surpassed by GRPO in later training stages.
 - Further analysis reveals that RAFT++ (which trains exclusively on positive samples) experiences a much more rapid decline in policy entropy and a faster increase in KL divergence from the initial policy. This suggests that without negative samples, the policy quickly becomes low-entropy, limiting exploration and leading to a performance plateau.
 - The "clip higher" technique (asymmetric clipping) was found to help stabilize policy entropy and allowed RAFT++ to maintain performance longer, further supporting the idea that maintaining exploration is crucial.

3. **Ablation Study on GRPO's Effectiveness:**
 - **Harmful Prompts:** The most impactful finding from the ablation study is that GRPO's primary advantage over vanilla Reinforce stems from its *implicit filtering of prompts where all sampled responses are entirely incorrect*. The variant "Reinforce + Remove all wrong" showed the most significant performance improvement, clearly indicating that training on these "incorrect samples" can "significantly harm performance" due to high variance and potentially misleading gradients.
 - **Minor Role of Reward Normalization:** In contrast, reward normalization techniques (mean and standard deviation normalization, which are defining features of GRPO's advantage function) were found to have minimal impact. "Reinforce + Mean Zero" increased KL divergence and did not improve reward, suggesting instability. Adding standard deviation normalization ("Reinforce + Remove both + Normalize Std") yielded little additional gain over just removing bad samples. This directly challenges the assumption that GRPO's normalization is its key strength.
 - **Reinforce-Rej (Reinforce + Remove both):** Motivated by these insights, the authors proposed "Reinforce-Rej," which filters out prompts with *either* all correct *or* all incorrect responses. This method achieved comparable final performance to GRPO while demonstrating superior KL efficiency and better-behaved entropy loss, balancing exploration and exploitation effectively. The intuition is that prompts with all correct samples provide diminishing returns for improvement, while prompts with all incorrect samples are actively harmful.

In summary, the results highlight that sample selection, particularly the intelligent filtering of harmful or uninformative samples, is a more critical factor for performance in reward-based LLM post-training than complex algorithmic designs or sophisticated reward normalization schemes.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research offers significant insights and potential impacts across several dimensions of LLM fine-tuning and reinforcement learning:

1. **Challenging RL Dogma and Simplifying LLM Training:** The most profound impact is the direct challenge to the common belief that all negative feedback is inherently beneficial in RL for LLMs. By showing that a minimalist, positive-only approach (RAFT/RAFT++) is highly competitive and that complex normalization in GRPO is less impactful than its implicit data filtering, the paper advocates for a more nuanced understanding of reward signals. This simplifies the LLM post-training landscape by demonstrating that effective results can be achieved with less algorithmic complexity, potentially lowering the barrier to entry for researchers and practitioners.

2. **Redefining the Role of Negative Samples:** The findings strongly suggest that the *quality* and *selection* of negative samples are far more crucial than their mere presence. The detrimental effect of prompts with entirely incorrect responses highlights the need for "more principled designs for incorporating negative samples," moving away from indiscriminate use. This shifts the research focus from designing intricate advantage functions to intelligent data curation and filtering mechanisms.

3. **Providing Robust and Interpretable Baselines:** The paper champions RAFT and its improved variant RAFT++ as "robust and interpretable baselines." Their strong empirical performance, despite their simplicity, makes them excellent candidates for future comparisons and practical applications. Similarly, Reinforce-Rej is proposed as a "lightweight yet effective alternative" to more complex RL algorithms, offering a strong balance between performance and simplicity. These baselines can accelerate research by providing clear benchmarks and reduce the computational burden often associated with PPO-like methods.

4. **Demystifying GRPO's Success:** The paper provides a clear, evidence-based explanation for the empirical success of GRPO, widely used in models like DeepSeek-R1. By attributing its effectiveness primarily to the implicit discarding of problematic samples (specifically, entirely incorrect ones) rather than its reward normalization, the research offers critical transparency. This understanding can guide future development of GRPO-like algorithms, allowing researchers to focus on the truly effective components.

5. **Guiding Future Research Directions:** The conclusion explicitly guides future work towards "more principled designs for incorporating negative samples" and intelligent sample selection. This could spur research into dynamic data filtering, curriculum learning based on sample difficulty, or more sophisticated methods for identifying "harmful" or "uninformative" negative examples in online training. The findings on entropy collapse in RAFT++ also highlight the importance of exploration and maintaining policy diversity, suggesting future avenues for combining simple positive-only learning with mechanisms that encourage exploration (e.g., through specific regularization or clever sample generation strategies).

6. **Practical Implications for LLM Deployment:** For practitioners, the insights mean that highly effective LLM fine-tuning might not always require heavy-duty RL frameworks. Simple, well-tuned rejection sampling (RAFT++) or a filtered policy gradient approach (Reinforce-Rej) could yield comparable results with less computational overhead and greater training stability. This can lead to more efficient and cost-effective deployment of specialized LLMs for reasoning tasks.

The open-sourcing of the project's code (https://github.com/RLHFlow/Minimal-RL) further amplifies its potential impact by facilitating replication, extension, and broader adoption of these minimalist yet powerful approaches. This transparency is crucial for fostering collective progress in the rapidly evolving field of LLM research.
