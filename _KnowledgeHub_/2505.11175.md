# Real-Time Verification of Embodied Reasoning for Generative Skill Acquisition

> [!summary] Summary
> VERGSA is a framework that integrates real-time verification into generative skill acquisition for embodied AI by formalizing embodied reasoning and automating reward labeling. It achieves a 24% increase in Average Task Success Rate for novel tasks and a 36% increase for encountered tasks on a baseline model, while also significantly reducing the number of subtasks needed to complete tasks.

## Problem

- Generative skill acquisition in complex 3D embodied environments is computationally inefficient due to extensive evaluation of candidate solutions.
- Embodied agents get limited guidance for novel tasks, struggling with scene configuration, task decomposition, and success metrics, since verification principles proven in mathematics are hard to apply to less-structured embodied reasoning.
- Traditional skill learning methods rely on arduous and unscalable manual reward engineering for labeling intermediate steps.

## Method

- VERGSA integrates real-time verification via a dynamic, task-agnostic "Exemplar Task Pool" that supplies contextually relevant tasks to the LLM policy model, improving its scene-configuration and subtask-supervision generation, plus granular success metrics for subtasks and goals.
- An automated, scalable reward labeling scheme (==ARLET-MCTS==) is introduced to synthesize dense reward signals from execution feedback, eliminating manual reward engineering.
- A fine-tuned Process Reward Model (PRM) acts as a critic, verifying and selecting the most promising scene configurations and subtask supervisions generated by the policy model.

## Results

- The exemplar task pool improved the Average Task Success Rate (ATSR) by **21%** (from **0.53** to **0.74**) and reduced the average number of subtasks from **17.8** to **9.0** for a baseline LLM.
- The Process Reward Model (PRM) increased the ATSR of the baseline model by **24%** for novel tasks (from **0.67** to **0.91**) and by **36%** for encountered tasks (from **0.56** to **0.92**).
- A dedicated PRM verifier consistently outperformed LLM-as-a-Judge baselines, achieving an ATSR of **0.91** in task-based splitting compared to the best LLM-as-a-Judge at **0.78**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Dynamic contextual guidance from an exemplar task pool significantly improves the efficiency and success rate of generative skill acquisition, leading to more concise task decompositions.
> - Real-time verification through a dedicated, task-specific fine-tuned Process Reward Model (PRM) reliably filters and selects superior solutions, outperforming generalist LLMs used as judges for embodied reasoning.
> - Automating reward labeling using an adapted Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) is a feasible and crucial strategy for scalable skill acquisition in embodied AI, eliminating the need for extensive manual supervision.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated within the field of Embodied Artificial Intelligence (AI), specifically addressing challenges in generative skill acquisition for embodied agents. Generative skill acquisition enables agents to autonomously learn and expand their repertoire of control skills, which is considered essential for the scalability and adaptability of large decision models.

Prior work in this domain has often relied on supervision signals, frequently derived from generalist agents such as Large Language Models (LLMs). However, applying these methods to complex 3D environments presents challenges, particularly regarding the efficiency and practicality of evaluating candidate solutions. Exhaustive evaluation of every potential skill acquisition pathway is computationally expensive and time-consuming, hindering the learning process. Additionally, these existing approaches offer limited inherent guidance for agents encountering novel tasks.

The paper draws inspiration from advancements in verification models, which have demonstrated effectiveness in mathematical problem-solving. In that context, verification models provide a mechanism to assess the correctness of intermediate reasoning steps or final answers, facilitating the self-improvement of LLMs. This paper identifies a gap: the principles of verification models, particularly for multi-step reasoning, have largely been unexplored in the context of embodied reasoning for skill acquisition. Embodied tasks introduce unique complexities compared to mathematical problems, such as their less structured nature, the need for scene configuration, and the challenges in defining success metrics for skill learning.

By proposing VERGSA (Verifying Embodied Reasoning in Generative Skill Acquisition), this work aims to bridge this gap. It seeks to integrate real-time verification into embodied skill learning, offering a systematic framework to validate scene configurations, task decompositions, training supervisions, and success indicators. This positions the research as an effort to enhance the efficiency, robustness, and scalability of generative skill acquisition by applying verification methodologies, which have proven valuable in other complex reasoning domains, to the specific challenges of embodied AI.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to optimize generative skill acquisition by integrating real-time verification models into embodied tasks. This overarching goal is driven by several motivations related to the limitations of current approaches in embodied AI:

1. **Addressing Computational Inefficiency in Skill Learning:** Current methods for generative skill acquisition in complex 3D environments often require extensive evaluation of candidate solutions. Iterating through every possible skill acquisition pathway or candidate solution is computationally prohibitive and time-inefficient. This substantial cost significantly impedes the learning process, particularly as the complexity and diversity of tasks increase. The motivation is to reduce this cost by intelligently filtering or prioritizing promising solutions.

2. **Enhancing Guidance for Novel Tasks:** Existing approaches frequently provide limited inherent guidance for embodied agents when confronted with entirely novel tasks. Without effective mechanisms to assess the quality or potential success of generated scene configurations or subtask supervisions, agents may struggle to learn efficiently or generalize to new scenarios. The objective is to provide a structured form of supervision that can guide the learning process even for tasks not previously encountered.

3. **Adapting Verification Principles to Embodied Contexts:** The paper acknowledges the success of verification models in more structured domains, such as mathematical reasoning. However, embodied tasks present distinct characteristics:
 - **Less Structured Nature:** Unlike symbolic mathematical problems, embodied tasks require dynamic scene configuration, adaptive decomposition of long-term goals into coherent subtasks, and generation of specific training supervisions and success metrics. The motivation is to extend the concept of verification to these less structured elements.
 - **Verification Challenges:** Evaluating the success of skill learning in embodied tasks is complex, requiring an external mechanism to observe and assess performance against dynamic success criteria. This differs from mathematical problems, which often have definitive, directly verifiable answers. The objective is to establish robust, automated methods for assessing the quality of intermediate steps and overall task completion in embodied environments.

4. **Eliminating Arduous Manual Reward Engineering:** Traditional methods for training agents in complex tasks often rely on carefully engineered reward functions or extensive human annotation to label intermediate steps. This process is time-consuming, expensive, and difficult to scale across diverse tasks and domains. A key motivation is to develop an automated and scalable reward labeling scheme that can synthesize dense reward signals without requiring significant manual intervention. This aims to simplify the creation of training datasets for verification models in embodied AI.

In summary, the research is motivated by the need to make generative skill acquisition in embodied AI more efficient, scalable, and adaptable, particularly by leveraging the power of verification models to provide real-time, structured feedback and automate the creation of training data.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework, Verifying Embodied Reasoning in Generative Skill Acquisition (VERGSA), systematically integrates real-time verification into embodied skill learning. It consists of several interconnected components designed to address the challenges of structured reasoning, scalable reward labeling, and robust skill acquisition.

#### 3.1. VERGSA Framework Overview

VERGSA's approach is twofold:
1. **Seamless Extension of Verification to Embodied Learning:** This involves dynamically incorporating contextually relevant tasks into prompts and defining success metrics for both subtasks and overall tasks, bridging the gap between mathematical and embodied reasoning verification.
2. **Automated, Scalable Reward Labeling:** A scheme is introduced to synthesize dense reward signals by iteratively finalizing the contribution of scene configuration and subtask learning to overall skill acquisition, thereby eliminating manual reward engineering.

#### 3.2. Exemplar Task Pool

To address the "less structured" nature of embodied tasks, VERGSA maintains a dynamic pool ($T$) of successfully executed tasks. This pool serves as a source of reference knowledge for the LLM (policy model) when generating solutions for novel tasks.
- **Representation:** For each task $\tau_i \in T$, its name ($n_i$) and description ($d_i$) are concatenated and encoded into an embedding vector ($e_i$) using a pre-trained encoder (e.g., Sentence-BERT).
- **Retrieval:** When a new task (with embedding $e_{new}$) is presented, its similarity to existing tasks in the pool is computed using cosine similarity. The top-K (typically K=2) most similar tasks are retrieved to provide contextual guidance.
- **Dynamic Updating:** Upon successful execution of a novel task, its configuration and supervisions are added to the pool. This mechanism ensures the pool continuously evolves and provides increasingly relevant exemplars.

#### 3.3. Task Specification and Prompts

The input to the policy model (Qwen2.5-Coder-32B-Instruct in experiments) for generating solutions is a comprehensive prompt comprising:
- **Novel Task Specification:** Includes the task name, description, an object articulation tree (detailing links, joints, and semantics, often from PartNetMobility), and the initial task configuration (relevant links and joints, potentially from RoboGen).
- **Reference Knowledge:** The top similar tasks retrieved from the Exemplar Task Pool are included.
- **Available APIs:** A list of permissible primitive APIs provided by the simulated environment.
- **Detailed Instructions:** Guidelines for generating subtask training supervisions and success indicators.
This structured input aims to equip the policy model with sufficient context to decompose the task into subtasks, generate training supervisions for each, and formulate success metrics for early termination.

#### 3.4. Training Supervisions

The policy model breaks down complex, long-horizon tasks into a sequence of shorter-horizon subtasks. For each subtask, the framework generates specific training supervisions. The paper notes two paradigms for subtask policy learning:
- **Reinforcement Learning (RL):** Guided by a reward function, suitable for contact-rich, interactive tasks.
- **Motion Planning:** Driven by an execution function that invokes pre-defined primitive APIs in the simulator, suitable for collision-free navigation.
The system also generates context-dependent success indicators for individual subtasks, allowing for granular evaluation.

#### 3.5. Automated Reward Labeling via MCTS (ARLET-MCTS)

To generate training data for the verification model without extensive human annotation, VERGSA adapts a Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) approach:
- **Base Solutions:** The policy model first generates N base solutions for a given task. Each solution consists of a scene configuration and a sequence of subtask supervisions.
- **Substep Completion:** For each subtask within a base solution, a "completer" (also Qwen2.5-Coder-32B-Instruct) is applied. This completer generates K-1 additional *completed solutions* by finalizing the subsequent subtasks, starting from the current subtask.
- **Execution and Labeling:** Each completed solution is then executed in the embodied environment. The environment returns a Bernoulli outcome (success or failure) for the overall task. A reward label of '1' is assigned to any subtask that is part of a successfully executed *completed solution*, provided that the subtask itself also succeeded according to its generated success metrics. Otherwise, a reward label of '0' is assigned. This process efficiently estimates reward signals from execution feedback, leveraging that success metrics are available for both subtasks and the overall task.
- **Dataset Construction:** By pairing each subtask with its generated training supervision and the derived reward label, a large and diverse dataset is assembled. This dataset facilitates the training of the Process Reward Model.

#### 3.6. Process Reward Model (PRM)

The PRM (implemented with Qwen2.5-1.5B) acts as a "critic" to the policy model, evaluating the quality of generated scene configurations and subtask supervisions.
- **Function:** Given a task specification ($g$) and a sequence of consecutive subtask supervisions ($z_{1:m}$), the PRM assigns a binary reward (0 or 1), representing the estimated success probability of that sequence.
- **Training:** The PRM is trained using binary cross-entropy loss. The target label for a sequence $z_{1:m}$ is determined by the combined success indicators of the m-th subtask ($y_m$) and the overall task ($\hat{y}$) it belongs to. Focal loss is used to mitigate class imbalance.
- **Deployment:** Once trained, the PRM is employed to verify and select the most promising solutions from multiple candidate scene configurations and subtask supervisions generated by the policy model, guiding the agent towards successful skill acquisition paths.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of the VERGSA framework, focusing on the exemplar task pool, the verification model, and a comparison with LLM-as-a-Judge baselines.

#### 4.1. Effectiveness of the Exemplar Task Pool (RQ1)

Experiments demonstrated that the exemplar task pool provided valuable guidance for generating scene configurations and subtask supervisions.
- **Performance Improvement:** For the BaseModel (Qwen2.5-Coder-32B-Instruct), the inclusion of the exemplar task pool increased the Average Task Success Rate (ATSR) by 21% (from 0.53 to 0.74) and the Average Subtask Success Rate (ASSR) by 25% (from 0.55 to 0.80).
- **Efficiency:** Concurrently, the average number of subtasks required to accomplish a task decreased from 17.8 to 9.0, indicating more concise and efficient task decompositions.
- **Generalizability:** Similar improvements were observed with a more advanced LLM, GPT-4o, where the exemplar task pool boosted ATSR by 5% (from 0.81 to 0.86) and ASSR by 4% (from 0.84 to 0.88), while reducing the average number of subtasks from 15.2 to 8.2. These results suggest that dynamic contextual guidance enhances both success rates and the efficiency of task decomposition across different policy models.

#### 4.2. Effectiveness of the Verification Model (RQ2)

The Process Reward Model (PRM) consistently improved the performance of the BaseModel in generative skill acquisition tasks.
- **Task-Based Splitting (Novel Tasks):** When the dataset was split by tasks (assessing generalization to unseen tasks), BaseModel-PRM variants significantly outperformed the BaseModel. The highest performance was achieved by BaseModel-PRM-last, which obtained an ATSR of 0.91 and an ASSR of 0.91, compared to the BaseModel’s 0.67 for both metrics. This represents a 24% increase in ATSR and ASSR for novel tasks.
- **Solution-Based Splitting (Encountered Tasks):** When the dataset was split by solutions (assessing performance on previously encountered tasks), the improvements were even more pronounced. BaseModel-PRM-last reached an ATSR of 0.92 and an ASSR of 0.97, compared to the BaseModel’s 0.56 and 0.62, respectively. This signifies a 36% increase in ATSR and 35% increase in ASSR for encountered tasks.
- **Strategy Impact:** Different strategies for grouping subtask rewards influenced performance. The sum (Σ) and geometric mean (Q) strategies provided balanced results. The minimum (min) strategy, which focuses on the weakest subtask, achieved higher success rates in task-based splitting. The maximum (max) strategy generally yielded the lowest scores among PRM variants. The 'last' strategy, which considers the predicted success probability of the final subtask, demonstrated robust performance across both task-based and solution-based partitions.

#### 4.3. LLM-as-a-Judge as an Alternative (RQ3)

The efficacy of LLM-as-a-Judge baselines, which directly assess the validity and correctness of scene configurations and subtask supervisions, was compared against the PRM verification model.
- **Performance Comparison:** While LLM-as-a-Judge models (including GPT-4o, DeepSeek-V3, Claude-3.5-Sonnet, Gemini-2.0-Flash, and Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct) showed effectiveness compared to the raw BaseModel, none of them surpassed the performance of the PRM verifier. For instance, in task-based splitting, the best LLM-as-a-Judge (GPT-4o or Claude-3.5-Sonnet) achieved an ATSR of 0.78, whereas the BaseModel-PRM-last achieved 0.91. Similarly, in solution-based splitting, the best LLM-as-a-Judge achieved an ATSR of 0.65 (GPT-4o), while BaseModel-PRM-last reached 0.92. This indicates that the fine-tuned, task-specific PRM outperformed generalist LLMs used as judges in the context of skill acquisition.
- **Qualitative Results:** Figure 4 provided qualitative examples of intermediate snapshots from 14 tasks, visually demonstrating the robotic arm’s skill acquisition guided by verified scene configurations and subtask supervisions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces VERGSA, a framework that formalizes and integrates real-time verification models into the process of embodied reasoning for generative skill acquisition. This approach presents several significant contributions and potential impacts:

1. **Enhanced Efficiency and Robustness of Skill Acquisition:** By providing a mechanism for real-time verification of scene configurations and subtask supervisions, VERGSA aims to accelerate the learning process. The ability to filter or prioritize more promising solutions before extensive execution in the embodied environment can reduce computational costs and the time required for skill acquisition. This fosters more robust and reliable skill learning by guiding the agent toward correct and efficient reasoning paths.

2. **Addressing Challenges in Embodied Reasoning:** The framework systematically extends verification principles from structured mathematical problems to the less structured and complex domain of embodied tasks. This involves dynamically incorporating contextually relevant tasks into prompts and generating granular success metrics for subtasks, which is a novel adaptation for this field. This approach addresses the inherent difficulties in defining clear success criteria and evaluating intermediate steps in dynamic, physical environments.

3. **Automation of Reward Engineering and Data Generation:** A core contribution is the development of an automated and scalable reward labeling mechanism using an adapted Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS). This method synthesizes dense reward signals for scene configurations and subtask supervisions based on execution feedback, significantly reducing the reliance on arduous and expensive manual reward engineering. This automation is crucial for scaling up skill acquisition to a vast array of diverse tasks.

4. **Creation of a Novel Training Dataset:** The methodology yields the first comprehensive training dataset specifically designed for verifying generative skill acquisition. This dataset, characterized by detailed scene configurations and subtask-level training supervisions with reward annotations, constitutes a valuable resource for future research and development in embodied AI, facilitating the training and evaluation of verification-driven skill learning systems.

5. **Improved Generalization for Embodied Agents:** The exemplar task pool provides task-agnostic guidance by leveraging contextually similar, successfully executed tasks. This dynamic resource enhances the policy model's ability to generate reasonable subtask supervisions for novel scenarios, improving generalization capabilities and reducing the number of ineffective subtasks.

6. **Benchmarking of Verification Approaches:** The comparative analysis with LLM-as-a-Judge baselines provides insights into the effectiveness of different verification strategies. The finding that a dedicated, trained Process Reward Model (PRM) outperforms general-purpose LLMs acting as judges in skill acquisition tasks underscores the value of domain-specific verification mechanisms.

**Potential Limitations and Future Directions:**
The paper acknowledges limitations such as the computational inefficiency of training motion planning or reinforcement learning policies for each subtask and the overhead of MCTS simulations. The sim2real gap, which hinders the transfer of skills from simulation to real-world applications, is also noted. Future work could explore meta-learning and hierarchical reinforcement learning to reduce training overheads, and investigate domain randomization, high-fidelity physics modeling, or hybrid training strategies to mitigate the sim2real gap. These advancements could further enhance the applicability and scalability of verification-driven generative skill acquisition in real-world robotic systems.
