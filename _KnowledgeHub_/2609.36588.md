# Cooperative Multi-Agent Vision-Language-Action Models via Reinforced Fine Tuning

> [!summary] Summary
> A three-stage reinforced fine-tuning recipe for cooperative multi-agent VLAs. Stage one adds human demonstrations only for initializations where the pretrained policy never succeeds. Stage two fine-tunes each agent only on its own positive-advantage actions. Stage three runs online RL in the frozen VLA's latent noise space via DSRL. Over a CHORUS supervised baseline, success rises +23.1% on RoboTwin, +16.4% on RoboFactory and +44% on two real Franka robots.

## Problem

- VLAs are pretrained on single-agent data and lack fine-grained low-level coordination, while multi-agent VLM planners hand execution to rule-based controllers or unchanged VLAs.
- CHORUS achieves multi-VLA cooperation through supervised fine-tuning on multi-robot demonstrations, but its performance stays bounded by that data and cannot improve from the policy's own experience.
- Single-agent online RL for flow-matching VLAs injects independent per-agent Gaussian noise and uses high-variance likelihood surrogates, which break coordination and destabilize concurrent multi-agent updates.

## Method

- ==Initialization-aware data collection==: rolls out the pretrained VLA several times per environment initialization, keeps all rollouts where success is nonzero, and requests human demonstrations only for zero-success initializations.
- ==Offline credit-filtered tuning==: a transformer ==distributional critic== regresses sequential agent-combined Q values, telescoping differences give per-agent advantages, and ==flow-matching== fine-tuning keeps only positive-advantage actions.
- ==Online latent-space RL==: extends ==DSRL== to multiple agents by freezing each VLA and training a small per-agent noise policy that steers the initial flow noise, keeping exploration in-distribution.

## Results

- Across **8** simulated tasks, offline RL adds +**8.1%** and the full pipeline +**19.7%** average success over CHORUS, with gains of +**18.9%** on π0 and +**20.6%** on π0.5.
- On four hard tasks DSRL averages **69.0%** success versus **61.5%** for Flow-Noise and **60.1%** for Flow-SDE. Raising exploration noise lowers flow-method success, and those methods occasionally crash.
- Human demonstrations at zero-success initializations add +**5.7%** over offline RL without them. On real Franka pairs, offline RL adds +**34%** and DSRL another +**10%** from only **50** rollouts.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Sparse-reward RL needs nonzero success to learn anything, so spending human demonstrations only where the policy never succeeds is a cheap way to make fine-tuning feasible.
> - A team outcome misjudges individual agents. Splitting the joint advantage per agent lets filtered imitation keep a good partner's actions in failed rollouts and drop bad ones in successes.
> - RL recipes that work for a single agent can fail with several, because independent action noise breaks coordination. Exploring in a frozen policy's latent noise space keeps the team's shared behavior intact.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

Two Franka robots attempt a handover. The receiving robot must grasp the object before the other lets go. In the pictured handover, the policy fine-tuned with supervision by CHORUS opens the gripper too early. Cooperative robot work fails at these low-level moments that require coordination. Single-robot pretraining does not teach this fine-grained coordination, and imitation from a fixed set of team demonstrations cannot learn from new experience. The authors instead teach the team from its own attempts. They add human demonstrations only at starting conditions where the initial policy never succeeds. After the added reinforcement stages, the reported handover success rate rises from 42% to 86%.

Success rate (%) for CHORUS, + Offline RL, and + Offline RL + DSRL: Handover 42/78/86, Lift 42/82/92, Handover & Drop 44/70/82. At right, CHORUS failures (early release, a missed handle grasp, unstable grasping) appear above trained-method examples. These are illustrative cases.

### 2. Learning from the team's own attempts

An initialization is the configuration of the scene at the start, such as object and robot poses. For each configuration, the authors run the pretrained policy for K episodes and estimate its success rate. If it succeeds at least once, they keep both successful and failed policy rollouts for later tuning. If it has zero successes, they request a human demonstration for that configuration. The authors judge that sparse-reward exploration is unlikely to bootstrap from repeated failures within a reasonable interaction budget. This is a targeted intervention rule. It does not show that the policy lacks every skill needed for that configuration.

A joint task can fail even when one robot did its part well. The offline stage therefore estimates each agent's advantage, meaning whether its action appears better than that agent's expected contribution. It fine-tunes on individual-agent examples whose estimated advantage is positive and filters out the rest. This advantage is an estimate, not a perfect measure of each robot's causal contribution.

### 3. Change the noise, not the VLA

A flow-matching vision-language-action (VLA) policy turns an initial random input, the latent noise, into an action chunk. In the online stage, called DSRL, the VLA stays frozen. A small policy learns over that noise instead of changing the large VLA weights.

The authors give a rationale for this choice. Independent noise injected directly into each agent's action can disrupt coordination. Estimating likelihoods through the flow denoising process can produce high-variance updates. Other agents' changing policies also make learning non-stationary. This is the authors' diagnosis, not a separately proven cause.

The decisive comparison uses four harder π0.5 tasks and measures success rate. DSRL averages 69.0%. Two methods from [[2510.25889|πRL]] average 61.5% (Flow-Noise) and 60.1% (Flow-SDE). The authors also report that flow-based training can occasionally crash, while DSRL improves more stably. The experiment compares complete methods. It does not establish noise or instability as the only cause of the difference.

### 4. What the gains establish

The full recipe was compared with CHORUS, a supervised-fine-tuning baseline, on eight simulation tasks and three real-world tasks. The authors report average success-rate gains of +23.1% on RoboTwin, +16.4% on RoboFactory, and +44% in the real world. These are averages across the named task groups and do not guarantee gains for every starting condition.

The real-world result uses two Franka robots on three tasks. It is a proof of concept: the online stage used 50 real-world rollouts and is described in the appendix as an offline version of DSRL. The paper does not measure a reduction in human time or total training cost.

### 5. Coordination has a learning signal

A failed handover is a joint outcome, but its failure need not mean both robots acted badly. The method turns team experience into agent-specific learning signals, then seeks further improvement without unfreezing the VLA. The reported gains show this recipe can help in the tested simulation tasks and two-robot real-world tasks. They do not establish gains beyond these tasks and conditions.
