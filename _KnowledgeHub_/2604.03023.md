# Behavior-Constrained Reinforcement Learning with Receding-Horizon Credit Assignment for High-Performance Control

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from TU Darmstadt and Porsche AG developed a behavior-constrained reinforcement learning framework for high-performance control, utilizing a receding-horizon trajectory predictor and contextual expert behavior modeling. The system achieved competitive lap times in high-fidelity race car simulations while maintaining expert-consistent driving styles, validated through human-grounded evaluations and capable of serving as a reliable digital driver for vehicle setup testing.

## Problem

- Reinforcement learning (RL) methods often yield high-performance policies but produce non-human-like or unintuitive behaviors, lacking interpretability or safety for real-world applications.
- Imitation learning (IL) produces human-like behaviors but is limited by demonstration data quality and coverage, struggling to adapt to unseen situations or surpass expert performance.
- Balancing task performance with adherence to expert-preferred control strategies in hybrid RL/IL approaches often requires challenging manual tuning of trade-off coefficients.

## Method

- A ==behavior-constrained policy optimization== framework maximizes task performance while enforcing a lower bound on trajectory consistency with expert demonstrations, using an automatically tuned ==Lagrangian multiplier== for the imitation reward.
- A lightweight ==receding-horizon trajectory predictor==, utilizing ==probabilistic Bezier curves==, provides look-ahead rewards to improve ==temporal credit assignment== and near-horizon awareness for the policy.
- ==Trajectory-conditioned expert behavior modeling==, coupled with ==Reference Trajectory Distribution== (RTD)-based data augmentation, allows the policy to represent and learn from a distribution of human-like behaviors.

## Results

- The method achieved a lap time of **66.229 +/- 0.0259** seconds with a Mean Reference Offset (MRO) of **0.2891 +/- 0.0052** meters, demonstrating a favorable balance between performance and expert behavior imitation, outperforming baselines in multi-objective optimization.
- Policies trained with the framework generalized effectively across different race tracks, consistently balancing performance and imitation requirements without the need for re-tuning trade-off coefficients.
- Human-grounded evaluations confirmed that the learned policies exhibited setup-dependent driving characteristics and control strategies consistent with professional driver feedback, proving suitable as 'digital driver models' for virtual vehicle setup testing.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly enforcing behavioral constraints during reinforcement learning enables agents to exceed expert performance while maintaining desired human-like characteristics, bridging the gap between raw performance and trustworthiness.
> - Incorporating a short-term trajectory predictor as an auxiliary task and for look-ahead rewards significantly enhances temporal credit assignment, leading to more robust and sophisticated control strategies in dynamic systems.
> - Modeling expert behavior as a distribution rather than a deterministic target, through contextual conditioning and data augmentation, improves the generalization and adaptability of learned policies to varying conditions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The research addresses a fundamental challenge in robotic control: developing high-performance policies that also adhere to expert or human-preferred behaviors. This problem sits at the intersection of Reinforcement Learning (RL) and Imitation Learning (IL), two prominent paradigms for acquiring control policies.

Traditional RL methods, while capable of discovering high-performing strategies by optimizing a reward function, often yield behaviors that diverge from human intuition, are difficult to interpret, or exploit simulator artifacts. Such "undesirable" strategies may be efficient in terms of raw task performance but lack the characteristics of human-like control, which is critical in applications requiring human interaction, safety, or trustworthiness. For instance, in domains like autonomous driving or professional motorsports, a policy must not only achieve speed but also maintain stability, predictability, and a driving "style" consistent with human experts.

Conversely, Imitation Learning (IL) directly learns from expert demonstrations, thereby intrinsically producing human-like behaviors. However, IL is fundamentally limited by the quality and coverage of the provided demonstration data. It struggles to adapt to unseen situations (covariate shift) and typically cannot surpass the performance level of the expert it imitates. This limitation is particularly evident in dynamic, high-performance systems where even minor changes in environment conditions or system parameters can render previously learned behaviors suboptimal or unsafe. Methods like Behavioral Cloning (BC) or Generative Adversarial Imitation Learning (GAIL) are susceptible to these issues.

Hybrid approaches that combine RL and IL objectives have been explored to leverage the strengths of both, often by adding imitation terms to RL losses. However, these methods frequently involve manual tuning of trade-off coefficients, which can be challenging, especially in complex dynamic systems where the optimal balance between performance and imitation may vary. Furthermore, effectively capturing "expert-consistent behavior" in such systems is complicated because desirable behavior is often expressed over entire trajectories rather than just instantaneous actions, and the consequences of suboptimal decisions can emerge only after a significant delay, posing a temporal credit assignment problem. Existing methods often model expert behavior as a single deterministic target, neglecting the natural variability inherent in human actions under different conditions.

This work positions itself as an advancement in Style-Biased Reinforcement Learning (SBRL), extending its application to complex vehicle control in motorsports. It aims to overcome the limitations of pure RL and IL by proposing a framework that explicitly controls deviation from expert behavior while optimizing performance, addresses temporal credit assignment, and models the distribution of expert-consistent behaviors. This contributes to the development of more adaptable, robust, and human-interpretable autonomous agents in safety-critical and performance-demanding domains.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable the learning of high-performance control policies that consistently align with expert behavior, particularly within dynamic, high-performance systems. The authors identify several motivations and specific challenges that their proposed framework aims to address:

1. **Balancing Performance and Behavior Consistency:** The core motivation is the inherent tension between maximizing task performance (e.g., fastest lap time) and ensuring that the learned control strategy remains consistent with human expert behavior. Purely reward-driven RL can discover highly effective but potentially undesirable or unsafe strategies that do not reflect human interaction with the system. Conversely, strict imitation learning methods are constrained by demonstration data quality and coverage, limiting their ability to adapt or improve beyond the expert's performance. The objective is to achieve high performance *while explicitly controlling* deviations from expert behavior.

2. **Addressing Trajectory-Level Behavior and Delayed Consequences:** In dynamic control problems like race car driving, desirable behavior is not solely determined by instantaneous actions but by sequences of decisions unfolding over time. Suboptimal choices may have delayed, cumulative consequences. This necessitates a mechanism for temporal credit assignment that can evaluate behavior over trajectories. The motivation is to provide the learning agent with a "near-horizon awareness" to better understand and optimize for the short-term future consequences of its actions, thereby facilitating more human-like and optimal trajectory execution.

3. **Modeling Natural Variability of Human Behavior:** Human behavior is not deterministic; it exhibits natural variability due to environmental disturbances, internal states, and adaptive strategies. Simply imitating a single "optimal" trajectory can lead to brittle policies that fail under slight deviations from the demonstrated conditions. The objective is to enable the policy to represent a *distribution* of expert-consistent behaviors rather than a single deterministic target, thereby enhancing robustness and generalization.

4. **Practical Application in High-Performance Domains (Motorsports):** Professional motorsports serve as a compelling and practically relevant benchmark. Race car driving involves operating a highly nonlinear system near its physical limits, where narrow performance margins, strong coupling between driver behavior and vehicle dynamics, and delayed effects make it exceptionally challenging. A particularly important application is "virtual setup testing," where engineers optimize vehicle parameters. For this, a digital driver model needs to be both fast and behaviorally consistent with a professional driver to provide meaningful feedback, rather than merely exploiting simulator quirks. The motivation is to create a digital surrogate for human decision-making that is reliable and interpretable in such engineering contexts.

To achieve these, the key objectives are formulated as:
- Developing a **behavior-constrained policy optimization** framework that maximizes task performance while enforcing a lower bound on trajectory consistency with expert demonstrations.
- Introducing a **receding-horizon credit assignment** mechanism, specifically a lightweight short-horizon trajectory predictor, to provide look-ahead rewards and improve temporal credit assignment.
- Implementing **trajectory-conditioned expert behavior modeling** to account for human variability by conditioning the policy on reference trajectories, coupled with targeted data augmentation.
- Conducting **human-grounded validation** in high-fidelity race car simulation and driver-in-the-loop evaluation to demonstrate the method's ability to reproduce setup-dependent driving characteristics consistent with professional driver feedback.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology integrates concepts from constrained reinforcement learning, predictive modeling, and data augmentation within a contextual Markov Decision Process (CMDP) framework tailored for high-performance control.

**A. Problem Formulation:**
The problem is formulated as a constrained optimization task, aiming to maximize task performance while explicitly limiting deviation from expert behavior. This is achieved by maximizing a combined reward function `r(s_t, a_t, c) = r_p(s_t, a_t, c) + α_s * r_s(s_t, a_t, c)`, where `r_p` is a performance reward and `r_s` is a style imitation reward, and `α_s` is a trade-off coefficient. Crucially, the problem is subject to a constraint `E_π[r_s_t] ≥ ˆR` for all time steps `t`, ensuring that the expected style imitation reward remains above a predefined lower bound `ˆR`. The coefficient `α_s` is treated as a Lagrangian multiplier and is dynamically updated during training to enforce this constraint, eliminating the need for manual tuning.

**B. Short-term Trajectory Representation and Prediction:**
To address the trajectory-level nature of expert behavior and improve temporal credit assignment, a lightweight receding-horizon trajectory predictor (`ψ`) is introduced.
- **Prediction Target:** The predictor forecasts the short-term future driving line in the car's local coordinate system, which is represented as task-critical states for prediction.
- **Bézier Curve Representation:** To manage the trade-off between prediction horizon and complexity, future trajectories are represented compactly using probabilistic Bézier curves. Instead of predicting every point, `ψ` outputs parameters (mean `μ_i` and covariance `Σ_i`) for `M+1` control points. These control points define a Bézier curve, from which `H` future points (each with its own mean `μ_ψ(t)` and covariance `Σ_ψ(t)`) are interpolated at equidistant phases.
- **Training:** The predictor is trained by maximizing the recency-weighted log-likelihood of target trajectories, given as a sequence of `H` points. This auxiliary task helps the policy's feature extractor learn robust representations of short-term dynamics.

**C. Contextual Markov Decision Process for Racing:**
The general framework is adapted to race car driving with domain-specific definitions for states, actions, context, and rewards.
- **Actions (`a_t`):** The policy predicts relative changes (`∆a_t`) to the current acceleration/brake (`α`) and steering wheel angle (`δ`). This parametrization is intended to help the agent focus on underlying dynamics. Absolute actions are then obtained by clipping `a_{t-1} + ∆a_t`. The relative actions are sampled from a Gaussian distribution with a state-dependent mean (`μ_π(s_t)`) and a state-independent but learnable diagonal covariance (`Σ_π`).
- **States (`s_t`):** The policy's input comprises four groups:
 - **Vehicle states:** Absolute velocity, longitudinal/lateral acceleration, average front/rear slip ratio/angle.
 - **Track perception:** Localized track boundary points at various distances.
 - **Global reference trajectory waypoint:** Waypoints from a global reference trajectory (`τ`) on a 5-second horizon, converted to the car's local coordinates.
 - **Current actions:** The current absolute values of throttle, brake, and steering.
- **Policy (`π`):** A neural network processes these inputs, with a feature extractor feeding latent variables to separate heads for the policy (predicting relative actions), value function, and trajectory predictor.
- **Context (`c`):** Spatial trajectories (`τ`) derived from expert demonstrations serve as the context.
- **Reward Function:** The total reward is a combination of performance and style imitation components, both current and future-oriented:
 - **Performance Reward (`r_p(s_t)`):** Measures progress along the reference trajectory (`τ`) as the arc-length distance between the projections of `s_t` and `s_{t+1}` onto `τ`. Penalties for off-track, car spin, and slow speed are included.
 - **Receding-horizon Performance Reward (`r_p_ψ(s_t)`):** The expected overall arc-length progress along the *predicted* trajectory (`ψ`) over `H` steps.
 - **Style Imitation Reward (`r_s(s_t)`):** Defined as `exp(-α_d * d_s(s_t, ϕ_τ(s_t))^2)`, where `d_s` is the Euclidean distance between the current state and its projection (`ϕ_τ`) onto the reference trajectory. This metric is decoupled from time to handle alignment errors.
 - **Receding-horizon Style Imitation Reward (`r_s_ψ(s_t)`):** The weighted sum of expected imitation distances for each predicted point along `ψ`.
 - **Combined Reward:** `r(s_t) = r_p(s_t) + r_p_ψ(s_t) + α_s * (r_s(s_t) + r_s_ψ(s_t))`. The look-ahead rewards from `ψ` enhance temporal credit assignment.

**D. Pretraining and Policy Fine-Tuning with Data Augmentation:**
A three-stage training pipeline is employed:
- **Data Augmentation:** Instead of directly using raw expert demonstrations, a Reference Trajectory Distribution (RTD) is fitted to the expert trajectories using Probabilistic Movement Primitives (ProMP). This allows sampling an arbitrary number of diverse yet human-like reference trajectories (`τ`) to serve as context during training, enhancing generalization.
- **Pretraining:** The policy and the trajectory predictor are jointly pretrained using supervised learning on a dataset of expert demonstrations. Behavioral Cloning (BC) minimizes the mean-squared error between expert relative actions and the policy's predicted means, with an added regularization term. The trajectory predictor is trained with its log-likelihood loss.
- **Policy Fine-Tuning:** The pretrained policy is fine-tuned using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). In each PPO update cycle, the trajectory predictor (`ψ`) is first updated, and then the rewards (`r_p_ψ` and `r_s_ψ`) are calculated based on its latest predictions. This ensures the policy learns from an up-to-date understanding of future consequences. Early stopping is applied to the predictor's training to prevent overfitting. Finally, the policy is updated using the standard PPO objective (clipped surrogate, entropy regularization, value function loss), and the `α_s` coefficient is adjusted based on the style imitation constraint.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of the proposed behavior-constrained reinforcement learning framework was conducted in a high-fidelity race car simulation, including human-grounded evaluations.

**A. Method Evaluation and Benchmarking (Track A):**
The proposed method (A-ours) was benchmarked against three baselines: a stepwise reward approach (B-step reward), an adversarial imitation learning method (C-adversarial reward/AMP), and a contextual reinforcement learning approach without explicit style imitation (D-CRL). All experiments were run with data augmentation.
- **Performance and Imitation:** A-ours achieved a lap time of 66.229 ± 0.0259 seconds with a Mean Reference Offset (MRO) of 0.2891 ± 0.0052 meters. This demonstrated a favorable balance between performance and imitation quality compared to baselines.
 - B-step reward achieved 66.961 ± 0.299 s and 0.4547 ± 0.0320 m MRO.
 - C-adversarial reward achieved 66.775 ± 0.0143 s and 0.1341 ± 0.0035 m MRO (best imitation, but slower).
 - D-CRL, lacking explicit imitation objectives, had the slowest lap time (67.209 ± 0.275 s) and highest MRO (0.7686 ± 0.0098 m).
- **Learning Progress:** Analysis of learning curves showed that A-ours effectively balanced imitation and performance, maintaining imitation quality while continuously optimizing lap time. Baselines B and D struggled to maintain imitation as performance increased, while C achieved high imitation but plateaued in performance optimization.
- **Multiobjective Optimization (Pareto Fronts):** The Pareto front analysis indicated that A-ours dominated the other baselines in the top-performance region (lap times between 66.0 and 66.4 seconds). This highlights the method's ability to automatically find optimal trade-offs between performance and style imitation without extensive manual tuning of reward coefficients, a common challenge for methods like B and C.

**B. Generalization (Tracks B and C):**
The method's generalizability was tested on two additional, complex race tracks using the same imitation-performance trade-off coefficient.
- A-ours consistently delivered strong performance and maintained imitation requirements across all tracks (Track B: 66.892 ± 0.0256 s, 0.2417 ± 0.0166 m MRO; Track C: 69.688 ± 0.0473 s, 0.2989 ± 0.0109 m MRO).
- In contrast, baselines B and C showed varying performance and imitation quality, suggesting that their trade-off coefficients would require re-tuning for each new environment. This demonstrated the robustness of the automatic trade-off mechanism in A-ours.

**C. Performance Analysis (Single Lap and Trajectory Imitation):**
- **Single Lap Comparison:** A time-series comparison of agent-driven laps with human reference laps (MRO of 0.34m) revealed that the agent closely followed speed and action profiles. However, the agent also optimized performance by subtly correcting "suboptimal" human actions, such as maintaining throttle longer before braking at specific track sections, leading to improved lap times.
- **Trajectory Imitation:** The agent generally adhered to randomly sampled reference trajectories. Minor local deviations, particularly in mid-corner sections, were observed. These deviations were attributed to the agent's performance optimization, indicating an adaptive strategy rather than strict replication of potentially suboptimal reference lines.

**D. Ablation Studies:**
- **Automatic Reward Scaling Factor Tuning (S):**
 - Experiments with fixed reward scaling coefficients (S-I: α_s=0.5; S-II: α_s=1.6) and automatic scaling (S-III: ˆR=0.009) demonstrated the benefit of the dynamic tuning mechanism.
 - S-III achieved a similar imitation quality to S-II (higher α_s, better imitation, but slower) while maintaining performance closer to S-I (lower α_s, faster, but worse imitation). This confirmed that automatic tuning avoids extensive parameter searches and dynamically guides exploration for better overall performance.
- **Data Augmentation (DA):**
 - Training with Reference Trajectory Distribution (RTD)-based data augmentation (DA-RTD) resulted in improved generalization compared to training directly on raw human demonstrations (DA-demos). DA-RTD policies outperformed DA-demos by approximately 0.2 seconds on a test dataset, underscoring the technique's role in enhancing robustness.
- **Prediction Module as Auxiliary Task (P):**
 - Using the trajectory prediction module as an auxiliary task (even without using its predictions for credit assignment initially) significantly enhanced learning.
 - In both pure performance (P-II vs P-I) and performance+style (P-IV vs P-III) settings, including the prediction module improved lap times (by ~0.25 seconds and ~0.2 seconds, respectively) and imitation quality (around 30% MRO improvement). This supports the hypothesis that near-horizon awareness and improved learned representations contribute to better performance and imitation.

**E. Human-Grounded Evaluation (Driver-in-the-Loop Simulator):**
The policies were evaluated on three distinct vehicle setups, mirroring a real-world DiL simulator protocol with professional drivers.
- **Performance and Consistency Analysis:** Policies exhibited performance and consistency trade-offs across setups consistent with expert feedback. For example, Setup 2 achieved the fastest individual lap times but with higher variance, while Setup 3 offered slightly slower peak performance but superior consistency (tighter lap time distribution), aligning with a professional driver's preference for stable handling.
- **Behavioral Consistency with Human Drivers:** The learned policies demonstrated systematic behavioral differences in control inputs and vehicle states that mirrored the qualitative feedback from professional drivers. For instance, policies trained on an oversteer-prone setup (Setup 2) showed earlier, smoother braking and more conservative acceleration, while those for an understeer-prone setup (Setup 1) exhibited more aggressive inputs. This indicates the framework's ability to capture not just task performance but also the nuanced, setup-dependent control strategies of human experts.
- **Implications:** These results suggest that the learned policies can serve as reliable proxies for expert drivers in evaluating vehicle setups, enabling repeatable, controlled experiments while retaining key characteristics of human behavior.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of robotic control and autonomous systems by addressing a critical gap in learning high-performance policies that are simultaneously consistent with expert behavior. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Bridging the Gap between Performance and Trustworthiness:** The work provides a framework that allows agents to surpass expert performance while explicitly constraining their behavior to remain within human-acceptable or expert-preferred norms. This is crucial for applications where mere task achievement is insufficient, and human trust, safety, and interpretability are paramount. It mitigates the common issue in RL where reward maximization can lead to unintuitive or brittle behaviors.

2. **Advancing High-Performance Control in Dynamic Systems:** By introducing receding-horizon credit assignment via probabilistic Bézier curves, the method effectively addresses the challenge of delayed consequences and trajectory-level behavior in dynamic systems. This look-ahead capability enhances the agent's ability to reason about future states and optimize long-term outcomes, leading to more robust and sophisticated control strategies.

3. **Modeling Human Variability:** The use of trajectory-conditioned policies and Reference Trajectory Distribution (RTD)-based data augmentation allows the policy to represent a distribution of expert-consistent behaviors, rather than a single deterministic target. This approach improves the robustness and generalization capabilities of learned policies, enabling them to adapt more effectively to disturbances and varying environmental conditions, reflecting the inherent flexibility of human drivers.

4. **Enabling Advanced Motorsport Development:** The practical application and human-grounded evaluation in high-fidelity race car simulation demonstrate a direct and significant impact on professional motorsports. The learned policies can serve as reliable "digital driver models" for virtual setup testing, allowing engineers to iteratively evaluate vehicle configurations without requiring constant presence of professional drivers in expensive simulator sessions. This could accelerate vehicle development, reduce costs, and provide more objective and repeatable insights into vehicle handling characteristics, corroborated by expert feedback.

5. **Generalizability to Broader Robotics and Autonomous Systems:** While motivated by the demands of race car driving, the underlying principles of behavior-constrained optimization, receding-horizon prediction, and contextual conditioning are generalizable. This framework could be applied to a wide range of continuous control tasks in robotics, such as robot locomotion, manipulation, or human-robot collaboration, where both performance and adherence to specific interaction styles or safety protocols are essential. This paves the way for future research into more capable, reliable, and human-compatible autonomous agents across various domains.

6. **Methodological Contributions:** The dynamic tuning of the style imitation coefficient (Lagrangian multiplier) removes the burden of manual hyperparameter tuning, which is often a significant challenge in multi-objective reinforcement learning. The integration of supervised pretraining with adversarial data augmentation and PPO fine-tuning also provides a robust and efficient training pipeline.
