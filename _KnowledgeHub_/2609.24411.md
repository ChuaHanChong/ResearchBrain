# Zeva-Ego: Egocentric Mid-Training with In-Context Causal Learning for Robot Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Zeva-Ego mid-trains a pi-0.5 vision-language-action policy on 10K hours of egocentric human video, using an Action-Centric Encoder that turns RGB frame pairs into continuous action-token supervision in a unified camera-frame end-effector space. At deployment, In-Context Causal Learning stores action-effect evidence in dual-timescale memory and conditions only the Action Expert, lifting RoboTwin success from 63.8% to 75.3% and cross-attempt success from 58% to 89% with frozen parameters.

## Problem

- Robot action-labeled trajectories are expensive to scale across tasks, environments and embodiments, so scaling robot datasets alone limits open-world generalization of vision-language-action policies.
- Egocentric video lacks robot-aligned actions; camera motion, occlusion and embodiment gaps make visually similar motions map to different trajectories, and prior latent-action methods stay sensitive to nuisance appearance changes.
- Deployed policies keep parameters fixed; test-time gradient updates are slow and unstable, while observation-level memory does not encode how an action changed the environment.

## Method

- **Action-Centric Encoder** (ACE): a two-stage ==DINOv2==-based encoder first learns an action-conditioned environment residual bottleneck, then distills a visual-only encoder whose 16 task tokens match physical-action neighborhood geometry via ==KL matching==, pseudo-labeling unlabeled Ego video.
- Unified 16D dual end-effector schema plus chunk-anchored camera-frame action chunks, with a ==virtual parallel-jaw gripper== derived from fingertip medians, multi-granularity instruction synthesis and a dataset-aware curation pipeline.
- **In-Context Causal Learning** (ICCL) engine: a ==GRU== Causal Transition Encoder emits phase and effect tokens into a ==Brief Interaction Trace== and ==Persistent Interaction Memory==; phase-retrieved causal context conditions only the ==flow-matching== Action Expert.

## Results

- Ego mid-training scales RoboTwin success **63.8%** to **75.3%** at 10K hours, matching **74.7%** from 2K hours of robot demonstrations, roughly **4-5:1**; RoboTwin Hard 50-task average **51.98%** versus **46.00%** for the base policy.
- ICCL raises success from **58%** at attempt one to **89%** at attempt four with frozen weights; LIBERO-PRO average **57.48%** vs **53.20%**, real ChemLab-Evo **85.0%** vs **83.3%** for the strongest prior method.
- Ablations: token distance correlates **0.801** with physical-action distance on held-out EgoDex-AgiBot pairs and **0.724** zero-shot on EgoVerse; broad 973-cluster coverage beats narrow, **65.7%** to **70.2%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Human video substitutes for robot data at a measured exchange rate, not parity: noisier supervision buys scale, so report ratios rather than claiming equivalence.
> - Semantic breadth of interactions matters more than duration: gains were nonlinear, appearing only at broad task coverage, not between narrow and intermediate.
> - Deployment-time adaptation need not touch weights: storing action-effect evidence and injecting it only at the low-level controller preserves high-level reasoning while correcting control.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

Zeva-Ego addresses two major problems in current vision-language-action (VLA) research: the limited scalability of robot action data and the difficulty of adapting policies after deployment. Recent VLA systems, including RT-series models, OpenVLA, Octo, and the π family, combine visual perception, language understanding, and robot action generation. Their performance generally improves with larger and more diverse robot datasets, but collecting action-labeled demonstrations requires teleoperation, specialized hardware, expert operators, and substantial engineering effort.

Egocentric human video offers a much larger and more varied source of interaction data. Datasets such as Ego4D, EgoDex, EgoVerse, and industrial egocentric collections contain human activities across household, laboratory, and factory environments. However, these videos do not usually provide robot-compatible action trajectories. They also contain camera motion, self-occlusion, differences between human wrists and robot end effectors, and visual changes unrelated to the intended manipulation. Prior work has therefore investigated latent-action learning and cross-embodiment alignment. Zeva-Ego extends this direction by using an Action-Centric Encoder (ACE) to convert visual transitions into continuous action-related representations.

The work also belongs to a growing area concerned with deployment-time adaptation. Conventional VLA policies normally remain fixed during inference. Test-time training can update model parameters using new experience, but gradient-based updates may be computationally expensive or unstable in a physical system. Other memory-based approaches retain observations, language summaries, or task histories, but may not explicitly encode the relationship between an action and its physical consequence. Zeva-Ego uses In-Context Causal Learning (ICCL), previously introduced in Zeva, to represent action-effect transitions and condition future low-level actions without changing the policy parameters.

The paper therefore combines offline physical-prior learning from human video with online, parameter-free adaptation from robot experience.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

#### 2.1. Scaling Physical Supervision

The first objective is to determine whether large-scale egocentric video can improve a robot policy even when most of the video lacks direct robot action labels. The authors seek to move beyond using human video only for semantic or visual pretraining. Their aim is to extract information about motion, contact, grasping, object transfer, and other physical interactions that can be transferred into robot control.

A central challenge is that a human wrist trajectory is not equivalent to a robot end-effector trajectory. Human hands have articulated fingers, different reachability, and different grasp mechanics. The paper addresses this by constructing a common end-effector representation and expressing actions in a camera-centered coordinate frame. The intended result is a representation of physical motion that is less tied to a particular body or robot embodiment.

#### 2.2. Enabling Adaptation During Deployment

The second objective is to enable a robot to improve through repeated interaction. In open-world settings, the robot may encounter unfamiliar friction, object compliance, camera viewpoints, grasping conditions, or scene layouts. A policy trained only offline may repeat an unsuccessful action because it lacks an explicit representation of what happened during previous attempts.

ICCL is designed to encode the observed consequences of actions. For example, a closed gripper accompanied by no object movement may indicate a failed grasp, while a pouring action that produces little liquid displacement may indicate an incorrect container orientation. The system should use such evidence to adjust subsequent low-level trajectories while retaining the VLA model’s high-level task interpretation.

#### 2.3. Evaluating Data Efficiency and Continual Improvement

The paper evaluates whether egocentric data can substitute for some robot data, how the amount and diversity of egocentric video affect performance, whether ACE preserves physical-action geometry, and whether ICCL improves success across repeated attempts.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Two-Stage Framework

Zeva-Ego consists of an offline egocentric mid-training stage followed by deployment-time ICCL adaptation. The starting point is the π^0.5 VLA model, which separates high-level subtask prediction from continuous low-level action generation through an Action Expert.

During mid-training, the full policy is trained on robot demonstrations, labeled human egocentric data, and unlabeled egocentric video converted into pseudo-action supervision. During deployment, the VLA parameters are frozen. Only the interaction memories and their resulting causal context are updated online.

#### 3.2. Data Sources and Standardization

The corpus combines several data types. Robot data include approximately 2,976 hours from AgiBot World and 7,434 hours of InternData-A1 synthetic manipulation data. Labeled human video includes approximately 829 hours from EgoDex and 1,362 hours from EgoVerse. Unlabeled video includes 3,670 hours from Ego4D and 4,439 selected hours from the 10,000-hour Egocentric-10K release.

The authors standardize human and robot data through a 16-dimensional dual-end-effector schema. Each arm is represented by a seven-dimensional pose consisting of three-dimensional translation and a four-dimensional quaternion, followed by one gripper value per arm. Future poses are represented as displacements from the beginning of an action chunk, while gripper targets remain absolute.

For human demonstrations, the wrist is treated as the end effector. A virtual parallel-jaw gripper is estimated from five fingertip positions. The thumb forms one jaw and the median location of the other four fingertips forms the opposing jaw. This construction reduces sensitivity to individual fingertip tracking errors.

Action chunks are expressed in the camera frame anchored at the first observation of the chunk. This prevents later camera motion from redefining the action coordinate system and maintains correspondence between what the camera observes and the direction of the intended motion. The authors also synthesize instructions at multiple levels, ranging from immediate actions to skill- and task-level descriptions.

#### 3.3. Action-Centric Encoder

ACE is trained in two stages. In the first stage, the model receives two visual observations, a known physical action chunk, and features from a frozen DINOv2 visual backbone. It learns an environment bottleneck intended to capture transition effects not explained by the action, including camera motion, occlusion, and uncontrolled scene changes.

In the second stage, ACE becomes visual-only. It receives only the two observations and produces environment tokens and task or action tokens. The environment representation is distilled from the first-stage teacher. A reconstruction objective ensures that the visual transition remains represented, while a neighborhood-matching objective encourages visual transitions with similar physical actions to occupy nearby locations in token space.

The resulting task tokens are used as pseudo-labels for unlabeled egocentric video. Thus, a large video corpus can provide action-centered supervision using only pairs of RGB observations at inference time.

#### 3.4. ICCL Adaptation

The ICCL engine contains a Causal Transition Encoder (CTE), a Brief Interaction Trace (BIT), and a Persistent Interaction Memory (PIM). After an action is executed, the system computes an observed state change between the pre-action and post-action observations. The CTE combines visual states, executed actions, and observed effects using a recurrent architecture. It produces a phase token representing task progress and an interaction signal representing local action consequences.

BIT stores recent evidence within the current attempt and is cleared between attempts. PIM stores phase-matched evidence across attempts within the same task instance. Similar interaction units are merged, while distinct evidence is retained to represent different failure modes or contact conditions.

At each decision step, the current phase token retrieves relevant entries from PIM. The memory context combines the task instruction, current phase, recent interaction trace, and retrieved history. This context is injected directly into the Action Expert through attention and causal prompting. It is not supplied to the high-level VLM pathway, so the system can modify low-level control without changing subtask prediction.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Benchmark Performance

Under the official RoboTwin 2.0 Hard comparison on ten reported tasks, Zeva-Ego achieves a 58.7% unweighted average success rate, compared with 51.2% for π^0.5. The full 50-task appendix reports a macro-average of 51.98% for Zeva-Ego versus 46.00% for π^0.5. Performance is uneven across tasks: Zeva-Ego performs strongly on placing cans in a plastic box, shaking bottles, and placing containers, but remains weak on tasks such as turning a switch and moving a can to a pot.

On LIBERO-PRO, Zeva-Ego obtains an overall average of 57.48%, compared with 53.20% for π^0.5. The largest relative improvement is reported on the Spatial suite, where Zeva-Ego reaches 76.70%. The results suggest that the method provides some robustness to position, appearance, semantic, task, and environmental changes, although it does not improve every suite equally.

On the real-world ChemLab-Evo Level 1 tasks, Zeva-Ego reaches an average success rate of 85.0% over test-tube pickup, beaker placement, and water pouring. The corresponding π^0.5 result is 73.3%, while the earlier Zeva system reaches 83.3%. Zeva-Ego performs particularly well on pouring water, with a 95% success rate.

#### 4.2. Scaling Egocentric and Robot Data

In a controlled RoboTwin scaling study, the π^0.5 baseline begins at 63.8% after the specified downstream training. Adding increasing amounts of egocentric mid-training data raises performance to 75.3% with 10,000 hours. A separate robot-data sweep reaches 74.7% with 2,000 hours of robot demonstrations. Under this experimental setup, the authors estimate that approximately four to five hours of egocentric video provide a comparable aggregate improvement to one hour of robot data.

The paper explicitly treats this as an empirical dataset-level ratio, not a general equivalence. Robot demonstrations contain direct embodiment-specific action labels, whereas egocentric video is noisier and requires pseudo-labeling.

A real-robot low-data experiment further examines 50 task-matched human episodes per task. With only ten robot demonstrations, adding the human episodes improves test-tube pickup by 55 percentage points and bottle placement by 40 percentage points compared with robot-only training. The estimated relationship in this setting is approximately 1.5 task-matched egocentric episodes per robot demonstration.

#### 4.3. Data Diversity and ACE Geometry

Under a matched training exposure, broad task coverage produces 70.2% RoboTwin success, compared with 65.7% for narrow coverage and 66.3% for intermediate coverage. This indicates that semantic breadth may matter in addition to total video duration.

ACE task-token distances correlate with physical-action distances at 0.801 on held-out EgoDex–AgiBot transitions. The correlation remains 0.724 when transferred to EgoVerse, which has different camera properties and visual backgrounds. These measurements support the claim that ACE captures more than superficial visual similarity, although correlation does not establish that the representation is sufficient for reliable robot control.

#### 4.4. Cross-Attempt Adaptation

With ICCL, success increases from 58% on the first attempt to 89% on the fourth attempt in the reported RoboTwin interaction study, without updating policy parameters. The improvement is attributed to PIM’s retention and retrieval of phase-relevant action-effect evidence.

Real-robot experiments also show higher success across repeated attempts for the ICCL system, particularly on beaker placement and water pouring. The evaluation uses fixed task initializations across attempts in the real-world setting, allowing the system to accumulate experience for the same task instance.

### 5. Significance, Potential Impact, and Limitations

Zeva-Ego presents a framework for combining scalable human interaction data with robot-specific feedback. Its main significance is methodological: it separates broad offline acquisition of physical priors from online adaptation of low-level control. If the approach generalizes, egocentric video could reduce dependence on costly robot teleoperation while providing broader coverage of household and industrial behaviors.

The ICCL component also offers a deployment strategy that avoids test-time gradient updates. This may be useful for systems with limited compute, strict safety requirements, or policies that should preserve their pretrained behavior while learning local corrections.

Several limitations qualify the findings. The reported data-efficiency ratios depend on particular datasets, sampling schedules, tasks, and training protocols. Most evaluations are simulated, and the real-world study is limited to three atomic laboratory tasks. The repeated-attempt experiments allow memory to persist for the same task instance, which may not reflect situations where objects, layouts, or task conditions change substantially. ACE also depends on the quality of pose annotations used during its teacher stage and may have difficulty under severe occlusion or unobserved contact. The authors identify tactile and force feedback as potential extensions. More broadly, future evaluations would need to test longer-horizon tasks, additional robot embodiments, safety under erroneous causal inference, and adaptation across changing task instances rather than repeated trials of a fixed configuration.
