# Accelerated Learning with Linear Temporal Logic using Differentiable Simulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of Maryland developed a method to enable gradient-based reinforcement learning with Linear Temporal Logic (LTL) specifications by making LTL rewards and automaton transitions differentiable. This approach allowed differentiable RL algorithms to learn complex LTL-specified robotic tasks significantly faster and more reliably, especially with intricate temporal requirements, compared to non-differentiable methods.

## Problem

- Reinforcement learning (RL) in safety-critical domains requires robust guarantees, which are difficult to ensure with traditional methods like constrained MDPs or state/action avoidance.
- Linear Temporal Logic (LTL) offers formal correctness for trajectory-based tasks but leads to sparse, non-differentiable rewards, hindering efficient gradient-based policy optimization in RL.
- Existing LTL-based RL approaches often struggle with learning efficiency for long-horizon tasks, requiring extensive exploration to discover rewarding behaviors.

## Method

- Developed a framework that transforms discrete **LTL** rewards and automaton transitions into a differentiable form compatible with gradient-based RL, leveraging differentiable simulators.
- Introduced "soft" probabilistic labeling for atomic propositions using a differentiable cumulative distribution function (e.g., sigmoid) and represented automaton states as probabilistic superpositions.
- Constructed a fully differentiable product MDP by defining differentiable label transitions and 	-actions, allowing first-order gradient computation for policy optimization.

## Results

- Differentiable RL algorithms, utilizing the proposed differentiable LTL rewards, consistently achieved near-optimal policies (e.g., >**0.8** LTL satisfaction) significantly faster (within **20M** steps) than non-differentiable baselines (often failing or taking **100M** steps) across various robotic control tasks.
- On the low-dimensional CartPole environment with complex LTL, differentiable methods converged while non-differentiable methods largely failed to learn any meaningful policy after **100M** steps due to extreme reward sparsity.
- An ablation study confirmed that the performance advantage of differentiable RL methods was primarily due to their ability to leverage LTL reward differentiability, as all methods performed similarly well under simplified LTL specifications.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Transforming LTL's formal correctness into a continuous, differentiable signal can bridge the gap between rigorous specification and efficient gradient-based learning in RL.
> - Probabilistic representations of atomic propositions and automaton states allow for the derivation of dense, continuous reward and discount functions from traditionally sparse LTL specifications.
> - The ability to compute first-order gradients for LTL-based objectives is crucial for accelerating learning in complex environments, particularly when LTL specifications introduce extreme reward sparsity.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of Artificial Intelligence (AI) systems for real-world deployment necessitates robust controllers that adhere to safety and reliability constraints. Reinforcement Learning (RL) has demonstrated capabilities in complex, uncertain environments but often struggles with formally specified safety requirements, as conventional approaches like constrained Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) or state-avoidance mechanisms can be challenging to define or lead to overly conservative policies.

Formal specification languages, such as Linear Temporal Logic (LTL), offer an alternative for defining explicit, unambiguous, trajectory-level requirements for RL objectives. LTL allows for correct-by-construction objectives and incorporates automaton-based memory, making it suitable for long-horizon tasks. However, a significant challenge in integrating LTL with RL is the inherent sparsity of rewards derived from logical satisfaction. Policies often receive non-zero rewards only upon satisfying complex conditions, which can require extensive exploration and hinder learning efficiency. While heuristic reward shaping can mitigate sparsity, it risks compromising the formal correctness of the specified objective.

Separately, differentiable simulators have emerged as a method to accelerate policy optimization in RL by providing gradient information directly from the simulation dynamics and reward functions. This allows for gradient-based learning, which can be more sample-efficient than traditional zeroth-order policy gradient methods. However, previous work combining temporal logics with differentiable RL has often relied on non-Markovian rewards (e.g., Signal Temporal Logic robustness scores) or techniques like Backpropagation Through Time (BPTT), which can suffer from vanishing/exploding gradients over long horizons.

This research addresses the gap between the formal correctness offered by LTL specifications and the learning efficiency required for complex continuous control tasks. It seeks to combine the benefits of LTL with the acceleration provided by differentiable simulators, specifically by transforming sparse, discrete LTL-derived rewards into differentiable signals, while maintaining objective soundness and Markovian properties suitable for long-horizon learning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an end-to-end framework that integrates LTL specifications with differentiable simulators to accelerate gradient-based policy learning in continuous control tasks. The motivations are multi-faceted:

1. **Addressing Sparse LTL Rewards**: A central challenge in LTL-guided RL is the sparsity of the reward signal. Agents receive rewards only when LTL conditions are met, which often occurs infrequently, especially early in training or for complex tasks. This sparsity makes learning difficult and inefficient, requiring extensive exploration. The objective is to mitigate this sparsity without resorting to heuristic reward shaping that might compromise the formal correctness of the LTL objective.
2. **Maintaining Objective Soundness**: While heuristic reward shaping can alleviate sparsity, poorly designed heuristics can misguide the learning process, leading to policies that do not truly satisfy the original LTL specification. A key objective is to ensure that any acceleration method preserves the correctness of the LTL objective, meaning that maximizing the new reward signal still corresponds to maximizing the satisfaction probability of the LTL formula.
3. **Leveraging Differentiable Simulators**: Differentiable simulators offer the potential for significant acceleration in RL by enabling the computation of first-order gradients of states and rewards with respect to actions. The motivation is to harness this capability for LTL-based objectives, which typically involve discrete logic and automaton transitions that are not inherently differentiable. The goal is to make LTL rewards and state representations differentiable.
4. **Enabling Scalable and Safe Learning**: By combining LTL's expressive power for complex trajectory-level requirements (e.g., safety, liveness, sequencing) with the efficiency of gradient-based learning, the research aims to enable the development of more reliable and scalable AI systems. This is particularly relevant for real-world applications where malfunctioning can have severe consequences, making formal guarantees critical.
5. **Generalization across Formal Methods**: The approach aims for broad applicability by demonstrating compatibility with general LTL and also showcasing its effectiveness with reward machines, which are derived from co-safe LTL and LTL$_{f}$ fragments, suggesting a generalizable method for automaton-based rewards.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology introduces an end-to-end differentiable framework for learning control policies from LTL specifications by transforming discrete LTL-derived rewards into differentiable signals.

**A. Product MDPs and Discrete LTL Rewards:**
The approach begins by formalizing the problem using a product MDP. Given a labeled MDP $M^+ = (S, A, f, p_0, \mathcal{A}, L)$ and an LTL formula $\varphi$, a Limit-Deterministic Büchi Automaton (LDBA) $A_{\varphi} = (Q, q_0, \Sigma, \delta, B)$ is constructed. A product MDP $\mathcal{M} = (\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, \mathcal{f}, \mathcal{p}_0, \mathcal{B})$ is then formed by augmenting the state space of the original MDP with the LDBA states ($S \times Q$) and augmenting actions with "$\varepsilon$-actions" representing non-deterministic automaton transitions. Maximizing the probability of satisfying $\varphi$ in the original MDP is reduced to maximizing the probability of satisfying the Büchi acceptance condition (visiting states in $\mathcal{B}$ infinitely often) in the product MDP.

Discrete LTL rewards are typically derived from this Büchi condition. The paper references an approach (Bozkurt et al., 2024) where a state-dependent reward function $R(s)$ and a discount function $\Gamma(s)$ are defined: $R(s) = 1-\beta$ if $s \in B$ (accepting state), $0$ otherwise; and $\Gamma(s) = \beta$ if $s \in B$, $\gamma$ otherwise. This structure encourages repeated visits to accepting states. However, these rewards are binary and sparse, leading to significant learning challenges.

**B. Differentiable LTL Rewards through Soft Labeling:**
To make the LTL rewards differentiable, the method introduces "soft labeling" and differentiable automaton transitions:

1. **Soft Labels for Atomic Propositions (APs)**: Instead of binary evaluation (an AP either holds or does not), the probability that an AP $a := 'g(s) > 0'$ holds for a given state $s$ is defined using a differentiable cumulative distribution function, specifically the sigmoid function: $Pr(a \in L(s)) = h(g(s)) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp(-g(s))}$. This allows for continuous, probabilistic evaluation of APs.
2. **Probabilistic Label Sets**: The probability of a state $s$ having a specific label set $l$ (a subset of all APs) is then computed as the product of individual AP probabilities: $Pr(L(s) = l) = \prod_{a \in l} Pr(a \in L(s)) \prod_{a \notin l} (1 - Pr(a \in L(s)))$.
3. **Differentiable Automaton Transitions**: These probabilistic labels induce probabilistic transitions in the LDBA. Instead of one-hot indicator vectors for automaton states, the automaton state is represented as a probability distribution vector $q$ over all possible LDBA states. A function $f_L(s, q)$ updates this probability vector based on the current state $s$ and the current automaton state probabilities $q$, effectively summing probabilities of transitions to each subsequent state.
4. **Differentiable $\varepsilon$-Actions**: Similarly, the binary $\varepsilon$-actions of the LDBA are made differentiable by representing them as probabilities $q_{\varepsilon}$ of taking specific $\varepsilon$-transitions. A function $f_{\varepsilon}(q, q_{\varepsilon})$ updates the automaton state probabilities based on these differentiable $\varepsilon$-actions.
5. **Composed Differentiable Transition Function**: The complete product MDP transition function $\mathcal{f}$ is then defined as a composition of the MDP's physical dynamics $f$, the differentiable $\varepsilon$-action update $f_{\varepsilon}$, and the differentiable label-triggered automaton transition $f_L$. This yields $\mathcal{f}(\langle s, q \rangle, \langle a, q_{\varepsilon} \rangle) := \langle f(s, a), f_L(s, f_{\varepsilon}(q, q_{\varepsilon})) \rangle$, which is fully differentiable with respect to $s, q, a, q_{\varepsilon}$.
6. **Differentiable Rewards and Discounting**: Finally, the discrete reward and discount functions from Theorem 1 are also made differentiable. The reward $R(\langle s, q \rangle)$ becomes $(1-\beta) \sum_{q' \in B} q_{q'}$, and the discount $D(q)$ becomes $\beta \sum_{q' \in B} q_{q'} + \gamma \sum_{q' \notin B} q_{q'}$. These functions now depend on the probability of being in an accepting state, providing a continuous reward signal.

**C. Theoretical Guarantees:**
The paper provides theoretical guarantees linking the discrete and differentiable LTL returns. Theorem 2 establishes an upper bound on the maximum discrepancy between the returns obtained from discrete ($G_{disc.}$) and differentiable ($G_{diff.}$) rewards: $|G_{disc.}(\sigma) - G_{diff.}(\sigma)| < \frac{1}{1 + \frac{1-\beta}{(1-h(\zeta))^{|A|}}}$, where $\zeta$ is a tolerance on signal bounds for APs, $h(\zeta)$ is the probability associated with $\zeta$, and $|A|$ is the number of atomic propositions. This bound quantifies how closely the differentiable rewards approximate the discrete ones, ensuring objective soundness, and extends to expected returns in stochastic settings.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrate that the proposed differentiable LTL framework substantially accelerates training and achieves higher returns compared to non-differentiable baselines across various continuous control tasks.

**A. Experimental Setup:**
- **Simulator**: PyTorch-based differentiable physics simulator dFlex.
- **Environments**: CartPole (low-dimensional, 5D state, 1D action) and three legged-robot environments: Hopper (10D state, 3D action), Cheetah (17D state, 6D action), and Ant (37D state, 8D action). These environments feature complex, nonlinear, contact-rich dynamics.
- **LTL Specifications**: Complex LTL formulas were designed for each environment, incorporating safety, repetition, reachability, and sequencing requirements. For example, the legged-robot tasks required maintaining torso height, achieving a forward velocity, and then coming to a stop.
- **Baselines**:
 - Non-differentiable RL (̸∂RL): Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Soft Actor-Critic (SAC).
 - Differentiable RL (∂RL): Short Horizon Actor-Critic (SHAC) and Adaptive Horizon Actor-Critic (AHAC), which represent the state-of-the-art in differentiable RL.
- **Metric**: LTL rewards, averaged over 5 seeds, serving as a proxy for LTL satisfaction probabilities (bounded between 0 and 1).

**B. Performance Comparison (Differentiable vs. Discrete LTL Rewards):**
- **Overall Trend**: ∂RL algorithms (SHAC, AHAC) leveraging differentiable LTL rewards consistently outperformed ̸∂RL algorithms (PPO, SAC) in terms of both maximum return achieved and speed of convergence.
- **CartPole**: In this low-dimensional but sparse environment, ∂RLs converged to near-optimal policies (Pr > 0.8) within 20 million steps. ̸∂RLs largely failed to learn meaningful policies even after 100 million steps, indicating the severe impact of sparsity.
- **Legged Robots (Hopper, Cheetah, Ant)**: As environment dimensionality and LTL complexity increased, the performance gap widened.
 - **Hopper**: ∂RLs achieved near-optimal policies (Pr > 0.8) within 20 million steps. PPO eventually converged after 100 million steps, while SAC showed inconsistent learning or got stuck in local optima.
 - **Cheetah**: ∂RLs reached optimal performance (Pr > 0.9). PPO converged to suboptimal policies, and SAC consistently failed.
 - **Ant**: ∂RLs learned near-optimal policies rapidly. ̸∂RLs only converged to suboptimal policies.
- The results indicate that the smooth, continuous gradients provided by differentiable LTL rewards enable ∂RL algorithms to efficiently navigate the policy landscape and overcome the sparsity issue that cripples ̸∂RLs.

**C. Generalization to Reward Machines:**
The method's compatibility with automaton-based rewards was demonstrated by applying it to Reward Machines (RMs) in the Cheetah environment. Using differentiable RMs with SHAC, the approach achieved significantly higher returns compared to discrete RM baselines reported in prior work, suggesting broad applicability across formal methods frameworks that use automata for reward generation.

**D. Ablation Studies:**
1. **Impact of LTL Reward Differentiability**: SHAC and AHAC were trained with discrete LTL rewards. This resulted in substantial performance degradation across all environments, with performance often worse than PPO and SAC and failing to obtain reasonable policies in high-dimensional settings. This finding supports that the performance gains observed in the main experiments are primarily attributable to the differentiability of LTL rewards, not merely the underlying ∂RL algorithms.
2. **Impact of LTL Formula Complexity**: All algorithms (∂RLs and ̸∂RLs) were tested with simplified LTL formulas. Under these simpler specifications, both ∂RLs and ̸∂RLs successfully learned near-optimal policies (Pr > 0.9). This confirms that the observed lower performance of ̸∂RLs in the main experiments was due to their inability to handle the complexity of the LTL formulas and the resulting reward sparsity, rather than inherent difficulties of the environments themselves.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework that bridges the gap between formal methods and deep reinforcement learning, offering a significant step towards developing more reliable, specification-driven autonomous systems.

**A. Enhanced Learning Efficiency and Correctness:**
The primary significance lies in mitigating the long-standing challenge of sparse rewards in LTL-guided RL without sacrificing the formal correctness of the objectives. By rendering automaton-based rewards differentiable, the method enables gradient-based optimization, which empirically leads to substantially accelerated learning and improved policy performance in complex continuous domains. This represents progress towards combining the high-level expressiveness and rigorous guarantees of LTL with the sample efficiency desirable for real-world applications.

**B. Broader Applicability and Generalization:**
The approach's compatibility with general LTL specifications, including Büchi acceptance conditions, extends its utility beyond simpler co-safe LTL or LTL$_{f}$ fragments. Its demonstrated generalization to reward machines further indicates that this differentiable approach can benefit a wider range of automaton-based formal methods in RL, making it a versatile tool for specifying and learning complex behaviors.

**C. Potential for Safe and Reliable AI Systems:**
The ability to efficiently learn policies directly from formally specified safety and reliability constraints is crucial for deploying AI systems in safety-critical applications (e.g., robotics, autonomous driving, medical systems). This work offers a pathway to develop systems that are "correct-by-construction" in their objectives, reducing the risk of unintended behaviors or misaligned incentives that can arise from hand-crafted, non-formal reward functions.

**D. Advancing Differentiable Simulation and RL:**
By developing a fully differentiable pipeline from LTL specifications to policy gradients, the research pushes the boundaries of differentiable simulation and its integration with RL algorithms. It suggests new directions for exploiting compositional structures of automata to further enhance exploration and transfer learning in differentiable RL.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The authors acknowledge several limitations:
- The method's performance is tied to the quality and efficiency of the underlying differentiable simulators and RL algorithms, and it is not directly applicable to inherently discrete MDPs without further adaptation.
- It introduces an additional hyperparameter (the activation function for probability estimation) that requires tuning.
- Formalizing LTL specifications still demands familiarity with formal logic and domain knowledge.
- Future work includes combining the approach with techniques like counterfactual experience replay and barrier nets, and designing LTL-specific differentiable RL methods that exploit the compositional structure of automata for improved exploration and transfer. Extensions to systems with partial differentiability are also noted as a necessary and exciting research direction.
