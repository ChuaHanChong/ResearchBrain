# STR Robot: Design of an Autonomous Mobile Robot from Simulation to Reality

> [!summary] Summary
> STR Robot is a simulation-to-real autonomy stack for an Ackermann-steered outdoor mobile robot. It combines LiDAR-inertial-visual localization with a dual-layer EKF plus ICP map correction, a turn-penalized improved A* planner, and an Ackermann Geometric MPC that formulates tracking error on SE(2) and optimizes input increments. Real deployment cuts tracking RMSE roughly in half versus standard MPC.

## Problem

- Classical A* yields paths hugging obstacles with sharp turns, unsuitable for non-holonomic vehicles; geometric variants assume structured indoor-like environments rather than outdoor terrain.
- Euclidean-error MPC trackers rely on strict temporal references, inducing oscillation or corner-cutting during sharp maneuvers on steered platforms with actuator limits.
- Accurate continuous-time LiDAR mapping is too heavy for embedded compute, while scan odometry runs near 10 Hz, below the feedback rate tracking controllers need.

## Method

- Improved ==A*== adds a turning-cost penalty, obstacle inflation by vehicle half-width, early goal-connection termination, and collinear-node pruning, then ==Bezier== segments for local dynamic obstacle avoidance.
- **Ackermann Geometric MPC** defines tracking error as a ==Lie-algebra== vector on ==SE(2)==, and augments the state with the prior input so the horizon optimizes input increments under steering bounds.
- Onboard localization uses dual-layer estimation: a local ==EKF== fusing LiDAR-inertial-visual odometry, encoders, and IMU, plus ==ICP== scan-to-map correction on a height-filtered 2D occupancy projection.

## Results

- Improved A* shortened paths from **25.52 m** to **22.35 m**, cut total turning angle from **3712.50** to **170.55** degrees and inflection points from **79.45** to **4.55**, costing **330 ms** planning.
- In simulation, A-GMPC reduced RMSE from **0.1215 m** to **0.0764 m** on lemniscate and **0.2427 m** to **0.1944 m** on square trajectories versus standard MPC.
- Real-world A-GMPC won at every speed; at **1.5 m/s** square tracking RMSE dropped from **1.1475 m** to **0.6182 m**, with both controllers under the **33.3 ms** control period.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Formulating tracking error on the pose manifold rather than in linearized Euclidean coordinates pays off most where curvature is high, such as sharp square corners.
> - Penalizing input increments rather than raw inputs is a cheap way to respect physical steering actuators, improving real robots more than it improves simulation.
> - Sim-to-real gaps widen with speed: simulation advantages shrank to modest gains at 0.6 m/s but grew substantially by 1.5 m/s on hard trajectories.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of autonomous mobile robots has seen increasing applications across various domains, including environmental monitoring, industrial inspection, logistics, and service robotics. A central challenge in this field, particularly for outdoor environments, lies in developing reliable navigation systems that can integrate real-time localization, path planning, and trajectory tracking while operating under real-world constraints such as sensing uncertainty, model inaccuracies, and limited computational resources. The prevalent approach emphasizes the development of these functionalities as a unified onboard system rather than as disparate modules.

A significant aspect of modern robotics research is the use of simulation tools to facilitate the development and validation of complex autonomous systems. Simulation offers a safer, more cost-effective, and repeatable environment for prototyping and evaluating robotic behaviors before deployment on physical hardware. This is particularly relevant for autonomous navigation, where direct physical testing can be time-consuming, expensive, and potentially hazardous during early development phases. However, transferring a navigation framework from a simulated environment to a real-world outdoor robot introduces challenges related to sensor discrepancies, the inherent complexity of natural environments, and the difficulty of perfectly modeling all physical phenomena.

Within the specific sub-fields of autonomous navigation, this work relates to advancements in:
- **Path Planning:** Classical algorithms like A* are efficient but can generate paths with sharp turns unsuitable for non-holonomic robots. Research has focused on improving path smoothness and safety clearance, often by incorporating geometric considerations or cost-aware strategies.
- **Path Tracking:** Model Predictive Control (MPC) is a common framework for trajectory tracking due to its ability to explicitly handle system constraints. Recent research has explored nonlinear MPC, and specifically, geometric MPC (GMPC), which formulates tracking errors directly on configuration manifolds like SE(2) to improve spatial consistency, particularly in dynamic or complex maneuvers.
- **Onboard Localization and 3D Mapping:** Accurate and real-time state estimation and mapping are crucial. While high-accuracy methods exist, their computational demands can limit real-time deployment on embedded platforms. Solutions like FAST-LIO2 and FAST-LIVO2 represent efforts to provide efficient LiDAR-inertial or LiDAR-inertial-visual solutions for real-time state estimation and 3D mapping.

This paper positions itself by addressing the simulation-to-real gap for an Ackermann-steered outdoor mobile robot. Instead of focusing on mechanical design, it concentrates on the software stack for onboard control, self-localization, and autonomous navigation. The integration of an enhanced path planning algorithm, a novel Ackermann Geometric MPC (A-GMPC), and a robust multi-sensor localization system within a unified simulation-to-real pipeline represents an effort to advance the practical deployment of autonomous navigation systems. The release of source code further contributes to the reproducibility and acceleration of research in this domain.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the challenges associated with developing and deploying autonomous navigation systems for outdoor mobile robots. Specifically, the authors identify the high costs, time consumption, and safety risks involved in testing directly on physical robots during early development stages. While simulation offers a viable alternative, the successful transfer of navigation frameworks from simulation to reality, especially for outdoor environments, remains an ongoing challenge due to factors such as sensor model differences, environmental complexity, and imperfect physical modeling.

Against this background, the key objectives of this paper are detailed as follows:

- **To present a practical and reproducible simulation-to-real pipeline for an autonomous navigation system.** The work aims to demonstrate a viable methodology for developing and validating robotic autonomy in a simulated environment before transitioning to real-world deployment on an existing mobile robot platform. This involves constructing a simulation environment that closely mimics the real-world experimental setup.
- **To develop and integrate a comprehensive onboard autonomy stack for an existing outdoor mobile robot platform.** The research focuses on the software components required for autonomous operation, specifically self-localization, map-based path planning, and path tracking. This involves creating a unified system rather than developing these modules in isolation.
- **To investigate the behavior and performance of modern planning and tracking modules within a complete onboard navigation system.** A specific emphasis is placed on examining the integration of an enhanced path planning algorithm and a novel geometric tracking formulation.
- **To integrate an Ackermann Geometric MPC (A-GMPC) tracking controller into a unified onboard autonomy stack.** The objective is to design a path tracking controller that is specifically tailored for Ackermann-steered vehicles and leverages geometric error formulations on the SE(2) manifold, augmented with input-increment optimization, to promote smoother and more accurate real-world actuation compared to standard MPC approaches.
- **To validate the proposed framework through extensive experimental evaluation in both simulation and real-world settings.** This objective includes demonstrating the feasibility of the simulation-to-real transfer and quantifying the performance improvements of the developed modules, particularly the A-GMPC controller.
- **To contribute to the broader robotics community by releasing the experimental setup, source code, and results.** This aims to facilitate further research and development in autonomous mobile robot navigation.

In summary, the motivation is to bridge the gap between simulation and real-world deployment for outdoor autonomous mobile robots by developing, integrating, and validating a robust onboard autonomy stack, with a particular focus on improving path planning and tracking performance for Ackermann-steered platforms.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed autonomous navigation framework integrates three core modules: onboard localization and mapping, global path planning, and path tracking. The entire system is designed with a simulation-to-real workflow, where development and initial testing occur in a simulated Gazebo environment before deployment on a physical robot.

**A. System Overview**
The architecture begins with an **onboard self-localization and mapping module** that processes sensor data (LiDAR, IMU, camera) to perform real-time state estimation and construct a 3D point-cloud map. For ground navigation, the robot's pose is simplified to planar motion, and the 3D map is projected into a 2D occupancy grid. This information feeds into the **global path planning module**, which generates a collision-free path from a start to a goal location. A **local obstacle avoidance module** can further refine this path during execution for immediate safety. Finally, the **path tracking module** computes control inputs to enable the robot to follow the planned trajectory, comparing a standard MPC baseline with the proposed Ackermann Geometric MPC (A-GMPC).

**B. Path Planning Module**
The environment is represented as a 2D grid map. An **improved A* algorithm** is employed for path generation.
- **Obstacle Expansion:** To ensure safety, obstacles are expanded based on the robot's maximum boundary distance and grid resolution.
- **Cost Function Modification:** The A* evaluation function (F(n) = G(n) + H(n)) is modified to include a penalty (C) for direction changes in the accumulated cost G(n), promoting smoother paths. The heuristic H(n) is the Euclidean distance to the goal.
- **Early Termination:** The algorithm enhances computational efficiency by checking if the current node can be directly connected to the goal without collision, allowing for early termination of the search.
- **Post-processing for Smoothness:** After generating the discrete path, redundant inflection points are removed by identifying and eliminating collinear intermediate nodes, reducing unnecessary turns.
- **Reference Trajectory Generation:** The simplified and smoothed global path is then used to generate a reference trajectory consisting of poses (T_ref,k ∈ SE(2)) and corresponding control inputs (u_ref,k).
- **Dynamic Obstacle Avoidance:** During real-time execution, a local module monitors the global path within a local costmap. If a moving obstacle is detected, the affected segment of the path is replaced by a C2-smooth Bezier curve. The start and end points of this curve are fixed on the original path, while intermediate control points are shifted to provide clearance from the obstacle, thus preserving the global route while enabling local adaptation.

**C. Path Tracking Module**
Two controllers are implemented for comparative evaluation:
- **Standard MPC Baseline:** This controller utilizes a linearized Euclidean error-state formulation and the Ackermann kinematic bicycle model. It solves a finite-horizon quadratic optimization problem to minimize lateral and heading errors, subject to steering limits.
- **Ackermann Geometric MPC (A-GMPC):**
 - **Geometric Error Formulation:** This approach defines the tracking error (E) directly on the robot's pose manifold SE(2) as E = T_ref^-1 * T, where T and T_ref are current and reference poses. The Lie algebra representation (ξ) of this error is used for control.
 - **Error Dynamics:** Continuous-time error dynamics are linearized around the reference trajectory, and then discretized using Euler approximation to obtain ξ_k+1 = A_d,k * ξ_k + B_d,k * ˜u_k, where ˜u_k is the input deviation from the reference.
 - **Input Increment Optimization:** To promote smoother actuation for physical Ackermann steering systems, the A-GMPC optimizes the *input increment* (∆u_k = ˜u_k - ˜u_k-1) rather than the control input directly. This is achieved by introducing an augmented state ¯ξ_k = [ξ_k^T, ˜u_k-1^T]^T, leading to an augmented prediction model.
 - **Optimization Problem:** The controller solves a finite-horizon quadratic optimization problem that minimizes a cost function comprising tracking error (∥ξ_k∥_Q^2), input deviation (∥˜u_k∥_R^2), and input increment (∥∆u_k∥_R∆^2), subject to the augmented dynamics and actuator bounds (e.g., maximum velocity and steering angle).
 - **Ackermann Steering Conversion:** The optimized linear (v) and angular (ω) velocity commands are then converted into Ackermann steering angle (δ) using the kinematic relation δ = arctan(ωL/v), where L is the wheelbase.

**D. Onboard Self-Localization and Mapping Module**
This module provides both high-rate feedback for control and globally consistent poses.
- **Dual-Layer Estimation:**
 - **Local EKF (High-Rate):** A local Extended Kalman Filter fuses low-rate scan-based odometry (LiDAR-inertial or LiDAR-inertial-visual from FAST-LIO2/FAST-LIVO2) with high-rate wheel encoder and IMU measurements. This provides smooth, high-frequency state feedback (e.g., >30 Hz) required by the controller.
 - **Map-Level Consistency:** A second estimator maintains global consistency by correcting the local odometry frame with respect to a global map frame. ICP-based scan-to-map matching against a 2D occupancy map provides map-referenced observations.
- **Map Generation:** The system builds a registered 3D point-cloud map. For ground navigation, this 3D map is height-filtered and then projected onto a 2D occupancy grid, suitable for the path planning module. The module supports both offline mapping (using a pre-built map) and online mapping for unknown environments.

The entire framework is developed and tested in Gazebo simulation, using a virtual environment designed to closely resemble the real-world setup. This allows for iterative refinement before deployment on the custom-built Ackermann-steered physical robot equipped with a 3D LiDAR, an RGB-D camera, and an IMU.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents findings from both simulation and real-world experiments, evaluating path planning, path tracking, and end-to-end autonomous navigation.

**A. Simulation Experiments**
1. **Simulation Setup:** Experiments were conducted in Gazebo using a non-holonomic robot model. The virtual environment was designed to emulate the real-world experimental setting, incorporating elements such as LiDAR, top-down, and camera views.
2. **Path Planning Evaluation:** The improved A* planner was compared against a baseline A* algorithm across 20 randomized start-goal queries.
 - The improved A* consistently generated **shorter paths** (average 22.35 m vs. 25.52 m for baseline).
 - It produced significantly **smoother paths** with a drastically reduced total turning angle (average 170.55° vs. 3712.50°) and fewer inflection points (average 4.55 vs. 79.45), indicating better suitability for non-holonomic robots.
 - This improvement came at the cost of a moderately **higher planning time** (average 330.54 ms vs. 221.15 ms), which was still within acceptable limits for typical robotic applications.
3. **Path-Tracking Validation in Simulation:** The proposed A-GMPC controller was compared against the standard MPC on lemniscate and square trajectories.
 - A-GMPC achieved **lower Root Mean Square Error (RMSE)** on both trajectories. For the lemniscate path, A-GMPC had an RMSE of 0.0764 m compared to 0.1215 m for standard MPC. For the square trajectory, A-GMPC achieved 0.1944 m compared to 0.2427 m for standard MPC.
 - The improvement was more pronounced on the lemniscate path. Qualitatively, A-GMPC exhibited more accurate tracking behavior.
4. **Dynamic Obstacle Avoidance:** Evaluation demonstrated the functionality of a hierarchical navigation framework. The global A* path was generated once, and a local avoidance module dynamically updated the reference path to circumvent moving obstacles. This allowed the robot to maintain its global route while performing real-time, local collision avoidance.

**B. Real-World Experiments**
Experiments were performed using a custom Ackermann-steered physical mobile robot equipped with a 3D LiDAR, RGB-D camera, and IMU.
1. **Real-World Path-Tracking Performance:** The architecture was deployed on the physical robot, and tracking accuracy was evaluated at average speeds of 0.6 m/s, 1.1 m/s, and 1.5 m/s on both lemniscate and square trajectories.
 - A-GMPC consistently demonstrated **lower RMSE than the standard MPC** across all tested trajectories and speeds.
 - For the lemniscate path, A-GMPC achieved RMSEs of 0.2349 m (0.6 m/s), 0.3048 m (1.1 m/s), and 0.3582 m (1.5 m/s), outperforming the standard MPC (0.3194 m, 0.3281 m, 0.5149 m, respectively).
 - The improvement was particularly notable on the square trajectory, which involves sharp turns. A-GMPC recorded RMSEs of 0.2588 m (0.6 m/s), 0.2777 m (1.1 m/s), and 0.6182 m (1.5 m/s), significantly better than standard MPC (0.4260 m, 0.4413 m, 1.1475 m, respectively).
 - Qualitative results indicated that A-GMPC produced more accurate tracking, especially during cornering maneuvers.
 - **Runtime statistics** showed that both standard MPC and A-GMPC executed within the 30 Hz control loop (approximately 33.3 ms), indicating real-time performance capability.
2. **Autonomous Navigation Demonstration:** An end-to-end experiment validated the complete autonomy pipeline in a real-world outdoor setting.
 - The onboard perception module successfully reconstructed a 3D point-cloud map of the experimental site, which was then converted into a 2D occupancy map for global planning.
 - The system demonstrated the ability to integrate onboard localization, mapping, path planning, and trajectory tracking within a unified framework, allowing the robot to autonomously navigate from a start to a goal.
 - The results showcased the robot's ability to follow a planned reference path effectively in a real-world environment.

In summary, the experimental results indicate that the improved A* planner generates superior paths, and the proposed A-GMPC controller consistently achieves better tracking accuracy than the standard MPC in both simulation and real-world scenarios, particularly for maneuvers requiring sharp turns. The end-to-end demonstration validated the feasibility of the integrated simulation-to-real autonomous navigation system.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work presented in this paper carries several points of significance and potential impact for the field of autonomous mobile robotics:

- **Validation of Simulation-to-Real Transfer:** The research provides empirical evidence supporting the feasibility and effectiveness of developing and validating complex autonomous navigation systems in simulation before deployment on physical robots. This simulation-to-real pipeline can reduce development costs, accelerate iteration cycles, and enhance safety during the prototyping phase for new robotic platforms or capabilities. The close resemblance between the simulated and real-world environments used in the experiments contributes to the credibility of this transfer.
- **Development of an Integrated Onboard Autonomy Stack:** The paper demonstrates the successful integration of critical autonomy modules—onboard localization and mapping, path planning, and path tracking—into a unified, real-time operating system. This holistic approach, rather than focusing on isolated components, is crucial for practical robotic deployment in dynamic and uncertain outdoor environments.
- **Advancement in Path Planning for Non-Holonomic Robots:** The improved A* algorithm, which incorporates turning costs and redundant point removal, addresses a known limitation of classical A* by generating smoother and safer paths. This directly benefits non-holonomic platforms like Ackermann-steered robots, where abrupt turns are kinematically challenging and energy-inefficient.
- **Enhanced Path Tracking Performance for Ackermann Vehicles:** The proposed Ackermann Geometric MPC (A-GMPC) represents a notable contribution to trajectory tracking control. By formulating tracking errors geometrically on the SE(2) manifold and optimizing input increments, it demonstrates consistently superior tracking accuracy compared to a standard MPC, particularly during complex maneuvers and at higher speeds in real-world conditions. This improvement is critical for applications requiring precise navigation and smooth motion, such as autonomous driving or agricultural robotics.
- **Robust Onboard Localization and Mapping:** The dual-layer EKF architecture for state estimation, combining high-rate local odometry with globally consistent map-referenced corrections, addresses the need for both immediate feedback for control and long-term accurate positioning. The integration of efficient LiDAR-inertial-visual odometry (FAST-LIVO2/FAST-LIO2) further contributes to reliable environmental perception and mapping.
- **Contribution to Open Science and Reproducibility:** The authors' commitment to releasing the source code and experimental details is a valuable contribution. This transparency can facilitate reproducibility of their results, enable other researchers to build upon their work, and potentially accelerate the development of similar autonomous systems within the robotics community.
- **Practical Implications for Outdoor Robotics:** The successful end-to-end autonomous navigation demonstration on a physical outdoor robot highlights the practical applicability of the developed framework. This work can serve as a foundation for developing autonomous capabilities in various outdoor robotic platforms, ranging from service robots to larger autonomous vehicles in unstructured or semi-structured environments.

Future research, as suggested by the authors, will focus on robustness against dynamic obstacles (humans and moving objects) and navigation in more complex environments, indicating the potential for this work to be a baseline for further advancements in real-world autonomous navigation.
