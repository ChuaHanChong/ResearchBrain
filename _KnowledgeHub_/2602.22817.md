# Hierarchy-of-Groups Policy Optimization for Long-Horizon Agentic Tasks

> [!summary] Summary
> Hierarchy-of-Groups Policy Optimization (HGPO), developed by researchers at Nanyang Technological University and Southeast University, introduces a reinforcement learning algorithm that resolves historical context inconsistency in stepwise group-based training for long-horizon agentic tasks. It achieved state-of-the-art performance with a 94.85% in-distribution success rate on ALFWorld and improved generalization capabilities by refining advantage estimation through context-aware hierarchical grouping.

## Problem

- Stepwise group-based reinforcement learning (RL) for long-horizon agentic tasks suffers from historical context inconsistency, where steps sharing the same current state can have different historical prompts, leading to biased advantage estimation.
- Existing stepwise group-based methods like GRPO and GiGPO implicitly assume consistency in historical contexts when grouping steps, causing a degradation in policy optimization and generalization.
- Using only "Oracle" steps (identical current states and historical contexts) provides unbiased estimates but is impractical for stable training due to their scarcity and resulting high variance.

## Method

- **HGPO** introduces context-aware ==hierarchical grouping==, organizing rollout steps into multiple levels based on the consistency of their k-step historical contexts, ranging from 0-context (current state only) to K-context (full consistent history).
- An adaptive weighting advantage estimation scheme combines advantages from these different hierarchical groups, assigning higher weights to groups with longer, more consistent historical contexts to prioritize more accurate relative advantage comparisons.
- The final, context-aware advantage signal is integrated into a PPO-like clipped objective function for robust and stable policy optimization.

## Results

- HGPO consistently achieved state-of-the-art performance, with a **94.85%** in-distribution success rate on ALFWorld and a **90.64%** task score on WebShop using Qwen2.5-**1.5B**-Instruct, surpassing baselines including closed-source LLMs like GPT-4o and previous RL methods.
- The algorithm demonstrated improved generalization to out-of-distribution tasks in ALFWorld, showing less performance degradation compared to prior methods.
- HGPO yielded larger performance gains on smaller LLMs (e.g., an average **3.41%** gain on Qwen2.5-**1.5B**) and introduced minimal computational overhead, increasing execution time by less than **0.001%** compared to GRPO and GiGPO.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Historical context inconsistency is a fundamental source of bias in advantage estimation for stepwise group-based RL in multi-turn LLM agent tasks.
> - A bias-variance trade-off exists: more consistent historical contexts yield lower-bias advantage estimates but are less frequent, while less consistent contexts are abundant but introduce higher bias.
> - By systematically aggregating advantage estimates across different levels of context consistency, HGPO effectively interpolates this trade-off, providing a more robust and accurate training signal.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper addresses a critical challenge in the rapidly evolving field of Large Language Model (LLM)-based decision-making agents, particularly for *long-horizon agentic tasks*. These agents are expected to perceive, reason, and act over extended sequences of interactions in dynamic environments, moving beyond simple single-turn responses.

**Evolution of LLM Agents:** The field has seen a progression from fixed pre-trained LLMs guided by structured prompting (e.g., ReAct, Reflexion) to models enhanced with memory, retrieval mechanisms, and tool integration. While powerful, these prompting-based methods often lack the specialized knowledge and adaptability required for complex, domain-specific tasks.

**Role of Reinforcement Learning:** Reinforcement Learning (RL) has emerged as a key paradigm to post-train and adapt LLMs for dynamic environments. Early applications involved classic RL algorithms like DQN and PPO in text games and embodied tasks. More recently, RL has been integrated into LLM training itself, most notably with Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) for alignment.

**Group-based RL for Scalability:** A significant development in scaling RL for LLMs has been the advent of *group-based RL algorithms* (e.g., GRPO, RLOO, DAPO). These methods are designed to compute advantages directly from groups of sampled trajectories, eschewing the need for a separate value network, which reduces computational overhead. This makes them highly attractive for large-scale LLM training, especially in tasks like mathematical reasoning and code generation.

**The Long-Horizon Challenge and Context Explosion:** For *long-horizon agentic tasks*, two main RL frameworks have emerged:
1. **Trajectory-wise Policy Optimization:** This concatenates the full interaction history, but suffers from "context explosion" as the context length rapidly grows with the number of turns, limiting scalability.
2. **Stepwise Policy Optimization:** This framework treats each step independently while using a memory module to retain a *limited* historical context (K recent interactions). This design offers better context management and scalability.

**The Identified Gap: Context Inconsistency:** This paper critically identifies a fundamental limitation in existing *stepwise group-based RL methods* like GRPO and its extension GiGPO. Even when steps share the same *current state* (as assumed by some step-level grouping methods), their *historical contexts* in the memory module can differ. This "historical context inconsistency" leads to biased advantage estimation, which in turn degrades policy optimization and generalization, especially in complex, long-horizon tasks.

HGPO directly addresses this gap by refining how advantages are estimated in stepwise group-based RL, aiming to provide a more accurate and stable training signal without increasing computational burden significantly. It builds on the principles of group-based RL and stepwise optimization but adds a crucial layer of context awareness.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to **develop a novel and robust policy optimization algorithm that effectively addresses the issue of historical context inconsistency in stepwise group-based reinforcement learning for long-horizon agentic tasks.** The ultimate goal is to enable more accurate advantage estimation, leading to significantly improved and more stable policy optimization for LLM agents in complex, multi-turn environments.

The motivation for this work stems from several key observations and challenges:

1. **Prevalence of LLM Agents in Long-Horizon Tasks:** LLMs are increasingly deployed as versatile agents in complex environments (e.g., embodied navigation, web browsing, interactive games) that require multi-turn interaction and robust decision-making over long horizons.
2. **Limitations of Existing RL for LLMs:** While group-based RL methods have shown promise for single-turn tasks and offer computational efficiency, their extension to multi-turn, long-horizon settings faces difficulties. Trajectory-wise approaches suffer from context explosion. Stepwise approaches, while more scalable, implicitly assume consistency in historical contexts when grouping steps, which is often not true.
3. **Empirical Evidence of Bias:** The authors conduct a pilot study revealing that both trajectory-level and existing step-level advantage estimations (e.g., in GRPO and GiGPO) exhibit significant bias due to inconsistent historical contexts. This bias severely degrades the quality of the optimization signal.
4. **Scarcity of "Oracle" Steps:** An ideal solution would be to only group and compare "Oracle" steps that share *identical* current states and *identical* historical contexts. However, the empirical analysis shows that such Oracle steps are rare, leading to small group sizes and high variance in advantage estimates, making this approach impractical for stable training.
5. **Need for a Better Bias-Variance Trade-off:** The challenge is to devise an advantage estimator that can simultaneously reduce bias (by considering context consistency) and maintain low variance (by utilizing sufficient sample sizes). Current methods either have high bias (step-level with inconsistent context) or high variance (sparse Oracle groups).

By addressing these motivations, HGPO aims to provide a more reliable and efficient mechanism for training LLM agents, pushing the boundaries of their performance and generalization capabilities in complex, real-world scenarios.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Hierarchy-of-Groups Policy Optimization (HGPO) algorithm is designed to tackle the historical context inconsistency issue in stepwise group-based RL. It introduces two core components: context-aware hierarchical grouping and adaptive weighting advantage estimation.

#### 3.1. The Issue of Historical Context Inconsistency

The paper first articulates the problem: in stepwise policy optimization, even if multiple rollout steps share the same *current state*, their *historical contexts* (stored in the memory module) can differ. This means the effective prompts for generating actions are not identical, leading to biased relative advantage estimations if these steps are grouped together naively. The authors empirically demonstrate this bias using "Oracle groups," which are defined by identical current states *and* identical historical contexts. They show that while Oracle groups provide unbiased estimates, they are too scarce and small to be practical for stable policy optimization, leading to high variance.

#### 3.2. Hierarchy-of-Groups Policy Optimization (HGPO)

HGPO addresses this by structuring advantage estimation in a more nuanced way:

**a. Context-aware Hierarchical Grouping:**
This component organizes steps into multiple levels of groups based on the consistency of their historical contexts.
- **Definition of k-step context:** For any step `t` in trajectory `i`, `C_k(s_t^(i))` returns the `k` historical states immediately preceding and including the current state `s_t^(i)`. If `t < k`, it includes all available preceding states. This allows defining contexts of varying lengths.
- **Hierarchical Group Formation:** For a given step `(s_t^(i), a_t^(i))`, its `k`-th hierarchical group, `G_H_k(s_t^(i))`, comprises all other steps `(s_n^(j), a_n^(j))` from the entire rollout group that share the *exact same k-step context* as `s_t^(i)`.
- **Hierarchy Structure:** This naturally forms a hierarchy: `G_H_0(s_t^(i)) ⊇ G_H_1(s_t^(i)) ⊇ ··· ⊇ G_H_K(s_t^(i))`. `G_H_0` represents grouping solely by current state (similar to basic step-level grouping in GiGPO), while `G_H_K` (where `K` is the maximum context length) represents grouping by the fullest possible consistent historical context, approaching the "Oracle" ideal.
- **Efficiency:** This grouping process is efficient, performed offline using hashmap lookups over existing rollouts, requiring no additional models or data collection.

**b. Adaptive Weighting Advantage Estimation:**
This component combines the advantages estimated from different hierarchical groups.
- **Individual Advantage Estimation:** For each `k`-th hierarchical group `G_H_k`, an advantage `A_H_k(s_t^(i))` is computed using a standard group-relative advantage formula (similar to GRPO), comparing the reward of `s_t^(i)` against the average reward of all steps within that specific `k`-th group.
- **Stepwise Reward:** To enable step-level credit assignment, a stepwise reward `r_t^(i)` is computed as the discounted sum of future rewards from step `t` onwards, `r_t^(i) = Σ_(j=t)^T γ^(j-t) r_j^(i)`.
- **Adaptive Aggregation:** The final advantage `A_H(s_t^(i))` for a step `s_t^(i)` is an adaptively weighted sum of the advantages from all its hierarchical groups: `A_H(s_t^(i)) = Σ_(k=0)^K w_k A_H_k(s_t^(i))`.
- **Weighting Scheme:** The adaptive weight `w_k = (k+1)^α / Σ_p (p+1)^α` (with `α ≥ 0`) prioritizes higher-level groups (i.e., those with longer, more consistent historical contexts) by assigning them larger weights. This is based on the intuition that more consistent contexts lead to more accurate relative advantage comparisons. The authors note `α=1` as default.

**c. Policy Optimization Objective:**
HGPO uses a PPO-like clipped objective function, but crucially, it incorporates the aggregated, context-aware advantage `A_H(s_t^(i))`:
`J_HGPO(θ) = E[min(ρ_θ(a_t^(i))A_H(s_t^(i)), clip(ρ_θ(a_t^(i)), 1 ± ϵ)A_H(s_t^(i)))] - βD_KL(π_θ(·|x) || π_ref(·|x))`
where `ρ_θ` is the importance sampling ratio, `ϵ` is the clipping parameter, and `β` is the KL penalty coefficient.

**d. Bias-Variance Trade-off (Proposition 4.1):**
The authors formally argue that HGPO achieves a favorable bias-variance trade-off. By combining groups with varying levels of context consistency, HGPO interpolates between the high-bias, low-variance characteristics of `G_H_0` (basic step-level grouping) and the low-bias, high-variance characteristics of `G_H_K` (Oracle-like grouping). The adaptive weighting `w_k` is key to balancing these, as it allows prioritizing lower-bias estimates from higher-level groups without suffering from their potentially high variance due to small group sizes.

In essence, HGPO systematically leverages the information across different levels of context consistency, providing a more robust and accurate advantage signal for policy updates, which is vital for effective RL in complex agentic tasks.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of HGPO on two challenging agentic benchmarks, ALFWorld (embodied tasks) and WebShop (web navigation), using Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, yielded several significant findings:

1. **Superior Performance Across Benchmarks and Models:** HGPO consistently achieved state-of-the-art results compared to all baselines, including closed-source LLMs (GPT-4o, Gemini-2.5-Pro), prompting agents (ReAct, Reflexion), and other RL training methods (PPO, RLOO, GRPO, GiGPO).
 - On ALFWorld, HGPO (K=4) with Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct achieved an in-distribution success rate of 94.85% (vs. GiGPO's 93.29%) and an out-of-distribution success rate of 92.12% (vs. GiGPO's 91.53%).
 - On WebShop, HGPO (K=4) with Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct scored 90.64% in task scores (vs. GiGPO's 86.80%) and 78.12% in task success rates (vs. GiGPO's 73.24%).
 - Similar trends, though with smaller margins, were observed with the larger Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct model, with HGPO still maintaining superior performance.

2. **Robust Generalization to Out-of-Distribution Tasks:** HGPO demonstrated less performance degradation on out-of-distribution tasks in ALFWorld compared to GiGPO. This suggests that HGPO's context-aware advantage estimation leads to a more robust policy that generalizes better to unseen scenarios, addressing a key challenge in agentic AI.

3. **Larger Gains on Smaller Models:** HGPO yielded more substantial improvements when applied to the smaller Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct model (average gain of 3.41% for K=2 and 2.72% for K=4) than with the larger Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct model (average gain of 0.74% for K=2 and 0.13% for K=4). The authors attribute this to smaller models generating longer, more redundant rollouts, which amplify the bias in advantage estimation. HGPO's hierarchical grouping proves more critical in such scenarios to refine the signal and stabilize optimization.

4. **Effectiveness of Increasing Context Length (K):** Increasing the maximum context length `K` generally improved the performance of both GiGPO and HGPO, indicating the value of richer historical context. HGPO effectively leverages this larger memory, demonstrating its scalability with context size.

**Further Analysis:**

- **Distribution of Hierarchical Group Sizes:** The analysis showed that `0-context` groups (ignoring history) are generally larger, while `k-context` groups (especially higher `k`) become progressively smaller. This empirically supports the initial motivation that "Oracle" steps (high `k` contexts) are scarce and would lead to high variance if used exclusively.
- **Step Utilization Ratio:** The utilization ratio of steps decreases as `k` increases (fewer steps fit into higher-consistency context groups), again highlighting the scarcity of highly consistent contexts and validating the need for HGPO's multi-level aggregation.
- **Computational Efficiency:** HGPO introduces only minimal additional computational overhead. The increased time cost (approximately 0.425-0.472 seconds per iteration) and slight memory increase are negligible (less than 0.001% of total execution time) compared to GRPO and GiGPO. This is due to the offline nature of the context-aware grouping process, relying primarily on hashmap lookups.
- **Parameter Analysis (α):** The weighting coefficient `α` in the adaptive weighting scheme influences the balance between bias and variance. `α=1` was found to be a good default, effectively weighting higher-level groups. While `α=0` (uniform weights) sometimes performed well for smaller `K`, `α=1` or `α=2` generally scaled better with increasing `K`, demonstrating that up-weighting more accurate, higher-level groups is beneficial, but excessive emphasis can hurt performance due to their smaller size and higher variance. This confirmed the existence of a bias-variance trade-off in hierarchical groups.
- **Ablation Study:**
 - **"w/o G_H_1:K" (removing hierarchical grouping beyond 0-context):** This configuration led to significant performance degradation, particularly on ALFWorld (2.8% in-success, 5.8% out-success drop for K=2/4). This directly validates that directly using the current state for grouping without considering historical context leads to biased advantage estimation.
 - **"w/o Ada. w_k" (uniform weighting, α=0):** Performance sometimes increased for small K but decreased for larger K compared to `α=1`. This highlights the importance of adaptive weighting as K increases to appropriately balance the contributions of different hierarchical groups.
 - **"w Eq. (1)" (adding trajectory-level advantage):** This generally decreased performance, supporting the claim that trajectory-level advantages are often too biased to provide effective information for fine-grained policy optimization.
 - Attempts to train with "Oracle" steps alone (high `K` groups) failed due to high variance, reinforcing the need for HGPO's approach.

In summary, HGPO offers a robust, efficient, and empirically validated solution to a critical problem in long-horizon LLM agent training, significantly boosting performance and generalization without substantial computational cost.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Hierarchy-of-Groups Policy Optimization (HGPO) marks a significant advancement in the training of large language model (LLM) agents for complex, long-horizon tasks. Its contributions and potential impact are multifaceted:

1. **Addressing a Fundamental Problem:** HGPO identifies and rigorously addresses the crucial issue of "historical context inconsistency" in stepwise group-based reinforcement learning. This is not merely an incremental improvement but a rectification of a core flaw in how advantages were previously estimated for multi-turn LLM interactions. By revealing that even steps sharing the same *current state* can have different *effective prompts* due to varying historical contexts, the paper provides a deeper understanding of the challenges in this domain.

2. **Improved Performance and Generalization:** The consistent and substantial performance gains demonstrated across challenging benchmarks (ALFWorld and WebShop) and different LLM sizes underscore HGPO's effectiveness. Crucially, its superior generalization to out-of-distribution tasks is a major step forward, as robust generalization is a key bottleneck for deploying AI agents in real-world, open-ended environments.

3. **Enhanced Training Stability and Efficiency:** By achieving a more favorable bias-variance trade-off in advantage estimation, HGPO contributes to more stable and reliable policy optimization. The methodology is computationally efficient, requiring no additional models or costly rollouts, and incurring only minimal extra time and memory for hashing lookups. This efficiency is critical for the large-scale training of LLMs, which are inherently resource-intensive.

4. **Broader Applicability to Agentic AI:** The principles behind HGPO—context-aware grouping and adaptive weighting—are generalizable. They offer a blueprint for future RL algorithms that aim to train agents in environments where the "true" state representation or effective context is multi-faceted and historical information plays a critical role. This can impact various agentic AI domains, including robotics, software engineering, and general computer control.

5. **Democratization of Advanced RL for Smaller Models:** The observation that HGPO yields particularly large gains on smaller LLMs (Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct) is highly significant. It suggests that HGPO can help lower the barrier to entry for developing capable LLM agents, making advanced RL training more effective for models that might otherwise struggle with long-horizon tasks due to their limited inherent agentic capabilities. This could enable more widespread research and application of agentic LLMs without requiring access to the largest, most expensive foundation models.

6. **Guidance for Future Research:** The paper also opens several promising avenues for future research, such as exploring hierarchical structures for agents with summarized memory (where raw historical contexts are not directly divisible) and developing more sophisticated, uncertainty-aware adaptive weighting schemes. This indicates that HGPO is not an endpoint but a foundational step, inspiring further innovations in context-sensitive RL.

In conclusion, HGPO makes a vital contribution by providing a robust, scalable, and empirically validated solution to a previously unaddressed problem in LLM agent training. Its impact is likely to be felt in both the academic research community, by offering a new paradigm for advantage estimation, and in practical applications, by enabling the development of more capable and reliable AI agents for complex real-world challenges.
