# Agent Learning via Early Experience

> [!summary] Summary
> The "early experience" paradigm enables autonomous language agents to learn and continuously improve from their own interactions with the environment, leveraging implicit feedback from observed future states rather than explicit reward signals. This approach consistently improves task effectiveness, enhances out-of-domain generalization, and provides a robust foundation for subsequent reinforcement learning.

## Problem

- Existing language agent training (supervised fine-tuning) is limited by expensive, non-scalable human expert data, leading to brittle agents with poor generalization.
- Applying reinforcement learning to complex, real-world language agent tasks is challenging due to absent/sparse reward signals and inefficient long-horizon rollouts.
- There is a critical need for language agents to learn continuously from their own interactions and consequences in reward-free, unstructured environments.

## Method

- The =="early experience" paradigm== collects interaction data by executing alternative agent actions and observing the resulting future states, forming a reward-free `D_rollout` dataset.
- ==Implicit World Modeling (IWM)== trains the agent to predict future states from current states and actions, thereby implicitly learning environment dynamics directly within the policy.
- ==Self-Reflection (SR)== generates natural language chain-of-thought explanations that compare expert actions to suboptimal alternatives based on their observed outcomes, providing contrastive supervision for refining agent reasoning.

## Results

- Both IWM and SR consistently improve agent success rates across eight diverse language-agent environments and multiple model sizes, with gains ranging from **+2.3%** to **+18.4%** success rate.
- Early experience methods substantially enhance out-of-domain generalization, helping agents recover a significant portion of performance loss in novel scenarios compared to imitation learning.
- Models pre-trained with early experience provide a superior initialization for subsequent reinforcement learning, achieving higher ultimate performance ceilings during the RL phase.
- The paradigm reduces reliance on expert data, demonstrating comparable or superior performance with significantly fewer human demonstrations, and scales effectively across various model sizes (**3B to 70B** parameters).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Agents can achieve substantial self-improvement and develop a deeper understanding of environment dynamics by learning from the observed consequences of their own actions, even without explicit reward signals.
> - Integrating implicit world modeling and grounded self-reflection provides scalable, reward-free supervision that enhances policy robustness and decision-making beyond rote imitation.
> - "Early experience" serves as a practical, effective bridge, enabling experience-driven learning before full reinforcement learning infrastructure is available and providing a superior warm-start for downstream RL.
> - The effectiveness of these methods stems from converting off-expert rollouts into grounded supervision derived from actual environment outcomes, unlike ungrounded reasoning methods.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of autonomous agents capable of learning and improving through their own experience in complex, real-world tasks is a long-standing objective in artificial intelligence. Recent advancements in large language models (LLMs) have enabled the emergence of "language agents" that can perform various tasks, including navigating websites, using tools, and assisting in scientific research.

Traditionally, two primary paradigms have been explored for training these agents:
1. **Reinforcement Learning (RL)**: This approach trains agents to optimize for long-term rewards obtained from the environment. While highly successful in structured domains like games (e.g., AlphaGo), applying RL to real-world language agents is challenging. Many environments (e.g., websites) lack verifiable or dense reward signals, and tasks often involve long interaction sequences with delayed or ambiguous outcomes, making credit assignment and training inefficient or unstable.
2. **Supervised Fine-tuning (SFT) / Imitation Learning (IL)**: This paradigm bypasses the need for reward signals by training agents on expert-curated demonstrations. While straightforward and efficient, SFT has inherent limitations. Agents trained this way do not interact with the environment during training, restricting their ability to learn from failures, refine decision-making, or generalize to novel situations. This approach also assumes the availability of optimal or near-optimal expert data, which is difficult and costly to scale and covers only a narrow range of scenarios.

This research addresses the limitations of existing paradigms by proposing a "middle-ground" approach called "early experience." It aims to bridge the gap between static imitation learning and full reinforcement learning by enabling agents to learn from self-generated interaction data without requiring explicit reward signals. This positions the work as an attempt to advance experience-driven learning for language agents in environments where traditional RL is impractical, while overcoming the inherent passive nature and scaling challenges of pure imitation learning. The paper contextualizes this as a necessary step towards an "era of experience" for language agents, leveraging scalable data from agent-environment interaction.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable language agents to learn and improve through their own experiences in complex, real-world tasks, particularly in environments that do not provide explicit reward signals or where traditional reinforcement learning is impractical. The authors identify several motivations for this objective:

1. **Addressing the Reward Scarcity Problem**: Many real-world environments, such as websites or open-ended tool-use scenarios, either lack verifiable or dense reward signals entirely, or provide only sparse and delayed feedback. This absence of reliable feedback significantly hinders the application of reinforcement learning, which depends on such signals for optimization.
2. **Overcoming Limitations of Supervised Fine-tuning (SFT)**: While SFT on expert demonstrations is a common workaround, it inherently limits an agent's capabilities. Agents trained with SFT are passive learners; they do not interact with the environment during training, cannot observe the consequences of their own actions, and therefore cannot learn from failures or refine their decision-making. This leads to poor generalization to out-of-domain or unseen situations, as the agent is restricted by the imagination and coverage of its static training data.
3. **Enhancing Data Scalability**: Scaling high-quality human expert demonstrations for SFT is challenging and expensive. The motivation is to find a scalable source of supervision that does not rely on extensive human labeling, thereby moving beyond the constraints of "human data."
4. **Bridging to Reinforcement Learning (RL)**: The authors envision an "era of experience" where agents continuously learn from their interactions. However, the current practical difficulties of applying RL necessitate a more robust starting point. The research is motivated by the need for a paradigm that can serve as a practical and scalable bridge, providing a stronger initialization for future RL applications when verifiable rewards eventually become available.
5. **Improving Generalization and Robustness**: By allowing agents to explore the environment and learn from the outcomes of their own actions, even suboptimal ones, the objective is to improve their understanding of environment dynamics and decision-making principles. This is intended to lead to more robust policies that can generalize better to unseen or unexpected scenarios, mitigating the distribution shift problems inherent in imitation learning.

To achieve these objectives, the paper introduces the "early experience" paradigm and proposes two distinct strategies: Implicit World Modeling and Self-Reflection, both designed to convert self-generated interaction data (future states) into scalable, reward-free supervision signals.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces the "early experience" paradigm within the framework of a Markov Decision Process (MDP), where states and actions in language agent environments are represented in natural language. The core idea is to augment traditional imitation learning with interaction data generated by the agent itself, utilizing the resulting future states as supervision without requiring explicit reward signals.

**1. Foundation: Learning without Rewards (Imitation Learning)**
The baseline approach is Supervised Fine-tuning (SFT), also known as imitation learning. Given a dataset of expert demonstrations $D_{expert} = \{(s_i, a_i)\}$, where $s_i$ is a state and $a_i$ is the expert action, an agent's policy $\pi_\theta$ (parameterized by $\theta$) is trained to minimize the supervised learning loss: $L_{IL}(\theta) = -\sum \log \pi_\theta(a_i | s_i)$. This method is efficient but suffers from distribution shift and lacks awareness of action consequences.

**2. Early Experience Paradigm: Data Generation**
To overcome the limitations of static expert data, the "early experience" paradigm involves generating interaction data:
- **Alternative Action Sampling**: For each expert state $s_i$ in $D_{expert}$, the current agent policy $\pi_\theta(\cdot | s_i)$ samples $K$ alternative actions, $A_i = \{a_1^i, a_2^i, \dots, a_K^i\}$. Crucially, these actions are typically different from the expert action $a_i$.
- **Environment Interaction**: Each alternative action $a_j^i$ is executed in the environment from state $s_i$, leading to a new state $s_j^i$ (sampled from the environment's transition function $T(s_i, a_j^i)$).
- **Rollout Dataset Construction**: These interactions are collected into a rollout dataset $D_{rollout} = \{(s_i, a_j^i, s_j^i)\}$, capturing the immediate consequences of the agent's proposed (including non-expert) actions. This dataset provides rich, reward-free supervision signals.

**3. Strategies for Leveraging Early Experience**
The paper proposes two distinct strategies to convert the future states from $D_{rollout}$ into supervision:

**a. Implicit World Modeling (IWM)**
- **Concept**: This strategy formulates world modeling as an auxiliary prediction task, directly integrated into the agent's policy, allowing it to internalize environment dynamics. Instead of a separate world model for planning, the policy itself learns to predict next states.
- **Process**: For each triplet $(s_i, a_j^i, s_j^i) \in D_{rollout}$, the model is trained to predict the resulting next state $s_j^i$ given the current state $s_i$ and the action $a_j^i$. The training objective is a standard next-token prediction loss: $L_{IWM} = -\sum \log p_\theta(s_j^i | s_i, a_j^i)$.
- **Training Pipeline**: A two-stage process is adopted:
 1. **Stage 1**: Train the agent with $L_{IWM}$ to internalize environment dynamics. This stage includes transitions from expert actions $(s_i, a_i, s_{i+1})$ as well.
 2. **Stage 2**: Fine-tune the model on $D_{expert}$ using $L_{IL}$. This ensures the policy is grounded in expert behavior while having an improved understanding of the environment.
- **Rationale**: By predicting future states, the model captures transition regularities, side effects, and outcomes of invalid actions, making it more robust to distribution shifts and less reliant on brittle expert trajectories.

**b. Self-Reflection (SR)**
- **Concept**: This strategy enables agents to learn from their own exploratory outcomes by comparing expert actions with alternatives and generating natural language explanations for why the expert action is superior.
- **Process**:
 1. For each expert state $s_i$, the expert action $a_i$ leads to $s_{i+1}$, and alternative actions $a_j^i$ lead to $s_j^i$.
 2. The language model is prompted to generate a chain-of-thought explanation $c_j^i$ detailing why the expert action $a_i$ is preferable to the alternative action $a_j^i$, grounded in the observed differences between $s_{i+1}$ and $s_j^i$.
 3. These triplets $(s_i, a_j^i, c_j^i)$ form the reflection dataset $D_{refl}$.
- **Training Pipeline**: The agent is trained to jointly predict the generated chain-of-thought $c_j^i$ and the expert action $a_i$ given the state $s_i$. The loss is $L_{SR} = -\sum \log p_\theta(c_j^i, a_i | s_i)$. This data $D_{refl}$ is mixed with $D_{expert}$ (retaining expert CoT if available) for training using a standard next-token prediction loss.
- **Rationale**: This method provides richer, transferable supervision than just expert actions. By explicitly reasoning about the optimality of expert actions against suboptimal alternatives, the agent internalizes generalized decision principles and improves its capacity for logical decision-making.

**4. Experimental Setup**
- **Models**: The methods are evaluated across Llama-3.2-3B, Qwen-2.5-7B, Llama-3.1-8B, and larger models (Llama-3.3-70B, Qwen-2.5-72B) for scaling studies.
- **Environments**: Eight diverse benchmarks are used: ALFWorld (embodied navigation), ScienceWorld (scientific simulation), TravelPlanner (long-horizon planning), BFCLv3 (multi-turn tool use), Tau-Bench (multi-domain APIs), SearchQA (multi-hop QA), WebShop (web navigation), and WebArena-Lite (complex web navigation).
- **Evaluation**: Performance is measured primarily by success rate (or F1 for SearchQA). Out-of-domain generalization and compatibility with post-hoc reinforcement learning (using the GRPO algorithm) are also assessed. Hyperparameters are kept consistent for fair comparisons, with training budgets (e.g., number of steps) matched to the imitation learning baseline.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides extensive empirical evidence supporting the effectiveness and versatility of the early experience paradigm.

**Overall Effectiveness:**
- Both Implicit World Modeling (IWM) and Self-Reflection (SR) consistently outperform purely imitation learning (IL) baselines across all eight diverse environments and multiple model families (Llama-3.2-3B, Qwen-2.5-7B, Llama-3.1-8B).
- IWM demonstrates consistent gains, particularly in structured environments with predictable dynamics, such as ALFWorld (+2.3% to +5.5% success rate) and WebShop (+11.3% to +18.4% success rate). This indicates its success in helping policies internalize transition regularities.
- SR yields larger performance improvements in tasks requiring multi-step reasoning and constraint satisfaction, such as TravelPlanner (+12.8% to +15.0% success rate), ScienceWorld (+13.3% for Llama-3.1-8B), and BFCLv3 (+8.0% for Llama-3.2-3B). This suggests its strength in refining logical decision-making.
- The benefits extend across different action spaces (closed, structured, open) and observation complexities (simple textual descriptions to noisy web states), indicating broad applicability.

**Out-of-Domain (OOD) Generalization:**
- Early experience methods consistently improve robustness in OOD settings. Although OOD scores generally drop compared to in-domain performance, early experience recovers a substantial portion of this gap.
- In some benchmarks (e.g., SearchQA), the relative OOD gains surpass in-domain gains, suggesting that self-generated experience better prepares policies for novel situations not covered in expert demonstrations.
- IWM is more effective when environment dynamics remain stable, while SR shows stronger performance when distribution shifts involve changes in tool availability or arguments.

**Reinforcement Learning (RL) Compatibility:**
- When a subsequent RL stage (using the GRPO algorithm) is applied to checkpoints, models initialized with early experience (IWM or SR) consistently achieve higher post-RL performance ceilings compared to those starting from imitation learning checkpoints.
- The performance gap between early experience and IL often widens or is maintained during RL training, demonstrating that early experience provides a superior foundation.
- Direct RL from raw pretrained models without any supervised stage performed worst and exhibited unstable training, underscoring the importance of robust initialization.

**Impact of Human Data Amount:**
- Early experience maintains a consistent performance lead over imitation learning even when the amount of expert data is reduced.
- On WebShop, early experience with only 1/8 of the expert demonstrations surpassed the performance of imitation learning trained on the full dataset. Similar trends were observed on ALFWorld with 1/2 of the demonstrations. This indicates that early experience can effectively augment limited expert data.

**Impact of Branching Factor (K):**
- For IWM, performance steadily improves as the branching factor K (number of alternative actions sampled per expert state) increases, supporting the idea that more diverse transition regularities are learned.
- For SR, performance improves at small to moderate K but shows diminishing returns at very large K. This is attributed to limitations in reasoning over excessively many alternatives and the potential for including other success-leading actions, which reduces the contrast with the expert.

**Comparison to Baselines:**
- Early experience methods consistently outperform other reasoning enhancement baselines, including test-time "Long CoT" prompting, "STaR-style" training with ungrounded rationales, and Direct Preference Optimization (DPO).
- Long CoT provided only modest gains and could degrade performance when applied to IL models without inherent rationales.
- STaR-style training, which uses ungrounded rationales, often led to performance degradation, highlighting the criticality of grounding feedback in actual environment interactions.
- DPO was found to be weaker and more brittle, suggesting that preference signals over off-expert rollouts may be less effective than the direct grounding provided by IWM or the detailed rationales in SR.

**Model Scaling:**
- The benefits of early experience persist and are observed across various model sizes (3B, 8B, 70B), with the performance gap over imitation learning consistently maintained or amplified.
- Early-experience checkpoints consistently form the upper bound of performance curves as model scale increases, indicating that the approach complements larger models rather than simply substituting for scale.
- The methods remain effective even when using parameter-efficient fine-tuning techniques like LoRA.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "early experience" paradigm presents a notable advancement in the training of autonomous language agents, offering a practical and scalable solution to several long-standing challenges in the field.

**Scientific Significance:**
- **Novel Bridge Paradigm**: The work formally introduces and validates "early experience" as a distinct training paradigm that effectively bridges the gap between reward-free imitation learning (learning from human data) and reward-driven reinforcement learning (learning from agent experience). This addresses a critical need in environments where explicit rewards are scarce or impractical for full RL.
- **Leveraging Self-Generated Data**: It demonstrates that an agent's own exploratory actions and their resulting future states can be effectively converted into scalable, reward-free supervision signals. This highlights the potential for autonomous self-improvement without continuous human intervention or elaborate reward engineering.
- **Mechanisms for Experience-Driven Learning**: The proposed strategies, Implicit World Modeling and Self-Reflection, offer concrete mechanisms for how agents can internalize environment dynamics and refine decision-making through self-generated experience. IWM directly grounds the policy in observed state transitions, while SR enhances reasoning by prompting the agent to reflect on its own suboptimal choices.

**Practical Significance and Potential Impact:**
- **Improved Agent Capabilities**: The consistent performance gains across diverse tasks—including embodied navigation, scientific simulation, long-horizon planning, multi-turn tool use, and web navigation—indicate that agents trained with early experience are more effective and robust than those relying solely on imitation learning.
- **Enhanced Generalization**: The improved out-of-domain generalization demonstrates that learning from early experience helps agents perform better in novel or unforeseen situations, a crucial requirement for real-world deployment.
- **Accelerated RL Adoption**: By providing a stronger foundation and initialization for subsequent reinforcement learning, early experience can accelerate the development of fully experience-driven agents. When RL becomes viable in new environments, agents can immediately leverage these gains without starting from scratch. This makes the transition to the "era of experience" more feasible.
- **Reduced Data Dependency and Cost**: The ability of early experience to achieve comparable or superior performance with less expert data (e.g., 1/8 to 1/2 of demonstrations) significantly reduces the reliance on costly, high-quality human annotations. This can lower the barriers to training capable agents.
- **Scalability**: The demonstrated effectiveness across various model sizes (from 3B to 70B) and its compatibility with parameter-efficient fine-tuning techniques suggest that the paradigm is scalable and applicable in resource-constrained settings.

**Societal and Ethical Considerations (as per Impact Statement):**
- **Advancement of Autonomous Agents**: The work contributes to the development of more capable autonomous agents, which could have broad positive impacts across various industries and applications by automating complex tasks.
- **Dual-Use Potential**: Reducing the data requirements for training agents could also lower the barriers to training agents for malicious purposes. This necessitates careful consideration of deployment guidelines, access controls, and ethical frameworks as general agents mature, especially in open-ended environments like the web. The potential for misuse of highly capable agents, if not adequately controlled, could pose risks.
