# World Observer: Joint Actor-Observer Generation for Persistent World Modeling

> [!summary] Summary
> World Observer is a video world model that separates observing from acting. A single diffusion transformer jointly generates the agent's perspective actor view and one or more low-resolution 360° panoramic observer streams that share one world state. Both streams are grounded by depth-warping a shared panorama, and an Observer Sink adds high-resolution perspective references. Objects that leave the actor's view keep evolving, giving the strongest out-of-view dynamics among the compared world models.

## Problem

- Video world models remain actor-centric: once an object leaves the agent's view, the model loses direct visual evidence, producing frame-locked, lost, frozen, or impostor states on re-entry.
- Memory, generative-prior, and explicit-extrapolation approaches infer unseen evolution from past observations, so state drift and temporal inconsistency grow with longer unobserved intervals and more complex scene dynamics.
- Existing out-of-view benchmarks focus on single centered targets and VLM-judged plausibility, never measuring whether multiple moving objects evolve coherently in 3D while unobserved.

## Method

- ==Joint actor-observer generation==: actor and panoramic observer latents are concatenated in one fine-tuned ==Cosmos-Predict2.5 DiT== with shared ==RoPE== temporal positions, view embeddings, separate prompts, and a ==flow-matching loss==.
- ==Panoramic warping== plus **Observer Sink**: the depth-unprojected initial panorama is rendered along each stream's trajectory as a geometric condition, while four fixed high-resolution perspective crops supply fine appearance through ==shared attention==.
- Flexible multi-observer placement with world-space ==OOV metrics==: CARLA data trains decoupled or N=2 fixed observers, and OOV-F, OOV-D_gt, and OOV-D_self lift objects to 3D using ==SAM3== and metric depth.

## Results

- On Synthetic-OOV-Bench, the 2B single-observer model reaches FID **19.65** and FVD **196.2**, versus **28.75** and **333.3** for the 28B LingBot-World, with the best OOV-D_gt of **0.531**.
- On real panoramic scenes, OOV-D_gt **0.426** and OOV-D_self **0.408** beat all baselines, including FantasyWorld (**0.382**/**0.379**) and the HyDRA memory model (**0.274**/**0.292**).
- Ablations: removing the observer drops OOV-D_gt to **0.387**, an impostor tendency; removing the actor cuts image quality to **0.433**; adding a second observer raises OOV-F from **0.580** to **0.722**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Persistent world state can come from keeping regions observed rather than remembering them: generating extra synchronized viewpoints beats inferring unseen evolution from history.
> - Auxiliary state-keeping streams can run at low resolution, since coarse layout, motion, and visibility suffice for tracking, while a fixed high-resolution reference restores appearance when needed.
> - Re-entry rate alone hides failures: models can bring objects back in impostor states, so out-of-view evaluation should check against both ground truth and pre-exit motion.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position in the research landscape

Video world models generate how an environment changes in response to an agent’s actions, often producing future views conditioned on camera movement, text, or other controls. Many current systems are actor-centric: they model the world primarily through the agent’s camera. This creates a specific problem when an object leaves the field of view. The model may fail to represent how it changes while unseen, and therefore may lose the object, stop its motion, keep it unnaturally fixed in the image, or bring it back in a plausible but inconsistent state.

Prior approaches address persistent state in several ways, including retrieving past observations, maintaining spatial or geometric memory, or using learned priors and explicit state extrapolation. These methods can preserve information that was previously observed, but they do not necessarily provide direct evidence of what happened during an interval when a dynamic object was out of view. The paper’s central distinction is to generate an additional, ongoing view of the world rather than relying only on stored observations or inferred motion.

The work also relates to panoramic video generation. Some panorama-based models generate a panoramic output as the principal representation, which can make resolution and viewpoint control challenging. World Observer instead uses panoramic streams as observers, while retaining a perspective video as the actor’s primary output. The paper further contributes to evaluation research by proposing metrics intended to measure whether objects move coherently in 3D while outside the actor’s view, rather than relying only on visual plausibility judgments.

### 2. Objectives and motivation

The paper’s main objective is to improve the persistence of dynamic world state when the acting agent looks elsewhere. The authors frame this as a limitation of actor-centric world models: because the model’s representation is tied to the current observation, an object outside the camera view has no continuously generated visual record. Longer unseen intervals and more complex scene motion can therefore increase uncertainty about the object’s state when it reappears.

The authors distinguish four failure patterns. In a **frame-locked** failure, an object remains attached to the image frame instead of moving out of view as the camera turns. In a **lost** failure, it does not reappear. In a **frozen** failure, it reappears near its last observed state because its motion stopped while it was unseen. In an **impostor** failure, it returns with a plausible but incorrect trajectory or state. These cases motivate an approach that does more than remember the object’s last appearance: it should keep the relevant region observable while that region is outside the actor’s view.

The proposed solution is to separate acting from observing. A perspective actor renders what the agent sees, while one or more panoramic observers maintain selected areas of the world. Because the streams are generated together, objects that leave the actor’s view can continue to evolve in an observer stream and subsequently be reflected in the actor’s view. The authors also aim to let observers be placed independently of the actor, so that they can monitor regions the actor cannot see, and to let observer prompts influence events that occur outside the actor’s current view.

A further objective is to evaluate this capability directly. The paper argues that general video-quality scores and vision-language-model judgments do not reliably determine whether a moving object followed the correct path while it was unseen. It therefore introduces world-space metrics and benchmarks designed to test exit, continued motion, and re-entry.

### 3. Methodology and approach

#### 3.1. Joint actor-observer generation

World Observer fine-tunes a pretrained video Diffusion Transformer, Cosmos-Predict2.5, to generate an actor stream and one or more observer streams jointly in the latent space of a 3D variational autoencoder. In the basic configuration, the actor produces perspective video and the observer produces a 360-degree panoramic video. Both streams receive camera-trajectory and text conditions, as well as recent history latents during autoregressive, chunk-by-chunk generation.

The streams are concatenated and processed by a shared transformer. Learnable view embeddings distinguish actor and observer tokens. Corresponding actor and observer latents receive matching temporal positions in rotary positional encoding, enabling attention between their views at synchronized times. This design is intended to let an actor frame refer to the observer’s representation of a region that is currently out of the actor’s view.

The streams use separate prompts. The actor prompt describes events relevant to the local perspective view, while the observer prompt can describe events across the surrounding world. This division is intended to avoid forcing an actor to represent an off-screen event in its own view, and to allow the observer prompt to control unseen dynamics.

#### 3.2. Geometric grounding and appearance references

To connect the two views geometrically, the authors warp an initial panorama into the actor and observer camera trajectories. The process uses metric-scale depth estimated from the panorama, along with camera poses. The warped videos and validity masks are supplied as conditioning, and Plücker ray maps encode camera viewpoints. Since both streams are grounded in the same panoramic source, their overlapping regions have a shared geometric reference.

The observer is generated at lower resolution than the actor: 640 × 320 pixels versus 1280 × 704. This reduces the cost of representing a full surround, while the actor retains higher-resolution output. The authors note, however, that panoramic distortion can make the observer insufficient for restoring fine appearance detail. To address this, they introduce an **Observer Sink**: four fixed, high-resolution perspective crops from the initial panorama, spaced around the view. These references are appended as tokens that generated content can attend to. In the proposed design, the observer supplies evolving state, while the sink provides fine appearance information from the initial scene.

The framework also supports multiple observers. Each has its own panoramic stream and a learned identity embedding. In the multi-observer configuration, streams are generated together, and the actor’s geometric source is selected from the observer panorama closest to its current position. Multiple observers can thus cover locations that are separated or occluded from one another.

#### 3.3. Data, training, and evaluation

Training data combine real panoramic video with synthetic CARLA simulator data. The real data provide real-world appearance but use co-located actor and observer views. CARLA supplies synchronized captures with observers placed independently of the actor, including a two-observer configuration. The single-observer model is trained on 138,000 clips sampled from 23,000 real videos and 72,000 clips from 12,000 synthetic videos. The multi-observer model is fine-tuned on synthetic data. Training uses 77-frame clips and flow matching, with AdamW optimization on eight NVIDIA H100 GPUs.

The evaluation benchmarks contain 100 real and 100 synthetic sequences, each 77 frames long. Camera motion turns away from the starting direction and then returns, creating opportunities for one or more objects to leave and re-enter the actor’s view. Evaluation is conducted within a single generation chunk, so the test focuses on maintaining out-of-view dynamics rather than retrieving information from long-term history.

The paper defines **OOV-F** as the fraction of target objects that validly exit and re-enter in the generated video. For those objects, **OOV-D_gt** scores agreement between generated out-of-view displacement and ground-truth displacement, while **OOV-D_self** scores agreement with the object’s own pre-exit motion. The displacement estimates are obtained by segmenting objects, estimating depth, and lifting their positions into 3D.

### 4. Main findings and results

The paper reports that World Observer improves out-of-view motion consistency relative to the tested baselines while maintaining competitive scores on visual fidelity, camera control, and static-scene 3D adherence. For the single-observer model, OOV-D_gt is 0.426 on the real benchmark and 0.531 on the synthetic benchmark; OOV-D_self is 0.408 and 0.526, respectively. These values exceed the listed baselines for both motion-consistency measures. OOV-F for the single-observer model is 0.492 on real data and 0.580 on synthetic data. The multi-observer model, evaluated on synthetic scenes, raises OOV-F to 0.722, with OOV-D_gt of 0.528 and OOV-D_self of 0.457.

These results should be interpreted across the metrics rather than through OOV-F alone. Some baselines achieve higher OOV-F in particular settings, but their lower OOV-D values indicate that objects that do re-enter may not follow the reference motion. Conversely, OOV-D is calculated only for objects that successfully exit and return, which is why the authors report it alongside OOV-F. The measures are intended to distinguish failure to re-enter from incorrect motion during the unseen interval.

On other metrics, the single-observer model records FID values of 29.30 on real data and 19.65 on synthetic data, and FVD values of 291.7 and 196.2. It also reports strong static-region PSNR and LPIPS scores. Camera control results vary by benchmark: its translation and rotation errors are low on synthetic data, while some baselines have lower real-data rotation error. Thus, the paper’s results support a specific improvement in out-of-view dynamics rather than uniform superiority across all evaluation criteria.

The ablation study supports the proposed components. Removing observer generation weakens out-of-view consistency, with the authors identifying a tendency for objects to return in an inconsistent state. Removing actor generation leaves a panorama-only model with stronger out-of-view scores in some cases, but poorer perspective fidelity and camera control. The full joint model combines the two streams. Removing the Observer Sink reduces several appearance and geometric metrics, consistent with the authors’ account that panoramic state alone does not provide enough high-resolution appearance detail. Multi-observer generation improves synthetic OOV-F, plausibly because observers cover more relevant regions.

### 5. Significance, potential impact, and limitations

The paper’s main contribution is a generative formulation in which unseen regions can remain represented through an evolving observer stream, rather than being reconstructed solely from the actor’s last observation. This could be useful in embodied navigation, interactive simulation, and long-horizon planning, where an agent may need to reason about dynamic entities or events outside its current view. Flexible observer placement also offers a way to allocate limited coverage to regions of interest.

The work has limitations. It currently assumes panoramic observations as input, which restricts its use where panoramic capture is unavailable. The authors suggest constructing panoramas from perspective inputs as a possible extension, but do not establish that solution here. Observer coverage is also limited by the number and placement of observer streams; it does not amount to continuous observation of every part of an unrestricted world. Multi-observer training and evaluation rely on synthetic data because synchronized real-world captures are difficult to obtain.

Finally, the proposed world-space metrics depend on segmentation and monocular depth estimation. Errors in either component may affect estimated displacement, and the displacement-based measures do not fully characterize object identity, physical interaction, or long-term state. The evaluation is also limited to controlled, single-chunk sequences. The results therefore demonstrate progress on a defined out-of-view task, while leaving questions about long-horizon deployment, broader scene coverage, and robustness to perception errors for future work.
