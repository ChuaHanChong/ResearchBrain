# DySL-VLA: Efficient Vision-Language-Action Model Inference via Dynamic-Static Layer-Skipping for Robot Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> DySL-VLA presents an efficient inference framework for Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models in robotic manipulation, leveraging dynamic-static layer skipping and knowledge distillation. This method achieves up to a 3.75x speedup and a 23.2Hz control frequency on edge devices like Jetson Orin, significantly reducing inference latency and training costs compared to existing approaches while maintaining or improving task performance.

## Problem

- Large Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models exhibit high inference latency and computational costs, preventing their real-time deployment on resource-constrained robotic platforms which require high control frequencies.
- Current VLA acceleration techniques often apply uniform optimization across all actions, overlooking that different actions in a task have varying levels of importance and sensitivity to errors.
- Existing dynamic inference methods like early exit can compromise accuracy by discarding critical information too soon or incur high training costs for specialized components.

## Method

- Introduces dynamic-static layer skipping, categorizing VLA layers into consistently executed "static" layers and selectively skippable "dynamic" layers, with lightweight adapters approximating skipped layer outputs.
- Implements prior-post skipping guidance, which uses trajectory continuity to infer action importance, dynamically adjusting a "skipping-allow point" and employing post-skip verification for critical actions.
- Utilizes skip-aware two-stage knowledge distillation to efficiently train controllers and adapters, freezing the VLA model's backbone to reduce training costs and accelerate convergence.

## Results

- Demonstrated improved task performance, with a 54.5% increase in average successful length on CALVIN and a 41.3% higher average success rate on LIBERO compared to FlexiDepth.
- Achieved a substantial reduction in inference latency, enabling up to a 3.75x speedup for the LLM backbone and a control frequency of 23.2Hz on a Jetson Orin edge device.
- Showcased significantly lower training costs, utilizing 85.7x fewer trainable parameters and 13.7x fewer training steps than DeeR-VLA 3B on CALVIN.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Different layers within VLA models contribute heterogeneously to action prediction, where identifying and preserving "informative" layers is crucial for performance, while others can be selectively skipped.
> - The success of robotic manipulation tasks is highly sensitive to the accuracy of specific, critical actions, indicating that computational resources should be dynamically allocated rather than uniformly.
> - Trajectory continuity provides a low-overhead, effective signal for identifying crucial action phases, enabling adaptive layer skipping and verification mechanisms.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models represent an emerging paradigm for end-to-end robotic control, building upon the successes of Vision-Language Models (VLMs). These models integrate the reasoning capabilities of language models with the 3D understanding of vision models to enable robots to interpret natural language instructions and generate control commands based on visual observations. VLA models have demonstrated generalization capabilities in complex robotic tasks such as manipulation, leveraging knowledge acquired from large-scale internet data.

Despite their performance, VLA models present significant computational challenges. Their large size and complexity result in high inference latency and power consumption, which are substantial impediments for deployment on real-world robotic platforms characterized by limited computational resources and battery life. Existing VLA systems, such as RT-2 and OpenVLA, operate at low action generation speeds (e.g., 1-5 Hz), which contrasts with the much higher control frequencies (e.g., 20-50+ Hz) required for real-time physical interaction.

Current approaches to accelerate VLA inference include methods like quantization, pruning, and knowledge distillation. However, these techniques often apply a uniform optimization strategy across all actions, overlooking the observation that different actions within a task may have varying levels of importance. For instance, critical actions like grasping or releasing an object may require higher precision, while preparatory movements may tolerate more variance. Early exit methods attempt to dynamically adjust computational load but risk discarding crucial information by exiting too early, potentially compromising accuracy. This research aims to address these limitations by proposing a more nuanced, dynamic, and importance-aware acceleration framework for VLA models.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to reduce the computational cost and inference latency of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, thereby enabling their deployment on resource-constrained robotic platforms for real-time manipulation tasks. The motivation for this objective stems from several key observations and existing limitations:

1. **High Computational Demands of VLA Models**: VLA models, particularly their Large Language Model (LLM) backbones, possess a significant number of parameters, leading to high computational costs during inference. This results in substantial latency and power consumption, which are incompatible with the real-time control requirements and limited resources of most robotic systems. The LLM backbone alone accounts for a substantial portion of parameters and latency (e.g., 84.3% for OpenVLA, 75.4% for OpenVLA-oft).

2. **Varying Importance of Actions in Robotic Tasks**: The authors observe that not all actions within a robotic manipulation task are equally critical for task success. For example, grasping or releasing an object is often more sensitive to errors than pre-grasp movements. Applying uniform computational resources to all action predictions, as done by many existing acceleration methods, is inefficient, as it wastes computation on less important actions. Figure 1 in the paper illustrates this by showing that adding noise at important action steps causes a faster drop in task completion rate.

3. **Heterogeneous Contribution of VLA Layers**: The research identifies that different layers within VLA models contribute unequally to action prediction. Specifically, some "informative" layers significantly alter the activation distribution, while others contribute less dramatically. Directly skipping these informative layers can lead to substantial performance degradation (Figure 3). Existing layer-skipping or early-exit methods often fail to account for this heterogeneity, either by skipping layers uniformly or by exiting before critical information is processed, leading to accuracy trade-offs or high training costs to recover performance.

4. **Inefficiency and Inaccuracy of Existing Dynamic Inference Methods**: Traditional layer skipping methods often skip layers with the same interval or simply discard all final layers, which can result in sub-optimal performance due to information loss. Early-exit methods, while dynamic, may require training multiple action heads or adapters, incurring high training costs and potentially affecting the generalization ability of VLA models. Moreover, simple skipping controllers, when used alone, may lead to insufficient layer retention for important actions and can introduce non-negligible serial latency overhead (Figure 4).

To address these motivations, the DySL-VLA framework aims to:
- **Dynamically adjust computational allocation**: Prioritize high precision for critical actions while reducing computation for less important ones.
- **Intelligently select layers for skipping**: Distinguish between informative ("static") layers that must be kept and less critical ("dynamic") layers that can be skipped.
- **Ensure correctness of critical actions**: Implement mechanisms to guide skipping decisions based on action importance and verify predictions during critical phases.
- **Reduce training cost and improve convergence**: Develop an efficient training strategy that avoids extensive fine-tuning of the entire VLA model backbone.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

DySL-VLA addresses the computational efficiency of VLA models through a multi-faceted approach comprising dynamic-static layer skipping, prior-post skipping guidance, and skip-aware two-stage knowledge distillation.

#### 3.1. Observations Informing the Approach

The methodology is founded on two key observations:
- **Varying Layer Importance**: Analysis of VLA models (e.g., RoboFlamingo-3B and -9B) revealed that certain layers significantly change the activation distribution, indicating their "informative" nature. Skipping these layers directly results in notable performance drops (Figure 3).
- **High Sensitivity to Important Actions**: Robotic task success is highly sensitive to the accuracy of a few key actions. Conventional skipping controllers, often regularized to maximize skipping, tend to skip too many layers, including during critical phases, leading to task failures (Figure 4a). Furthermore, serial execution of skipping controllers adds non-negligible latency (Figure 4b, 4c).

#### 3.2. Dynamic-static Layer Skipping

To overcome the limitations of existing layer skipping methods, DySL-VLA introduces a dynamic-static layer skipping mechanism:
- **Layer Categorization**: VLA layers are categorized into two types:
 - **Static Layers**: These are the "informative" layers identified as critical for maintaining activation distribution and overall model performance. They are consistently executed during inference.
 - **Dynamic Layers**: These are the less informative layers that can be selectively skipped.
- **Skipping Mechanism**: Instead of skipping individual layers or all final layers, DySL-VLA skips a block of dynamic layers and directly proceeds to the next static layer if a skipping decision is made. This "chunked" skipping offers higher speedup compared to fine-grained skipping.
- **Adapters**: Lightweight feedforward layers, termed "adapters," are trained to summarize the information of the skipped dynamic layers. When dynamic layers are skipped, the corresponding adapter processes the input of the first skipped dynamic layer to generate an activation that approximates the output for the next static layer. This is deemed feasible because the skipped dynamic layers are observed to cause less significant changes in activation distribution. This approach avoids retraining the entire LLM backbone or multiple action heads, reducing training costs.

#### 3.3. Prior-post Skipping Guidance

This mechanism determines when to skip dynamic layers, particularly in the context of action importance. It consists of:
- **Action Importance via Trajectory Continuity**: The importance of a current action is approximated by its "continuity" within the recent trajectory. Continuity ($C_t$) is defined as the negative average squared difference between consecutive actions over a window of $k$ previous steps (Equation 1). High continuity signifies smooth movements (less important actions), while a drop in continuity indicates fine operations (e.g., grasping, releasing), which are critical and prone to disrupting the natural flow of motion (Figure 6).
- **Pre-skip Prediction**:
 - **Skipping-allow Point**: Between any two adjacent static layers, a "skipping-allow point" is defined. Layers before this point are forcibly kept, with skipping controllers disabled. Layers after this point can be subjected to skipping decisions by activated controllers.
 - **Dynamic Adjustment**: The skipping-allow point's position ($l_i$) is dynamically adjusted based on continuity changes:
 - If continuity significantly decreases ($C_t - C_{t-1} < -\eta$), the point rapidly shifts forward by an adaptive stride ($\delta l$), prioritizing action accuracy during critical phases.
 - If continuity significantly improves ($C_t - C_{t-1} > \eta$), the point gradually moves backward.
 - This hysteresis-like system aims to protect essential actions while enabling skipping when possible. The trajectory of $k$ previous actions (not just a single step) is considered to capture sustained changes in action importance. Pre-skip prediction also reduces latency by disabling controllers that are likely to decide against skipping.
- **Post-skip Verification**: To address scenarios where a critical action's correctness cannot be guaranteed before its prediction (especially with action chunking), a feedback mechanism is introduced. If a significant decrease in continuity is detected for the first time ($C_t - C_{t-1} < \eta_1$ and $C_{t-1} - C_{t-2} > \eta_1$), the current action is re-predicted without any layer skipping. This re-prediction ensures the accuracy of the initial critical action and refines continuity degradation detection, incurring minimal overhead as critical actions are infrequent.

#### 3.4. Skip-aware Two-stage Knowledge Distillation

To efficiently train the lightweight skipping controllers and adapters without retraining the LLM backbone, a two-stage knowledge distillation process is employed:
- **Stage 1: Adapter Training**: In the first stage (Figure 9a), only the adapters are trained. Each adapter learns to summarize the information of the dynamic layers it is designed to replace, by minimizing the Frobenius norm difference between its output and the output of the full sequence of skipped dynamic layers (Equation 4). This step establishes the basic capability of adapters independently, preventing initial training instability caused by mutually untrained controllers and adapters.
- **Stage 2: Joint Controller and Adapter Training**: Once adapters have acquired basic capabilities, controllers and adapters are trained together (Figure 9b). To ensure all controllers are adequately trained and avoid the model continuously skipping only the first few dynamic layers, a specific training strategy is used:
 - Between two static layers, only one dynamic layer $i$ is randomly selected for which the controller predicts the skipping probability.
 - Dynamic layers are selected with a higher probability for earlier layers (e.g., using Harmonic decay probability), as their adapters need to summarize more layers and thus require more training.
 - During forward propagation, the output to the next static layer ($x_{s_i}$) is a weighted sum of the adapter's output and the full processing path, where weights are determined by the controller's skipping probability (Equation 5). This makes the skipping decision module differentiable.
 - A normalization loss is added to encourage controllers to skip layers (Equation 6), promoting computational efficiency while maintaining task performance.

This two-stage approach minimizes training cost by freezing the LLM backbone and reduces training steps compared to methods that fine-tune the entire model.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of DySL-VLA was evaluated through extensive experiments on the CALVIN and LIBERO benchmarks, using RoboFlamingo and OpenVLA-oft models, respectively.

#### 4.1. Accuracy Comparison

- **CALVIN Dataset (RoboFlamingo 3B)**:
 - DySL-VLA achieved an average successful length of 2.89 on the D→D dataset and 2.83 on ABC→D.
 - This represents a 54.5% improvement in average successful length over FlexiDepth (which uses only skipping controllers and adapters before each layer).
 - Compared to DeeR-VLA 3B, DySL-VLA showed a 2.1% improvement in average successful length (2.89 vs. 2.83 on D→D).
 - In contrast, a "Random Skip" baseline yielded significantly lower performance (0.38 average successful length).
- **LIBERO Dataset (OpenVLA-OFT 7B)**:
 - DySL-VLA achieved an average success rate (SR) of 96.5% across various sub-datasets (Spatial, Object, Goal, Long).
 - This indicates a 41.3% average SR improvement over FlexiDepth (96.5% vs. 55.2%).
 - Compared to DeeR-VLA 7B, DySL-VLA showed a 1.2% average SR improvement (96.5% vs. 95.3%).
 - "Random Skip" on LIBERO also resulted in very low performance (3.1% average SR).

#### 4.2. Latency Comparison

- **CALVIN Dataset (RoboFlamingo 3B)**:
 - DySL-VLA achieved an LLM backbone latency of 13.6ms on an RTX 4090 GPU.
 - This represents a 3.75× latency reduction compared to the full RoboFlamingo 3B model (51.0ms).
 - It showed a 2.03× latency reduction compared to FlexiDepth (27.6ms) and a 1.42× reduction compared to DeeR-VLA (19.3ms).
- **LIBERO Dataset (OpenVLA-OFT 7B)**:
 - On an A6000 GPU, DySL-VLA achieved 27.4ms latency, representing 1.93× reduction over full OpenVLA-OFT (53.0ms). It also showed 1.54× and 1.47× reductions compared to FlexiDepth (42.2ms) and DeeR-VLA (40.2ms), respectively.
 - On a Jetson Orin platform (relevant for real-world robotics), DySL-VLA achieved 345ms latency, translating to a 1.96× reduction over full OpenVLA-OFT (676ms) and 1.46× and 1.43× reductions over FlexiDepth (502ms) and DeeR-VLA (495ms), respectively.
 - The control frequency on Jetson Orin for DySL-VLA reached 23.2Hz, leveraging action chunking (8 actions per inference).

#### 4.3. Training Efficiency

- DySL-VLA 3B on CALVIN achieved these results with a significant reduction in training resources:
 - **Trainable Parameters**: 14M, which is 85.7× fewer than DeeR-VLA 3B (1.2B) and 13.5× fewer than FlexiDepth (19M).
 - **Training Steps**: 6.7 × 10^3, which is 13.7× fewer than DeeR-VLA 3B (9.2 × 10^4).
 - **Training Cost**: 7 GPU hours, significantly lower than DeeR-VLA 3B (112 GPU hours).
- DySL-VLA 7B on LIBERO used 226M trainable parameters, 31.4× fewer than DeeR-VLA 7B (7.1B) and 1.7× fewer than FlexiDepth (390M).

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies

- **Individual Component Influence (Table 4)**:
 - Removing Post-skip Verification led to an accuracy drop (2.79 vs. 2.89 Avg Length).
 - Removing Pre-skip Prediction resulted in a notable accuracy drop (2.42 Avg Length) and increased latency (20.1ms vs. 13.6ms), indicating its importance for efficiency and accuracy.
 - Removing Skip-aware Two-stage Knowledge Distillation led to higher latency (74.0ms) due to controllers refusing to skip, confirming its role in convergence and efficiency.
 - Removing Dynamic-static Layer Skipping (effectively reverting to FlexiDepth-like single-layer skipping) caused a significant accuracy drop (1.87 Avg Length), highlighting the benefit of distinguishing static and dynamic layers.
- **Static Layer Ratio (Table 5)**: A static layer ratio of 20% was found to be appropriate, balancing accuracy and latency. Lower ratios decreased latency slightly but with a small accuracy drop, while higher ratios increased latency with minimal accuracy gain.
- **Skipping-allow Point Moving Stride ($\delta l$) (Table 6)**: The adaptive moving stride in pre-skip prediction outperformed constant strides, achieving an SR of 98.0% on LIBERO Spatial, demonstrating its effectiveness in protecting essential actions.
- **Trajectory Length (k) in Pre-skip Prediction (Table 7)**: A value of $k=5$ for trajectory length in continuity calculation showed good model performance (2.89 Avg Length). Too small or too large values of $k$ resulted in performance drops, indicating the importance of an appropriate historical window for reflecting action importance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of DySL-VLA offers several points of significance and potential impact within the fields of robotics, artificial intelligence, and efficient model deployment:

1. **Enabling Real-time Robotic Manipulation on Edge Devices**: The primary significance of DySL-VLA lies in its ability to substantially reduce the inference latency of VLA models while maintaining or improving task performance. Achieving a 3.75× speedup over RoboFlamingo and reaching a control frequency of 23.2Hz on a Jetson Orin (an edge device commonly used in robotics) brings VLA models closer to practical deployment on real-world robots. This addresses a critical bottleneck, moving VLA models from computationally intensive research tools to viable solutions for autonomous manipulation in resource-constrained environments.

2. **Bridging the Gap Between Model Capability and Practicality**: VLA models have demonstrated impressive generalization and end-to-end control capabilities. However, their high computational cost has limited their real-world applicability. DySL-VLA's efficiency gains help bridge this gap, allowing the robust capabilities of VLA models to be realized in applications requiring high-frequency control and low latency.

3. **Advancing Efficient AI Model Inference**: The proposed dynamic-static layer skipping, prior-post skipping guidance, and skip-aware two-stage knowledge distillation represent contributions to the broader field of efficient AI inference. The insight that computation can be dynamically allocated based on "action importance" and "layer informativeness" can be generalized to other sequential decision-making tasks beyond robotics, where different steps or predictions might carry varying criticality. The specific mechanisms, such as continuity-based guidance and two-stage distillation for training lightweight components, offer novel approaches to model compression and acceleration.

4. **Reduced Training Costs and Resource Requirements**: By leveraging skip-aware two-stage knowledge distillation, DySL-VLA significantly reduces the number of trainable parameters (e.g., 85.7× fewer than DeeR-VLA) and training steps (e.g., 13.7× fewer). This lowers the barriers to entry for developing and adapting VLA models, making it more feasible for researchers and practitioners with limited computational resources to train and deploy these advanced robotic control systems. It also preserves the generalization capabilities of the large, pre-trained VLA backbones by avoiding extensive fine-tuning.

5. **Robustness and Reliability in Critical Actions**: The prior-post skipping guidance mechanism, particularly the dynamic adjustment of skipping-allow points and post-skip verification, enhances the robustness of the system. By explicitly accounting for action importance and re-verifying critical predictions, DySL-VLA mitigates the risk of task failures that could arise from aggressive skipping strategies in sensitive manipulation phases. This focus on "correctness for important actions" is crucial for reliable robotic operation.

6. **Future Research Directions**: The work opens avenues for further research into dynamic computational allocation, adaptive inference, and efficient training strategies for complex multimodal models. Future work could explore more sophisticated metrics for action importance, investigate different architectures for adapters and controllers, or apply similar dynamic execution principles to other components of VLA models or different domains of embodied AI.
