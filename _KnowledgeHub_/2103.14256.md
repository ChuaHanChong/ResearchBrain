# Learning Reactive and Predictive Differentiable Controllers for Switching Linear Dynamical Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Carnegie Mellon University developed a hybrid predictive-reactive control framework for contact-rich robotic manipulation, integrating learned switching linear dynamical systems (SLDS) with differentiable LQR and model predictive control. The system demonstrated enhanced robustness to model inaccuracies and generated smoother, more cost-effective trajectories across simulation and real-world robot tasks compared to purely reactive or predictive approaches.

## Problem

- Modeling and controlling robots for contact-rich tasks are challenging due to discontinuous dynamics and inherent model inaccuracies, leading to instability or failure.
- Existing control methods struggle with the trade-off between model accuracy (model-based) and data efficiency (model-free reinforcement learning).
- Purely predictive or reactive control strategies for hybrid systems can be brittle to unpredicted events or result in abrupt, suboptimal movements.

## Method

- Models tasks as ==Switching Linear Dynamical Systems (SLDS)==, where discrete modes are identified by a neural network classifier and explicit ==impact dynamics== handle velocity discontinuities during contact.
- Learns differentiable control policies using a ==discrete-time differentiable LQR== framework, where mode-specific cost functions and goal configurations are extracted from expert demonstrations.
- Deploys a ==hybrid predictive-reactive Model Predictive Control (MPC)== scheme that combines ==iLQR== planning with posterior mode estimation to anticipate and adapt to dynamic changes.

## Results

- Achieved **100%** success rates in Box2D simulation tasks (e.g., 1-object, 2-object, sliding pickup) with lower LQR costs compared to purely predictive or reactive controllers.
- Demonstrated an **80%** success rate (8 out of 10 trials) in a real-world dynamic pickup task with a Franka-Emika Panda robot, outperforming a purely reactive eMOSAIC baseline (**60%** success rate).
- The explicit modeling of discontinuous impact dynamics (C matrix) was shown to reduce control costs and improve trajectory smoothness, particularly in tasks with complex ground interactions.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A hybrid predictive and reactive control strategy is crucial for handling inaccuracies in learned dynamics models and adapting to unanticipated contact events in complex robotic tasks.
> - Explicitly modeling impact dynamics, which capture discontinuous velocity changes at contact, improves predictive accuracy and reduces control costs, especially for tasks involving sliding or impacts.
> - Leveraging differentiable LQR for policy learning facilitates data-efficient imitation of expert behaviors and allows for generalization by adapting goal configurations while preserving learned control characteristics.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robotic manipulation tasks often involve complex interactions with the environment, leading to discontinuous changes in dynamics as contact is made or broken. Examples include grasping objects, pushing, or navigating in cluttered spaces. Accurately modeling these dynamics, particularly in regions near contact, presents a significant challenge. Traditional model-based control approaches can suffer from model inaccuracies, leading to unstable behavior or failures. Conversely, purely model-free reinforcement learning methods, while avoiding explicit dynamics modeling, typically require large amounts of data and can struggle with sample efficiency.

This work situates itself at the intersection of model-based and learning-based control for hybrid systems. It addresses the limitations of relying solely on learned dynamics models by combining them with robust control schemes. Specifically, it builds upon the concept of Switching Linear Dynamical Systems (SLDS) to represent tasks with discrete dynamic modes and discontinuous transitions. Existing SLDS approaches either model discontinuous transitions explicitly (e.g.,) or implicitly (e.g.,). Some methods learn separate controllers for each mode and switch based on predictive conditions, which can be sensitive to inaccuracies in these conditions. Reactive control schemes, such as eMOSAIC, forgo predictive models and adapt based on observed state changes.

The paper draws inspiration from recent advancements in differentiable control, which integrate optimization-based controllers into end-to-end learning frameworks, enabling data-efficient policy learning by leveraging prior structure. By using discrete-time Linear Quadratic Regulator (LQR) as a differentiable policy class, this work aims to capture expert behaviors and generalize them. The presented framework contributes to the landscape by offering a hybrid predictive-reactive control approach that accounts for contact-induced discontinuities within a learned SLDS, thereby aiming to improve robustness to model inaccuracies and enhance generalization capabilities compared to purely predictive or reactive counterparts. It offers a structured way to learn control policies for tasks that involve distinct phases of interaction, such as free motion followed by contact and manipulation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the observation that many complex human-like manipulation tasks involve intricate interactions with the environment, resulting in dynamically changing conditions. For instance, picking up an object while moving involves distinct phases: approach, contact, and manipulation, each with different underlying dynamics. Modeling these dynamics accurately, especially around contact events, is inherently difficult, and even minor model inaccuracies can lead to system instability or task failure.

To address these challenges, the authors established two key objectives for their work:

1. **Develop a method for modeling and learning switching contact-based linear dynamics and using it for efficiently learning a differentiable feedback controller.** This objective focuses on establishing a robust representation of hybrid dynamics inherent in contact-rich tasks. The authors aim to model the system as a Switching Linear Dynamical System (SLDS), where discrete modes correspond to different phases of interaction (e.g., free motion, contact). Crucially, this involves explicitly encoding contact-induced discontinuities as switching conditions between modes. By leveraging discrete-time LQR as a differentiable policy class, the goal is to enable data-efficient learning of control policies that operate across these multiple dynamic modes, account for discontinuities, and generalize to new scenarios. The motivation here is to capture the "behavior" of an expert through learned cost functions, allowing the controller to adapt to different task instances while maintaining the desired overall behavior (e.g., slowing down before grasping).

2. **Design a control scheme that is robust to inaccuracies in the learned dynamics model and can predict, as well as react to, unanticipated dynamic contact events during execution.** Recognizing that learned models, even with system identification, will likely contain inaccuracies, particularly in real-world scenarios, a core motivation is to build resilience into the control strategy. This objective specifically targets the ability of the controller to handle discrepancies between predicted and actual mode transitions. The approach combines predictive capabilities (forecasting mode switches using a learned classifier) with reactive capabilities (adapting to observed, unanticipated mode changes via posterior estimation and replanning). This hybrid approach is motivated by the limitations of purely predictive methods (which can fail if predictions are wrong) and purely reactive methods (which might lead to non-smooth, suboptimal, or unstable behavior due to lack of foresight). The ultimate goal is to enable robots to perform complex, interactive tasks reliably and stably even in the presence of imperfect models.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework integrates system identification, imitation learning, and model predictive control to learn and execute differentiable controllers for switching linear dynamical models. The methodology can be broken down into several key components:

**A. Modeling the Switching Linear Dynamical System (SLDS)**
The system state `x_t` (e.g., `[q_t, ˙q_t]`, comprising configuration and velocities of the robot and objects) and control action `u_t` define transitions. The dynamics are dependent on a hidden discrete mode `ρ_t`.

1. **Mode Prediction:** A fully-connected neural network acts as a classifier `M(x_t)`, taking the current state `x_t` as input and outputting a one-hot vector representing the predicted mode `ρ_t`. The classifier learns to identify conditions, such as relative gripper-object position, that define mode boundaries. The number of modes in the model is set to be greater than or equal to the actual number of modes required by the task.

2. **Transition and Interaction Dynamics:** The dynamics are modeled as `x_t+1 = A_ρt C_ρt,ρt-1 x_t + B_ρt u_t`.
 - `A_ρt` and `B_ρt` are mode-specific transition and control input matrices for mode `ρ_t`.
 - `C_ρt,ρt-1` represents impact or interaction dynamics, which are active only when a mode switch occurs (`ρ_t-1 ≠ ρ_t`). If `ρ_t-1 = ρ_t`, `C` is an identity matrix (`I`).
 - Impact dynamics are critical for modeling discontinuities, particularly instantaneous velocity changes (momentum transfer) upon contact while positions remain constant. The paper draws inspiration from models mapping pre-impact to post-impact velocities (e.g.,). The `C` matrix is parameterized to depend on system properties like masses and coefficient of restitution, making it learnable from demonstrations and applicable across scenarios. Only `C` matrices corresponding to observed mode switches are learned.

**B. Learning the Switching Dynamics Model**
Expert demonstrations, provided as trajectories `{(x*t, u*t, x*t+1)}`, are used for system identification.
- For each time step, the mode `ρ_t` is predicted using `M(x*t)`.
- A predicted next state `x_pred_t+1` is computed using the current state, action, and mode-dependent `A`, `B`, `C` matrices.
- A system identification loss `L_SysID = ||x*1:T - x_pred_1:T||^2` is minimized. This process jointly learns the parameters of `A_ρt`, `B_ρt`, `C_ρt,ρt-1`, and the mode classifier `M(·)`.

**C. Modeling the Differentiable Policy**
A discrete-time LQR controller is used as the differentiable policy class. The LQR problem is formulated to minimize a quadratic cost function:
`min Σ_t ((x_t - x_f_ρt)^T Q_ρt (x_t - x_f_ρt) + u_t^T R_ρt u_t)` subject to the learned dynamics.
- `x_f_ρt`, `Q_ρt`, and `R_ρt` are the goal configuration and cost matrices, respectively, for mode `ρ_t`.
- This mode-specific parameterization allows the controller to capture distinct behaviors for each mode (e.g., varying stiffness or desired velocities) and to generalize by shifting goal configurations `x_f` while maintaining the learned behavior characteristics encoded in `Q` and `R`.

**D. Learning the Control Parameters**
Using the learned dynamics (`A`, `B`, `C`, `M`), imitation learning is employed to learn the control parameters (`x_f_ρ`, `Q_ρ`, `R_ρ`).
- For each expert initial condition, the LQR problem (from Section C) is solved using discrete-time Riccati equations, yielding an LQR-generated trajectory (`x_LQR`, `u_LQR`).
- An imitation loss `L_Imitation = ||x*0:T - x_LQR_0:T||^2 + ||u*0:T-1 - u_LQR_0:T-1||^2` is minimized. The differentiability of the LQR controller allows gradients to be propagated back to learn the `x_f_ρ`, `Q_ρ`, and `R_ρ` parameters.

**E. Execution**
The learned model and controller are deployed using an iterative and robust control scheme.

1. **Iterative Linear Quadratic Regulator (iLQR):** For new task instances with different initial conditions, iLQR is used to evaluate the control sequence. At each iteration, modes are predicted along the current trajectory estimate, and the corresponding dynamics, impact, and cost parameters are used to solve the LQR problem. This iterative process refines the trajectory and control sequence. The learned `Q` and `R` matrices capture the desired behavior, and new goal configurations `x_f` can be specified to generalize to different tasks with the same mode sequence.

2. **Model Predictive Control (MPC):** To enhance robustness against inaccuracies in the learned mode predictor `M(x_t)`, MPC is implemented.
 - During execution, the controller replans over a short horizon `T_LQR` at regular intervals.
 - A posterior probability `p(ρ_t|x_t, u_t-1, x_t-1)` over the current mode `ρ_t` is estimated using observed state transitions (`x_t`, `x_t-1`, `u_t-1`) and the learned mode classifier `p(ρ_t|x_t)`. This incorporates reactive feedback into the mode estimation.
 - The updated mode estimate is then used in the iLQR replanning step. This combination of predictive (classifier-based) and reactive (observation-based posterior) control makes the system robust to unanticipated mode changes and model inaccuracies during execution.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluated the proposed framework through simulation experiments in Box2D and real-world experiments with a Franka-Emika Panda robot arm.

**A. Simulation Experiments (Box2D)**
Three tasks were performed: 1-object pickup, 2-object pickup, and a pickup task involving sliding along a table. The proposed controller (referred to as "Our controller") was compared against:
1. **eMOSAIC:** A purely reactive controller.
2. **Predictive Controller:** A purely predictive version of "Our controller" without reactive MPC.
3. **Our controller without C:** The proposed controller but without explicitly modeling discontinuous impact dynamics (the `C_ρt,ρt-1` matrix).

Performance was measured by success rate (picking up objects and moving them to a goal) and LQR cost. Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) for learned masses and goal configurations indicated the accuracy of system identification.

- **Task 1: 1-object pickup:** The classifier learned to predict contact slightly *after* actual impact.
 - **Success Rate:** "Our controller" (with and without `C` matrix) and eMOSAIC achieved 100% success, demonstrating robustness to this inaccuracy. The purely predictive controller suffered, failing to switch modes when contact occurred before predicted.
 - **Cost:** "Our controller" yielded lower costs than others. Its predictive capability allowed smoother, earlier trajectory adjustments. The cost was slightly higher when the `C` matrix was omitted, indicating the benefit of modeling discontinuous velocity jumps.
- **Task 2: 2-object pickup:** The classifier predicted contacts slightly *earlier* than actual impact.
 - **Success Rate:** "Our controller" (with and without `C`) and eMOSAIC maintained 100% success. The purely predictive controller again showed lower success rates, as the gripper would move past objects without grasping due to premature mode switching.
 - **Cost:** For this task, eMOSAIC sometimes exhibited lower costs than "Our controller." This was attributed to the inaccurate classifier causing suboptimal short-term predictions for "Our controller."
- **Task 3: Pickup task with ground interactions:** This task emphasized complex behaviors exploiting contact (sliding along a table).
 - **Success Rate:** "Our controller" (with and without `C`) and eMOSAIC achieved 100% success. The purely predictive controller's success rate improved compared to other tasks due to better alignment reducing relative position uncertainty.
 - **Cost:** The cost significantly increased for "Our controller without C," highlighting the importance of modeling ground contact dynamics for accurate prediction of velocity changes. eMOSAIC also showed high costs, as its lack of a predictive component led to overshooting and requiring larger corrective forces. "Our controller" demonstrated smooth trajectories and better cost performance.

**B. Robot Experiments (Franka-Emika Panda)**
A dynamic pickup task was conducted using a 7DOF Franka robot arm, with object location tracked via April tags and a Kinect sensor. Ten human-guided kinesthetic demonstrations were used for training.
- The classifier learned two modes. Learned goal conditions had RMSEs of 0.015m (first mode) and 0.079m (second mode).
- **Performance Comparison:**
 - **Our controller:** Achieved 8/10 successful pickups, with an average LQR cost of 1.1789 over successful trials.
 - **eMOSAIC controller:** Achieved 6/10 successful pickups, with an average LQR cost of 1.272 over successful trials.
- The paper attributes the higher failure rate and cost of eMOSAIC to its purely reactive nature, leading to sharp, abrupt movements immediately after contact. Such behaviors were observed to be less effective and could lead to failed grasps in real-world scenarios, unlike in simulation. "Our controller" exhibited smoother trajectories due to its predictive capabilities.

In summary, the results indicate that the proposed framework generally outperforms purely reactive or purely predictive controllers, particularly in terms of robustness to model inaccuracies and the ability to generate smoother, more cost-effective trajectories. The explicit modeling of impact dynamics (`C` matrix) was shown to be beneficial for capturing complex contact behaviors and reducing control costs. The hybrid predictive-reactive MPC scheme improved robustness to learned model imperfections.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework that addresses critical challenges in robotic manipulation, particularly for tasks involving contact and switching dynamics. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Robustness to Model Inaccuracies:** The integration of Model Predictive Control (MPC) with a posterior mode estimation mechanism allows the controller to be both predictive of mode switches (using a learned classifier) and reactive to unanticipated contact events (using observations). This hybrid approach directly tackles the common problem of imperfect learned dynamics models, which is crucial for deploying robots in real-world, often unpredictable, environments. This robustness can lead to more reliable robotic systems that can adapt to small variations or errors in their understanding of the environment.

- **Data-Efficient Learning:** By leveraging discrete-time LQR as a differentiable policy class, the framework benefits from the inherent structure of optimal control problems. This allows for data-efficient learning of control policies and cost functions from a limited number of expert demonstrations, a significant advantage over purely model-free reinforcement learning methods that typically require extensive data.

- **Generalization of Behaviors:** The ability to learn mode-specific cost functions (Q, R matrices) and goal conditions (x_f) from demonstrations allows the controller to capture the "intent" or "behavior" of the expert. This learned behavior can then be generalized to unseen scenarios or different initial/goal configurations by simply adjusting the target goal states while retaining the learned behavioral characteristics. This enhances the reusability of learned policies for variations of a task.

- **Explicit Modeling of Discontinuous Dynamics:** The explicit incorporation of impact/interaction dynamics through the `C` matrix is a key contribution. This directly accounts for the discontinuous changes in velocities that occur during contact events, leading to more accurate predictions and control, especially for tasks involving impacts or sliding. The experimental results clearly demonstrated the importance of this component for reducing control costs in complex contact-rich tasks.

- **Smooth and Stable Trajectories:** Compared to purely reactive controllers, which can generate abrupt and potentially unstable movements upon mode switches, the proposed framework produces smoother trajectories. This is particularly important for real-world robotic systems where jerky motions can lead to task failure, damage, or safety concerns.

The potential impact of this work extends to various domains requiring intelligent and adaptive robotic manipulation. This includes:
- **Industrial Automation:** Robots performing assembly, handling delicate objects, or interacting with unpredictable workpieces.
- **Service Robotics:** Robots assisting in households or healthcare, where interaction with humans and various objects is common.
- **Exploration and Disaster Response:** Robots navigating complex terrains or interacting with unknown objects.

Future work suggested by the authors, such as planning over modes for more complex task generalization and extending to non-linear dynamics, indicates avenues for further enhancing the capabilities and applicability of this framework.
