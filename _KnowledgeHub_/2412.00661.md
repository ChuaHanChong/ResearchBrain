# Mean-Field Sampling for Cooperative Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Georgia Institute of Technology, Stanford University, and Carnegie Mellon University developed SUBSAMPLE-MFQ, an algorithm for cooperative multi-agent reinforcement learning that overcomes the curse of dimensionality by learning from a small subsample of agents. It achieves an optimality gap of Õ(1/√k) independent of the total number of agents, demonstrating an exponential speedup in computation.

## Problem

- Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) suffers from the "curse of dimensionality," where the joint state and action spaces grow exponentially with the number of agents, *n*.
- Existing mean-field MARL methods, while polynomial in *n*, still face computational challenges as the exponent of *n* depends on the local agent's state-action space size.
- Finding an exact optimal policy in large-scale MARL systems is computationally infeasible for most real-world applications due to these scaling issues.

## Method

- The **SUBSAMPLE-MFQ** algorithm learns an approximately optimal policy by ==subsampling== a small subset (*k*) of local agents from the total *n* agents.
- An offline learning phase (Algorithm 1) computes a Q-function for this *k*-agent subsystem using adapted Bellman operators and *m* samples.
- An online execution phase (Algorithm 2) deploys the learned policy stochastically: the global agent and each local agent independently sample subsets of agents to determine their actions.

## Results

- The algorithm achieves an optimality gap of *Õ*(*1/√k*) with high probability, which is independent of the total number of agents *n*, indicating convergence to optimality as *k* increases.
- By selecting *k = O*(log *n*), SUBSAMPLE-MFQ achieves a polylogarithmic runtime in *n*, representing an exponential speedup over previous mean-field MARL methods.
- Numerical experiments on "Constrained Exploration" and "Gaussian Squeeze" show that the learned policy's cumulative rewards improve monotonically with *k* and demonstrate significant computational efficiency.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The optimal Q-function for a *k*-agent subsampled system exhibits Lipschitz continuity with respect to the total variation distance of empirical distributions.
> - The difference between the empirical distributions of the subsampled *k* agents and the full *n* agents can be rigorously bounded using a generalized Dvoretzky-Kiefer-Wolfowitz inequality.
> - An adapted performance difference lemma provides a framework to bound the optimality gap of the learned policy by accounting for approximation errors from subsampling and Bellman noise.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has demonstrated capabilities in single-agent sequential decision-making problems across various domains. However, extending RL to multi-agent settings (MARL) presents significant computational challenges. A primary difficulty in MARL is the "curse of dimensionality," where the size of the joint state and action spaces increases exponentially with the number of agents, *n*. For instance, traditional Q-learning requires storing a Q-table of size (∣S∣∣A∣)^*n*, making it intractable for even moderately large *n*.

Previous research has explored several approaches to mitigate this scalability issue:
- **Succinctly Described MDPs:** This category includes problems where the state/action space is a product of individual agent spaces, and agents interact to maximize an objective.
- **Sparse Networked Instances:** Some works exploit sparse interaction structures, where agents only interact with immediate neighbors. By leveraging properties like correlation decay, these methods simplify the problem by searching over policy spaces derived from truncated graphs. However, these algorithms become intractable when the underlying network structure is dense or agents have many local interactions.
- **Mean-Field Reinforcement Learning (MF-RL):** An emerging approach that addresses dense multi-agent interactions under assumptions of agent homogeneity. In MF-RL, individual agent interactions are approximated by a two-agent setting, where each agent interacts with a representative "mean agent" (the empirical distribution of states of all other agents). This reduces the sample complexity to O(*n*^|S||A| * |S||A|), which is polynomial in *n* but remains exponential in the size of the local state and action spaces (∣S_l∣, ∣A_l∣). While an improvement over direct MARL, this can still be prohibitive for large *n* or moderate local state/action space sizes.
- **Factored MDPs and V-learning:** These methods aim to reduce the complexity of the action space, but often do not address the joint state space complexity comprehensively.
- **Centralized Training with Decentralized Execution (CTDE):** A paradigm where policies are learned using centralized information but executed using only local information. This area focuses on designing performant decentralized policies from centralized learning.
- **Function Approximation:** Approximating the Q-table using neural networks (e.g., Deep Q-Networks) can handle large state spaces. However, providing strong theoretical guarantees on performance loss due to function approximation without restrictive assumptions remains an open challenge.

This paper fits into this landscape by directly addressing the intractability of MARL for a large number of agents, specifically focusing on the cooperative setting. It proposes a novel approach that extends mean-field MARL by incorporating *subsampling*. Rather than relying on the full empirical distribution of all *n* agents or being limited to sparse networks, the proposed method learns from a small, sampled subset of *k* agents. This differentiates it from existing mean-field methods by aiming for subpolynomial runtimes in *n* through direct sampling from the mean-field distribution and reducing the complexity of the joint state space beyond previous achievements. The work also contributes to the CTDE regime by learning a policy using centralized information that can be executed decentralizely.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this work arises from the computational challenges inherent in Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) systems, particularly when dealing with a large number of agents. The objectives are framed around overcoming the "curse of dimensionality" that leads to exponentially growing state-action spaces.

The key objectives and motivations are:

1. **Addressing the Scalability of Cooperative MARL:** The fundamental problem is that for *n* agents, each with a state space *S_l* and action space *A_l*, and a global agent with *S_g* and *A_g*, the global state-action space is of size (|S_g| |S_l|^n |A_g| |A_l|^n). This exponential growth in *n* renders exact solutions computationally infeasible for most real-world applications. The paper aims to develop a scalable algorithm for cooperative MARL.

2. **Improving Upon Existing Mean-Field MARL Approaches:** While mean-field MARL offers a significant improvement by reducing complexity from exponential to polynomial in *n* (O(*n*^|S_l||A_l|)), this polynomial still involves an exponent equal to the size of the local agent's state-action space (∣Z_l∣ = ∣S_l∣∣A_l∣). For systems with a large number of agents or even moderately sized local state/action spaces, this can still be computationally prohibitive. The motivation is to achieve further complexity reduction, specifically subpolynomial runtimes in *n*.

3. **Designing an Approximately Optimal Policy:** Given the intractability of finding an exact optimal policy in large-scale MARL, the objective is to learn a policy that is *approximately optimal*. The paper seeks to provide rigorous theoretical guarantees on the optimality gap, demonstrating that the learned policy's performance is close to the true optimum.

4. **Balancing Computational Cost and Policy Optimality:** The work is motivated by exploring a fundamental trade-off: how much computational complexity can be reduced through approximation (specifically, subsampling) while still ensuring a quantifiable and acceptable deviation from optimal performance.

5. **Modeling Cooperative Systems with Global and Local Decision-Making:** The paper frames the problem within a specific cooperative structure: a global decision-making agent and *n* homogeneous local agents. The motivation includes scenarios where a central entity guides local agents towards a shared objective, such as:
 - **Gaussian Squeeze:** Coordinating vehicle flow to avoid congestion in traffic systems, where individual controllers (local agents) manage vehicle input (actions) to optimize a global objective.
 - **Constrained Exploration:** Guiding robotic agents in hazardous environments (e.g., warehouse cleanup after collapse), where a global agent constrains local agents' movements to ensure efficient and safe exploration, even if local agents are disincentivized to explore risky regions.

6. **Capturing Agent Heterogeneity (Indirectly):** Although assuming homogeneous agents in the core model, the paper notes that heterogeneity can be captured by incorporating agent types as part of the local agent's state. This allows for more realistic modeling while still benefiting from the subsampling approach.

In essence, the paper is motivated by the need for a MARL algorithm that can practically handle systems with a very large number of agents, where both traditional and existing mean-field methods fall short due to computational complexity, while still providing strong theoretical assurances about the quality of the learned policy.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology addresses the high dimensionality of Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) by introducing a subsampling approach, SUBSAMPLE-MFQ, for cooperative systems comprising a global agent and *n* homogeneous local agents.

**Problem Formulation:**
The system consists of a global agent (*g*) and *n* local agents (indexed *i* ∈ [*n*]). At time *t*, the global state is *s_g(t)* ∈ *S_g* and each local agent *i* has state *s_i(t)* ∈ *S_l*. Actions are similarly defined (*a_g(t)* ∈ *A_g*, *a_i(t)* ∈ *A_l*). State transitions are stochastic and independent: *s_g(t+1)* depends on *s_g(t)*, *a_g(t)*; *s_i(t+1)* depends on *s_i(t)*, *s_g(t)*, *a_i(t)*. The system receives a structured global reward: *r(s, a) = r_g(s_g, a_g) + (1/n) Σ_{i∈[n]} r_l(s_i, s_g, a_i)*. The objective is to learn a policy *π* that maximizes the *γ*-discounted cumulative reward. Key assumptions include finite state/action spaces and bounded rewards.

**Foundation in Q-learning and Mean-Field Q-learning:**
The approach builds upon the Q-learning framework.
- **Standard Q-learning:** Aims to learn the optimal Q-function *Q*(s,a) for the entire system. The update complexity is O(|S_g||S_l|^n |A_g||A_l|^n), exponential in *n*.
- **Mean-Field Q-learning:** Addresses homogeneity by representing local agent states and actions via an empirical distribution function *F_z[n]*. This transforms the Q-function to *Q̂*(s_g, s_1, a_g, a_1, *F_z[n]\1*). The update complexity is O(|S_g||A_g||Z_l|^n * |Z_l|), polynomial in *n* but still exponential in |Z_l| (the size of a single local agent's state-action pair).

**Proposed Algorithm: SUBSAMPLE-MFQ**
The core idea is to learn an approximately optimal policy by subsampling a small number (*k* ≤ *n*) of local agents. This involves two main components: offline learning and online execution.

1. **Algorithm 1: SUBSAMPLE-MFQ: Learning (Offline Planning)**
 - **Subsystem Definition:** A subset ∆ of *k* local agents is selected. A surrogate reward *r_∆(s, a) = r_g(s_g, a_g) + (1/|∆|) Σ_{i∈∆} r_l(s_g, s_i, a_i)* is used.
 - **Empirical Distribution Function:** For the subsampled agents, *F_z∆(x)* denotes the proportion of agents in ∆ at each local state-action pair *x*.
 - **Learning Process:** The algorithm iteratively learns a Q-function *ˆQ_t_k,m* for this *k*-local-agent subsystem. The learning mechanism adapts based on the relative size of the state-action space:
 - **Regime 1 (Large State/Action Space, |Z_l|^k ≤ |Z_l|k^|Z_l|):** A subsampled version of *standard Q-learning* is applied. The Q-function *ˆQ_t_k,m* maps from (s_g, s_∆, a_g, a_∆) to R. An empirically adapted Bellman operator *˜T_k,m* uses *m* random samples to approximate expectations, converging to *ˆQ_est_k,m*. The policy *ˆπ_est_k,m(s_g, s_∆)* is derived greedily.
 - **Regime 2 (Large Number of Agents, |Z_l|^k > |Z_l|k^|Z_l|):** A subsampled version of *mean-field Q-learning* is applied. The Q-function *ˆQ_t_k,m* maps from (s_g, s_1, *F_z̃∆*, a_g, a_1) to R, where *F_z̃∆* is the empirical distribution of *k-1* other local agents (excluding agent 1). An empirically adapted mean-field Bellman operator *ˆT_k,m* approximates expectations using *m* random samples, converging to *ˆQ_est_k,m*. The policy *ˆπ_est_k,m(s_g, s_i, F_s̃∆)* is derived greedily.
 - **Contractivity:** Both *˜T_k,m* and *ˆT_k,m* are *γ*-contractive, ensuring convergence of the value iteration process.

2. **Algorithm 2: SUBSAMPLE-MFQ: Execution (Online Implementation)**
 - **Randomized Policy Deployment:** The learned deterministic policy *ˆπ_est_k,m* from Algorithm 1 is deployed in a stochastic manner across the full *n*-agent system.
 - **Global Agent Action:** At each time step *t*, the global agent uniformly samples a *k*-sized subset ∆(*t*) from the *n* local agents and takes action *a_g(t) = [ˆπ_est_k,m(s_g(t), s_∆(t))]_g*.
 - **Local Agent Action:** Simultaneously, each local agent *i* uniformly samples a (*k-1*)-sized subset ∆_i(*t*) from the other *n-1* local agents and takes action *a_i(t) = [ˆπ_est_k,m(s_g(t), s_i(t), s_∆_i(t))]_l*.
 - This combination forms the deployed stochastic policy *π_est_k,m*.

**Theoretical Analysis Approach:**
The analysis hinges on three main steps:
1. **Lipschitz Continuity Bound:** Establishing that the optimal Q-function for the *k*-agent subsystem (*ˆQ*^∗^*_k*) is Lipschitz continuous with respect to the total variation (TV) distance between empirical distributions of agent states/actions. This quantifies how changes in the agent population affect the Q-function.
2. **Bounding Total Variation Distance:** Quantifying the difference between the empirical distribution of a subsampled set of *k* agents (*F_z∆*) and the full set of *n* agents (*F_z[n]*). This is achieved by leveraging a generalization of the Dvoretzky-Kiefer-Wolfowitz (DKW) inequality for sampling without replacement.
3. **Performance Difference Lemma:** Adapting the classical performance difference lemma to the multi-agent context to bound the optimality gap between the learned policy *π_est_k,m* and the true optimal policy *π*. This involves a careful probabilistic argument that also accounts for the "Bellman noise" (ϵ_k,m), which is the error introduced by using a finite number of samples (*m*) in the Bellman operator approximations.

This methodical breakdown allows for the derivation of theoretical guarantees on the policy's performance relative to its computational complexity.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper presents significant findings regarding the scalability and theoretical guarantees of SUBSAMPLE-MFQ for cooperative multi-agent reinforcement learning.

#### 4.1. Optimality Gap and Convergence Rate

The primary theoretical result is a high-probability bound on the optimality gap between the value function of the optimal policy (*V_π*(*s_0*)) and the policy learned by SUBSAMPLE-MFQ (*V_π_est_k*(*s_0*)).
- **Theorem 4.4:** For any initial state *s_0*, and with high probability (at least 1 - 1/100e^k), the optimality gap is bounded by:
 *V_π*(*s_0*) - *V_π_est_k*(*s_0*) ≤ *Õ*(*1/√k*)
- This demonstrates that the learned policy *π_est_k* converges to the optimal policy *π* as the number of subsampled agents *k* increases.
- **Crucially, this bound is independent of the total number of agents *n***, which signifies that the algorithm's performance guarantee does not degrade with an increasing total number of agents, provided *k* is sufficiently large. The term *Õ* suppresses polylogarithmic factors in problem parameters except *n*.

#### 4.2. Control of Bellman Noise

The accuracy of the learned Q-function is influenced by the Bellman noise (*ϵ_k,m*), which arises from using a finite number of samples (*m*) in the empirical Bellman updates.
- **Lemma 4.3:** For a sufficiently large number of samples *m*, the Bellman error *ϵ_k,m* also decays on the order of *Õ*(*1/√k*) with high probability. The required *m* scales polynomially with *k* (specifically, *k*^(3.5 + |S_l||A_l|)) and other system parameters. This ensures that the approximation errors in the learning phase do not dominate the overall optimality gap.

#### 4.3. Computational Complexity and Speedup

The core benefit of subsampling lies in its ability to reduce computational complexity.
- The size of the Q-table *ˆQ_k* scales polynomially with *k* (as opposed to polynomially with *n* in mean-field MARL or exponentially with *n* in standard Q-learning).
- **Exponential Speedup:** If *k* is chosen as *O*(log *n*), SUBSAMPLE-MFQ achieves a polylogarithmic runtime in *n*. This represents an exponential speedup compared to previously known polynomial-time mean-field MARL methods (which were polynomial in *n* but with an exponent related to |Z_l|) and a substantial improvement over traditional value iteration (which is exponential in *n*). The optimality gap in this scenario decays as *O*(1/√log*n*).
- **Heterogeneity Capacity:** For *k = O*(log *n*), the algorithm can handle a number of agent types (*|E|*) up to *O*(log *n* / log log *n*), surpassing previous methods limited to constant *|E|*.

#### 4.4. Numerical Experiments

The theoretical findings are supported by numerical simulations on two cooperative MARL tasks:
- **Constrained Exploration:** Shows monotonic improvement in the learned policy's cumulative discounted rewards as *k* approaches *n*. The performance of SUBSAMPLE-MFQ for *k < n* is only marginally less than that of the full value iteration (*k=n*).
- **Gaussian Squeeze:** Demonstrates both an exponential improvement in computational complexity (runtime) and a monotonic improvement in cumulative rewards as *k* increases, outperforming mean-field value iteration benchmarks.

#### 4.5. Generalizations and Extensions

The paper extends its core results to more complex scenarios:
- **Stochastic Rewards (Section H):** The *Õ*(*1/√k*) decay rate for the optimality gap is maintained in expectation, even when rewards are stochastic.
- **Off-Policy Learning (Section I):** The algorithm can be adapted for off-policy learning from historical data, rather than requiring a generative oracle. Under suitable ergodicity assumptions, the *Õ*(*1/√k*) expected optimality gap is preserved.
- **Continuous State/Action Spaces (Section J):** For non-tabular settings with infinite continuous state/action spaces, under the assumption of a linear MDP model and utilizing function approximation (e.g., spectral features), the optimality gap is shown to still decay as *Õ*(*1/√k*) plus an additional error term dependent on the number of samples for function approximation (*M*).

The main findings collectively establish SUBSAMPLE-MFQ as a scalable and theoretically sound algorithm for cooperative MARL in large-scale systems, offering substantial computational advantages while guaranteeing approximate optimality.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work presented in "Mean-Field Sampling for Cooperative Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning" offers several significant contributions and has notable potential impacts:

**1. Addresses a Fundamental Scalability Challenge:** The most significant contribution is the development of an algorithm, SUBSAMPLE-MFQ, that directly tackles the curse of dimensionality in cooperative MARL for systems with a large number of agents. By introducing a principled subsampling mechanism, the paper provides a method to move beyond the limitations of both traditional Q-learning (exponential complexity in *n*) and existing mean-field MARL (polynomial complexity in *n* but with an exponent dependent on local state/action space sizes).

**2. Provides Strong Theoretical Guarantees:** The paper establishes a rigorous theoretical foundation for its approach. The proven *Õ*(*1/√k*) optimality gap, which is notably independent of the total number of agents *n*, is a key result. This guarantee provides confidence in the algorithm's performance as *n* grows, which is crucial for real-world deployment in large-scale systems. The detailed analysis of Bellman noise and the application of the performance difference lemma enhance the theoretical robustness.

**3. Achieves Significant Computational Speedup:** The ability to learn an approximately optimal policy in polylogarithmic time with respect to *n* (by setting *k = O*(log *n*)) represents an exponential speedup over previously best-known mean-field MARL methods. This breakthrough in computational efficiency could enable MARL to be applied to problems that were previously intractable.

**4. Extends Capabilities for Agent Heterogeneity:** The model's capacity to incorporate mild heterogeneity by treating agent types as part of the state, particularly under the *k = O*(log *n*) regime, expands its applicability to more realistic multi-agent scenarios than purely homogeneous models.

**5. Versatility and Adaptability:** The extensions to stochastic rewards, off-policy learning from historical data, and continuous state/action spaces (under linear MDP assumptions) demonstrate the algorithm's versatility. These adaptations make SUBSAMPLE-MFQ relevant to a wider array of real-world scenarios where generative oracles are unavailable, rewards are inherently uncertain, or state spaces are continuous.

**Potential Impact:**

- **Enabling Large-Scale MARL Applications:** This work has the potential to unlock the application of MARL in domains characterized by a massive number of interacting agents. Examples include large-scale robotic swarms, traffic management systems, smart city infrastructure, resource allocation in cloud computing, and certain aspects of federated learning, where previous algorithms faced computational barriers.
- **Inspiration for New Algorithmic Designs:** The paper's novel use of mean-field sampling as a core technique for scalability may inspire new lines of research in MARL and related fields, particularly concerning the trade-off between randomness, sampling, and computational complexity.
- **Advancement in Theoretical Understanding:** The detailed analysis, particularly the use of TV distance over KL-divergence and the adaptation of standard RL lemmas to a subsampled multi-agent context, contributes to the theoretical understanding of approximation in complex dynamic systems.

**Limitations and Future Work:**

The authors acknowledge several limitations that also point towards future research directions:
- **Assumptions on Cooperation and Dynamics:** The current work assumes full cooperation among agents and a specific dynamic model. Extending the algorithms and analysis to weaker network assumptions (e.g., using expander-graph decompositions), competitive agents, or more complex forms of agent heterogeneity would broaden applicability.
- **Online No-Regret Setting:** Generalizing the framework to the online no-regret setting, where agents learn and act simultaneously without a prior learning phase, is an open challenge.
- **Randomness-Performance Trade-off:** Exploring the minimum amount of randomness required for SUBSAMPLE-MFQ to perform well, and formally quantifying the trade-off between random bits and policy performance, is a theoretical question of interest.
- **Practical Implementations and Benchmarking:** While numerical experiments provide initial validation, extensive practical implementations and benchmarking in diverse, high-dimensional real-world or simulated environments would further solidify the impact.

In summary, this paper offers a significant theoretical and algorithmic advancement for scalable cooperative MARL, providing a framework that could fundamentally alter how complex multi-agent systems are approached in practical applications.
