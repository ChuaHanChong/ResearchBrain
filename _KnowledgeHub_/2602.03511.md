# CMR: Contractive Mapping Embeddings for Robust Humanoid Locomotion on Unstructured Terrains

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers introduce the Contractive Mapping for Robustness (CMR) framework, which maps high-dimensional, noisy observations into a contractive latent space to enable stable humanoid locomotion on unstructured terrains. This approach mitigates exponential error growth under noise, outperforming existing methods in terms of stability, energy efficiency, and generalization across diverse environments.

## Problem

- Achieving stable humanoid locomotion on unstructured terrains is difficult due to pervasive sensor noise, model mismatch, and actuation perturbations.
- Existing model-based and learning-based control methods struggle with high-dimensional, noisy observations and suffer from exponential error growth over long control horizons when system dynamics are non-contractive.
- Prior noise robustness techniques, like filters or Lipschitz-constrained policies, are often insufficient for complex tasks or require extensive tuning, limiting generalization.

## Method

- **CMR** maps high-dimensional, noisy observations into a contractive latent space, designed to attenuate local perturbations over time rather than amplify them.
- The framework uses a combined loss function comprising Lipschitz regularization to enforce dynamic contraction, contrastive learning (InfoNCE) to preserve task-relevant information, and ==Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)== for policy learning.
- Theoretical analysis demonstrates that this contractive embedding bounds the return gap under noise independently of the control horizon, overcoming exponential error growth.

## Results

- CMR achieved superior noise rejection and stability, demonstrating lower joint power consumption, smoother control actions, and longer travel distances compared to baseline methods on diverse unstructured terrains.
- The framework exhibited robust generalization capabilities, maintaining high performance under random velocity commands and out-of-distribution conditions.
- Zero-shot policy transfer from Isaac Gym to MuJoCo environments confirmed CMR's effectiveness, showing better command tracking and more energy-efficient control than previous approaches.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The paper theoretically shows that observation noise can cause exponential performance degradation over long control horizons in systems with non-contractive dynamics.
> - Creating a contractive latent space, where differences between state trajectories shrink, can effectively mitigate this exponential error growth, leading to more robust long-horizon control.
> - A balance between enforcing dynamic contraction and preserving task-relevant semantic information through contrastive learning is essential for robust and discriminative latent representations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Humanoid robots are recognized for their potential in operating within human-centric environments. Achieving stable locomotion for these robots on unstructured terrains presents a significant challenge. This difficulty stems from several factors, including terrain irregularities, actuation perturbations, and pervasive perception noise, which are particularly pronounced in extreme environments where sensor data can be unreliable and model mismatch becomes a critical issue.

Current approaches to robust humanoid locomotion generally fall into two categories: model-based control and learning-based methods. Model-based techniques, such as those relying on the Zero Moment Point (ZMP), Linear Inverted Pendulum (LIP), Spring-Loaded Inverted Pendulum (SLIP), and Simplified Rigid Body Dynamics (SRBD) controllers, offer interpretability. However, they are often constrained by simplifying assumptions (e.g., fixed Center of Mass height, simplified centroidal dynamics) that limit their effectiveness on variable or complex terrains. Furthermore, these methods typically solve deterministic optimal control problems and thus struggle with measurement uncertainty, leading to degraded performance under noisy perceptions and imprecise actuation.

Learning-based methods, particularly those leveraging deep reinforcement learning (RL) with large-scale parallel simulations, have demonstrated the ability to synthesize complex behaviors. Early work focused on proprioceptive feedback to avoid visual processing complexity, with some approaches introducing internal embeddings via contrastive learning for robustness without exteroception. While these can achieve performance on relatively flat terrains, solely relying on proprioception is often insufficient for robust sim-to-real transfer or navigating highly challenging environments like stairs, stepping stones, or balance beams. Augmenting these systems with environmental estimation or exteroceptive sensing (e.g., cameras, radar) has been explored, but these external sensors inherently introduce additional noise.

Existing methods that specifically address observation noise also have limitations. Traditional noise mitigation techniques, such as low-pass filters or smoothness rewards, often require extensive tuning to balance smooth behavior with task completion. Filters can also reduce exploration capacity, which may impede transferability to diverse robotic systems. More recent work, like Lipschitz-constrained policies (LCP), applies Lipschitz constraints through differentiable gradient penalties. However, LCP has been demonstrated primarily for basic walking behaviors on low-complexity terrains.

The current work by Zeng et al. aims to bridge a identified gap: systematically addressing the problem of noise robustness in complex humanoid locomotion, especially in scenarios involving long control horizons and high-dimensional, noisy exteroceptive observations. The paper highlights that for systems where the Lipschitz constant of the dynamics (L_f) is greater than or equal to 1, small observation noise can lead to an exponential growth of errors over time (proportional to L_f^H, where H is the horizon length), causing significant performance degradation. This study proposes a framework to counteract this exponential error growth by mapping observations into a contractive latent space.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a framework that enables robust humanoid locomotion on unstructured terrains despite the inherent challenges of unreliable sensing and model mismatch, particularly in the presence of observation noise.

The motivation for this work is rooted in several critical issues within the field of humanoid robotics:
1. **Robustness to Observation Noise**: Humanoid robots operating in real-world, unstructured environments frequently encounter noisy sensor readings from exteroceptive devices (e.g., cameras, radar) and proprioceptive sensors. This noise can destabilize learned policies and severely degrade locomotion performance.
2. **Addressing Exponential Error Growth**: A significant theoretical and practical challenge identified in this paper is that, under observation noise, the performance degradation (return gap) can scale exponentially with the control horizon (H) and the Lipschitz constant of the system dynamics (L_f^H). This implies that even minor noise can lead to severe performance loss over extended periods, making long-horizon control tasks intractable for systems with non-contractive dynamics. The authors specifically aim to overcome this exponential scaling.
3. **Limitations of Existing Robustness Methods**:
 - **Filtering and Smoothing**: While low-pass filters or smoothness rewards can mitigate noise, they often require extensive tuning, can reduce the robot's exploration capacity, and may hinder transferability across different robotic systems.
 - **Lipschitz Constraints**: Existing Lipschitz-constrained policy methods (e.g., LCP) primarily focus on controlling input-output sensitivity at the policy level and have been demonstrated on relatively simple locomotion tasks, suggesting limitations in handling complex, high-dimensional dynamics under severe noise.
4. **Preserving Task-Relevant Information**: When designing noise-robust systems, a balance must be struck between attenuating noise and retaining sufficient task-relevant information in the robot's state representation. Overly aggressive noise reduction might discard crucial environmental or proprioceptive details necessary for complex locomotion.
5. **Seamless Integration with Deep RL**: To facilitate widespread adoption and practical utility, the proposed solution needs to be compatible with modern deep reinforcement learning pipelines, allowing for straightforward integration without substantial additional engineering effort.

To address these motivations, the authors propose the Contractive Mapping for Robustness (CMR) framework. The core objectives of CMR are:
- To map high-dimensional, disturbance-prone observations into a latent space where local perturbations are systematically attenuated over time, rather than amplified.
- To couple contrastive representation learning with Lipschitz regularization to explicitly control sensitivity while preserving task-relevant geometric information within the latent space.
- To provide theoretical analysis and bounds on the return gap under observation noise when the induced latent dynamics are contractive, thereby offering a formal guarantee of robustness.
- To enable practical implementation as an auxiliary loss term within existing deep reinforcement learning frameworks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors model humanoid locomotion as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP) defined by states (S), actions (A), dynamics (f), reward (r), discount factor (γ), and observations (O). The true state `s_t` is corrupted by zero-mean uniform noise `δ_t^s` to form the observed state `˜s_t`. The objective is to find a robust policy `π*` that maximizes the expected discounted return `J(π)`, with robustness assessed by the return gap `J(π*) - J(π)`.

**Motivating Analysis (Theorem 1)**:
The paper first provides a theoretical analysis of the return gap under observation noise without contractive embeddings. It establishes that, for a policy `π` and an optimal policy `π*`, the return gap `J(π*) - J(π)` is bounded by `O(HL_r L_f^H) * M * δ_max`. Here, `H` is the horizon, `L_r` is the Lipschitz constant of the reward function, `L_f` is the Lipschitz constant of the system dynamics, `M` bounds the policy Jacobian, and `δ_max` is the maximum noise magnitude. This theorem highlights that if `L_f ≥ 1`, the `L_f^H` term leads to exponential error growth over the horizon, causing severe performance degradation from minor noise. This finding motivates the development of a mechanism to attenuate error propagation.

**Analysis of Contractive Embedding Space (Theorem 2)**:
To counter the exponential error growth, the CMR framework proposes mapping high-dimensional states into a contractive latent space via an embedding projection `ϕ`. In this space, disturbances are designed to attenuate over time. A contractive embedding `ϕ` is defined such that `0 < κ < 1`, and the latent dynamics satisfy `||ϕ(s_{t+1}) - ϕ(s'_{t+1})|| ≤ κ||ϕ(s_t) - ϕ(s'_t)|| + ϵ_t`, where `ϵ_t` accounts for action discrepancies between policies.
Theorem 2 demonstrates that in such a contractive latent space, the return gap is bounded by `O(η / (1 - κ))`, where `η` bounds the expected action discrepancy. A crucial aspect of this bound is its independence from the horizon length `H`, which makes it significantly more tractable for long-horizon control tasks compared to the bound in Theorem 1.
The implications are:
- **Global Stability**: The contraction property (`κ < 1`) ensures that differences between trajectories in the latent space shrink over time, making state transitions more regular and predictable. This regularization simplifies policy optimization.
- **Long-Horizon Robustness**: By limiting error propagation, the framework enables robustness for extended tasks, as the return gap does not grow exponentially with `H`.
- **Alignment with Policy Optimization**: The approach transforms complex, high-dimensional observations into a lower-dimensional latent space with controlled `κ < 1` dynamics, providing a geometry-aware representation for deep RL algorithms.

Furthermore, the paper derives bounds for the bias and variance of the policy-gradient feature `g(s)` under uniform observation noise in the contractive space: `E_δ[g(˜s)] - g(s) ≤ κL_π ζ sqrt(d/3)` for bias and `Var_δ[g(˜s)] ≤ (ζ^2 d / 3) κ^2 L_π^2` for variance, where `L_π` is the Lipschitz constant of the policy, `ζ` is the noise range, and `d` is the state dimension. These bounds suggest enhanced policy robustness.

**Objective Functions for Contractive Embeddings**:
The CMR framework optimizes three components:
1. **Lipschitz Regularization (`L_Lipschitz`)**: This term explicitly enforces dynamic contraction in the embedding space. It is formulated as `L_Lipschitz = E_... [ ||S_{t+1} - κ^2 S_t||_+ ]`, where `S_t = ||ϕ(s_t) - ϕ(s'_t)||^2` and `[x]_+ = max(x, 0)`. This penalty is applied only when the contraction condition is violated, encouraging the learning of a contractive mapping `ϕ(s)` with `0 < κ < 1`.
2. **Contrastive Learning (`L_InfoNCE`)**: To prevent excessive contraction from causing information loss, contrastive learning is used to ensure semantic discrimination. The InfoNCE loss (`L_InfoNCE`) encourages embeddings of semantically similar states (positive samples) to be close, while embeddings of dissimilar states (negative samples) are pushed apart.
3. **Proximal Policy Optimization (`L_PPO`)**: The standard PPO objective is used for learning the humanoid locomotion policy.

The total training objective function for CMR is a combination of these terms: `L_CMR = L_InfoNCE + λ * L_Lipschitz + L_PPO`. The hyperparameter `λ` serves as a trade-off coefficient between semantic discrimination and dynamic contraction.

**Training Settings and Sim-to-sim Transfer**:
The full observation space `˜s_t` at each time step includes:
- Command inputs (`c_t`): Desired linear and angular velocities.
- Proprioceptive observations (`o_proprio_t`): Base angular velocity, joint positions, and joint velocities.
- Perceptive observations (`o_percept_t`): An egocentric elevation map.
- Previous action (`a_{t-1}`): Provides temporal continuity.
The experiments consider two robot configurations using the Unitree G1: a 12-DOF lower-body control and a 29-DOF whole-body control.
During training, noise is selectively injected into perceptive observations with a moderate global noise level `α = 1` to mitigate the sim-to-real gap. For robustness stress-testing in experiments, noise configurations are further amplified. The policy is trained using a double critic framework with PPO on an NVIDIA A100 GPU within the Isaac Gym simulator. For generalization validation, the trained policy is transferred zero-shot to the MuJoCo simulation environment under noisy disturbances. Key hyperparameters for the framework include a temperature `τ = 0.04`, Lipschitz parameter `λ = 0.05`, and contractive factor `κ = 0.1`.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments evaluated CMR against baselines (HIM, LCP, Naive PPO) across various disturbance configurations, robot complexities, and terrain types using metrics such as Joint Power, Action Rate, Action Smoothness, DoF Velocity, DoF Acceleration, Distance Traveled, and Command Tracking Error.

**Noise Rejection and Stability (Table I)**:
- In Noise Case I (high-level perceptual noise, α=2) and Noise Case II (mild mixed proprioceptive and perceptual noise, α=0.01), CMR demonstrated superior performance. It achieved the lowest joint power consumption, smoothest control actions, most stable internal dynamics, and longest travel distances compared to all baselines.
- Naive PPO consistently exhibited the poorest performance, indicating the benefit of incorporating embedding components.
- LCP showed competitive joint power in Noise Case II but generally underperformed CMR in other metrics, such as distance traveled and action smoothness.
- A comparison between Noise Case I and II results suggested that proprioceptive noise has a greater impact on performance degradation than purely perceptual noise.

**Random Velocity and Out-of-distribution Conditions (Table II)**:
- Under random velocity commands and out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios (e.g., "Walking Random OOD"), CMR maintained the longest travel distance and lowest Action Smoothness, while operating with the lowest joint power. This indicates the framework's generalization capability and noise immunity.
- The authors hypothesize that the contractive embedding space (`ϕ(s)`) imparts a self-stability attribute to the robot.
- An observation was noted where, under amplified noise, the robot occasionally executed locomotion tasks more thoroughly, leading to unexpectedly greater walking distances.

**Discriminative Embeddings via t-SNE Visualization (Fig. 3, 4)**:
- t-SNE visualizations of latent embeddings revealed that CMR's trajectories exhibited significantly less deviation under noise (Noise Case I) compared to HIM. This supports the framework's effectiveness in maintaining robot self-stability and resilience within the contractive space.
- Across diverse terrains under amplified noise, CMR yielded more distinctly separated clusters in the latent space. This indicates that the contractive space retained sufficient expressive capacity for environmental identification, balancing dynamic contraction with semantic expressiveness.

**Adaptability Across Diverse Terrain Types (Fig. 1, 5)**:
- Experiments on six challenging terrains, including uneven terrains, stepping stones, and balance beams, under high noise levels (Noise Case IV/V) showed that the robot equipped with CMR consistently covered markedly longer distances than all baseline approaches. This demonstrates its environmental adaptation and locomotion capability in unstructured settings.

**Ablation Studies (Fig. 6)**:
- Ablation studies on key hyperparameters (temperature `τ`, Lipschitz parameter `λ`, contractive factor `κ`) revealed consistent trade-offs.
 - Increasing `τ` generally improved accuracy (Distance) but increased energy consumption (Joint Power) and reduced motion dynamics.
 - Stronger `λ` (Lipschitz regularization) enhanced efficiency and smoothness but could limit responsiveness.
 - Higher `κ` values suppressed oscillations and improved stability, albeit with increased energetic overhead.
- These interdependencies validated the default configuration (`τ = 0.04, λ = 0.05, κ = 0.1`) as a balanced compromise for robust and efficient performance.

**Sim-to-sim Transfer (Table III)**:
- Zero-shot transfer of policies trained in Isaac Gym to the MuJoCo simulation environment (under Noise Case I with random commands) was performed.
- CMR demonstrated superior performance over HIM, exhibiting better command tracking and consuming less joint power, suggesting more energy-efficient control. These results support CMR's strong zero-shot transfer ability.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The CMR framework introduces a novel approach to addressing a fundamental challenge in humanoid locomotion: robust disturbance rejection, particularly under observation noise on unstructured terrains.

The **significance** of this work is multi-faceted:
- **Theoretical Foundation**: It is the first framework to integrate the contraction mapping theorem into learning-based robust humanoid locomotion. The theoretical analysis bounding the return gap under contractive latent dynamics offers a principled way to understand and mitigate the exponential error growth seen in non-contractive systems, which is a key contribution to the theoretical understanding of robustness in reinforcement learning.
- **Enhanced Robustness and Stability**: The experimental results consistently demonstrate that CMR significantly outperforms existing locomotion algorithms in rejecting noise and maintaining stability across a range of disturbance levels and challenging terrains. This includes lower power consumption, smoother control actions, and more stable internal dynamics.
- **Improved Generalization**: CMR exhibits strong generalization capabilities, performing effectively under out-of-distribution conditions (e.g., random velocity commands) and showing robust zero-shot transferability between different simulation environments (Isaac Gym to MuJoCo).
- **Practical Integrability**: The formulation of CMR as an auxiliary loss term that integrates seamlessly into modern deep RL pipelines makes it accessible for practitioners, requiring minimal additional technical effort.
- **Balancing Contraction and Expressiveness**: The framework successfully combines Lipschitz regularization for dynamic contraction with contrastive learning for semantic discrimination, ensuring that while noise is attenuated, task-relevant information is preserved in the latent space.

The **potential impact** of this research is substantial:
- **Advancing Humanoid Deployment**: By providing a robust and generalizable control framework, CMR could enable humanoid robots to operate more reliably and effectively in real-world, unpredictable environments where sensing is inherently noisy and terrains are complex. This could expand the practical applications of humanoids in fields such as disaster response, logistics, and service robotics.
- **Influencing RL for Robotics**: The demonstration of contraction mapping for robustness could inspire new research directions in reinforcement learning, particularly in developing robust policies for high-dimensional, dynamic systems beyond humanoid locomotion.
- **Energy Efficiency**: The observed lower joint power consumption indicates the potential for more energy-efficient control, which is crucial for extended operation of battery-powered robots.

Future work outlined by the authors includes validating transfer performance through real-robot experiments to solidify its practical credibility and augmenting CMR with multi-modal perception (e.g., depth cameras) to enhance performance on even more precise and complex terrains. These steps would further advance the readiness of such systems for real-world deployment.
