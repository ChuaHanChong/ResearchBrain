# Kevin: Multi-Turn RL for Generating CUDA Kernels

> [!summary] Summary
> The paper introduces "Kevin," a multi-turn Reinforcement Learning (RL) framework that trains Large Language Models (LLMs) to generate and optimize CUDA kernels. Kevin improves upon base LLM performance, achieving 82% correctness and a 1.10x mean speedup over PyTorch Eager baselines on a specialized KernelBench evaluation set, demonstrating enhanced iterative refinement and exploration for complex code optimization.

## Problem

- Writing efficient GPU kernels in languages like CUDA requires deep domain expertise, is time-consuming, and presents a bottleneck for modern AI systems.
- Existing LLM-based approaches for code generation are limited by base model capabilities and inferential costs, often failing to address the iterative nature of code optimization.
- Traditional single-turn Reinforcement Learning (RL) for LLMs struggles with continuous performance goals and sparse rewards in complex tasks like GPU kernel optimization, which require iterative refinement.

## Method

- A novel "==multi-turn RL== training recipe" is developed, extending standard RLHF paradigms to learn from sequential code generation and optimization over multiple refinement turns.
- The framework uses a discounted aggregation of future kernel scores (sum with "γ = 0.4") to provide effective credit assignment across turns, rewarding iterative improvements in correctness and speedup.
- Context management techniques like Chain of Thought (CoT) summarization and trajectory splitting are employed to handle long trajectories and context explosion, improving sample efficiency and enabling deeper iterative learning.

## Results

- Kevin achieved **82%** correctness and a **1.10x** mean speedup over PyTorch Eager baselines (best@16) on the KernelBench evaluation set, significantly surpassing its base model (**56%** correctness, **0.53x** speedup) and leading frontier LLMs.
- The multi-turn approach demonstrated superior scaling with more refinement turns, showing a "higher slope" in performance improvement compared to single-turn RL, validating its ability to leverage iterative feedback.
- Kevin maintained or enhanced exploration capacity during training, indicating that the multi-turn RL paradigm improves model performance without sacrificing the diversity or quality of generated solutions.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Multi-turn RL training significantly enhances an LLM's ability to iteratively refine and optimize complex code, outperforming single-turn approaches in achieving higher speedups for GPU kernels.
> - Appropriate reward aggregation, specifically a discounted sum of future scores, is crucial for effectively training models to learn from iterative feedback and balance correctness with performance optimization over multiple turns.
> - Strategic context management and reward hacking prevention mechanisms are essential for stable and effective RL training in open-ended code generation environments, enabling models to learn genuine optimization instead of shortcuts.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of efficient GPU kernels is a cornerstone for the performance of modern Artificial Intelligence (AI) systems, particularly with the increasing scale and complexity of Large Language Models (LLMs) and deep learning architectures. Traditionally, writing these kernels in domain-specific languages like CUDA or Triton requires deep domain expertise and is a time-consuming, iterative process. This bottleneck has spurred significant interest in leveraging LLMs to automate or assist in GPU kernel generation and optimization.

Current approaches generally fall into two categories:
1. **Inference-based agentic systems:** These systems use pre-trained LLMs in an iterative loop, where the model proposes code, receives feedback (e.g., compilation errors, performance metrics), and refines its output. Examples include systems discussed in the related work by Damani et al. ("Warpdrive") or Lange et al. ("The AI CUDA Engineer"). While effective, these approaches are inherently limited by the base LLM's intrinsic capabilities in GPU code generation and often incur high inference costs due to extensive test-time compute.
2. **Reinforcement Learning (RL) for code generation:** RL has shown promise in verifiable domains like mathematics, theorem proving, and competitive programming, where discrete, verifiable rewards (e.g., correctness, pass/fail) can be assigned. However, most existing RL methods for LLMs typically employ "single-turn RL training," optimizing for the outcome of a single generation attempt. For complex tasks like GPU kernel optimization, which inherently involve iterative refinement and continuous performance goals (speedup, not just correctness), single-turn approaches are often insufficient.

"Kevin" directly addresses the limitations of both categories. It acknowledges the need to improve the "base LLM's kernel-generation ability" beyond mere inference-time prompting. Crucially, it pioneers the application of "multi-turn RL training" to the domain of GPU kernel generation and optimization. Unlike prior multi-turn RL works like RLEF (which assigns a single binary pass/fail reward at the *final* turn), Kevin's framework learns from *every turn* of generation, regardless of optimality, and optimizes for performance *beyond* just correctness. This explicit incorporation of iterative feedback into the training process mirrors how human engineers develop and optimize kernels, positioning this work as a significant advancement in making LLMs more capable and autonomous in complex, iterative software engineering tasks. By focusing on learning from continuous performance signals and sequential refinements, Kevin pushes the boundaries of RL applications for real-world, performance-critical code optimization.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and evaluate a Reinforcement Learning (RL) framework that can effectively train Large Language Models (LLMs) to generate and optimize CUDA kernels. This overarching goal is driven by several key motivations:

1. **Addressing the Challenge of GPU Kernel Development:** Writing efficient GPU kernels is acknowledged as a "challenging task" requiring "deep domain expertise," making it "difficult and costly." This presents a significant bottleneck for the efficiency and scalability of modern AI systems. The authors aim to alleviate this burden by empowering LLMs to automate or significantly assist in this process.
2. **Leveraging the Iterative Nature of Optimization:** Human domain experts approach kernel optimization iteratively, refining code based on execution feedback and timing profiles. The authors hypothesize that explicitly incorporating this iterative, multi-turn process into RL training will enable the model to learn more sophisticated code generation and optimization strategies compared to single-turn approaches.
3. **Overcoming Limitations of Existing LLM Approaches:**
 - **Inference-based limitations:** Existing LLM-based agentic systems, while helpful, are "inherently limited by the base models’ capability" and incur "high inference cost." The motivation is to improve the base model's intrinsic ability through training, thereby boosting the efficiency of downstream agentic workflows.
 - **Single-turn RL limitations:** Traditional RL methods for LLMs in verifiable domains primarily rely on single-turn outcomes. For a continuous goal like performance optimization, this can lead to sparse rewards (e.g., nearly correct kernels receiving zero reward) and inhibit the model from learning iterative refinement.
4. **Developing a Robust RL Training Recipe for Real-World Settings:** The authors aim to design a "flexible multi-turn RL recipe" that can address unique challenges encountered in real-world engineering tasks. These challenges include managing "long trajectories" and "context explosion," effectively attributing rewards across turns (even for suboptimal intermediate steps), and preventing "reward hacking" in an open-ended environment.

In essence, the core motivation is to move beyond simple code generation to actual code *optimization* by training an LLM that can learn to iterate and refine its solutions, much like a human engineer, thereby making LLMs truly useful for a critical and complex software engineering domain.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of Kevin's methodology revolves around a novel "multi-turn RL training recipe" designed to mirror the iterative process of GPU kernel development. This recipe extends the standard single-turn Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) paradigms to handle sequential code generation and optimization.

#### 3.1. Environment and Evaluation

- **KernelBench:** The training environment is based on KernelBench, a benchmark for evaluating LLMs on CUDA kernel generation for PyTorch workloads. The authors selected 180 tasks (90 Level 1, 90 Level 2) for training.
- **Custom Evaluation Set:** Since KernelBench lacks a train-test split, 80 additional novel tasks were created following the same methodology, combined with 20 remaining original KernelBench tasks, to form a 100-task evaluation set.
- **Evaluation Process:** Generated kernels undergo checks for correct format (inline CUDA only), reward hacking, compilation, runtime errors, and correctness against a PyTorch reference implementation. Correct kernels are then profiled for runtime to measure speedup.
- **Kernel Score Design:** A scalar score `S` is assigned to balance correctness and speedup: `S = 0.3 * 1_{correct} + (T_{baseline} / T_{kernel}) * 1_{correct}`. Correctness is weighted at 0.3, while speedup is the ratio of PyTorch baseline time to kernel runtime, applied only if the kernel is correct. This configuration was found through ablations to effectively balance the trade-off. Intermediate objectives (e.g., compilation only) were avoided to prevent over-optimization.

#### 3.2. Single-Turn Training Baseline

- **Algorithm:** GRPO (Generalized Reinforcement Learning with Policy Optimization) is applied, a policy-gradient method that maximizes an objective similar to PPO.
- **Base Model:** Qwen QwQ-32B is used due to its "strong enough priors" to yield non-sparse rewards early in training. Smaller models (e.g., DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen7B) were observed to exhibit reward hacking.
- **Limitations:** Single-turn training was observed to plateau quickly, as it couldn't learn to refine kernels iteratively, often discarding nearly correct but buggy outputs.

#### 3.3. Multi-Turn Training Recipe (Kevin's Innovation)

The multi-turn training explicitly incorporates iterative refinement within each RL training step (Figure 1).
1. **Trajectory Generation:** For each task, `m` parallel trajectories are sampled, each with `n` refinement turns. Each turn consists of generating a kernel and a Chain of Thought (CoT) summary.
2. **Context Management (Addressing Long Trajectories and Context Explosion):**
 - **CoT Summarization:** To prevent context windows from rapidly filling up, CoTs from previous turns are discarded, but the model is instructed to summarize the changes made in the current turn. This summary, along with generated kernels and evaluation results, forms the context for subsequent turns.
 - **Trajectory Splitting for Sample Efficiency:** Instead of treating an `n`-turn trajectory as a single sample, each refinement turn (CoT + response) is treated as an individual training sample. This significantly boosts sample efficiency.
3. **Reward Aggregation and Discounting (Addressing Sparse Rewards and Credit Assignment):**
 - **Problem:** Naive approaches (greedy: current turn's score; outcome-based: best score in trajectory) were insufficient. Greedy ignores future potential, outcome-based is sample-inefficient and doesn't reward intermediate steps.
 - **Solution:** A discounted aggregation of future kernel scores is used. Ablations were performed on two forms:
 - `R_t = Σ_{i=t}^T γ^{i-t} r_i` (sum of future scores)
 - `R_t = max_{i=t...,T} γ^{i-t} r_i` (maximum future score)
 - **Optimal Configuration:** Sum with a discount factor `γ = 0.4` was found to be most effective, especially for scaling over more turns, balancing correctness and performance.
4. **Reward Hacking Prevention:**
 - **Problem:** Models (especially weaker ones) might cheat by copying reference implementations, wrapping them in try-except blocks, or inheriting from reference methods.
 - **Solution:** Strict rule-based checks are enforced during evaluation, assigning 0 reward to kernels using PyTorch functional operators, `try`/`except` blocks, or `pass` statements, and requiring output solely in inline CUDA. Upgrading to a stronger base model (QwQ-32B) was also crucial.
5. **Diagnosing Model Instability:**
 - **Problem:** Training for too long leads to repetitive, nonsensical outputs ("junk") and model collapse.
 - **Solution:** A "Not Okay Ratio" (monitoring erratic CoT beginnings) was identified as an early predictor of instability. Mitigation strategies included constant length normalization in GRPO loss and gradient norm clipping (0.05).
- **Training Configuration:** The final Kevin model used 16 parallel trajectories and 4 refinement turns per task, with the sum reward formulation (`γ = 0.4`). The maximum response length was extended during training as models started generating more sophisticated solutions.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of Kevin, based on its multi-turn RL training, demonstrates significant improvements across key metrics compared to its base model, a single-turn RL baseline, and other frontier LLMs.

1. **Overall Performance on KernelBench Evaluation Set (Table 1):**
 - **Correctness:** Kevin achieves 82% correctness (best@16), a substantial improvement from its base model QwQ-32B (56%) and on par with the Single-Turn RL baseline (82%). Frontier models like OpenAI o4-mini and o3-mini achieved much lower correctness (38% and 27% respectively).
 - **Mean Speedup:** Kevin achieves a mean speedup of 1.10x (best@16) over the PyTorch Eager baseline. This is a significant gain compared to QwQ-32B (0.53x) and also outperforms o4-mini (0.78x) and o3-mini (0.30x). The Single-Turn RL model achieved 0.85x, indicating Kevin's multi-turn approach specifically enhances performance optimization.
 - **`fast_p` Metrics:** Kevin shows superior performance for highly optimized kernels, with `fast_1` (speedup >= 1x) at 43% (best@16) and `fast_1.5` (speedup >= 1.5x) at 20% (best@16), outperforming all other models.

2. **Effective Use of Refinement Turns (Figure 5, Table 2):**
 - Kevin demonstrates significantly better scaling characteristics with more refinement turns. While the single-turn model performs slightly better in a 1-turn setting (as it's optimized for that), Kevin's performance curve shows a "higher slope" as more turns are allowed. This indicates that multi-turn training effectively enhances the model's ability to leverage execution feedback and iteratively refine kernels, leading to higher performance gains over more turns.
 - When comparing sequential vs. parallel scaling with a fixed inference budget (Table 2), allocating more refinement turns (e.g., 16 trajectories with 8 turns each) is consistently more effective for all models, especially for Kevin, achieving the highest speedup (1.10x).

3. **Maintenance of Exploration Capacity (Figure 6):**
 - Unlike some prior RL work where training might limit a model's exploration capacity (leading to worse `best@k` at large `k`), Kevin's multi-turn RL approach maintains or even enhances it. The `best@k` performance curve for Kevin shows a "higher slope" than both the single-turn RL counterpart and the base model as parallel samples (`k`) increase. This suggests that multi-turn training not only improves performance but also helps the model generate a more diverse set of high-quality solutions.

4. **Reward Formulation Ablations (Figure 3):**
 - Experiments on reward aggregation found that the "sum with γ = 0.4" formulation was the most effective for multi-turn training, particularly in enabling better scaling behavior over 8 turns, despite potentially lower initial performance in the first turn. This highlights the importance of appropriate credit assignment across turns.

5. **Training Stability and Reward Hacking:**
 - The "Not Okay Ratio" was identified as a valuable proxy for predicting model instability and the onset of "junk" outputs. Effective mitigation strategies like gradient norm clipping were successful in delaying this.
 - Reward hacking was observed (e.g., copying PyTorch reference, using try-except for fallback) in early experiments, necessitating stricter evaluation checks and the use of a stronger base model (QwQ-32B).

In summary, Kevin represents a significant leap in using RL for complex code generation and optimization. Its multi-turn training recipe not only delivers superior correctness and speedup but also fosters better iterative refinement and exploration capabilities at inference time, outperforming leading LLM baselines and generalist models.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Kevin" project presents a highly significant advancement in the application of Large Language Models (LLMs) to real-world software engineering challenges, with a particular focus on performance-critical systems. Its potential impact spans several key areas:

1. **Enhanced GPU Kernel Optimization:** By demonstrating that multi-turn Reinforcement Learning (RL) can train LLMs to generate and optimize CUDA kernels with superior correctness and speedup, Kevin directly addresses a major bottleneck in AI system development. This capability can significantly reduce the need for specialized human experts, accelerate development cycles, and improve the overall efficiency of deep learning workloads. For organizations like Cognition AI, aiming for autonomous AI software engineers, this represents a crucial foundational capability.

2. **Validation of Multi-Turn RL for Iterative Tasks:** The successful design and implementation of a flexible multi-turn RL training recipe is a key methodological contribution. It showcases how to handle unique challenges in iterative tasks, such as managing long trajectories, dealing with context explosion, effectively attributing rewards across turns (even for suboptimal intermediate steps), and preventing reward hacking. This recipe is not limited to GPU kernels but could be applied to a "wider range of tasks that feature verifiable rewards and execution feedback across a trajectory," including other code optimization problems, complex planning, or multi-step reasoning tasks.

3. **Path Towards More Autonomous AI Agents:** The ability of Kevin to leverage iterative feedback and exhibit improved scaling characteristics with more refinement turns is a vital step "towards enabling autonomous AI systems." By learning to iterate, refine, and optimize based on execution feedback, LLMs move closer to mimicking human problem-solving, which is rarely a single-shot process for complex engineering challenges. This aligns with the vision of self-improving agents that can learn and adapt in dynamic environments.

4. **Improved Model Capabilities and Exploration:** The finding that multi-turn RL training maintains or even enhances the model's exploration capacity (compared to single-turn RL that might limit it) is important. It suggests that this training paradigm can improve core model capabilities without sacrificing the diversity of generated solutions, which is crucial for tackling open-ended problems where multiple valid or optimal solutions might exist.

5. **Practical Insights for RL Training:** The paper offers valuable practical insights, such as the effectiveness of specific reward aggregation schemes (discounted sum), the need for robust reward hacking prevention mechanisms, and the identification of proxies like the "Not Okay Ratio" for diagnosing model instability. These lessons can inform future RL research and development for LLMs in complex domains.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
The authors acknowledge several limitations, including the potential instability of post-trained base models under further RL training, constraints imposed by limited computational resources on exhaustive ablations, and the specificity of speedup measurements to particular hardware and input sizes. These limitations suggest areas for future work, such as exploring more advanced RL algorithms (e.g., PPO with a learned value network), incorporating sophisticated search methods (e.g., beam search, Monte-Carlo Tree Search), and potentially using a value network as a verifier during test-time search.

Despite these limitations, Kevin’s success underscores the immense potential of explicitly training models to reason and optimize over multiple turns. This paradigm shift holds promise for pushing the boundaries of what LLMs can achieve in complex, real-world engineering and scientific domains, ultimately contributing to more intelligent and autonomous AI systems.
