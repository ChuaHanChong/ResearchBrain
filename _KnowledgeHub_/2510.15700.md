# ProofOptimizer: Training Language Models to Simplify Proofs without Human Demonstrations

> [!summary] Summary
> ProofOptimizer introduces a system for training language models to simplify formal Lean proofs through self-supervision, eliminating the need for human demonstrations. It achieves substantial length reductions, such as 87% on miniF2F and 49% on complex Seed-Prover IMO proofs, which in turn improves the quality of training data for future provers and significantly reduces verification times.

## Problem

- AI-generated formal proofs are often excessively long, convoluted, and difficult for humans to comprehend or extract insights from.
- These complex proofs are suboptimal as training data for future proof generation models and lead to increased computational cost for mechanical verification.
- A major challenge for training dedicated proof simplification models is the scarcity of human-annotated "before-and-after" simplification datasets.

## Method

- A multi-component system combines a ==symbolic linter==, a fine-tuned language model (Qwen-2.5-7B-Instruct), and an iterative inference-time shortening algorithm.
- A novel four-stage pipeline generates a large dataset of 145,000 theorem-proof pairs, serving as self-supervised training data for simplification without human demonstrations.
- Two self-supervised training paradigms are used: Expert Iteration (**ProofOptimizer**-ExpIt) selects the shortest correct simplification, and Online Reinforcement Learning (ProofOptimizer-RL) directly optimizes for relative proof shortening using Lean's verifier as a reward function.

## Results

- Achieved average proof length reductions of **87%** on miniF2F, **57%** on PutnamBench, and **49%** on complex Seed-Prover IMO 2025 proofs.
- Outperformed off-the-shelf LLMs (e.g., GPT-4o, Gemini-2.5-Pro) in proof simplification, with ProofOptimizer-ExpIt achieving **74.9%** reduction (@32) on miniF2F compared to Gemini-2.5-Pro's **62.0%**.
- Resulting simplified proofs frequently demonstrated faster mechanical verification times (e.g., **28%** of proofs showed at least a **1.5x** speedup, with some up to **4.7x**) and the method successfully optimized for Lean heartbeats as an alternative complexity metric.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Leveraging Lean's mechanical verification capabilities as an automated feedback mechanism effectively overcomes the data scarcity problem for training proof simplification models.
> - Simplified proofs serve as higher-quality training data, leading to a 2% improvement in pass@32 accuracy for downstream proof generation models.
> - The iterative proof shortening workflow is highly effective, enabling progressive reductions in proof length and demonstrating steady improvements across multiple iterations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated at the intersection of **neural theorem proving (NTP)**, **large language models (LLMs)**, and **program synthesis/optimization**. In recent years, LLMs have achieved remarkable success in generating formal proofs in systems like Lean, even reaching capabilities comparable to IMO gold medalists (e.g., Seed-Prover). These advancements represent a significant leap towards automated mathematical reasoning.

However, a critical bottleneck has emerged: the proofs generated by these powerful LLMs are often **excessively long, convoluted, and difficult for humans to comprehend**. While mechanically verifiable by formal systems like Lean, their inscrutability limits their usefulness for mathematicians seeking insight, makes them suboptimal as training data for future models (as learning from overly complex examples can be inefficient), and renders them computationally expensive to compile and integrate into formal libraries such as `mathlib`.

Prior work on proof simplification, such as ImProver (Ahuja et al., 2024) and GPT-f (Polu and Sutskever, 2020), primarily relied on agentic scaffolding around off-the-shelf LLMs (e.g., GPT-4o) or focused on relatively shorter proofs. These methods typically struggle with the **extremely long and complex proofs** produced by state-of-the-art reinforcement learning (RL)-trained LLM provers, which is precisely where simplification is most needed. A significant challenge has been the **scarcity of suitable training data** for proof simplification – specifically, aligned pairs of "before" and "after" simplified proofs.

ProofOptimizer addresses this gap by introducing the **first language model specifically trained to simplify Lean proofs without requiring human demonstrations** or additional human supervision. It moves beyond agentic scaffolding of general-purpose LLMs to a dedicated, self-supervised training paradigm. This positions ProofOptimizer as a crucial component in the end-to-end pipeline of AI-assisted formal mathematics, bridging the gap between correctness and utility for both human users and other AI systems. By focusing on Lean, a powerful and expressive proof assistant, the work contributes directly to the formal verification community and the development of robust mathematical AI. The work also aligns with the broader trend of using formal systems to provide reliable feedback signals for training AI, mitigating issues like hallucination common in open-ended LLM generation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of ProofOptimizer is to develop an **LLM-based system capable of transforming long and convoluted formal proofs in Lean into simpler, more concise forms while rigorously preserving their correctness.**

This primary objective is driven by several compelling motivations:

- **Enhancing Human Comprehension and Mathematical Insight:** Excessively long proofs, even if correct, are difficult for human mathematicians to read, understand, and extract insights from. Simplifying these proofs makes them more accessible, facilitating collaboration between AI and human mathematicians, and enabling AI-generated proofs to contribute meaningfully to mathematical discovery and education.
- **Improving the Quality of Synthetic Training Data:** When LLMs are trained on existing proofs, convoluted or redundant examples can hinder learning efficiency and lead to the generation of similarly suboptimal proofs. Simplifying these proofs before they are used as training data can lead to more effective and efficient learning for future proof generation models.
- **Boosting Computational Efficiency:** Longer proofs require more computational resources and time for mechanical verification (compilation and checking) in formal systems like Lean. Reducing proof length directly translates to faster verification times, which is particularly important for integrating AI-generated proofs into large, existing formal libraries like `mathlib`, where compilation speed is a practical concern.
- **Addressing Data Scarcity through Self-Supervision:** A major hurdle for training dedicated proof simplification models is the lack of "before-and-after" human-annotated simplification datasets. ProofOptimizer's methodology explicitly aims to overcome this by using Lean's mechanical verification capabilities to provide automated feedback and generate its own training data, eliminating the need for expensive human demonstrations.
- **Generalizability to Diverse Complexity Measures:** While focusing on proof length (number of tokens) as the primary metric for simplicity, the authors explicitly state that their approach is "agnostic to the choice of simplicity measure L," provided it is deterministic and automatically computable. This motivation points towards a flexible framework for future optimization goals, such as optimizing for proof execution time.

In essence, ProofOptimizer seeks to make AI-generated formal proofs not just correct, but also **useful, efficient, and intuitive**, thereby unlocking their full potential in the evolving landscape of AI-assisted mathematics.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

ProofOptimizer integrates a multi-component system comprising a symbolic linter, a fine-tuned language model, and an iterative inference-time shortening algorithm. The approach is distinguished by its reliance on self-supervision to bypass the scarcity of human-annotated simplification data.

#### 3.1. Lean Base Model and Training Data Generation

The foundation is a general-purpose Lean model, **Qwen-2.5-7B-Instruct**, fine-tuned on approximately 1 billion Lean tokens. This base model is trained on a diverse mix of five tasks:
- Natural language problem solving
- Lean 4 code completion
- Auto-formalization (of problems and solutions)
- Formal theorem proving
- Tactic/proof state prediction

To generate proof simplification training data, the authors employ a novel **four-stage pipeline** to create a dataset of 145,000 theorems with corresponding proofs:
1. **Problem Collection:** Curating problems from various benchmarks like Goedel-Pset, filtering for proof-oriented problems.
2. **Proof Sketching:** Training a model to translate natural language solutions into high-level Lean proof sketches, where complex details are replaced by `sorry` tactics.
3. **Theorem Extraction and Filtering:** Extracting each `sorry` goal from these sketches into separate sub-theorems. A custom `AUTO` tactic then filters out trivial theorems solvable by simple automation, leaving more challenging ones.
4. **Proof Generation:** Using Goedel-Prover-V2-32B (a strong open-source prover) to generate full Lean proofs for these extracted sub-theorems, which then serve as the "original, long proofs" for simplification.

#### 3.2. Training Paradigms for ProofOptimizer

Two distinct self-supervised training recipes are explored:

- **ProofOptimizer-ExpIt (Expert Iteration):** This approach leverages a STaR-like iterative algorithm.
 1. The current model (`π_i`) samples multiple candidate simplifications for a given proof.
 2. Lean's compiler verifies these candidates. The *shortest correct* simplification (meeting a threshold, e.g., 80% reduction) is selected.
 3. A new training dataset is created from these (original, simplified) proof pairs.
 4. The model is then fine-tuned on this new dataset to produce the next iteration (`π_{i+1}`). This allows for continual improvement without human labels.

- **ProofOptimizer-RL (Online Reinforcement Learning):** This method directly optimizes for proof shortening.
 1. The task is to produce a valid, shorter proof for a given statement and initial proof.
 2. The reward function is defined as the *relative shortening* (`(|y|-|x|)/|x|`) if the generated proof `y` is valid and shorter than the original `x`, and 0 otherwise.
 3. An asynchronous variant of the GRPO algorithm is employed for training, using a baselined advantage function.

#### 3.3. Inference-Time Techniques

Given an input proof, ProofOptimizer applies a sequence of techniques:

- **Symbolic Linter:** An initial pass identifies and removes "extraneous tactics" that do not change the proof state, using Lean's `linter.unusedTactic`.
- **Iterative Proof Shortening:** This is the primary inference strategy.
 1. The model samples `k` candidate simplifications from the current shortest proof.
 2. The shortest valid candidate is chosen to replace the current proof.
 3. This process is repeated for a set number of iterations, progressively reducing proof length.
- **Test-Time RL:** A specialized RL setup where the model's weights are directly tuned on successful simplifications encountered during evaluation, fixing the input proof.
- **Repair with Execution Feedback (for comparative analysis):** This technique involves using a powerful prover (Goedel-Prover-V2-32B) to fix incorrect simplifications generated by ProofOptimizer, leveraging Lean's error messages as feedback. This approach was evaluated to understand its effectiveness but was found to be less efficient than re-sampling.

#### 3.4. Complexity Measure

The primary measure of complexity (and thus simplicity) is **proof length**, defined as the number of tokens generated by a Lean-specific tokenizer. The authors, however, emphasize the method's agnosticism to the specific metric, demonstrating flexibility by also optimizing for **Lean heartbeats** (a proxy for execution time). Evaluation metrics used are `min@k` (minimum shortened length) and `red@k` (maximum relative length reduction).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

ProofOptimizer demonstrates significant advancements in formal proof simplification, validated across standard benchmarks and real-world complex proofs.

- **Substantial Proof Length Reduction:**
 - **miniF2F:** Achieved an average proof length reduction of **87%** with iterative shortening.
 - **PutnamBench:** Achieved an average proof length reduction of **57%** with iterative shortening.
 - **Seed-Prover IMO 2025 Proofs:** Successfully reduced the length of these highly complex, multi-thousand-line proofs by an average of **49%** (ranging from 43.8% to 53.8% for individual problems). This highlights the model's capability on proofs generated by state-of-the-art RL-trained provers.

- **Superior Performance over Baselines:**
 - ProofOptimizer significantly **outperformed off-the-shelf LLMs** like Qwen-2.5-Instruct (7B/32B), GPT-4o, and Gemini-2.5-Pro, even when these models were augmented with "Chain-of-States" like prompting for context. For example, on miniF2F, ProofOptimizer-ExpIt achieved 74.9% reduction (@32) compared to Gemini-2.5-Pro's 62.0%.

- **Comparison of Training Paradigms:**
 - **ProofOptimizer-RL** (Reinforcement Learning) excelled at single-sample performance (`red@1`), achieving 63.6% on miniF2F and 14.9% on PutnamBench. However, this came at the cost of **reduced diversity**, with a smaller gap between `@1` and `@32` metrics, a common issue in RL training leading to mode collapse.
 - **ProofOptimizer-ExpIt** (Expert Iteration) provided a better balance, yielding strong multi-sample performance (`red@32`) of 72.3% on miniF2F and 26.3% on PutnamBench.
 - **Test-Time RL** further boosted `red@1` metrics (e.g., 72.5% on miniF2F), confirming the benefits of direct optimization during inference.

- **Effectiveness of Iterative Shortening:** The iterative proof shortening workflow proved highly effective, demonstrating **steady improvements across multiple iterations**. Increasing the number of samples (`k`) per iteration further enhanced reduction, particularly in later stages of simplification when proofs became harder to shorten.

- **Limited Efficacy of Repair with Execution Feedback:** While capable of correcting incorrect simplifications, the repair mechanism using Goedel-Prover-V2-32B often produced **repaired proofs that were longer than the originals**. Only a small percentage of repaired proofs were ultimately shorter than the best found by direct simplification, indicating that re-sampling for new simplifications is generally more effective than attempting to fix incorrect ones.

- **Significant Downstream Benefits:**
 - **Improved Prover Performance:** Training the base LLM on the **simplified proofs** (average length 85 tokens) led to a **2% improvement** in `miniF2F pass@32` accuracy compared to training on the original, longer proofs (average length 147 tokens). This confirms the hypothesis that simplified proofs serve as better learning examples.
 - **Reduced Proof Execution Time:** Simplified proofs frequently demonstrated **faster mechanical verification times** in Lean. 28% of proofs showed at least a 1.5x speedup, and some proofs exhibited dramatic reductions (e.g., 4.7x speedup for `imo_1983_p6`).
 - **Optimizing for Heartbeats:** When the complexity measure was switched to **Lean heartbeats** (a proxy for execution time), the optimizer successfully produced proofs with even faster execution times, with 38% of miniF2F proofs showing >1.5x speedup. This validated the method's versatility beyond just token length.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

ProofOptimizer represents a significant step forward in making AI-generated formal mathematics more practical, understandable, and efficient. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Bridging the Human-AI Gap in Formal Mathematics:** The primary impact lies in making the opaque and often inscrutable outputs of powerful AI provers accessible to human mathematicians. By simplifying proofs, ProofOptimizer enables greater human comprehension, facilitates mathematical insight, and fosters more effective collaboration between human experts and AI systems in formal verification. This is crucial for integrating AI into educational and research settings.

- **Enhancing the Formal Theorem Proving Ecosystem:**
 - **Improved Training Data:** The finding that training on simplified proofs leads to better downstream prover performance suggests a virtuous cycle. ProofOptimizer can clean up existing AI-generated proofs, which then become higher-quality training data for subsequent generations of provers, potentially leading to models that generate concise proofs from the outset.
 - **Increased Computational Efficiency:** Reducing proof length directly translates to faster compilation and verification times within formal systems like Lean. This is vital for the scalability and maintainability of large formal libraries (`mathlib`), making it more feasible to integrate complex AI-generated formal content.
 - **Scalable Self-Supervision:** By demonstrating effective training without human demonstrations, ProofOptimizer provides a scalable solution to the data scarcity problem in proof simplification. This self-improvement mechanism (expert iteration and RL with Lean verification) can drive continuous progress in the field.

- **Advancing AI for Symbolic Reasoning:** The success of ProofOptimizer in a complex symbolic domain like formal mathematics showcases the growing capabilities of LLMs in structured reasoning tasks. It highlights how formal verifiers can act as robust "reward functions" for training AI, mitigating issues like hallucination and ensuring correctness in high-stakes applications.

- **Foundation for Future Research and Applications:**
 - **Beyond Length:** The demonstrated flexibility to optimize for other metrics (like heartbeats) opens avenues for multi-objective optimization (e.g., balancing readability and execution speed).
 - **Structural Simplification:** The current work focuses on simplifying existing proof steps; future work could explore structural simplifications, such as identifying and formalizing lemmas from complex proofs.
 - **Integration into Prover Architectures:** ProofOptimizer could be integrated directly into the proof generation loop of LLM provers, potentially guiding them to produce simpler proofs in real-time, rather than only as a post-processing step.

In conclusion, ProofOptimizer addresses a critical, previously underserved area in neural theorem proving. By making AI-generated proofs concise and readable, it moves the field closer to a future where AI tools are not only correct but also genuinely useful and integrated companions for mathematical discovery and rigorous verification.
