# Unsupervised Online Learning for Robotic Interestingness with Visual Memory

> [!summary] Summary
> This work develops an unsupervised, online learning system enabling robots to identify 'interesting' scenes in unknown, dynamic environments by mimicking human attention. It introduces a translation-invariant 4-D visual memory and a three-stage learning architecture, achieving real-time processing at 69 FPS and outperforming prior unsupervised and some weakly supervised methods in robotic tasks.

## Problem

- Existing interestingness recognition methods for robots rely on extensive human annotations (supervised learning) and struggle to adapt in unknown or previously unseen environments.
- Current systems often treat 'interestingness' as a static property, failing to dynamically lose interest in repetitive scenes or adapt to context-dependent novelty.
- Memory-augmented neural networks typically use flattened vector representations, which leads to a loss of spatial structural information and hinders robust scene recognition in the presence of large translational movements in robot video streams.

## Method

- A three-stage learning architecture combines an unsupervised autoencoder with a novel 4-D visual memory module that retains spatial information (channel, height, width).
- The 4-D visual memory incorporates translation-invariant reading via Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) for efficient cross-correlation, making it robust to large positional shifts.
- An online learning mechanism enables real-time adaptation: novel scene features are written into memory, and subsequent similar observations yield higher reading confidence, causing the system to dynamically 'lose interest' in repetitive stimuli.

## Results

- The system achieved real-time online processing at **69** FPS on a single GPU, with short-term adaptation completing in approximately 3 minutes for 912 uninteresting images.
- It demonstrated higher performance on the SubT dataset than state-of-the-art unsupervised methods (e.g., FFP by 2.3-**6.9%** in AUC-OP) and surpassed a weakly supervised method (NetVLAD by 6.4-**8.0%** in precision for strict cases).
- Ablation studies confirmed that online learning (+18.9-**31.9%** accuracy), writing sparsity (+11.1-**24.0%** accuracy), and translational invariance (+20.3-**38.4%** accuracy) were critical contributors to performance gains across categories.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Dynamically attenuating interest in repetitive scenes is crucial for effective online interestingness recognition in autonomous robots, allowing focus on genuinely novel information.
> - Designing a visual memory with spatial awareness and translation invariance significantly improves robustness for robots operating in dynamic environments with continuous movement.
> - Unsupervised, multi-stage learning, including fast short-term adaptation from negative samples, allows for rapid deployment and performance comparable to or exceeding some weakly supervised methods without extensive human labeling.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Autonomous exploration is a fundamental capability for mobile robots. A crucial aspect of this capability is "interesting scene recognition," which impacts decision-making and robotic cooperation. Prior research in this domain has primarily focused on tasks such as saliency detection, anomaly detection, novelty detection, and general interestingness prediction, often tailored for human perception or social media content.

Many existing approaches to interestingness recognition rely on supervised learning, requiring extensive human annotations for training. These methods often struggle when deployed in unknown or previously unseen environments because they cannot adapt to novel situations or differentiate between truly interesting unique scenes and merely repetitive, uninteresting ones that might initially appear novel. For instance, while a tunnel wall might be interesting upon first observation, it quickly becomes uninteresting if encountered repeatedly.

Furthermore, current memory-aided neural networks, such as Neural Turing Machines (NTM) or Differentiable Neural Computers (DNC), typically define memory as flattened vectors, which leads to a loss of spatial structural information. This limitation hinders their application in robotic vision, where continuous video streams often involve significant translational movements that require robust translation invariance for effective scene recognition. Existing anomaly detection methods, like MemAE and Future Frame Prediction (FFP), also face challenges in dynamic robotic environments due to their design for primarily static surveillance scenarios and their inability to perform online adaptation to changing distributions of "interestingness."

This work positions itself within this landscape by addressing the need for an unsupervised, online learning system that can adapt to unknown environments, learn from negative (uninteresting) samples, and lose interest in repetitive scenes in real-time. It aims to bridge the gap between human-like visual attention and the practical requirements of autonomous robotic exploration by introducing a novel visual memory structure and a multi-stage learning architecture designed to overcome the limitations of prior methods, particularly regarding online adaptation, translation invariance, and computational efficiency for embedded systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust and efficient method for autonomous robots to detect "interesting" scenes in real-time, especially in unknown and unstructured environments where prior training data and human supervision are scarce. This capability is critical for robots to make informed decisions during exploration, identify significant locations, and efficiently share relevant data in cooperative tasks.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges observed in current robotic interestingness recognition systems:

- **Adaptation to Unknown Environments:** Robots frequently operate in environments where pre-collected training data or predefined labels for "interesting" scenes are unavailable. Traditional supervised learning methods, dependent on such labels, perform poorly in these novel contexts. The system needs to discern what is interesting *without* explicit prior knowledge.
- **Dynamic Nature of "Interestingness":** What constitutes an "interesting" scene is dynamic and context-dependent. A scene initially deemed interesting might become uninteresting after repeated observation. For example, a robot entering a new tunnel may find unique geological features interesting, but should quickly lose interest in repeatedly observed tunnel walls or tracks. Existing methods, including anomaly or saliency detection, typically treat interestingness as a static property, failing to adapt to this temporal aspect.
- **Computational Efficiency for Real-time Operation:** Autonomous robots, particularly mobile platforms with limited computational resources, require low-latency responses. Current deep learning approaches often rely on computationally intensive back-propagation algorithms, making them unsuitable for real-time online adaptation.
- **Translational Invariance:** Robots move continuously, leading to video streams rich in translational movements. The visual memory system needs to be robust to these large translations to accurately recall previously seen scenes, a capability often not explicitly addressed by prior memory-augmented neural networks.
- **Incremental Learning from Negative Samples:** For practical deployment, it is advantageous if a robot can quickly learn what is *uninteresting* before a mission, without requiring extensive positive examples. This enables rapid adaptation to specific task environments and efficient resource allocation.

To address these motivations, the authors propose a three-stage learning architecture with specific goals for each stage:

- **Long-term learning:** To acquire general, "human-like" experiential knowledge from large, unlabeled datasets offline. This stage establishes a foundational understanding of common visual patterns.
- **Short-term learning:** To enable rapid adaptation to task-specific knowledge by learning from a small set of negative (uninteresting) samples within minutes before a mission. This helps the robot quickly identify what is *not* relevant in its immediate operating environment.
- **Online learning:** To facilitate real-time adaptation during mission execution, allowing the system to detect novel scenes and objects, and critically, to lose interest in repetitive observations, mimicking human cognitive processes.

A core technical motivation is the development of a novel translation-invariant 4-D visual memory module that can achieve fast learning without back-propagation, efficiently utilize memory space, and handle large translational movements in video streams. The paper also aims to introduce a new evaluation metric, Area Under the Curve of Online Precision (AUC-OP), to better assess the real-time, online performance of such systems, accounting for data leakage issues prevalent in prior evaluation methodologies.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology involves a three-stage learning architecture built upon a reconstruction model and a novel 4-D visual memory module.

**A. Overall Architecture:**
The system comprises an autoencoder for the reconstruction task and a visual memory module. The autoencoder consists of an encoder, which extracts visual features, and a decoder, which reconstructs images from these features. The visual memory module interacts with the features generated by the encoder.

**B. Visual Memory:**
The visual memory (M) is defined as a 4-D tensor (R^(n x c x h x w)), where `n` is the number of memory cubes (capacity), and `c`, `h`, `w` are the channel, height, and width of each cube, respectively. This structure allows the memory to retain spatial information of visual inputs. It supports two main operations: writing and reading.

1. **Memory Writing:**
 - The writing protocol (Eq. 1) updates each memory cube `M_i` as a moving average of its previous state and the current visual input `x(t)`. The learning speed is controlled by a writing rate `γw`.
 - The writing weight vector `w` (Eq. 2) determines which memory cubes are updated. It uses cosine similarity `D` between the current input `x(t)` and the memory cubes `M(t-1)`, passed through a `tan` operator and a `softmax` function. The `tan` operator maps the cosine similarity range [-1, 1] to [-inf, inf], promoting sparsity in writing by focusing updates on fewer, more relevant cubes. This facilitates incremental learning by minimizing interference with other memory elements.
 - To enhance memory space utilization, a `memory usage vector u(t)` (Eq. 4) is introduced. This vector tracks how much each memory cube has been used. The writing weight `w_i` (Eq. 5) is then modified to favor less-used memory cubes, ensuring that novel inputs preferentially occupy underutilized memory slots.

2. **Memory Reading:**
 - Memory reading `f(t)` (Eq. 6) is a weighted sum of the memory cubes, where each cube `M_i` can be circularly translated (`M^(x,y)_i`). This translation aims to achieve robustness to large positional shifts in the input.
 - The reading weight vector `r` (Eq. 7) is determined by the maximum cosine similarity (`S`) between the current visual input `x(t)` and all possible translated versions of each memory cube `M^(a,b)_i`.
 - To efficiently compute translation invariance, the maximum cosine similarity `S_i` is calculated using the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) for cross-correlation (Eq. 8). This reduces the computational complexity from O(ch²w²) to O(chw log hw) for each memory cube, enabling faster processing. The specific translation `(x, y)` (Eq. 9) for each cube corresponds to the location of the maximum cross-correlation response.

**C. Three-Stage Learning Architecture:**

1. **Long-term Learning:**
 - **Purpose:** Acquire general, human-like knowledge and a good generalization ability.
 - **Mechanism:** An autoencoder model is trained offline in an unsupervised manner using a large dataset (e.g., COCO dataset). The encoder architecture is based on VGG-16, and the decoder uses 5 deconvolutional blocks. The training objective is reconstruction loss (Mean Squared Error, MSE).
 - **Outcome:** The learned weights of the encoder and decoder are frozen after this stage, representing "unforgettable" knowledge. The encoder provides detailed and semantic feature maps.

2. **Short-term Learning:**
 - **Purpose:** Acquire task-dependent environmental knowledge and enable quick robot deployment by learning from uninteresting scenes.
 - **Mechanism:** The frozen autoencoder's encoder feeds features into the visual memory module. For each negative (uninteresting) training sample, the encoder's output is *first written* into the memory, and then the memory's reading output is fed to the decoder. The reconstruction error guides the memory's learning, but without back-propagation to the autoencoder weights.
 - **Outcome:** The memory rapidly learns to encode these "uninteresting" features. Incremental learning is achieved without catastrophic forgetting due to the sparse writing mechanism and fixed autoencoder parameters. This stage is designed to be fast, typically completing in minutes using a limited number of negative samples. Training can be early-stopped based on visual reconstruction quality or loss convergence.

3. **Online Learning:**
 - **Purpose:** Real-time environmental adaptation, identification of novel interests, and attenuation of interest in repetitive scenes.
 - **Mechanism:** During mission execution, only the frozen encoder and the visual memory are active. For each incoming video frame, the encoder extracts features. The memory *first performs a read operation* based on these features. A low reading confidence (e.g., averaged cosine similarity between input features and memory output) indicates a novel, potentially interesting scene. After reading, the current scene's features are *written* into the memory.
 - **Outcome:** Novel scenes result in low reading confidence, signaling high interestingness. Once a scene is written to memory, subsequent similar observations will yield higher reading confidence, leading to a decrease in perceived interestingness. This mechanism enables online adaptation and the "loss of interest" in repetitive stimuli. The reading rate `γr` and writing rate `γw` are hyper-parameters that control this behavior.

**D. Evaluation Metric:**
To address limitations of prior evaluation methods regarding online performance and data leakage, the authors introduce the **Area Under the Curve of Online Precision (AUC-OP)**. This metric measures precision for subsequences of frames processed sequentially, without using information from future frames. It jointly considers online response, precision, and recall. A parameter `δ` allows flexibility, where true positives are accepted if they rank within the top `δ * K_t-n` (where `K_t-n` is the number of interesting frames in the ground truth subsequence).

**E. Datasets:**
- **Long-term:** COCO dataset for unsupervised training of the autoencoder.
- **Short-term & Online:**
 - SubT dataset (UGV, subterranean environments) for quantitative evaluation, featuring varied challenges and human-annotated interestingness levels.
 - Drone Filming dataset (UAV, aerial filming) for qualitative evaluation, characterized by dynamic backgrounds and object tracking.
 - Scott Reef 25 dataset (AUV, underwater exploration) for testing generalization ability.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper presents several findings regarding the efficiency, performance, and module effects of the proposed unsupervised online learning algorithm for robotic interestingness.

**A. Efficiency:**
The system demonstrated efficient operation across its stages. Long-term learning on the COCO dataset required approximately 3 days of unsupervised training. For short-term learning, the model processed 912 uninteresting images from the SubT dataset in about 3 minutes, indicating rapid adaptation suitable for mission deployment. Crucially, online learning achieved a processing speed of approximately 14.58 ms per frame (equivalent to 69 FPS) on a single Nvidia GeForce GTX 1080Ti GPU. This represents a significant improvement in speed compared to the previous version of this work (72.01 ms/frame or 14 FPS), primarily attributed to the introduction of the memory usage vector, which allowed for a reduction in memory capacity from 1000 to 100 memory cubes without sacrificing accuracy. This enhanced efficiency suggests applicability to low-power embedded systems common in mobile robots.

**B. Performance Evaluation (AUC-OP on SubT Dataset):**

1. **Comparison with Unsupervised Methods:**
 The proposed method demonstrated higher performance than state-of-the-art unsupervised anomaly detection algorithms, Future Frame Prediction (FFP) and MemAE, on the challenging SubT dataset.
 - Compared to FFP, the method achieved an average of 2.3% to 3.9% higher accuracy in the "normal" interestingness category and 3.4% to 6.9% higher accuracy in the "difficult" category (across `δ=1, 2, 3` values of AUC-OP). FFP was noted to be fine-tuned on mixed sequences, whereas the proposed method operated unsupervised and online.
 - MemAE showed comparatively poor performance, only slightly outperforming the proposed model without translational invariance. This was attributed to MemAE's design for static surveillance scenarios and its lack of online adaptation to dynamic, repetitive robotic environments.
 - Using traditional metrics like Precision and AUC-ROC, the method maintained its superiority, achieving 1.5% to 5.3% higher precision and 1.5% to 3.7% higher AUC-ROC than FFP in normal and difficult categories, respectively.

2. **Comparison with Weakly Supervised Methods:**
 The unsupervised online learning algorithm exhibited performance comparable to, and in some cases surpassing, a weakly supervised method for place recognition, NetVLAD. When compared against NetVLAD (trained on a portion of the SubT dataset), the proposed unsupervised method achieved an average of 6.4% and 8.0% higher precision in the most strict `δ=1` case for normal and difficult categories, respectively (averaged over test sequences II-VII). This outcome is notable given that NetVLAD requires pre-training, significant human annotation efforts, and a longer training time (approximately 2 days), while the proposed method operates unsupervised and online.

**C. Effect of Different Modules (Ablation Studies on SubT Dataset - AUC-OP):**
Ablation studies quantified the contribution of different components to the overall performance:
- **Online Learning:** The presence of online learning significantly improved performance, yielding 18.9% to 23.8% higher accuracy in the normal category and 17.5% to 31.9% higher accuracy in the difficult category. This indicates its effectiveness in losing interest in repetitive scenes.
- **Writing Sparsity (Tangent Operator):** The tangent operator, which promotes sparse memory writing, contributed to a 11.1% to 21.7% increase in performance for the normal category and 15.5% to 24.0% for the difficult category. This suggests that focused memory updates lead to safer and faster incremental learning.
- **Translational Invariance:** The translation-invariant reading mechanism, leveraging cross-correlation via FFT, provided the largest performance gains. It resulted in 20.5% to 33.0% higher accuracy in the normal category and 20.3% to 38.4% higher accuracy in the difficult category. This highlights the critical role of handling large translations in dynamic robotic environments.
- **Short-term Learning:** Short-term learning improved performance by 4.4% to 14.6% in the normal category and 8.6% to 14.2% in the difficult category. The performance was observed to be stable with respect to the number of short-term learning epochs (Fig. 7), suggesting efficiency and flexibility in deployment, as good performance can be achieved even with minimal training epochs.

**D. Density Map Generation:**
The system's ability to identify novel interests was further demonstrated through the generation of density maps. These maps visually highlight interesting objects (e.g., surveillance cameras, survivors, ambulances) at their first appearance, effectively localizing them within the scene.

**E. Generalization Ability (Underwater Exploration):**
The visual memory module demonstrated generalization capabilities in an underwater robot exploration task (coral reef detection). When trained on sand images, the method successfully distinguished coral reefs from sand, outperforming MemAE by 14.75% in AUC-PR and 13.72% in precision. This indicates its adaptability to new and varied domains beyond subterranean and aerial environments.

**F. Loss of Interest:**
Qualitative assessments on the Drone Filming dataset confirmed that the writing rate (`γw`) hyper-parameter effectively controls the speed at which the system loses interest in repetitive scenes. A higher writing rate led to a faster decline in interestingness for similar observations, verifying the desired online adaptation behavior.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents an unsupervised online learning framework for robotic interestingness recognition that addresses several critical limitations of existing approaches. Its significance and potential impact span multiple dimensions:

**A. Enhanced Autonomous Robot Capabilities:**
By enabling robots to autonomously identify "interesting" scenes in real-time and without prior labeling, this work significantly advances the capabilities of robots in exploration, search and rescue, environmental monitoring, and other unsupervised tasks. The ability to adapt to unknown, unstructured, and dynamic environments is crucial for practical robotic deployment in scenarios like subterranean tunnels or disaster zones, where human supervision and pre-existing maps are often unavailable.

**B. Operational Efficiency and Resource Optimization:**
The proposed three-stage learning architecture (long-term, short-term, online) allows for flexible deployment. Long-term learning leverages large datasets offline, while short-term learning enables rapid adaptation (minutes) to specific mission contexts using minimal negative samples. Crucially, the online learning component operates in real-time (69 FPS), making it suitable for low-power embedded systems common in mobile robots (e.g., UAVs, UGVs). This efficiency translates to reduced computational costs and more agile robotic operations.

**C. Robustness to Dynamic Environments:**
The introduction of a novel translation-invariant 4-D visual memory, coupled with cross-correlation using FFT, provides significant robustness against large translational movements inherent in continuous video streams from moving robots. This addresses a key challenge where traditional methods or memory networks often struggle, leading to more reliable interestingness detection. The sparse writing mechanism and the usage vector also enhance memory efficiency and mitigate "catastrophic forgetting," a common issue in incremental learning, ensuring that learned knowledge is retained while new information is integrated.

**D. Superior Performance in Challenging Scenarios:**
The reported performance, exceeding state-of-the-art unsupervised methods (FFP, MemAE) and demonstrating comparability or superiority to some weakly supervised methods (NetVLAD) on complex datasets like SubT, underscores the efficacy of this approach. Its generalization ability, as demonstrated in underwater exploration for coral reef detection, suggests broad applicability across diverse robotic domains and visual modalities.

**E. Improved Decision-Making and Cooperation:**
Identifying interesting scenes can directly inform higher-level robotic tasks. For instance, in exploration, it can guide path planning towards novel areas, prioritize data collection at significant locations, or indicate which data is most valuable to share with human operators or other robots in a cooperative mission. The dynamic "loss of interest" mechanism ensures that robot attention is continually re-focused on genuinely novel or critical events, rather than wasting resources on repetitive observations.

**F. Advancement in Evaluation Methodologies:**
The introduction of the AUC-OP metric is significant for the field, as it provides a more rigorous and appropriate way to evaluate online learning systems. By explicitly disallowing data leakage and focusing on real-time sequential processing, AUC-OP encourages the development of algorithms that are truly responsive and adaptable, aligning better with the demands of autonomous robotic systems.

In conclusion, this work offers a practical, efficient, and robust solution for robotic interestingness recognition. It has the potential to significantly impact the autonomy, adaptability, and decision-making capabilities of robots operating in complex, unknown, and dynamic real-world environments.
