# Eliciting Reasoning in Language Models with Cognitive Tools

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at IBM Research - Zurich and ETH Zurich developed "cognitive tools," a modular agentic framework inspired by cognitive psychology, to elicit advanced reasoning in Large Language Models. This approach enhanced LLM performance on mathematical reasoning benchmarks, with GPT-4.1 augmented by these tools achieving 53.0% accuracy on AIME 2024, surpassing the 44.6% of the RL-trained o1-preview model.

## Problem

- The precise mechanisms and origins of advanced reasoning in LLMs, particularly in closed models, remained opaque.
- Existing structured reasoning approaches like "cognitive prompting" lacked explicit modularity, potentially leading to interference and limiting flexible operation sequencing.
- There was an ongoing debate on whether LLM reasoning capabilities are primarily inherent (latent) or largely instilled through post-training methods like Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF).

## Method

- The authors developed "cognitive tools," a set of ==modular functions== encapsulating specific reasoning operations (e.g., understand question, recall related, examine answer, backtracking) within an agentic tool-calling framework.
- These tools are executed internally by the LLM itself in a sandboxed context, with structured intermediate results fed back to the main LLM to guide further reasoning.
- The framework allows the LLM to autonomously and flexibly select and orchestrate cognitive operations as needed, fostering adaptive problem-solving without additional model training.

## Results

- Individual cognitive tools consistently improved LLM performance on the Smolbenchmark dataset, with the "understand question" tool yielding up to a +**26.7%** accuracy increase for Llama3.3-**70B** Instruct.
- The full cognitive tools pipeline consistently enhanced pass@1 accuracy across various open-weight models (e.g., Qwen2.5, Llama3.1, Llama3.3) and challenging math benchmarks (AIME 2024, MATH500, AMC), with Qwen2.5-**32B** Instruct's AIME 2024 accuracy increasing from **17.2%** to **32.1%**.
- GPT-4.1 augmented with cognitive tools achieved **53.0%** pass@1 accuracy on AIME 2024, outperforming its baseline (**32.0%**) and surpassing the **44.6%** achieved by the RL-trained o1-preview model.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - LLMs possess strong latent reasoning capabilities that can be elicited through structured, modular cognitive operations, suggesting that pre-training instills foundational reasoning abilities.
> - Modularity and compartmentalization in reasoning workflows, as implemented by cognitive tools, consistently outperform monolithic, predefined prompting approaches like cognitive prompting.
> - Integrating principles from cognitive architectures with modern agentic tool-calling frameworks can enhance LLMs' intrinsic reasoning capabilities by extending the tool-calling paradigm to internal cognitive operations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a current area of inquiry in the field of Large Language Models (LLMs) concerning the origins and mechanisms of their reasoning capabilities. The advent of models like OpenAI's o1-preview, which demonstrate advanced reasoning through unfolding test-time computation and chain-of-thought (CoT) traces, spurred significant interest. Initially, there was speculation regarding the underlying mechanisms in closed models, followed by efforts from the open-source community to replicate these capabilities. DeepSeek-R1's demonstration that supervised fine-tuning on reasoning traces combined with reinforcement learning (RL) could effectively replicate reasoning on base LLMs partially settled the debate regarding the role of post-training methods.

However, a more recent critical reanalysis by Liu et al. (2025) and Yue et al. (2025) proposed that base models already possess strong latent reasoning capabilities. These studies suggest that RL's role might be to bias generation towards high-reward reasoning traces rather than to instill new reasoning abilities. This perspective motivates the exploration of alternative strategies to elicit or "uncover" existing reasoning capacities within LLMs.

This research builds upon the recognition by Kramer and Baumann (2024) that cognitive psychology and cognitive sciences offer relevant frameworks for investigating reasoning mechanisms. Specifically, it draws inspiration from foundational cognitive architectures like ACT-R (Anderson et al., 1997), which postulate that human reasoning arises from the orchestrated, sequential execution of modular cognitive operations. Kramer and Baumann (2024) translated these ideas into "cognitive prompting," a prompt engineering technique that structures LLM prompts into stages such as goal clarification and decomposition. This paper extends this line of research by arguing that cognitive prompting lacks explicit modularity and compartmentalization, which are identified as important principles for reducing interference and enabling compositional generalization in neural networks (Soldal, 2012; Ito et al., 2022).

By integrating insights from cognitive architectures with modern agentic tool-calling frameworks, this work aims to provide a modular and flexible approach to elicit reasoning, contributing to the understanding of LLM reasoning mechanisms and offering a potentially complementary or alternative method to RL-based fine-tuning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to explore and demonstrate an alternative method for eliciting reasoning capabilities in Large Language Models. This method is based on the integration of principles from cognitive psychology and cognitive architectures with modern agentic tool-calling frameworks.

The motivation for this work stems from several key observations and gaps in the existing research landscape:

1. **Elucidating Reasoning Mechanisms:** Despite the advanced reasoning capabilities observed in recent LLMs, the precise underlying mechanisms, particularly in closed models, remain opaque. The paper aims to provide a theoretical framework and practical implementation that could help elucidate these internal processes by structuring reasoning into discrete, interpretable operations.
2. **Addressing the Role of Post-Training:** The ongoing debate regarding whether LLM reasoning capabilities are primarily inherent (latent in base models) or largely instilled through post-training methods like Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) provides a significant motivation. If reasoning is largely inherent, then alternative, potentially more transparent and efficient, methods for eliciting these capabilities are valuable to explore. The paper aims to demonstrate that structured modular workflows can surface these latent abilities without additional training.
3. **Enhancing Existing Structured Reasoning Approaches:** Previous efforts, such as "cognitive prompting" (Kramer and Baumann, 2024), introduced the idea of structuring prompts to guide LLMs through reasoning stages. However, this monolithic prompting approach was identified as lacking explicit modularity and compartmentalization, potentially leading to interference between reasoning stages and limiting the flexibility of operation sequencing. The authors aim to overcome these limitations by encapsulating cognitive operations into discrete, self-contained tools.
4. **Leveraging Cognitive Science Principles:** The long-standing literature in cognitive psychology and cognitive architectures (e.g., ACT-R) posits that human reasoning arises from the orchestrated, sequential execution of modular, predetermined cognitive operations. The research is motivated by translating this fundamental principle into an LLM framework, expecting that modularity would reduce interference and support compositional generalization.
5. **Practical Performance Gains:** Beyond theoretical understanding, a key motivation is to develop a practical method that can improve LLM performance on complex reasoning tasks, specifically mathematical problem-solving, by providing a structured and guided problem-solving approach.
6. **Extending Agentic AI Frameworks:** Current agentic tool-calling frameworks primarily focus on enabling LLMs to interact with external tools (e.g., calculators, search engines). This work is motivated by the need to extend this paradigm to internal cognitive operations, thus enriching the LLM's intrinsic reasoning capabilities within a modular agentic structure.

By addressing these motivations, the authors aim to contribute both to the theoretical understanding of LLM reasoning and to the development of more robust, interpretable, and adaptable AI agents.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on endowing Large Language Models (LLMs) with a set of "cognitive tools" that encapsulate specific reasoning operations. This approach is implemented within a modern agentic tool-calling framework, distinguishing itself from conventional tool-calling by having the cognitive tools execute operations *within* the LLM itself, rather than invoking external functions or APIs.

**Core Idea and Implementation:**
The central concept is that reasoning emerges from the orchestrated, sequential execution of modular cognitive operations. Each cognitive tool is a self-contained function whose schema includes a prompt template designed to isolate and guide a specific cognitive operation. When the main LLM decides to invoke a tool, its generation is paused, the tool's encapsulated prompt is executed by the *same LLM instance* in a sandboxed context, and the structured intermediate result is then fed back to the main LLM, which continues its reasoning. This process allows the LLM to autonomously and flexibly select and apply cognitive operations as needed.

**Four Identified Cognitive Tools:**

1. **Understand Question:**
 - **Inspiration:** Goal management in human reasoning, as emphasized by cognitive architectures.
 - **Functionality:** Prompts the LLM to break down the problem by identifying main concepts, extracting relevant information, and highlighting useful properties, theorems, or techniques. It guides the LLM to rephrase the problem into a step-by-step sequence without providing the final answer.
 - **Prompt Elements:** Focuses on core mathematical concepts, symbols, variables, functions, step-by-step rephrasing, and known useful techniques.

2. **Recall Related:**
 - **Inspiration:** Analogical reasoning, drawing from Yasunaga et al. (2024).
 - **Functionality:** For a given problem, the tool provides relevant related knowledge from similar, already-solved questions, including their full problem statements and step-by-step solutions. Its objective is to guide the LLM's reasoning by offering analogous examples. It does not solve the current problem but provides context.
 - **Prompt Elements:** Instructs the LLM to identify 2-3 similar problems, provide their full statements and step-by-step solutions, and highlight final answers, all without solving the current problem.

3. **Examine Answer:**
 - **Inspiration:** Self-reflection, a demonstrated effective reasoning operation (Shinn et al., 2023).
 - **Functionality:** Critically analyzes the LLM's current reasoning trace for potential flaws, wrong assumptions, miscalculations, or overlooked constraints. It follows a structured verification process, including understanding the problem, verifying the solution (step-by-step breakdown, logical consistency, mathematical correctness, edge cases, problem-derived checks), suggesting improvements without solving, and providing a judgment (correct/incorrect) with justification.
 - **Prompt Elements:** Tasks the LLM to verify and improve solutions, not solve, focusing on problem interpretation, logical steps, mathematical correctness, test cases, and suggesting precise fixes.

4. **Backtracking:**
 - **Inspiration:** Adapting from flawed thought processes or incorrect solutions, similar to Monte Carlo Tree Search (Liang et al., 2024).
 - **Functionality:** When an incorrect solution or flawed reasoning is detected, this tool enables the LLM to backtrack to a previously correct step and explore alternative paths. It prompts the LLM to summarize and break down the current reasoning, identify the incorrect step, and propose alternative approaches or directions.
 - **Prompt Elements:** Instructs the LLM to analyze reasoning, summarize steps, identify the first error, propose revisions from that point, or suggest a new strategy if the entire approach is invalid.

**Cognitive Tools Prompt (System Prompt):**
A comprehensive system prompt orchestrates the use of these tools. It instructs the LLM on its role as an expert assistant, encourages tool usage for complex tasks, and defines rules for tool calling (e.g., use Python syntax, only available tools, wait for responses, do not hallucinate, provide final answer in a specific format). It also includes a placeholder for tool signatures and motivational phrases (though ablation studies show these do not negatively impact performance).

**Additional Modular Tool: Code Generation:**
To leverage LLMs' code generation capabilities, the framework also allows the LLM to generate code as an additional modular reasoning tool. This code is then executed, and its output is fed back into the main reasoning loop, enabling computational problem-solving.

**Comparison with Cognitive Prompting:**
The paper explicitly contrasts its approach with Kramer and Baumann's (2024) cognitive prompting. While both draw from cognitive architectures, cognitive prompting employs a monolithic prompt that dictates a fixed order of steps. Cognitive tools, conversely, emphasize modularity and compartmentalization, allowing the LLM to flexibly choose and orchestrate tools, reducing potential interference between operations and fostering adaptive problem-solving strategies.

**Computational Aspects:**
The methodology introduces a computational overhead. Experiments indicate that using cognitive tools incurs a cost in terms of output tokens, with an average of 4,200 output tokens per question for GPT-4.1 on AIME2024, compared to 2,000 for the baseline, reflecting the increased compute for structured reasoning.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings regarding the effectiveness of cognitive tools in eliciting reasoning capabilities in LLMs across various mathematical reasoning benchmarks.

#### 4.1. Individual Cognitive Tool Effectiveness

Experiments on the Smolbenchmark dataset (Table 1) demonstrated that each of the four cognitive tools—"understand question," "recall related," "examine answer," and "backtracking"—individually contributed to performance gains over the baseline LLMs.
- Improvements were observed across Qwen2.5 (7B, 32B), Llama3.1-8B, and Llama3.3-70B Instruct models.
- The "understand question" tool yielded a substantial increase, for instance, a +26.7% jump for Llama3.3-70B Instruct (from 52.8% to 79.5%).
- The impact of each tool varied across different models, indicating that different LLMs may benefit more from specific cognitive operations depending on their inherent reasoning patterns. Overall, the individual tools consistently outperformed the baseline, with statistically significant differences (p < 0.05).

#### 4.2. Cognitive Tools vs. Cognitive Prompting

A comparison on the Smolbenchmark (Table 2) showed that the modular cognitive tools approach consistently surpassed the performance of "cognitive prompting" (Kramer and Baumann, 2024), which uses a monolithic, predefined prompt structure.
- While cognitive prompting typically matched or outperformed the baseline, cognitive tools delivered superior performance.
- Performance increases ranged from +4.2% on Qwen2.5-7B Instruct to +27.2% on Llama3.3-70B over their respective baselines, confirming the benefits of modularity and flexible tool orchestration.

#### 4.3. Overall Performance on Math Benchmarks

The full cognitive tools pipeline, with all tools available to the LLM, was evaluated on challenging math benchmarks: AIME 2024, MATH500, and AMC (Table 3, Figure 2).
- **Consistent Improvements:** Cognitive tools consistently improved pass@1 accuracy across all tested open-weight models (Qwen2.5-7B/32B, Llama3.1-8B, Llama3.3-70B Instruct) and all benchmarks.
- **Challenging Datasets:** Significant improvements were observed even on highly difficult datasets like AIME 2024. For example, Qwen2.5-32B Instruct's accuracy on AIME 2024 increased from 17.2% to 32.1% with cognitive tools. Llama3.3-70B Instruct increased from 13.1% to 29.8%.
- **Synergy with Code Generation:** Even when models were equipped with code generation capabilities, the addition of cognitive tools further enhanced reasoning performance, indicating a complementary relationship rather than a substitutive one. For instance, Qwen2.5-32B Instruct + code achieved 50.2% average accuracy, while Qwen2.5-32B Instruct + cognitive tools achieved 58.9%.

#### 4.4. Comparison with RL-trained Reasoning Models

A key finding involved the comparison of GPT-4.1 (a closed model) augmented with cognitive tools against o1-preview (an RL-trained reasoning model) on AIME 2024 (Table 4).
- GPT-4.1 with cognitive tools achieved 53.0% pass@1 accuracy, significantly outperforming its baseline of 32.0%.
- Remarkably, GPT-4.1 with cognitive tools also surpassed the performance of o1-preview, which achieved 44.6% on the same benchmark.
- This result suggests that cognitive tools can elicit high-level reasoning comparable to or exceeding RL-trained models in certain contexts, without requiring additional model training.

#### 4.5. Tool Usage Statistics (Appendix B.2)

Analysis of tool calls revealed task-dependent and model-dependent usage patterns (Table 6, Table 7).
- **Task Difficulty:** The "understand question" tool was called more frequently for harder benchmarks like AIME 2024 (80% of samples) compared to easier ones like Smolbenchmark (14%). Similarly, "examine answer" was used more on AIME 2024 (60%) than Smolbenchmark (20%).
- **Code Tool:** The "use code" tool was frequently invoked across all datasets, suggesting its utility in mathematical problem-solving (e.g., 77% on AIME 2024).
- **Model Differences:** Llama models tended to use "understand question" and "examine answer" more frequently than Qwen models.
- **Correlated Usage:** "Backtracking" and "recall related" tools, though less frequently used individually, displayed the highest correlation in co-occurrence (Pearson correlation of 0.18 on Smolbenchmark) (Table 8). This may indicate a "recovery workflow" where models recall information and backtrack upon failure.

#### 4.6. Computational Overhead (Appendix B.3)

The use of cognitive tools led to an increased computational cost. For GPT-4.1 on AIME 2024, the average number of output tokens per question was 4,200 with cognitive tools, more than double the 2,000 tokens for the baseline, reflecting the additional steps involved in structured reasoning.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work demonstrates a modular approach to eliciting reasoning in Large Language Models, which carries several implications for both theoretical understanding and practical applications of AI.

**Theoretical Contributions:**
The findings contribute to the ongoing discussion regarding the inherent capabilities of LLMs versus the role of post-training methods in developing reasoning. By showing that base models, when endowed with structured modular workflows, can achieve substantial reasoning gains and even surpass some RL-trained models, the research lends support to the hypothesis that pre-training instills latent reasoning capabilities. This suggests that post-training might primarily serve to uncover or refine these existing abilities rather than entirely instilling new ones, offering an alternative perspective to RL's role in reasoning. The cognitive tools framework provides a more interpretable mechanism for examining how reasoning unfolds within LLMs, as each step is associated with a distinct, predefined cognitive operation.

**Practical Implications:**
The proposed method offers a practical and effective strategy for enhancing the reasoning performance of both open-weight and closed LLMs without requiring extensive additional training or fine-tuning. This could reduce the computational and data annotation demands typically associated with RL-based reasoning models, thereby democratizing access to advanced reasoning capabilities. The observed gains on challenging mathematical benchmarks highlight the utility of this approach for domains requiring precise, multi-step problem-solving.

**Advancements in Agentic AI:**
This research bridges a gap in current agentic AI frameworks. While existing frameworks primarily focus on enabling LLMs to interact with external tools (e.g., search engines, calculators), this work extends the tool-calling paradigm to encapsulate and orchestrate *internal* cognitive operations. This integration of internal, modular reasoning steps within an agentic architecture represents a step towards more sophisticated and autonomous AI agents capable of deliberative problem-solving.

**Enhanced Transparency and Explainability:**
By structuring the reasoning process into discrete, tool-associated steps, the framework inherently promotes greater transparency and explainability in AI agents. Each decision to invoke a specific cognitive tool (e.g., "understand question," "examine answer," "backtracking") provides a trace of the LLM's thought process, making it potentially easier to audit, understand, and debug AI decision-making. This is particularly crucial for real-world applications in high-stakes domains such as healthcare, education, and legal analysis, where interpretable reasoning is paramount.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
Despite its contributions, the method has identified limitations. The current cognitive tools are manually defined, and their prompt templates may require specific engineering efforts for different model families or domains. The evaluation has primarily focused on mathematical reasoning tasks, and generalizability to other complex reasoning domains needs further investigation. Future work could explore the automated discovery or generation of cognitive operations, integrate cognitive tools with reinforcement learning to potentially combine their respective strengths, and expand applications to a broader range of reasoning tasks beyond arithmetic.

In summary, the cognitive tools framework presents a robust and interpretable method for eliciting and enhancing LLM reasoning, contributing to both the theoretical understanding of AI capabilities and the development of more transparent and effective AI systems.
