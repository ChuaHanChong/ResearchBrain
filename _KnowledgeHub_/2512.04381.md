# FALCON: Actively Decoupled Visuomotor Policies for Loco-Manipulation with Foundation-Model-Based Coordination

> [!summary] Summary
> FALCON enables robust loco-manipulation for legged robots by actively decoupling locomotion and manipulation into separate visuomotor policies. A frozen Vision-Language Model acts as a semantic coordinator, allowing the system to achieve 87% and 100% success rates on complex manipulation and mobile manipulation tasks respectively, outperforming centralized baselines.

## Problem

- The inherent complexity of legged loco-manipulation requires simultaneous dynamic stability and precise manipulation, often with conflicting control objectives and heterogeneous sensor streams.
- Monolithic whole-body policies for legged robots are difficult to train, generalize poorly to out-of-distribution conditions, and struggle with diverse observations and control objectives.
- Decoupled control systems face the challenge of maintaining coherent, task-aware coordination between independent policies that do not share raw observations or action spaces.

## Method

- The system uses actively decoupled visuomotor diffusion policies, with one specialized for quadruped locomotion and another for robotic arm manipulation, each operating in its optimal observation and control space.
- A frozen CLIP Vision-Language Model (VLM) functions as a semantic coordinator, encoding multi-modal inputs and language instructions into a shared latent embedding that conditions both decoupled policies.
- A language-defined phase and progress head provides zero-shot temporal grounding, and a coordination-aware contrastive loss enforces cross-subsystem compatibility between the arm and quadruped actions.

## Results

- FALCON achieved an 87% overall success rate on a precise manipulation task and a 100% overall success rate on a mobile manipulation task, consistently outperforming centralized diffusion (CDP), ACT, and LatentToM baselines.
- Ablation studies confirmed that both the language-defined phase and progress head and the coordination-aware contrastive loss are critical, significantly contributing to the framework's performance and stability.
- The framework demonstrated strong generalization capabilities to out-of-distribution scenarios and superior robustness and coordination quality in human-in-the-loop evaluations for both tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Actively decoupling locomotion and manipulation in legged robots, rather than using a monolithic policy, improves robustness and generalization for complex, asymmetric tasks.
> - Foundation models can be effectively leveraged as lightweight semantic coordinators, utilizing their pre-trained knowledge to guide sub-policies without requiring extensive end-to-end training.
> - Language-defined phase and progress signals, combined with contrastive learning, offer effective mechanisms to ensure temporal coherence and mutual consistency within decoupled robotic architectures.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

FALCON is situated at the intersection of several rapidly evolving fields within robotics: robot learning, foundation models (FMs), and mobile manipulation, specifically focusing on the challenging domain of legged loco-manipulation.

The broader landscape of robotics is increasingly moving towards general-purpose robots capable of operating in unstructured environments. Recent advancements in "Large Behavior Models (LBMs)" and "Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models" (e.g., Boston Dynamics' Atlas LBM, Figure AI's Helix, NVIDIA's GR00T N1.5, Physical Intelligence's π0/π0.5, Flexion's Reflect, and Sunday Robotics' Memo) aim to create generalist models that can consume raw sensor data and language instructions to directly output actions. These models often adopt an end-to-end approach, integrating perception, planning, and control into a single monolithic policy.

However, FALCON challenges this monolithic trend in the context of loco-manipulation on *legged* platforms. While most deployed mobile manipulators are wheeled (e.g., Mobile ALOHA, Memo, π0) due to their simpler dynamics and mature navigation stacks, legged robots (like quadrupeds) offer unparalleled versatility in traversing complex terrains such as stairs, clutter, and uneven surfaces. This versatility, however, comes at the cost of significantly increased complexity, as the robot must simultaneously manage dynamic stability, long-horizon navigation, and precise manipulation. Existing monolithic policies struggle with this asymmetry, often facing difficulties in training, sensitivity to dataset coverage, and poor generalization to out-of-distribution (OOD) conditions due to the fusion of heterogeneous observations and control objectives.

FALCON proposes a significant departure from these norms:
- **Decoupled Control:** Instead of a single whole-body policy, it advocates for *actively decoupling* locomotion and manipulation into two specialized visuomotor policies. This contrasts with many learning-based whole-body control approaches and aligns with ideas of modularity seen in multi-arm cooperation like LatentToM.
- **Foundation Model as Coordinator:** Unlike end-to-end VLA models (e.g., π0, GR00T N1) where the foundation model directly outputs actions, FALCON employs a *frozen Vision-Language Model (VLM)* (specifically CLIP) purely as a *semantic coordinator*. This VLM encodes global context and language instructions into a shared latent embedding that conditions the decoupled policies, providing high-level guidance without dictating low-level actions. This approach leverages the powerful semantic priors of FMs without incurring the computational and data demands of training large-scale VLA models from scratch or risking catastrophic interference.
- **Phase-Aware and Contrastive Learning:** The introduction of a language-defined phase-progress head and a coordination-aware contrastive loss explicitly structures the latent space, offering a novel way to provide temporal grounding and cross-subsystem compatibility. This addresses a critical challenge in decoupled systems: maintaining coherence between independent modules.

Therefore, FALCON distinguishes itself by presenting a modular, robust, and generalizable architecture for legged loco-manipulation. It intelligently harnesses the strengths of diffusion policies for visuomotor control and foundation models for semantic coordination, offering a compelling alternative to the prevailing monolithic end-to-end VLA paradigm, particularly for complex, asymmetric robotic tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of FALCON is to enable robust and generalizable loco-manipulation for legged robots, specifically quadruped mobile manipulators, in unstructured environments using only RGB observations, proprioceptive states, and sparse language instructions. The authors identify several key challenges and motivations for their approach:

1. **Addressing the Complexity of Legged Loco-Manipulation:** Legged robots are desired for their ability to navigate complex terrains (stairs, clutter, uneven ground) and actively re-position their base to enhance manipulability, extending beyond the limitations of wheeled platforms. However, this simultaneously demands dynamic stability and precise manipulation, often with conflicting control objectives and heterogeneous sensor streams. This inherent complexity is a major hurdle.

2. **Mitigating Drawbacks of Monolithic Whole-Body Policies:** Conventional whole-body control methods, whether classical or learning-based, often attempt to fuse all observations into a single policy. The authors argue that such monolithic approaches suffer from:
 - **Training Difficulty:** Optimizing a single policy for diverse and often conflicting locomotion and manipulation objectives is notoriously hard.
 - **Sensitivity to Dataset Coverage:** These policies tend to overfit and generalize poorly, especially when one subsystem encounters out-of-distribution (OOD) conditions (e.g., unexpected terrain), which can corrupt the shared latent representation.
 - **Observation Mismatch:** Forcing a single policy to handle heterogeneous observations (e.g., global navigation cues vs. fine-grained end-effector visuals) and control objectives (e.g., open-loop velocity for base vs. precise position for arm) can lead to performance degradation.

3. **Restoring Coordination in Decoupled Systems:** While decoupling offers benefits in specialization and robustness for individual subsystems, it creates a new challenge: how to ensure coherent, task-aware coordination between independent policies that no longer share raw observations or action spaces.

4. **Leveraging Foundation Models Effectively:** The paper questions the prevalent end-to-end VLA (Vision-Language-Action) formulation of foundation models in robotics, which often requires massive datasets and compute, and whose internal coordination mechanisms remain opaque. The motivation is to explore a more targeted and efficient use of FMs.

To address these motivations, FALCON sets out with three core design principles:
- **Deliberate Decoupling:** Explicitly separate locomotion and manipulation into independent visuomotor policies, allowing each to operate in its most natural observation and control space.
- **Foundation-Model-Guided Coordination:** Utilize a frozen VLM (CLIP) as a *semantic coordinator* rather than an end-to-end controller. This provides a lightweight, semantically rich consensus channel by encoding global observations and language instructions into a shared latent embedding that conditions both policies.
- **Phase-Aware Semantic Coordination:** Introduce a phase-progress head to infer discrete task phases and continuous progress estimates from textual descriptions (without manual labels), and incorporate a coordination-aware contrastive loss to explicitly encode cross-subsystem compatibility between arm and base actions. This aims to provide temporal grounding and enforce mutual consistency.

By pursuing these objectives, FALCON seeks to achieve improved robustness, generalization, and modularity in complex loco-manipulation tasks, offering a practical and flexible alternative to fully monolithic approaches.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

FALCON's methodology is built upon a modular, decoupled architecture that integrates diffusion policies with a vision-language foundation model for high-level coordination.

**A. Decoupled Loco-Manipulation System:**
The core idea is to treat locomotion and manipulation as two independent visuomotor subsystems, each with its own diffusion policy, observation space, and control regime.
- **Locomotion Subsystem:** A high-level diffusion policy (π_quad) generates velocity and pose commands (linear/angular velocity, body pitch, body height). These commands are executed by a *pre-trained low-level RL joint controller* (π_LLC) that handles the complex, dynamically consistent joint-angle control of the quadruped (Unitree Go2 base). This shields the high-level policy from low-level dynamics. The locomotion policy primarily uses head/body camera RGB views and base proprioceptive states.
- **Manipulation Subsystem:** A separate diffusion policy (π_arm) outputs high-level end-effector position targets, which are then converted to joint commands via an Inverse Kinematics (IK) solver for the robotic arm (Airbot arm). This policy relies on wrist/body camera RGB views and arm proprioceptive states.

**B. Vision-Language Foundation Model for Coordination:**
A frozen CLIP model serves as the central semantic coordinator, generating a shared latent embedding (z_t) that conditions both decoupled diffusion policies.
- **Input Fusion:** CLIP processes multi-modal inputs including multi-view RGB images (from wrist, body, and head cameras), proprioceptive robot states (end-effector pose, robot base state), and natural language task instructions.
- **Global Embedding:** A shallow Transformer encoder fuses CLIP image features from each camera view, MLP-encoded proprioception, and a CLIP text embedding of the language instruction into a global embedding (h_t).
- **Frozen Backbone:** CLIP is kept frozen and used as a feature extractor, with only some trainable projectors for task-specific adaptation. This prevents catastrophic interference and leverages CLIP's extensive pre-trained semantic knowledge.

**C. Phase-Aware Semantic Coordination with Contrastive Alignment:**
Two key mechanisms enhance the coordination:
1. **Language-Defined Phase and Progress Head:**
 - Human-interpretable task phases (e.g., "move," "align," "place," "close" for the drawer tasks) are defined by natural language "ongoing" and "done" textual prompts.
 - The model (using the frozen CLIP text encoder) computes phase logits (ℓ_t,k) and probabilities (ρ_t) by comparing the current image embedding (f_t) with the phase prompts.
 - Continuous progress estimates (c_t,k) within each phase are derived from the similarity difference between "done" and "ongoing" prompts.
 - A scalar progress variable (p_t) is then calculated based on the most probable phase and its completion.
 - The final conditioning vector (z_t) passed to the diffusion policies concatenates the global embedding (h_t), phase probabilities (ρ_t), and scalar progress (p_t). This entire mechanism is zero-shot, requiring no manual phase labels.

2. **Coordination-Aware Contrastive Loss (L_coord):**
 - This loss explicitly enforces cross-subsystem compatibility.
 - For each training sample, aggregated latent features (¯z_i) and aggregated joint actions (u_i = [¯a_arm_i; ¯a_quad_i]) are mapped into a common contrastive space.
 - An InfoNCE-style loss encourages the observation embedding (v_i) to align with its *correctly coordinated* joint action (w_i) while repelling it from *mismatched* negative pairs. Mismatched pairs are created by shuffling arm and quadruped action summaries across different samples (e.g., [¯a_arm_i; ¯a_quad_λ(i)]), serving as hard negatives.
 - The total training loss (L_tot) combines the diffusion policy losses for the manipulator and quadruped (L_(arm)diff + L_(quad)diff) with this coordination regularizer (δL_coord).

**D. System Deployment and Training:**
- **Hardware:** Unitree Go2 quadruped with an Airbot arm. Tri-camera setup (wrist-mounted Intel RealSense D435i, head-mounted Intel RealSense D455, body-mounted GoPro Hero 11) provides RGB-only inputs.
- **Inference:** Offboard on an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU, with 10 Hz policy inference. Low-level controllers run at higher frequencies (50 Hz for base RL controller, 100 Hz for arm IK).
- **Data Collection:** Policies are trained on 50 teleoperated demonstrations collected on the real robot. Domain randomization is applied during low-level controller training.
- **Baselines:** Centralized Diffusion Policy (CDP), Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT), and LatentToM are used for comparison, all trained under identical conditions.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

FALCON was rigorously evaluated on two challenging loco-manipulation tasks: a precise manipulation task (Task 1: opening a drawer, picking up a toy, and placing it inside) and a mobile manipulation task (Task 2: navigating to an open drawer, placing a held toy inside, and closing the drawer). Experiments included comparisons with baselines, ablation studies, and human-in-the-loop evaluations, spanning both in-distribution (InD) and out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios.

**A. Overall Performance (Tasks 1 & 2):**
- **Superior Success Rates:** FALCON consistently achieved the highest success rates across both tasks and various initial configurations.
 - **Task 1 (Precise Manipulation):** FALCON achieved an 87% overall success rate, significantly outperforming LatentToM (27%), CDP (20%), and ACT (0%). ACT failed to complete the task even once.
 - **Task 2 (Mobile Manipulation):** FALCON achieved a 100% overall success rate, surpassing LatentToM (67%), CDP (40%), and ACT (60%).
- **Decoupling Advantage:** A key finding was the substantial performance gap between decoupled methods (FALCON, LatentToM) and centralized methods (CDP, ACT). This was attributed to the inherent asymmetry between the quadruped's velocity-controlled locomotion (prone to stability issues and long-horizon challenges) and the arm's position-controlled manipulation (requiring high precision in a limited workspace). Decoupling allowed each subsystem to specialize and maintain robustness, leading to more effective task completion.

**B. Contributions of Individual Components (Ablation Studies):**
- **Phase & Progress Head (Language-Defined Coordination):** Removing the phase and progress head (FALCON w/o Phase & CL) resulted in a substantial performance drop across all stages in both tasks. This highlights the critical role of these language-defined, zero-shot signals in providing an inductive bias, stabilizing training, and decomposing complex multi-modal action distributions into more manageable conditional distributions.
- **Coordination-Aware Contrastive Loss:**
 - **Task 1 (InD):** The contrastive loss provided a larger performance gain, indicating its effectiveness in shaping the latent space for mutual consistency under more predictable, in-distribution conditions.
 - **Task 2 (OOD):** In OOD scenarios, the version without contrastive loss (FALCON w/o CL) sometimes performed comparably to, or even slightly better than, the full FALCON model in the navigation stage. The authors hypothesize that the contrastive loss, by enforcing stronger coupling, might reduce the base's generalization capability when it becomes overly dependent on manipulation-specific latent cues in unfamiliar OOD conditions. However, the full FALCON model still maintained a slightly higher overall success rate, suggesting that the arm's robustness can compensate for minor navigation errors.

**C. Generalization to Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Scenarios:**
- FALCON (and FALCON w/o CL) demonstrated strong generalization capabilities, especially in Task 2, where initial robot positions were sampled from regions (left, right) rarely encountered during training. This indicates the framework's ability to adapt to novel starting conditions, which is crucial for real-world deployment.

**D. Human-in-the-Loop Evaluation:**
- FALCON achieved a 100% success rate across all ten trials in human-in-the-loop experiments for both tasks, where either the base or the arm was teleoperated.
- FALCON-controlled subsystems exhibited significantly better robustness and coordination quality compared to LatentToM when paired with human-generated behavior. This validates the modularity and practical utility of the decoupled framework in mixed-autonomy or collaborative scenarios, allowing for human intervention on specific subsystems without compromising overall task performance. Failures observed for FALCON (e.g., camera obstruction by teleoperated arm) were attributed to human errors rather than policy limitations.

**E. Baseline Limitations:**
- **ACT:** Showed reasonable success in navigation and toy placement but consistently failed in drawer-closing due to poor whole-body coordination, leading to arm collisions when the base tilted.
- **LatentToM:** Performed well in in-distribution navigation but suffered substantial performance drops during manipulation stages, suggesting its coordination mechanism might be less effective for the strong asymmetries of loco-manipulation compared to multi-arm manipulation.
- **CDP:** Demonstrated comparable performance in in-distribution scenarios but experienced a noticeable decline under OOD conditions.

In summary, FALCON's empirical results strongly support the efficacy of its actively decoupled architecture, foundation-model-guided coordination, and phase-aware semantic alignment. It provides a robust, generalizable, and modular solution for complex loco-manipulation tasks, particularly on dynamically challenging legged platforms.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

FALCON represents a significant contribution to the field of robot learning and mobile manipulation, with several potential impacts:

1. **Advancing Legged Loco-Manipulation:** The work addresses one of the most challenging areas in robotics by enabling robust and precise loco-manipulation on legged platforms. This is critical for deploying robots in complex, unstructured human environments (e.g., homes, industrial sites, disaster zones) where wheeled robots are limited. By successfully handling the asymmetry and coordination challenges between locomotion and manipulation, FALCON pushes the boundaries of what legged mobile manipulators can achieve.

2. **Novel Paradigm for Foundation Model Integration:** FALCON offers a compelling alternative to the prevailing end-to-end VLA paradigm for foundation models in robotics. By leveraging a frozen VLM (CLIP) as a *semantic coordinator* rather than a full action generator, it demonstrates that powerful FMs can be integrated in a lightweight, efficient, and robust manner. This approach avoids the massive data and computational requirements of training large VLA models from scratch and prevents catastrophic forgetting, making FM integration more accessible and practical for diverse robotic applications. This could inspire new architectural designs for generalist robots.

3. **Validation of Decoupled Control:** The paper provides strong empirical evidence for the benefits of *actively decoupled control* in complex, asymmetric robotic systems like loco-manipulators. It shows that by allowing specialized policies to operate in their natural observation and control spaces, performance, robustness, and generalization can be significantly improved compared to monolithic approaches. This challenges the conventional wisdom of always seeking a single, unified policy and highlights the power of modularity when coupled with intelligent coordination.

4. **Enhanced Robustness and Generalization:** FALCON's superior performance in OOD scenarios and human-in-the-loop evaluations underscores its practical utility. Robots often encounter unforeseen conditions, and the ability to generalize to novel situations and collaborate effectively with humans is paramount for real-world deployment. The framework's robustness to varying initial conditions and human-induced variability is a major step towards deployable, adaptable robotic systems.

5. **Structured Learning with Language-Defined Phases:** The zero-shot, language-defined phase and progress head is an innovative method for incorporating high-level task structure into visuomotor policies without requiring manual phase annotations. This technique provides a crucial inductive bias that stabilizes and accelerates learning, making it easier to scale to more complex, multi-step tasks. It further demonstrates the power of language as an interface for shaping robot behavior.

6. **Future of Hybrid Autonomy:** The success in human-in-the-loop settings highlights FALCON's potential for hybrid autonomy, where humans and robots can seamlessly collaborate. The modular design allows for intuitive human intervention or supervision on specific subsystems, enabling human operators to easily take over problematic parts of a task without needing to control the entire robot. This flexibility is vital for safe and efficient human-robot interaction in practical settings.

In essence, FALCON presents a well-reasoned and empirically validated framework that pushes the frontier of general-purpose robotics. Its innovative use of foundation models for coordination and its robust decoupled architecture pave the way for more capable, adaptable, and deployable legged mobile manipulators in real-world environments.
