# SIRI-Bench: Challenging VLMs' Spatial Intelligence through Complex Reasoning Tasks

> [!summary] Summary
> This research from Sun Yat-sen University introduces SIRI-Bench, a new benchmark comprising 9,078 video-based 3D geometry problems, to evaluate Vision-Language Models' structural spatial intelligence. It reveals that state-of-the-art VLMs struggle with extracting implicit spatial information from videos and performing multi-step reasoning, with the best model achieving only 31% accuracy within a 40% error margin, significantly lagging behind text-only models and human performance.

## Problem

- Current VLM benchmarks for spatial abilities often focus on intuitive perception (e.g., distance, layout) rather than complex, multi-step structural reasoning.
- Existing reasoning benchmarks for LLMs/VLMs primarily use abstract textual descriptions or 2D diagrams, providing explicit conditions that do not necessitate sophisticated visual interpretation from realistic 3D scenes.
- There is a need for robust evaluation of VLMs' capacity to derive complex mathematical conditions and spatial relationships directly from visual input in realistic 3D environments, crucial for real-world applications like robotics.

## Method

- Introduces **SIRI-Bench**, a benchmark of 9,078 ==video-question-answer triplets== derived from solid geometry problems where mathematical conditions are implicitly embedded within realistic 3D scenes.
- Develops an ==Automatic Scene Creation Engine==, a ==multi-agent LLM system==, to automatically generate these 3D scenes and render them into videos, transforming abstract geometry problems into visual data at scale.
- Designs the benchmark to replace 2D diagrams and explicit textual conditions with ==dynamic 3D videos== and ==color-coded visual markers==, forcing VLMs to infer spatial information from visual input.

## Results

- State-of-the-art VLMs achieved limited performance on SIRI-Bench, with the best model having only **31%** of predictions within a 0–**40%** error margin, and over **33%** exceeding **100%** error.
- Text-input LLMs significantly outperformed video-input VLMs, with text-only models achieving **89.2%** within a **20%** error margin vs. **18.4%** for vision models.
- VLMs frequently misidentified geometric types (e.g., GPT-4o misidentified in **~79%** of problems), indicating struggles with basic geometric recognition and inference.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Current state-of-the-art VLMs possess limited structural spatial intelligence, consistently underperforming on complex 3D spatially-grounded reasoning tasks compared to text-only models and human baselines.
> - The primary bottleneck for VLMs on such tasks is the difficulty in accurately extracting and interpreting implicit spatial information (like object types, dimensions, and relationships) from dynamic 3D visual inputs.
> - Explicit multi-step reasoning ("Deep-Thinking") offers some performance benefits for VLMs when processing complex visual inputs, but does not bridge the fundamental gap in spatial perception and inference.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Recent advancements in Large Language Models (LLMs) and Vision-Language Models (VLMs) have enabled progress in structural reasoning across various complex tasks, often through extensive training on domains like mathematics and programming. This progress has led to the development of numerous benchmarks designed to assess the reasoning capabilities of these models. However, the authors identify a gap in the current research landscape: most existing benchmarks for LLMs and VLMs primarily focus on abstract reasoning tasks such as algebra, program synthesis, or geometric reasoning based on two-dimensional (2D) diagrams. These benchmarks often overlook visual-based reasoning tasks, particularly those requiring complex spatial intelligence, which is fundamental for VLMs to effectively interact with and operate within real-world environments.

Spatial intelligence, defined as the capacity to interpret spatial information, encompasses skills like perceiving shapes, understanding transformations, and applying spatial knowledge. This capability is considered essential for VLMs in applications such as robotics and autonomous systems. While some studies have explored spatial intelligence in VLMs, these efforts have largely concentrated on "intuitive intelligence," focusing on tasks like distance estimation or layout understanding. The current work differentiates itself by addressing "structural spatial intelligence," which requires complex reasoning processes to solve spatially grounded problems.

The paper introduces SIRI-Bench to bridge this gap. Unlike conventional benchmarks that rely on text or 2D diagrams where conditions are explicitly described, SIRI-Bench embeds key conditions and spatial relations implicitly within realistic 3D scenes presented as videos. This design forces VLMs to engage in both spatial perception and structural reasoning to extract information and solve problems, thereby providing a more rigorous and real-world-proximate evaluation of their capabilities in complex spatial reasoning. By focusing on 3D geometry problems rendered in dynamic visual contexts, this research aims to extend the evaluation of VLM reasoning beyond abstract symbolic or static 2D visual inputs.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from the observation that while spatial intelligence is a critical capability for Vision-Language Models (VLMs) in real-world interaction, their complex spatial reasoning ability remains systematically underexplored. Existing benchmarks for VLMs and Large Language Models (LLMs) tend to concentrate on abstract reasoning tasks (e.g., algebra, program synthesis, 2D geometric reasoning) rather than visual-based reasoning tasks that necessitate a deep understanding of spatial relationships within realistic contexts.

Specifically, the authors identify the following limitations in the current evaluation landscape:
- **Focus on Intuitive Spatial Intelligence**: Previous studies on spatial intelligence in VLMs have predominantly addressed "intuitive intelligence," such as perceiving object shapes, estimating distances, or understanding basic layouts. This level of understanding is considered insufficient for complex problem-solving in dynamic, three-dimensional environments.
- **Reliance on Explicit Information**: Many reasoning benchmarks provide problem conditions either explicitly in text or through abstract 2D diagrams, which can be interpreted through methods like optical character recognition (OCR) or direct symbolic mapping. This approach does not adequately challenge VLMs to infer implicit spatial information from visual cues in a realistic setting.
- **Lack of Structural Spatial Reasoning Evaluation**: There is a recognized need to evaluate "structural spatial intelligence," which involves multi-step inference, abstract thinking, and integration of knowledge derived from visual spatial information, akin to how LLMs have been advanced through reinforcement learning on complex mathematical and programming tasks.

To address these limitations, the key objectives of this research are:
- **Introduce SIRI-Bench**: To develop and release the Spatial Intelligence ReasonIng Benchmark (SIRI-Bench), a dataset specifically designed to evaluate VLMs' structural spatial intelligence. This benchmark features spatially-grounded reasoning tasks embedded in realistic 3D scenes rendered as videos, requiring both spatial comprehension and structural reasoning.
- **Enable Large-Scale Data Synthesis**: To develop an Automatic Scene Creation Engine that can efficiently translate abstract mathematical problems, particularly those from solid geometry, into faithful and realistic 3D scenes and render them into video-question-answer triplets. This engine aims to overcome the labor-intensive nature of manual data generation.
- **Benchmark State-of-the-Art VLMs**: To conduct comprehensive experiments with a range of contemporary VLMs on SIRI-Bench. The objective is to assess their current performance, identify specific weaknesses in extracting spatial information from visual inputs, and uncover limitations in their ability to perform complex reasoning within a spatially-grounded context.
- **Highlight a Research Direction**: To draw researchers' attention to the challenges of spatially-grounded reasoning and motivate advancements in VLMs for visual problem-solving, thereby contributing to models that can robustly handle real-world spatial interactions.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research revolves around the creation and application of SIRI-Bench, a novel benchmark designed to evaluate VLMs' structural spatial intelligence.

#### 3.1. SIRI-Bench Design and Data Collection

SIRI-Bench is composed of 9,078 video–question–answer triplets. Each problem is derived from solid geometry mathematics questions, chosen for their inherent requirement for multi-step and structural reasoning. Unlike traditional benchmarks that use textual descriptions or 2D diagrams, SIRI-Bench embeds key mathematical conditions implicitly within realistic 3D scenes, presented as 8-second videos. This design necessitates that VLMs extract relevant information through spatial perception and reasoning, rather than relying on explicit textual cues.

Data collection involved sourcing 3D geometry math problems from publicly available online educational resources. These problems span difficulty levels from middle to high school. To maintain consistency and focus on quantitative reasoning, problems that were low-quality, proof-based, or non-numerical were filtered out, leaving only those requiring explicit value computation, which were then converted into open-ended problem-solving tasks.

#### 3.2. 3D Spatial Representation

The core of SIRI-Bench’s methodology is its 3D spatial representation, which transforms original geometry problems into realistic 3D scenes. An original geometry problem is typically broken down into five components: (1) Illustration Diagram, (2) Geometry’s Category, (3) Primary Conditions, (4) Auxiliary Conditions, and (5) Final Question. Each component undergoes distinct processing steps for the 3D spatial representation:
- **Illustration Diagram**: Replaced by a dynamic 3D realistic video of the scene.
- **Geometry’s Category**: The main geometric entity is visually presented in the 3D scene. Its textual name (e.g., “pyramid,” “cube”) is replaced with a generic term like “the object,” requiring the model to identify its shape visually. Vertex indices (e.g., ‘S’, ‘A’, ‘B’) are replaced with color-based markers (e.g., ‘P_orange’, ‘P_red’) placed within the 3D scene to ensure unambiguous visual references.
- **Primary Conditions**: These conditions, such as dimensions and spatial relations of the main geometric entity, are converted into concrete geometric sizes or spatial positions and directly visualized in the 3D scene. Models must infer these conditions through observation.
- **Auxiliary Conditions and Final Question**: These remain in textual form due to their diverse and complex nature, or to specify the ultimate goal of the problem.

This approach contrasts with methods that embed conditions into 2D diagrams by grounding the problems in realistic 3D environments with precise geometric properties, demanding genuine spatial perception and problem-solving.

#### 3.3. Automatic Scene Creation Engine

To overcome the challenges of manually creating a large-scale dataset, the authors developed an Automatic Scene Creation Engine, a multi-agent system leveraging Large Language Models (LLMs). The workflow includes:
1. **Dimension Solving**: A math-specialist LLM agent (Qwen-Math) parses the original problem to identify the main geometric entity and solve for all necessary conditions to define its dimensions. These dimensions are then scaled to fit within a unified camera configuration (0.5 to 2 meters).
2. **Scene Generation and Rendering**: A code-specialist LLM agent (Qwen-LM) generates bpy scripts for Blender to construct the 3D scene, incorporating the computed dimensions and placing colored vertices. The scene is then rendered into 8-second videos, recorded at 6 frames per second (fps) with a resolution of 1200x900 pixels. A camera orbits the main object along a circular path (8 meters radius, 30 degrees downward tilt), with backgrounds sourced from 3D-Front.
3. **Problem Text Refinement**: An LLM agent (Qwen-LM) refines the problem descriptions. It removes key geometric conditions pertaining to the main entity from the text, ensuring that VLMs must rely on spatial comprehension. It also replaces letter-based vertex indices with color-based indices.
4. **Answer Conversion and Scaling**: Another LLM agent converts symbolic mathematical answers into numerical values. These values are adjusted for geometric scaling: linearly for lengths, quadratically for areas, and cubically for volumes.

This engine processed 9,078 problems, with manual filtering for conversion errors, demonstrating scalability for data generation.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

**Evaluation Metrics**: Performance is assessed using the relative error `|ŷ - y| / y * 100%`, where `ŷ` is the predicted numerical answer and `y` is the ground truth. Errors are categorized into seven intervals (0–20%, 20–40%, 40–80%, 80–100%, 100–200%, and >200%) to analyze the distribution of predictions.
**Baselines**: A range of state-of-the-art VLMs were evaluated, including models from OpenAI (OpenAI-o3, GPT-4o, GPT-4v), Qwen (Qwen2.5-VL variants, QVQ-72B, Qwen-VL-Max), Doubao (Doubao-1.6-vision, Doubao-1.5-vision), InternVL (InternVL2.5-8B, 26B), LLaVA-Next-Video (LLaVA-NV-7B, 34B), and SpaceQwen-3B. Additionally, Doubao-1.6 (Textual Rep.), an LLM provided with full textual mathematical conditions, served as a control to differentiate between structural reasoning and spatial perception capabilities. Videos for inference were downsampled to 8 or 16 frames as image sequences.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted on SIRI-Bench revealed several findings regarding the structural spatial intelligence of Vision-Language Models (VLMs).

#### 4.1. Overall Performance

All evaluated state-of-the-art VLMs exhibited poor performance on SIRI-Bench. The best-performing VLM, Doubao-1.6-Vision, achieved only 31% of its predictions within a 0–40% error margin. Furthermore, over 33% of its predictions had errors exceeding 100% relative to the ground truth. No consistent correlation was observed between model performance and parameter size; for instance, while Qwen2.5-VL showed improvement from 3B to 7B parameters, InternVL2.5’s performance decreased when scaling from 7B to 26B. SpaceQwen-3B, a smaller model fine-tuned for spatial perception, showed competitive performance relative to larger models (37% of predictions under 60% error) but its accuracy remained insufficient, suggesting its fine-tuning focused on intuitive perception rather than complex structural reasoning. This consistent failure across models indicates limitations of current VLMs in structural spatial reasoning.

#### 4.2. Ablation on Input Representation

A comparison between models receiving textual input (full mathematical conditions provided as text) and their VLM counterparts receiving video-text input (3D spatial representation) revealed a notable disparity. Text-input models consistently and substantially outperformed video-input models. For example, the proportion of predictions within a 20% error margin nearly doubled for models provided with textual input (e.g., Doubao-1.6-Textual-Rep. achieved 89.2% within 20% error, compared to Doubao-1.6-Vision’s 18.4%). This finding suggests that while state-of-the-art LLMs can manage complex reasoning when explicit conditions are provided linguistically, current VLMs struggle to extract sufficient, accurate spatial information from video inputs to perform comparable reasoning. This outcome validates the design of SIRI-Bench in challenging models' spatial perception capabilities.

#### 4.3. Ablation on Deep-Thinking

An ablation study on "Deep-Thinking"—an explicit deep reasoning phase before generating the final answer—indicated its role in spatially grounded reasoning. When using 3D spatial representation as input, disabling Deep-Thinking for Doubao-1.6 reduced performance, with predictions within a 40% error margin decreasing from 31% to 25%. In contrast, Deep-Thinking had minimal impact when inputs were textual, suggesting that direct answering may be sufficient for most problems when conditions are explicitly stated. These results imply that explicit reasoning steps are more critical for problems requiring inference from visual spatial cues.

#### 4.4. Error Distribution by Problem Type

Analysis of error distribution across five problem types (angle, ratio, length, area, volume) showed varied performance. All methods performed poorly on volume-related problems, with fewer than 20% of predictions achieving an error below 40%. This is hypothesized to be due to the cubic amplification of spatial estimation errors in volume calculations. Doubao-1.6-Vision demonstrated the best overall performance, notably excelling in angle and ratio problems, where its success rate improved by 30-40% over the second-best model. This suggests Doubao-1.6's relative strength in relational geometric reasoning, as angle and ratio problems primarily involve spatial relations rather than absolute dimensions.

#### 4.5. Full Response Analysis

A detailed analysis of VLMs’ full reasoning responses identified three common types of mistakes: mathematical calculation errors, erroneous assumptions, and geometric type misjudgments. The occurrence of any of these mistakes consistently correlated with higher prediction errors. Erroneous assumptions, in particular, significantly increased the median prediction error (e.g., for Qwen-VL-Max, from 0.56 without to 0.90 with such assumptions). Furthermore, VLMs frequently misidentified the geometric type of the object; GPT-4o, for instance, made such mistakes in approximately 79% of problems. These findings indicate that failures in recognizing geometric types and a tendency to introduce unsupported assumptions are substantial factors limiting the accuracy of VLMs’ reasoning.

#### 4.6. Comparison with Human Performance

A comparison with human participants on a subset of SIRI-Bench samples demonstrated a significant gap between current VLMs and human capabilities. Human participants successfully solved over 30% of problems with errors within 20% and about 70% within a 60% error margin. None of the evaluated VLMs achieved this level of accuracy. While some human errors exceeding 100% were observed, likely due to cumulative deviations in distance estimation, the overall results indicated that VLMs consistently fall short of human performance in structural spatial reasoning.

#### 4.7. Case Study

A case study with GPT-4o illustrated its difficulties. When tasked with finding the area of a cross-section of a cube, GPT-4o oversimplified the problem by assuming a triangular cross-section instead of a rectangle. It also made unjustified assumptions about the triangle being right-angled and the cube having a unit-length side, without effectively extracting these cues from the video. This indicates that GPT-4o struggled to interpret geometric constraints from implicit visual cues and tended to proceed with incomplete reasoning, highlighting a limitation in its problem-solving approach within the visual domain.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of SIRI-Bench and the accompanying research carry several significances and potential impacts on the field of Vision-Language Models (VLMs) and artificial intelligence.

First, SIRI-Bench establishes a novel framework for evaluating VLMs’ structural spatial intelligence. By presenting complex reasoning tasks grounded in realistic 3D scenes delivered through video, it moves beyond abstract textual or 2D diagram-based evaluations. This approach provides a more rigorous and ecologically valid assessment of a VLM’s ability to process and reason about implicit spatial information, which is crucial for real-world applications. The benchmark highlights that current models, despite their advancements, struggle significantly when spatial information must be inferred from dynamic visual inputs, rather than being explicitly stated in text.

Second, the development of the Automatic Scene Creation Engine represents a methodological contribution. This engine, by leveraging collaborative LLM agents, automates the translation of abstract mathematical problems into faithful 3D scenes and their rendering into video. This capability substantially reduces the cost and labor associated with large-scale data generation for spatially-grounded tasks. Its flexibility in customizing various scene parameters—such as object scales, backgrounds, lighting, and camera configurations—enables the creation of diverse evaluation settings for future research and dataset expansion. This could foster the development of even larger and more varied benchmarks, accelerating progress in the field.

Third, the experimental results provide a clear diagnosis of current VLM limitations. The findings indicate that state-of-the-art VLMs struggle to extract critical spatial information—including object types, geometric dimensions, and spatial relations—from visual inputs when solving complex reasoning tasks. The substantial performance gap between VLMs and their text-only counterparts (which received explicit mathematical conditions) underscores an over-reliance on textual cues and an underdeveloped capacity for visual spatial perception and inference. This diagnostic insight points to fundamental challenges that need to be addressed in VLM architectures and training methodologies.

Finally, this work's significance lies in its potential to redirect research efforts towards building VLMs with more robust structural spatial intelligence. By demonstrating the inadequacy of current models in this critical area, the research motivates the development of new techniques for integrating visual perception with complex reasoning. This includes enhancing models' abilities in geometric type recognition, preventing erroneous assumptions based on incomplete visual cues, and improving mathematical calculation accuracy when dealing with visually derived dimensions. Advancing VLMs in spatially-grounded reasoning is essential for applications requiring deep understanding and interaction with the physical world, such as robotics, autonomous navigation, and augmented reality, where models must interpret and act upon dynamic spatial information.
