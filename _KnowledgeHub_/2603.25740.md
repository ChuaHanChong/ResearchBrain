# Drive My Way: Preference Alignment of Vision-Language-Action Model for Personalized Driving

> [!summary] Summary
> Drive My Way (DMW) proposes a Vision-Language-Action (VLA) framework for personalized autonomous driving, learning long-term driver preferences from user profiles and adapting to real-time natural language instructions. The system demonstrated superior alignment with both consistent driver habits and immediate style commands in complex driving scenarios, achieving higher user ratings and performance compared to existing baselines.

## Problem

- Current autonomous driving systems optimize for generalized objectives, failing to account for individual, context-dependent human driving behaviors and preferences.
- Existing personalization approaches are limited to rigid preset driving modes or manual adjustments, and struggle to interpret real-time, natural language instructions from users.
- Many language-driven personalization methods neglect a driver's long-term habits and cumulative experiences, while also lacking robust evaluation in complex, dynamic driving scenarios.

## Method

- Develops a Vision-Language-Action (VLA) framework integrating visual observations, user profiles for long-term habits, and natural language instructions for real-time adaptation.
- Constructed a ==Personalized Driving Dataset== (PDD) with 30 drivers across diverse scenarios to capture individual driving styles and behaviors.
- Employs reinforcement fine-tuning with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) for policy specialization and a style-aware reward adaptation mechanism to align with short-term instructions.

## Results

- DMW significantly improved driving performance and efficiency, achieving an **18.77%** efficiency gain for aggressive instructions compared to **3.70%** for SimLingo, while also demonstrating distinct and appropriate behavioral shifts for conservative instructions.
- The framework achieved higher Alignment Scores and average user ratings for both in-distribution and out-of-distribution drivers, indicating superior capture and generalization of individual long-term driving preferences.
- DMW consistently received the highest user ratings across various styles and scenarios, confirming its ability to adapt to short-term natural language instructions and provide a more human-aligned driving experience compared to baselines.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating a user embedding learned from long-term profiles with real-time natural language instructions enables a VLA model to achieve a comprehensive and adaptive personalized driving policy.
> - Dynamically adjusting reward components and thresholds based on driving instructions and context is crucial for both reliable and personalized driving behaviors, allowing for flexible trade-offs between safety, efficiency, and comfort.
> - Contrastive learning with carefully designed encoders can effectively learn user embeddings that capture distinct long-term driving habits, which can then generalize to unseen drivers and scenarios.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

End-to-end autonomous driving systems have demonstrated capabilities in mapping sensor inputs to driving actions, leveraging unified architectures and foundation models. However, these systems commonly optimize for generalized objectives such as safety and efficiency, often overlooking the individual and context-dependent nature of human driving behaviors. Drivers exhibit unique habits in acceleration, braking, and overtaking, which vary based on situations, purposes, or emotional states. Current systems typically offer a limited number of preset driving modes (e.g., "sport," "comfort," "eco") or rely on manual parameter adjustments, which are insufficient for capturing nuanced and evolving user preferences or interpreting natural language instructions like "I’m tired" or "I’m late for work."

Existing research in personalized autonomous driving largely falls into two categories: data-driven methods and language-driven approaches. Data-driven methods, such as those employing behavior cloning or inverse reinforcement learning, learn style-conditioned policies from human demonstrations. While these methods can mimic human-like styles, they often require extensive, diverse datasets and may not scale efficiently to a growing number of users with heterogeneous preferences. Some approaches mitigate this through multi-objective reinforcement learning for runtime adjustment of preference weights, but they typically lack the ability to handle real-time human interaction via natural language.

Language-driven approaches utilize large language models (LLMs) to interpret passenger commands for instruction-based personalization. While promising in simplified scenarios, these methods often operate in low-interaction settings and have not been systematically evaluated in complex, dynamic driving contexts such as merging or emergency braking, where trade-offs among safety, comfort, and efficiency are critical and scene-adaptive. Furthermore, existing LLM or vision-language model (VLM) based personalization methods often do not account for a driver's long-term habits or cumulative experiences, which are integral to a comprehensive understanding of personalized driving.

Drive My Way (DMW) aims to bridge these gaps by developing a Vision-Language-Action (VLA) framework that integrates visual observations, contextual user profiles for long-term habits, and real-time natural language instructions for short-term adaptation. This approach seeks to provide a more holistic and adaptive personalization compared to existing methods, addressing limitations in both capturing implicit, long-term preferences and enabling explicit, real-time instruction-based adjustments in complex driving environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a personalized end-to-end autonomous driving framework that can align with a user's long-term driving habits while simultaneously adapting to real-time, natural language-based user instructions. The authors aim to enable autonomous vehicles to provide a more human-centered driving experience, enhancing user trust, comfort, and satisfaction.

The motivation for this work stems from several limitations identified in current autonomous driving systems:
1. **Lack of Personalization:** Existing end-to-end autonomous driving systems primarily optimize for generic objectives like safety and efficiency. They often fail to account for the inherent individuality of human driving behavior, where individuals exhibit distinct styles in maneuvers such as accelerating, braking, merging, or overtaking. These styles are shaped by long-term habits, personal preferences, and situational intentions.
2. **Inflexible Driving Modes:** Current systems that attempt personalization typically offer only a few predefined driving modes (e.g., "sport," "comfort," "eco") or require manual parameter adjustments. Such rigid presets are insufficient to capture the subtle, evolving, and diverse preferences of individual passengers.
3. **Inability to Interpret Natural Language Intent:** A significant gap exists in the ability of autonomous driving systems to interpret intuitive, real-time natural language instructions from users (e.g., "I'm tired," "I'm in a rush," "drive carefully"). Bridging this gap is crucial for enabling a more natural and responsive human-vehicle interaction.
4. **Neglect of Long-term Driving Habits:** Many personalized driving approaches, particularly language-driven ones, focus on immediate instructions but often overlook a driver's cumulative experiences and consistent, long-term driving habits. These habits form a crucial context for truly personalized behavior.
5. **Limited Evaluation in Complex Scenarios:** While some language-based methods show promise, their evaluation has largely been confined to simple, low-interaction settings. The challenge of integrating personalized adaptation in complex, dynamic scenarios (e.g., merging, overtaking, emergency braking) where trade-offs among safety, comfort, and efficiency are critical, remains largely unexplored.

To address these motivations, the DMW framework proposes to learn a user embedding that encapsulates long-term driving habits from a newly collected personalized driving dataset. This embedding conditions the driving policy during planning. Concurrently, natural language instructions provide real-time, short-term guidance, allowing the system to adapt to immediate user intents within the context of their established preferences.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The DMW framework proposes a Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model designed to integrate visual observations, contextual user profiles, and real-time natural language instructions to generate adaptive, personalized driving actions.

#### 3.1. Personalized Driving Dataset (PDD)

To facilitate the learning and evaluation of personalized driving policies, the authors constructed a new Personalized Driving Dataset (PDD).
- **Participants:** Thirty drivers with diverse backgrounds and driving experience levels were recruited.
- **Profile Data:** Each participant completed a structured questionnaire covering demographic information, driving history, and typical driving purposes. This collected data forms a semantic context for long-term driving habits.
- **Scenarios:** Participants performed a standardized set of twenty driving scenarios in the CARLA simulator, categorized into four types: overtaking, merging into traffic, handling intersections, and navigating pedestrian crossings or vehicle cut-ins.
- **Control Interface:** Drivers operated the vehicle using a Logitech G-series steering wheel and pedal setup for realistic control inputs.
- **Recorded Data:** PDD records ego-vehicle motion states, scene perception (surrounding vehicles, pedestrians, cyclists, hazards), traffic context (signal states, stop signs, route geometry, speed limits). An expert target speed, derived from PDM-Lite, was recorded to describe behavioral style deviations.
- **Auxiliary Information:** Data was recorded at 5 Hz, including RGB camera images (1024x512, 110° FoV), ego-vehicle kinematics (acceleration, velocity, speed, pose, control commands, speed limit), surrounding agent details (leading vehicle info, nearby dynamic agents, traffic lights, stop signs), expert supervision (throttle, brake, steer, target speed from PDM-Lite), and route geometry (two upcoming waypoints).

#### 3.2. Problem Formulation

Personalized driving is formulated as a Markov Decision Process (MDP). The policy aims to maximize expected cumulative discounted reward by learning to map observations to continuous actions (throttle, brake, steering). The observation includes front-view images, ego-vehicle state, language instruction, navigation target (route waypoints), and the active driver's profile. Personalization is achieved by inferring long-term behavioral tendencies from historical data and profiles, and adapting decisions based on a user embedding and current instructions.

#### 3.3. VLA Backbone

The SimLingo model is used as the VLA backbone. It is based on InternVL2-1B, which combines an InternViT-300M-448px vision encoder with a Qwen2-0.5B language model. This backbone is trained to predict temporal and geometric waypoints, which are then converted into target speed and steering commands, supervised by privileged expert demonstrations from PDM-Lite and auxiliary reasoning signals.

#### 3.4. Personalization via Reinforcement Fine-tuning

Imitation learning on the VLA backbone alone tends to produce generalized behaviors. To achieve user-specific driving styles, DMW employs Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) for fine-tuning. GRPO promotes policy specialization by sampling a group of outputs and computing normalized group advantages.
- **Residual Decoder:** A residual decoder is introduced to generate a rich set of candidate actions for GRPO. Learnable residual query tokens are integrated into the language model alongside vision, language, navigation, user embedding, and motion query tokens.
- **Action Generation:** The residual features are decoded by a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) into two discrete residual adjustments: speed change and steering change. The final action `a_t` is the sum of a base action `a_base_t` (derived from predicted waypoints via a PID controller) and the personalized residual `a_Δ_t`. This allows the policy to maintain safe planning while expressively adapting to different drivers and situational intentions.

#### 3.5. Long-term Preference Learning and Alignment

##### 3.5.1. User Embedding Learning

To model and align VLA with personalized driving styles, a long-term preference encoder `f_p(·)` learns user embeddings `z_p^m` from a driver's profile `P^m`. This encoder uses a DeBERTaV3 text processor followed by a projection head. A route processor `f_b(·)`, a temporal encoder with multi-head self-attention, processes a past trajectory window `ξ_t^m` (images, ego-states, actions) to output a behavior embedding `z_b,t^m`.
- **Contrastive Learning:** InfoNCE is used to align these embeddings. It encourages `z_p^m` and `z_b,t^m` from the same driver to be similar while pushing embeddings from different drivers apart.

##### 3.5.2. Preference Alignment

After learning user embeddings, the VLA policy is conditioned on `z_p^m` and further adapted through reinforcement fine-tuning. This user embedding serves as a latent personalization prior.
- **Trajectory Augmentation:** To enhance behavioral diversity and align the policy with individual preferences, trajectories are augmented for each target driver `m`. This involves conditioning the user embedding on `m`'s own data and data from another driver `u` (chosen as the least similar to `m` based on user embedding).
- **Reward Formulation:** The reward `R(s_t^m, a_t)` is formulated based on behavioral similarity. When conditioned on `P^m`, it measures similarity between the model's action `a_t` and `m`'s actual action `a_t^m`. When conditioned on `P^u`, it measures similarity between `a_t` and an augmented action `~a_t^m` (scaled version of `a_t^m` based on route-level action statistics of `m` and `u`).

#### 3.6. Human-Vehicle Personalized Interaction

##### 3.6.1. Style Instruction

A style instruction set was constructed for twenty driving scenarios, incorporating style intent and scene-specific semantics. Each scenario includes nine stylistic instructions covering three driving styles (Conservative, Neutral, Aggressive) expressed at three levels of directness. This allows the model to interpret both explicit commands and subtle linguistic cues.

##### 3.6.2. Style-Aware Reward Adaptation

A reward function integrates driving performance metrics with instruction-dependent style alignment: `R(s_t, a_t) = w_s · R_safety + w_e · R_efficiency + w_c · R_comfort`.
- **Reward Components:**
 - `R_safety`: Penalizes risky interactions based on Time-to-Collision (TTC) with an instruction-dependent safety threshold `β_safety`.
 - `R_efficiency`: Encourages maintaining a speed consistent with the desired style, based on actual speed `v_t` and instruction-dependent preferred speed `v_pref`.
 - `R_comfort`: Evaluates action smoothness based on instruction-dependent lateral `β_lat` and longitudinal `β_long` comfort thresholds.
- **Adaptive Weighting:** Reward parameters (`w_s`, `w_e`, `w_c`, `β_safety`, `v_pref`, `β_lat`, `β_long`) are dynamically mapped based on the instruction and driving context. Aggressive instructions, for instance, lead to a higher `w_e` and `v_pref`.
- **Parameter Generation:** This mapping is achieved through a multi-stage approach:
 1. Advanced LLMs (e.g., GPT-5) infer initial reward weights and thresholds from the scenario description and instruction, initialized within predefined bounds for each style.
 2. Expert review refines these parameters to ensure consistency with command semantics and safe driving behaviors.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments evaluated DMW's effectiveness in user alignment, instruction adaptation, and overall driving performance using the Bench2Drive benchmark and user studies.

#### 4.1. Adaptation to Style Instruction

Table 1 shows closed-loop evaluation results on Bench2Drive.
- **Performance Improvement:** DMW-Vanilla (fixed reward weights) improved Driving Score (DS) and Success Rate (SR) over baselines (SimLingo, StyleDrive) due to safety rewards.
- **Style-Aware Adaptation:** DMW (with style-aware reward weights) significantly outperformed SimLingo in style adaptation. For aggressive instructions, DMW yielded an 18.77% efficiency gain compared to 3.70% for SimLingo and 6.00% for StyleDrive. For conservative instructions, DMW achieved notable gains in DS and SR while maintaining efficiency.
- **Behavioral Diversity:** DMW showed consistent reduction in acceleration and lane-change counts under conservative instructions, validating distinct stylistic behaviors. StyleDrive induced smaller metric shifts and had a lower DS, suggesting the benefit of DMW's style-sensitive adaptation.
- **Qualitative Observations (Figure 6, Figure 7):** DMW demonstrated distinct reactions to aggressive and conservative instructions in safety-critical scenarios. For a road blockage, aggressive instructions led to immediate overtaking (higher speed), while conservative instructions resulted in deceleration and waiting. In hard-braking scenarios, aggressive instructions maintained tighter following distances, whereas conservative ones prompted slower speeds and more yielding. Similar divergences were observed in control loss, oncoming vehicle intrusion, parked vehicle overtaking, and unsignalized left turn scenarios, driven by adaptive reward weights and thresholds (e.g., TTC threshold).

#### 4.2. Long-term Preference Alignment

- **Driver-Specific Behavior (Table 2, Table 5):** The policy conditioned on individual driver profiles exhibited consistent motion statistics across different scenarios. Drivers (e.g., D1, D4) with higher efficiency and acceleration reflected an aggressive preference, while others (e.g., D2, D3) showed lower acceleration and larger headways, indicating a safety/comfort preference. These distinctions were maintained across various scenarios, suggesting alignment with persistent behavioral traits.
- **Alignment Score and User Ratings (Table 3):** DMW achieved higher Alignment Scores (AS) and average user ratings compared to MORL-PD, both for in-distribution (ID) and out-of-distribution (OOD) drivers. This indicates that more DMW roll-outs were correctly classified into the target driver's cluster, and evaluators consistently recognized behaviors reflecting the corresponding driver's habits. MORL-PD showed lower alignment, especially on unseen drivers, suggesting DMW's user embedding better captures semantic profile context for stronger generalization.
- **Combined Adaptation:** Table 2 also demonstrated that while long-term preferences defined a base style, language style instructions could provide additional, short-term adaptation, shifting driving behavior towards immediate preferences. User studies (Table 6) further confirmed that DMW consistently achieved the highest ratings across all styles and scenarios, indicating superior alignment with short-term style instructions compared to SimLingo and StyleDrive.

#### 4.3. Ablation Study

- **Adaptive Average Pooling (AAP) (Table 4):** Replacing the masked Adaptive Average Pooling (AAP) module in the preference encoder with an unmasked global mean reduced the expressiveness of user embeddings. DMW with AAP yielded higher diversity in motion statistics across user profiles and achieved higher AS, indicating more consistent reproduction of behavioral traits. This improvement is attributed to AAP's ability to preserve semantically important embeddings.
- **Style-aware Reward Adaptation (Table 1):** When reward components were fixed (DMW-Vanilla), DS and SR were high, but sensitivity to style was reduced, as aggressive and conservative instructions resulted in similar driving. In contrast, DMW with adaptive reward weights and thresholds produced clear behavioral distinctions (e.g., higher speeds and shorter headways for aggressive, smoother control and larger safety margins for conservative), demonstrating the necessity of dynamic weighting for both reliable and personalized driving behaviors.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The DMW framework represents a step towards personalized, human-centered autonomous driving. By integrating long-term driver preferences derived from user profiles with real-time natural language instructions, the system offers a more nuanced and adaptive driving experience compared to existing methods. The creation of the Personalized Driving Dataset (PDD) provides a valuable resource for future research in developing and evaluating human-centered driving models.

The demonstrated ability of DMW to align with distinct long-term driving habits and adapt to various short-term style commands in complex scenarios has several potential impacts:
- **Enhanced User Trust and Comfort:** A vehicle that drives according to an individual's preferences and responds to natural language commands can significantly increase user comfort and trust, addressing a key barrier to wider adoption of autonomous vehicles.
- **Improved Human-Vehicle Interaction:** DMW's capacity to interpret subtle linguistic cues and adapt its behavior accordingly enables more intuitive and flexible interaction, moving beyond rigid preset modes.
- **Foundational for Adaptive Systems:** The framework's modular design, incorporating user embeddings and adaptive reward mechanisms, provides a foundation for developing more sophisticated personalized AI agents in various robotic applications.

While the current validation is within the CARLA simulator, the demonstrated capabilities suggest that DMW could contribute to autonomous vehicles that behave as preferred by their users, thereby improving the overall driving experience. Future work aims to address the sim-to-real gap and expand the dataset to encompass a broader diversity of driving behaviors, which could further enhance the robustness and generalizability of personalized preference alignment.
