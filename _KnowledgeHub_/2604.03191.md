# The Compression Gap: Why Discrete Tokenization Limits Vision-Language-Action Model Scaling

> [!summary] Summary
> A study from Shibattic Inc. reveals that fixed-capacity discrete action representations create an information bottleneck, termed the 'Compression Gap,' which prevents advanced vision encoders from fully improving downstream robotic manipulation performance, unlike continuous action methods.

## Problem

- Vision encoder upgrades consistently improve performance in vision-language tasks, but their impact on visuomotor policy performance in robotics appears less straightforward.
- The precise mechanisms by which visual information is leveraged for action generation, particularly concerning different action representations (continuous vs. discrete), remain underexplored.
- A fundamental question exists regarding whether improvements in upstream vision encoders universally translate to better downstream task performance, irrespective of the action representation used.

## Method

- The research introduced the ==Compression Gap hypothesis==, explaining that scaling behavior and performance improvements in visuomotor pipelines are dictated by the location of the tightest ==information bottleneck==.
- An ==information-theoretic framework==, based on the ==data processing inequality==, was formalized to explain how mutual information between observation and action is bounded by the minimum flow through any stage.
- Experiments compared ==discrete tokenization== (OAT) and ==continuous diffusion== (Diffusion Policy) action representations on the LIBERO benchmark, varying vision encoder quality and discrete codebook capacity.

## Results

- Diffusion Policy showed substantial performance increases (up to **26.0** percentage points) when upgrading the vision encoder from ResNet-18 to SigLIP, while OAT's gains were significantly attenuated (maximum **10.4** percentage points).
- Diffusion Policy performance improved monotonically with higher-quality encoders (e.g., **36.4%** for ResNet-18 to **63.8%** for DINOv2), whereas OAT's performance did not show a systematic trend.
- Increasing OAT's codebook capacity from 1000 to 1920 led to an asymmetric response, changing the encoder sensitivity from **+3.6%** to **+15.2%** (SigLIP vs. ResNet-18), providing causal evidence for the codebook as the bottleneck.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Fixed-capacity discrete action representations introduce a hard upper bound on information flow, becoming the binding bottleneck and preventing improvements from advanced vision encoders from propagating.
> - Continuous action representations allow vision encoder quality to directly translate to performance gains, as the vision encoder typically remains the primary information bottleneck.
> - A 'crossover effect' exists where discrete methods can outperform continuous ones with weak vision encoders, but continuous methods excel with stronger encoders, highlighting a dependency on upstream visual information quality.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work by Shiba addresses a fundamental question within the field of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models and robotic manipulation: how do improvements in upstream vision encoders translate to downstream task performance, and what factors might limit this translation?

Current research in vision-language modeling broadly supports the premise that scaling up vision encoders, such as by moving from simpler architectures like ResNet-18 to richer ones like SigLIP, consistently leads to improved understanding and generation capabilities. This has led to an expectation that a similar principle should apply to robotic manipulation policies. However, the exact mechanisms by which visual information is leveraged for action generation, particularly concerning different action representations, remain an area of active investigation.

The landscape of action representations for visuomotor policies is diverse, encompassing both continuous and discrete approaches. Continuous methods, like Diffusion Policy (Chi et al., 2025) and flow matching techniques (Black et al., 2024), model actions as continuous variables. Discrete methods, on the other hand, quantize actions into tokens. Examples include per-dimension binning (Brohan et al., 2022; Zitkovich et al., 2023), frequency-domain compression (FAST; Pertsch et al., 2025), learned latent quantization (VQ-BeT; Lee et al., 2024; QueST; Mete et al., 2024), and ordered action tokenization (OAT; Liu et al., 2026). While these works often compare the task performance of various representations, the systematic study of their *sensitivity to upstream vision encoder quality* has been less explored.

This paper integrates concepts from information theory, particularly the data processing inequality (Cover and Thomas, 2006), to explain performance dynamics in visuomotor pipelines. It also draws parallels with observations from large-scale multimodal pretraining, where studies (e.g., Tong et al., 2026) have identified asymmetries in scaling behavior—for instance, VAE-based representations saturating while semantic encoders continue to improve with increased model capacity. The current research specifically investigates if a similar structural asymmetry, termed the "Compression Gap," manifests at the policy level in robotics, depending on the action representation. By focusing on the location of information bottlenecks, this work seeks to provide a theoretical and empirical framework for understanding and optimizing scaling strategies in Physical AI.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate whether the expectation that upgrading a vision encoder improves downstream robotic manipulation performance holds universally, regardless of the chosen action representation. The authors hypothesize that this expectation is generally false, and that the nature of the action representation significantly influences how encoder improvements propagate.

The core motivation stems from an observed discrepancy: while vision encoder upgrades consistently enhance performance in vision-language tasks, their impact on visuomotor policy performance appears less straightforward. The paper aims to explain this discrepancy through an information-theoretic principle termed the "Compression Gap." This principle posits that in any visuomotor pipeline, scaling behavior and performance improvements are dictated by the location of the tightest information bottleneck.

Specifically, the key objectives are:
1. **To demonstrate the "Compression Gap":** Show empirically that when actions are represented continuously (e.g., using Diffusion Policy), the vision encoder acts as the binding information bottleneck. Consequently, upgrading the encoder directly widens this bottleneck, leading to substantial improvements in manipulation performance. Conversely, when actions are discretized via a fixed-capacity codebook (e.g., using Ordered Action Tokenization, OAT), the codebook itself becomes the binding bottleneck. In this scenario, improvements to the vision encoder cannot propagate past the codebook's fixed capacity, resulting in attenuated or blocked performance gains.
2. **To formalize the mechanism:** Provide an information-theoretic explanation based on the data processing inequality, detailing how the mutual information between observation and action is bounded by the minimum information flow through any stage of the pipeline. This framework explains why a fixed-capacity discrete quantization stage can cap the total information throughput, rendering upstream encoder improvements ineffective beyond that cap.
3. **To provide empirical validation:** Test the Compression Gap hypothesis through a series of controlled experiments on the LIBERO benchmark:
 - **Vision Encoder Sensitivity:** Quantify the performance difference between continuous and discrete action representations when exposed to identical upgrades in vision encoder quality (e.g., ResNet-18 to SigLIP).
 - **Encoder Quality Gradient:** Extend the binary comparison to multiple encoders of varying quality to observe if the sensitivity or insensitivity of each representation holds monotonically.
 - **Codebook Size Variation:** Investigate causally whether relaxing the capacity of the discrete codebook in OAT restores sensitivity to vision encoder quality, thereby confirming the codebook as the binding bottleneck.

Ultimately, the research is motivated by the need to understand the fundamental limitations and optimal scaling strategies for Physical AI. The authors argue that merely increasing model or data size may not be sufficient for scalable AI in robotics; instead, identifying and addressing specific information bottlenecks within the pipeline is critical.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a controlled experimental design to test the Compression Gap hypothesis, comparing two representative action representation families: discrete tokenization (OAT) and continuous diffusion (Diffusion Policy). The experiments are conducted on the LIBERO benchmark.

**Action Representations:**
- **Discrete: Ordered Action Tokenization (OAT):** This is a two-stage framework. First, a learned tokenizer `T` compresses a continuous action chunk (`a_1:Ha`) into a sequence of discrete tokens (`T_1:Hl`, `T_i ∈ V`). This is achieved by aggregating action sequences into latent vectors using a transformer encoder with learnable register tokens, followed by Finite Scalar Quantization (FSQ) with default levels ``, yielding a codebook size `|V| = 1000`. The default latent token horizon `Hl` is 8, leading to an approximate information capacity of 80 bits per action chunk. Second, an autoregressive policy generates these tokens sequentially. The FSQ bottleneck is identified as the critical feature, limiting information flow regardless of upstream observation richness.
- **Continuous: Diffusion Policy (DP):** This method models the action distribution as a denoising process in continuous space. A learned noise prediction network `εθ` iteratively denoises from Gaussian noise towards a clean action chunk. The entire pipeline operates in continuous space, without any discrete quantization stage, allowing continuous information flow from observations to actions.

**Information-Theoretic Formalism:**
The study formalizes the information pathway `O → Z → A` (Observation → Learned Representation → Action). Using the data processing inequality, it establishes that `I(O; A) ≤ min(I(O; Z), I(Z; A))`.
- **Continuous Pathway (DP):** `O → f_enc → Z → εθ → A`. The binding bottleneck is typically `I(O; Z)` (vision encoder), as `I(Z; A)` is not combinatorially bounded. Upgrading `f_enc` directly widens `I(O; Z)`.
- **Discrete Pathway (OAT):** `O → f_enc → Z → Q → T → T^-1 → A`. The quantization stage `Q` introduces a hard upper bound `I(Z; T) ≤ H_l log2 |V|` (approx. 80 bits for default OAT). This becomes the binding bottleneck, preventing `f_enc` improvements from propagating if `I(Z; T)` is saturated.

**Experimental Variables:**
1. **Action Representation:** OAT (discrete) vs. Diffusion Policy (continuous). The OAT codebase's Diffusion Policy baseline is used for fair comparison.
2. **Vision Encoder:**
 - **Factorial Design:** ResNet-18 (64-dimensional feature, limited) vs. SigLIP (1152-dimensional semantic representation, pretrained on web-scale). Pre-cached features for SigLIP for efficiency.
 - **Encoder Quality Gradient (Supplementary):** Extends the comparison to DINOv2 ViT-L/14 (1024-d, self-supervised) and SigLIP 2 (1152-d, updated V-L pretraining), evaluated at model size M on LIBERO-10.
3. **Model Size:**
 - M (Medium): 4-layer transformer decoder, embedding dimension 256, 4 attention heads.
 - L (Large): 6-layer transformer decoder, embedding dimension 384, 6 attention heads.
 This variable controls for the possibility of policy capacity being a confound.
4. **Codebook Size (Supplementary for Causal Evidence):** For OAT (M size, LIBERO-10), FSQ levels are varied to change `|V|`:
 - `|V|=1000` (levels ``, default, ~80 bits/chunk).
 - `|V|=1920` (levels ``, ~87 bits/chunk).
 - `|V|=4375` (levels ``, ~97 bits/chunk).
 The change in encoder sensitivity (`∆enc = SR(SigLIP) - SR(ResNet)`) is measured.

**Benchmark:**
- LIBERO [Liu et al., 2023] is used, specifically LIBERO-10 (10 tasks, 50 demonstrations per task).
- Robot: Franka Emika Panda with 7-dimensional action space.
- Policies predict `Ha = 32` action chunks, executing the first 16 steps before re-inference.

**Implementation Details:**
- Official OAT codebase.
- Shared policy backbone architecture; identical observation encoder pipeline across conditions.
- ResNet-18 uses default configuration (64-d output). SigLIP, DINOv2, SigLIP 2 use pre-cached features.
- Training: AdamW optimizer (policy LR: `5 × 10^-5`, encoder LR: `1 × 10^-5`), no weight decay, 300 epochs on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU per run.
- Evaluation: Success rate (SR) every 50 epochs via 500 rollouts (50 per task). Peak success rate across training is reported.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides empirical evidence supporting the Compression Gap hypothesis through three lines of experimentation.

**Vision Encoder Sensitivity (Factorial Experiment):**
The results from the factorial experiment (Table 1) reveal a distinct interaction between action representation and vision encoder quality.
- **Diffusion Policy (Continuous Pathway):** Showed substantial performance improvements when upgrading the vision encoder from ResNet-18 to SigLIP. For model size M, the success rate increased by 21.2 percentage points (from 36.4% to 57.6%). For model size L, the increase was 26.0 percentage points (from 44.0% to 70.0%). This demonstrates that the continuous pathway effectively leverages richer visual representations, consistent with the vision encoder being the binding bottleneck.
- **OAT (Discrete Pathway):** Exhibited significantly attenuated gains from the same encoder upgrade. For model size M, the success rate improved by only 3.6 percentage points (from 53.8% to 57.4%). For model size L, the gain was 10.4 percentage points (from 48.0% to 58.4%). These smaller gains, compared to Diffusion Policy, align with the hypothesis that the fixed-capacity codebook in OAT acts as a bottleneck, limiting the propagation of upstream visual information improvements. The inconsistent scaling gain for OAT across encoders with increased model size further supports that policy capacity is not the primary limiting factor, but rather the codebook.

**Encoder Quality Gradient:**
To investigate the continuous nature of this divergence, experiments were conducted with four vision encoders of varying quality (Table 2), fixing model size to M.
- **Diffusion Policy:** Performance tracked encoder quality monotonically: 36.4% (ResNet-18), 57.6% (SigLIP), 62.8% (SigLIP 2), and 63.8% (DINOv2 ViT-L/14). This continuous improvement confirms that for continuous action representations, a better vision encoder directly translates to better task performance.
- **OAT:** Performance did not show a systematic relationship with encoder quality, fluctuating without a clear trend: 53.8% (ResNet-18), 57.4% (SigLIP), 44.2% (SigLIP 2), and 51.0% (DINOv2 ViT-L/14). This insensitivity, even with orders-of-magnitude increase in representational dimensionality, provides continuous evidence that the codebook bottleneck prevents upstream information from being fully utilized.
- A notable **crossover effect** was observed: with the weakest encoder (ResNet-18), OAT outperformed DP by 17.4 percentage points. However, with the strongest encoder (DINOv2), DP surpassed OAT by 12.8 percentage points. This reversal suggests that the relative advantage of discrete vs. continuous representations is contingent on the quality of the upstream visual information.

**Codebook Size Variation:**
To provide causal evidence for the codebook as the binding bottleneck, the capacity of OAT's FSQ codebook was varied (Table 3).
- At the default codebook size (`|V|=1000`), the difference in performance between ResNet-18 and SigLIP for OAT (`∆enc = +3.6%`) was small, indicating masked encoder quality.
- When the codebook capacity was increased to `|V|=1920` (approximately 87 bits/chunk), a notable asymmetry emerged: SigLIP-based OAT performance remained stable at 57.8%, while ResNet-18-based OAT performance sharply dropped to 42.6%. This 15.2 percentage point `∆enc` indicates that relaxing the codebook bottleneck partially exposed the underlying difference in encoder quality. The ResNet-18 performance approached that of Diffusion Policy with ResNet-18 (36.4%), suggesting the codebook's masking effect was reduced.
- At a further increased capacity (`|V|=4375`), ResNet-based OAT recovered to 54.6% and SigLIP-based OAT rose to 58.6%. While this specific result exhibited some non-monotonicity (attributed to potential modelability-capacity trade-offs), the asymmetric response at `|V|=1920` provided causal evidence that codebook capacity directly mediates the relationship between encoder quality and task performance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research identifies the "Compression Gap" as a structural limitation in scaling Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, directly linking it to the location of information bottlenecks within the visuomotor pipeline. This finding has several implications for the design and development of scalable Physical AI systems.

**Impact on Discrete vs. Continuous Action Representations:** The study's central contribution is demonstrating that fixed-capacity discrete action representations fundamentally limit the benefits derived from increasingly powerful vision encoders. This suggests that while discrete tokenization methods like OAT may offer advantages in certain contexts (e.g., integration with large language models), their inherent information bottleneck (e.g., ~80 bits in OAT's default configuration) prevents upstream visual information improvements from fully propagating to downstream task performance. This conclusion extends beyond OAT to other discrete tokenization schemes that impose similar fixed-capacity constraints. Conversely, continuous action representations, such as Diffusion Policy, are shown to fully leverage richer vision encoder outputs, enabling a more direct scaling path where component upgrades translate to end-to-end performance gains.

**Relevance to Multimodal Scaling Laws:** The observed "Compression Gap" parallels findings in multimodal pretraining, where certain representations (e.g., VAE latents) saturate while others (e.g., semantic encoders) continue to scale with increased model capacity. This suggests that the principle of bottleneck identification is a general structural property of complex information-processing systems, extending from large-scale pretraining to robot learning.

**Guiding Future Research and Development:**
- **For Discrete Methods:** The research indicates that future work on discrete action representations should explore mechanisms to overcome the fixed-capacity bottleneck. This could involve adaptive codebooks, hierarchical tokenization, or hybrid architectures that combine discrete reasoning with continuous action decoding to balance the benefits of both approaches.
- **For Vision Encoders in Robotics:** The observation that Diffusion Policy's performance plateaus even with state-of-the-art vision encoders (SigLIP 2, DINOv2), which are primarily designed for semantic understanding or self-supervised representation learning, suggests a need for specialized vision encoders tailored to the unique information demands of physical interaction. These demands include understanding contact geometry, object affordances, and spatial dynamics, which may not be fully captured by existing general-purpose visual representations.
- **Holistic System Design:** More broadly, the information-theoretic framework presented in this paper underscores that scaling Physical AI is not merely about increasing model or data size uniformly. Instead, it requires a systematic approach to identify and eliminate specific information bottlenecks at any lossy compression stage within the entire visuomotor pipeline—be it in observation encoding, action representation, or inter-module communication.

In summary, this work provides a framework for understanding the limitations of current VLA scaling strategies and offers directions for designing more effective and scalable robotic AI systems by focusing on the principled management of information flow.
