# Unsupervised Learning of Efficient Exploration: Pre-training Adaptive Policies via Self-Imposed Goals

> [!summary] Summary
> ULEE, an unsupervised meta-learning algorithm, pre-trains adaptive policies using self-imposed goals and a novel post-adaptation task-difficulty metric. This approach significantly enhances exploration capabilities, improves few-shot adaptation by up to 3x, and provides superior initializations for fine-tuning on diverse downstream tasks and novel environment structures.

## Problem

- Reinforcement learning agents suffer from sample inefficiency and poor generalization when faced with novel tasks.
- Existing unsupervised RL methods for autotelic learning often rely on predefined goal sets or immediate success metrics, limiting their applicability to unknown or out-of-distribution tasks.
- There is a need for 'foundation policies' that can efficiently adapt to broad distributions of downstream tasks, rather than just performing well initially.

## Method

- The **ULEE** (Unsupervised Learning of Efficient Exploration) framework pre-trains an ==unconditioned==, adaptive policy using a black-box meta-learning approach over 'lifetimes' of episodes.
- A novel goal curriculum guides training using a post-adaptation task-difficulty metric, defined as the complement of the policy's success rate after an adaptation budget within a lifetime.
- An adversarial Goal-search Policy is trained to propose challenging yet achievable goals, while a Difficulty Predictor estimates goal difficulties to manage computational costs.

## Results

- ULEE's pre-trained policy explored more than double the percentage of goals compared to random behavior and DIAYN baselines within a 20-episode budget.
- The policy achieved up to a **3x** increase in mean return by the 30th episode in few-shot adaptation, consistently outperforming all baselines in post-adaptation return.
- ULEE pre-training provided a superior initialization for fine-tuning on fixed tasks and for supervised meta-reinforcement learning, outperforming training from scratch and DIAYN for up to 1 billion steps.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Optimizing for efficient multi-episode exploration and adaptation through a meta-learning framework is effective for acquiring transferable skills.
> - Guiding the curriculum via post-adaptation performance, rather than immediate success, is crucial for developing policies robust to novel and challenging tasks.
> - Adversarial goal generation combined with a difficulty predictor allows for maintaining training at the frontier of the agent's capabilities without relying on external rewards.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work is situated within the broader context of developing robust and generalizable reinforcement learning (RL) agents, drawing inspiration from the successes of large-scale unsupervised pre-training in other deep learning domains such as computer vision and natural language processing. The prevailing paradigm in RL, which often involves training agents from scratch for each new task, faces challenges related to sample inefficiency and a lack of generalization. This research aims to address these fundamental challenges by investigating unsupervised RL as a means to acquire "foundation policies"—agents equipped with transferable knowledge.

A promising direction within unsupervised RL involves designing agents that autonomously generate and pursue their own goals, a concept known as autotelic learning. This approach is closely related to automatic curriculum learning, where the learning process is guided by progressively challenging tasks. However, existing methods in this area often rely on predefined goal sets or goal spaces that are closely aligned with evaluation tasks, or they evaluate goal desirability based on immediate performance. This can limit their applicability to scenarios where downstream objectives are unknown, out-of-distribution, or require significant adaptation. The paper positions itself at the intersection of unsupervised RL, meta-learning, and automatic curriculum learning, specifically focusing on how self-imposed goals can be leveraged for pre-training policies that are broadly capable and adaptable to novel, unseen tasks requiring multi-episode interaction and adaptation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an unsupervised pre-training methodology that equips reinforcement learning agents with transferable knowledge, thereby accelerating learning and improving generalization in a broad distribution of downstream tasks. The motivation stems from the limitations of current RL approaches, particularly their sample inefficiency and poor generalization when faced with novel tasks, and the desire to move towards "foundation policies."

The paper highlights several key challenges that motivate its approach:
- **Effective Goal Generation and Learning**: The core problem in autotelic learning is determining how to effectively generate, select, and learn from self-imposed goals. Existing methods often fall short in scenarios where target tasks lie outside the pre-training distribution or are unknown to the agent.
- **Broad Task Distributions**: The research explicitly focuses on broad distributions of downstream tasks, acknowledging that solving every task zero-shot is infeasible. This necessitates policies that can adapt efficiently rather than merely perform well initially.
- **Robustness to Novelty**: A general base policy must be capable of addressing not only novel objectives but also scenarios with limited information, such as partial observability, uncertain dynamics, or unspecified goals. This requires effectiveness across a spectrum of difficulty, from quick performance on easy tasks to sample-efficient adaptation on complex ones over extended timescales.
- **Limitations of Immediate Performance Metrics**: Much prior work evaluates goal desirability based on immediate success without allowing for task-specific adaptation. The authors argue that this misalignment with the intended evaluation setting (where adaptation is crucial for novel tasks) necessitates a new metric.

To address these motivations, the paper sets out to:
1. **Optimize for Efficient Multi-Episode Exploration and Adaptation**: This is achieved within a meta-learning framework, which explicitly trains for efficient learning over sequences of interactions.
2. **Guide Curriculum via Post-Adaptation Performance**: Introduce a novel metric for goal difficulty that relies on the agent's performance *after* an adaptation budget, rather than its immediate success. This encourages training on more challenging tasks and broadens the distribution of achievable goals.
3. **Adversarial Goal Generation**: Develop a strategy that uses an adversarially trained system to propose challenging yet achievable goals, maintaining training at the frontier of the agent's capabilities and avoiding trivial or unsolvable goals.

These objectives collectively aim to bridge the gap between unsupervised skill acquisition and meta-learning, ultimately working towards foundation policies capable of robust and adaptable behavior in open-ended environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, ULEE (Unsupervised Learning of Efficient Exploration), is an unsupervised meta-learning algorithm designed to pre-train an adaptive policy using an automatic curriculum of self-imposed goals. It operates within partially observable Markov decision processes (POMDPs), where pre-training occurs without extrinsic rewards and goals are determined intrinsically.

The ULEE framework consists of three main interacting components: a Pre-trained Policy, a Goal Curriculum mechanism, and a Difficulty Predictor.

**Setting and Notation:**
- Pre-training and evaluation are conducted on distributions of POMDPs, denoted by $\mu_{\text{train}}$ and $\mu_{\text{eval}}$, respectively.
- The agent interacts with environments over discrete time steps. Observations $o_t$ are obtained from underlying states $s_t$.
- During pre-training, rewards are derived from the agent's intrinsic goals, defined by a mapping function $f: S \rightarrow G$ that transforms states into goals.
- The system grants the goal-generation mechanism and intrinsic reward function privileged access to full state information during pre-training.

#### 3.1. Pre-trained Policy ($\pi$)

- **Unconditioned Policy**: Unlike many prior works that learn goal-conditioned policies, ULEE trains an unconditioned policy. This design choice is made to address scenarios where the target goal is unknown, difficult to encode, or out-of-distribution, allowing for direct deployment without needing goal representation at test time.
- **Meta-learning Approach**: To optimize for exploration and adaptation over multiple episodes, a black-box meta-learning approach is adopted. The agent learns to adapt "in-context" by conditioning its actions on its entire interaction history (past observations, actions, and rewards) within a sequence of contiguous episodes, termed a "lifetime."
- **Objective**: The policy $\pi$ is trained to maximize its expected discounted lifetime return across environments and self-imposed goals. The objective function aggregates rewards over $H$ episodes within a lifetime, where rewards $r_t^{(j)}$ are goal-conditioned.

#### 3.2. Goal Curriculum

The goal curriculum dynamically adjusts the distribution of self-imposed goals $p(g|M)$ to maintain intermediate difficulty, preventing the agent from focusing on trivial or unsolvable tasks. This is achieved through three interconnected subsystems:

- **Difficulty Metric**: A central innovation is the definition of goal difficulty $d(g; \pi, M)$ as the complement of $\pi$'s expected success rate over the *last K episodes* of a lifetime. This metric explicitly considers the agent's ability to adapt to a goal, leveraging the initial $H-K$ episodes for exploration and learning, and is a key differentiator from methods based on immediate performance.
- **Goal Proposal (Goal-search Policy $\pi_{\text{gs}}$)**: An adversarial Goal-search Policy ($\pi_{\text{gs}}$) is trained to identify and reach challenging goals. It maximizes a discounted sum of rewards where each reward $r_{\text{gs},t}$ is the predicted difficulty of the candidate goal $f(s_t)$ encountered. $\pi_{\text{gs}}$ runs for a few episodes, collecting a multiset of candidate goals $GC_M$ for each environment $M$.
- **Goal Selection**: From the generated $GC_M$, a goal $g_M$ is sampled uniformly if its predicted difficulty lies within a specified lower bound (LB) and upper bound (UB). If no goals meet this criterion, sampling occurs uniformly from all candidates. This mechanism ensures that the pre-trained policy learns from goals that are challenging but still achievable after adaptation.
- **Difficulty Prediction**: To overcome the computational cost of empirically estimating goal difficulties for every candidate, a Difficulty Predictor network ($\hat{d}_{\phi}$) is introduced. This network estimates $d(g; \pi, M)$ and is trained with a supervised L2 regression loss. It uses a buffer $B_g$ of recent (goal, environment info, empirical difficulty) triplets, where empirical difficulties are obtained from the Pre-trained Policy's actual training lifetimes. The predictor takes the goal state and initial environment state as input, replacing true difficulties in the Goal Proposal and Selection steps.

#### 3.3. Overall Algorithm (ULEE)

The ULEE algorithm proceeds iteratively:
1. Sample a batch of environments from $\mu_{\text{unsup}}$.
2. For each environment, run the Goal-search Policy ($\pi_{\text{gs}}$) to collect candidate goals ($GC_M$).
3. Use the Difficulty Predictor to estimate difficulties for all candidates and select a goal $g_M$ within the intermediate difficulty range.
4. Train the Pre-trained Policy ($\pi$) using these sampled goals, updating its parameters to maximize the meta-RL objective over lifetimes.
5. After $\pi$ completes its lifetime on the batch, compute empirical difficulty estimates for the goals (post-adaptation success rate) and add them to the buffer $B_g$.
6. Train the Difficulty Predictor using data from $B_g$.
7. Train the Goal-search Policy ($\pi_{\text{gs}}$) using rewards based on the Difficulty Predictor's estimates, aiming to find harder goals.

The benchmarks used are XLand-MiniGrid environments, offering procedural generation and partial observability. Two intrinsic goal mappings are investigated: $f_{\text{counts}}$ (matching object counts) and $f_{\text{grid}}$ (matching exact grid configurations).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of ULEE focused on four key research questions regarding exploration, fast adaptation, and fine-tuning capabilities, as well as generalization to novel environment structures, on XLand-MiniGrid benchmarks (4Rooms-Trivial, 4Rooms-Small, 6Rooms-Small). Baselines included PPO, RND, DIAYN, and RL$^2$, with several ULEE ablations.

#### 4.1. Exploration Capabilities (Q1)

ULEE's Pre-trained Policy demonstrated superior exploration abilities compared to random behavior and DIAYN pre-training. Across all benchmarks, ULEE achieved more than double the percentage of goals reached within a budget of 20 episodes. Ablation studies indicated that the adversarial Goal-search Policy and a curriculum guided by post-adaptation performance (rather than immediate success, as in ULEE (SED)) were crucial for these gains, with their benefits increasing with benchmark difficulty. Bounded goal sampling also proved effective when the goal search was random.

#### 4.2. Fast Adaptation (Few-shot) (Q2)

ULEE's Pre-trained Policy exhibited strong gradient-free, few-shot adaptation capabilities over 30-episode lifetimes. It consistently improved performance, achieving up to a 3x increase in mean return by the 30th episode, significantly outperforming all baselines and ablations in post-adaptation return (mean over the last 10 episodes). The performance gains scaled favorably with increased pre-training budgets (up to 5 billion steps), a trend not observed in DIAYN, which showed stagnation or decline. However, the hardest out-of-distribution tasks remained challenging, with no return on 60% of tasks in some benchmarks. The choice of intrinsic goal mapping ($f_{\text{counts}}$ over $f_{\text{grid}}$) during pre-training was also shown to provide an advantageous inductive bias for ULEE.

#### 4.3. Fine-tuning on Fixed Tasks (Q3)

Beyond few-shot scenarios, ULEE pre-training provided a strong initialization for longer fine-tuning processes (up to 1 billion steps) on a fixed set of evaluation tasks. ULEE consistently outperformed training from scratch (PPO, RND) and DIAYN, maintaining higher mean, 40th percentile, and 20th percentile returns. This indicated that the transferable capabilities learned by ULEE offer a sustained advantage even with substantial additional task-specific training, highlighting the inherent difficulty of the 4Rooms-Small and 6Rooms-Small benchmarks where 40% of tasks remained unsolved without ULEE.

#### 4.4. Fine-tuning with Supervised Meta-learning (Q4)

When used as an initialization for supervised meta-reinforcement learning, ULEE's Pre-trained Policy yielded superior performance compared to meta-learning from scratch (RL$^2$). For budgets up to 5 billion steps of meta-learning on the training distribution, ULEE initialization resulted in higher mean, 40th percentile, and 20th percentile returns on evaluation tasks. The benefits of using $f_{\text{counts}}$ were observed early in fine-tuning, though the difference from $f_{\text{grid}}$ diminished with extended training.

#### 4.5. Generalization to New Environment Structures

To assess generalization beyond novel goals and dynamics, the methods were evaluated on classical MiniGrid tasks with different grid layouts and room numbers, after pre-training on 4Rooms-Small. ULEE ($f_{\text{counts}}$) achieved the best results in few-shot evaluations, attaining a non-zero return on all environments. ULEE ($f_{\text{grid}}$) and DIAYN ($f_{\text{grid}}$) also showed substantial improvements over a random policy, demonstrating ULEE's ability to generalize to new environment structures.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research introduces ULEE, an unsupervised meta-learning approach that significantly advances the development of agents with transferable capabilities. By integrating an in-context learner with an adversarial goal-generation strategy guided by a novel post-adaptation task-difficulty metric, ULEE demonstrates a robust method for pre-training policies without reliance on extrinsic rewards.

The primary significance of this work lies in its empirically validated contributions:
- **Novel Difficulty Metric**: The introduction of a post-adaptation task-difficulty metric for guiding automatic goal generation is a key methodological contribution. This approach more accurately reflects the utility of a pre-trained policy for novel, challenging tasks where initial exploration and adaptation are necessary.
- **Effective Unsupervised Pre-training**: ULEE demonstrates that unsupervised pre-training can effectively equip agents with valuable transferable skills, enabling improved exploration, faster adaptation in few-shot settings, and providing strong initializations for downstream fine-tuning processes (both on fixed tasks and in supervised meta-learning scenarios).
- **Enhanced Generalization**: The policy pre-trained with ULEE shows robust generalization to novel objectives, environment dynamics, and even entirely new grid structures, indicating a step towards developing more broadly capable RL agents.
- **Foundation Policies**: This work contributes to the long-term objective of creating "foundation policies" in RL—agents that possess generalized skills applicable across a wide array of tasks and environments without task-specific engineering.

The potential impact of ULEE includes reducing the sample inefficiency inherent in many RL applications and enabling the deployment of agents in open-ended, dynamic environments where external reward signals are sparse, delayed, or unavailable. Future work could build upon ULEE by introducing hierarchical structures into the meta-learned policy to address longer-horizon tasks, or by integrating vision-language models (VLMs) into goal-proposal and reward-specification mechanisms to align pre-training more closely with human-relevant tasks and real-world applicability.
