# Improving Reasoning for Diffusion Language Models via Group Diffusion Policy Optimization

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Georgia Institute of Technology and Morgan Stanley developed Group Diffusion Policy Optimization (GDPO), a reinforcement learning algorithm that uses a Semi-deterministic Monte Carlo scheme to efficiently estimate sequence-level likelihoods for Diffusion Language Models. GDPO consistently enhances reasoning performance across mathematical, planning, and coding benchmarks, achieving these improvements with modest computational resources.

## Problem

- Applying reinforcement learning (RL) to Diffusion Language Models (DLMs) is challenging due to the absence of a simple autoregressive structure, making token-level and sequence-level likelihood estimation intractable.
- Existing RL adaptations for DLMs either rely on computationally efficient but biased token-level likelihood approximations or computationally expensive and high-variance sequence-level estimators (Evidence Lower Bound - ELBO).
- Accurately estimating sequence-level likelihoods for DLMs faces a "variance-cost dilemma," where principled methods are prohibitively costly and unstable due to high variance.

## Method

- Introduces Group Diffusion Policy Optimization (GDPO), an RL algorithm adapting ==Group Relative Policy== Optimization (GRPO) for DLMs by using sequence-level likelihoods instead of token-level approximations.
- Develops a Semi-deterministic Monte Carlo (SDMC) scheme that addresses the "variance-cost dilemma" in ELBO estimation by replacing random time sampling with deterministic numerical integration.
- Decomposes ELBO variance, identifies "Random Time" as the dominant source (96.2%), and demonstrates that SDMC with a small number of quadrature points significantly reduces bias and variance.

## Results

- GDPO consistently surpasses pretrained models and token-level RL baselines like diffu-GRPO across mathematical reasoning, planning, and coding tasks.
- The method achieves significant performance gains, such as an **80.86%** accuracy on Countdown (512 generations) and a **10%** accuracy increase on the MBPP coding benchmark (**50.6%** for 256 generations).
- GDPO demonstrates improved generalization to longer sequences and operates efficiently, allowing training on modest hardware (e.g., 2 H100 GPUs) while delivering superior results.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Variance in the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO) estimator for DLMs is primarily driven by the random sampling of diffusion timesteps (96.2%), rather than the specific token masks.
> - A Semi-deterministic Monte Carlo (SDMC) approach, replacing random time sampling with deterministic integration, dramatically reduces both bias and variance in sequence-level likelihood estimates for DLMs.
> - The quality and design of the likelihood estimator are critical for effective Diffusion Language Model (DLM) fine-tuning, with more accurate ELBO approximations directly leading to greater performance improvements.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of large language models (LLMs) has advanced significantly, typically employing a two-stage training process: pretraining on vast text corpora for broad knowledge acquisition, followed by post-training (alignment) to enhance reliability and usability. Reinforcement Learning (RL) has emerged as a particularly effective alignment technique for LLMs, especially for complex reasoning tasks. Algorithms such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and its variant Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) have been successfully applied in this context, as exemplified by models like DeepSeekMath. These methods have demonstrated the ability of RL to reduce the computational burden of alignment while substantially improving performance on tasks requiring mathematics, planning, and coding.

Despite their capabilities, traditional autoregressive LLMs exhibit several limitations, including slow inference, rigid left-to-right generation, and the propagation of early errors. In response, discrete diffusion models, often referred to as Diffusion Language Models (DLMs), have gained attention as a flexible alternative. DLMs offer potential advantages such as faster inference, iterative refinement through token revisions, and effective knowledge transfer, sometimes outperforming autoregressive LLMs in data-constrained scenarios.

However, adapting established RL methods for LLMs to DLMs presents a significant challenge: the absence of a straightforward autoregressive structure complicates the estimation of token-level and sequence-level likelihoods. Many RL objectives fundamentally rely on these likelihoods for policy updates. Prior work has attempted to bridge this gap:
- **Heuristic Token-level Approximations:** Methods like diffu-GRPO (Zhao et al., 2025) adapted GRPO for DLMs by approximating token-level likelihoods using one-step unmasking. While computationally efficient, this approach introduces bias by not fully capturing sequential dependencies.
- **DPO Extensions:** Other approaches extended DPO to DLMs (Zhu et al., 2025), but these often require numerous network evaluations, limiting their practical applicability.
- **Improved Token-level Approximations:** Further work (Gong et al., 2025b) refined token-level approximations by incorporating complementary random timesteps to improve accuracy.

This paper addresses the core "variance-cost dilemma" associated with sequence-level likelihood estimation in DLMs. While sequence-level objectives are considered more principled than token-level heuristics, their accurate evaluation using methods like the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO) is computationally expensive and prone to high variance with standard Monte Carlo sampling. The work aims to develop an RL algorithm for DLMs that leverages the benefits of sequence-level likelihoods while overcoming their computational and variance challenges.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce Group Diffusion Policy Optimization (GDPO), a novel reinforcement learning algorithm specifically designed to enhance reasoning capabilities in Diffusion Language Models (DLMs).

The motivation for developing GDPO stems from several limitations and challenges in the existing landscape of RL fine-tuning for DLMs:

1. **Intractable Likelihoods in DLMs:** Unlike autoregressive LLMs, DLMs lack a simple, factorizable structure for calculating token or sequence likelihoods. This intractability is a fundamental barrier to applying many standard RL algorithms that rely on accurate likelihood estimation for importance sampling and policy gradients.

2. **Bias in Existing Approximations:** Pioneering efforts like diffu-GRPO (Zhao etal., 2025) addressed the intractability by approximating token-level likelihoods using a computationally efficient one-step unmasking approach. However, this mean-field approximation is severely biased because it fails to account for important token correlations inherent in sequential generation, potentially leading to suboptimal policy updates.

3. **Computational Prohibitions of Principled Methods:** A more mathematically principled approach involves using sequence-level likelihoods, for which the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO) serves as a suitable surrogate. Despite its theoretical soundness, ELBO-based methods have seen limited adoption in DLMs due to their prohibitive computational cost. Accurate ELBO estimation often requires a large number of samples, leading to a substantial increase in network evaluations, as noted by previous works (e.g., Nie et al., 2025, suggesting up to 128 samples; Zhu et al., 2025, reducing it to eight but still costly).

4. **High Variance in ELBO Estimators:** The computational burden of ELBO estimation is exacerbated by a "variance explosion" when using vanilla double Monte Carlo sampling. This high variance makes policy optimization unstable and inefficient, requiring an even larger number of samples to obtain reliable estimates. There has been a lack of systematic understanding regarding the sources of this variance.

5. **Need for Efficient and Low-Variance Estimators:** The core challenge identified is designing ELBO estimators that are simultaneously efficient (requiring fewer network evaluations) and low-variance (providing stable gradient signals) to enable practical and effective sequence-level RL fine-tuning for DLMs.

By addressing these points, the authors aim to develop an RL algorithm that capitalizes on the flexibility of DLMs and the principled nature of sequence-level objectives, without incurring the excessive computational cost or instability associated with existing methods.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology developed in this paper, Group Diffusion Policy Optimization (GDPO), focuses on creating an efficient and low-variance estimator for sequence-level likelihoods in Diffusion Language Models (DLMs) to facilitate effective reinforcement learning.

#### 3.1. Masked Diffusion Language Models (MDMs) Preliminaries

The paper first defines MDMs, outlining their forward and reverse processes. In the forward process, a clean sequence `y_0` is progressively corrupted by masking tokens with probability `t`. The reverse process, performed by the model `π_θ`, aims to reconstruct `y_0` from a corrupted `y_t`. The training objective is a denoising loss, which serves as an Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO) for the log-likelihood of a sequence `log π(y|q)`. This ELBO is expressed as an expectation over random time `t` and random masks `y_t` given `y`.

#### 3.2. Reinforcement Learning without Value Networks (GRPO)

The paper draws inspiration from Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), an RL algorithm that removes the need for a separate value network by estimating advantages from group statistics of multiple sampled answers. GRPO uses token-level importance ratios and sequence-level rewards. However, the order-agnostic nature of DLM generation makes both exact sequence likelihoods and token-level ratios intractable, posing a challenge for direct GRPO application. Previous adaptations like diffu-GRPO used fast, but biased, mean-field approximations of token-level likelihoods.

#### 3.3. Readdressing Sequence-Level Likelihood for DLMs

The authors argue that while token-level approximations are computationally efficient, they fail to capture sequential dependencies and can overweight individual tokens. Sequence-level objectives are more faithful but difficult to apply due to the order-agnostic generation. The ELBO provides a principled surrogate for the sequence likelihood, but its direct estimation suffers from a "variance-cost dilemma": accurate estimates are expensive, while cheaper ones are high-variance or biased.

#### 3.4. Disentangling Variance in ELBO

To address the variance-cost dilemma, the authors analyze the sources of variance in the ELBO estimator. They decompose the total variance into two components:
1. **Random Time:** Variance arising from sampling the timestep `t`, which determines the overall masking level.
2. **Random Masking:** Variance from selecting which specific tokens are masked at a given `t`.

Empirical analysis on the OpenWeb dataset (Figure 2) reveals that the "Random Time" component accounts for the majority (96.2%) of the total variance. The mean loss as a function of time exhibits a smooth, simple structure, suggesting suitability for deterministic integration. Variance across timesteps is observed to be relatively stable, peaking near `t=0` and stabilizing for intermediate masking ratios.

#### 3.5. Variance Reduction via Semi-deterministic Monte Carlo (SDMC)

Motivated by the variance decomposition, the authors propose a Semi-deterministic Monte Carlo (SDMC) scheme to mitigate variance.
- **Deterministic Time:** Instead of Monte Carlo sampling for `t`, the expectation over `t` is replaced by a deterministic integral over the interval ``. This directly addresses the dominant source of variance.
- **Numerical Quadrature:** The integral is then approximated using a numerical quadrature rule with `N` points (`t_n`) and associated weights (`w_n`). Gaussian Quadratures are chosen for their fast convergence rates.
- **Single Monte Carlo for Inner Integral:** For the inner expectation (over `y_t` at each `t_n`), a single Monte Carlo estimate is employed, as the variance within timesteps is stable.
This means that the total number of network evaluations per likelihood computation is equal to the number of quadrature points, `N`.

An experimental evaluation comparing SDMC to Double Monte Carlo (Double-MC) on sequence likelihood estimation from the OpenWeb dataset (Figure 3) demonstrates that SDMC consistently achieves lower bias and variance. Notably, most of these gains are obtained with as few as 2 or 3 quadrature points, which makes the method computationally efficient.

#### 3.6. Group Diffusion Policy Optimization (GDPO) Algorithm

Building on the SDMC scheme, GDPO is introduced as an adaptation of GRPO for DLMs. The GDPO loss function (Equation 6) is similar to GRPO but crucial differences are:
- **Sequence-level Likelihoods:** Instead of token-level approximations, GDPO uses sequence-level ELBO estimates (computed via SDMC) for the importance weights `r_g(x)`.
- **Unnormalized Advantage Estimates:** Unnormalized advantage estimates (`A_g`) are used to avoid bias.

The full GDPO algorithm (Algorithm 1) involves iterating through prompts, generating multiple completions `y_g`, estimating rewards and advantages, and then performing gradient updates. Within each update, the ELBO for each completion is calculated using the SDMC scheme: looping through `N` quadrature points, and at each point `t_n`, performing a single Monte Carlo mask sampling and network evaluation to compute the inner loss `ℓ(π_θ; y_g, q, t_n)`.

#### 3.7. Overview of Theoretical Results

The paper provides a theoretical analysis of the SDMC estimator, focusing on its asymptotic error bounds.
- **MSE Decomposition:** The Mean Squared Error (MSE) is decomposed into Monte Carlo variance and the square of the integration bias.
- **Variance Rate:** The variance term scales as O(1/(NK)), or faster at O(1/(N^2K)) under additional N-dependent decay conditions on the log-likelihood.
- **Bias Rate:** For a generic Riemann sum, the squared integration bias scales as O(1/N^2). However, by using quadrature rules (which are justified if the integrand is sufficiently smooth), the squared integration bias can achieve rates of O(1/N^4) or O(1/N^8), making it practically negligible.
- **Integrand Properties:** The integrand `g(t)` (the expected loss at time `t`) can be related to the derivative of a KL divergence, which can be shown to be infinitely differentiable (C-infinity). Furthermore, under certain "Increasing Conditional Divergence" assumptions on the likelihood ratio, the integrand can be proven to be convex and monotone in `t`, supporting the empirical observations and justifying the use of standard quadrature rules for faster convergence.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted evaluate GDPO's performance against pretrained checkpoints and state-of-the-art baselines on various reasoning, planning, and coding tasks. The base model used is LLaDA-8B-Instruct (Zhu et al., 2025), an open-sourced DLM.

#### 4.1. Benchmarks Used

- **Mathematical Reasoning:** GSM8K (grade school math problems) and MATH500 (problems from the MATH dataset).
- **Planning:** 4x4 Sudoku puzzles and Countdown (arithmetic operations to reach a target number).
- **Coding:** HumanEval (Python algorithmic challenges) and MBPP (crowd-sourced Python programming tasks).

#### 4.2. Core Performance Improvements

- **Consistent Gains over Baselines:** GDPO consistently improves upon pretrained checkpoints and outperforms diffu-GRPO, a leading token-level RL method, across the majority of math, reasoning, and coding benchmarks (Figure 1, Tables 2 and 3).
- **Outperformance with Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT):** When combined with SFT, GDPO with 3 quadrature points surpasses existing RL baselines (including diffu-GRPO and its SFT variant) on mathematical and planning tasks, indicating its effectiveness in enhancing reasoning performance. For instance, on Countdown, GDPO (N=3) achieves 80.86% accuracy for 512 generations, significantly higher than SFT+diffu-GRPO's 42.2%.
- **Enhanced Reasoning in Coding Tasks:** On coding benchmarks, GDPO shows substantial performance improvements. On the MBPP benchmark, RL fine-tuning with GDPO yields a notable 10% accuracy gain over the pretrained model without SFT, achieving 50.6% for 256 generations compared to 41.2% for the LLaDA baseline.

#### 4.3. Generalization to Longer Contexts

- GDPO demonstrates improved generalization to longer sequences. For 512-token sequences, GDPO consistently outperforms all baselines while maintaining performance at shorter lengths. This is attributed to the use of sequence-level likelihoods, which are hypothesized to promote more uniform improvements across token positions, mitigating generation-order biases often seen in token-level methods.

#### 4.4. Computational Efficiency

- The research indicates that GDPO achieves its results with relatively modest computational resources, specifically training on 2 H100 GPUs. This suggests that the method can be viable even with limited computational budgets, making advanced RL fine-tuning more accessible. An ablation study on computational cost (Table 5) shows that while increasing quadrature points reduces the total number of training iterations within a fixed time budget, even with N=3, GDPO achieves a substantial fraction (77%) of iterations compared to diffu-GRPO (N=1), providing added benefits of improved likelihood approximation.

#### 4.5. Importance of ELBO Approximation Accuracy

- An ablation study focusing on the Countdown dataset (Figure 4) directly compares the impact of different ELBO approximation methods on test accuracy over training iterations. Estimators with higher accuracy, such as SDMC-2 and SDMC-3, consistently lead to greater performance improvements in the RL pipeline compared to naive Double-MC. This finding underscores that the quality and design of the likelihood estimator are critical for effective GDPO, rather than just the sheer number of function evaluations. SDMC-3 is shown to outperform Double-MC-4, despite Double-MC-4 technically having more function evaluations (4 vs 3).

#### 4.6. Comparison with DiffuCoder

- A comparison with `coupled-GRPO` (Gong et al., 2025b), a method that uses multiple network evaluations for improved token-level likelihoods, shows that GDPO obtains better results across Countdown and Sudoku benchmarks (Table 7). While both methods aim for variance reduction, GDPO's use of sequence-level probabilities appears to offer an advantage.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents Group Diffusion Policy Optimization (GDPO) as a significant contribution to the field of reinforcement learning for Diffusion Language Models (DLMs), with implications for both theoretical understanding and practical applications.

#### 5.1. Theoretical Advancement in DLM RL

The work provides a principled solution to the "variance-cost dilemma" that has hindered the application of sequence-level reinforcement learning to DLMs. By meticulously disentangling the sources of variance in the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO) estimator, the authors identify "Random Time" as the dominant factor. The introduction of the Semi-deterministic Monte Carlo (SDMC) scheme, which replaces random time sampling with deterministic integration and leverages numerical quadratures, offers a theoretically sound method for achieving provably lower variance and faster convergence rates compared to conventional double Monte Carlo methods. The theoretical analysis, including the decomposition of MSE and the rates under various conditions, strengthens the foundation for RL in discrete diffusion models.

#### 5.2. Enhanced Reasoning Capabilities for DLMs

Empirically, GDPO consistently demonstrates superior performance over strong baselines, including previous state-of-the-art RL methods like diffu-GRPO, across a diverse set of challenging reasoning, planning, and coding tasks. This indicates that a more accurate and stable estimation of sequence-level likelihoods directly translates to improved model alignment and enhanced abilities in complex problem-solving. This improvement is particularly notable in scenarios requiring generalization to longer contexts, suggesting that sequence-level objectives foster more robust learning.

#### 5.3. Practical Feasibility and Accessibility

Despite its theoretical sophistication, GDPO is shown to be computationally efficient enough to be trained on a limited number of GPUs (e.g., 2 H100s). This makes advanced RL fine-tuning for DLMs more accessible to researchers and practitioners who may not have access to very large computational clusters. The finding that significant gains can be achieved with a small number of quadrature points (N=2 or N=3) is a crucial practical insight, balancing accuracy with computational cost.

#### 5.4. New Paradigm for DLM Alignment

GDPO establishes a robust and effective paradigm for aligning DLMs. By moving beyond heuristic token-level approximations to a more principled sequence-level objective, the research opens new avenues for fine-tuning DLMs for complex tasks where global coherence and semantic understanding are critical. This approach could unlock the full potential of DLMs, making them more competitive with or even superior to autoregressive LLMs in specific applications.

#### 5.5. Future Research Directions

The paper explicitly highlights future research opportunities, such as exploring more advanced deterministic-stochastic integration schemes and data-driven quadrature methods. These suggestions indicate a pathway for further optimizing ELBO estimation, potentially leading to even greater variance reduction and efficiency gains, further solidifying the role of DLMs in the evolving landscape of generative AI.
