# Learning Differentiable Reachability Maps for Optimization-based Humanoid Motion Generation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at CNRS-AIST JRL developed differentiable reachability maps (DRMs) for humanoid robots, enabling kinematically feasible motion planning by integrating these continuous, scalar functions as inequality constraints in optimization frameworks. The DRMs demonstrated high accuracy, with Intersection over Union (IoU) scores up to 0.983 for a 2-DoF manipulator, and were successfully applied to complex bipedal footstep planning and multi-contact tasks for an HRP humanoid.

## Problem

- High computational cost and repeated Inverse Kinematics (IK) evaluations are required for checking reachability in humanoid motion planning.
- Existing discrete reachability maps are discontinuous and unsuitable for direct integration as constraints in continuous trajectory optimization problems.
- Approximating reachable regions with simple geometric shapes (e.g., boxes) is often conservative, limiting the robot's full workspace utilization.

## Method

- Learning differentiable reachability maps (DRMs) as continuous, scalar-valued functions using ==Neural Networks (NNs)== or Support Vector Machines (SVMs) via a binary classification approach.
- Integrating the learned DRMs directly as inequality constraints (f_R(x) ">= 0) into gradient-based continuous optimization frameworks for motion generation.
- Generating training data through both Forward Kinematics (FK)-based (for one-class SVMs) and IK-based (for NNs and regular SVMs) sampling methods.

## Results

- Achieved high accuracy on a planar 2-DoF manipulator with IoU scores of **0.983** for One-Class SVM and **0.971** for NNs, closely matching analytical reachable regions.
- NN models demonstrated superior performance in high-dimensional SE(3) task spaces with **89.3%** IoU and faster inference (**0.062** ms per sample) compared to SVMs for a UR10 manipulator.
- Successfully applied the framework to various complex humanoid tasks, including robot placement, bipedal footstep planning, multi-contact motion planning, and loco-manipulation for an HRP humanoid robot in simulation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Formulating reachability as a classification problem, rather than density estimation, provides a more robust indicator of the reachability boundary, as uniform joint-space sampling does not guarantee uniform task-space density.
> - Differentiable reachability maps enable the use of efficient gradient-based optimization methods by providing analytical gradients for reachability constraints.
> - One-Class SVMs can effectively learn reachability boundaries even when trained solely on reachable samples, making them suitable for scenarios where only positive data is readily available from forward kinematics.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Humanoid robot motion generation is a complex field that often involves solving high-dimensional Inverse Kinematics (IK) problems, which are computationally intensive and must be addressed repeatedly during trajectory or whole-body motion planning. The existing research landscape offers various approaches to manage the computational burden and integrate reachability considerations into motion planning.

Traditional methods for representing kinematic reachability, often termed "reachability maps," primarily rely on discrete representations. These involve discretizing the task space into grids and pre-solving IK for each grid point to determine feasibility. Examples include methods that save successful IK points or use convolution of joint movable regions. While these discrete maps are effective for evaluating the feasibility of a *given* trajectory or for robot placement, their discontinuous nature makes them unsuitable for direct inclusion as constraints or objective functions in continuous trajectory optimization, which is crucial for *generating* new, optimal trajectories. Furthermore, the "curse of dimensionality" poses a significant challenge for grid-based methods, as the number of grid points grows exponentially with task-space dimension.

In continuous optimization-based motion planning, end-effector admissible regions are sometimes approximated using simpler geometric shapes, such as boxes or convex polyhedra. This approach simplifies constraint formulation and reduces the dimensionality of optimization variables. However, these approximations are often conservative, limiting the robot's full utilization of its reachable workspace. They also present ambiguities in representing orientation constraints, particularly for surface contacts common in humanoid applications.

To overcome the limitations of discrete maps and conservative approximations, learning-based continuous representations of reachability have been explored. Some studies utilize Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs) to represent reachable regions as probability density functions or feasibility fields, which can be integrated into optimization objective functions. Other approaches employ Neural Networks (NNs) to classify reachability, approximate inverse reachability maps, or generate joint configurations. While these learning-based methods offer continuity, some density-based approaches assume that the density of task-space samples directly correlates with reachability, an assumption that may not hold due to the nonlinear mapping from joint space to task space.

This work contributes to this landscape by introducing a novel, data-driven method for learning a *differentiable* reachability map. The key distinction is its explicit focus on differentiability, which allows it to be directly embedded as an inequality constraint in continuous optimization problems. By framing reachability as a binary classification problem rather than a density estimation task, the proposed method aims to learn a scalar-valued function whose sign accurately indicates reachability, and whose gradients can be leveraged by optimization solvers, thereby addressing the core limitations of prior discrete and non-differentiable continuous representations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce and develop a method for learning "differentiable reachability maps" (DRMs) for robot kinematic reachability. A DRM is defined as a scalar-valued function over the task space that takes positive values in kinematically reachable regions and negative values elsewhere, while being continuous and differentiable with respect to task-space coordinates.

The motivation behind developing DRMs stems from several challenges in humanoid motion generation:

1. **Computational Cost Reduction:** Traditional motion planning often necessitates repeated Inverse Kinematics (IK) evaluations to check the feasibility of candidate end-effector poses. Solving IK is a computationally expensive non-linear optimization problem. The authors aim to replace this iterative IK solving with a fast, data-driven lookup from a pre-trained DRM, significantly reducing online computational cost during motion planning.
2. **Integration with Continuous Optimization:** Existing discrete reachability maps, while useful for evaluation, cannot be directly incorporated into continuous optimization problems as differentiable constraints. This limits their utility for generating novel trajectories. DRMs, by their continuous and differentiable nature, enable their seamless integration as inequality constraints in gradient-based optimization frameworks, thereby allowing for the generation of complex, feasible motions.
3. **Flexible Representation of Reachable Regions:** Approximating reachable regions with simple geometric primitives (e.g., boxes, convex polyhedra) can be overly conservative, restricting the robot's full workspace and potentially leading to suboptimal or infeasible plans. DRMs are designed to represent arbitrarily shaped feasible regions accurately, allowing for less constrained and more agile motion generation.
4. **Addressing Limitations of Density-based Learning:** Previous learning-based approaches sometimes model reachability using density estimation (e.g., GMMs). However, uniform sampling in joint space does not necessarily lead to uniform density in task space, meaning that areas of high sample density might not correlate with proximity to the true reachability boundary. This work motivates a classification-based approach, where the learned function's sign directly indicates reachability, offering a more direct and robust interpretation for optimization constraints.
5. **Applicability to Diverse Humanoid Tasks:** The goal is to develop a versatile framework applicable to various complex humanoid motion planning problems, including footstep planning, multi-contact planning, and loco-manipulation, which all inherently depend on accurate and efficient reachability assessment.

By achieving these objectives, the research aims to enhance the efficiency and capability of optimization-based motion planning for high-degree-of-freedom humanoid robots.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core methodology involves formulating the construction of a differentiable reachability map as a binary classification problem. Given a dataset of task-space points labeled as reachable or unreachable, the aim is to learn a continuous and differentiable scalar-valued function $f_R(x)$ that indicates reachability based on its sign.

**A. Learning Models:**
The paper employs two types of machine learning models for learning DRMs:

1. **Neural Networks (NNs):** A multilayer perceptron (MLP) architecture is used. It consists of fully connected layers with non-linear activation functions (ReLU) and a linear output layer, producing an unconstrained scalar output $f_R(x; \theta)$. The network is trained using a smooth classification loss function, specifically the `softplus` function, $L = \log(1 + \exp(-y_i f_R(x_i; \theta)))$. The differentiability of the NN allows for automatic computation of gradients $\partial f_R(x; \theta)/\partial x$ via backpropagation.
2. **Support Vector Machines (SVMs):**
 - **Regular SVM:** Utilizes a Radial Basis Function (RBF) kernel. The decision function $f_R(x)$ is a sum of kernel functions centered at support vectors. This formulation provides a differentiable function, and its gradient can be computed analytically. Regular SVMs require a dataset with both positive (reachable) and negative (unreachable) samples.
 - **One-Class SVM (OC-SVM):** This variant is used when only positive samples (reachable points) are available, for instance, from forward kinematics sampling. OC-SVM learns a decision boundary that encloses the support of the positive samples, treating the origin of the feature space as an implicit negative class. It also yields a differentiable decision function with an analytically computable gradient.

**B. Sample Set Generation:**
Two strategies are used to generate labeled task-space samples for training:

1. **Forward Kinematics (FK)-based Method:**
 - Joint configurations are randomly sampled within the robot's joint limits.
 - Forward kinematics are used to compute the corresponding end-effector poses.
 - Samples resulting in self-collisions are discarded.
 - This method exclusively yields *reachable* poses and is used to train one-class SVMs.
2. **Inverse Kinematics (IK)-based Method:**
 - End-effector poses are randomly sampled directly from the task space.
 - Inverse kinematics is solved for each sampled pose to determine if a valid joint configuration exists (satisfying joint limits and self-collision avoidance).
 - Poses with a valid IK solution are labeled as reachable (+1); otherwise, they are labeled as unreachable (-1).
 - This method generates datasets containing *both reachable and unreachable* samples, suitable for training NNs and regular SVMs.

To mitigate the "curse of dimensionality," random sampling is preferred over grid sampling. For tasks involving lower-dimensional subspaces of the full task space (e.g., SE(2) for footstep planning), samples are generated directly within that subspace for efficiency.

**C. Motion Generation Formulation with Reachability Constraints:**
The learned differentiable reachability map $f_R(x)$ is incorporated into continuous optimization problems as an inequality constraint: $f_R(x(r)) \geq 0$, where $r$ is the end-effector pose and $x(r)$ transforms it into the input format for the learned model.

The optimization problems are solved iteratively using a sequential local approximation approach, where at each step, a Quadratic Program (QP) is formulated by linearizing the objective function and constraints around the current solution estimate. The QP is then solved to update the solution.

Two primary formulations are presented:

1. **Simultaneous Reachability:** This formulation (Equation 9) addresses scenarios where multiple end-effector target poses must be reachable from a common baselink pose (e.g., robot placement). It minimizes the sum of squared distances to target end-effector poses, with an optional regularization term for the baselink pose. Constraints ensure that each end-effector's relative pose to the baselink is reachable.
2. **Sequential Reachability:** This formulation (Equation 11) is used for planning sequences of end-effector poses where each pose must be reachable from its immediate predecessor (e.g., footstep planning, multi-contact, loco-manipulation). The objective minimizes deviations from start/end targets and regularizes transitions between successive poses. Constraints ensure sequential reachability.

**D. Treatment of Orientation:**
For task spaces involving orientation (e.g., SE(2), SE(3)), the elements of the corresponding rotation matrix are flattened into a vector. This representation is chosen to avoid singularities common with Euler angles or unit quaternions and to provide a suitable distance metric in the feature space for the learning models. Relative poses are similarly constructed using the relative orientation matrix.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings regarding the performance of differentiable reachability maps and their application to humanoid motion planning.

**A. Numerical Evaluation with Planar 2-DoF Manipulator:**
Using a planar 2-DoF manipulator with an analytically known reachable region:
- **Accuracy:** The learned maps from NN, regular SVM, and one-class SVM closely matched the analytical ground truth boundary. Intersection over Union (IoU) scores were 0.971 for NN, 0.982 for regular SVM, and 0.983 for one-class SVM.
- **Inference Time:** SVMs demonstrated faster per-sample inference times (approximately 10 μs) compared to NNs (approximately 40 μs).
- **Optimization Parameter:** A small positive offset (ρ = 0.1) in the SVM decision function was found to improve classification accuracy, particularly for the one-class SVM, by better aligning the decision boundary.

**B. Comparison with Baseline Classifiers (UR10 Manipulator):**
Evaluations across various task spaces (R², SE(2), R³, SE(3)) using a Universal Robots UR10 manipulator revealed:
- **Overall Performance:** The proposed methods (NN, regular SVM, one-class SVM) generally matched or outperformed baseline classifiers like k-NN, one-class nearest neighbors (OC-NN), and a convex-hull-based classifier in terms of IoU.
- **High-Dimensional Performance:** In the SE(3) task space, the NN model demonstrated superior accuracy (89.3% IoU) and inference efficiency (0.062 ms per sample) compared to SVMs (R-SVM: 77.7% IoU, 8.7 ms; OC-SVM: 81.1% IoU, 7.9 ms) and baselines. NN also exhibited lower training costs for higher dimensions.
- **One-Class SVM Efficacy:** The one-class SVM performed comparably to classifiers trained with both positive and negative samples, despite being trained solely on reachable data, highlighting its utility for FK-based sampling scenarios.

**C. Applications to Humanoid Motion Planning:**
The proposed framework was applied to various humanoid motion planning problems:
- **Robot Placement Planning:** Demonstrated for a UR10 manipulator, planning base poses in R², R³, and SE(2) such that the end-effector could follow a prescribed trajectory. The method successfully found feasible base poses, with planning costs independent of the number of joints. Challenges were noted for SE(3) due to sparsity of feasible configurations.
- **Bipedal Footstep Planning:** Applied the sequential reachability formulation to generate footstep sequences for an HRP humanoid. Using SE(2) reachability maps, the method planned kinematically feasible footsteps, including obstacle avoidance, and successfully generated whole-body motions in a dynamics simulator.
- **Multi-contact Motion Planning:** Extended the sequential reachability formulation to include both foot and hand contacts for the HRP humanoid. The planner generated contact sequences for walking in a narrow corridor while maintaining hand contact on a wall, with successful execution in simulation.
- **Loco-manipulation Planning:** Adapted the sequential reachability formulation for the HRP humanoid to open a door while walking. The method planned footstep sequences that enabled the hand to maintain grasp and follow a predefined door-opening trajectory, even without a predefined foot goal, and generated corresponding whole-body motions.

**D. Discussion Points:**
- **Classification over Density:** The authors illustrated that uniform joint-space sampling results in non-uniform task-space density, meaning density-based methods (GMMs, KDE) do not accurately reflect the margin to the reachability boundary. This observation reinforces the choice of a classification-based approach where the function's sign directly indicates reachability.
- **Gradient Behavior:** The SVM-based reachability map exhibits near-zero gradients far from the reachable boundary. While this is efficient, it can hinder optimization if an initial solution is deep in an infeasible region. This was mitigated by imposing trust region bounds on the update step in the QP, preventing large, potentially invalid steps. The ideal behavior of a signed distance function, with gradients always pointing towards the boundary, was acknowledged as an open challenge for high-dimensional spaces.
- **SE(3) Computation Challenges:** Lower classification accuracy in SE(3) was noted, particularly near the boundary. This suggests areas for future improvement, such as stricter handling of 3D orientation representations and adaptive sampling techniques focusing on critical boundary regions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a notable advancement in the field of robot motion planning, particularly for complex humanoid systems.

The **primary significance** lies in the introduction and empirical validation of the **differentiable reachability map (DRM)**. This representation is a key innovation because it bridges the gap between the computational efficiency of pre-computed reachability data and the analytical requirements of continuous optimization. By providing a continuous and differentiable scalar function, DRMs allow kinematic reachability to be directly integrated as an inequality constraint in gradient-based motion planning, a capability previously limited by discrete maps or conservative geometric approximations.

The **potential impact** is multifaceted:

1. **Reduced Computational Burden:** The ability to replace iterative, expensive Inverse Kinematics (IK) calculations with a rapid lookup from a pre-trained differentiable map can substantially decrease the online computational cost of motion planning. This is crucial for real-time applications and for tackling problems involving many degrees of freedom or long-horizon trajectories.
2. **Enhanced Motion Planning Fidelity and Flexibility:** DRMs enable the representation of arbitrarily complex and irregular reachable regions, unlike simplified bounding boxes or polyhedra. This allows for more precise and less conservative motion planning, enabling robots to fully utilize their kinematic capabilities and generate more agile and dexterous motions.
3. **Broad Applicability to Humanoid Tasks:** The demonstrated success across various complex humanoid tasks – including robot placement, bipedal footstep planning, multi-contact motion planning, and loco-manipulation – indicates the versatility of the proposed framework. This suggests that DRMs could become a fundamental tool for solving a wide array of existing and future humanoid locomotion and manipulation challenges.
4. **Foundation for Advanced Optimization:** By providing analytical gradients of the reachability constraint, the method facilitates the use of sophisticated continuous optimization techniques (like Sequential Quadratic Programming). This opens avenues for combining reachability with other complex constraints (e.g., dynamic balance, collision avoidance) in a unified optimization framework.
5. **Scalability and Adaptability:** The use of random sampling addresses the "curse of dimensionality" inherent in high-dimensional task spaces better than grid-based methods. The choice between Neural Networks and Support Vector Machines also offers flexibility, allowing researchers to select the most appropriate model based on task-space characteristics and data availability (e.g., using one-class SVM for FK-only data).

While challenges remain, particularly concerning accuracy in high-dimensional orientation spaces like SE(3) and the gradient behavior far from the decision boundary, the work establishes a robust foundation. Future research building upon DRMs could focus on developing more advanced learning models, adaptive sampling strategies for critical boundary regions, and integrating signed distance function properties to enhance optimization performance. Overall, this work represents a significant step towards more efficient, capable, and practical motion generation for humanoid robots.
