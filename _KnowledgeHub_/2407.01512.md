# Open-TeleVision: Teleoperation with Immersive Active Visual Feedback

> [!summary] Summary
> Open-TeleVision introduces an immersive teleoperation system for humanoid robots that integrates VR headsets with active stereoscopic visual feedback. This system enables robust, multi-finger, bimanual control and facilitates the collection of high-quality data for training imitation learning policies, achieving superior task success rates and demonstrating cross-country remote operation.

## Problem

- Acquiring high-quality, diverse, and scalable real-robot data is a bottleneck for learning-based robotic manipulation policies.
- Existing teleoperation systems suffer from perceptual limitations (visual occlusion, lack of stereoscopic depth, static camera views), which are further compromised in remote operation from distant locations, hindering precise manipulation.
- Achieving precise and intuitive control for multi-finger dexterous hands on various robot platforms using off-the-shelf VR devices remains challenging.

## Method

- The system streams human hand, head, and wrist poses from devices like ==Apple VisionPro== for immersive VR teleoperation, retargeting motion for bimanual, multi-finger control on humanoid robots (Unitree H1, Fourier GR-1) via inverse kinematics.
- A key feature is the real-time, ego-centric active stereoscopic video feedback streamed from the robot's head-mounted camera back to the operator's VR headset.
- Collected teleoperated data is used to train imitation learning policies, leveraging an Action Chunking Transformer with a DinoV2 visual backbone for enhanced performance.

## Results

- Open-TeleVision achieved the highest IL success rates with DinoV2 and stereo input (90-**92%** vs. 46-**63%** baselines); a user study confirmed stereo vision gives faster completion and higher success (**100%** vs. **71%**, Can Insertion) than monocular.
- Active sensing and focused observation reduced computational costs by **2x** for both training and deployment inference, enabling a robust 60Hz control frequency.
- The system successfully facilitated cross-country remote teleoperation (MIT to UCSD) and enabled operators to perform diverse, complex tasks requiring bimanual coordination and fine-grained manipulation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Active stereoscopic visual feedback is crucial for intuitive teleoperation, leading to faster task completion and higher success rates for human operators, and can be effectively achieved remotely without sacrificing depth perception, enabling global collaboration in data collection.
> - Utilizing powerful self-supervised visual backbones like DinoV2 significantly enhances the performance and generalization capabilities of learned policies in robot manipulation tasks.
> - Immersive VR teleoperation enables the collection of high-quality, diverse real-robot data for complex, long-horizon tasks on humanoid robots, improving learned policy robustness and efficiency due to focused relevant visual inputs.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in "Open-TeleVision" addresses a critical bottleneck in the advancement of learning-based robotic manipulation: the acquisition of high-quality, diverse, and scalable real-robot data. Over the past few years, learning-based approaches, particularly imitation learning, have shown remarkable progress in robotics. However, unlike fields that benefit from massive synthetic datasets or readily available human data (e.g., natural language processing, computer vision), robotics often faces a significant "Sim2Real" gap, making real-world data indispensable. Teleoperation has emerged as a dominant paradigm for collecting such on-robot demonstrations.

Existing teleoperation systems, as summarized in Table 4 of the paper, generally fall into categories based on their actuation and perception mechanisms:
- **Actuation**: Some systems use joint-copying (e.g., ALOHA, AirExo, GELLO) which offers high precision but often requires dedicated hardware, is non-remote, and has been primarily limited to grippers rather than multi-finger hands. Other systems rely on visual tracking (e.g., AnyTeleop, Telekinesis) or motion-capture gloves, which can be noisy or lack certain pose estimations. VR-based systems (e.g., OPEN TEACH, HATO) leverage integrated hand-tracking, offering better stability. Open-TeleVision falls into the VR-tracking category, but uniquely extends it to multi-finger dexterous hands and bimanual control.
- **Perception**: Many systems use a third-person view, which suffers from occlusion. First-person views (e.g., Mobile ALOHA) improve intuition but often rely on static cameras, limiting the field of view or requiring multiple cameras. Critically, remote teleoperation often sacrifices depth perception if only monocular video streams are provided, or demands physical proximity for direct human stereoscopic vision.

Open-TeleVision carves out a unique niche by tackling several of these limitations simultaneously. It aims to bridge the gap between high-precision, low-cost teleoperation and intuitive, immersive feedback. By integrating **active stereoscopic visual feedback** with **bimanual, multi-finger hand retargeting** on humanoid platforms, it directly addresses the challenges of intuitive spatial understanding, occlusion, and the need for diverse, complex manipulation data. It positions itself as a versatile, general-purpose framework that can operate different VR devices on various robots, distinguishing itself from concurrent works focusing solely on humanoid teleoperation (e.g., Omnih2o, Humanplus). This work directly contributes to the broader goal of making robot learning more accessible and scalable by improving the fundamental process of data acquisition.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of Open-TeleVision is to propose and validate an "immersive teleoperation system" that significantly enhances the intuitiveness, ease of use, and quality of on-robot data collection for robot learning from demonstrations.

The key motivations stem from the inherent challenges in current teleoperation systems:
1. **Data Quality and Scalability**: High-quality, diverse, and large-scale real-robot data is crucial for training robust and generalizable robot learning policies. Existing teleoperation methods often fall short in providing such data due to their limitations in intuitiveness and ease of use, leading to less precise, less natural, or more time-consuming demonstrations.
2. **Perceptual Limitations**: Many systems suffer from visual occlusion, especially in complex manipulation tasks where robot arms or body parts might block the view of the object. Static third-person or first-person camera views limit the operator's ability to focus on specific regions of interest or understand spatial relationships, making fine-grained manipulation challenging. The lack of stereoscopic (3D) vision in remote setups further exacerbates this issue.
3. **Actuation Constraints**: While some systems offer high-precision control, they often require specialized, costly hardware (e.g., joint-copying setups) or are limited to simpler end-effectors like grippers, restricting the complexity of tasks that can be demonstrated. Achieving precise control for multi-finger dexterous hands on various robot platforms using off-the-shelf VR devices is a significant challenge.
4. **Remote Operation**: The ability to teleoperate robots remotely is vital for scalable data collection, allowing experts to operate robots from anywhere, without needing to be physically present in the lab. However, achieving remote control without sacrificing crucial perceptual cues (like depth) has been a persistent challenge.

Open-TeleVision aims to overcome these limitations by enabling operators to "actively perceive the robot’s surroundings in a stereoscopic manner" and by "mirroring the operator’s arm and hand movements on the robot," creating an immersive experience akin to an operator's mind being transmitted to a robot embodiment. This directly translates to more natural, precise, and efficient data collection, which can then be used to train more capable imitation learning policies.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Open-TeleVision adopts a VR-based teleoperation framework designed for versatility and immersiveness, validated through imitation learning experiments on two humanoid robots.

**Hardware Configuration:**
- **VR Devices**: The system is designed to be agnostic to specific VR devices, but experiments primarily use Apple VisionPro. These devices stream the operator's hand, head, and wrist poses in SE(3) (3D position and orientation).
- **Robots**: Two distinct humanoid robots are employed:
 - **Unitree H1**: Equipped with two 7 DoF arms and 6 DoF multi-finger Inspire hands. For active sensing, a custom-designed gimbal with 2 revolute DoFs (yaw and pitch) is mounted on its torso, driven by DYNAMIXEL motors, holding a stereo camera.
 - **Fourier GR-1**: Features two 7 DoF arms, 1 DoF jaw grippers, and a manufacturer-provided 3 DoF neck (yaw, roll, pitch) for active sensing.
- **Stereo Camera**: A ZED Mini stereo camera is used with both robots to provide real-time stereoscopic RGB streaming.

**System Architecture and Control Loop (Figure 2):**
The system is built around a web server (Vuer based) that acts as the central hub:
1. **Input from Operator**: VR devices stream human poses (hand, head, wrist) to the server at 60 Hz.
2. **Motion Retargeting (Human-to-Robot)**: The server translates human movements into robot joint commands.
 - **Arm Control**: Human wrist poses are retargeted to robot end-effector positions. Inverse Kinematics (IK), specifically Closed-loop Inverse Kinematics (CLIK) based on Pinocchio, computes robot arm joint angles. SE(3) group filters and joint angle offsets (in nullspace) are used for stability and to mitigate IK failures.
 - **Hand Control**: Human hand keypoints are translated into robot joint angles using `dex-retargeting`, an optimization-based library. For dexterous hands (Inspire), seven keypoint vectors synchronize movements. For grippers (GR-1), a single vector between thumb and index fingertips controls opening/closing. SLSQP algorithm is used for real-time optimization.
3. **Active Visual Feedback**: The robot streams real-time, ego-centric stereo video (480x640 per eye) from its head-mounted camera back to the VR device. The robot's head actively follows the operator's head movements, providing an intuitive, first-person, 3D view of the workspace.
4. **Robot Execution**: The retargeted joint position targets are sent to the robot, completing the 60 Hz control loop.

**Imitation Learning Paradigm (Figure 2, Right):**
- **Data Collection**: Human operators use Open-TeleVision to collect demonstrations for various tasks (Can Sorting, Can Insertion, Folding, Unloading). An "ending gesture" signifies successful completion of an episode.
- **Policy Training**: An Action Chunking Transformer (ACT) serves as the imitation learning algorithm.
 - **Visual Backbone**: ResNet18 (ImageNet pre-trained) is replaced with a more powerful, self-supervised pre-trained vision transformer, DinoV2, to process visual inputs.
 - **Input**: Two stereo images (left and right eye) and proprioception (current joint positions) are fed into the transformer encoder.
 - **Output**: The transformer decoder outputs action sequences (absolute joint positions) of a certain chunk size (e.g., 60 or 100 steps).
 - **Action Space**: 28 dimensions for H1 (arms, hands, neck) and 19 for GR-1 (arms, grippers, neck).
 - **Training Details**: AdamW optimizer (5e-5 LR, batch size 45, 25k iterations) on a single RTX 4090 GPU.

This methodology emphasizes leveraging advanced VR technology for intuitive control, robust motion retargeting for complex robot morphologies (especially dexterous hands), and crucial active stereoscopic vision for both teleoperation comfort and effective policy learning.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper presents compelling results across teleoperation performance, imitation learning capabilities, and user studies, demonstrating the effectiveness of Open-TeleVision's design choices.

#### 4.1. Imitation Learning Results (Autonomous Policy Performance)

Ablation studies were conducted on four long-horizon, precision-sensitive tasks (Can Sorting, Can Insertion, Folding, Unloading) using H1 and GR-1 robots, comparing the full Open-TeleVision setup (DinoV2 + Stereo Input) against baselines: `w. ResNet18` (original ACT visual backbone) and `w/o Stereo Input` (only left image).

- **Overall Superiority**: Open-TeleVision (DinoV2 + Stereo) consistently achieved the highest success rates across all tasks and sub-tasks (Table 1).
- **Impact of DinoV2 Backbone**: `w. ResNet18` performed significantly worse than `w. DinoV2 (Ours)` in most tasks, highlighting the importance of a powerful, pre-trained visual backbone like DinoV2 for processing complex visual information and enabling generalization.
- **Cruciality of Stereo Input**: `w/o Stereo Input` generally performed poorly, especially in tasks requiring precise spatial reasoning like picking cans (`H1 Can Sorting` pick success: 46% vs. 92% for ours) and can insertion (`Can Insertion` pick/insert: 47%/63% vs. 90%/87% for ours). This confirms that implicit depth information from stereo images is vital for both teleoperation and learned policies.
- **GR-1 Can Sorting Nuances**: While Open-TeleVision outperformed baselines, absolute success for placing cans with GR-1 was lower (60%) compared to H1 (88%). This was attributed to visual occlusion by the gripper making it hard for the camera to see the can's color after grasping. A follow-up experiment (Appendix B) with color-labeled cans significantly improved GR-1's placing success (to 100%), validating the occlusion hypothesis and highlighting the dependence on unoccluded visual cues for color-based sorting.

#### 4.2. Generalization Capabilities

The H1 and GR-1 models demonstrated good generalization for object picking in `Can Sorting` across a 4x4 grid of randomized can placements (Figure 6, Figure 10 Right). The policy maintained a high success rate (100% in covered areas) even in peripheral regions rarely seen in demonstrations, indicating robust learning from diverse teleoperated data.

#### 4.3. Benefits of Active Sensing and Computational Efficiency

- **Focused Observation**: Active camera movement (mimicking human head movement) allows the robot to focus on points of interest (PoIs) more effectively than static wide-angle cameras (Figure 7). This provides relevant visual information for policy learning and reduces unnecessary computation.
- **Computational Savings**: Comparing `Cropped Active (Ours)` vs. `Wide Angle`, Open-TeleVision's active vision setup significantly reduced computational costs (Table 2). Training time per batch was 2x faster (0.1s vs. 0.32s with batch size 10, or 0.41s vs. 0.32s with batch size 45 for ours vs. 10 for wide angle) and deployment inference speed was 2x faster (83Hz vs. 42Hz), leaving ample time for IK and retargeting, enabling the crucial 60Hz control frequency.

#### 4.4. Teleoperated Performance and Remote Operation

- **Complex Task Execution**: The system enabled operators to perform diverse, complex tasks beyond the primary four, including `Wood-board Drilling` (heavy tool, precise trigger control), `Earplugs Packing` (agile bimanual coordination), and `Pipette` (high precision, anthropomorphic hand-specific actions) (Figure 8). This showcases the system's dexterity, responsiveness, and capability for fine-grained manipulation, even with robots known for less precise motors (H1).
- **Remote Control**: Open-TeleVision successfully facilitated cross-country teleoperation, with an operator at MIT (Boston, East Coast) controlling the H1 robot at UC San Diego (West Coast), demonstrating its practical utility for remote data collection and deployment (Figure 9).

#### 4.5. User Study Results

A user study with four participants comparing stereo vs. monocular (Mono) video streaming in the VR headset yielded clear results (Table 3):
- **Faster Completion**: Participants completed tasks significantly faster with `Stereo` vision (e.g., `H1 Can Sorting` mean time: 66s for Stereo vs. 91s for Mono).
- **Higher Success Rate**: `Stereo` vision led to much higher success rates, particularly in precision-demanding tasks like `Can Insertion` (100% for Stereo vs. 71% for Mono) and `Unloading` (100% for Stereo vs. 50% for Mono).
- **Qualitative Feedback**: Users overwhelmingly preferred stereo, finding it remarkably better for spatial understanding and alleviating discomfort during teleoperation. This validates that human intuition, while adaptive to mono vision, is insufficient for active interaction during teleoperation without depth cues.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

Open-TeleVision represents a significant step forward in the realm of robot teleoperation and its application to robot learning. Its contributions and potential impact are multifaceted:

1. **Enabling Scalable and High-Quality Data Collection**: The most direct impact is on accelerating robot learning from demonstrations. By providing an intuitive, precise, and immersive teleoperation system, Open-TeleVision facilitates the collection of higher-quality, more diverse, and larger volumes of on-robot data. This data is critical for training robust and generalizable policies for complex manipulation tasks, moving beyond limited laboratory settings.
2. **Advancing Humanoid Robot Capabilities**: The system's successful application to multi-finger dexterous hands on humanoid robots (H1) and its bimanual capabilities demonstrate its potential to unlock more sophisticated and human-like manipulation skills in these complex platforms. Tasks like drilling or pipetting, which are challenging for simpler grippers, become feasible.
3. **Redefining Remote Teleoperation**: Open-TeleVision is noted as the first system to offer simultaneous remote control and depth sensing via stereo video streaming. This innovation is transformative, as it allows expert operators to control robots from anywhere in the world without sacrificing crucial spatial understanding, which was previously a trade-off. This capability is essential for large-scale, distributed robotics operations and research collaborations.
4. **Optimizing Perception for Policy Learning**: The integration of active stereoscopic vision directly benefits the learned policies. By mimicking human attention (moving the robot's head to focus on PoIs), the system provides policies with more relevant, focused visual inputs, reducing computational burden and improving real-time control. Training with stereo images also inherently provides implicit depth cues, leading to more accurate and robust manipulation.
5. **Accessibility and Open Science**: Open-sourcing the system (as indicated by the project website) is a crucial contribution. It democratizes access to advanced teleoperation technology, allowing other researchers and practitioners to build upon this work, replicate experiments, and integrate it into their own robotic platforms. This fosters collaborative research and accelerates the pace of innovation in robot learning.

**Limitations and Future Work**:
The authors acknowledge several limitations, which also point to avenues for future research:
- **Lack of Haptic Feedback**: The current system lacks haptic feedback, which is crucial for tactile-intensive tasks and for providing a more complete sense of immersion, especially when visual occlusion occurs.
- **No Expert Data Relabeling**: The system does not currently support relabeling expert data, a feature that could further increase the success rate of learned policies.
- **Focus on Upper Body DoFs**: The current system only utilizes the upper body DoFs of humanoid robots. Extending it to whole-body teleoperation (including locomotion and balance) would be a natural next step to fully exploit the capabilities of humanoid platforms.

Despite these limitations, Open-TeleVision represents a significant methodological and empirical contribution, paving the way for more intuitive, efficient, and scalable robot learning paradigms, particularly for complex manipulation tasks with humanoid robots.
