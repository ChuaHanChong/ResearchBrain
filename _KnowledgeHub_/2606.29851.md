# TACO: A Test and Check Framework for Robust Pose Graph Optimization

> [!summary] Summary
> The TACO framework introduces a two-stage "Test and Check" mechanism for robust Pose Graph Optimization (PGO) in SLAM, combining an incremental online consistency evaluation with a periodic error recovery module. It achieves high success rates (around 90% in 2D and 83% in 3D) at outlier rates up to 50% while maintaining real-time mean convergence times of approximately 45 ms in 2D and 100 ms in 3D.

## Problem

- Pose Graph Optimization (PGO) is highly vulnerable to outliers (incorrect loop closure associations), leading to significant degradation of robot trajectory estimates.
- State-of-the-art robust PGO methods, while accurate, are often computationally expensive and unsuitable for real-time, online Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM).
- Incremental PGO approaches typically lack mechanisms to re-evaluate previously accepted loop closures, potentially allowing erroneous measurements to accumulate.

## Method

- The **TACO** framework integrates ==Incremental Probabilistic Consensus (IPC)== for online, incremental validation of incoming loop closures based on subgraph consistency.
- It employs ==Switchable Outlier Sanitization (SOS)== to periodically re-evaluate and filter out previously accepted, but erroneous, loop closures using ==switchable constraints==.
- The approach avoids explicitly modeling inliers/outliers during online processing and instead focuses on approximating a maximal consistent measurement set.

## Results

- TACO achieved success rates of approximately **90%** in 2D and over **83%** in 3D for outlier rates up to **50%**, rivaling state-of-the-art offline robust PGO methods.
- It maintained real-time mean convergence times (around **45 ms** for 2D, **100 ms** for 3D), demonstrating a **150x** speedup over GNC while remaining stable with increasing outlier ratios.
- The framework consistently achieved high precision (**≥99.7%**) in outlier rejection and exhibited stable performance even under extreme outlier rates (**60-90%**), outperforming other methods in runtime efficiency.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A dual-component "Test and Check" framework, combining incremental validation with periodic retrospective sanitization, achieves high robustness while maintaining online efficiency.
> - Operating on minimal independent subgraphs during online processing significantly reduces computational load compared to full graph re-optimization.
> - The framework demonstrates that robust PGO can maintain accuracy and precision in outlier rejection even under high noise levels without excessively increasing convergence times.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM) is a fundamental problem in robotics, where a robot concurrently builds a map of its environment and localizes itself within that map. A widely adopted approach for solving the Maximum a Posteriori (MAP) problem in SLAM is Pose Graph Optimization (PGO). In PGO, the problem is formulated as a graph where nodes represent robot poses and edges encode spatial constraints derived from sensor measurements. These constraints typically include relative motion estimates from odometry and detections of previously visited locations, known as loop closures.

Despite the widespread use of PGO, the underlying Non-Linear Least Squares (NLLS) methods employed are inherently sensitive to initialization and particularly to the presence of outliers. Outliers, in this context, are spurious loop closures that incorrectly associate distinct places, often arising from perceptual aliasing in complex environments. Even a single outlier can substantially degrade the quality of the estimated robot trajectory.

Existing efforts to address outlier sensitivity in PGO generally fall into several categories:
- **Robust Cost Functions (M-Estimators):** These replace standard quadratic error functions with robust alternatives (e.g., Huber, Cauchy) to reduce the influence of large errors, but they introduce additional non-linearity, can be sensitive to initialization, and require careful selection of thresholds and cost functions.
- **Graduated Non-Convexity (GNC):** This iterative framework addresses non-convex problems by progressively introducing non-convexity, often performing better than direct M-estimator application. However, GNC-based methods typically incur increased convergence times, making them more suitable for offline processing.
- **Augmented Pose Graph Optimization:** Approaches like Switchable Constraints (SC) introduce additional variables to explicitly model the inlier/outlier status of measurements. While effective, these additions increase the problem's complexity, potentially leading to more irregular convergence basins.
- **Maximal Consistent Set of Measurements:** These methods aim to identify the largest subset of mutually consistent measurements without explicitly classifying inliers or outliers. However, determining this set is combinatorially complex, often requiring effective heuristics or approximations, and many existing solutions are designed for offline operation, repeatedly reprocessing the full pose graph.

This research introduces TACO, a framework positioned within the "consistent set maximization" paradigm, but with a specific focus on enabling robust PGO in *online* settings. The paper identifies a gap: many state-of-the-art robust methods achieve high accuracy but are computationally expensive, making them unsuitable for real-time SLAM. TACO aims to bridge this gap by combining an incremental online consistency evaluation with a periodic, targeted error recovery mechanism, thereby seeking to attain robustness comparable to offline methods while maintaining the computational efficiency necessary for online deployment. This work also contributes to the critical assessment of evaluation metrics for Robust PGO.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this work stems from the observed vulnerability of Pose Graph Optimization (PGO) to outliers, which are incorrect loop closure associations. While front-end SLAM components attempt to minimize these errors, perceptual aliasing in complex environments makes it unrealistic to completely eliminate them. Therefore, the back-end PGO system must proactively manage erroneous measurements. The overarching goal is to enable robust and accurate robot localization and mapping in challenging real-world scenarios.

The key objectives of the TACO framework are detailed as follows:
1. **Develop a robust optimization framework:** The central objective is to design a system capable of effectively filtering outliers from PGO, thereby preventing the degradation of estimated trajectories. This framework is explicitly designed to operate without explicitly modeling measurements as either inliers or outliers.
2. **Achieve incremental consistent set approximation:** Rather than processing the entire graph offline, TACO aims to find an approximation of the maximally consistent set of measurements incrementally, which is crucial for online performance in SLAM systems.
3. **Combine online efficiency with offline robustness:** A significant objective is to develop a method that offers robustness comparable to state-of-the-art offline robust PGO techniques, while simultaneously preserving the computational efficiency and responsiveness required for online deployment in real-time robotic applications. This includes reducing reliance on potentially outdated estimates during the integration of new data.
4. **Address limitations of incremental approaches:** Specifically, the paper aims to extend the authors' previous Incremental Probabilistic Consensus (IPC) algorithm by introducing an error recovery module. This module is intended to revise decisions on previously accepted loop closures, correcting any inconsistencies that IPC might have mistakenly included and which could degrade subsequent performance.
5. **Propose suitable evaluation metrics for Robust PGO:** The work also seeks to highlight the limitations of traditional SLAM evaluation metrics, such as Absolute Trajectory Error (ATE) and Relative Pose Error (RPE), for assessing robustness in the presence of outliers. Consequently, a further objective is to propose and utilize metrics more specifically designed to evaluate the performance of robust PGO systems accurately.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The TACO (Test And Check Optimization) framework is structured into two complementary components: the "test" component, Incremental Probabilistic Consensus (IPC), and the "check" component, Switchable Outlier Sanitization (SOS). The framework operates under two main assumptions: (A1) odometry measurements are considered inliers, affected only by Gaussian noise, and (A2) there is a prevalence of inliers, meaning the majority of loop closure measurements are correct. It also incorporates two heuristics: (H1) starting with a simpler problem version to obtain a meaningful initial solution for the more complex problem, and (H2) employing compatibility tests to reject outliers while preserving inliers.

#### 3.1. The Incremental Probabilistic Consensus (IPC) Algorithm (Test Component)

IPC is designed for online evaluation of incoming loop closures. Its role is to incrementally build an approximation of the maximal set of consistent measurements, referred to as the consensus set (C).
1. **Odometry Integration:** The algorithm first integrates odometry measurements and generates new pose nodes as needed (A1).
2. **Independent Subgraph Identification:** For each new incoming loop closure measurement `z_i,j`, IPC identifies a minimal yet informative independent subgraph, denoted `G_I(a,b)`. An independent subgraph is defined as one connected to its complementary partition only through odometry edges at its endpoints. This property allows the subgraph to be treated as a self-contained subproblem for consistency evaluation (H1). The subgraph is initialized with the nodes of the new loop closure (`a=i, b=j`) and expanded iteratively to include any existing loop closures from `C` that violate the independence condition, along with their connecting odometry.
3. **Subproblem Optimization:** A PGO problem is then solved exclusively for the identified independent subgraph (H1).
4. **Measurement Consistency Test:** The incoming loop closure `z_i,j` is accepted and added to `C` only if all measurements within the independent subgraph (including existing odometry and consensus loop closures) pass a χ² test (A1, A2, H1, H2). This test verifies that their residual errors are consistent with their Gaussian noise models.
 - To guide the optimization process and strengthen the influence of reliable odometry constraints, IPC introduces a fixed scaling factor `s > 1` to the information matrices of odometry measurements. This differs from robust cost functions by not introducing additional non-linearities or requiring individual re-weighting factors. Consistency evaluation, however, uses the original, unscaled measurement noise models.
5. **Solution Propagation:** If `z_i,j` is accepted, the refined pose estimates from the subgraph optimization are propagated to the rest of the graph via forward odometry composition. If rejected, the subgraph nodes are restored to their pre-optimization state.

A known limitation of IPC is that accepted loop closures are not re-evaluated. This can lead to the accidental inclusion of outliers in `C`, especially in early stages or over long open-loop trajectories, potentially degrading future consistency checks.

#### 3.2. Switchable Outlier Sanitization (SOS) (Check Component)

SOS is designed to overcome IPC's limitation by retrospectively revising decisions on previously accepted loop closures, thereby sanitizing (filtering out) mistakenly included outliers.
1. **Periodic Execution:** SOS runs periodically on an "unrevised set" `U_r`, which contains the last `M` loop closures accepted by IPC but not yet checked by SOS.
2. **Trusted Subgraph Identification:** Similar to IPC, SOS identifies a minimal trusted subgraph `G_T` (H1). This subgraph includes all nodes and edges related to `U_r`. Crucially, `G_T` must remain connected even if the loop closures in `U_r` are temporarily disregarded, relying on odometry edges and loop closures already confirmed as inliers in the "revised set" `R_c`. Dijkstra's algorithm is employed to prune unnecessary nodes and odometry edges, leveraging `R_c` as shortcuts. This strategy aims to reduce problem complexity while maintaining solution quality.
3. **Augmented PGO with Switchable Constraints:** The loop closure edges within `U_r` are replaced by their switchable variants, as introduced in the Switchable Constraints (SC) framework. This introduces switch variables `φ_i,j` (ranging from 0 to 1) and associated penalty terms into the optimization problem. The prior belief for these switch variables `η_i,j` is set to 1, indicating an initial expectation that these pre-filtered loop closures are inliers. This augmented PGO problem is solved on the `G_T`.
4. **Outlier Sanitization:** After optimization, the `φ_i,j` values are interpreted as consistency scores. Loop closure edges in `U_r` with a `φ_i,j` score below a threshold (`th_c = 0.9`) are identified as inconsistent and discarded from the consensus set `C` (H2).
5. **Recovery from Degraded Estimates:** If any loop closure in `U_r` is classified as inconsistent, TACO re-optimizes the entire relevant portion of the graph `G(1,b)` using only the confirmed inliers (`E_o ∪ C`) to recover from potentially degraded trajectory estimates.

#### 3.3. Evaluation Metrics

The paper critically examines standard SLAM metrics, Absolute Trajectory Error (ATE) and Relative Pose Error (RPE), noting their limitations in evaluating robust PGO due to their sensitivity to outlier-induced trajectory distortions. Instead, it proposes:
- **Success Rate (SR):** The ratio of successful runs (where ATE is below a defined threshold, `th_ATE = 0.75 m`) to total runs. This provides a probabilistic measure of a method's ability to converge to a good solution.
- **Precision (pr), Recall (rc), and F1 Score:** These metrics quantify the accuracy of outlier rejection and inlier retention. Precision (true positives / (true positives + false positives)) is emphasized as more critical than recall (true positives / (true positives + false negatives)) for RPGO, as accepting outliers (low precision) can be more detrimental than rejecting inliers (low recall).
- **Mean Convergence Time:** The average time taken for the optimizer to converge after integrating a new loop closure, crucial for assessing online feasibility.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

- **Baselines:** TACO is benchmarked against six state-of-the-art RPGO methods: MAXMIX, Switchable Constraints, Realizing-Reversing-Recovering (RRR), Dynamic Covariance Scaling (DCS), Huber, and Graduated Non-Convexity (GNC). All methods are implemented within the `g2o` framework.
- **Datasets:**
 - **2D SLAM:** Intel, Csail, Freiburg Building (FR079), Freiburg University Hospital (FRH).
 - **3D Visual SLAM:** KITTI 00, KITTI 05 (from KITTI visual odometry dataset), TUM FR1 DESK (from TUM RGB-D SLAM dataset). Odometry and loop closures for 3D datasets are generated using ORB-SLAM3.
- **Outlier Injection:** Outliers are artificially injected into the datasets using the Vertigo package, ranging from 10% to 50% (in 5% increments) of total loop closures. Additionally, extreme outlier rates (60% to 90%) are used for a dedicated ablation study. Ten independent corrupted instances are generated for each difficulty level.
- **Implementation Details:** TACO is implemented in C++ using `g2o`. Default parameters are `s = 10` for IPC and `M = 10` for SOS, determined via grid search on representative datasets. Optimization is performed using Gauss-Newton. Loop closure detections are provided sequentially to simulate online operation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of TACO was conducted on both 2D and 3D Visual SLAM datasets, comparing its performance against several state-of-the-art robust PGO methods under varying outlier rates.

#### 4.1. Performance on 2D SLAM Datasets

- **Success Rate (SR):** TACO achieved an SR of approximately 90% across all outlier percentages up to 50%. This performance was comparable to offline methods such as GNC, SC, and DCS, with GNC typically exhibiting the highest SR (exceeding 97%). IPC alone showed an SR above 80% up to 25% outliers, then declining to around 65%. The integration of SOS with IPC increased the SR by approximately 15% on average and stabilized performance across different outlier levels.
- **Mean Convergence Time:** TACO demonstrated a mean convergence time of approximately 45 ms, which represents an average speedup of about 150x compared to GNC. Unlike other methods (GNC, SC, DCS, MAXMIX), TACO's runtime remained nearly constant (from 39.8 ms to 45.5 ms) even with increasing outlier ratios. The introduction of SOS incurred negligible overhead, keeping TACO's runtime close to that of IPC.
- **Precision:** TACO, GNC, DCS, and SC consistently maintained precision at or above 99.7%, indicating effective outlier rejection. Methods with lower precision, such as RRR and HUBER, exhibited poor SR.
- **Recall:** TACO achieved a recall of around 90%, which was lower than GNC, DCS, and SC (close to 100%). However, TACO's improved precision, relative to IPC, contributed to a higher overall SR. MAXMIX showed poor SR linked to excessive inlier rejection (low recall).
- **Metrics Discussion:** The study reinforced the finding that ATE alone can be a misleading metric for RPGO, as it might suggest better performance for severely distorted trajectories. Precision and recall, when considered separately, provided more insightful diagnostics of RPGO system failures.

#### 4.2. Performance on 3D Visual SLAM Datasets

- **SR:** On 3D datasets, TACO consistently maintained an SR above 83%, remaining competitive. DCS and SC achieved over 90% SR. GNC's SR was significantly impacted by the increase in dimensionality, being outperformed by DCS, SC, and TACO.
- **Mean Convergence Time:** TACO maintained an approximately constant mean convergence time of 100 ms across all outlier levels. DCS (~120 ms) and SC (~140 ms) also showed efficient runtimes, attributed in part to the conservative nature of the ORB-SLAM3 loop detector used to generate these datasets, which limited the number of true loop closures. RRR and GNC remained the most computationally expensive.

#### 4.3. Performance on Extreme Outlier Rates (60-90%)

- Under extreme outlier rates (exceeding 50%), TACO's SR degraded, dropping from 80% at 60% outliers to 53% at 90% outliers. This indicated that its performance is affected when the assumption of "prevalence of inliers" (A2) is significantly violated.
- SC demonstrated the best overall performance in this regime, achieving 100% SR at 60% and 70% outliers, and remaining above 86% SR thereafter. DCS and GNC also showed decreasing SR trends.
- Despite the SR degradation, TACO maintained a mean convergence time below 40 ms, offering a substantial runtime improvement compared to GNC and DCS, whose runtimes escalated significantly (e.g., GNC from 1.4s to 67s).

#### 4.4. Ablation Study: Effect of Parameter `s` (IPC Reweighting)

- IPC was found to be sensitive to the `s` parameter in the range, achieving its highest SR at `s=10`.
- TACO, in contrast, exhibited stable performance over a broader range of `s` values [0, 50), with SOS compensating for IPC's more permissive behavior at lower `s` values.
- For `s > 50`, the performance of both methods converged, with TACO showing slightly lower SR, precision, and recall, attributed to SOS becoming overly conservative in poorly constrained scenarios. This study indicated that SOS reduces the complexity of tuning `s`.

#### 4.5. Ablation Study: Effect of Parameter `M` (SOS Batch Size)

- The SR for TACO remained consistently above 80% across varying `M` values, confirming SOS's reliability in recovering from degenerate solutions.
- The parameter `M` primarily governed the trade-off between the mean recovery time and the number of recovery operations. Small `M` led to frequent, fast operations on small subgraphs, while large `M` resulted in less frequent, slower operations on larger subgraphs.
- The mean convergence time remained approximately constant (~43 ms), suggesting that `M` primarily influences the system's reactivity to degraded solutions rather than the quality of the final estimate.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The TACO framework contributes to the field of robotic navigation and mapping by addressing a critical challenge in Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM): the robust handling of outliers in Pose Graph Optimization (PGO). The significance and potential impact of this work can be summarized as follows:

1. **Bridging the Gap between Online Efficiency and Offline Robustness:** TACO demonstrates that it is feasible to achieve high robustness, comparable to state-of-the-art offline methods, while preserving the computational efficiency necessary for online, real-time SLAM systems. This capability is crucial for deploying autonomous systems in dynamic and unstructured environments where rapid decision-making and continuous localization are required.
2. **Enhanced Reliability of SLAM Systems:** By implementing a two-stage "Test and Check" mechanism, TACO provides a more resilient PGO backend. The Incremental Probabilistic Consensus (IPC) component offers fast, online validation of loop closures, while the Switchable Outlier Sanitization (SOS) component acts as a retrospective corrective mechanism, ensuring that even mistakenly accepted outliers are eventually identified and removed. This dual-component architecture contributes to overall system reliability.
3. **Improved Performance in Challenging Environments:** The framework's ability to maintain high success rates (above 90% in 2D and 83% in 3D) across substantial outlier rates (up to 50%) without extensive parameter retuning indicates its robustness to significant perceptual aliasing, which is common in real-world scenarios like urban canyons, repetitive factory floors, or visually degraded conditions.
4. **Practical Deployment Feasibility:** With mean convergence times of approximately 45 ms in 2D and 100 ms in 3D, TACO meets the responsiveness requirements for online robotic applications. This allows SLAM systems to integrate new data and update pose estimates in a timely manner, reducing reliance on outdated information, which is critical for safe and effective navigation.
5. **Refined Evaluation Methodology for Robust SLAM:** The paper highlights and addresses the limitations of traditional SLAM metrics (ATE and RPE) when evaluating robust optimization techniques. By proposing and utilizing metrics like Success Rate, Precision, and Recall, the research encourages a more nuanced and accurate assessment of robust PGO systems, distinguishing between incorrect outlier acceptance and insufficient drift correction.
6. **Foundation for Future Research:** The framework provides a solid foundation for further exploration. Identified future directions, such as developing adaptive mechanisms for tuning parameters (e.g., using Graduated Non-Convexity principles), enhancing trusted subgraph connectivity heuristics, and devising more advanced recovery mechanisms (including the re-inclusion of erroneously rejected inliers), open avenues for continuous improvement and the potential for even greater robustness and efficiency.
7. **Open-Source Contribution:** The release of an open-source implementation of TACO fosters wider adoption, reproducibility of results, and collaborative development within the research community, accelerating progress in robust SLAM.

In summary, TACO represents a significant step towards practical and reliable robust PGO, making advanced SLAM capabilities more accessible for real-world autonomous systems by ensuring high performance without compromising real-time constraints.
