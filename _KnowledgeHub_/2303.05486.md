# Learning Arm-Assisted Fall Damage Reduction and Recovery for Legged Mobile Manipulators

> [!summary] Summary
> A learning-based framework from ETH Zürich enables legged mobile manipulators to reduce fall damage and recover using their robotic arm, employing an asymmetric actor-critic reinforcement learning setup. The system achieves lower base contact impulse and acceleration during falls, demonstrated by a 98.9% success rate for arm-assisted recovery on hardware.

## Problem

- Legged mobile manipulators are susceptible to significant damage during falls, particularly to specialized payloads and the manipulator itself, which limits their widespread practical deployment.
- Traditional fall damage reduction methods often rely on restrictive assumptions like inelastic collisions or specific fall planes, making them less applicable to heavier robots with limited joint velocity.
- Existing fall recovery techniques struggle with unreliable state estimation, uncertain contact conditions after a fall, and challenges in reward-tuning for learning-based approaches, affecting motion smoothness and timely task completion.

## Method

- A generic learning-based approach is developed using an asymmetric actor-critic architecture with Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) to train a control policy for fall damage reduction and recovery.
- The robot's arm is strategically incorporated to both mitigate impact forces during a fall and actively assist the robot in returning to a stable stance configuration.
- A time-invariant actor policy is trained with time-varying task rewards and time-invariant behavior rewards, which simplifies the reward-tuning process while promoting consistent and smooth recovery movements.

## Results

- The policy reduced base contact impulse and the 95th percentile of base acceleration during falls compared to freezing and damping drive baselines, without increasing peak joint internal forces.
- Arm-assisted recovery achieved a **98.9%** success rate in returning to a stable stance (base height 

**0.5** m, max joint velocity 

**0.01** rad/s) and decreased average leg torque consumption by **9.17%** compared to a frozen-arm policy.
- The training pipeline demonstrated adaptability by successfully learning other state-transition tasks like resting and self-righting, and proved robust to reward scaling changes, with validations performed on the ALMA robot hardware.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The asymmetric actor-critic architecture, utilizing privileged observations for the critic, is essential for accurately estimating the value function in finite-horizon tasks with time-varying rewards, even with a minimally-observed actor.
> - A time-invariant actor policy, which does not observe the remaining episode time, leads to more robust and temporally consistent recovery behaviors compared to a time-variant actor that might exploit reward timing for sub-optimal, abrupt actions.
> - Leveraging the robotic arm for fall mitigation and recovery not only significantly increases recovery success rates but also reduces the torque burden on the robot's legs, making the recovery process more efficient.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Legged mobile manipulators possess capabilities for manipulation and traversal of unstructured terrains, suggesting a high potential for various practical applications. However, a significant limitation to their widespread deployment is their susceptibility to damage during falls, particularly to specialized payloads and the manipulator itself. This fragility underscores fall damage reduction and recovery as critical, unresolved challenges in legged robotics.

Previous research into fall damage reduction, often conducted in the context of humanoid robots, commonly formulates the problem as an optimization to minimize parameters such as momentum change, impact force, or acceleration. These methods frequently rely on restrictive assumptions, including inelastic and non-sliding collisions, falling within specific planes (e.g., sagittal or frontal), or assuming highly agile limbs capable of tracking complex contact sequences. These assumptions can limit applicability to real-world scenarios, especially for heavier robots with limited joint velocity.

Similarly, prior work on fall recovery for legged robots often employs planning-based methods that introduce simplifications and heuristics. These approaches can be challenged by unreliable state estimation and uncertain contact conditions following a fall, which further complicate their implementation. Recently, reinforcement learning (RL) has emerged as a promising alternative for fall recovery in legged robots, as it can mitigate the need for many hand-designed heuristics and simplifications. However, existing RL-based recovery methods have faced challenges in reward-tuning, specifically in balancing motion smoothness with the successful completion of the recovery task within a given timeframe.

This work contributes to the field by addressing these limitations. It extends previous learning-based recovery methods by integrating the robot arm to both mitigate fall damage and assist in recovery. The proposed approach aims to develop a generic, learning-based solution that operates with minimal simplifications, thereby enhancing the robustness and applicability of legged mobile manipulators in dynamic and uncertain environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the inherent fragility of legged mobile manipulators when operating in real-world environments. While these robots possess advanced capabilities, the risk of damage to their hardware and payloads during falls significantly restricts their practical deployment. Current emergency response strategies often involve simplifying assumptions or complex, robot-specific engineering, which are not universally applicable or robust. The researchers identify adaptive fall damage reduction and reliable recovery as crucial for extending the operational envelope and reliability of these robots.

The key objectives of this study are:

- **Develop a Generic Learning-Based Approach:** To create a generalizable, learning-based framework for reducing fall damage and facilitating recovery in legged mobile manipulators, aiming to minimize reliance on restrictive assumptions or simplifications often found in traditional methods.
- **Leverage Robot Arm for Fall Mitigation and Recovery:** To extend existing learning-based recovery techniques by specifically incorporating the robot's arm as an adaptive tool for both absorbing impact during falls and actively assisting in the recovery process. This aims to utilize the full kinematic capabilities of mobile manipulators.
- **Train a Time-Invariant Policy with Time-Varying Rewards:** To design and implement a single, time-invariant fall-recovery policy using an asymmetric actor-critic reinforcement learning structure. This approach uses time-varying reward functions to address the challenge of balancing motion smoothness with task completion, thereby simplifying the reward-tuning process.
- **Quantitatively Evaluate Fall Damage Reduction:** To conduct a quantitative comparison of the proposed policy against commonly deployed emergency controllers (freezing and damping drives). The evaluation focuses on critical damage criteria, including peak instantaneous impulse on the base, peak joint internal forces, and base acceleration during a fall.
- **Demonstrate Adaptive Fall Recovery:** To validate, through simulated and hardware experiments, the policy's ability to adaptively use the arm to halt a fall and perform arm-assisted recovery, returning the robot to a stable stance configuration within a defined time horizon. Hardware validation on the ALMA robot is a key component.
- **Conduct Ablation Studies:** To investigate the impact of different observation configurations for the actor and critic on policy performance and behavior, particularly examining the differences between time-variant and time-invariant actor policies and the benefits of a privileged critic.
- **Demonstrate Adaptability to Other Tasks:** To show that the core training pipeline can be easily modified to learn other state-transition tasks, such as transitioning to a resting configuration from a stance or self-righting from a completely fallen state, further demonstrating the generality of the method.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a learning-based approach to train a control policy for fall damage reduction and recovery, utilizing an asymmetric actor-critic architecture with Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). The task is formulated as a finite-horizon Markov Decision Process (MDP).

**Training Pipeline Overview:** The policy is trained to reduce fall damage from randomized initial falling configurations and recover to a stable stance within a fixed time horizon. Rewards are provided at the end of each episode, primarily based on minimizing damage criteria and achieving a stance posture.

**State Initialization and Rollout:**
The system design assumes that task-specific controllers detect falls and initiate the fall-recovery policy. To account for various fall scenarios, the robot's initial base and joint states are randomized. Additionally, joint actuators are disabled for a random duration (0.04 s to 1.50 s) at the beginning of each episode. This simulates delayed fall detection and ensures the policy learns to react from challenging, pre-fall configurations, including those where the robot has already partially or fully fallen. Episodes conclude at the end of the MDP's time horizon.

**Asymmetric Actor-Critic Architecture:**
The learning process uses an asymmetric actor-critic setup to improve value function estimation for the critic while maintaining a deployable actor policy.
- **Actor Observation:** The actor receives minimal robot state observations, including base orientation, base angular velocity, and joint states. Base linear velocity is intentionally excluded due to its high uncertainty during and after a fall. To enhance robustness against real-world sensor noise, Gaussian noise is added to the actor's observations during training. Crucially, the actor's policy is designed to be time-invariant, meaning it does not observe the remaining time in the episode.
- **Critic Observation:** The critic receives noiseless versions of the actor's observations, augmented with "privileged observations" available only in simulation. These privileged observations include the remaining time in the episode, foot contact states, a binary flag indicating actuator activation, and the time left in the initial actuator-disabled phase. This privileged information aids the critic in accurately estimating the value function for the finite-horizon MDP.

**Actions:**
The policy's output `a` is scaled by a factor `s` and added to a default joint position `q̃` to compute the target joint position `(s * a + q̃)`. This target is then fed to the robot's joint PD controllers. The default joint positions and action scaling factor are selected to encourage exploration of self-righting behaviors during the initial training phases.

**Reward Function:**
The reward function is composed of time-variant task rewards and time-invariant behavior rewards, designed to minimize damage and promote recovery.
- **Time-Variant Task Rewards:** These rewards are only active during the final 2.0 seconds of each episode to focus recovery efforts towards the end of the horizon.
 - **Base Height:** Rewards are provided for achieving a torso height of at least 0.5 m.
 - **Joint Position:** Penalties are applied for deviations from the ALMA robot's default stance joint positions.
 - **Base Orientation:** Rewards are given for aligning the base orientation, specifically by penalizing roll and pitch angles.
- **Time-Invariant Behavior Rewards:** These rewards are active throughout the episode (when actuators are enabled) to encourage smooth and damage-minimizing behaviors.
 - **Body Collision:** Penalizes high contact forces on specified robot links (shanks, thighs, base, arm links).
 - **Momentum Change:** Penalizes large changes in body momentum.
 - **Body Yank:** Penalizes rapid changes in contact forces.
 - **Action Rate, Joint Velocity, Torques, Acceleration:** Additional regularization terms are included to promote smooth motions and prevent excessive joint velocities, torques, and accelerations.
 During the initial phase when actuators are disabled, all task and behavior rewards are set to zero as the policy has no control.

**Sim-to-Real Transfer Techniques:**
To facilitate the transfer of the learned policy from simulation to the ALMA robot hardware, several techniques were employed:
- **Actuator Model:** A specific actuator model for the leg drives was incorporated into the simulator. For the arm drives, which are pseudo-direct drives with higher transparency, a simpler approach involving randomization of friction and addition of torque delays was used.
- **Terrain Randomization:** Training environments featured uneven terrain to introduce variability in ground contact normal directions and encourage the policy to maintain adequate ground clearance.
- **Observation Noise:** Gaussian noise was consistently added to the actor's observations during training to make the policy robust against sensor noise encountered on the physical robot.
- **Robot Randomization:** The robot's base mass was randomized (uniform distribution between -5 kg and +5 kg additional mass), and friction coefficients for robot bodies were varied across training episodes.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents findings from both simulated experiments and hardware validations on the ALMA robot, demonstrating the policy's effectiveness in fall damage reduction and recovery, as well as its robustness and adaptability.

**A. Fall Damage Reduction**
The policy's performance in fall damage reduction was evaluated against two baseline emergency controllers (freezing and damping drives) across 2560 test episodes with randomized initial fall configurations and base masses.
- **Base Contact Impulse:** The proposed policy exhibited a reduction in time steps with base contact impulses exceeding 0.05 Ns compared to baseline controllers (Fig. 5a). The damping controller showed two distinct peaks, while the freezing controller presented a flat tail in the distribution, indicating a non-negligible probability of high impulse events.
- **Base Acceleration:** The 95th percentile of base acceleration during a fall was significantly lower for the learning-based policy compared to both baseline controllers (Fig. 5b), suggesting a reduction in peak impact forces.
- **Peak Joint Internal Forces:** The policy demonstrated a marginal improvement in the distribution of peak joint internal forces compared to the damping controller and a previously developed fall-recovery controller. A significant decrease in peak internal forces was observed relative to the freezing strategy (Fig. 6a, 6b).
Overall, the proposed method reduced base contact impulse and acceleration without increasing peak internal joint forces.

**B. Fall Recovery**
The policy learned to adaptively utilize the robot's arm for both fall mitigation and recovery.
- **Adaptive Arm Use:** An example illustrated the policy's adaptive behavior: after an initial unsuccessful quick recovery attempt where the robot lost balance, it adapted its strategy by using the arm and a front knee to stabilize the fall. Subsequently, the robot pushed against the ground with its arm to regain an upright posture (Fig. 4).
- **Recovery Success Rate:** The arm-assisted recovery policy achieved a 98.9% success rate in returning to a stable stance (base height ≥ 0.5 m, max joint velocity ≤ 0.01 rad/s), outperforming a baseline learning-based controller with a tugged (frozen) arm, which achieved 95.2% success.
- **Leg Torque Consumption:** Arm-assisted recovery resulted in a 9.17% reduction in average leg torque consumption across all leg drives compared to the arm-tugged policy (Fig. 7), indicating a more efficient recovery process.

**C. Ablation for Observation Configurations**
Ablation studies were conducted to compare different observation configurations for the asymmetric actor-critic setup.
- **Privileged Critic Importance:** A configuration where the critic did not observe episode progress or privileged robot states failed to learn the task, achieving a mean episode return of -3.88 and a high value error of 0.0902. This indicated the non-privileged critic's inability to accurately evaluate the time-variant value function. In contrast, the proposed configuration with a privileged critic achieved a mean episode return of 12.9 and a significantly lower value error of 0.00379.
- **Time-Variant vs. Time-Invariant Actor:** Incorporating the remaining episode time into the actor's observation (time-variant actor) did not substantially alter the overall episode return (12.9) but led to an undesirable recovery behavior. This policy often settled in an intermediate state, resting on joint limits, and then rapidly reorganized its legs just before task rewards were activated (Fig. 8a). This behavior, while optimizing rewards, resulted in non-uniform motion and reduced resilience to failed attempts. The proposed time-invariant actor, lacking knowledge of episode time, exhibited more temporally consistent recovery behavior, always attempting to recover (Fig. 8b), which is preferable for practical deployment.
- **Asymmetric Actor-Critic vs. Privileged Policy:** The proposed asymmetric actor-critic setup (non-privileged, time-invariant actor) yielded a 3.0% lower mean episode return compared to a fully privileged policy where the actor also had access to privileged information. However, the fully privileged policy is not directly deployable on hardware and would require an additional distillation step.

**D. Reproducibility and Adaptation to Other Tasks**
The method demonstrated robustness to reward scaling and adaptability to new tasks.
- **Reward Scaling Robustness:** Tripling the time-variant task rewards did not significantly alter the recovery profile, indicating the robustness of the time-based reward scheme and the policy's ability to optimize behavior rewards while still satisfying task objectives (Fig. 8c).
- **Task Adaptability:** The training pipeline was successfully modified to train policies for "resting" (transitioning to a ground resting configuration) and "self-righting" (from a fallen state to a default resting position). For both tasks, the pipeline discovered natural contact sequences that minimized impact while achieving the desired final configuration. Hardware validations for these tasks were also performed.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of legged robotics by providing a robust and adaptive solution for fall damage reduction and recovery in legged mobile manipulators. The findings have several implications and potential impacts:

- **Enhanced Robot Durability and Reliability:** The demonstrated ability to significantly reduce base contact impulse, base acceleration, and peak joint internal forces during a fall directly translates to less mechanical stress and potential damage to the robot and its payload. This enhances the overall durability and reliability of legged mobile manipulators, reducing maintenance costs and downtime.
- **Expanded Operational Domains:** By addressing one of the major limitations for deploying legged mobile manipulators in dynamic and unstructured environments—the risk of fall-induced damage—this work facilitates their use in a wider range of applications, including industrial inspection, disaster response, and field exploration, where falls are a common occurrence.
- **Improved Recovery Efficiency and Success:** The arm-assisted recovery strategy not only increases the success rate of recovery maneuvers but also reduces the torque burden on the robot's legs. This could lead to more energy-efficient recovery operations and potentially extend the lifespan of leg actuators.
- **Advancements in Learning-Based Control:** The novel application of an asymmetric actor-critic with time-varying rewards for training a time-invariant policy provides a method for learning complex, contact-rich behaviors. This approach addresses the challenge of reward tuning in RL for such tasks, offering a more resilient and deployable control strategy compared to traditional RL or model-based methods.
- **Generalizable Skill Acquisition:** The demonstrated adaptability of the training pipeline to learn various state-transition tasks (e.g., resting, self-righting) by only modifying high-level settings suggests a generic framework for developing diverse robot skills. This generalizability could reduce the development effort and time required to equip robots with new behaviors.
- **Stronger Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The comprehensive set of sim-to-real transfer techniques employed, including detailed actuator models, extensive randomization (terrain, robot parameters), and observation noise, highlights best practices for bridging the gap between simulation-trained policies and their successful deployment on physical hardware. This is crucial for practical robotic applications.
- **Foundational Work for Future Resilience:** This research lays a foundation for future work on robot resilience, particularly in scenarios where damage might lead to dysfunctional components. Extending the policy to adapt to such unforeseen damages, as noted by the authors, represents a logical next step that could further enhance robot autonomy in unpredictable environments.

In summary, this research advances the state of the art in robust control for legged mobile manipulators, making them more resilient to failures and therefore more viable for complex real-world tasks.
