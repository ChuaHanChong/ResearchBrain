# Real2Sim2Real for Vision-Language-Action Manipulation: An AMD ROCm-Based Pipeline

> [!summary] Summary
> An end-to-end embodied-manipulation stack running entirely on AMD ROCm plus PyTorch, spanning Radeon PRO simulation GPUs and Ryzen AI edge silicon. Four demos: Genesis-simulated grasping data training a SmolVLA policy deployed on a real Franka arm, language-grounded object selection, a Real2Sim pipeline fusing 3D Gaussian Splatting scenes with Genesis physics, and legged-locomotion RL benchmarked across hardware.

## Problem

- Vision-language-action training and deployment pipelines are widely assumed to need CUDA-locked toolchains, leaving non-NVIDIA silicon untested for full embodied stacks.
- Purely synthetic simulation assets carry a distinctive rendered look, widening the visual sim-to-real gap for manipulation policies trained on them.
- Cross-hardware reinforcement-learning throughput for legged robots is usually reported as one aggregate number, hiding per-algorithm and host-CPU bottlenecks.

## Method

- ==Sim-to-real loop==: Genesis physics generates grasp demonstrations, converted to LeRobot dataset format, used to fine-tune a ==SmolVLA-450M== policy, then deployed closed-loop on a Franka Panda.
- ==Real2Sim pipeline==: ==3D Gaussian Splatting== reconstructs a real scene as static photorealistic background, Genesis injects an articulated Franka and physical objects, exporting multi-view RGB-D, masks, poses, and language annotations.
- ==Domain randomization controls== over layout, material, lighting, viewpoint, initial pose, and goal augment reconstructed scenes; Unilab trains Go2 and G1 locomotion with ==FastSAC==, ==FlashSAC==, and PPO.

## Results

- SmolVLA-450M fine-tuning converged in roughly **7-11 minutes** over **4,000** steps with peak VRAM under **2.4GB**, and the whole synthetic-to-real Franka loop finished in under **one hour**.
- Synthetic data generation hit **100%** success on the seeding grasp distribution, and the simulation-only policy transferred to the physical arm with no real-world fine-tuning.
- RL throughput is task-dependent: RTX 4090 led PPO G1 Flip at **109.0** steps/s but trailed at **6.0** on FlashSAC Go2 Joystick, where the integrated AI MAX 395 reached **4.2** versus **1.1**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Desktop-class GPUs suffice for compact VLA policies, so a sub-2.4GB training footprint lets simulation, training, and validation share one machine instead of a cluster.
> - Hybrid scenes with reconstructed static backgrounds and procedurally physical dynamic objects give visual realism and cheap unlimited rollouts simultaneously.
> - Legged RL is partly host-bound, so unified-memory edge parts can beat discrete GPUs, and benchmarks must be read per algorithm.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research positions itself within the evolving trajectory of artificial intelligence, specifically focusing on the emergent domain of "Physical AI." The authors define Physical AI as systems capable of perceiving, reasoning, and acting within the physical world, distinguishing it from prior AI paradigms like Generative AI and Agentic AI, which primarily operate in digital contexts. This shift is presented as a significant frontier in AI development, supported by references to industry figures.

The computational demands of Physical AI are highlighted as considerably higher than previous AI waves. These demands include:
- Massive parallel computation for photorealistic and physically accurate simulation environments.
- Large-memory accelerators for the training of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies.
- Low-latency, memory-efficient inference capabilities for deployment directly on or near robotic agents at the edge.

Within this landscape, the paper presents an alternative and comprehensive technology stack. A key aspect of this work is its demonstration that the training and deployment of VLA-based manipulation policies do not necessitate a proprietary, CUDA-locked ecosystem. Instead, it proposes and validates an end-to-end solution built entirely on AMD's hardware portfolio and its open ROCm software stack. This contributes to the broader research landscape by offering a validated, open-source-compatible platform that aims to lower the barriers to entry for Physical AI research and development, fostering rapid iteration and community involvement outside of single-vendor ecosystems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce and validate an end-to-end technology stack for embodied manipulation that is fully accelerated by AMD hardware and unified by the open ROCm software stack. This encompasses the entire pipeline from data center training to edge deployment.

The motivation for this work stems from several factors:
- **The Emergence of Physical AI:** The paper explicitly states that Physical AI is the "next major frontier for AI," characterized by the integration of large VLA models with embodied agents interacting in the real world. This necessitates a robust and comprehensive computational infrastructure.
- **Addressing Computational Demands:** Physical AI imposes substantial computational requirements across simulation, VLA policy training, and edge inference. The authors are motivated to demonstrate that AMD's diverse hardware portfolio—including Data Center Series GPUs, Radeon PRO Series GPUs for simulation and rendering, and Ryzen AI Series for edge compute—is well-suited to meet these demands across the entire pipeline.
- **Promoting an Open Ecosystem:** A core motivation is to challenge the perception that Physical AI development is reliant on a single, proprietary hardware and software ecosystem. By showcasing a complete AMD-accelerated pipeline, the paper aims to prove that a "CUDA-locked ecosystem" is not a prerequisite for training and deploying VLA-based manipulation policies. This objective is driven by a desire to foster an open and hardware-agnostic environment for AI research.
- **Enabling Rapid Iteration:** Physical AI research often involves numerous simulation-training-deployment cycles to bridge the sim-to-real gap. The authors are motivated to provide an open, unified compute stack that facilitates rapid iteration, thereby accelerating progress in the field.
- **Validation and Reproducibility:** The paper aims to functionally and quantitatively demonstrate the capabilities of the AMD stack through a series of progressive applications, while also emphasizing reproducibility by making all pipelines available on a free cloud platform.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a demonstrative approach, presenting four progressive technology demonstrations that collectively illustrate an end-to-end Physical AI pipeline built exclusively on AMD's hardware and the ROCm software stack. All pipelines are stated to run natively on ROCm + PyTorch on RDNA4 (Radeon AI PRO R9700) and RDNA3.5 (Radeon PRO W7900) hardware, and are designed for reproducibility on the Radeon Cloud Platform.

The methodology is structured around these four demonstrations:

#### 3.1. Demo 1: Sim-to-Real Manipulation

- **Objective:** To showcase an end-to-end VLA workflow from robot simulation to policy training and real-world deployment.
- **Approach:**
 1. **Synthetic Data Generation:** Robot demonstrations for grasping and pick-and-place tasks are generated within the Genesis physics simulator.
 2. **Dataset Conversion:** The simulated demonstration data is converted into the LeRobot dataset format.
 3. **Policy Training:** A SmolVLA policy is trained using this dataset entirely on the AMD platform.
 4. **Deployment:** The trained policy is deployed to a physical Franka robotic arm for closed-loop manipulation in the real world.
- **Validation:** The pipeline is validated for grasping and continuous pick-and-place tasks, observing performance and resource utilization.

#### 3.2. Demo 2: One-of-Three, Semantic Select

- **Objective:** To evaluate the language-conditioning capabilities of the VLA policy family in scenarios requiring semantic grounding.
- **Approach:**
 1. **Builds on Demo 1:** This demonstration utilizes the same underlying SmolVLA backbone and ROCm training pipeline established in Demo 1.
 2. **Task Design:** A Franka arm is presented with multiple candidate objects on a table. The policy is instructed in natural language to select and place a specific target object while ignoring distractors.
- **Focus:** This task specifically stresses the VLA policy's ability to fuse language embeddings with visual scene information to resolve reference ambiguity.

#### 3.3. Demo 3: Real2Sim Synthetic Training Data Pipelines

- **Objective:** To create a pipeline that reconstructs real environments and integrates them into simulation for generating photorealistic and physically interactive training data at scale.
- **Approach:**
 1. **3DGS Reconstruction:** AMD Radeon GPUs and the ROCm stack are used to perform high-fidelity 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) reconstruction of a real environment, producing a real-time renderable Gaussian scene representation.
 2. **Integration with Physics Engine:** The 3DGS Gaussian point cloud is loaded into the Genesis simulator as a static, photorealistic background. A Franka Emika Panda arm and interactive objects, endowed with physical properties (mass, friction, collision geometry), are introduced into this scene.
 3. **Automated Data Generation:** Genesis’s physics simulation automatically generates VLA-relevant training signals for each rollout. These signals include end-effector trajectories, multi-view RGB images, depth maps, segmentation masks, end-effector poses, joint states, and discrete/continuous actions, exported in LeRobot-compatible format. Synchronized language annotations and task descriptions are associated with each trajectory.
 4. **Domain Randomization:** The pipeline incorporates randomization controls over object layout, material/texture, lighting, camera viewpoint, robot initial pose, and task goal. This aims to increase training sample diversity while preserving the visual fidelity of the real-world scene.
- **Contribution:** This hybrid approach combines the visual realism of captured scenes with the scalable and controllable data generation of simulation, aiming to narrow the sim-to-real gap.

#### 3.4. Demo 4: RL Training with Unilab on the AMD Platform

- **Objective:** To benchmark AMD’s platform for locomotion-focused reinforcement learning (RL) tasks.
- **Approach:**
 1. **Framework:** The Unilab framework is used for RL training.
 2. **Tasks:** Quadruped (Unitree Go2) and humanoid (Unitree G1) robots are trained in parallel, massively-batched simulations. Tasks include whole-body tracking (WBT), walking, joystick-commanded locomotion, and a dynamic flip maneuver.
 3. **Algorithms:** A mix of RL algorithms, including FastSAC, FlashSAC, and PPO, are used, matched to task characteristics.
 4. **Hardware Benchmarking:** Throughput (steps-per-second) is reported across various hardware configurations: RTX 4090 (baseline), RTX 4090 + AMD 9950X3D, AMD 8060S + AI MAX 395, and Apple M5 Max.
- **Analysis:** The performance is analyzed to understand hardware-software interactions, algorithm-specific sensitivities, and the impact of unified memory architectures.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents findings from four demonstrations, collectively substantiating the capabilities of the AMD hardware and software stack for Physical AI.

#### 4.1. Demo 1: Sim-to-Real Manipulation

- **Functional Validation:** The end-to-end VLA workflow for robot simulation, policy training, and real-world deployment was successfully validated. Autonomous object grasping in the Genesis simulator achieved a 100% success rate for the training task distribution. The trained policy successfully transferred to a physical Franka robotic arm for real-world object grasping without additional real-world fine-tuning. A continuous pick-and-place task was also demonstrated in simulation.
- **Performance Metrics:** Fine-tuning the SmolVLA-450M backbone converged in approximately 7–11 minutes for 4,000 training steps. Peak VRAM usage was observed to be under 2.4GB, indicating that policy training, simulation rollout, and validation can be performed on a single desktop-class Radeon PRO GPU. The entire loop, from synthetic demonstration generation to real-Franka deployment, was completed in under one hour.

#### 4.2. Demo 2: One-of-Three, Semantic Select

- **Semantic Grounding:** This demonstration validated the language-conditioning branch of the VLA policy. The model successfully fused natural-language instructions with visual scene information to identify and select specific objects among visually similar distractors.
- **Resource Efficiency:** Since the underlying SmolVLA backbone and ROCm training pipeline were shared with Demo 1, this task required no additional infrastructure cost. The same 450M-parameter checkpoint and sub-2.4GB VRAM training footprint were utilized.

#### 4.3. Demo 3: Real2Sim Synthetic Training Data Pipelines

- **Hybrid Data Generation:** The pipeline successfully demonstrated the automated generation of photorealistic, physically-interactive robot training data by fusing 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) reconstructions of real scenes with the Genesis physics engine. This approach preserved the geometry and appearance of real environments while allowing for controllable physical interactions and domain randomization.
- **Unified Compute:** The pipeline confirmed that diverse computational tasks—including 3DGS reconstruction, neural rendering, physics simulation, and VLA model training—could all be executed on the same Radeon PRO GPU via the ROCm software stack, without the need for transitions to separate, specialized toolchains. This demonstrated the robustness of AMD's unified compute architecture for heterogeneous workloads.

#### 4.4. Demo 4: RL Training with Unilab on the AMD Platform

- **Task-Dependent Throughput:** Throughput, measured in steps-per-second, was found to be highly dependent on the specific task and RL algorithm rather than solely on the hardware platform. For instance, the RTX 4090 baseline led significantly on the PPO/G1 Flip task (109.0 steps/s) but was the slowest configuration for FlashSAC/Go2 Joy (6.0 steps/s). This suggests that algorithm-specific CPU/GPU synchronization and batch-size sensitivity are influential factors.
- **CPU Impact on Discrete GPU Performance:** The configuration pairing an NVIDIA discrete GPU (RTX 4090) with an AMD CPU (9950X3D) consistently underperformed the pure RTX 4090 baseline across all four tasks. This result indicates that RL training in Unilab can be at least partially CPU/host-bound (e.g., due to simulation stepping, environment resets, or data transfer), implying that CPU choice materially affects end-to-end throughput.
- **Unified Memory Architecture Advantage:** The integrated AMD Ryzen AI MAX 395 (featuring an 8060S iGPU and a unified memory architecture, UMA) delivered competitive, and in some cases superior, throughput compared to the discrete-GPU-plus-AMD-CPU configuration. This performance was attributed to the UMA design's elimination of host-device copy overhead, which provides a notable advantage for RL workloads characterized by tight simulate-train loops.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presents several areas of significance and potential impact within the field of Physical AI.

- **Validation of an Open-Source Ecosystem:** The primary significance lies in the comprehensive demonstration that an end-to-end Physical AI pipeline—encompassing 3D reconstruction, neural rendering, physics simulation, VLA policy training, reinforcement learning, and real-robot deployment—can be built and operated entirely on AMD’s open hardware and software stack. This provides a validated alternative to proprietary, CUDA-locked ecosystems, which has the potential to broaden access to advanced AI development tools.
- **Lowering Barriers to Entry:** By providing an open compute stack (ROCm + HIP, upstreamed into PyTorch and JAX, and portable from CUDA) and making all demonstrated pipelines reproducible free of charge on the Radeon Cloud Platform, the work aims to lower the barrier for researchers and developers to engage with Physical AI. This increased accessibility can foster community growth, collaborative development, and accelerated innovation.
- **Addressing the Sim-to-Real Gap:** The paper contributes to solving the sim-to-real gap through two complementary approaches:
 - The Sim-to-Real manipulation demo demonstrates that policies trained with simple procedural randomization in simulation can directly transfer to physical robots for basic tasks.
 - The Real2Sim pipeline offers a more systematic method to bridge this gap by combining the visual fidelity of real-world captures (via 3DGS) with the scalable, physically interactive data generation of simulation. This hybrid approach has the potential to produce more diverse and realistic training data, which can lead to more robust policies that generalize better to real-world deployment.
- **Unified Compute for Heterogeneous Workloads:** The successful execution of diverse, compute-bound tasks (neural scene representations, physics simulation, VLA model training) on a single Radeon PRO GPU via ROCm signifies that AMD’s platform can serve as a unified compute environment. This eliminates the need for complex hand-offs between different hardware or software stacks, streamlining the development workflow for complex embodied AI systems.
- **Broadening Application Scope:** The demonstrations cover both manipulation and locomotion tasks, showcasing the versatility of the AMD platform across different facets of embodied intelligence. This indicates that the proposed stack is not limited to a narrow set of applications but can support a wider range of Physical AI challenges.
- **Edge-to-Cloud Continuum for AI:** The competitive performance of the Ryzen AI MAX platform for RL training, despite being an edge-class part, highlights its potential beyond just inference. This reinforces AMD's ability to provide an integrated solution spanning data center, workstation, and edge computing, facilitating seamless development and deployment across the entire AI hardware continuum.
- **Future Infrastructure for Physical AI:** The authors position their work as essential infrastructure for embodied intelligence research at scale, particularly as the field progresses towards Physical AI. The emphasis on an open, iterative, and hardware-diverse compute stack is presented as a fundamental requirement for advancing this new frontier.
