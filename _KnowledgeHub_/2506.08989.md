# SwS: Self-aware Weakness-driven Problem Synthesis in Reinforcement Learning for LLM Reasoning

> [!summary] Summary
> The SwS framework enhances Large Language Model reasoning by enabling self-aware, weakness-driven problem synthesis for Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR). It generates targeted, high-quality training data, leading to substantial performance gains on mathematical benchmarks compared to baseline RL.

## Problem

- High scarcity and cost of acquiring high-quality, human-labeled, verifiable data for training Large Language Models (LLMs) via Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR).
- Existing synthetic datasets are often suboptimal for RLVR due to a lack of rigorous verification for reference answers, which are crucial for generating robust training signals.
- Inefficient problem augmentation strategies that indiscriminately expand datasets, resulting in problems that are either too trivial (no learning signal) or too challenging (gradient vanishing), hindering effective RL training.

## Method

- The SwS framework employs a ==three-stage pipeline: self-aware weakness identification, targeted problem synthesis, and augmented RLVR training==.
- It identifies an LLM's weaknesses by analyzing consistently failed problems during a preliminary RL training phase, classifying problems with ==low and decreasing accuracy== as targets.
- New problems are generated by ==extracting and recombining concepts from failed problems==, with the augmentation budget dynamically allocated based on category-specific failure rates, and then rigorously filtered for quality, verifiable answers, and appropriate difficulty.

## Results

- SwS consistently yielded average absolute performance improvements of **+10.0%** for 7B models and **+7.7%** for 32B models across a range of mathematical reasoning benchmarks (e.g., GSM8K, MATH, AIME).
- The framework effectively mitigated identified weaknesses, showing significant gains of **+20%** in 'Intermediate Algebra' and **+5%** in 'Geometry' for previously failed problems.
- Ablation studies confirmed that 'Medium' and 'Hard' synthetic problems provided more sustained and significant performance gains compared to 'Simple' problems, highlighting the importance of appropriate challenge levels.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Consistently failed problems in initial RL training reveal specific model weaknesses that can be systematically leveraged to guide more efficient and focused learning.
> - Targeted problem synthesis, by extracting and recombining concepts from identified weaknesses, creates high-quality, difficulty-tailored training data that significantly enhances an LLM's reasoning abilities and generalization.
> - Rigorous filtering of synthetic problems based on solvability and appropriate difficulty (e.g., accuracy within 25%-75%) ensures that the generated data provides meaningful gradient signals and prevents issues like gradient vanishing.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is situated at the intersection of Large Language Models (LLMs) and Reinforcement Learning (RL), specifically focusing on enhancing LLM reasoning capabilities, such as mathematical problem solving, through Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR).

#### 1.1. Current State of RLVR for LLMs

RLVR has emerged as a highly effective paradigm for training LLMs on complex reasoning tasks, where simple rule-based rewards derived from verifiable answers can induce sophisticated reasoning skills. Unlike Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), which often relies on rote memorization of reasoning patterns, RL trains models on their own generated outputs, fostering stronger generalization and preventing issues like hallucinated rationales. Recent algorithms like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) have demonstrated robust performance in these applications. The success of RLVR, however, critically depends on the availability of high-quality, diverse, and precisely verifiable problem sets.

#### 1.2. Limitations of Existing Data Augmentation

Despite the promise of RLVR, the paper identifies three key limitations in the existing landscape that hinder its scalability and effectiveness:
1. **Scarcity of High-Quality Human-Labeled Data:** Well-crafted mathematical problems with precise, verifiable answers are expensive and time-consuming to collect, limiting the scale of RLVR training.
2. **Suboptimal Synthetic Datasets:** Most existing synthetic datasets, often created for SFT distillation, lack the rigorous verification of reference answers required for robust RLVR. The correctness of the final answer is paramount for RL training signals, making these datasets less effective.
3. **Inefficient Problem Augmentation Strategies:** Current problem synthesis methods often involve indiscriminate expansion of the problem set without considering the model’s specific capabilities. This leads to generating problems that are either too trivial (providing no learning signal as the model has mastered them) or too challenging (leading to consistent failures and gradient vanishing, thus no useful learning signal). Such inefficient data generation wastes computational resources and limits model improvement.

The authors highlight that these indiscriminate strategies fail to align the difficulty of training tasks with the model's current abilities, a crucial aspect for efficient RL training. This gap motivates the need for a more self-aware and targeted approach to problem synthesis.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core motivation behind this research stems from the understanding that while consistently failed problems in RL training represent challenges, they also inherently reveal the model's specific weaknesses. Leveraging these weaknesses can guide more efficient and focused learning. The paper poses a central research question: "How can we effectively utilize these consistently failed cases to address the model’s reasoning deficiencies? Could they be systematically leveraged for data synthesis that targets the enhancement of the model’s weakest capabilities?"

To address this, the key objectives of the "Self-aware Weakness-driven Problem Synthesis" (SwS) framework are:
1. **Systematically Identify Model Deficiencies:** To develop a mechanism to pinpoint specific areas where the LLM consistently fails or learns inefficiently during preliminary RL training.
2. **Leverage Weaknesses for Targeted Problem Augmentation:** To extract the core concepts from these identified failure cases and use them as a basis for synthesizing new, relevant problems.
3. **Tailor Problem Difficulty and Allocation:** To generate new problems with difficulty levels specifically tailored to the model’s current capabilities and to allocate augmentation budget across different problem categories based on the model’s relative performance in those areas.
4. **Enhance Robust Generalization in RL:** To enable the LLM to self-identify and progressively overcome its weaknesses through targeted, augmented training, thereby improving its overall reasoning abilities and generalization without relying on external knowledge distillation.

By achieving these objectives, SwS aims to create a more efficient and effective RL training paradigm for LLMs, moving beyond brute-force data expansion to a smart, adaptive, and self-improving approach.

### 3. Methodology and Approach (SwS Framework)

The SwS framework is designed as a three-stage pipeline that generates targeted training samples to enhance an LLM's reasoning capabilities within the RLVR paradigm.

#### 3.1. Self-aware Weakness Identification

The process begins with a preliminary RL training phase on an initial problem set (e.g., MATH-12k). During this phase, the policy model itself is used to identify its weakest capabilities. For each problem, the model's average accuracy across responses at each training epoch is recorded. A problem is classified as a "weakness" (i.e., a consistently failed problem) if it meets two criteria:
1. The model never achieves a response accuracy of 50% at any training epoch.
2. The accuracy trend for that problem decreases over time, indicated by a negative slope.
These identified failed problems form a "failed set," which serves as the seed for subsequent problem synthesis, as they represent areas where the model struggles to learn effectively despite iterative sampling during RL.

#### 3.2. Targeted Problem Synthesis

This stage focuses on generating new problems that specifically target the identified weaknesses:
- **Concept Extraction and Recombination:** The underlying concepts from the failed problems are extracted and categorized by mathematical domain (e.g., Algebra, Geometry). To ensure coherence and semantic fluency, these concepts are strategically sampled and recombined based on co-occurrence probabilities and embedding similarities. The augmentation budget (total number of synthetic problems) is dynamically allocated across different categories based on the model’s category-specific failure rates from the preliminary training phase. This ensures that categories where the model is weakest receive more targeted synthetic problems.
- **Problem Generation and Quality Verification:** A strong instruction model (e.g., LLaMA-3.3-70B-Instruct) is then prompted to generate new problems using the recombined concepts and category labels. Instructions are provided to ensure the problems are suitable for RLVR (e.g., no multiple-choice, multi-part, or proof-based questions). The generated problems undergo rigorous quality verification by additional general instruction LLMs, which assess concept coverage, factual accuracy, and solvability, assigning ratings of "bad," "acceptable," or "perfect." Only problems with high "perfect" ratings and no "bad" ratings are retained.
- **Reference Answer Generation:** Given the critical importance of verifiable answers in RLVR, a strong reasoning model (e.g., QwQ-32B) generates multiple responses for each synthetic problem using a self-consistency paradigm. Answers are verified for equivalence using Math-Verify, and only problems with at least 50% consistent answers are retained, ensuring reliable ground truth for training.
- **Difficulty Filtering:** To prevent issues of gradient vanishing (where all responses are either perfectly correct or perfectly incorrect), problems of "appropriate difficulty" are selected. The initially trained model samples multiple responses for each synthetic problem, and only those whose accuracy falls within a target range (e.g., [25%, 75%]) are retained. This ensures that the model is exposed to learnable problems that provide meaningful gradient signals.

#### 3.3. Augmented Training and RL Setup

The filtered, high-quality synthetic problems are then incorporated into the initial training set, forming an augmented training set. The initially trained LLM then undergoes a second stage of augmented RLVR training on this combined dataset. The RL algorithm used is Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), adapted for efficiency by omitting the KL term and incorporating clip-higher, token-level loss, and dynamic sampling strategies. Policy updates are performed only on prompts where the online policy model's accuracy falls within a specified range (e.g., 10% to 90%) to maximize training signal utility.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted across Qwen2.5-base models ranging from 3B to 32B parameters demonstrate the effectiveness and robustness of the SwS framework.

#### 4.1. Overall Performance Improvements

SwS consistently yields significant performance gains across a wide range of mathematical reasoning benchmarks (GSM8K, MATH-500, Minerva Math, Olympiad-Bench, Gaokao-2023, AMC23, AIME24, AIME25). The 7B and 32B models, when trained with SwS, achieved substantial average absolute improvements of +10.0% and +7.7% respectively, compared to their baseline RL counterparts. Notably, SwS-enhanced models often surpassed even sophisticated models trained on carefully curated human-labeled problem sets, highlighting the quality and targeted nature of the synthetic data. For instance, the Qwen2.5-7B model showed impressive gains of +16.7% on AIME24 and +13.3% on AIME25, indicating that the synthetic problems effectively target the model's weaknesses even on competition-level tasks.

#### 4.2. Effectiveness of Weakness Mitigation

A key analysis using the Qwen2.5-7B model demonstrated the direct impact of SwS on mitigating identified weaknesses. After training on the augmented problem set, the model was able to solve a greater proportion of previously failed problems across most domains. The most significant gains were observed in "Intermediate Algebra" (+20%), "Geometry" (+5%), and "Precalculus" (+5%), which were identified as the model's weakest areas. This improvement occurred despite each original problem being sampled four times less frequently in the augmented set, underscoring the efficiency and targeted nature of SwS-generated synthetic problems.

#### 4.3. Insights from Extended Applications

The paper further explores the adaptability of SwS through several extensions:
- **Weak-to-Strong Generalization:** The study showed that even a generally weaker teacher model (Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct) could successfully label reference answers for synthetic problems to improve a stronger student model (Qwen2.5-7B-Base). This surprising result is attributed to the "difficulty filtering" stage, which effectively filters out mislabeled or overly complex problems, retaining only those where the weaker teacher is reliable and the stronger student genuinely struggles.
- **Self-evolving Targeted Problem Synthesis:** By enabling the policy model itself to generate synthetic problems (Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct), the framework demonstrated self-improvement, yielding a 1.2% overall performance increase. However, this setting showed limitations on highly challenging benchmarks like AIME. Analysis revealed that the self-evaluating model tends to overestimate the quality and complexity of its own generations, leading to simpler synthetic problems that provide diminishing returns for advanced reasoning.
- **Weakness-driven Selection:** When augmenting the training set by selecting problems from a large external dataset (Big-Math) based on identified weaknesses, the "weakness-driven selection" strategy consistently outperformed random selection. This approach provided richer and more sustained learning signals, preventing the rapid saturation observed with indiscriminately chosen problems.

#### 4.4. Impact of Question Difficulty

An ablation study on the difficulty levels of synthetic problems revealed crucial insights for RL training:
- "Simple" synthetic problems led to faster initial performance gains but quickly saturated, providing no further improvement.
- "Medium" and "Hard" synthetic problems, while resulting in slower initial convergence, led to more sustained and significant performance gains on evaluation benchmarks.
- The hardest problems provided the longest-lasting training benefits, indicating that a continuous supply of appropriately challenging problems is vital for pushing the model's reasoning boundaries.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The SwS framework represents a significant advancement in the training of LLMs for complex reasoning tasks, with broad implications for the field:
- **Enabling Scalable RLVR:** SwS directly addresses the critical bottleneck of data scarcity and quality in RLVR. By automating the generation of high-quality, verifiable, and difficulty-appropriate training data, it paves the way for more scalable and efficient reinforcement learning for LLMs.
- **Promoting Self-Improvement and Adaptive Learning:** The "self-aware" nature of SwS allows LLMs to identify and target their own weaknesses. This shifts the paradigm from passive data consumption to active, adaptive learning, potentially leading to more robust and continuously improving AI systems.
- **Optimizing Training Efficiency:** By focusing computational resources on "learnable" problems—those that are neither too easy nor too hard—SwS significantly improves the stability and efficiency of RLVR training, reducing wasted effort on tasks that yield minimal learning signals.
- **Reducing Reliance on Human Annotation:** The framework automates a substantial portion of the data curation process, decreasing the cost and time associated with creating high-quality, human-labeled datasets for complex tasks.
- **Broader Applicability:** While demonstrated on mathematical reasoning, the core principles of weakness identification, concept extraction, and targeted synthesis could be generalized to other complex domains requiring verifiable reasoning, such as scientific problem-solving, logical inference, or even code generation.

### 6. Limitations and Future Work

The authors acknowledge several limitations and outline directions for future research:
- **Computational Cost:** The framework, especially the problem and answer generation stages, relies on strong instruction and reasoning models, which can be computationally intensive.
- **Focus on RL Setting:** The current framework is primarily designed for RL, aiming to activate inherent reasoning abilities without distilling external knowledge. Future work could explore adapting this pipeline for enhancing capabilities through fine-tuning or distillation.
- **Complexity of Synthetic Problems:** Synthetic problems generated by current open-source instruction models may still lack the complexity required to elicit deeper reasoning capabilities, particularly for the most challenging problems. This was evident in the "Self-evolving" setting. Future work could integrate techniques like Evolve-Instruct to iteratively refine problem difficulty.
- **Advanced Weakness Identification:** The current definition of "weakness" is based on simple answer accuracy. Future research aims to identify model weaknesses from multiple perspectives to further improve sample efficiency.
- **Generalization Beyond Reasoning:** The framework could be extended to more general tasks beyond reasoning by incorporating off-the-shelf reward models, removing the reliance on verifiable answers.
- **Integration with Advanced LLM Architectures:** Implementing SwS with models equipped with Long-CoT (Chain-of-Thought) capabilities could further push the boundaries of open-source large reasoning models.

In conclusion, SwS represents a significant step towards enabling LLMs to learn more effectively and independently by systematically addressing their self-identified reasoning deficiencies, paving the way for more intelligent and adaptable AI systems.
