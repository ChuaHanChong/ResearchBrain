# FILIC: Dual-Loop Force-Guided Imitation Learning with Impedance Torque Control for Contact-Rich Manipulation Tasks

> [!summary] Summary
> FILIC is a force-guided imitation learning framework for contact-rich manipulation. A Transformer (ACT-style) outer-loop policy fuses RGB images, end-effector pose, and estimated contact wrench via cross-attention to predict 6-DOF pose chunks at 25 Hz, while a 2 kHz inner-loop joint-space impedance controller executes them compliantly. A digital-twin Jacobian inversion estimates end-effector force without any force/torque sensor, lifting success from 68% to 90% in simulation.

## Problem

- Conventional imitation policies output positions or poses, inducing excessive internal contact forces that risk damaging both the robot and the manipulated environment.
- Wrist force/torque sensors give richer contact feedback but are costly, scarce, and require extra hardware design, limiting scalability on affordable collaborative arms.
- Existing force-aware policies treat force as an auxiliary input for position control, assume sensor-rich setups, and rarely deliver stable torque-level compliant execution.

## Method

- **FILIC**'s dual-loop force-in force-out architecture: ==ACT==-style Transformer outer loop predicts pose chunks at 25 Hz; joint-space ==spring-damper impedance law== runs at 2 kHz with fixed stiffness and damping gains.
- ==Sensorless end-effector wrench estimator== inverting the ==Jacobian transpose== via SVD pseudo-inverse on the residual between measured torques and torques predicted by a synchronized ==MuJoCo digital twin==.
- Multimodal fusion: dual ==ResNet== image encoders ==cross-attend== to MLP-embedded force features with pose embeddings and a ==CVAE== style latent, fed by a handheld teleoperation rig whose vibrotactile cues encode estimated force magnitude.

## Results

- Simulated peg-in-hole over 50 trials: **68.0%** with pose only, **80.0%** adding raw joint torque, **90.0%** adding estimated end-effector force, a **+22.0 pp** gain over the position baseline.
- Across four real tasks (30 trials each), estimated force beat position-only by **+33.3 pp** plug, **+40.0 pp** E-stop, **+46.6 pp** clamp, **+53.4 pp** bottle cap, reaching **76.6-83.3%**.
- Estimated end-effector force beat raw joint torque under identical architecture and hyperparameters (**+10.0 pp** in simulation, **+10.0 to +23.3 pp** real), with static-load tests bounding Z-axis estimator error against known gravitational loads.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Representation matters more than raw signal availability: projecting joint torques into end-effector wrench space yields a more task-aligned cue than feeding torques directly.
> - A high-fidelity synchronized simulator can substitute for expensive sensing hardware by supplying model-predicted torques as the baseline for residual force estimation.
> - Compliance is better enforced at the torque level beneath the policy than learned in the action space, and haptic feedback during teleoperation makes operators record the corrective force variation that trains it.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation is progressing towards achieving human-like dexterity in complex, unstructured environments, with contact-rich tasks being a foundational component. Imitation Learning (IL) has emerged as a significant paradigm, enabling robots to acquire skills from human demonstrations. However, a notable challenge in current IL methods is their predominant focus on position or pose control, which can lead to excessive internal forces during contact. This can result in damage to the robot, the environment, or the manipulated object.

Efforts to incorporate force awareness into robotic control have historically relied on classical model-based strategies like impedance or admittance control, which provide principled mappings between motion and interaction forces. While effective in structured settings, their performance can degrade in unstructured environments due to modeling errors, hybrid contact dynamics, and the "reality gap" between simulation and real-world conditions. Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) has also been explored, offering solutions for robust locomotion, but applying it to manipulation tasks faces challenges such as sim-to-real transfer, multimodal sensing requirements, and sparse reward structures.

More recent advancements in IL and DRL have begun to integrate force inputs. Some approaches treat force as an auxiliary signal for position control, while others develop policies that operate on 6D wrenches or learn variable impedance/admittance. These methods often require sensor-rich setups, specifically wrist-mounted force/torque (F/T) sensors, which are costly and add hardware complexity, thereby limiting their scalability and accessibility for widespread deployment on more affordable collaborative robot arms. This reliance on expensive hardware represents a barrier to the broader adoption of advanced force-aware manipulation techniques.

FILIC positions itself within this landscape by directly addressing the gap of enabling compliant, force-informed, torque-executed manipulation in low-cost, sensorless settings. It aims to bridge the capabilities of advanced IL policies, multimodal perception, and compliant torque control, while circumventing the need for dedicated wrist F/T sensors. The work integrates a sensorless force estimation method with a dual-loop control architecture, combining a high-level imitation policy with a low-level impedance controller. This approach contributes to making robust and compliant contact-rich manipulation more accessible and practical for a wider range of robotic platforms.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the challenges associated with enabling robots to perform contact-rich manipulation tasks effectively and safely in real-world environments. Such tasks, including assembly, insertion, and in-hand manipulation, are fundamental for advanced robotic autonomy but often require precise force regulation that conventional imitation learning (IL) policies struggle to provide.

The key objectives and motivations are detailed as follows:

1. **Addressing the Limitations of Position-Centric Imitation Learning:**
 - **Motivation:** Standard IL policies typically output joint positions or end-effector poses. While effective for free-space motion, this position-centric approach can lead to significant problems during contact. When a robot, guided by a position-only policy, encounters an obstacle or tries to perform a tight insertion, it may generate excessive internal forces. These forces can damage the robot, the object being manipulated, or the surrounding environment. There is a need for IL policies that explicitly incorporate force awareness to enable safer and more compliant interactions.

2. **Overcoming Hardware Costs and Dependency on Dedicated Force/Torque Sensors:**
 - **Motivation:** Many existing force-aware control and learning methods rely on expensive wrist-mounted force/torque (F/T) sensors to provide direct measurements of interaction forces. The high cost and the need for additional hardware integration and design limit the scalability and widespread adoption of these solutions, particularly for smaller businesses or research groups using more affordable collaborative robots. The objective is to develop a cost-effective alternative that can infer interaction forces without requiring dedicated F/T sensors.

3. **Achieving Stable and Compliant Torque-Level Execution in Low-Cost Settings:**
 - **Motivation:** For successful contact-rich manipulation, robots require not only force awareness in their decision-making but also the ability to execute actions with compliance at the torque level. This ensures stable interaction, adapts to environmental uncertainties, and absorbs unexpected impacts. Integrating compliant torque control with IL policies, especially in settings constrained by cost and sensor availability, presents a significant challenge. The goal is to design a robust control architecture that can provide high-frequency, compliant torque control based on force-informed IL outputs.

4. **Developing a Novel Dual-Loop Framework for Force-Guided IL:**
 - **Motivation:** There is a need for a holistic framework that can effectively combine high-level task learning with low-level compliant execution. Existing approaches often treat force as an auxiliary signal or partially decouple vision from force control. The objective is to propose a dual-loop structure where a high-level IL policy processes multimodal (visual and force) inputs to predict adaptive task-level motions, which are then precisely executed by a low-level impedance controller, creating a "force-in, force-out" system.

5. **Enabling Effective Force Estimation without Dedicated Sensors:**
 - **Motivation:** To address the cost and hardware dependency issue, a core objective is to develop a method for estimating end-effector wrenches solely from readily available robot internal sensor data (e.g., joint torques). This method should be robust enough for real-world deployment, accounting for factors like sensor noise and mechanical compliance.

In summary, the key objectives are to develop a Force-guided Imitation Learning framework (FILIC) that:
- Integrates explicit force awareness into IL for compliant manipulation.
- Utilizes a cost-effective, sensorless method for end-effector force estimation.
- Combines a Transformer-based IL policy with an impedance torque controller in a robust dual-loop architecture.
- Demonstrates superior, safer, and more adaptable performance in contact-rich tasks compared to position-only or joint-torque-based methods.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The FILIC framework addresses the identified challenges by integrating a sensorless force estimation method with a dual-loop control architecture, combining a high-level imitation learning (IL) policy with a low-level impedance torque controller.

**A. Dual-Loop Imitation-Impedance Control**
FILIC employs a hierarchical dual-loop structure to achieve compliant manipulation.

1. **Outer-Loop Imitation Learning Policy:**
 - **Purpose:** To generate adaptive task-level motion commands from multimodal observations.
 - **Architecture:** A Transformer-based model is used, drawing inspiration from methods like Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT).
 - **Inputs:** The policy receives multimodal sensory inputs at 25 Hz:
 - **Proprioceptive Data:** Joint positions, velocities, and torques from the robot's internal sensors. These are converted via forward kinematics and dynamics into an end-effector Cartesian pose and an *estimated external wrench* (F_ext).
 - **Visual Data:** RGB images from two external cameras.
 - **Feature Processing:**
 - Visual streams are encoded using dual ResNet backbones.
 - The estimated force vector is processed through a Multilayer Perceptron (MLP).
 - Visual and force features are fused via a cross-attention module.
 - This fused representation is then concatenated with MLP-projected end-effector pose features and a Conditional Variational Autoencoder (CVAE) latent style variable (Z, which is omitted during inference).
 - **Output:** The Transformer model outputs a sequence of target 6-DOF end-effector Cartesian poses at 25 Hz. Action chunking with temporal ensemble processing is applied to enhance robustness and smoothness.

2. **Inner-Loop Impedance Controller:**
 - **Purpose:** To enforce stable and compliant execution of the high-level motion commands at the torque level.
 - **Input:** The 6-DOF Cartesian target pose from the outer-loop policy (at 25 Hz), and high-frequency real-time joint positions (q) and velocities (v) from the robot's internal sensors (at 250 Hz).
 - **Control Law:** The target pose is mapped to desired joint angles (ˆq) through real-time inverse kinematics. A simplified joint-space impedance control law computes torque commands (τ_cmd) at 2 kHz. The model assumes quasi-static tasks, thus omitting the inertial component, and uses a spring-damper formulation:
 τ_cmd = B(ˆv - v) + K(ˆq - q) + ˆτ
 Where K and B are preset stiffness and damping coefficients, ˆv (desired velocity) is typically set to 0, and ˆτ is the feedforward compensation torque (accounting for gravity and Coriolis forces), computed at 250 Hz.
 - **Execution:** The computed torque commands are directly provided to the robot's motor control function in MIT mode. The stiffness (K) and damping (B) coefficients are fixed to prioritize stable deployment.

**B. External Force Estimation via Digital Twin**
To enable force awareness without dedicated F/T sensors, FILIC reconstructs the 6-dimensional end-effector wrench.

1. **Jacobian-Based External Force Estimation:**
 - **Principle:** Leverages the inverse relationship between end-effector wrenches and joint torques, mediated by the robot's Jacobian matrix.
 - **Formula:** The external end-effector force (F_ext) is estimated using:
 F_ext = J(q)^T+ * (τ_obs - τ_inv)
 Where τ_obs are the observed joint torques from robot sensors, τ_inv are the expected dynamics-based joint torques, and J(q)^T+ is the pseudo-inverse of the Jacobian transpose. This allows stable approximation even with dimensional misalignment.

2. **Synchronous Digital Twin:**
 - **Purpose:** To robustly compute the expected joint torques (τ_inv) and infer F_ext, compensating for real-world factors like sensor noise, friction, and mechanical compliance.
 - **Implementation:** A high-fidelity robotic arm model is instantiated within the MuJoCo simulator. Measured joint positions, velocities, and torques from the physical robot are continuously synchronized to this digital twin in real-time. MuJoCo's precise dynamics simulation then calculates τ_inv and infers F_ext.
 - **Advantages:** Eliminates the need for additional high-precision force or acceleration sensors, minimizes hardware dependency, and can be generalized to different robotic platforms by updating the simulation model.

**C. Demonstration Data Collection with Vibro-Haptic Feedback**
High-quality demonstration data, particularly with informative force variations, is crucial for IL.
- **Method:** A low-cost handheld teleoperation interface with vibrotactile haptic feedback is used.
- **Feedback Mechanism:** The estimated end-effector forces (F_ext) are mapped to haptic vibration cues on the controller. The vibration frequency increases discretely with the magnitude of the estimated force, providing intuitive feedback to the human demonstrator about contact intensity.
- **Outcome:** This mechanism enables demonstrators to adjust their actions effectively, resulting in collected trajectories that capture meaningful force variations, thereby improving the robustness of the learned IL policy.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of FILIC focused on three aspects: the accuracy of the force estimator, qualitative analysis of estimated force traces, and policy-level performance ablations in both simulated and real-world environments.

**A. Quantitative Accuracy of External Force Estimation**
The external force estimator was quantitatively assessed using a controlled static-load test on a 6-DOF AIRBOT Play manipulator.
- **Methodology:** The robot arm was held stationary at multiple poses under various known static loads (from 0.5 kg to 3.0 kg). For each load condition, 30 repeated estimates of the Z-axis external force were recorded. The prediction error was defined as the difference between the estimated Z-axis force and the true gravitational load.
- **Results:** Figure 4 illustrates that the mean error in estimated Z-axis force was consistently low across the tested load range, generally falling within ±0.5 N. The standard deviations were also small, indicating a high degree of precision and accuracy in the estimation process under static conditions. This suggests the digital twin-based estimation method provides reliable force information.

**B. Qualitative Analysis of Estimated Force Traces**
Real-world force and torque traces were recorded for four distinct contact-rich tasks to qualitatively demonstrate how interaction events are reflected in the estimated signals.
- **Observations (Figure 3):**
 - **Plug adapter into socket:** The estimated Z-axis force increased progressively during insertion, reaching a peak when the adapter was fully engaged, aligning with the contact and fitment phases.
 - **Press emergency stop button and release:** A pronounced peak in Z-axis force was observed during the downward press, followed by a sharp change corresponding to the spring-driven release mechanism.
 - **Fix C-clamp to table:** Wrist rotation produced a rapid rise in the X-axis torque, which then gradually increased to a maximum as the clamp was tightened, indicating the accumulation of resistive forces.
 - **Tighten bottle cap:** Similar to the C-clamp, the X-axis torque showed an abrupt increase upon initial contact and then climbed progressively, peaking during the final tightening phase.
- **Conclusion:** In all tasks, key interaction stages (e.g., pressing, releasing, locking, tightening) directly corresponded to local extrema or abrupt transitions in the estimated force/torque trajectories. This indicates that the estimated signals provide directly observable and task-relevant contact cues.

**C. Policy Evaluation Results**
FILIC's policy performance was evaluated against baselines in simulation (peg-in-hole task) and on a real robot (four diverse contact-rich tasks). All models used identical architectures and hyperparameters, differing only in the proprioceptive contact cue provided.

1. **Proprioceptive Input Settings Compared:**
 - End-effector (EE) position only (baseline).
 - EE position + raw joint torque.
 - EE position + estimated EE force (FILIC).

2. **Simulation Results (Table II – Peg-in-Hole Task):**
 - **Position-Only:** Achieved a success rate of 68.0%. Failures were typically due to inability to recover from lateral misalignment during insertion.
 - **EE position + joint torque:** Improved to 80.0% success (+12.0 percentage points, pp, over position-only).
 - **EE position + estimated EE force (FILIC):** Achieved the highest success rate of 90.0% (+22.0 pp over position-only, +10.0 pp over joint torque). This indicates that the estimated end-effector force is a more effective input than raw joint torques for this task.

3. **Real-Robot Results (Table III – Four Tasks):**
 - **Position-Only Baseline:** Achieved success rates ranging from 26.6% (Tighten bottle cap) to 46.7% (Plug adapter into socket). Consistent failure modes included lack of recovery from misalignment, inconsistent final actions (emergency stop button), and inability to complete tightening from loose initial states. This confirms the limitations of position-only observations for reliable contact-rich control.
 - **Impact of Force-Related Proprioception:**
 - Adding force-related proprioception (either joint torque or estimated EE force) consistently improved success rates across all tasks.
 - **EE position + estimated EE force (FILIC)** significantly outperformed the position-only baseline:
 - Plug adapter: 80.0% (+33.3 pp)
 - E-stop button: 83.3% (+40.0 pp)
 - C-clamp: 76.6% (+46.6 pp)
 - Bottle cap: 80.0% (+53.4 pp)
 - **Estimated EE Force vs. Raw Joint Torque:**
 - FILIC, using estimated EE force, consistently outperformed policies using raw joint torque:
 - Plug adapter: 80.0% vs 63.3% (+16.7 pp)
 - E-stop button: 83.3% vs 60.0% (+23.3 pp)
 - C-clamp: 76.6% vs 63.3% (+13.3 pp)
 - Bottle cap: 80.0% vs 70.0% (+10.0 pp)
- **Overall Conclusion:** The results consistently demonstrate that the estimated end-effector force provides a more task-aligned and effective contact signal than raw joint torque. Integrating this estimated force, coupled with the dual-loop impedance control, significantly improves policy performance, safety, compliance, and adaptability in contact-rich manipulation tasks compared to vision-only or joint-torque-based methods.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The FILIC framework represents a notable contribution to the field of robotic manipulation, particularly in addressing the practical challenges of deploying intelligent robots for contact-rich tasks. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Enhanced Accessibility and Cost-Effectiveness of Force-Aware Robotics:** By introducing a sensorless method for end-effector wrench estimation, FILIC democratizes access to force-aware control. It obviates the need for expensive, specialized wrist force/torque (F/T) sensors, which are a common barrier to entry for many researchers and industrial applications. This significantly lowers hardware costs and reduces the complexity of robot design and integration, making compliant manipulation capabilities available to a broader range of affordable collaborative robot platforms.

2. **Improved Performance and Robustness in Contact-Rich Tasks:** The experimental results demonstrate a consistent and substantial improvement in success rates across various contact-rich tasks in both simulation and real-world scenarios. By integrating explicit force representation into the imitation learning policy and coupling it with an impedance controller, FILIC enables robots to perform tasks such as insertion, button pressing, and tightening with greater reliability, compliance, and adaptability. This leads to more robust task execution, especially when faced with uncertainties or misalignments.

3. **Increased Safety in Human-Robot and Environment Interaction:** The dual-loop architecture, with its inner-loop impedance torque control, ensures compliant and stable interaction during contact. This reduces the risk of generating excessive forces that could damage the robot, the manipulated objects, or the surrounding environment. For collaborative robots, enhanced compliance is fundamental for safe human-robot co-existence and interaction.

4. **Novel Integration of IL and Impedance Control:** FILIC's dual-loop framework is a methodological advance, effectively unifying high-level, adaptive task learning (via a Transformer-based IL policy) with low-level, compliant torque-level execution (via an impedance controller). This "force-in, force-out" structure represents a coherent approach to leveraging multimodal sensory inputs, including estimated forces, for intelligent and compliant manipulation.

5. **Practical Tool for the Robotics Community:** The open-source availability of the wrench estimator implementation provides a valuable resource for other researchers and practitioners. This encourages further development and adoption of sensorless force estimation techniques and related compliant control strategies.

6. **Foundation for Future Research:** The work establishes a robust baseline for force-guided imitation learning without F/T sensors. It also outlines clear avenues for future research, such as integrating variable impedance to allow for dynamic compliance in more complex interactions, enhancing force estimation robustness against modeling inaccuracies, and exploring implicit force reasoning within policies.

In summary, FILIC represents a significant step towards enabling intelligent robots to perform dexterous, contact-rich manipulation tasks with high reliability, safety, and compliance, all while addressing economic and hardware constraints. Its impact is likely to be felt in various applications, from industrial assembly and maintenance to domestic assistance and human-robot collaboration, by making advanced robotic capabilities more accessible and practical.
