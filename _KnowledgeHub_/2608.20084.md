# Evidence-Gated Task and Motion Planning with Vision-Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> EAFG augments VLM+TAMP long-horizon manipulation with a pre-planning evidence-acquisition loop: a VLM proposes exploratory subgoals (open doors/cabinets) executed via TAMP, then a feasibility gate decides ready-to-plan, need-more-evidence, or halt, grounding task plans in verified observations and avoiding action on unconfirmed objects.

## Problem

- VLM+TAMP pipelines assume full observability and generate subgoals from the VLM's prior knowledge rather than verified perception, risking plans on unobserved objects.
- No mechanism exists to actively verify whether goal-relevant objects are actually present before committing to task planning under partial observability.
- Prior partial-observability planners (POMDP, belief-space) and plan verifiers check logical flaws but don't gather evidence then gate planning versus halting on missing objects.

## Method

- ==Evidence Acquisition==: VLM generates exploratory subgoals restricted to reversible actions (opening doors/cabinets, moving occluders), each instantiated and executed via ==TAMP (PDDLStream)== to collect observation images.
- ==Evidence state update==: VLM regenerates a structured state (subgoal history, observed-fact summary, retained findings) from new observation collages, removing outdated or contradicted descriptions.
- ==Feasibility Gating==: VLM outputs ready-to-plan/need-more-evidence/halt per iteration (max 5), routing to evidence-conditioned task-subgoal generation, further exploration, or execution halt.

## Results

- Underspecified goal (S2): Recipe completion rate rose **0.05** to **0.40** (GPT-5.5) and **0.00** to **0.20** (Gemini-3.5-Flash), with salt/pepper step completion jumping to **0.85-1.00** from as low as 0.05.
- Missing-object goal (S3): Halt success rate rose **0.45** to **0.90** (GPT-5.5) and **0.40** to **1.00** (Gemini-3.5-Flash); missing-object attempt count fell from **4.00** to **0.55** and **2.40** to **0.00** respectively.
- Explicit-and-present goal (S1): EAFG stayed competitive, raising GPT-5.5 recipe completion **0.20** to **0.45** with pepper/salt steps reaching **1.00**, though Gemini recipe completion dipped slightly (0.15 to 0.10).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Gating task planning on actively-acquired visual evidence, not just VLM prior knowledge, curbs unsupported-assumption failures under partial observability.
> - Restricting exploratory subgoals to reversible, non-task actions lets a system safely probe hidden regions before committing to irreversible manipulation steps.
> - Recognizing and halting on infeasible goals is as valuable as completing feasible ones, cutting wasted planning/execution attempts toward absent objects.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robotics research aims to enable autonomous agents to perform complex, long-horizon manipulation tasks within unstructured, everyday environments based on natural language instructions. This objective requires robots to simultaneously interpret the semantic meaning of human language and ensure the physical feasibility of their actions within the environment.

Task and Motion Planning (TAMP) has emerged as a fundamental approach for achieving long-horizon manipulation planning. TAMP integrates discrete symbolic reasoning, which defines high-level task sequences, with continuous motion planning, which addresses geometric, kinematic, collision, grasping, and placement constraints. Systems like PDDLStream extend symbolic planning by interfacing with black-box procedures for continuous parameter generation and verification, ensuring geometrically feasible action sequences.

Concurrently, the development of Vision-Language Models (VLMs) has significantly advanced the ability of robots to interpret open-ended language and visual information. VLMs provide commonsense semantics, object grounding, and spatial reasoning capabilities, allowing them to translate perceptual and linguistic context into planner-facing structures such as formal goals, symbolic representations, and task constraints. A common integration strategy involves VLMs generating high-level, semantically meaningful subgoals from language and visual input, which TAMP then refines into geometrically feasible manipulation actions. This hierarchical approach, exemplified by methods such as VLM-TAMP, represents a promising direction for natural-language-driven robot manipulation.

However, a critical limitation in existing VLM-TAMP systems arises under conditions of partial observability. When goal-relevant objects are hidden, located in enclosed storage spaces, or entirely absent from the environment, VLMs may rely on their prior knowledge or commonsense assumptions to infer object locations and generate plausible task steps. TAMP, by its design, typically operates on an assumed fully observable, object-oriented state and does not inherently include mechanisms for actively verifying unobserved objects prior to planning. Consequently, plans generated under partial observability may ignore crucial unobserved objects or attempt to interact with non-existent ones, leading to execution failures, inefficiencies, and outcomes that do not align with the instruction.

Previous work on planning under partial observability has explored techniques such as Partially Observable Markov Decision Processes (POMDPs), belief-space planning, and explicit information-gathering actions (e.g., revealing occlusions, inspecting containers). Additionally, Large Language Model (LLM)-based pre-execution plan verification has been proposed to detect logical or procedural inconsistencies in robot plans. While these methods contribute to addressing aspects of uncertainty, they often focus on either low-level perception, belief state maintenance, or post-hoc plan validation, rather than proactively integrating evidence acquisition and feasibility assessment *before* committing to a main task plan.

This research contributes to the broader landscape by addressing the specific gap of requiring an explicit mechanism for robots to actively gather observational evidence and assess task feasibility *prior* to commencing long-horizon task planning. It integrates the semantic understanding of VLMs with the geometric reasoning of TAMP, but crucially, it places an evidence-gathering and gating phase before the traditional task planning process. This aims to ground VLM-generated subgoals in verified observations, thereby mitigating the risks associated with planning based on unsupported assumptions in partially observable environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the robustness and reliability of robot manipulation systems operating in partially observable environments, specifically when executing long-horizon tasks from natural-language instructions. Existing VLM-TAMP approaches, while powerful in integrating semantic understanding with geometric feasibility, often make unsupported assumptions about the environment's state, particularly regarding the presence and location of goal-relevant objects.

The key motivations for this work stem from the following observed limitations:

1. **Reliance on Prior Knowledge under Partial Observability:** In real-world settings, robots frequently encounter situations where goal-relevant objects are not immediately visible. They might be stored inside cabinets, refrigerators, or be occluded. Current VLM-TAMP systems, when faced with an instruction like "make soup," may infer the need for ingredients like "salt" based on their training data and commonsense knowledge. However, if "salt" is not directly observable, the VLM might generate subgoals that implicitly assume its presence and location, without any observational confirmation. This can lead to plans that are not grounded in the actual environment.

2. **Inefficiency and Failure from Unsupported Plans:** When a robot generates and attempts to execute a plan for an unobserved or non-existent object (e.g., trying to pick up salt from a closed cabinet without first checking if salt is there, or repeatedly attempting to manipulate a missing object), it leads to execution failures, wasted time, and potentially unintended outcomes. There is a need for a mechanism to prevent robots from committing to extensive task plans that are fundamentally infeasible due to missing environmental information.

3. **Lack of Active Information Gathering:** While prior work exists in planning under partial observability, a cohesive framework that actively and autonomously directs a robot to gather specific evidence relevant to a high-level language goal, and then uses that evidence to make a go/no-go decision for task planning, was identified as a critical missing component in VLM-TAMP integration. Robots need to be able to "ask questions" of their environment through exploratory actions before engaging in irreversible task steps.

To address these motivations, the authors propose the **Evidence Acquisition and Feasibility Gating (EAFG)** framework, with the following detailed objectives:

- **Introduce a Pre-planning Evidence-Acquisition Mechanism:** The objective is to design a system where a VLM can be prompted to generate information-gathering subgoals. These subgoals aim to reveal hidden regions, such as opening cabinet doors or inspecting container interiors, and to verify the actual presence and status of goal-relevant objects in the environment. This acquisition phase occurs *before* the main task planning commences.
- **Propose a Feasibility-Gating Mechanism:** The goal is to establish a decision-making component that evaluates the observational evidence gathered during the acquisition phase. This "feasibility gate" should enable the robot to decide whether the acquired evidence sufficiently supports a feasible task plan, requires further exploratory observations, or necessitates halting the entire process if the goal requirements remain unsupported by available evidence. This mechanism aims to prevent the generation of ungrounded or infeasible task plans.
- **Improve Grounded Task Completion and Infeasibility Detection:** Through empirical evaluation, the research aims to demonstrate that EAFG can improve the robot's ability to complete tasks by actively discovering necessary but unmentioned objects in underspecified natural language instructions. Furthermore, it seeks to show that EAFG can promote appropriate halt decisions when explicitly required objects are absent, thereby reducing futile and repetitive attempts to interact with non-existent entities.
- **Integrate with Existing VLM-TAMP Frameworks:** The design aims for compatibility, allowing the evidence state (summarizing acquired information) to be provided as textual context to existing VLM- or LLM-guided TAMP frameworks, facilitating its adoption.

In summary, the core motivation is to move beyond passive reliance on prior knowledge in VLM-TAMP systems towards an active, evidence-driven approach that ensures task plans are robustly grounded in the observed reality of the physical environment.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology is centered around the **Evidence Acquisition and Feasibility Gating (EAFG)** framework, designed to ensure that robot task planning is grounded in verified observational evidence rather than solely relying on prior VLM knowledge under partial observability.

### 4. Overview of EAFG

EAFG operates as an iterative pipeline (Figure 1 in the paper). Upon receiving a natural-language goal (G_eng), the system first enters an evidence-acquisition phase. In this phase, a VLM proposes exploratory subgoals to gather information about the environment. These subgoals are executed by a TAMP system, and the resulting observations are collected. Based on the accumulated evidence, a feasibility gate, also mediated by the VLM, determines the next course of action: proceed to task planning, acquire more evidence, or halt.

### 5. Evidence Acquisition

This component functions as an iterative loop (indexed by *t*) where the robot executes exploratory actions to update its understanding of the environment.

- **Subgoal Generation:** At each iteration *t*, the VLM generates a sequence of evidence-acquisition subgoals (G_EA_t) based on the language goal, current observations, and the accumulated evidence state. These subgoals are specifically designed to inspect potentially relevant regions or objects (e.g., "open(fridge)", "open(cabinet)", "pick(pot lid)") rather than directly progressing the main task. A key constraint is that the VLM is restricted to generating only "safe," reversible exploratory actions, excluding irreversible or main-task actions (e.g., cooking, sprinkling) to ensure the robot solely gathers information.
- **TAMP Instantiation and Execution:** Each generated evidence-acquisition subgoal is passed to a TAMP module (e.g., PDDLStream). The TAMP module attempts to instantiate the symbolic subgoal into a geometrically and kinematically feasible low-level action sequence. If TAMP succeeds, the robot executes these actions. If TAMP fails to find a feasible sequence, execution for that specific subgoal is skipped, and the system proceeds to the next evidence-acquisition iteration.
- **Observation Collection:** After each subgoal execution, the resulting observation image from the robot's camera is recorded. These images are then arranged chronologically into an image collage (I_t) for subsequent VLM inference.
- **Evidence State Update:** The system maintains an evidence state (E_t = (H_t, O_t, F_t)).
 - H_t: History of executed subgoals.
 - O_t: Textual summary of observed facts.
 - F_t: Set of retained findings relevant for later inference.
 The VLM updates O_t+1 and F_t+1 from E_t and the new observation collage I_t. This update process involves regenerating the textual components, allowing outdated, redundant, or contradicted descriptions to be removed, thus refining the robot's understanding incrementally.

### 6. Feasibility Gating

After each evidence-acquisition iteration, Feasibility Gating is applied to determine if sufficient evidence has been collected to proceed with task planning.

- **VLM Decision:** The VLM receives the natural-language goal (G_eng), the current evidence state (E_t), and the observation collage (I_t). It then outputs one of three feasibility statuses (s_t):
 - **"ready to plan"**: If the VLM determines that the acquired evidence is sufficient for planning towards G_eng, the system exits the evidence-acquisition loop and proceeds to evidence-conditioned task planning.
 - **"need more evidence"**: If the current evidence is insufficient, but unexplored regions or information relevant to verifying required objects or conditions remain, the VLM outputs this status. Simultaneously, it generates an additional sequence of evidence-acquisition subgoals (G_EA_t+1) to obtain the missing information, and the system returns to the evidence-acquisition loop.
 - **"halt"**: This status is selected if Evidence Acquisition fails to verify essential objects or conditions for G_eng, and further exploratory subgoals are deemed unlikely to help. For example, if a required object remains unobserved after inspecting all plausible source locations, the system halts to prevent generating task subgoals based on unsupported assumptions or making repeated attempts to manipulate a non-existent object.

### 7. Evidence-Conditioned Planning

When the Feasibility Gating outputs "ready to plan" at iteration *T*, the system proceeds to task planning.

- **Grounded Task Subgoal Generation:** The task planner receives the natural-language goal (G_eng) and the accumulated evidence state (E_T) as explicit textual context. E_T summarizes all executed exploratory subgoals, verified observations, and retained findings. This textual context is appended to the planner prompt, enabling the task planner (e.g., a VLM- or LLM-guided TAMP framework) to generate a task-subgoal sequence (G_task) that is explicitly grounded in the verified observations collected during Evidence Acquisition. Each subgoal (g_task_m) specifies a goal-directed manipulation objective, such as picking a verified ingredient or heating a pot.

### 8. Implementation Details

- **VLMs:** The experiments employed `gpt-5.5-2026-04-23` and `gemini-3.5-flash` with a temperature of 0.2.
- **Evidence Acquisition Restrictions:** During evidence acquisition, only reversible exploratory operations (e.g., opening doors, moving occluding objects, inspecting containers) were permitted. Actions directly advancing the main task (e.g., placing ingredients, heating) were disallowed.
- **Observation Processing:** Observation images from executed subgoals were arranged into a top-to-bottom image collage and annotated with subgoal names before being presented to the VLM.
- **TAMP Configuration:** PDDLStream was used in diverse planning mode, considering up to 12 plan skeletons. If TAMP failed for a subgoal, up to three replanning attempts were made, gradually increasing the set of observed objects included in planning.
- **Iteration Limit:** A maximum of five Evidence Acquisition iterations was allowed before a final decision from Feasibility Gating.

This methodological approach aims to provide a proactive and evidence-driven foundation for long-horizon robot manipulation, addressing the challenges of partial observability by integrating active perception with high-level planning.

### 9. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluated the EAFG framework across three distinct kitchen task settings, designed to assess different aspects of its performance under varying conditions of object observability and goal specification. The experiments used a kitchen environment for making chicken soup, involving five movable objects, eight support surfaces, two storage spaces (initially closed), and six articulated objects. Crucially, objects were added to the PDDL problem only after they appeared in the robot's observation images. The robot operated with a single left arm, necessitating careful handling of unobserved regions, closed storage, and geometric constraints.

### 10. Experimental Settings and Metrics

- **S1: Explicit-and-present (RQ1 - Completing feasible recipes with explicit, present objects):**
 - **Goal:** "make chicken soup with salt and pepper." (Both salt and pepper are present).
 - **Metrics:** Step Completion Rate (CR) for five steps (chicken, salt, pepper, water, stove), and Recipe CR (all five steps completed in one trial).
- **S2: Underspecified (RQ2 - Acquiring information and completing recipes under underspecified goals):**
 - **Goal:** "make chicken soup." (Salt and pepper are present but not explicitly mentioned).
 - **Metrics:** Same as S1.
- **S3: Explicit-but-missing (RQ3 - Halting appropriately for absent objects and reducing repeated attempts):**
 - **Goal:** "make chicken soup with salt, pepper, and carrot." (Carrot is absent).
 - **Metrics:** Halt Success Rate (SR) (system halts after inspecting relevant source regions like fridge/cabinet), and Missing-object Attempt Count (mean number of TAMP calls involving the absent object).

### 11. Results Overview

EAFG provided the most substantial benefits in scenarios where initial information was incomplete or ambiguous (S2 and S3), while remaining competitive in the fully specified scenario (S1).

### 12. Detailed Results per Setting

- **S1: Explicit-and-present (Table 2)**
 - **GPT-5.5:** EAFG improved Recipe CR from 0.20 to 0.45. Step CR for pepper and salt reached 1.00, demonstrating more reliable execution of object-specific steps. The water step CR decreased from 0.80 to 0.55, suggesting a potential trade-off or increased complexity in other planning aspects for this specific step.
 - **Gemini-3.5-Flash:** EAFG resulted in a slight decrease in Recipe CR from 0.15 to 0.10. However, like GPT-5.5, step CR for pepper and salt reached 1.00, and the water step CR remained high at 0.85.
 - **Finding:** EAFG maintained competitive performance when all required objects were explicitly stated and present, demonstrating its ability to robustly handle the task and even improve reliability for object-use steps that directly depend on specified ingredients.

- **S2: Underspecified (Table 3)**
 - **GPT-5.5:** EAFG significantly increased Recipe CR from 0.05 (without EAFG) to 0.40. This improvement was attributed to the Evidence Acquisition phase discovering the unmentioned but task-relevant salt and pepper. Their respective Step CRs rose from 0.10 to 1.00 and from 0.30 to 1.00.
 - **Gemini-3.5-Flash:** EAFG also improved Recipe CR from 0.00 to 0.20. Step CR for salt increased from 0.05 to 0.95, and for pepper from 0.05 to 0.85.
 - **Finding:** EAFG demonstrated its capacity to actively acquire missing task-relevant information through exploration and successfully integrate this evidence into a complete recipe plan, which was rarely achieved by the baseline without explicit mentions of these ingredients.

- **S3: Explicit-but-missing (Table 4)**
 - **GPT-5.5:** EAFG substantially increased Halt SR from 0.45 to 0.90, indicating a more reliable detection of infeasible goals. Concurrently, it reduced the Missing-object Attempt Count from 4.00 to 0.55, minimizing futile TAMP calls for the absent "carrot."
 - **Gemini-3.5-Flash:** EAFG achieved a Halt SR of 1.00 (up from 0.40) and completely eliminated Missing-object Attempt Count, reducing it from 2.40 to 0.00.
 - **Finding:** EAFG proved effective in both reliably identifying tasks rendered infeasible by missing objects and preventing the robot from repeatedly attempting to plan or execute actions related to those absent objects.

In summary, the experimental results indicate that EAFG primarily enhances robustness in scenarios involving partial observability and underspecified goals. It enables robots to proactively gather necessary information, leading to improved task completion for underspecified goals and more appropriate halting behavior for infeasible tasks, while remaining functionally comparable in fully specified, observable contexts.

### 13. Significance and Potential Impact

The Evidence Acquisition and Feasibility Gating (EAFG) framework addresses a fundamental challenge in robot autonomy: operating reliably in real-world environments characterized by partial observability and uncertainty. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Enhanced Robustness in Partially Observable Environments:** EAFG provides a structured mechanism for robots to actively gather information and verify environmental conditions before committing to complex task plans. This is crucial for real-world deployment, where objects may be hidden, in storage, or entirely absent. By integrating active perception directly into the planning pipeline, EAFG reduces the likelihood of failures arising from ungrounded assumptions.

- **Improved Task Completion for Ambiguous Instructions:** The framework's ability to discover task-relevant but unmentioned objects in underspecified natural language goals (as demonstrated in the "make chicken soup" scenario) significantly enhances robot adaptability. This allows robots to interpret and complete tasks more flexibly, moving beyond strict literal interpretations to infer and verify necessary components, aligning better with human-robot interaction paradigms.

- **Prevention of Futile Actions and Resource Conservation:** By incorporating a feasibility gate that can decide to halt execution when a required object is confirmed absent, EAFG prevents robots from repeatedly attempting to plan or execute actions towards infeasible subgoals. This conserves computational resources (TAMP calls) and robot energy, reduces wear and tear, and prevents potentially hazardous or frustrating repetitive failures. The demonstrated reduction in "Missing-object Attempt Count" highlights this efficiency gain.

- **Increased Safety and Predictability:** Planning based on verified evidence rather than mere assumptions contributes to safer robot operation. Unintended outcomes that could arise from misinterpretations or interactions with non-existent objects are minimized. The robot's behavior becomes more predictable and grounded in its current observations.

- **Broad Applicability with Existing Frameworks:** EAFG is designed to be compatible with existing VLM- and LLM-guided TAMP frameworks, accepting the accumulated evidence state as explicit textual context for task planning. This modularity suggests that the principles of EAFG could be readily integrated into a wide range of current and future robot planning systems, amplifying its potential impact across various domains.

- **Advancement in Active Perception for Robotics:** This work advances the concept of active perception, where robots purposefully manipulate their environment to gain information, by tightly coupling it with high-level task and motion planning. It moves beyond passive observation to goal-directed information gathering, a critical step towards more intelligent and autonomous robotic agents.

Despite the current limitation regarding explicit recovery from manipulation failures during evidence acquisition, the foundational contribution of EAFG lies in establishing a proactive, evidence-driven planning paradigm. As underlying planning and feedback mechanisms in robotics continue to mature, frameworks like EAFG are poised to enable more robust, intelligent, and human-friendly robotic systems capable of operating effectively in complex, dynamic, and partially known environments, from domestic assistance to industrial automation.
