# Vysics: Object Reconstruction Under Occlusion by Fusing Vision and Contact-Rich Physics

> [!summary] Summary
> Vysics, developed by researchers from the University of Pennsylvania's GRASP Laboratory and Amazon, presents a system that reconstructs complete 3D object models by fusing visual data with physical contact information, even when objects are significantly occluded. The framework integrates a vision-based shape reconstruction pipeline with a physics-based model learning approach, demonstrating improved geometric accuracy and dynamics prediction compared to vision-only or physics-only methods.

## Problem

- Robotic perception struggles with reconstructing complete object models when objects are partially occluded by other objects, the robot itself, or environmental elements.
- Vision-only methods cannot infer geometry of hidden regions, while contact-only methods lack detailed geometric information.
- Without accurate and complete object models, robots face difficulties in predicting object behavior and performing precise manipulation in real-world, cluttered environments.

## Method

- A framework named **Vysics** combines a vision-based component (modified ==BundleSDF==) for estimating visible geometry from RGBD video with a physics-inspired component (modified ==PLL==) for inferring physically plausible geometry from object trajectories and contact dynamics.
- The two components engage in bidirectional information exchange via specialized loss functions, where visual data supervises the physics model, and physics-derived constraints guide the visual reconstruction of occluded areas.
- The system processes RGBD video and robot proprioception data, then optimizes a unified object model represented by ==Signed Distance Functions (SDFs)== and ==Deep Support Functions (DSFs)== to satisfy both visual consistency and physical plausibility.

## Results

- Vysics reduced Chamfer distance by an average of **55%** compared to the vision-only BundleSDF baseline, indicating significantly more accurate geometric reconstruction.
- Vysics achieved an average volumetric Intersection over Union (IoU) of **0.54**, substantially outperforming BundleSDF's **0.24**, showing better volume estimation.
- Models built with Vysics demonstrated improved dynamics prediction, exhibiting lower average pose error, longer time before simulation divergence, and higher temporal IoU of contact activation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating vision-based neural implicit shape representation (BundleSDF) with physics-inspired learning (PLL) allows for more complete object model reconstruction than either modality alone.
> - Bidirectional supervision, where vision guides physics and physics guides vision, enables inferring occluded geometry that is both visually consistent and physically plausible.
> - Representing visible geometry with Signed Distance Functions (SDFs) and 'physible' geometry with Deep Support Functions (DSFs) facilitates the fusion of visual and contact information.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

#### 1.1. Introduction

Robotic manipulation in real-world environments demands comprehensive understanding of object properties, including geometry and dynamics. Yet, robots frequently encounter scenarios where objects are partially occluded, making accurate perception challenging. The paper "Vysics" presents a novel approach to this problem by combining visual data with physical contact information to reconstruct complete object models even when significant portions are hidden from view.

Developed by researchers from the University of Pennsylvania's GRASP Laboratory and Amazon, Vysics addresses a fundamental limitation in current robotic perception systems: reliance on either vision alone (which struggles with occlusions) or contact alone (which provides limited geometric information). By fusing these complementary data sources, Vysics creates more accurate and complete object models that improve manipulation performance.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

#### 2.1. Challenges in Robotic Perception

Robotic perception systems face several significant challenges when operating in real-world environments:

1. **Visual Occlusion**: Objects are often partially hidden from camera view by other objects, the robot itself, or environmental elements.
2. **Geometric Ambiguity**: From visual data alone, a robot cannot confidently determine the full geometry of partially visible objects.
3. **Dynamics Prediction**: Without accurate geometric and physical models, robots struggle to predict how objects will behave during manipulation.
4. **Model Building Speed**: Robots need to quickly build models of new objects to operate efficiently in changing environments.

Traditional vision-only approaches like simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM) or neural radiance fields (NeRF) can reconstruct visible portions of scenes but struggle to infer occluded geometry. Meanwhile, physics-based approaches that use contact information can detect object boundaries but typically lack detailed geometric information.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. The Vysics Framework

Vysics bridges this gap by integrating two complementary systems:

1. **Vision-Based Component (BundleSDF)**: A modified version of BundleSDF that combines pose tracking with neural implicit shape representation. This component processes RGBD video to estimate the trajectory and visible geometry of objects.

2. **Physics-Inspired Component (PLL)**: A modified Physics Learning Library that infers "physible" geometry (geometry that obeys physical laws) from object trajectories and contact dynamics. It uses a deep support function (DSF) to represent the object's convex hull and contact surfaces.

The key innovation lies in how these components collaborate:

- Vision helps physics: The visible geometry from BundleSDF supervises PLL's DSF, preventing physically plausible but visually incorrect reconstructions.
- Physics helps vision: The "physible" geometry from PLL guides BundleSDF to reconstruct occluded regions based on contact interactions.

This bidirectional information flow enables Vysics to infer complete object models even with significant occlusion, creating unified object geometry that satisfies both visual consistency and physical plausibility constraints.

#### 3.2. Vision and Physics Integration

The integration of vision and physics in Vysics involves several key steps:

1. **Data Collection**: A robot arm interacts with objects on a table, collecting RGBD video and proprioception data (joint positions, velocities, and torques).

2. **Vision Processing**: BundleSDF processes the RGBD video to estimate object poses and visible geometry, representing shapes using Signed Distance Functions (SDFs).

3. **Physics Processing**: PLL processes the estimated object trajectory along with robot states to infer physically plausible geometry and inertial properties.

4. **Bidirectional Supervision**: The two components exchange information through specialized loss functions to create a consistent and complete object model.

5. **URDF Generation**: The final model, including geometry and inertial properties, is exported as a Unified Robot Description Format (URDF) file for use in simulation and robot control.

The researchers evaluated Vysics on a dataset of everyday objects including egg cartons, milk containers, and food boxes, deliberately creating significant occlusions during data collection.

#### 3.3. Mathematical Foundations

Vysics relies on several mathematical representations and techniques:

#### 3.3.1. Signed Distance Functions (SDFs)

SDFs represent geometry by encoding the distance from any point in space to the nearest surface, with negative values inside the object and positive values outside. For a point in space $\mathbf{s}$:

$$\text{SDF}(\mathbf{s}) = 0 \text{ for points on the surface}$$
$$\text{SDF}(\mathbf{s} + \hat{\mathbf{n}}l) = l \text{ for points at distance } l \text{ along the normal}$$

#### 3.3.2. Deep Support Functions (DSFs)

DSFs represent convex shapes by encoding the distance from the origin to the supporting hyperplane with normal direction $\hat{\mathbf{n}}$:

$$\text{DSF}(\hat{\mathbf{n}}) = d$$
$$\nabla\text{DSF}(\hat{\mathbf{n}}) = \mathbf{s}$$

Where $\mathbf{s}$ is the support point in direction $\hat{\mathbf{n}}$, and $d$ is the distance from the origin to the supporting plane.

#### 3.3.3. Loss Functions

Several specialized loss functions enable the bidirectional supervision between vision and physics:

1. **BundleSDF Supervision Loss**: Guides the DSF representation using visible geometry:
 $$\mathcal{L}_{\text{bsdf}} = \|\mathbf{s}^v - \mathbf{s}^p\|$$
 Where $\mathbf{s}^v$ is a surface point from vision and $\mathbf{s}^p$ is the corresponding point from physics.

2. **Hyperplane-Constrained Loss**: Ensures geometric consistency with contact observations:
 $$\mathcal{L}_{\text{hc}} = \min(0, \text{SDF}(\mathbf{s}^{v'}) - l')^2$$

3. **Support Point Loss**: Ensures that the vision model matches physics predictions:
 $$\mathcal{L}_{\text{sp}} = (\text{SDF}(\mathbf{s}^v) - l)^2$$

#### 3.4. Implementation Details

Vysics is implemented as a multi-stage optimization process:

1. **Initial Model Building**: BundleSDF processes the RGBD video to create an initial shape estimate and trajectory.

2. **Physics Integration**: PLL processes the estimated trajectory to create a physics-consistent model.

3. **Iterative Refinement**: Vision and physics components exchange information through specialized loss functions, gradually improving the overall model.

4. **Inertial Property Estimation**: In addition to geometry, the system estimates mass, center of mass, and inertia tensor.

The researchers collected data using a Franka Emika Panda robot arm equipped with an Intel RealSense D435i RGB-D camera, specifically creating scenarios with substantial visual occlusion.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Experimental Results

The researchers evaluated Vysics against several baselines:

1. **BundleSDF**: The vision-only component of Vysics
2. **PLL**: The physics-only component of Vysics
3. **GT Geometry**: Using ground truth geometry with physics learning

They used multiple metrics to assess performance:

1. **Geometric Accuracy**: Chamfer distance and volumetric IoU with ground truth
2. **Dynamics Prediction**: Average pose error, time-before-divergence, and temporal IoU of contact activation

The results demonstrate significant improvements across all metrics:

The qualitative results clearly show Vysics' ability to reconstruct complete object geometry even under significant occlusion. For the bottle shown in Figure 6, the vision-only approach (BundleSDF) captures only a small portion of the object, while Vysics reconstructs the full bottle shape by leveraging contact information.

#### 4.2. Performance Comparison

Quantitative analysis confirmed the superior performance of Vysics:

1. **Geometric Accuracy**: Vysics reduced Chamfer distance by an average of 55% compared to BundleSDF, indicating much closer alignment with the true object geometry.

2. **Volumetric IoU**: Vysics achieved an average IoU of 0.54 compared to BundleSDF's 0.24, demonstrating more accurate volume estimation.

3. **Dynamics Prediction**: Models built with Vysics showed significantly better performance in predicting object behavior:
 - Lower average pose error (position and rotation)
 - Longer time before simulation divergence
 - Higher temporal IoU of contact activation

The analysis of trajectory divergence (Figure 10) further illustrates Vysics' advantage: at the 60% trajectory mark, Vysics correctly tracks approximately 60% of test cases, while BundleSDF correctly tracks only about 30%.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

#### 5.1. Applications and Future Directions

The capabilities demonstrated by Vysics have several important applications:

1. **Warehouse Automation**: Robots can more effectively manipulate partially occluded objects during picking and packing tasks.

2. **Household Robotics**: Home robots could better interact with objects in cluttered environments like kitchen cabinets or refrigerators.

3. **Manufacturing**: Robots could handle complex parts even when portions are not fully visible to cameras.

4. **Robotic Exploration**: Systems could build more complete environmental models by combining visual data with contact information.

Future research directions mentioned by the authors include:

1. **Active Information Gathering**: Teaching robots to deliberately interact with objects to reduce uncertainty.

2. **Multi-Object Scenes**: Extending Vysics to handle multiple interacting objects simultaneously.

3. **Deformable Objects**: Adapting the framework to model objects that change shape during interaction.

4. **Faster Inference**: Optimizing the computational efficiency for real-time robot operation.

#### 5.2. Conclusion

Vysics represents a significant advancement in robotic perception by successfully fusing vision and physics for object reconstruction under occlusion. By combining complementary information sources, it overcomes the limitations of vision-only or physics-only approaches, creating more complete and accurate object models.

The key contributions of this work include:

1. A novel framework that integrates vision-based tracking and reconstruction with physics-based model learning.

2. Specialized loss functions that enable bidirectional supervision between vision and physics components.

3. Experimental validation showing substantial improvements in geometric accuracy and dynamics prediction.

4. A new dataset of RGBD videos showing robot-object interactions with significant occlusions.

This research demonstrates that by reasoning about both visual appearance and physical interactions, robots can build better models of the world, enabling more effective manipulation in complex, real-world environments.
