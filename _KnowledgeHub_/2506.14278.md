# Whole-Body Control Framework for Humanoid Robots with Heavy Limbs: A Model-Based Approach

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the Chinese University of Hong Kong developed a model-based whole-body control framework for humanoid robots with heavy limbs. This framework achieves stable dynamic walking at speeds up to 1.2 m/s, resists external forces up to 60 N, and navigates uneven terrains by integrating a real-time kino-dynamics planner with a hierarchical optimization scheme that prioritizes limb motion tracking and ensures compliance with full robot dynamics.

## Problem

- Humanoid robots with heavy limbs (40-60% of total mass) exhibit strong base-limb coupling, leading to significant balance and stability challenges during dynamic movements.
- Existing model-based whole-body control methods struggle with real-time computation due to high dimensionality or suffer from a disconnect between high-level planning and low-level control, resulting in unaddressed limb errors and instability.
- Learning-based approaches often lack interpretability and their high-gain feedback controllers can degrade performance due to the nonlinear dynamics of humanoid robots.

## Method

- A two-component ==model-based control framework== integrating a ==kino-dynamics planner (MPC)== operating at 100 Hz and a ==hierarchical optimization problem (Hierarchical Quadratic Programming)== operating at 800 Hz.
- The kino-dynamics planner simplifies system dynamics and constraints to ensure real-time performance while explicitly accounting for the impact of heavy limbs on mass and inertia distribution via ==centroidal momentum==.
- The hierarchical optimization prioritizes ==limb motion tracking== to minimize control errors and ensures explicit compliance with the ==full floating-base robot dynamics== and the planner's policy.

## Results

- The framework enabled stable dynamic walking at speeds up to **1.2 m/s** in simulation and successfully navigated uneven terrain (**5 cm and 10 cm** stages) on a physical Orca I humanoid robot, outperforming comparative methods.
- It demonstrated robust external disturbance rejection, successfully resisting applied forces up to **60 N** in simulation.
- The kino-dynamics planner achieved an average computation time of **6.57-7.89 ms**, and the hierarchical optimization achieved **0.52-0.58 ms**, meeting the real-time operating frequencies of **100 Hz** and **800 Hz** respectively.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Effective whole-body control for heavy-limbed humanoids requires explicit modeling of base-limb coupling and ensuring compliance between high-level planning and low-level control execution.
> - Strategic simplification of dynamics and constraint handling (e.g., converting inequalities to penalty costs) in an MPC planner can enable real-time performance without sacrificing critical physical considerations.
> - A hierarchical optimization structure that prioritizes limb motion tracking in the low-level controller is crucial to prevent the amplification of limb control errors and their destabilizing inertial effects on the robot's base.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Humanoid robots are designed for operation in human-centric environments, presenting a broad range of potential applications. A common design characteristic of these robots is the substantial mass of their limbs, which often accounts for 40% to 60% of the total robot mass, primarily due to the integrated servomotors and gearboxes. This significant limb mass leads to a critical coupling between the robot's base and its limbs, which profoundly influences overall balance and stability, particularly during dynamic movements or interaction with irregular terrains. The primary challenge in controlling such robots lies in managing the fluctuating mass and inertia distribution caused by limb motion and mitigating the destabilizing effects of limb control errors.

Current approaches to whole-body control for humanoid robots can be broadly categorized into learning-based and model-based methods, both typically involving a planning and a tracking module. Learning-based methods, utilizing techniques such as reinforcement learning or imitation learning, implicitly capture base-limb coupling. However, these methods often lack interpretability, and their tracking modules, which commonly employ high-gain Proportional-Integral-Derivative (PID) controllers, exhibit performance degradation due to the nonlinear dynamics of humanoid robots.

Model-based methods, in contrast, explicitly model the base-limb coupling. Their planning modules frequently formulate Model Predictive Control (MPC) schemes based on high-fidelity kinematics and dynamics. A significant limitation of these MPC approaches is the high dimensionality of optimization variables and the extensive number of constraints, which render real-time computation challenging. The tracking modules in model-based systems, consisting of instantaneous feedback controllers, aim for improved tracking performance by considering nonlinear dynamics. However, these controllers often operate with independent objectives and constraints, failing to explicitly ensure compliance with the policy generated by the planner. This discrepancy can result in unresolved limb control errors and subsequent robot instability.

This work addresses the limitations observed in existing model-based control frameworks by proposing a real-time, model-based whole-body control solution. The proposed framework aims to explicitly account for the impact of heavy limbs on mass and inertia distribution, minimize limb control errors, and ensure compliance between the planner's policy and the controller's execution, thereby enhancing stability and performance beyond previous methods.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to develop a whole-body control framework specifically tailored for humanoid robots equipped with heavy limbs, to effectively address the associated balance challenges during dynamic locomotion and on uneven terrains.

The specific objectives underpinning this goal include:
- To account for the impact of heavy limbs on the robot's mass and inertia distribution within a control framework.
- To enable real-time planning of motion and contact forces, overcoming the computational intensity often associated with detailed robot models.
- To minimize control errors in the robot's limbs, which can otherwise induce instability in the robot's base.
- To ensure explicit compliance between the high-level motion policy generated by the planner and the low-level torque commands executed by the controller.
- To demonstrate the framework's capability to achieve dynamic walking speeds.
- To enhance the robot's ability to reject external disturbances.
- To maintain robot balance and stability on challenging terrains.

The motivation for pursuing these objectives arises from several critical issues in humanoid robot control:
- The significant mass ratio of limbs (40-60%) in many humanoid robots, driven by actuation requirements, inherently creates strong coupling between the base and limbs.
- This base-limb coupling leads to considerable fluctuations in the robot's Center of Mass (CoM) during rapid or large-range limb movements, making balance maintenance challenging.
- Mitigating this risk by reducing limb speed or range compromises robot responsiveness and operational efficiency, limiting its applicability.
- Control errors in the limbs are amplified by inertial effects and propagate to the base, causing deviations from desired trajectories, overshooting, and oscillations, particularly during dynamic tasks.
- Existing learning-based control approaches, while capable, often lack the interpretability required for understanding and predicting robot behavior.
- Traditional model-based MPC methods, despite their explicit modeling, are often too computationally demanding for real-time execution in full-dynamics scenarios.
- Furthermore, a disconnect often exists in model-based systems between the planner and the instantaneous feedback controller, leading to unaddressed limb control errors and potential instability. The proposed framework seeks to bridge this gap.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed whole-body control framework employs a model-based approach structured into two primary components: a kino-dynamics planner and a hierarchical optimization problem. The framework is designed to operate in real-time on a humanoid robot with heavy limbs, specifically validated on the Orca I robot, which has 28 Degrees of Freedom (DoF) and limbs constituting approximately 64% of its total mass. Of these, 24 DoF corresponding to manipulators and legs are controlled.

**A. Kino-Dynamics Planner**
The kino-dynamics planner is implemented as a Model Predictive Control (MPC) scheme, operating at approximately 100 Hz. Its design prioritizes real-time performance by simplifying system dynamics and constraints while still accounting for the influence of heavy limbs on the robot's mass and inertia distribution.

1. **System Dynamics:** The robot's dynamics are described using a full kinematics model combined with centroidal dynamics (Equations 1a-1c). This formulation explicitly includes the centroidal momentum, comprising both linear and angular momentum, to capture the coupling between limb motion and overall robot balance. A key simplification compared to previous studies is the assumption of only four possible contact points and the omission of contact moments on the feet. This choice reduces the dimensionality of optimization variables and eliminates the need for additional contact wrench consistency constraints, thereby facilitating real-time computation. The state vector for the MPC includes centroidal momentum, base pose, and actuated limb joint positions, while the input vector consists of contact forces and actuated limb joint velocities.

2. **Equality Constraints:** These constraints are defined based on the contact state of each foot. For feet in contact with the ground, the planner enforces zero linear velocity. For swing feet, it sets contact forces to zero and generates reference swing trajectories for position and velocity in operational space. The desired footstep locations for swing legs are calculated using a combination of the Raibert heuristic, a velocity-based feedback term from the capture point, and a centrifugal formulation (Equations 3-4b).

3. **Inequality Constraints:** To ensure dynamic feasibility and prevent hardware damage, several inequality constraints are established (Equations 5a-5e). These include:
 - Friction pyramid constraints for contact forces to prevent slipping.
 - Bounds on vertical contact forces.
 - Joint position and velocity limits for actuated limbs.
 - Collision avoidance. The collision model for lower limbs is simplified to consider only the distance between the right and left feet (Equation 6), thus preventing foot-to-foot collisions. Self-collisions in other links, such as upper limbs, are primarily managed by joint position limits to reduce computational overhead.

4. **Cost Function:** The planner's cost function (Equation 7) comprises a state tracking term, an input regularization term, and an inequality constraint penalty term. To simplify the optimization problem, all inequality constraints are converted into penalty costs using Radial Basis Functions (RBFs), which act as log-barriers in the feasible space and switch to quadratic functions near the constraint boundaries (Equations 8-9). This transformation allows the problem to be treated as an equality-constrained optimization.
 The kino-dynamics planner is solved using Sequential Quadratic Programming (SQP) via the OCS2 toolbox.

**B. Hierarchical Optimization**
The hierarchical optimization problem, based on Hierarchical Quadratic Programming (HQP), operates at approximately 800 Hz. Its purpose is to accurately track the reference motion and forces generated by the kino-dynamics planner, specifically prioritizing limb motion tracking to minimize errors and ensure compliance with the planner's policy. The optimization variable consists of generalized accelerations and contact forces. The tasks are prioritized as follows (from highest to lowest):

1. **Physical Consistency:** This highest-priority task ensures that the planned motion and forces are physically feasible by constraining them to the manifold defined by the full floating-base dynamics of the robot (Equations 10-11).

2. **Limb Motion Tracking:** This task is divided into:
 - Swing leg motion tracking in operational space, where PD control is used to track the reference swing trajectory (Equation 12).
 - Upper limb motion tracking in joint space, using PD control to track reference joint trajectories (Equation 13).

3. **Base Motion Tracking:** The base's reference pose and velocity are tracked in operational space using PD control (Equation 14).

4. **Contact Forces Tracking:** This task, assigned the lowest priority, regulates contact force distribution by tracking the reference contact forces (Equation 15).

Additional constraints within the HQP include:
- **Contact Motion Consistency:** Ensures zero accelerations at the contact points of stance legs (Equation 16).
- **Torque Limits:** Enforces the physical actuation limits of the robot's joints (Equation 17).
- **Friction Constraints:** Keeps contact forces within an approximated friction cone to prevent slipping.

Upon solving the hierarchical optimization problem for optimal accelerations and contact forces, the desired torque commands for the actuators ($\tau^d_j$) are computed using the actuated part of the robot's dynamics (Equation 18).
The hierarchical optimization problem is solved using QPOASES.

The entire control framework integrates user input, a state estimator (Kalman filter fusing motor encoders, IMU, and Lidar data at 800 Hz), the kino-dynamics planner, and the hierarchical optimization module, running on an Intel Core i7-13650HX processor. The kinematics and dynamics computations are handled by the Pinocchio library.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The proposed whole-body control framework was validated through a series of experiments conducted in both simulation (Gazebo environment) and on a physical Orca I humanoid robot. Comparisons were made against two other model-based methods: `cI` (SRBD-based MPC planner with inverse dynamics tracker) and `cNH` (the proposed kino-dynamics planner combined with a non-prioritized Quadratic Programming tracker from). The proposed method is referred to as `cH`.

**A. Simulation Results for Comparison**

1. **Dynamic Walking:**
 - **Performance:** `cH` demonstrated stable dynamic walking at speeds up to 1.2 m/s. It enabled coordinated upper limb motion, which was not observed with `cI`.
 - **Comparison with `cI`:** `cI` failed at a desired velocity of 0.5 m/s. This failure was attributed to significant model error in its Single Rigid-Body Dynamics (SRBD) planner, which struggled to account for the rapidly changing mass and inertia distribution caused by heavy limb motion.
 - **Comparison with `cNH`:** `cNH` fell at a desired velocity of 1.2 m/s. At 1.0 m/s, `cNH` exhibited increased limb tracking errors and struggled to stabilize the robot, as shown in Figure 4. Specifically, `cNH` attempted to utilize limb inertia to reduce the base roll tracking error, which consequently amplified limb tracking errors. This resulted in larger velocity tracking errors and base state tracking errors (Figure 4a, 4c, 4e, 4f) compared to `cH`, which demonstrated better base-limb coupling.

2. **Disturbance Rejection:**
 - **Performance:** `cH` successfully resisted external force disturbances up to 60 N.
 - **Comparison with `cI`:** `cI` failed when subjected to an external force of 20 N, indicating its SRBD model was insufficient for generating appropriate stabilizing motions and forces for robots with heavy limbs under disturbance.
 - **Comparison with `cNH`:** `cNH` failed at an external force of 60 N. When a 40 N force was applied, `cNH` showed considerable deviation along the y-axis and height oscillation after the disturbance was removed (Figure 5b, 5c). This behavior was attributed to large limb control errors, which, amplified by inertial effects, caused the base to deviate and made balance maintenance difficult. `cH`, in contrast, demonstrated more monotonic and smoother motion responses following the removal of external forces, indicating improved base-limb coupling.

**B. Hardware Results**
Hardware experiments were performed using the Orca I robot in real-world scenarios.
- **Uneven Terrain:** The robot successfully navigated uneven terrain consisting of a 5 cm and a 10 cm high stage, maintaining stability throughout the process (Figure 6). This demonstrates the robustness of the proposed framework in challenging real-world environments.
- **Other Scenarios:** The video submission further showcased the robot's ability to walk in outdoor environments and resist disturbances in hardware, consistent with simulation findings.

**C. Real-time Performance**
The computational efficiency of the proposed framework was quantified:
- The average computation time for the kino-dynamics planner was approximately 6.57 ms to 7.89 ms across various experiments. This falls within the target 100 Hz operating frequency (10 ms cycle time).
- The average computation time for the hierarchical optimization was approximately 0.52 ms to 0.58 ms. This is well within its target 800 Hz operating frequency (1.25 ms cycle time).
These results confirm that the simplifications in system dynamics and constraints, coupled with the hierarchical optimization structure, enable the framework to achieve real-time performance while maintaining control efficacy.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of humanoid robotics by addressing a fundamental challenge: the stable and dynamic control of robots with heavy limbs. The proposed whole-body control framework offers a model-based solution that mitigates the inherent balance issues arising from substantial limb mass and base-limb coupling.

The significance of this work is demonstrated through several key aspects:
- **Enhanced Performance Metrics:** The framework enables humanoid robots to achieve dynamic walking speeds of up to 1.2 m/s, resist external disturbances up to 60 N, and maintain stability on challenging terrains such as uneven surfaces and outdoor environments. These performance metrics exceed those of comparative methods, indicating an advancement in robot capabilities.
- **Real-time Operability:** By strategically simplifying system dynamics and constraints within the MPC planner and utilizing a hierarchical optimization for tracking, the framework achieves real-time computational efficiency. This makes the approach practical for deployment on physical humanoid robots, moving beyond purely theoretical or off-line planning solutions.
- **Improved Control Fidelity and Compliance:** The hierarchical optimization explicitly prioritizes limb motion tracking and ensures compliance with the planner's policy. This mechanism effectively minimizes limb control errors and prevents the undesirable exploitation of limb inertia for base stabilization in underactuated directions, a common issue identified in previous approaches. This explicit coupling between planning and control execution contributes to overall system stability and predictability.
- **Model-Based Interpretability:** As a model-based approach, the framework retains the advantage of interpretability, allowing engineers to understand and predict robot behavior, which can be a limitation of some learning-based methods.

The potential impact of this research is substantial for the advancement and deployment of humanoid robots. By enabling more agile, robust, and reliable operation, the framework could facilitate the use of humanoids in a wider range of applications, including:
- **Logistics and Manufacturing:** Robots capable of dynamic movement and disturbance rejection can operate more efficiently and safely in complex industrial environments.
- **Domestic and Healthcare Assistance:** Enhanced stability and control would improve the safety and effectiveness of robots interacting with humans in homes or clinical settings.
- **Disaster Response and Exploration:** Robots with improved locomotion on uneven terrain and robust disturbance rejection would be better suited for hazardous or unstructured environments.

Furthermore, the methodology presented offers insights for future control system designs for complex robotic systems, particularly those with significant internal dynamics and underactuated degrees of freedom. The framework lays a foundation for future extensions to more complex human-like walking patterns and other intricate motions, furthering the capabilities of humanoid robots.
