# Model or Harness? An Interaction-Centric Taxonomy for Localizing Agent Failures

> [!summary] Summary
> An interaction-centric taxonomy that localizes agent failures to the interaction edge where they originate plus a fault side naming the component to repair. It defines eight components, 41 failure modes, and a root-cause rule labeling the earliest unrecovered failure. Four frontier-model judges reproduce human labels, the best reaching Cohen's kappa of 0.76 on category labels.

## Problem

- Outcome-level evaluation reduces agent failures to system-level results, hiding where the fault originated and which repair - post-training, harness, environment, or benchmark - would help.
- Existing taxonomies are benchmark-specific or setting-specific flat lists, giving fine-grained modes without shared structure or any indication of which component is at fault.
- Module-based schemes classify by affected internal module, so identical visible symptoms with different causes collapse together and direct fixes at the wrong part of the system.

## Method

- Component vocabulary of eight endpoints - model, owner, grader, third party, context, memory, tool, local and external environment - grouped into User, Harness, Environment families, with peer/subagent as model roles.
- Edge-plus-fault-side label format writing each failure as COMP1 - COMP2 with a fault side, organizing 41 failure modes into a hierarchy where 36 are model-side and five component-side.
- Backward root-cause attribution rule plus an agent-as-a-judge validation pipeline of evidence reconstruction, classification, and reflection, with selective voting across four judges.

## Results

- On 40 worked examples, GPT-5.5 reached 0.80 category accuracy and kappa of 0.76 against human labels; three Claude Opus judges each hit 0.75 accuracy, kappa 0.70 to 0.71.
- Inter-judge agreement matched human agreement, peaking at kappa of 0.84 between two Opus versions; full failure-mode labels agreed less, at 0.62 to 0.72 accuracy.
- Selective voting traded coverage for precision: three-of-four agreement gave 0.83 category precision at 90% coverage, unanimity gave 0.96 precision at 68% coverage.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Identical observable failures can demand opposite interventions; separating where a failure surfaces from which component caused it is what makes failure labels actionable.
> - Judges systematically over-blame the model when evidence is incomplete, so attribution schemes need abstention or ensembling rather than forced labeling of every case.
> - Descriptive taxonomies say nothing about frequency, and sparse sources like system cards or issue reports often cannot pin a unique root cause.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The deployment of Large Language Models (LLMs) in increasingly autonomous and long-running applications has expanded the complexity of agent systems. These systems interact repeatedly with various components, including users, tools, memory, harnesses, and environments, leading to an enlarged "failure surface." When an agent fails in such complex scenarios, identifying the origin of the failure and the responsible component for repair presents a significant challenge. This difficulty creates what the authors term a "repair-assignment problem," where a single observed failure might require diverse interventions, such as model post-training, harness engineering, environment redesign, or benchmark repair, depending on its root cause.

Existing approaches to classifying agent failures often reduce these issues to system-level outcomes, obscuring the specific interaction or component responsible. Prior work includes taxonomies tied to particular benchmarks (e.g., Deng et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2026a), analyses focused on specific settings like multi-agent coordination (e.g., Cemri et al., 2025; Lin et al., 2025), or flat lists of failure modes (e.g., Vinay, 2025). While valuable within their scope, these taxonomies typically do not explicitly indicate which system component is at fault, thereby limiting their utility for directing specific interventions. For instance, a broad label like "Execution Failure" might conflate an unrecoverable external service error with a model's failure to recover from a transient issue, each requiring a different type of repair.

Some research has attempted module-specific classification, such as Zhu et al. (2025a), which categorizes errors by the internal agent module affected (e.g., memory, reflection, planning, action). However, without a clear distinction between where a failure manifests and which component caused it, outcome-level labels can still group disparate causes. Other related work includes failure localization in execution traces, such as Barke et al. (2026) which identifies critical unrecoverable events, and Qiao et al. (2026) which verifies failure hypotheses against interaction traces. The security literature, exemplified by Microsoft AI Red Team (2025) and Shah et al. (2026), organizes failures by threats and consequences, often identifying causes at producer-consumer boundaries.

This paper positions itself as complementary to these efforts. It addresses the need for a general framework that maps each failure to the necessary intervention by providing an "interaction-centric taxonomy." This approach aims to explicitly identify the causal event, localize it to an interaction between components, and determine the component responsible for the fault. This distinction is intended to differentiate failures that require model-level interventions from those demanding harness engineering or adjustments to the evaluation setup.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and validate an interaction-centric taxonomy for localizing agent failures. The motivation stems from the challenges associated with diagnosing and resolving failures in complex LLM-based agent systems. As agents operate in increasingly autonomous and interactive environments, their behavior emerges from intricate interactions among multiple components, including the LLM itself, the harness, users, tools, memory, and the environment.

The authors identify a critical problem: current evaluation methods and failure taxonomies frequently reduce agent failures to system-level outcomes. This reduction makes it difficult to ascertain the precise origin of a fault, leading to ambiguity in determining the most effective intervention. For example, an agent might fail to follow an instruction. This could be due to the harness erroneously removing the instruction from the context, or the model itself failing to process an available instruction. Both scenarios result in the same observed failure, but the former requires a harness fix, while the latter demands a model-level intervention. Without accurate fault localization, developers face a "repair-assignment problem," potentially directing repair efforts to the wrong part of the system.

To address this, the paper aims to provide a shared, structured framework for understanding agent failure modes that is directly actionable. The specific contributions articulated by the authors are:

1. **Introduction of an interaction-centric taxonomy:** To organize 41 agent failure modes by assigning each to an interaction edge between two components and a fault side, indicating the responsible component. The taxonomy explicitly treats interactions between components as the unit of analysis.
2. **Grounding the taxonomy with worked examples:** To illustrate the failure modes using real-world cases drawn from public benchmarks, model system cards, published reports, and logged agent trajectories. This aims to demonstrate the practical applicability of the taxonomy across diverse agent architectures (e.g., coding assistants, long-horizon personal assistants, multi-agent systems) and modalities.
3. **Evaluation of operational reproducibility:** To test whether independent reasoning agents can consistently apply the taxonomy and recover human-assigned categories. This validation seeks to provide evidence that the categories capture a shared structure rather than subjective labeling preferences.

By achieving these objectives, the research intends to equip practitioners with a rigorous diagnostic tool that complements task-specific analyses, supports consistent failure diagnosis across various agent systems, and directly informs targeted repair strategies.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed a multi-stage methodology involving the iterative development of the taxonomy, a defined attribution rule, and a validation process using independent reasoning agents.

**Taxonomy Design and Structure:**
The core of the methodology is an "interaction-centric" view of agent failures. An agent system is conceptualized as a set of interacting components, each defined in Table 1 of the paper. These components include:
- **Model:** The LLM policy.
- **Owner:** The human or upstream system setting the task.
- **Grader:** The mechanism evaluating task success, typically invisible to the agent.
- **Third party:** External actors encountered during execution.
- **Harness-related components:** Context (active information), Memory (persistent storage), Tool (bidirectional interface).
- **Environment-related components:** Local environment (execution setting), External environment (remote services).
In multi-agent settings, other models are described by their roles (e.g., peer, subagent) interacting with the focal model.

Failures are localized to an "interaction edge" between two components (e.g., `TOOL — MODEL`) and assigned a "fault side" indicating the component responsible for the failure (e.g., `fault: MODEL`). This structure is visually represented as a radial map, with the model at the hub and other components grouped into "User," "Harness," and "Environment" families. The taxonomy defines 41 specific failure modes, each mapped to a particular edge and fault side. For instance, `TOOL — MODEL · fault: TOOL` denotes a failure on the tool side of the tool-model interaction, while `TOOL — MODEL · fault: MODEL` indicates a model-side fault within the same interaction.

The taxonomy was developed iteratively. Initial definitions were refined through reviewing failures from public benchmarks, model system cards, published reports, and logged agent trajectories. Overlaps and ambiguities led to adjustments in component definitions and failure modes until stability was achieved. For failures with a safety or security impact, a separate annotation was added, drawing primarily from the OWASP Top 10 for LLM and Agentic Applications.

**Failure Attribution Rule:**
To ensure consistency in labeling, a fixed attribution rule was established for cases involving cascading failures. When a single trajectory contains multiple errors, the process begins with the observed system-level failure. Its causal chain is traced backward to identify the earliest failure from which execution does not recover. This "root-cause principle" ensures that the taxonomy label is assigned to the event where an intervention would have altered the outcome, rather than to downstream symptoms.

**Grounding with Worked Examples:**
The taxonomy was grounded using 40 curated worked examples. These examples were selected to illustrate various interaction edges and failure modes across different settings, including coding agents, personal assistants, and multi-agent systems. The selection was illustrative, not exhaustive, and the examples served as the evaluation set for the validation phase.

**Validation with Agent-as-a-Judge:**
To evaluate the operational reproducibility of the taxonomy, the authors employed an "agent-as-a-judge" setup. Four frontier LLMs (GPT-5.5, Claude Opus 4.6, 4.7, and 4.8) were configured as independent judges.
- **Task:** For each worked example, a judge agent received the taxonomy definitions and a reference to the original failure source (e.g., GitHub issue, blog post, logged trajectory), but not the human-assigned label. The judge's task was to independently review the source, identify the earliest unrecovered failure, and predict: 1) the interaction category (`COMP1 — COMP2 · fault: FAULT`); and 2) the complete failure-mode label (`COMP1 — COMP2 · fault: FAULT · Failure Mode`).
- **Pipeline:** Each judgment was produced in a three-turn session:
 1. **Evidence Reconstruction:** The judge retrieved relevant evidence from the source and organized it into a chronological account.
 2. **Failure Classification:** Using the reconstructed account and taxonomy definitions, the judge identified the root-cause failure and assigned an interaction edge, fault side, and failure mode.
 3. **Reflection and Disambiguation:** The judge checked its proposed label against predefined disambiguation rules (e.g., distinguishing context rationale erosion from over-initiative) and either confirmed or revised it. The final label from this turn was used for evaluation.
- **Evaluation Metrics:** The judges' predictions were compared against human-assigned labels using exact-match accuracy, macro-averaged F1 score, and Cohen's κ for inter-annotator agreement. Category-level evaluation required the correct interaction edge and fault side, while failure-mode evaluation additionally required the correct named failure mode.
- **Selective Voting:** To improve precision, an ensemble approach using selective voting was explored. Labels were assigned only when a minimum number (k) of judges agreed on a category, with the failure mode determined by majority vote among the agreeing judges.

This comprehensive methodological approach aimed to ensure both the theoretical coherence and practical applicability of the proposed failure taxonomy.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research produced an interaction-centric taxonomy for agent failures, grounded in empirical examples, and validated its reproducibility using reasoning agents.

**Taxonomy Structure and Coverage:**
The developed taxonomy comprises 41 distinct failure modes. These modes are organized hierarchically by "family" (User, Harness, Environment), then by specific component within each family, and finally by the interaction edge between the model and that component. Each failure mode is assigned a clear fault side, indicating whether the model or the other interacting component is primarily responsible.
- An observation from the taxonomy's structure is that 36 of the 41 role-specific failure modes are assigned to the model side, with only five attributed to surrounding components. This imbalance is partly explained by the attribution rule, which assigns fault to the model when a more capable model could have avoided or recovered from a failure under the given conditions.
- The taxonomy was grounded in 40 worked examples drawn from various sources, covering nearly all defined failure modes. These examples illustrate the application of the taxonomy across diverse agent types and scenarios.

**Reproducibility Validation with Agent-as-a-Judge:**
The agent-as-a-judge experiments assessed whether independent reasoning agents could consistently apply the taxonomy definitions to identify failure modes.
- **Agreement with Human Labels (Category):** For category labels (interaction edge and fault side), GPT-5.5 demonstrated the highest agreement with human annotations, achieving a Cohen's κ of 0.76. Claude Opus 4.6 and 4.7 each reached κ = 0.71, followed by Claude Opus 4.8 at κ = 0.70. These scores suggest that the agents recovered human labels well above chance, indicating a shared underlying structure in the categories rather than annotator-specific preferences.
- **Agreement Among Judges (Category):** Pairwise agreement among the LLM judges themselves was comparable to their agreement with human annotators, with the highest pairwise κ reaching 0.84 between Claude Opus 4.6 and 4.8.
- **Agreement with Human Labels (Failure Mode):** Agreement on the complete failure-mode label, which includes the specific named failure, was generally lower across all pairs compared to category agreement. For example, GPT-5.5 achieved κ = 0.71 for failure modes.
- **Sources of Disagreement:**
 - **Heterogeneous Source Material:** The primary reason for disagreement was attributed to the variability in the original failure sources. Some sources, such as brief incident reports, did not provide sufficient evidence for judges to confidently identify a unique root cause.
 - **Difficulty in Root-Cause Attribution:** Even with available evidence, determining the earliest unrecoverable failure proved challenging. The example in Appendix A.2 illustrates a case where the judge misattributed a harness defect (undelivered scripted email) to the model (failure to check for the reply). This aligns with findings from other benchmarks, such as OpenRCA 2.0, which highlight models' difficulty in reconstructing verified causal propagation paths.
 - **Increased Label Space:** Predicting the specific named failure mode introduced additional complexity due to the larger set of possible labels and the potential for similar symptoms from different failure modes. When judges were provided with the correct category (interaction edge and fault side), their failure-mode accuracy improved for the Opus models, suggesting that some errors originated at the category selection stage.
- **Selective Voting Performance:** Implementing selective voting, where labels are assigned only when a certain number of judges agree, improved precision at the cost of coverage. For instance, requiring agreement from at least three out of four judges yielded an 83% category precision at 90% coverage. Unanimous agreement (four out of four judges) further increased precision to 96% but reduced coverage to 68%.

Overall, the findings indicate that the interaction-centric taxonomy provides a reproducible framework for classifying agent failures, with frontier LLMs demonstrating a notable capacity to apply these distinctions consistently.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The proposed interaction-centric taxonomy offers several significant contributions and potential impacts on the development, evaluation, and improvement of autonomous agent systems.

**Actionable Failure Localization:**
The primary significance lies in its direct actionability. By localizing failures to specific interaction edges and assigning responsibility to a particular component, the taxonomy transforms abstract failure observations into concrete targets for intervention.
- **Model-side failures:** Directly indicate areas for model post-training, fine-tuning, or architectural improvements (e.g., enhancing reasoning, instruction-following, or recovery policies).
- **Harness-side failures:** Point to necessary changes in the agent's scaffolding, context management, memory systems, or tool integration (e.g., improving context compaction logic, memory writing strategies, or tool wrapper robustness).
- **Environment or Grader failures:** Highlight issues outside the agent's direct control, such as unreliable external services, stale data delivery, or flaws in the evaluation setup. These require interventions like redesigning evaluation conditions or addressing external system dependencies before they can accurately judge agent capabilities. This distinction is crucial for efficient debugging and resource allocation, preventing misdirected efforts.

**Clearer Distribution of Responsibility:**
The taxonomy provides a structured view of how responsibility for failures is distributed across the agent system. The observation that most failure modes are attributed to the model side (36 out of 41) suggests that while significant improvements can be made within the LLM itself, the remaining non-model failures identify critical points where model improvement alone will not suffice. This clarifies the scope for different development teams (e.g., model developers, harness engineers, evaluation designers).

**Enhanced Reproducibility and Consistency in Diagnosis:**
The validation using independent reasoning agents as judges demonstrates that the taxonomy's categories capture a shared, reproducible structure. The high Cohen's κ scores (up to 0.76 for category agreement with human labels and up to 0.84 among judges) suggest that the distinctions are not subjective or annotator-specific. This consistency is valuable for:
- **Standardized Communication:** Providing a common language for researchers and practitioners to discuss, categorize, and diagnose agent failures.
- **Automated Diagnosis:** Laying groundwork for more robust automated failure analysis systems. While the agent-as-a-judge framework has limitations (e.g., reduced coverage with higher precision requirements, difficulty in root-cause attribution from limited evidence), the results indicate a promising direction for using LLMs to assist in failure localization.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
The authors acknowledge several limitations. The taxonomy is descriptive rather than quantitative, meaning it organizes failures but does not estimate their relative frequencies. Its current form is derived from reviewed cases and may need expansion as agent architectures and harnesses evolve. Furthermore, the effectiveness of the taxonomy depends on the quality and completeness of available evidence in agent traces or reports. The practical deployment of the agent-as-a-judge framework may also face challenges due to current accuracy limitations and the trade-off between coverage and precision in ensembling strategies.

Despite these limitations, the paper offers a principled framework that moves beyond outcome-level failure reporting to facilitate a more granular, component-specific understanding of why agent systems fail. This has the potential to accelerate the development of more robust, reliable, and deployable autonomous agents by enabling targeted and efficient problem-solving.
