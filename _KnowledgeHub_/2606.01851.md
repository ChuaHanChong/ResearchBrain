# PHASOR: Phase-Anchored Universal Action Representations for Humanoid Embodiments

> [!summary] Summary
> PHASOR, a framework that leverages the intrinsic periodic structure of motion to create a unified, structured action representation for humanoids, enables robust cross-embodiment transfer and improves robot policy learning, achieving 90.3% R@1 for Human→Robot motion retrieval and reducing Mean Per Joint Position Error to 1.62 mm in motion imitation tasks.

## Problem

- Current action representations are often entangled with specific robot morphologies, hindering effective transferability of learned policies across diverse humanoid platforms.
- Existing latent action spaces frequently lack temporal organization and interpretability, complicating fine-grained control and analysis of robot behaviors.
- Prior periodic motion representations struggle with compressing whole-body motion into a single signal and often lack the static postural context necessary for comprehensive motion capture.

## Method

- **PHASOR** decomposes motion into per-body-part phase parameters and a complementary pose branch, using a differentiable Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) for periodic feature extraction.
- A human-anchored multi-embodiment alignment strategy aligns robots to a frozen human-pretrained backbone through lightweight, robot-specific input adapters.
- The framework employs a hierarchical InfoNCE loss (incorporating both hard and soft semantic targets) and geometric penalties to ensure robust ==cross-embodiment== alignment and trajectory consistency within the phase manifold.

## Results

- PHASOR achieved **90.3%** R@1 for Human→Robot motion retrieval and **93.9%** MRR, significantly outperforming baseline methods in cross-embodiment alignment tasks.
- The method resulted in the lowest Mean Per Joint Position Error (1.62 mm) for next-frame robot pose prediction in motion imitation and was the only approach to outperform a raw-kinematics baseline in teleoperation (64.75 mm MPJPE).
- In a reinforcement learning locomotion task, PHASOR-guided robots consistently learned stable, forward walking motions, demonstrating the effectiveness of the phase embedding as a robust reward signal.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decomposing motion into per-body-part phase parameters (amplitude, frequency, phase shift) creates an intrinsically morphology-invariant and semantically interpretable action space for humanoid robots.
> - Anchoring the shared phase manifold to human motion data, combined with robot-specific lightweight adapters, enables efficient and robust cross-embodiment transfer of behaviors.
> - Separating periodic (phase) and non-periodic (pose) components, with controlled interaction during decoding via FiLM, allows for a 'pose-free' phase manifold crucial for cross-embodiment alignment while still providing necessary postural context.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of scalable robot policy learning is contingent upon effective action embedding spaces. Current methodologies for learning action representations often treat action latents as task-specific or as unstructured sequences of per-frame joint states. This approach results in several limitations, particularly in the context of diversified humanoid robot platforms.

Existing action encoders tend to entangle the learned representations with the specific morphology of the training robot. Consequently, a behavior learned on one robot may not transfer effectively to another, even when both are humanoid, because the same physical action maps to distinct regions in the latent space. Furthermore, these unstructured latent spaces often lack temporal organization. Proximity within the latent space may not reflect temporal proximity in the actual motion, which can impede interpretability, precise control, and the generalizability of policies across different robotic embodiments.

Within the domain of latent action representations, contemporary robot policies, including vision-language-action models and diffusion-based controllers, generally rely on learned action spaces as an intermediate layer between perception and motor execution. These methods, while effective for certain tasks, typically treat motion as a generic sequence and do not explicitly leverage motion-specific structural priors, such as periodicity.

The field of periodic representations in motion has explored decomposing motion into components like amplitude, frequency, and phase. Early work modulated network weights based on foot-contact heuristics, evolving to unsupervised learning of multi-dimensional phase manifolds via FFT bottlenecks. These methods have found application in character animation, style modulation, and skill discovery. However, these periodic representations have largely remained confined to character animation and have not been systematically applied as universal action representations for humanoid robots or as a substrate for cross-embodiment alignment. Key limitations of prior periodic approaches include compressing whole-body motion into a single representation, which can blur distinct periodicities of individual joints, and relying solely on velocity, which omits static postural context.

For humanoid cross-embodiment transfer, various alignment mechanisms exist. Kinematic retargeting maps reference motion to a target body, primarily for data generation rather than shared representation learning. Shared-latent approaches project motions into a common space through methods like contrastive learning on extrinsic kinematics, scaled per-part embeddings, differentiable retargeting, visual transfer, or language-guided features. These typically focus on per-frame similarity based on extrinsic or task-specific features, rather than leveraging motion-intrinsic structure.

PHASOR addresses these gaps by explicitly positioning the action embedding space itself as a primary design target, focusing on its intrinsic quality. It integrates the concept of motion periodicity, which is hypothesized to be intrinsic to behavior rather than embodiment, into a structured, interpretable, and transferable action representation for humanoid robots. By adopting a human-anchored approach and introducing per-body-part periodic decomposition with complementary pose conditioning, PHASOR aims to create a unified action embedding space that facilitates robust cross-embodiment transfer and improves downstream robot policy learning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind PHASOR is to overcome the limitations of current action representations, which struggle with interpretability, controllability, and transferability across diverse humanoid robot platforms. The core objectives stem from addressing the identified shortcomings:

1. **Bridging the Generalization Gap Across Embodiments**: The proliferation of humanoid robot platforms necessitates action representations that can generalize effectively from one robot morphology to another, or from human demonstrations to robots. Current action encoders often produce representations that are inherently entangled with the specific kinematics and morphology of the robot on which they were trained. This embodiment-specific nature acts as a bottleneck, severely limiting the transfer of learned policies or behaviors. A key objective is to develop representations that are intrinsically morphology-invariant.

2. **Enhancing Interpretability and Controllability of Latent Spaces**: Existing action latents are frequently unstructured, lacking clear temporal organization or interpretable semantic meaning in their dimensions. This makes it challenging for researchers and engineers to analyze trajectories, understand the underlying behavior, or exert fine-grained control over the robot's actions within the latent space. The motivation is to create an action space where proximity reflects temporal alignment and semantic similarity, and whose dimensions carry interpretable meaning, thereby facilitating analysis and control.

3. **Exploiting Intrinsic Motion Structure**: Unlike data types such as text or images, motion is characterized by fundamental periodic components. For instance, walking involves rhythmic oscillations of limbs. Current encoders often overlook this inherent physical structure, treating motion as a generic sequence. A central motivation is to leverage this intrinsic periodicity, specifically amplitude, frequency, and phase shift, as a shared and morphology-invariant substrate for aligning action embeddings. The hypothesis is that phase encodes "where in the action the body is," while amplitude and frequency encode "what action is performed," a distinction unstructured encoders conflate.

4. **Addressing Limitations of Prior Periodic Approaches**: While periodic representations exist, they face two specific challenges in the cross-embodiment robot context:
 - **Whole-body compression**: Previous methods often compress the entire body into a single representation, which can blur the distinct, independent periodicities of individual body parts (e.g., arms swinging independently of legs). An objective is to decompose the phase encoding by body part to preserve each limb's unique periodic signature, which can serve as a natural unit for cross-embodiment correspondence.
 - **Lack of Postural Context**: Velocity-based periodic encoding captures *how* a body moves but not *where* it is (its static posture). This can lead to motions with similar oscillation but different poses (e.g., upright vs. crouched walking) collapsing to nearby points in the latent space. A specific objective is to introduce a separate pose branch to supply non-periodic postural context to the decoder without contaminating the phase manifold itself, ensuring the phase representation remains "pose-free."

5. **Enabling Human-Anchored Multi-Embodiment Alignment**: Given the abundance and diversity of human motion data compared to fragmented robot data, a practical objective is to anchor the shared, structured motion manifold in human motion. This allows new robots to be integrated through lightweight adapters, rather than requiring them to learn the entire manifold from scratch, promoting efficient knowledge transfer.

In summary, PHASOR aims to construct an action embedding space that is inherently structured, interpretable, controllable, and fundamentally embodiment-agnostic by design, thereby providing a more robust and scalable foundation for robot policy learning and cross-embodiment transfer.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

PHASOR (PHase Action Space for cross-embOdiment Representation) is a framework designed to learn a structured action embedding across human and humanoid robot embodiments by leveraging the intrinsic periodic structure of motion. The methodology decomposes motion into a phase manifold, capturing periodic dynamics, and a complementary pose branch, handling non-periodic configuration details. The phase manifold functions as a shared coordinate system for cross-embodiment alignment, while the pose branch remains per-embodiment.

The training process is bifurcated into two stages: establishing a human oracle, followed by aligning robots to this oracle.

#### 3.1. From Periodic Prior to Structured Action Manifold

The task is to generate a phase manifold P(τ) ∈ R^(T × 2M) and a complementary pose code such that semantically equivalent motion windows from different embodiments map to nearby trajectories within a shared coordinate system, while maintaining interpretability and per-embodiment controllability.

#### 3.2. Per-Part Periodic Decomposition

To address the issue of conflating independent joint rhythms, PHASOR partitions each embodiment into five semantic kinematic parts: {Left Arm (LA), Right Arm (RA), Trunk (TK), Left Leg (LL), Right Leg (RL)}. Each part `b` is assigned its own phase encoder Φ_b, which models the part's velocity signal v_b(τ) as K_b sinusoids. The periodic parameters (Amplitude A_b,c, Frequency F_b,c, Bias B_b,c) are extracted via a differentiable Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) layer, and the phase shift S_b,c is regressed. The total number of channels M is 16, distributed among parts based on their rhythmic complexity (e.g., legs receive more channels). Each channel is then projected onto an amplitude-scaled circle, and these are concatenated across all parts and channels to form the part-structured phase manifold P(τ) ∈ R^(T × 32). This design ensures that independent encoders preserve each part's rhythmic signature without cross-part contamination. Root linear velocity is concatenated to the trunk input for periodicity extraction.

#### 3.3. Complementary Pose Conditioning

To overcome the limitation of velocity-based phase encoding lacking static postural context, a complementary pose branch is introduced. This branch encodes 6D joint rotations and root positions into a compact token sequence z_pose ∈ R^(L_p × d_p), capturing window-level postural configuration. The interaction between the phase and pose branches is confined to the decoding process, utilizing bidirectional FiLM (Feature-wise Linear Modulation). Phase context conditions the pose decoder, and pose context modulates the reconstructed velocity signal before the phase decoder. Crucially, P(τ) is constructed directly from FFT parameters prior to any modulation, maintaining its pose-free nature, which is essential for cross-embodiment alignment.

#### 3.4. Cross-Embodiment Alignment on the Phase Manifold

The morphology-invariant nature of P(τ) channels allows it to serve as a shared coordinate system. Robots are mapped onto this manifold and aligned through semantic correspondence, facilitated by anchoring the system to a human-pretrained backbone.

#### 3.5. Human-Anchored Encoder

Given the abundance of human motion data, the per-part phase encoders E_h = {Φ_b}_b∈B and the phase-to-pose FiLM head are initially pretrained on human motion data and then frozen. This frozen human backbone acts as a shared oracle and anchor. For each robot `k`, a lightweight linear input adapter E_k_r: R^(D_k) → R^66 maps the robot's joint velocities into the human input space. The shared, frozen encoder E_X (identical to E_h) then extracts the phase parameters for the robot, ensuring P_R_k(τ) adheres to the human phase prior. Robot joints are mapped to the same five-part partition.

#### 3.6. Decoder Adaptation and Pose Isolation

The decoding process allows for controlled adaptation. The human phase decoder D_h remains frozen, while robots share a unified phase decoder D_X. D_X is initialized from D_h and fine-tuned with per-robot output adapters D_k_r: R^66 → R^(D_k). Since posture is morphology-dependent, each robot maintains its own dedicated pose branch, supervised solely by reconstruction loss. This design choice ensures that morphology-specific details are kept separate from the shared-manifold alignment.

#### 3.7. Alignment Objective

All alignment supervision is applied to P(τ), independent of the pose branch.
- **Alignment Head (π)**: The phase manifold P(τ) is concatenated with a temporally pooled pose token z_pool_pose. This combined feature is then projected onto a unit sphere S^63 via a lightweight head π, producing z(τ). The pose token helps disambiguate motions with similar rhythms but different postures; its associated weights in π are initialized near zero to prioritize phase alignment initially.
- **Hierarchical Pair Loss (L_pair)**: An InfoNCE objective is used for latent alignment. Its target (T_mix) is a blend of a hard diagonal label (for direct paired targets) and a soft target derived from a pretrained motion-semantic prior (LAMP). This loss is applied at two granularities: per-window for fine motion variation and per-clip (mean-pooled z) for coarser semantic meaning, with their symmetric cross-entropy summed into L_pair.
- **Trajectory Consistency**: Two geometric penalties regularize the temporal shape of z(τ) on the unit sphere: L_speed matches per-frame step size (implicit frequency synchronization), and L_shape matches half-period distance (implicit amplitude synchronization).
- **Total Alignment Loss (L_align)**: The sum of L_pair, L_speed, and L_shape across all robots.

#### 3.8. Training

The framework is optimized in two continuous phases:
1. **Human Pretraining**: The human backbone is initially trained using a weighted Mean Squared Error (MSE) for per-part velocity reconstruction (L_phase), a geodesic distance and MSE for pose reconstruction (L_pose), and a forward-kinematics consistency loss (L_FK) to link the two branches.
2. **Robot Alignment**: After freezing the human oracle, robot components (input/output adapters, shared phase decoder D_X, per-robot pose branches, and alignment head π) are jointly trained. This stage uses a total loss (L_total) comprising L_phase, L_pose, L_FK, and L_align. A short reconstruction-only warm-up period precedes alignment to stabilize robot phase manifolds.

**Data**: The system utilizes 12,616 human motion clips (approximately 40.4 hours) from AMASS CMU and KIT datasets. These clips are retargeted using GMR to four distinct humanoid robots: Unitree G1 (32 DOF), H1.2 (28 DOF), Booster T1 (24 DOF), and Berkeley Humanoid Lite (25 DOF), resulting in 50,464 synchronized human-robot pairs. All experiments use 121-frame (approximately 2-second) motion chunks.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of PHASOR was conducted along two primary axes: embedding quality and downstream utility in robot policy learning.

#### 4.1. Cross-Embodiment Retrieval

The ability of PHASOR to align motions across different embodiments was quantified using a chunk retrieval task. This involved querying chunks from one embodiment against a gallery of another, with each query having one temporally identical positive match. Retrieval accuracy (Recall@k and Mean Reciprocal Rank, MRR) was reported for Human→Robot (H→R), Robot→Human (R→H), and Robot→Robot (R→R) directions.

- **Comparison to Baselines**: The MLP baseline, which employs an unstructured projection of per-embodiment features, achieved reasonable exact-match scores (e.g., 79.9% R@1 H→R). In contrast, the VQ baseline, using a discrete codebook, performed substantially worse (e.g., 37.6% R@1 H→R), attributed to its codebook collapsing fine-grained distinctions necessary for precise retrieval.
- **Impact of Alignment Loss Design (Table 1A)**:
 - The "Only Phase" configuration, utilizing the frozen human-only manifold without cross-embodiment training, demonstrated a strong morphology-invariant temporal prior by surpassing MLP on H→R R@1 (83.0% vs. 79.9%).
 - Introducing cross-embodiment InfoNCE ("Hard only") improved performance across all directions.
 - Further incorporation of LAMP soft targets (semantic motion prior) consistently enhanced accuracy. The combination of coarse clip-level and fine window-level soft targets ("Soft Coarse+Fine") yielded the highest R@1 score in this ablation set, reaching 86.9% for H→R. Notably, gains in R→R retrieval (e.g., 79.3% R@1 for "Soft Coarse+Fine") indicated effective transitive alignment through the shared human anchor, even without direct robot-to-robot training.
- **Impact of Alignment Target (Table 1B)**:
 - With the optimal "Soft Coarse+Fine" loss, incorporating raw root position (X_root) into the alignment head increased H→R R@1 to 88.5%.
 - The proposed method, utilizing the pooled, FiLM-modulated pose token (z_pose) alongside P(τ), achieved the highest performance across all metrics and directions (e.g., 90.3% R@1, 93.9% MRR on H→R). This indicated that the modulated pose token provided relevant postural context without introducing raw morphological details, effectively separating motions with similar rhythms but different postures.

#### 4.2. Manifold Visualization and Interpretability

PCA projections of the learned embedding `z` and raw phase manifold P(τ) revealed several properties (Figure 3, left):
- **Intra-clip Continuity**: Within a single motion clip, the advancing phase ϕ_c(τ) traced a smooth counter-clockwise orbit, with the radius of the orbit reflecting the motion's amplitude. This structured temporal organization is absent in unstructured embeddings.
- **Cross-Embodiment Overlap**: Matched timestamps from different embodiments clustered tightly in the latent space, providing visual confirmation of the human-anchored alignment's effectiveness.
- **Motion Type Separation**: The projections demonstrated that different motion types (e.g., walk, run, jump, kick) were distinguishable by their distinct orbit radii and positions within the manifold.
- **Interpretable Phase Control**: By manipulating the phase shift S_c on a running clip, predictable temporal offsets in the retrieved motion windows were observed (Figure 3, right). This confirmed that the embedding captures motion timing and supports per-embodiment phase control, signifying an interpretable and controllable temporal coordinate.

#### 4.3. Downstream Tasks

The utility of the frozen PHASOR embedding was assessed on three downstream robot tasks, with only a lightweight policy head trained in each case to isolate the impact of representation quality.

- **Motion Imitation and Teleoperation (Figure 4, left)**:
 - **Motion Imitation**: In predicting the G1 robot's next-frame pose from its proprioceptive state and the learned embedding `z`, PHASOR achieved the lowest Mean Per Joint Position Error (MPJPE) of 1.62 mm, outperforming VQ (1.69 mm), MLP (1.83 mm), and a raw proprioceptive baseline (1.83 mm). This suggests PHASOR's explicit phase parameterization effectively pins down the gait-cycle position.
 - **Teleoperation**: When a human motion window was mapped directly to a matching G1 robot pose using `z` alone, PHASOR was the only method to outperform the raw-kinematics baseline (64.75 mm MPJPE vs. 64.85 mm). MLP (66.79 mm) and VQ (65.17 mm) performed worse, indicating that unstructured or quantized projections can discard critical information for teleoperation.
- **RL Locomotion with Phase Reward (Figure 4, right)**:
 - In an IsaacSim locomotion task, where the embedding shaped the reward for robot motion alignment with a human reference, PHASOR-guided robots consistently walked forward steadily on both feet. In contrast, VQ-guided robots under-used one leg and drifted in circles, while MLP-guided robots failed to locomote effectively. This outcome indicates that PHASOR's phase embedding provides an effective and robust reward signal for learning periodic, walking-like motions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

PHASOR introduces a significant conceptual shift by treating the intrinsic periodic structure of motion as a primary design prior for action representations, rather than relying solely on reconstruction from raw sensor data. This approach yields several notable contributions and potential impacts on robotics research and application.

#### 5.1. Significance of the Work

1. **Structured and Interpretable Action Space**: By decomposing motion into per-body-part phase parameters, PHASOR creates a compact, semantically interpretable, and controllable action space. The coordinates of this space directly expose behavior timing (phase shift) and action identity (amplitude and frequency), allowing for intuitive analysis and manipulation of robot movements. This contrasts with many existing latent spaces that are often opaque and difficult to interpret.
2. **Robust Cross-Embodiment Transfer**: The human-anchored multi-embodiment alignment strategy, utilizing a frozen human-pretrained oracle and lightweight robot-specific adapters, demonstrates strong cross-embodiment retrieval capabilities. This method effectively translates knowledge from diverse human motion data to various robot morphologies, addressing a critical challenge in generalist robot policy learning. The alignment emerges transitively through the shared human anchor, indicating the robustness of the phase manifold as a universal substrate.
3. **Demonstrable Downstream Utility**: The structured embedding directly translates into concrete performance gains in practical robot control tasks. Its superior performance in motion imitation, teleoperation, and reinforcement learning, even with a frozen encoder and lightweight policy heads, validates the quality and utility of the learned representation for real-world robotic applications.
4. **Novel Integration of Motion Priors**: PHASOR's methodology represents a novel integration of domain-specific physical priors (motion periodicity) with deep learning techniques. This structured approach provides an alternative to purely data-driven, unstructured latent learning, potentially leading to more sample-efficient and robust learning for complex behaviors.

#### 5.2. Potential Impact

1. **Accelerating Generalist Robot Development**: By providing a unified, transferable action embedding space, PHASOR could significantly accelerate the development of generalist humanoid robots capable of performing a wide array of tasks across different physical platforms. It offers a standardized "language" for motion that can be shared and understood by diverse robot bodies.
2. **Reducing Robot-Specific Data Requirements**: Leveraging abundant human motion data as a shared anchor can substantially reduce the need for extensive, often costly and time-consuming, robot-specific data collection for training. This could democratize access to advanced robot control methodologies.
3. **Enhanced Human-Robot Collaboration and Interaction**: The interpretable and controllable nature of the phase manifold could facilitate more intuitive human teleoperation and programming of robots. Humans could specify motions at a higher, more semantic level (e.g., "increase walking frequency" or "shift phase of left leg") rather than manipulating individual joint angles, fostering more natural human-robot interaction.
4. **Foundation for Complex Behavior Composition**: The structured action space may serve as a fundamental building block for composing and adapting complex rhythmic behaviors, potentially enabling robots to learn and perform intricate sequences of actions with greater ease and flexibility.

#### 5.3. Limitations and Future Work (as identified by the authors)

1. **Behavioral Scope**: PHASOR is inherently built around periodic motion structure. While highly effective for rhythmic, locomotion-style behaviors, its utility and the structure it provides might be less informative for transient, impulsive, or largely aperiodic motions. Future work could explore hybrid representations that integrate periodic and aperiodic components.
2. **Pose Branch Embodiment-Specificity**: The complementary pose branch, which handles non-periodic postural details, currently remains per-embodiment. This means non-periodic posture information is not directly shared or transferred across different bodies. Further research could aim to generalize or align aspects of the pose representation across embodiments.
3. **Validation Environment**: The current validation of PHASOR is confined to simulation environments. The transition and evaluation on physical humanoid robot hardware represent a natural next step and a critical test of the framework's robustness and real-world applicability.

The authors view these limitations as avenues for future exploration rather than fundamental barriers, suggesting continued development building upon the established foundation.
