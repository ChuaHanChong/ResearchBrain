# From Curiosity to Caution: Mitigating Reward Hacking for Best-of-N with Pessimism

> [!summary] Summary
> A new method, "caution," introduces an inference-time pessimistic reward estimate for Best-of-N sampling, effectively preventing reward hacking by penalizing out-of-distribution responses. This approach consistently improves large language model performance on mathematical reasoning tasks, yielding up to a 15.5% increase in true reward accuracy while maintaining monotonic gains with more samples.

## Problem

- Large language models often suffer from "reward hacking" during Best-of-N sampling, where performance initially improves but then degrades for larger numbers of generated samples (N) due to exploitation of reward model imperfections.
- Existing solutions either require constant improvement of inherently fragile reward models or employ overly conservative regularization techniques that penalize genuinely high-quality but atypical responses.
- Reward models, trained on limited human preference data, can learn spurious correlations that are exploited by increasing N, leading to selections that are out-of-distribution (OOD) and of lower true quality.

## Method

- The authors introduce "caution," an inference-time scheme that modifies ==Best-of-N== selection by using a pessimistic reward estimate: the original reward minus a penalty proportional to the uncertainty of that reward estimate.
- Uncertainty is quantified by the prediction error of a lightweight predictor network, trained offline to reconstruct hidden features from a specific, frozen layer of the reward model using in-distribution data.
- At inference, the response with the highest pessimistic reward is selected from the N generated candidates, effectively penalizing responses for which the reward model's internal features are unfamiliar or OOD.

## Results

- Caution successfully mitigated reward hacking on the GSM8K mathematical reasoning dataset, demonstrating monotonically improving performance with increasing N and achieving a **15.5%** improvement over standard Best-of-N's final accuracy at N=512.
- The method proved effective even on out-of-distribution (MATH-500) and out-of-domain (BigBench-Hard) prompts, where a "pessimism-only" variant (prioritizing distributional familiarity) sometimes outperformed the combined reward model and pessimism.
- Ablation studies confirmed the approach's robustness to hyperparameter choices for the pessimism weight and highlighted that using semantically grounded features from the reward model significantly outperforms traditional random network distillation for OOD detection.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The principle of curiosity-driven exploration, typically used for intrinsic motivation in online reinforcement learning, can be effectively repurposed for offline pessimistic learning and out-of-distribution detection in language models.
> - Semantic grounding of the uncertainty signal is crucial; distilling features from the reward model itself, rather than random network features, enables the uncertainty to capture task-relevant out-of-distribution patterns.
> - A practical balance can be achieved between mitigating reward hacking and fully leveraging inference-time compute, avoiding the over-conservatism of broad distributional regularization methods.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The research addresses a significant challenge in the application of large language models (LLMs), particularly concerning inference-time compute scaling. Recent advancements have shown that increasing compute during inference, rather than solely model size, can substantially enhance LLM performance across various reasoning tasks. A prominent method for leveraging this additional compute is Best-of-N (BoN) sampling, where multiple candidate responses are generated by an LLM, scored by a reward model ($\hat{r}$), and the highest-scoring response is selected.

While BoN sampling often yields performance improvements, its effectiveness is fundamentally limited by the fidelity of the reward model. A well-documented issue with BoN is "reward hacking" (also known as over-optimization or Goodhart's Law), where performance initially improves with increasing N (number of samples) but then degrades for larger N. This degradation occurs because the selection mechanism begins to favor responses that exploit imperfections or spurious correlations in $\hat{r}$, rather than reflecting genuine improvements in quality or alignment with the true reward ($r^\star$). These "hacked" responses are often out-of-distribution (OOD) relative to the data on which the reward model was trained, leading to unreliable reward estimations.

Prior attempts to mitigate reward hacking have generally fallen into two categories:
1. **Improving reward models:** This involves developing stronger, more robust reward models that are less susceptible to hacking. However, this approach faces inherent difficulties, as exhaustively characterizing all potential reward hacking strategies is intractable.
2. **Regularizing the selection process:** Methods like strong information-theoretic distributional regularization (e.g., using $\chi^2$-divergence or Wasserstein distance) aim to keep the distribution of selected responses close to the in-distribution data. While these approaches can provide theoretical guarantees and ensure monotonicity with N, they often prove overly conservative in practice, potentially preventing the selection of genuinely high-quality responses that happen to be slightly OOD.

This work positions itself within this landscape by seeking a solution that effectively mitigates reward hacking without being excessively conservative, thereby enabling LLMs to fully exploit the benefits of inference-time scaling. It draws inspiration from reinforcement learning's principle of pessimism and a reinterpretation of curiosity-driven exploration for OOD detection in an offline setting.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an inference-time BoN sampling scheme that effectively mitigates reward hacking while concurrently allowing LLMs to leverage the full benefits of additional inference-time compute. The authors aim to achieve this by introducing "caution," a novel approach based on pessimistic reward estimation.

The motivation for this objective stems from several limitations observed in existing reward hacking mitigation strategies:
- **Inherent fragility of reward models:** Reward models, despite their utility, are often trained on limited datasets of human preferences and can learn spurious correlations (e.g., favoring verbosity or specific formatting over correctness). As BoN explores a wider space of responses with increasing N, it becomes more likely to encounter responses that exploit these correlations, leading to degradation in true quality.
- **Asymmetric difficulty of improving reward models:** While it is feasible to curate examples of high-quality responses, comprehensively capturing all possible ways an imperfect reward model can be exploited for reward hacking is challenging, making continuous improvement of reward models a difficult arms race.
- **Conservatism of distributional regularization:** Existing regularization techniques that constrain the output distribution to remain close to the reward model's training distribution are often too broad. They penalize all OOD responses equally, regardless of whether a response is genuinely poor or simply novel and high-quality but outside the learned distribution. This conservatism can stifle the benefits of generating more diverse candidates, which is the core idea of inference-time scaling.

The authors motivate their "caution" approach by identifying a dual relationship to "curiosity" from deep reinforcement learning. Curiosity mechanisms, such as Random Network Distillation (RND), incentivize exploration by rewarding prediction error, signaling novelty. In contrast, for the offline setting of BoN, OOD responses are undesirable because their true rewards are uncertain. Therefore, caution is conceptualized as penalizing prediction error, signaling distributional uncertainty, to avoid OOD responses. The goal is to develop a computationally efficient and practical method that can discern responses likely to exploit $\hat{r}$ from those that genuinely improve quality, even if they are somewhat atypical, thereby striking a balance between robustness and leveraging compute scaling.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology introduces "caution," an inference-time instantiation of the pessimism principle, designed to penalize out-of-distribution (OOD) responses.

**Problem Formulation:**
The setup involves a language model $\pi( \cdot | x)$ that generates responses $y$ for a given prompt $x$. A learned reward model $\hat{r}(x, y)$ scores these responses, while the true, unknown reward is $r^\star(x, y)$. The standard Best-of-N (BoN) strategy selects $y_{\hat{i}} = \operatorname{argmax}_{i \in [N]} \hat{r}(x, y_i)$ from $N$ candidates. The core idea of caution is to modify this selection by introducing a pessimistic reward estimate: $\hat{r}_{LCB}(x, y) = \hat{r}(x, y) - \lambda \alpha(x, y)$, where $\alpha(x, y)$ quantifies uncertainty about $\hat{r}$'s estimate for $(x, y)$, and $\lambda > 0$ controls the pessimism strength. The final selection becomes $y_{\hat{i}} = \operatorname{argmax}_{i \in [N]} \hat{r}_{LCB}(x, y_i)$.

**Implementing "Caution" (Curiosity to Caution):**
Caution leverages a mechanism analogous to Random Network Distillation (RND) for OOD detection, but repurposed for a pessimistic objective in an offline setting. It involves two neural networks operating on the internal representations of the reward model $R(x, y)$:
1. **Target Network $T(x, y)$:** This is a fixed network defined as the hidden state extracted from a specific layer $L$ of the *frozen reward model*, i.e., $h_R^L(x, y)$. This choice ensures that the target features are semantically grounded in the reward model's learned representations, capturing task-relevant patterns.
2. **Predictor Network $P_\theta(x, y)$:** This is a separate, trainable network with parameters $\theta$. Its role is to learn to predict the output of the target network $T(x, y)$. The predictor network is designed to be lightweight for computational efficiency.

**Training Process:**
The predictor network $P_\theta$ is trained fully offline on a dataset $D_{\text{train}}$ of prompt-response pairs. This dataset is constructed by sampling responses $y \sim \pi(\cdot|x)$ from the base language model on the benchmark's training split. The training objective is a mean squared error (MSE) loss:
$L(\theta) = E_{(x,y) \sim D_{\text{train}}} [\|P_\theta(x, y) - T(x, y)\|^2]$
By training on this in-distribution data, the predictor learns to accurately reconstruct the reward model's features for "typical" responses. Conversely, for "atypical" (OOD) responses, the predictor is expected to exhibit high prediction error, as it has not been trained on such patterns. The data requirements for this training are relatively low, as it only needs prompts and responses from the base model, which are cheaper to obtain than human-annotated preference data.

**Pessimistic Reward Estimation at Inference Time:**
After training, the predictor $P_\theta$ is used to define the uncertainty estimate $\alpha(x, y)$ as its prediction error:
$\alpha(x, y) = \|P_\theta(x, y) - T(x, y)\|^2$
This error quantifies how "novel" or "out-of-distribution" a given response $(x, y)$ is from the perspective of the predictor's training data. The pessimistic reward $\hat{r}_{LCB}(x, y)$ is then calculated as:
$\hat{r}_{LCB}(x, y) = \hat{r}(x, y) - \lambda \cdot \|P_\theta(x, y) - T(x, y)\|^2$
At inference, among the $N$ generated candidates, the response with the highest $\hat{r}_{LCB}$ is selected. This process is computationally efficient, requiring only two additional forward passes (through $P_\theta$ and $T$) which can be parallelized, alongside the original $\hat{r}$ evaluation. The parameter $\lambda$ controls the balance between the original reward model score and the pessimism penalty.

**Theoretical Interpretation:**
The paper provides a theoretical analysis in a simplified linear setting (Section C in the appendix) to demonstrate the provable benefits of caution. Theorem 1 (informal version) states that under suitable conditions, the pessimistic selection strategy ($y_{i_{\text{pess}}}$) provably improves upon standard BoN ($y_{\hat{i}}$) in terms of expected true reward, and the suboptimality gap of caution relative to the true optimum ($y_{i^\star}$) tends to zero as $N$ grows, while the suboptimality gap of standard BoN does not. This analysis serves as a proof-of-concept for the efficacy of curiosity-style uncertainty signals in OOD detection for pessimistic policy learning.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical evaluation of "caution" was conducted on mathematical reasoning tasks using the Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct language model and the OASST reward model.

#### 4.1. Caution Mitigates Reward Hacking

- **In-domain performance (GSM8K):** Standard BoN sampling consistently exhibited reward hacking on the GSM8K dataset, characterized by performance degrading significantly at larger N (e.g., N=512), as shown in Figure 1. In contrast, "caution" successfully mitigated this phenomenon, demonstrating monotonically improving performance as N increased. This resulted in a substantial performance boost: 4.2% over the peak accuracy of the reward model alone and 15.5% over its final accuracy at N=512.
- **Out-of-distribution (OOD) and Out-of-domain performance (MATH-500, BigBench-Hard):** Caution demonstrated efficacy even when evaluated on OOD prompts (MATH-500, same domain but harder) and out-of-domain prompts (BigBench-Hard, different domain).
 - On MATH-500, standard BoN experienced a 3.0-point degradation from peak to final accuracy. Caution reduced this degradation, but interestingly, the "pessimism-only" variant (selecting based solely on $\alpha(x, y)$) outperformed the combined "RM + Pessimism" approach, achieving higher peak accuracy (13.6% vs. 12.3%) and lower degradation (1.7 vs. 2.3 points). This suggests that for significantly harder OOD problems, the reward model's estimates can become less reliable, making the pure OOD detection signal more valuable.
 - On BigBench-Hard, the reward model performed catastrophically, showing a 15.6-point degradation. Here, "pessimism-only" was the most performant method, significantly outperforming both standard BoN and "RM + Pessimism". This finding indicates that when a reward model is severely misaligned or unreliable on an OOD task, prioritizing distributional familiarity (via pessimism) over a potentially misleading reward signal yields better outcomes.

#### 4.2. Ablation Studies

- **Architectural Ablations (Table 3):**
 - Greater flexibility in the predictor network (e.g., simplified architecture with trainable embeddings) led to better reconstruction loss, but did not necessarily translate to better final performance.
 - Predictors that learned representations "from scratch" (e.g., lightweight with separate embeddings) tended to perform better than those inheriting or sharing pre-trained embeddings, suggesting that independent learning creates more sensitive distributional boundaries for OOD detection.
 - A linear projection layer between the predictor and target networks provided consistent, albeit modest, benefits, indicating that an information bottleneck can prevent pure memorization and improve signal quality.
 - Overall, the results highlight a trade-off: effective OOD detection requires a balance between reconstruction accuracy on in-distribution data and sensitivity to novelty for OOD samples. Over-fitting the predictor to the target's features can harm its ability to detect OOD responses.
- **Strength of Regularization (Table 2, Figure 6):** The approach demonstrated robustness to the choice of $\lambda$ (mixing weight). Moderate to high values (0.6-0.8) generally achieved optimal performance, suggesting that caution does not require precise hyperparameter tuning to be effective. For easier tasks, a slightly higher $\lambda$ helps filter out format-noncompliant responses even if they are factually correct.
- **Comparison to Traditional RND (Table 2, Figure 7):** When "caution" (distilling reward model features) was compared against traditional RND (distilling random network features), the latter exhibited significantly inferior performance across all $\lambda$ values. This provides evidence that effective distributional regularization in this context requires semantic grounding; using features from the reward model allows the uncertainty signal to capture task-relevant OOD patterns, whereas randomly initialized features do not.

#### 4.3. Case Study (Figure 4)

The case study illustrated how caution operates by contrasting selection behaviors.
- **Reward Hacking Example:** An incorrect response, verbose and with multiple reasoning steps but failing to provide the correct answer or follow required formatting, received a high reward model score (99.8th percentile). Caution, however, appropriately penalized this response as OOD, likely due to its formatting deviation.
- **Correct, Compliant Example:** A concise, accurate response adhering to formatting requirements received a lower reward model score but was recognized as "familiar" by pessimism, matching patterns of high-quality responses in the training data.
This demonstrates that reward models can conflate verbosity with quality, and caution effectively distinguishes between superficially sophisticated (but incorrect) responses and genuinely correct, compliant ones, by detecting distributional deviations.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work makes several contributions to the field of language model research and reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF):

- **Practical Solution to Reward Hacking:** The introduction of "caution" provides a simple, computationally efficient, and effective inference-time method for mitigating reward hacking in Best-of-N sampling. Unlike prior methods that are either too conservative or computationally expensive, caution enables LLMs to leverage increased inference-time compute while maintaining robustness against reward model imperfections. This is particularly relevant as inference-time scaling becomes a more prominent paradigm for improving LLM performance.
- **Reinterpretation and Efficacy of Curiosity-style Methods for OOD Detection:** The research successfully demonstrates that principles derived from curiosity-driven exploration (like RND) can be effectively repurposed for *pessimistic* learning and OOD detection in an offline setting, specifically for language models. The key finding that semantic grounding (distilling reward model features) is crucial for this efficacy resolves some prior debates about RND's utility for OOD detection in different contexts. This opens new avenues for applying such techniques beyond their traditional role in online RL exploration.
- **Enhanced LLM Alignment and Robustness:** By preventing the selection of "hacked" responses, caution contributes to better alignment of LLM outputs with true user intent or ground truth rewards. The improved performance and monotonicity with increasing N, especially in OOD scenarios where reward models fail, indicate a more robust and reliable LLM system. This robustness is critical for deploying LLMs in sensitive applications where unexpected and low-quality outputs due to reward hacking could have negative consequences.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Improved LLM Deployment:** Caution could be widely adopted in LLM serving pipelines to enhance the quality and reliability of generated responses, particularly when operating with imperfect reward models or in domains where OOD inputs are common.
- **Foundational for Future Research:** The demonstration that curiosity-style signals, when semantically grounded, are effective OOD detectors in LLM settings can inspire new research directions. This includes exploring their use as intrinsic reward signals for exploration in post-training (RLHF) settings, potentially overcoming limitations of previous attempts that did not use pre-trained features.
- **AI Safety and Alignment:** The ability to robustly detect and penalize OOD or "hacked" responses has implications for AI safety. Caution could contribute to developing more aligned LLMs by preventing models from exploiting system flaws, offering a promising approach to addressing issues like adversarial prompting and distributional shift, thereby ensuring more trustworthy AI systems.
