# Decompose and Reorganize: Planning with Primitives and Visuomotor Policies Learned from Demonstrations

> [!summary] Summary
> The DR-LfD framework combines symbolic task and motion planning with visuomotor policies learned from demonstrations to perform dexterous, long-horizon manipulation tasks. This system enables robots to achieve 100% success in position-shifted out-of-distribution scenarios and manage physical constraints while requiring fewer demonstrations than end-to-end imitation learning approaches.

## Problem

- Automating dexterous, long-horizon robotic manipulation tasks requires both abstract reasoning and precise, contact-rich execution.
- Traditional Task and Motion Planning (TAMP) methods rely on manually engineered models, precise perception, and are brittle in unstructured, contact-rich environments.
- End-to-end Imitation Learning (IL) policies struggle with out-of-distribution observations, suffer from compounding errors, and require exponentially large datasets for long-horizon generalization.

## Method

- The **DR-LfD** framework systematically decomposes human demonstrations into reusable ==atomic skills==, which are then reorganized by a ==TAMP solver== for long-horizon tasks.
- It learns ==object-centric primitives== for simple actions using ==SO(3)-equivariant diffusion models== and ==visuomotor policies== for contact-rich skills, converting them into ==PDDLStream-compatible functions==.
- An online interleaved ==perceive-plan-act-verify loop== facilitates real-time adaptation, error recovery, and enforces physical constraints like ==reachability and collision avoidance== through ==test streams==.

## Results

- DR-LfD achieved **100%** success in simulated peg-in-hole tasks with **50** demonstrations across in-distribution and position-shifted out-of-distribution object placements, outperforming baseline imitation learning methods.
- The framework demonstrated the ability to handle physical constraints, achieving **55%** success by planning regrasps for unreachable objects and **70%** success by moving obstacles in unsafe scenarios.
- DR-LfD successfully composed separately learned skills into long-horizon plans for complex novel tasks (e.g., **21-step** and **19-step** tasks) and showed superior data efficiency on bimanual tasks, outperforming baselines with **10x** fewer demonstrations.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating symbolic task and motion planning with learned visuomotor policies effectively combines high-level reasoning with fine-grained execution, mitigating the weaknesses of each approach.
> - Representing skills as object-centric primitives learned via SO(3)-equivariant models enables robust spatial generalization from limited demonstration data.
> - Explicitly modeling skill preconditions, effects, and physical constraints within a TAMP framework allows for online adaptation, error recovery, and the composition of learned skills into novel long-horizon plans.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

The field of robotic manipulation faces challenges in automating dexterous, long-horizon tasks that require both abstract reasoning and precise, contact-rich execution. Existing approaches generally fall into two categories: Task and Motion Planning (TAMP) and Imitation Learning (IL).

Traditional TAMP frameworks excel at symbolic planning and sequencing discrete skills for long-horizon tasks. However, their practical application is often constrained by the necessity for manually engineered models, precise perception, and robustness in contact-rich or unstructured environments. Methods like IDTMP and TAMPER, while incorporating feedback, still rely on accurate object pose estimation, which can be a point of failure.

In parallel, Learning-from-Demonstration (LfD) and advanced Imitation Learning methods offer an alternative by learning visuomotor policies directly from data, bypassing explicit modeling. These methods have shown efficacy in complex, multi-modal behaviors with visual feedback. However, end-to-end policies derived from LfD are susceptible to out-of-distribution (OOD) observations, leading to failures even with slight object displacements or unseen grasp poses. Errors tend to compound over long skill sequences. Efforts to mitigate this, such as large-scale pre-training of behavior models, often incur significant data and computational costs, posing a barrier to rapid deployment. A fundamental challenge for both paradigms is the combinatorial complexity of long-horizon tasks, which demands exponentially large datasets for end-to-end policies to generalize effectively across possible skill sequences and transitions.

The proposed DR-LfD framework aims to bridge the gap between these two research streams by integrating their complementary strengths. Symbolic planning offers long-horizon reasoning and explicit constraint satisfaction but can be brittle in execution. Visuomotor policies provide closed-loop adaptation to real-time feedback but lack inherent reasoning and often overlook physical constraints like collision avoidance. DR-LfD positions itself as a data-efficient and reliable solution for long-horizon manipulation by decomposing tasks into learnable atomic skills and reorganizing them using a TAMP solver. This approach aims to address the data inefficiency of end-to-end IL and the brittleness of traditional TAMP in contact-rich scenarios, particularly in bimanual manipulation settings where coordination is complex. The work also incorporates techniques for spatial generalization, leveraging equivariance models to enhance robustness to varied object placements without extensive data augmentation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a systematic framework that enables robots to perform dexterous, long-horizon manipulation tasks by effectively combining high-level reasoning with fine-grained execution. This objective is driven by a recognition of the inherent limitations of existing methodologies when applied to complex real-world scenarios.

The key motivations for this work are detailed as follows:

- **Reconciling Complementary Strengths:** Traditional Task and Motion Planning (TAMP) systems excel at symbolic reasoning, planning discrete skill sequences, and satisfying explicit constraints over long horizons. However, they frequently struggle with the complexities of contact-rich operations, requiring precisely engineered models and perfect perception, which makes them brittle in unstructured environments. Conversely, Imitation Learning (IL) methods are adept at replicating complex, contact-rich manipulation behaviors with visual feedback and operate in a closed-loop manner, adapting to real-time observations. Nevertheless, IL policies often exhibit limited spatial generalization, struggle with out-of-distribution observations, and face compounding errors over multi-stage operations, requiring vast amounts of data for effective generalization across diverse scenarios. The motivation is to integrate these two paradigms to leverage their respective advantages while mitigating their individual weaknesses.
- **Addressing Combinatorial Complexity and Data Efficiency:** Long-horizon tasks often involve a combinatorial number of possible skill sequences and transitions. An end-to-end IL policy would need to learn not only individual skills but also the complex decision-making for sequencing, timing transitions, and managing motion plans or regrasping operations. This demands exponentially large datasets, which are costly and time-consuming to collect, especially for complex bimanual tasks. A core motivation is to transform this learning problem from one requiring exponential data over skill sequences to one whose data burden scales linearly with the number of distinct skill types.
- **Improving Robustness and Generalization:** End-to-end visuomotor policies are vulnerable to slight displacements of objects or unseen grasp poses. TAMP, while robust in symbolic reasoning, relies on simplified abstractions of continuous actions, leading to open-loop execution that can be unsuited for dynamic, contact-rich interactions. The motivation is to create a system that is robust to varying initial setups, novel object placements, and physical constraints without requiring re-demonstration of entire tasks.
- **Systematizing Skill Acquisition and Representation:** There is a need for a systematic approach to decompose human demonstrations into atomic, reusable skills and represent them in a TAMP-compatible format. This includes carefully modeling the initiation, termination, and constraints of learned visuomotor policies to facilitate their integration into a symbolic planning framework.

To address these motivations, the DR-LfD framework proposes to:
1. **Devise a novel TAMP-gated system** that abstracts atomic skills from demonstrations and reorganizes them to achieve combinatorial goals.
2. **Leverage equivariant diffusion models** to train object-centric primitives, aiming for robust spatial generalization from minimal data.
3. **Systematically model the initiation, termination, and constraints** of learned visuomotor policies in a form compatible with a sampling-based TAMP framework.
4. **Demonstrate strong performance** through comprehensive real-world and simulated experiments on tasks involving multiple steps, unseen setups, and physical constraints.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

DR-LfD operates through an offline skill acquisition phase and an online plan composition phase, designed to decompose complex tasks into manageable sub-problems and integrate learning with planning.

#### 3.1. Skill Acquisition

The offline phase focuses on transforming raw human demonstrations into a repertoire of executable skills compatible with TAMP.

- **Skill Detection, Abstraction, and Partition:**
 - **VLM-assisted Grounding:** A Vision-Language Model (VLM), specifically Qwen-VL-Max, is employed to understand task semantics from video demonstrations and task descriptions. The VLM is prompted with a limited set of edge operations (representing gripper-object or object-object interactions) and example skills for in-context learning. It outputs an action-label sequence and associated contact edge operations, grounding static variables and labeling object properties (e.g., Surface, Container, Movable). This output is manually verified for correctness.
 - **Graph Representation for Skills:** Each VLM-detected skill (e.g., "robot\_picks\_box") is represented by a text description (λ\_a) and two contact signatures: a precondition graph (G\_pre) for the starting state and an effect graph (G\_eff) for the end state. These graphs abstract contact relationships using nodes (grippers, objects, world frame) and edges (fixed transforms, kinematic links). An algorithm iteratively updates the graph based on observed edge operations, ensuring graph interpretations for single-arm skills are unaffected by other arm movements.
 - **Contact-aware Timespan Partition:** Demonstration episodes are segmented into distinct skill phases by detecting contact change events (object-object or gripper-object distance thresholds). Object point clouds are obtained via GroundedSAM and XMem for real-world tasks, and gripper point clouds via forward kinematics. An "in-hand" status is also tracked to identify object attachment/detachment moments.

- **Learning Object-centric Primitives:** For skills with lower behavioral complexity (e.g., pick, place, drop), an open-loop object-centric primitive predictor (Φ\_τ) is trained.
 - **Network Structure:** Φ\_τ consists of an observation encoding network and a noise prediction network. Both leverage Vector Neurons for SO(3)-equivariance, allowing generalization to novel object placements.
 - **Observation Encoding:** Language inputs are embedded by BERT. Point clouds of involved objects at the primitive's start frame are processed by a VN-DGCNN encoder to extract rotation-invariant and rotation-equivariant features, which are then fused. An object-level padding technique accommodates skills with varying graph representations.
 - **Trajectory Distribution Learning:** Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPMs) are used to learn the distribution of end-effector pose trajectories, gripper actions, and contact states, conditioned on point cloud and language descriptions. A VNN-version U-Net approximates the score function for noise prediction. Canonicalization of per-object coordinates ensures translation-equivariance.

- **Learning Visuomotor Policies and Keyposes:** For contact-rich skills (e.g., wiping, handoff), a visuomotor policy (π) and a keypose predictor (Φ\_kp) are employed.
 - **Visuomotor Policy (π):** This policy maps system observations (robot proprioception, wrist/3rd-view camera images) to low-level control signals. The design allows for integration of arbitrary IL algorithms.
 - **Keypose Predictor (Φ\_kp):** To ensure policies are initiated and terminated in in-distribution observations, Φ\_kp learns the distribution of robot proprioception (keyposes) at the initial (G\_pre) and terminal (G\_eff) contact states, conditioned on the skill description (λ\_a). This is modeled using a light-weight MLP diffusion model.

- **Learned Skills as Streams:** Learned primitives and keyposes are encoded into PDDLStream-compatible stream functions (Table III).
 - **Primitive-relevant Streams:** `LearnedAttach(·)` and `LearnedDetach(·)` use Φ\_τ to compute full trajectories and extract contact poses (g) or placed poses (p). These computed poses are refined by online perception during execution.
 - **Policy-relevant Streams:** `LearnedUniKeyPose(·)` and `LearnedBiKeyPose(·)` use Φ\_kp to output initial and terminal keyposes (q, q') and a policy identifier (κ), which encapsulates execution logic and safety checks for the visuomotor policy.
 - **Policy-Trajectory Switching Stream:** `LearnedPostGrasp(·)` bridges policy-parameterized skills that end with an in-hand object to subsequent trajectory-parameterized skills by predicting the object's contact pose after placement using Φ\_τ.

#### 3.2. Plan Composition

The online phase leverages the acquired skill repertoire to compose and execute plans for novel, long-horizon tasks.

- **Schemata for Learned Skills:**
 - Traditional planned skills (e.g., Transit, Transfer) have hand-crafted PDDLStream schemata.
 - For VLM-detected skills, an automated workflow (Algorithm 2) constructs skill schemata (arguments, preconditions, effects). This involves translating G\_pre and G\_eff into symbolic predicates using `EdgeOps2Predicates(·)` and `Derived(·)` functions (e.g., `AtGrasp`, `On`, `HandEmpty`, `Surface`).
 - Stream functions are associated with skills based on their contact patterns using `MatchStreams(·)`, and their input/output predicates are integrated into the skill's precondition using `Bound(·)`, forcing the planner to call the stream at runtime.

- **Implementation of Constraint Stream Functions:** Physical constraints are enforced via test streams.
 - **Reachability Constraint:** `IsReachable(h, p₀)` checks if arm `h` can reach a pose `p₀`. This uses a precomputed 3D reachability map that stores coverage fractions of reachable orientations for each voxel. If a pose is unreachable, the TAMP solver receives feedback for alternative plans.
 - **Policy Safety Constraint:** `IsSafePolicy(a, κ)` verifies the safety of a visuomotor policy rollout. It employs a swept-volume strategy to construct a Minimum Distance Field (MDF) map from demonstration replays. The derived predicate `IsSafePolicy` evaluates if any movable object (`o`) at pose (`p`) is within a risky region using `CFreeMDF(o, p, a, κ)`. If falsified, the obstructing object's geometric information is fed back to the symbolic layer, allowing the planner to relocate it.
 - **Real-world Constraint Handling:** The system can replan extra movements, such as a regrasp to bring an object into a reachable area or a pick-and-place operation to remove an obstacle, demonstrating adaptation to unreachable or unsafe initial conditions.

- **Online Execution, Perception and Adaptation:** DR-LfD employs an interleaved perceive-plan-act-verify loop (Algorithm 3).
 - `Perceive(·)` continuously updates the current scene state.
 - `TeleportTAMP(·)` computes a coarse, skill-level plan skeleton (⃗a\_glb) based on the initial state and goal.
 - During execution, the system iterates through the skeleton. For each high-level action (a\_i), `DetailedTAMP(·)` solves a local TAMP problem, regenerating necessary stream outputs (trajectories, policies).
 - `ExecuteAndVerify(·)` executes the commands. A contact detector monitors the completion of learned primitives and policies.
 - If an action's verified effect is achieved, the system proceeds. Otherwise, it re-perceives the scene and re-invokes `DetailedTAMP(·)` for the same action, enabling error recovery from unmodeled dynamics, perception noise, or object displacement.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of DR-LfD was conducted through comprehensive real-world and simulated experiments, addressing key questions regarding spatial generalization, constraint handling, long-horizon task completion, and comparative performance against baselines.

#### 4.1. Spatial Generalization (Q1)

DR-LfD's performance in previously unseen tabletop setups was evaluated in both simulated and real-world environments.

- **Simulation (Peg-in-Hole task):** In a peg-in-hole insertion task, DR-LfD was compared against ACT and Diffusion Policy (DP). Using only 50 demonstration episodes, DR-LfD consistently outperformed both baselines across three object placement distributions:
 - **ID (In-Distribution):** DR-LfD achieved 100% success (ACT: 44%, DP: 54%).
 - **XY-OOD (Position-shifted OOD):** DR-LfD maintained 100% success (ACT: 38%, DP: 42%).
 - **XYH-OOD (Position and Orientation-shifted OOD):** DR-LfD achieved 88% success, significantly higher than DP's 12% and ACT's 2%.
 - Although DR-LfD exhibited a slightly longer average completion time, this was primarily attributed to baselines frequently failing prematurely. These results indicate DR-LfD's capability in executing bimanual, multi-stage tasks in unseen tabletop configurations.

- **Real-world (Object Handoff, Screwdriver Packing, Cup-sleeve Insertion):** On an ALOHA hardware platform with 20 demonstrations per task, DR-LfD was compared against DP and π\_0.5 (a pre-trained VLA model).
 - DR-LfD consistently outperformed DP in all "ID," "OOD," and "Leaky" (rotated, leaned against distractor) setups.
 - DR-LfD's performance was competitive with the stronger π\_0.5 baseline in "ID" and "Leaky" placements and superior in "OOD" placements across tasks. This suggests that the object-centric primitives contribute significantly to spatial generalization.
 - The "Cup-sleeve Insertion" task under "Leaky" conditions yielded lower success rates across all methods (DR-LfD: 40%), attributed to the task's fine-grained requirements, distribution shift, and noisy single-view depth sensing affecting learned primitives.

#### 4.2. Constraint Handling (Q2)

The ability of DR-LfD to manage reachability and collision-free constraints was demonstrated in simulated peg-in-hole scenarios.

- **Unreachable Scenario:** The initial object placement rendered one of the robot arms unable to reach the target object. DR-LfD achieved a 55% success rate by planning an additional regrasp movement to transport the socket to a reachable region for the designated arm. Baseline methods (ACT, DP) failed entirely.
- **Unsafe Scenario:** A pole was introduced as an unexpected obstruction. DR-LfD achieved a 70% success rate by planning an extra pick-and-place movement to reposition the pole, ensuring a collision-free bimanual insertion. Baselines failed entirely.
- Both scenarios showed increased computation time and slightly reduced success rates compared to the "ID" setup, indicating the overhead and challenges associated with more complex planning and noisy point-cloud observations.

#### 4.3. Combining Learned Skills in Novel Tasks (Q3)

DR-LfD's capacity to compose skills for long-horizon, human-specified goals was evaluated on two complex tasks:

- **Three-Tapes Task:** Using only a learned "Object Handoff" skill, DR-LfD completed a 21-step plan skeleton to place three tapes into a basket, demonstrating autonomous skill selection and repeated skill execution.
- **Cup-Sponge-Screwdriver Task:** This task combined three distinct bimanual skills (screwdriver packing, cup handoff, cup wiping) into a 19-step plan. DR-LfD successfully executed the task, achieving the goal of applying all skills and ensuring all objects were on the table.
- These results indicate that DR-LfD can sequence separately learned skills into long-horizon plans, covering tasks with many steps. The framework also supports isolated improvement of sub-skills; for instance, training the "screwdriver packing" skill with a cluttered background yielded better performance (50% success, 15.30 steps completed) than a variant trained with a clean background (35% success, 10.35 steps), highlighting the ability to diagnose and address specific skill weaknesses.

#### 4.4. Comparative and Ablation Studies (Q4)

Further evaluations on public datasets provided insights into DR-LfD's design choices.

- **LIBERO Dataset (Pick-and-Place):**
 - DR-LfD (closed-loop) matched or outperformed DP (trained with 10 or 50 demos per task) and NOD-TAMP across "Spatial," "Object," and "Long" task sets, demonstrating the effectiveness of its online perceive-plan-act strategy.
 - DR-LfD (open-loop) showed lower success rates, indicating the importance of online adaptation for unmodeled dynamics.
 - Ablations replacing learned primitives with generic grasp planners (GPD, M2T2) performed poorly, especially on tasks with complex object geometries, highlighting the necessity of learned, policy-compatible contact poses.

- **DexMimicGen Dataset (Bimanual Tasks):**
 - DR-LfD (trained with 100 demonstrations) significantly outperformed DP and SDP (both trained with 1000 demonstrations) on "Two-Arm Threading" (70% vs. 45% and 50%) and "Two-Arm Assembly" (90% vs. 70% and 60%). This demonstrates DR-LfD's data efficiency.
 - An ablated variant, DR-LfD (Planned Contact), which used a scripted top-grasp skill instead of learned primitives, performed poorly (0% on Threading, 35% on Assembly). This confirmed that a mismatch between scripted contact poses and the policy's demonstration distribution can lead to out-of-distribution observations for the visuomotor policy.

In summary, DR-LfD demonstrated robust performance across diverse scenarios, effectively addressing spatial generalization, physical constraints, and long-horizon task composition. Its modular design, combining TAMP with learned, policy-compatible primitives, proved more data-efficient and reliable than monolithic imitation policies or traditional TAMP variants.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The DR-LfD framework contributes to the field of robotic manipulation by systematically integrating the strengths of symbolic task and motion planning with visuomotor policies learned from demonstrations. This approach addresses several critical limitations of existing methodologies, offering advancements with significant potential impact.

The primary significance lies in its **data efficiency and robustness** for long-horizon and dexterous manipulation tasks. By decomposing demonstrations into atomic skills and enabling a TAMP solver to reorganize them, DR-LfD reduces the data requirement from an exponential scale (for end-to-end learning of entire sequences) to a linear scale (based on the number of distinct skill types). This makes it feasible to train and deploy robots for complex tasks with limited demonstration data, as evidenced by its superior performance with 100 demonstrations compared to baselines requiring 1000 on the DexMimicGen dataset.

DR-LfD enhances **spatial generalization** through its use of object-centric primitives and SO(3)-equivariant learning. This allows the system to adapt to previously unseen object placements and initial conditions, which is a common failure point for pure imitation learning policies. The framework's ability to handle **physical constraints** like reachability and collision avoidance, facilitated by specialized test streams and an online replanning mechanism, increases the reliability and safety of robot operations in cluttered or dynamic environments. The successful replanning examples (e.g., relocating an unreachable object or an obstacle) demonstrate a practical solution to common real-world challenges.

The **composability** of learned skills is another significant aspect. DR-LfD can combine separately learned skills to achieve novel, long-horizon goals specified by a human, showcasing flexibility beyond simply replicating demonstrated sequences. This modularity also allows for isolated improvement of individual sub-skills without re-training the entire task, streamlining the development and debugging process.

The research also offers **implications for foundation-model-based robot systems**. It suggests that the semantic understanding provided by Vision-Language Models (VLMs) is most impactful when translated into verifiable, structured action representations rather than being used for free-form plan generation. Furthermore, it advocates for decoupled skill representations, where different types of skills (e.g., SO(3)-equivariant primitives, visuomotor policies, planned motions) are realized by the most suitable underlying representation. This approach complements the capabilities of large behavior foundation models by providing robustness to OOD conditions and exposing continuous feasibility to the planner.

Despite these advancements, the paper acknowledges limitations, including the manual effort required for goal specification, the inherent exponential complexity of TAMP search, and a reliance on depth sensing which can be susceptible to noise and incompleteness. Addressing these areas represents important avenues for future research, such as integrating language models for goal translation, leveraging more efficient TAMP solvers, and developing more robust depth estimation or equivariant learning without depth.

In conclusion, DR-LfD presents a systematic and effective framework that advances the state of the art in robotic manipulation by judiciously combining symbolic planning with learning from demonstrations. Its contributions to data efficiency, generalization, constraint handling, and composability pave the way for more capable and reliable robotic systems in complex, real-world applications.
