# RLIF: Interactive Imitation Learning as Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> RLIF (Reinforcement Learning from Interventions) recasts interactive imitation learning as a reinforcement learning problem by utilizing human interventions as implicit negative reward signals. This approach enables learning from suboptimal human experts to produce policies that can surpass expert performance, achieving 100% success in robotic peg insertion and outperforming DAgger in various simulations.

## Problem

- Reinforcement Learning often requires hand-crafting precise reward functions, which is challenging and time-consuming for complex real-world tasks like robotics.
- Traditional interactive imitation learning methods like DAgger assume that human expert interventions are near-optimal, which is frequently not true in practical scenarios with suboptimal human operators.
- Behavioral cloning struggles with distribution shift, leading to compounding errors when the learned policy deviates from the expert's state distribution.

## Method

- **RLIF** frames interactive imitation learning within an RL paradigm, assigning a negative reward (-1) to state-action transitions immediately preceding a human intervention and a zero reward (0) otherwise.
- An off-policy RL algorithm (e.g., RLPD/SAC) is trained iteratively on this collected data to maximize cumulative rewards, effectively learning to avoid situations that trigger human interventions.
- Simulations use both random and more realistic value-based intervention models, where intervention probability is based on a reference value function detecting discrepancies between agent and expert actions.

## Results

- RLIF consistently outperformed DAgger and HG-DAgger baselines in high-dimensional continuous control tasks (Adroit, Gym Locomotion), showing up to a **5x** improvement with suboptimal experts and the capacity to surpass initial expert performance.
- Achieved a **100%** success rate for a 7-DoF Franka robot performing vision-based peg insertion within six interaction rounds (approximately 20 minutes) and a **95%** success rate for cloth unfolding in seven rounds with real human feedback.
- Theoretically, RLIF demonstrated a tighter asymptotic suboptimality gap bound compared to DAgger, particularly when the expert's judgment of optimal behavior is more accurate than their ability to execute it perfectly.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The act of human intervention itself, even if the subsequent correction is suboptimal, provides a valuable implicit negative reward signal about undesirable behavior.
> - It is often easier for a human expert to detect and react to a mistake than to consistently provide perfectly optimal actions or corrections.
> - By learning to avoid states that trigger negative intervention signals, an RL agent can potentially learn policies that not only mitigate distribution shift but also improve upon suboptimal expert performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The RLIF framework strategically positions itself within the intricate landscape at the intersection of reinforcement learning (RL) and imitation learning (IL), directly addressing key limitations inherent in their application to practical domains, especially robotics. Traditional RL, while powerful in scenarios with well-defined reward functions (e.g., games), faces significant hurdles in real-world robotics where manually specifying accurate, dense rewards is often intractable. Imitation learning offers an alternative, learning directly from expert demonstrations. However, naive behavioral cloning (BC) struggles with "distributional shift," where compounding errors arise as the learned policy deviates from the expert's state distribution.

To counter distributional shift, interactive imitation learning methods, such as the DAgger (Dataset Aggregation) family, iteratively collect new training data through online expert interventions. While theoretically robust and practically effective, DAgger critically relies on the assumption that the intervening expert is *near-optimal*. This assumption is frequently violated in real-world settings, as human operators performing complex tasks like robotic teleoperation are often suboptimal, lacking the precision of an idealized controller. Prior work combining IL and RL typically initializes RL with demonstrations before refining it with a separate, predefined task reward, still necessitating an explicit reward function. Other related areas, such as learning from human preferences, generally involve different forms of explicit human feedback.

RLIF distinguishes itself by proposing a novel synthesis: it leverages the *act of intervention itself* as an implicit negative reward signal for an RL agent. This innovative approach allows RLIF to mitigate distributional shift (like DAgger) and operate without explicit reward functions (like IL), crucially, *without assuming the expert's actions are optimal*. By learning to avoid states and actions that trigger human intervention, RLIF aims to empower suboptimal human experts and enable policies to potentially surpass the expert's performance. This positions RLIF as a significant advancement towards more robust and practical learning-based control in complex, real-world environments by bridging the methodological gap between accessible human feedback and the powerful optimization capabilities of RL.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives driving the RLIF research are rooted in overcoming two fundamental limitations prevalent in contemporary learning-based control systems: the inherent difficulty of defining precise reward functions for reinforcement learning (RL), and the often-unrealistic reliance on perfectly optimal human experts in interactive imitation learning (IL).

First, a central objective is to circumvent the **challenge of reward specification in RL**. For numerous complex, real-world tasks, particularly in robotics, manually crafting accurate, dense, and non-sparse reward functions is an exceedingly difficult and often brittle process. Such rewards are indispensable for guiding RL agents to discover optimal behaviors, yet their absence or poor design can severely impede learning progress. RLIF aims to enable RL to function effectively without requiring this cumbersome and often subjective reward engineering.

Second, RLIF seeks to **relax the stringent assumption of optimal expert interventions in interactive IL**. While methods like DAgger effectively address distributional shift by incorporating online expert corrections, they presuppose that the human expert provides near-optimal demonstrations. In practical robotic applications, human teleoperation is frequently suboptimal, lacking the dexterity or precision of an idealized controller. This inherent suboptimality of human experts fundamentally limits the performance ceiling for algorithms that merely imitate their actions. The paper explicitly poses the question: "Can we combine the best parts of reinforcement learning and interactive imitation learning, combining the reward-maximizing behavior of RL, which can improve over the best available human behavior, with the accessible assumptions of interactive imitation learning?"

The core insight and motivation underpinning RLIF is the observation that "it’s easier to detect a mistake than it is to optimally correct it." This means the *decision to intervene* by an expert, even if the subsequent corrective action is suboptimal, inherently conveys valuable information about an undesirable state or action. RLIF leverages this by treating such interventions as implicit negative rewards. The primary motivation is for the RL agent to learn to proactively avoid situations that trigger these negative signals. This paradigm shift allows the system to learn safer, more efficient behaviors that can intrinsically improve upon the human expert's baseline, ultimately achieving performance levels beyond what direct imitation of a suboptimal expert could offer.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The RLIF methodology innovatively re-frames interactive imitation learning within a reinforcement learning (RL) paradigm by deriving reward signals directly from human expert interventions. The core approach is iterative and inspired by DAgger, but it crucially replaces supervised learning updates with RL.

**Algorithm Flow:**
1. **Deployment:** The current learned policy is deployed in the target environment.
2. **Reward Generation:** If a human expert intervenes at timestep $t$, the preceding transition $(s_{t-1}, a_{t-1}, s_t)$ is assigned a **negative reward (-1)**, signifying an undesirable behavior. If no intervention occurs, the transition receives a **zero reward (0)**. Experts have the option to temporarily take control for multiple steps after an intervention.
3. **RL Training:** All collected transitions and their intervention-derived reward labels are aggregated into a cumulative dataset. An off-policy RL algorithm, often initialized with a small amount of offline data for warm-starting, then trains the policy to maximize these cumulative rewards. The agent's objective thus becomes to learn behaviors that minimize interventions. This iterative process repeats over multiple rounds.

**Intervention Strategies (for Simulation):**
To systematically evaluate RLIF's robustness, the authors explored two distinct intervention models in simulation:
- **Random Intervention:** This serves as a baseline, where interventions occur uniformly at random with a specified probability. It tests whether any feedback signal, regardless of its informational content, is sufficient.
- **Value-Based Intervention:** This more sophisticated model simulates a realistic expert by defining intervention probability based on a **reference value function** ($Q_{\pi_{ref}}$). An intervention is likely if $Q_{\pi_{ref}}$ indicates a significant discrepancy between the agent's chosen action and a potentially desired (though not necessarily optimal) expert action. This strategy allows for a nuanced evaluation, separating the expert's capacity for optimal action ($\pi_{exp}$) from their ability to identify suboptimal behavior ($\pi_{ref}$).

**Practical Implementation and Theoretical Underpinnings:**
For continuous control tasks, RLIF utilizes an off-the-shelf, robust off-policy RL algorithm capable of efficiently handling both on-policy data (generated by the learning agent) and potentially suboptimal off-policy data (from expert interventions). Specifically, the authors employed **RLPD (Reinforcement Learning with Pre-training and Data)**, a variant of Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) enhanced for sample efficiency and offline data integration. RLIF acts as a meta-algorithm, defining the reward structure and data collection loop, while leveraging an existing RL algorithm as its core learner.

The paper also provides a unified theoretical framework for analyzing RLIF alongside DAgger. This includes deriving **asymptotic suboptimality gap bounds** for both methods, demonstrating that RLIF's bound is tighter. This theoretical advantage is particularly evident when the expert's judgment of optimal behavior (captured by $Q_{\pi_{ref}}$) is more accurate than their ability to execute optimal actions ($Q_{\pi_{exp}}$), validating the core premise. Furthermore, **non-asymptotic sample complexity bounds** confirm RLIF's efficient learning capabilities, demonstrating that it can achieve an $\epsilon$-optimal policy without prohibitive data requirements. This integrated approach, combining novel reward generation, robust data aggregation, and theoretical grounding, positions RLIF as a powerful new paradigm for interactive learning.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of RLIF comprehensively demonstrates its superior performance and practical viability across diverse, challenging tasks, strongly supported by a robust theoretical analysis.

**Simulation Benchmarks (Adroit & Gym Locomotion):**
On high-dimensional continuous control tasks, including Adroit dexterous manipulation (Pen) and Gym locomotion (Walker2d, Hopper), RLIF consistently and significantly outperformed DAgger and HG-DAgger baselines. This advantage was notably pronounced when dealing with suboptimal experts, where RLIF exhibited up to a 5x improvement. Crucially, RLIF demonstrated the capacity to *surpass* expert performance; it improved policies initially guided by experts as low as 15% optimal to achieve over 100% of the reference optimal score. The choice of intervention strategy was also critical: RLIF with **Value-Based Interventions** (where interventions are triggered by a discrepancy observed via a reference value function) consistently far exceeded random interventions, emphasizing that the informational content of the "when to intervene" signal is paramount for effective learning. Ablation studies further confirmed that the quality of this reference judgment directly impacts RLIF's final policy performance.

**Real-World Robotic Manipulation Tasks:**
RLIF's practical utility was rigorously validated on a 7-DoF Franka robot performing complex, vision-based peg insertion and cloth unfolding tasks. These scenarios present significant real-world challenges, including tight tolerances, complex contact dynamics, deformable objects, and high-dimensional visual inputs, making programmatic reward specification difficult. With real human feedback, RLIF achieved a **100% success rate for the peg insertion task within six interaction rounds** (approximately 20 minutes of wall-clock time) and a **95% success rate for the cloth unfolding task in seven rounds**. These impressive results underscore RLIF's scalability and efficacy in overcoming real-world complexities and significantly reducing the cognitive and motor burden on human operators, who merely needed to identify undesirable behavior.

**Theoretical Contributions:**
The paper provides a unified theoretical framework for comparing RLIF and DAgger. RLIF's theoretical **suboptimality gap** is proven to be tighter than DAgger's. Specifically, RLIF's bound is established as $\min(V^\star(\mu) - V_{\pi_{ref}}(\mu), V^\star(\mu) - V_{\pi_{exp}}(\mu)) + \frac{\delta\epsilon}{1-\gamma}$, in contrast to DAgger's $V^\star(\mu) - V_{\pi_{exp}}(\mu) + \frac{\delta\epsilon}{1-\gamma}$. This formalizes RLIF's theoretical advantage, particularly significant when the expert's capacity for judging optimal behavior ($V_{\pi_{ref}}$) is superior to their ability to execute it perfectly ($V_{\pi_{exp}}$), aligning directly with the empirical findings for suboptimal experts. Additionally, **non-asymptotic sample complexity bounds** confirm RLIF's efficient learning capabilities, indicating that it can achieve an $\epsilon$-optimal policy without prohibitive data requirements. Collectively, these findings firmly establish RLIF as a robust and powerful method for learning complex skills from realistic human interventions, offering substantial improvements over existing interactive imitation learning techniques.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The RLIF framework represents a significant advancement in the field of learning-based control, particularly for robotics, by effectively addressing critical limitations of both reinforcement learning (RL) and interactive imitation learning (IL). Its potential impact spans theoretical understanding, practical application, and broader accessibility of advanced robotic capabilities.

**Bridging Gaps and Empowering Suboptimal Experts:** RLIF's most profound significance lies in its ability to bridge crucial gaps in current methodologies. It liberates RL from the often-impossible task of manually engineering precise reward functions, instead deriving implicit rewards from accessible human feedback. Concurrently, it frees interactive IL from the stringent and often unrealistic demand for *optimal* human experts. By cleverly leveraging the *act of intervention itself* as an implicit negative reward, RLIF enables learning from readily available, albeit imperfect, human supervision. This paradigm shift significantly reduces the cognitive and motor burden on human operators, who are only required to identify undesirable behavior rather than provide perfect demonstrations or corrections. This makes advanced learning algorithms applicable to a much wider array of real-world problems.

**Accelerating Real-World Deployment:** The demonstrated empirical success of RLIF on challenging real-world robotic tasks, such as high-tolerance peg insertion and deformable object manipulation, underscores its immense potential to accelerate the deployment of autonomous systems. Tasks that are difficult to program explicitly or where rewards are hard to define can now be taught more efficiently and robustly. The rapid learning observed (e.g., 100% success in 20 minutes for peg insertion) illustrates a viable pathway towards faster iterative development and the acquisition of robust robotic skills in complex, unstructured environments.

**Theoretical Foundations and Future Outlook:** Beyond its practical gains, RLIF provides a rigorous theoretical foundation for its advantages. By formally demonstrating a tighter suboptimality gap compared to DAgger, the paper validates the intuitive principle that learning to *avoid mistakes* is a more powerful and often more attainable learning objective than merely *imitating corrections*. While RLIF offers significant advancements, the authors acknowledge important limitations, particularly concerning safety challenges inherent in online exploration, which remains a crucial direction for future work. Further research can explore more nuanced interpretations of human intervention signals, develop adaptive intervention strategies, and investigate leveraging multi-modal human feedback (e.g., verbal cues, gestures) to refine the implicit reward signal, thereby fostering even more intuitive and robust human-robot collaboration. Ultimately, RLIF offers a pragmatic and powerful paradigm for achieving complex robotic autonomy through accessible and effective human-in-the-loop machine learning.
