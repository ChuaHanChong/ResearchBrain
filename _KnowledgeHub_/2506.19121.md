# CUPID: Curating Data your Robot Loves with Influence Functions

> [!summary] Summary
> CUPID introduces a data curation method for robot imitation learning that directly and causally links individual training demonstrations to a policy's closed-loop performance using influence functions. This approach effectively improves policy success rates, identifies robust strategies, and mitigates spurious correlations across simulated and real-world robot tasks.

## Problem

- Robot imitation learning faces an objective mismatch, as policies are trained with Behavior Cloning loss but evaluated on closed-loop expected return, making it hard to link specific data to performance.
- Existing robot data curation methods often rely on heuristic quality metrics or performance-correlated indicators, which may not establish strong causal links between training data and downstream task success.
- Even 'expert' demonstrations can contain redundancies, suboptimal strategies, brittle behaviors, or spurious correlations that can degrade a policy's performance and generalization.

## Method

- **CUPID** defines '==Performance Influence==' as the derivative of a policy's expected return with respect to an infinitesimal weight on a demonstration's loss, establishing a causal link between data and closed-loop behavior.
- The method decomposes performance influence into tractable '==Action Influences==' that can be estimated using a ==REINFORCE-style== approach by evaluating the trained policy with a small number of online rollouts.
- It applies these influence scores to two core curation tasks: filtering detrimental demonstrations from an existing dataset and selecting beneficial demonstrations from a larger pool to add to the training data.

## Results

- CUPID consistently matched or outperformed state-of-the-art baselines, improving policy success rates by an average of **38%** on a real-world 'Figure-8' task and sometimes surpassing Oracle performance.
- It transformed a **100%** failure rate to **84-88%** success on a 'TuckBox' task by filtering influential demonstrations that induced a brittle 'sliding' strategy, promoting a more robust 'pick-and-place' behavior.
- The method increased success from **44%** to **84%** on a 'Bookshelf' task by identifying and mitigating spurious correlations that caused incorrect pulling motions.
- Datasets curated by CUPID for single-task policies also improved the fine-tuned performance of generalist Vision-Language-Action models like 0, indicating the transferability of curation benefits.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Human-perceived data quality does not always align with optimal policy performance; a direct causal link to expected return is essential for effective curation.
> - CUPID can identify and filter demonstrations that lead to brittle behaviors or spurious correlations (e.g., relying on irrelevant background cues), even when other heuristic-based methods fail.
> - The approach is resource-efficient, requiring only a small number of policy rollouts (e.g., 25-100) for demonstration rankings to stabilize, making it practical for real-world robotics.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research sits at the intersection of several rapidly evolving fields: robot imitation learning (IL), data-centric AI, and machine learning interpretability (specifically data attribution). It directly addresses a critical challenge in modern robotics: leveraging large, diverse datasets effectively for robust policy learning.

**Imitation Learning (IL) in Robotics:** IL has seen significant breakthroughs, especially with the advent of large-scale datasets (e.g., Open X-Embodiment, DROID) and powerful policy architectures (e.g., Diffusion Policies, Vision-Language-Action models like RT-1, RT-2, Octo, π0). The success of these systems, much like large language and vision models, is increasingly tied not just to architectural innovation but also to the *quality and composition of their training data*. This paper directly engages with this "data problem" in IL.

**Data-Centric AI:** The broader AI community has recognized that simply scaling up data quantity does not always lead to improved performance; data quality and strategic curation are paramount. This shift, often termed "data-centric AI," emphasizes systematic approaches to improve data instead of solely focusing on model architecture. CUPID applies this philosophy to robotics, where data collection is expensive and noisy, and demonstrations, even from "experts," can be mixed-quality, redundant, or misleading.

**Data Attribution and Interpretability:** The methodology of CUPID is rooted in influence functions, a technique popularized in data attribution literature to understand which training data points causally affect a model's predictions or behavior. While influence functions have been used for model interpretability, data valuation, and machine unlearning in standard supervised learning, their application to complex, sequential decision-making tasks like robot imitation learning, particularly with a focus on *closed-loop performance*, is a significant extension.

**Addressing the "Objective Mismatch" in IL:** A core problem in imitation learning is the "objective mismatch." Policies are typically trained using a supervised learning objective (Behavior Cloning loss) that aims to mimic expert actions, but they are evaluated based on their *closed-loop performance* in an environment (e.g., task success rate, expected return). This paper highlights that the BC loss does not always reflect true task success. Prior work in robot data curation attempted to address this with heuristic quality metrics (e.g., mutual information in DemInf) or by learning performance-correlated heuristics from online experience (e.g., Demo-SCORE). However, these methods often lack a *strong causal link* between individual demonstrations and downstream policy performance or may misattribute causes of failure (causal confusion).

**CUPID's Contribution to the Landscape:** CUPID advances the state of the art by providing a **principled, causal mechanism** to link specific training demonstrations to the *policy's expected return* (closed-loop performance). It moves beyond heuristic-based or correlation-based curation by directly measuring the *influence* of each demonstration on the ultimate success criterion. Furthermore, it addresses both the **filtering of existing harmful data** and the **selection of beneficial new data**, offering a more complete solution compared to methods solely focused on filtering. By demonstrating its utility with state-of-the-art diffusion policies and generalist VLA models like π0, CUPID positions itself as a crucial tool for scaling and robustifying the next generation of robot learning systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of the CUPID research is to develop a data curation method for robot imitation learning that directly and causally links individual training demonstrations to a policy's downstream closed-loop performance, thereby maximizing its expected return.

The motivations behind this objective are multifaceted:

1. **Bridging the Performance-Data Gap:** In robot imitation learning, policy performance is inherently tied to the quality and composition of demonstration data. However, establishing a clear, quantitative understanding of *how* specific demonstrations contribute to task success or failure in closed-loop execution remains a significant challenge. Current methods often struggle to predict the true impact of data on a policy's real-world efficacy.

2. **Limitations of Existing Curation Methods:** Prior work in robot data curation primarily relies on:
 - **Task-agnostic heuristic quality metrics:** These methods (e.g., mutual information) make implicit assumptions that "high-quality" data, as defined by the heuristic, will translate to better policy performance—an assumption that often doesn't hold universally.
 - **Performance-correlated heuristics:** Some approaches learn to distinguish successful from failed policy rollouts, but they don't establish strong *causal links* between specific *training demonstrations* and observed *policy behaviors*, risking misattribution of success or failure. These methods might also require observing both successful and failed rollouts, which can be restrictive.

3. **Challenges with "Expert" Data:** Even "expert" demonstrations can contain redundancies, suboptimal strategies, brittle behaviors, or spurious correlations that, when learned, can *harm* a policy's performance or generalization capabilities. Simply scaling datasets without curation can exacerbate these issues.

4. **Need for Systematic Data Utility Extraction:** As robotics datasets grow larger and more diverse, extracting maximum utility from them becomes imperative for developing reliable, open-world robotic policies. This requires a precise method to identify which data points are truly beneficial and which are detrimental.

5. **Practical Curation Tasks:** The authors specifically aim to address two critical, practical data curation tasks for a policy debugging toolchain:
 - **Data Filtering (Task 1):** Identifying and removing existing training demonstrations that limit the performance of a base policy.
 - **Data Selection (Task 2):** Guiding the subselection of new demonstrations from a larger pool to maximally improve the base policy, given a fixed budget.

By developing CUPID, the researchers aim to provide a more principled and effective approach to robot data curation that directly optimizes for closed-loop performance, addressing the shortcomings of previous methods and facilitating the development of more robust and generalizable robot skills.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

CUPID (CUrating Performance-Influencing Demonstrations) is a novel data curation method for robot imitation learning that leverages influence functions to establish a causal link between training demonstrations and a policy's closed-loop performance.

The methodology can be broken down into the following key steps:

1. **Problem Formulation in Imitation Learning (IL):**
 - The paper considers a standard IL setup where a policy $\pi_\theta$ is trained via Behavior Cloning (BC) to minimize a loss $L_{bc}(\theta'; \mathcal{D})$ over a dataset $\mathcal{D}$ of $n$ expert demonstrations $\xi_i$. Each $\xi_i$ is a state-action trajectory.
 - The crucial distinction is that policies are trained to match successful behaviors in $\mathcal{D}$ but evaluated on their *expected return* $J(\pi_\theta)$ from closed-loop environment interactions. This objective mismatch is central to the problem CUPID addresses.
 - Data curation is formally defined as identifying demonstrations that maximize $J(\pi_\theta)$ for two tasks: filtering $k$ demonstrations from $\mathcal{D}$ (Task 1) and selecting $k$ demonstrations from a new holdout set $\mathcal{H}$ to add to $\mathcal{D}$ (Task 2). Policy testing and evaluation assume access to a small dataset of $m$ policy rollouts $\mathcal{D}_\tau$ and their associated binary returns (success/failure).

2. **Demonstration-Performance Influence (Ψ_π-inf_):**
 - CUPID introduces a novel concept: **Performance Influence**, defined as the derivative of the policy's expected return $J(\pi_\theta)$ with respect to an infinitesimal weight $\epsilon$ on a specific demonstration's loss $\ell_{traj}(\xi; \pi_{\theta'})$.
 - Mathematically, $\Psi_{\pi\text{-inf}}(\xi) := \frac{dJ(\pi_\theta)}{d\epsilon}\big|_{\epsilon=0} = -\nabla_\theta J(\pi_\theta)^\top H_{bc}^{-1} \nabla_\theta \ell_{traj}(\xi; \pi_\theta)$, where $H_{bc}$ is the Hessian of the BC objective. This aligns with the standard definition of influence functions.
 - **Challenge:** $J(\pi_\theta)$ depends on unknown transition dynamics and reward functions, making its direct computation difficult.
 - **Solution: Decomposition into Action Influences:** The paper proves a key proposition (Proposition 1) that decomposes the performance influence into a sum of *Action Influences* ($\Psi_{\text{a-inf}}$) weighted by the trajectory return $R(\tau)$.
 $\Psi_{\pi\text{-inf}}(\xi) = E_{\tau \sim p(\tau|\pi_\theta)} \left[ R(\tau) \sum_{(s',a') \in \tau} \sum_{(s,a) \in \xi} \Psi_{\text{a-inf}}((s',a'),(s,a)) \right]$.
 - **Action Influence ($\Psi_{\text{a-inf}}$):** This is the influence of a training state-action pair $(s,a)$ on the policy's log-likelihood $\log\pi_\theta(a'|s')$ for a test state-action pair $(s',a')$. This quantity is tractable to compute using existing data attribution methods.

3. **Estimation of Performance Influence:**
 - A REINFORCE-style estimator is used.
 - The policy $\pi_\theta$ (trained on $\mathcal{D}$) is evaluated online to collect $m$ rollouts $\mathcal{D}_\tau$ and their returns $R(\tau_i)$.
 - For each training demonstration $\xi$ and each rollout $\tau_i$, action influences $\Psi_{\text{a-inf}}((s',a'),(s,a))$ are computed.
 - These are then averaged across rollouts and weighted by their returns to yield an empirical estimate of $\hat{\Psi}_{\pi\text{-inf}}(\xi)$.

4. **Influence Functions for Diffusion Policies (Appendix A.1):**
 - Since state-of-the-art diffusion policies do not directly output log-likelihoods, a specific approximation is needed for $\Psi_{\text{a-inf}}$.
 - Instead of $\nabla_\theta \log\pi_\theta(a'|s')$, the method uses $\nabla_\theta \ell_{square}(s',a'; \pi_\theta)$, where $\ell_{square}$ is a surrogate, label-agnostic output function based on the $\ell_2$-norm of the noise prediction network's output. This approximation has been shown empirically to correlate better with actual loss changes and reduces computational cost.
 - The efficient TRAK framework is employed for computing these action influences, using random projections and a Gauss-Newton Hessian approximation.

5. **Data Curation with Performance Influence:**
 - The estimated performance influences are used to make first-order Taylor approximations for the "leave-one-out" (for filtering) and "add-one-in" (for selection) effects on expected return.
 - **Filter-k:** Selects the $k$ demonstrations with the least positive (or most negative) $\Psi_{\text{out}\pi\text{-inf}}$ (i.e., those predicted to harm performance the most if kept).
 - **Select-k:** Selects the $k$ demonstrations with the most positive $\Psi_{\text{in}\pi\text{-inf}}$ (i.e., those predicted to improve performance the most if added).
 - This forms a "linear datamodel" for curation.

6. **Additional Quality Metrics (CUPID-QUALITY):**
 - To potentially reduce variance in influence estimates, especially with limited rollouts, CUPID explores combining $\Psi_{\pi\text{-inf}}$ with a reward-agnostic, heuristic **quality score** ($\Psi_{\text{qual}}$). This quality score penalizes demonstrations with outlier or noisy action influence scores.
 - The combination is a convex one: $\alpha\Psi_{\pi\text{-inf}} + (1-\alpha)\Psi_{\text{qual}}$, with $\alpha=1$ being pure CUPID and $\alpha=1/2$ for CUPID-QUALITY. All scores are normalized before combination.

In essence, CUPID provides a principled, data-driven framework that directly optimizes for desired closed-loop robotic behavior by attributing performance outcomes back to individual training examples, moving beyond heuristic-based data selection.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments, conducted across three simulated tasks from the RoboMimic benchmark and three real-world Franka FR3 manipulator tasks, demonstrate CUPID's effectiveness in diverse and challenging imitation learning settings, consistently outperforming state-of-the-art baselines.

1. **Improving Policy Performance in Mixed-Quality Regimes:**
 - **RoboMimic:** On "Lift MH," "Square MH," and "Transport MH" tasks (with ground-truth quality labels), CUPID policies consistently matched or outperformed baselines like DemInf (which targets quality). This crucial finding showed that human-perceived data quality does not always translate directly to maximum downstream policy success.
 - **CUPID-QUALITY (combined performance & quality metric)** performed exceptionally well in these settings, achieving the highest policy success rates, even surpassing an Oracle baseline in 3 out of 5 cases. Notably, it achieved higher success rates on "Transport MH" using less than 33% of the original data and 10% of model parameters.
 - **Real-world "Figure-8" Task:** CUPID improved the base policy's success rate by an average of 38% (across filtering and selection). Again, CUPID-QUALITY further strengthened performance, validating the utility of combining performance and quality metrics in mixed-quality scenarios.

2. **Identifying Robust Test-time Strategies from Policy Failures:**
 - **Real-world "TuckBox" Task:** This task involved a distribution shift that rendered the dominant "sliding" strategy unreliable, leading to a 100% failure rate for the base policy.
 - CUPID, even when observing only failures, successfully linked these failures to the influential "sliding" demonstrations. By filtering these, CUPID yielded a policy exhibiting the more robust "pick-and-place" behavior, achieving 84-88% success, comparable to the Oracle.
 - Crucially, baselines like DemInf and CUPID-QUALITY failed here because they incorrectly filtered out the higher-variance "pick-and-place" demonstrations, perceiving them as lower quality, leading to policies defaulting to the brittle sliding strategy. This highlights CUPID's ability to discern *robustness* over superficial "quality."

3. **Disentangling Spurious Correlations in Demonstration Data:**
 - **Real-world "Bookshelf" Task:** The base policy achieved only 44% success due to a spurious correlation (white background $\rightarrow$ horizontal pulling, even when a vertical pull was needed for a stacked book).
 - CUPID achieved an 84% success rate by identifying demonstrations that causally drove failure (horizontal pull with white background). It enabled dataset rebalancing that mitigated the effect of these spurious correlations.
 - In contrast, Demo-SCORE appeared to misattribute failure to rollout correlates (e.g., stacked book) rather than causal factors (white background), while DemInf and CUPID-QUALITY favored the lower-variance horizontal motion, resulting in negligible performance gains.

4. **Resource Efficiency for Effective Curation:**
 - CUPID demonstrated practical applicability under realistic rollout budgets, requiring only $m \in$ rollouts for simpler tasks ("Lift MH," "Square MH") and $m \in$ for more complex ones ("Transport MH") for demonstration rankings to stabilize. For real-world Franka tasks, $m=25$ rollouts were sufficient. This validates its feasibility for real-world robotics where rollouts can be expensive.

5. **Curated Data Strengthening Generalist Policy Performance:**
 - Experiments showed that datasets curated by CUPID for single-task diffusion policies could significantly improve the fine-tuned performance of a generalist Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model, π0. This indicates that the benefits of curation are transferable across different policy architectures.
 - This also suggests that large-scale pre-training of VLA models does not inherently make them robust to low-quality or brittle demonstrations, emphasizing that data curation remains critical even for generalist robot policies.

In summary, CUPID provides a robust and general method for data curation that directly optimizes for policy performance, demonstrating its efficacy in improving success rates, identifying robust strategies, and mitigating spurious correlations, both in simulation and on real robot hardware.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

CUPID represents a significant advancement in robot imitation learning and the broader field of data-centric AI, with substantial potential impact across several dimensions:

1. **Causal Understanding of Robot Policy Behavior:** The most profound contribution of CUPID is its ability to establish a **formal, causal link** between individual training demonstrations and a robot policy's closed-loop performance. This moves beyond correlational or heuristic-based methods, providing a deeper, more actionable understanding of *why* a robot behaves in a certain way or fails a task. This causal attribution is critical for developing more reliable and interpretable robot systems.

2. **Robustness and Generalization in Robot Learning:** By precisely identifying demonstrations that induce brittle strategies, exhibit spurious correlations, or are simply low-quality, CUPID enables the creation of more robust and generalizable policies. This is vital for deploying robots in unpredictable real-world environments where policies must perform reliably under varying conditions and avoid relying on non-causal features. The "TuckBox" and "Bookshelf" experiments are compelling demonstrations of this capability.

3. **Enhanced Data Efficiency and Scalability:** CUPID allows researchers and practitioners to achieve state-of-the-art policy performance with significantly *less* training data, often just a fraction of the original dataset. This has immense practical implications, as data collection in robotics is notoriously expensive, time-consuming, and labor-intensive. Reducing the reliance on massive, unfiltered datasets can accelerate research, lower development costs, and make advanced robot learning more accessible.

4. **Powerful Debugging and Diagnostic Tool:** CUPID provides an invaluable debugging tool for robot learning systems. When a policy performs suboptimally, it can attribute failures back to specific demonstrations, helping developers pinpoint problematic data. This insight can guide targeted data collection or labeling efforts, streamlining the iterative process of developing and refining robot skills.

5. **Broader Impact on Data-Centric AI:** The work extends the utility of influence functions to a complex, sequential decision-making domain with an inherent objective mismatch (BC loss vs. expected return). This methodology could inspire similar data attribution and curation techniques in other machine learning domains facing analogous challenges, especially those with decoupled training and evaluation objectives.

6. **Guidance for Future Data Collection:** While the current work focuses on curating existing data, the insights gained from identifying influential demonstrations could actively inform future data collection strategies. For instance, if certain types of demonstrations are consistently highly influential, data collectors could be instructed to prioritize collecting more such examples, or conversely, to avoid examples that consistently lead to negative influence.

7. **Advancing Generalist Robot Policies:** The finding that CUPID-curated datasets improve the fine-tuned performance of generalist VLA models like π0 is particularly impactful. It indicates that even large-scale pre-training does not eliminate the need for careful data curation, suggesting that CUPID's methodology will remain relevant and crucial as robot learning moves towards more generalized, multi-task systems. Furthermore, it suggests a promising path for scalable curation, where smaller, specialized policies might curate data for larger, more expensive generalist models.

In conclusion, CUPID offers a principled and practically effective solution to a fundamental problem in robot learning. By enabling precise, performance-based data curation, it is poised to significantly accelerate the development of robust, reliable, and intelligent robotic systems capable of operating effectively in the real world.
