# Supervised Reinforcement Learning: From Expert Trajectories to Step-wise Reasoning

> [!summary] Summary
> The Supervised Reinforcement Learning (SRL) framework enables smaller Large Language Models (LLMs) to learn complex multi-step reasoning by providing dense, step-wise similarity rewards from expert demonstrations. It notably improved greedy average accuracy on math reasoning benchmarks by 3.0% over the base model, and showed a 74% relative improvement in resolve rate on agentic software engineering tasks.

## Problem

- Small-scale, open-source LLMs struggle to solve challenging multi-step reasoning problems effectively with current training paradigms.
- Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) often leads to overfitting on complex expert demonstrations, sometimes causing performance degradation for smaller models.
- Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) faces sparse reward signals on difficult problems, hindering effective learning due to a lack of positive advantage estimates.

## Method

- Introduced ==Supervised Reinforcement Learning (SRL)==, which reformulates problem-solving as a sequential decision-making process by decomposing expert solutions into logical 'actions'.
- Generated ==step-wise training data== from expert trajectories and provided a ==dense sequence similarity reward== based on the model's predicted action, allowing for flexible internal monologues.
- Optimized the policy using a ==Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)== objective with dynamic sampling and explored a ==curriculum learning strategy== combining SRL with RLVR.

## Results

- SRL alone achieved a **27.6%** greedy average accuracy on math reasoning benchmarks, a **3.0%** increase over the base Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct model.
- The SRL → RLVR pipeline delivered the strongest performance, reaching **28.3%** greedy average accuracy, a **3.7%** increase over the base model and outperforming all baselines.
- SRL generalized to agentic software engineering tasks, achieving a **14.8%** resolve rate in an oracle file editing setting, representing a **74%** relative improvement over an SFT baseline.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Dense, step-wise supervision, through sequence similarity rewards on discrete actions, effectively guides LLMs on complex problems where sparse rewards or rigid imitation fail.
> - SRL encourages flexible and sophisticated reasoning patterns, including planning, on-the-fly adjustments, and reflective verification, without merely increasing output length.
> - The combination of SRL for initial robust guidance and subsequent RLVR refinement forms a powerful curriculum learning approach for maximizing performance on challenging tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of Large Language Models (LLMs) has demonstrated capabilities across various domains, including mathematical problem-solving, search-result integration, tool-use, and long-horizon agentic interaction. A significant focus in enhancing these capabilities involves reinforcement learning (RL), particularly methods that leverage verifiable rewards (RLVR) based on outcomes like final answer accuracy. This approach provides a scalable framework for fostering complex problem-solving behaviors, including self-reflection.

However, the effectiveness of outcome-based RL methods is constrained by the policy model's ability to discover correct solutions within a limited rollout budget. For challenging problems requiring multi-step reasoning, where the model's success rate is low (e.g., pass@k near zero), positive reward signals become sparse. This scarcity can lead to uninformative policy gradients, making such problems intractable for standard RL methods. Conversely, Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), an alternative imitation learning approach, also faces limitations. While SFT can impart reasoning behaviors by imitating expert demonstrations, its next-token prediction objective often enforces rigid, token-level imitation. This can lead to overfitting and shallow reasoning, particularly when training data is limited or the model is less capable, hindering generalization. This is particularly problematic for smaller, open-source models attempting to learn complex tasks.

This work addresses the identified gap where both SFT and outcome-based RL methods struggle to train smaller models effectively on difficult, multi-step reasoning problems. It proposes a novel framework designed to provide more granular and consistent learning signals, thereby enabling models to learn challenging problems that were previously difficult for existing methods.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable small-scale, open-source LLMs to effectively learn and solve complex, multi-step reasoning problems that are currently intractable for conventional Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) methods.

The motivation for this objective stems from several identified limitations of current approaches:

- **Limitations of RLVR for Hard Problems:** For problems requiring complex, multi-step reasoning, LLMs often struggle to sample correct solutions even after numerous attempts, resulting in a near-zero success rate (pass@k rate). In such scenarios, positive reward signals from verifiable final outcomes (as used in RLVR) become extremely sparse or non-existent. This scarcity prevents effective policy gradient updates, making it difficult for models to learn. Furthermore, uniformly penalizing all incorrect final outputs can introduce training instability and impede progress.
- **Limitations of SFT for Complex Demonstrations:** While SFT utilizes expert demonstrations to teach reasoning, its token-by-token imitation objective can lead to issues. It often results in rigid reproduction of long demonstrations, limiting the model's ability to generalize beyond the training data. For small models with modest training data, SFT can overfit to specific patterns in complex expert trajectories, resulting in shallow reasoning and, in some cases, performance degradation compared to the base model.
- **Need for Flexible and Robust Learning Signals:** There is a critical need for a training framework that can provide richer, smoother, and more dense learning signals for multi-step reasoning tasks. This framework should guide models through intermediate reasoning steps, allowing for flexibility in internal thought processes while ensuring external actions align with expert strategies.
- **Empowering Smaller Models:** The research specifically aims to address these challenges for small-scale open-source models, which typically have fewer parameters and computational resources compared to very large proprietary models, making efficient and robust learning even more crucial.

To overcome these challenges, the authors propose Supervised Reinforcement Learning (SRL), a framework that reformulates problem-solving as a sequence of logical "actions" and provides fine-grained, step-wise supervision.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology introduces Supervised Reinforcement Learning (SRL), a framework that reframes complex problem-solving as a sequential decision-making process. SRL aims to provide dense, step-level feedback by teaching the model to reproduce expert actions while allowing for flexible internal reasoning.

#### 3.1. Action-based Problem Formulation

The core idea is to decompose an expert solution trajectory (y) into a sequence of logical "actions" (y_step_n). Each y_step_n represents a concrete decision or operation. This formulation is designed to be domain-agnostic, meaning an "action" could be an algebraic manipulation in math or an executed command in software engineering.

#### 3.2. Step-wise Training Data Construction

From a single N-step expert solution, SRL constructs N-1 partial trajectories as training instances. For each step *k*, an input prompt (x_step_k) is created, comprising the original problem (x) and all preceding expert steps (y_step_1..., y_step_k-1). The model's task is then to predict the subsequent step, y_step_k. This process significantly expands the training data from each expert solution, providing varied intermediate states for learning.

#### 3.3. Learning with Sequence Similarity Reward and Inner Monologue

When presented with a partial context (x_step_k), the policy model (p_θ) is prompted to generate the subsequent action step. This generation process includes an internal monologue (y'_think), encapsulated within "<think>" tags, followed by the logical action (y'_step_k). The internal monologue provides the model flexibility to develop its own reasoning style, while the reward signal is computed solely on the quality of the generated logical action.

The reward function, R(y'_step_k, y_step_k), measures the similarity between the model's predicted action and the corresponding expert action. This similarity is calculated using a metric derived from Python's `difflib.SequenceMatcher`, which computes the ratio:
R = (2 * M) / T
where:
- T (Total elements) is the sum of the lengths of the two sequences (|S1| + |S2|).
- M (Matched elements) is the total count of elements in all non-overlapping matching blocks between the two sequences. This involves finding the longest common subsequence and recursively identifying matches in the remaining segments.
The resulting reward R is a value between 0 and 1. A negative reward (-1) is assigned if the model's output fails to adhere to the required format. The policy p_θ is then optimized using this reward signal through the Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) objective function (Equation 1 in the paper), a common RL algorithm for LLMs.

#### 3.4. Dynamic Sampling for SRL

SRL incorporates a dynamic sampling strategy to filter out training samples that yield rollouts with near-zero reward variance. This prevents model updates from uninformative gradients, similar to how dynamic sampling is used in outcome-based RL (e.g., DAPO). For SRL, a sample is retained if the standard deviation of its rollouts' sequence similarity reward scores exceeds a predefined threshold (ε > 0). This ensures that only samples providing meaningful learning signals contribute to training, and batches are continuously filled by re-sampling until this condition is met.

#### 3.5. Comparison with Baselines

The SRL framework is compared against:
- **SFT:** Trained on complete reasoning traces (R1 reasoning) or final solutions (R1 outline).
- **RLVR:** Implemented with GRPO and dynamic sampling.
- **SFT → RLVR:** RLVR applied after an initial SFT phase.
- **R3 (Reverse Curriculum Reinforcement Learning):** An alternative method that structures step-by-step learning by working backward from demonstrations.

SRL is evaluated both as a standalone technique and in a sequential configuration where it initializes training before refinement with RLVR (SRL → RLVR).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents detailed experimental results across mathematical reasoning and software engineering domains, demonstrating the effectiveness of the Supervised Reinforcement Learning (SRL) framework.

#### 4.1. Main Results: Math Reasoning

Experiments were conducted using Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, fine-tuned on the s1K-1.1 dataset (1,000 challenging math questions with detailed reasoning traces from DeepSeek R1). Models were evaluated on AMC23, AIME24, AIME25, and Minerva Math benchmarks, reporting both greedy and average@32 scores.

- **SFT Performance Degradation:** Direct application of SFT on the s1K-1.1 dataset, whether on full reasoning traces or outlines, led to a notable performance degradation compared to the base Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct model (e.g., S1K-7B averaged 11.7% greedy, while the base model averaged 24.6%). This indicates the difficulty of the dataset and the limitations of SFT in preventing overfitting on complex, small-scale data.
- **RLVR Marginal Gains:** While RLVR (using GRPO) maintained generalization, its performance gains over the base model were marginal (e.g., RLVR averaged 24.5% greedy, a slight decrease from the base model's 24.6% average).
- **SRL Outperformance:** The proposed SRL method substantially outperformed both SFT and RLVR. SRL achieved an average greedy score of 27.6%, representing a 3.1% absolute improvement over RLVR.
- **SRL → RLVR Pipeline:** The combination of SRL for initialization followed by RLVR refinement yielded the strongest overall performance, achieving an average greedy score of 28.3%, an absolute increase of 3.8% over RLVR and 3.7% over the base model, using only 1k training data.
- **Comparison with R3:** While R3 (reverse curriculum RL) was competitive on AMC23, it lagged behind SRL on more challenging benchmarks like AIME24 (SRL 16.7% vs. R3 13.3% greedy). This difference is attributed to SRL's dense, intrinsic step-wise supervision versus R3's reliance on a sparse outcome reward facilitated by reverse curriculum.

#### 4.2. Analysis: Math Reasoning

Further analysis elucidated the mechanisms contributing to SRL's performance:

- **Effect of Dynamic Sampling:** Dynamic sampling, which filters out samples with low reward variance, was shown to provide non-trivial performance improvement for SRL. SRL with dynamic sampling achieved an average greedy score of 27.6%, compared to 24.7% without it, demonstrating its critical role in the effectiveness of the RL training loop.
- **Guidance Granularity:** The effectiveness of SRL was attributed to its fine-grained, multi-step guidance. When compared to a holistic, one-step sequence similarity reward, the multi-step SRL approach achieved superior performance (SRL averaged 27.6% greedy vs. one-step similarity 25.9%). This indicates that decomposing the problem into discrete steps with dense similarity rewards is more effective than evaluating the entire solution at once, especially for challenging data.
- **Model Size Variation:** SRL demonstrated consistent performance boosts across different model sizes. Applied to Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct, SRL → RLVR improved the average score by 3.1% over the base model and surpassed RLVR, showing its generalizability even with limited capacity, particularly on tractable tasks like AMC23 and Minerva Math.
- **Interleaved Reasoning Behaviors:** Qualitative analysis revealed that SRL-finetuned models, especially when refined with RLVR, developed distinct and adaptive reasoning trajectories. These models dynamically interleave reasoning steps throughout generation, exhibiting behaviors such as structured planning, dynamic adjustment, and reflective verification, without an increase in overall solution length. This suggests an enhancement in planning and quality of reasoning rather than mere verbosity.

#### 4.3. Extension: Software Engineering Agentic Reasoning

The SRL framework was also applied to software engineering tasks, specifically resolving programming issues on a dataset derived from agent trajectories generated by claude-3-7-sonnet-20250219. The "action" in this domain was defined as an environment-consumable command (e.g., bash calls).

- **Performance on SWE-Bench-Verified:** SRL substantially outperformed baselines on the SWE-Bench-Verified benchmark. In the oracle file editing evaluation, SRL achieved a 14.8% resolve rate, a 74% relative improvement over the SWE-Gym-7B baseline (8.4%) and significantly higher than the base Qwen2.5-Coder-7B-Instruct (5.8%). The performance gains were consistent in the more challenging end-to-end evaluation, where SRL obtained an 8.6% resolve rate, double that of SWE-Gym-7B (4.2%).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Supervised Reinforcement Learning (SRL) framework introduces a novel approach for training Large Language Models (LLMs) on complex, multi-step reasoning tasks. Its significance lies in several key aspects:

- **Enabling Learning for Difficult Problems:** SRL addresses a critical gap where conventional Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) often overfits or leads to performance degradation on challenging datasets, and Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) struggles due to sparse reward signals on problems with low success rates. By providing dense, step-wise supervision based on sequence similarity, SRL makes previously intractable problems learnable for small models.
- **Robustness and Versatility:** The demonstrated effectiveness of SRL across diverse domains—mathematical reasoning and agentic software engineering—suggests its robustness and versatility as a general training framework for reasoning-oriented LLMs. Its ability to improve performance on tasks requiring complex, multi-turn interactions and deep reasoning indicates a broad applicability.
- **Flexible Reasoning:** SRL encourages models to develop flexible reasoning patterns, such as interleaved planning, dynamic adjustment, and reflective verification. This "think-and-act" mechanism allows models to generate their own internal monologues while ensuring external actions align with expert strategies, leading to higher-quality solutions without simply increasing output length.
- **Curriculum Learning Strategy:** The research highlights the synergistic potential of combining SRL with RLVR. Initializing training with SRL before refining with RLVR (SRL → RLVR pipeline) consistently yields the strongest overall performance, establishing a powerful curriculum learning strategy. This suggests SRL can serve as an effective warm-start for more outcome-oriented RL methods.
- **Bridging Imitation Learning and RL:** SRL effectively bridges the gap between imitation learning and reinforcement learning. It leverages the structured guidance from expert demonstrations (like SFT) but frames it within an RL objective, allowing for iterative refinement and generalization beyond strict imitation.

**Potential Impact:** This work has the potential to significantly advance the capabilities of open-source and smaller LLMs in complex reasoning tasks. By making it feasible for these models to learn from challenging, multi-step problems, SRL could contribute to the development of more capable and versatile AI agents in fields such as scientific discovery, automated software development, and advanced problem-solving.

**Limitations:** Despite its strengths, SRL's effectiveness is subject to certain constraints. It relies on the availability of high-quality, decomposable expert trajectories, assuming that complex reasoning can be cleanly partitioned into discrete, logical steps. This dependency may limit its applicability in domains where expert demonstrations are sparse, unstructured, or involve holistic intuition rather than a sequential chain-of-thought. Furthermore, the success of the self-reflective loop is contingent on the student model possessing a baseline instruction-following capability, as coherent rollout samples are necessary to yield non-trivial reward signals. Future research efforts are suggested to address these dependencies, explore adaptation to ambiguous action definitions, and investigate the impact of different similarity functions.
