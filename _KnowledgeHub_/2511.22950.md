# RobotSeg: A Model and Dataset for Segmenting Robots in Image and Video

> [!summary] Summary
> RobotSeg introduces the first foundation model for segmenting robots in images and videos, accompanied by the large-scale Video Robot Segmentation (VRS) dataset. The model integrates structure-enhanced memory and a robot prompt generator, achieving state-of-the-art accuracy, temporal consistency, and label efficiency across diverse robot embodiments and tasks.

## Problem

- Existing segmentation models struggle with challenges inherent to robots, including embodiment diversity, appearance ambiguity, structural complexity, and rapid shape changes during operation.
- General foundation models like SAM 2 lack specific modeling for articulated robot structures, rely on manual user prompts, and require expensive per-frame video mask annotations, hindering autonomous robotic perception.
- A lack of large-scale, temporally annotated datasets for video robot segmentation exists, making it difficult to train robust models for dynamic robotic environments.

## Method

- **RobotSeg**, a foundation model built upon ==SAM 2==, is introduced with three key innovations: a Structure-Enhanced Memory Associator (SEMA), a Robot Prompt Generator (RPG), and a Label-Efficient Training (LET) strategy.
- The Video Robot Segmentation (VRS) dataset is constructed, featuring 2,812 videos (138,707 frames) with hierarchical annotations, using a semi-supervised approach where only the first frame is fully annotated for training efficiency.
- SEMA integrates temporal context and robot-aware structural cues for robust feature representation, while RPG autonomously generates class and object tokens from memory, eliminating the need for manual prompts.

## Results

- RobotSeg achieved state-of-the-art performance on the VRS dataset, scoring **85.1** J&F for whole robot segmentation in the automatic setting, surpassing fine-tuned RoboEngine by **4.9** J&F.
- The model demonstrated robust generalization across **10** diverse robot categories, consistently ranking among the top methods for whole robot, arm, and gripper segmentation, outperforming baselines which showed larger performance drops on specific robot types.
- RobotSeg maintained competitive performance on the image-level RoboEngine dataset (**87.9** J&F automatic whole robot) and offered high computational efficiency, processing frames at >**10** FPS, supporting practical real-world deployment.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating robot-specific structural priors and temporal memory into a foundation segmentation model greatly improves accuracy and consistency for segmenting articulated robots in dynamic scenes.
> - Autonomous prompt generation, combining class-level semantics with dynamically extracted object-level cues from historical memory, enables fully automatic and temporally coherent robot segmentation without manual intervention.
> - Label-efficient training strategies, leveraging cycle, semantic, and patch consistency losses, can effectively train robust video segmentation models using only first-frame ground truth, making large-scale video dataset annotation practical.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Accurate robot segmentation is identified as a foundational capability for various aspects of robotic perception, including visual servoing in vision-language-action (VLA) systems, scalable robot-centric data augmentation, precise real-to-simulation transfer, and safety monitoring in human-robot environments. Despite the advancements in general segmentation models, segmenting robots presents distinct challenges due to their diverse embodiments, appearance ambiguity, structural complexity, and rapid shape changes during operation.

Existing work in robot segmentation, such as RoboEngine and RoVi-Aug, has focused primarily on static images and utilized language-conditioned or finetuned general segmentation models. These approaches often overlook the temporal coherence required for dynamic video perception and struggle with the aforementioned robot-specific complexities.

The broader field of computer vision includes several relevant areas. Semi-supervised video object segmentation (VOS) traditionally relies on a manually annotated mask in the first frame, which is then propagated through the video. However, this manual annotation requirement poses scalability challenges for large-scale robotic datasets. Language-conditioned segmentation models, like CLIPSeg, LISA, and EVF-SAM, predict masks based on textual prompts but often lack robust temporal modeling or incur significant computational overhead from large language models. Promptable visual segmentation, exemplified by the Segment Anything Model (SAM) and its video counterpart SAM 2, allows flexible mask generation using user-provided prompts (e.g., clicks or bounding boxes). While powerful, SAM 2 still depends on manual prompts for initialization, limiting autonomy, and requires extensive per-frame video mask annotations for training, which is resource-intensive for diverse robotic scenarios.

RobotSeg positions itself at the intersection of these fields by developing a dedicated foundation model for robot segmentation in both images and videos. It aims to overcome the limitations of existing general and robot-specific segmentation methods by introducing robot-aware mechanisms and a label-efficient training strategy, thereby contributing a specialized solution to the general challenge of perception for real-world robotic deployment. The introduction of the Video Robot Segmentation (VRS) dataset also addresses a gap in publicly available large-scale, temporally annotated robot video data.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust and efficient foundation model for segmenting robots in both image and video data, termed RobotSeg, alongside a comprehensive video robot segmentation dataset, VRS.

The motivation stems from several critical challenges observed when applying current state-of-the-art segmentation models, including foundation models like SAM 2.1 and robot-specific methods like RoboEngine, to robotic perception tasks:

1. **Challenges Intrinsic to Robot Segmentation:**
 - **Embodiment Diversity:** Robots exhibit significant variations in shape, size, and appearance across different platforms (e.g., Franka, Fanuc Mate, UR5, Sawyer). This diversity makes it difficult for general models to generalize across robot types.
 - **Appearance Ambiguity:** The visual textures and colors of robots can frequently resemble cluttered backgrounds, leading to confusion and errors in differentiating robot regions from the surrounding environment.
 - **Structural Complexity:** Robots are composed of articulated structures with multiple parts and joints. Existing methods often struggle to segment these complex structures completely, resulting in broken, fragmented, or incomplete masks.
 - **Rapid Shape Changes:** During manipulation tasks, robots undergo dynamic and rapid shape changes. These unstable visual patterns present difficulties for segmentation models to maintain consistent and accurate masks over time.

2. **Method-Level Deficiencies of Existing Foundation Models (e.g., SAM 2) for Robotic Perception:**
 - **Lack of Articulated Robot Structure Modeling:** General segmentation models are not specifically designed to capture the inherent geometric regularities and articulated priors of robot structures, which can lead to incomplete or structurally inconsistent masks.
 - **Reliance on Manual Prompts:** Models like SAM 2 require manual prompts (e.g., clicks, bounding boxes) to initiate segmentation. This manual intervention limits the autonomy and scalability required for real-world robotic applications that demand continuous, automatic perception.
 - **Costly Per-Frame Video Mask Annotations:** Training robust video segmentation models typically necessitates large-scale video datasets with ground-truth mask annotations for every frame. Obtaining such fine-grained annotations for diverse robot embodiments and dynamic environments is labor-intensive, costly, and impractical at scale.

To address these motivations, the research aims to deliver a "structure-aware, automatic, and training-label-efficient foundation model" for robot segmentation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology of this research involves the development of the RobotSeg model and the construction of the Video Robot Segmentation (VRS) dataset.

#### 3.1. RobotSeg Model

RobotSeg is built upon the SAM 2 foundation model, enhancing it with three key innovations tailored for robot segmentation in image and video:

- **Structure-Enhanced Memory Associator (SEMA):** SEMA is designed to improve feature representations by integrating temporal context and structural information, particularly for articulated robots. It encodes features and masks from previous frames into a memory representation ($M_t$), which serves as a reference for temporal association. The current frame features ($F_t$) are refined through self-attention, cross-attention with $M_t$, and MLP operations. Concurrently, a structure branch extracts an edge map ($E_t$) from the current image using a Canny filter, modulating the current-frame features. These edge-enhanced features ($F^{edge}_t$) are processed by a structure perceiver (multi-scale feature extractor and cross-attention with $M_t$) to capture robot-aware structural cues, generating a structure map ($S_t$). Finally, $S_t$ modulates the temporally enhanced features to produce the structure-enhanced features ($F''_t$).
 - **Memory Encoder:** This module transforms previous-frame masks and image embeddings into a compact memory representation. It processes the previous mask via a downsampling branch and projects previous image features, fusing them with lightweight convolutional operations to integrate spatial and semantic cues.
 - **Structure Perceiver:** This component uses the memory feature to extract robot-aware structural cues. Edge-enhanced features undergo multi-scale depthwise convolutions (3x3, 5x5, 7x7) to capture diverse edge patterns. These aggregated features are aligned with the memory feature through a cross-attention layer, leading to the prediction of the structure map via a 3x3 convolution.

- **Robot Prompt Generator (RPG):** The RPG aims to enable autonomous and temporally consistent robot segmentation without manual prompts. It generates two types of robot tokens:
 - **Class Tokens:** These are retrieved from a learnable token bank based on the specified target category (e.g., "robot arm," "robot gripper," "whole robot"), providing class-level semantic priors.
 - **Object Tokens:** Dynamically extracted from historical memory, these tokens convey temporal cues. A hierarchical clustering strategy (Algorithm 1) is employed, which first groups memory foreground into region-level segments and then further decomposes each region into subclusters. These object tokens capture both coarse structures and fine-grained details. The combined class and object tokens serve as segmentation prompts for the mask decoder.

- **Label-Efficient Training Strategy (LET):** To address the high cost of per-frame mask annotation, LET supervises the model using only the ground-truth mask from the first frame of each video. It comprises three consistency losses:
 - **Cycle Consistency Loss ($L_{cyc}$):** The model propagates masks forward from frame 0 to frame $t$, and then backward from frame $t$ to frame 0. The predicted masks for frame 0 (both forward and backward, $M^f_0$, $M^b_0$) are supervised by the ground-truth mask ($G_0$), encouraging cross-frame mask propagation.
 - **Semantic Consistency Loss ($L_{sem}$):** This loss enforces object-level coherence for intermediate frames. For each intermediate predicted mask ($M_x$), an object semantic embedding ($f_x$) is computed by spatially averaging image features within the mask. This embedding is then aligned with the first-frame object semantics ($f_0$) using cosine similarity, ensuring semantic consistency throughout the video.
 - **Patch Consistency Loss ($L_{patch}$):** This loss enhances spatial alignment by supervising intermediate predicted masks ($M_x$) with pseudo-labels ($P_x$). These pseudo-labels are propagated from the first-frame ground truth using DINOv3 patch similarity. An IoU loss is applied between the downsampled predicted masks and the pseudo-labels.
 - The total mask loss ($L_{mask}$) is a weighted sum of these three hierarchical losses.

- **Mask Decoder:** The mask decoder, an extension of the SAM mask decoder, generates segmentation masks conditioned on image embeddings and prompt tokens. It integrates both optional user guidance (point/bbox prompts) and the automatically generated robot tokens. It employs a sequence of two-way transformer blocks for bidirectional attention between image embeddings and tokens. The decoder predicts multiple candidate masks and an IoU score for each, retaining the one with the highest score. An MLP-based occlusion prediction head is also included.

#### 3.2. Video Robot Segmentation (VRS) Dataset

The VRS dataset is constructed to provide a benchmark for video robot segmentation.
- **Data Collection:** Data is collected from 10 existing robotics datasets (e.g., DROID, Berkeley Fanuc Manipulation, MobileALOHA), focusing on continuous RGB video sequences capturing robot manipulation. Videos are manually reviewed for quality.
- **Annotation:** Professional annotators followed a hierarchical labeling protocol, providing masks for the whole robot, robot arm, and gripper separately.
- **Statistics:** VRS contains 2,812 videos with 138,707 frames, which is 38 times larger than the existing image-level RoboEngine dataset.
- **Splits:** The dataset is split into 105 testing videos (7,203 fully annotated frames) and 2,707 training videos (131,504 frames with only the first frame annotated). This semi-supervised annotation strategy balances scale and labeling cost.
- **Diversity:** VRS encompasses diverse scenes, robot embodiments (e.g., Franka, UR5, WindowX), and lighting conditions, supporting robust training and comprehensive evaluation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents extensive experimental results to evaluate RobotSeg's performance on both image and video robot segmentation tasks, compared against state-of-the-art methods.

#### 4.1. Performance on VRS Dataset (Video Robot Segmentation)

- **Overall Performance (Table 2):** RobotSeg consistently achieved state-of-the-art performance across all evaluation settings (automatic, 1-click, 3-click, bounding-box, online-interactive) and for all segmentation targets (robot arm, gripper, whole robot).
 - In the automatic setting (AU), RobotSeg achieved 85.1 J&F for the whole robot, outperforming RoboEngine (finetuned) by 4.9 J&F and language-conditioned models like EVF-SAM by 21.2 J&F.
 - For robot arm segmentation (AU), RobotSeg scored 75.6 J&F, exceeding EVF-SAM by 30.9 J&F. Similarly, for robot gripper segmentation (AU), RobotSeg achieved 76.0 J&F, surpassing EVF-SAM by 52.2 J&F.
 - RobotSeg also demonstrated strong promptable capabilities, further improving accuracy with user-provided prompts. For instance, in the 3-click setting, it achieved 86.3 J&F for the whole robot, compared to SAM 2.1 (finetuned) at 82.5 J&F.
- **Category-Wise Analysis Across Robot Embodiments (Tables 5, 6, 7):** RobotSeg consistently ranked among the top-performing methods within each of the 10 robot categories, for whole robot, robot arm, and robot gripper segmentation. It ranked first in 7, 8, and 6 out of 10 robot types for whole robot, robot arm, and robot gripper segmentation, respectively. This indicates robust generalization across diverse robot embodiments, whereas other methods showed more significant performance variations across categories. For example, SAM 2.1 (finetuned) for robot arm segmentation dropped from an overall 66.2 J&F to 38.7 J&F on the MobileALOHA robot.

#### 4.2. Performance on RoboEngine Dataset (Image Robot Segmentation)

- **Overall Performance (Table 3):** RobotSeg also achieved strong performance on the existing image-level RoboEngine dataset, demonstrating its capability for both video and image segmentation. In the automatic setting, RobotSeg scored 87.9 J&F for the whole robot, outperforming RoboEngine (finetuned) by 1.3 J&F and EVF-SAM by 18.7 J&F. With prompt-based inputs (e.g., 3-click), RobotSeg reached 93.5 J&F, exceeding SAM 2.1 (finetuned) at 90.2 J&F.

#### 4.3. Ablation Study (Table 4)

A detailed ablation study affirmed the contribution of each proposed component:
- **Robot-Specific Fine-Tuning:** Fine-tuning on the robot dataset significantly improved performance from 38.2 to 73.6 J&F (SAM 2.1 original vs. finetuned), highlighting domain adaptation's importance.
- **Label-Efficient Training (LET):** Each component of LET (cycle, semantic, patch consistency losses) contributed positively, with the full LET strategy achieving 77.4 J&F, validating its effectiveness under first-frame-only supervision.
- **Robot Prompt Generator (RPG):** Introducing class tokens enabled autonomous segmentation (79.8 J&F), and further incorporating object tokens (derived from memory) improved performance to 83.1 J&F, demonstrating the value of semantic and temporal guidance.
- **Structure-Enhanced Memory Associator (SEMA):** The added structure enhancement branch yielded clear gains. Multi-scale perception boosted performance to 83.7 J&F, and memory-guided structure modulation further enhanced it to 85.1 J&F, confirming the importance of structure awareness for articulated robots.

#### 4.4. Qualitative Comparisons (Figures 9, 13-17)

Qualitative results illustrated that RobotSeg produced more accurate, structurally consistent, complete, and temporally stable masks compared to RoboEngine, EVF-SAM, and SAM 2.1. This was observed across diverse embodiments and cluttered scenes, for both automatic and prompt-guided settings, and for whole robot, arm, and gripper segmentation. Baselines often exhibited fragmented masks, confusion with backgrounds, or temporal instability.

#### 4.5. Computational Efficiency (Table 8)

RobotSeg (41.3M parameters, 319.8G FLOPs) offered a favorable balance between performance and efficiency. Its FLOPs were substantially lower than most robot-specific (e.g., RoboEngine: 1753.4G FLOPs) and language-conditioned models (e.g., LISA: 61820.4G FLOPs). While slightly higher than SAM 2.1 (284.3G FLOPs), RobotSeg provided significantly enhanced capabilities (fully automatic segmentation, higher accuracy). It achieved an average inference time of 94.2 ms per frame (>10 FPS), supporting practical deployment.

#### 4.6. Application Example: Robot-Centric Data Augmentation (Figure 10)

The paper demonstrated RobotSeg's practical value by showing that its clean, complete, and temporally stable robot masks enabled high-fidelity robot compositing for data augmentation. Inaccurate masks from other methods led to visually implausible augmented images with broken or distorted robot geometry.

#### 4.7. Comparison with SAM 3 (Tables 5, 6, 7, Section 13)

While SAM 3, a concept segmentation model, could segment robots via noun-phrase prompts, its performance on the VRS dataset was notably lower than RobotSeg, particularly for fine-grained arm/gripper separation and temporal consistency. This indicated that dedicated robot-specific modeling remained crucial for high-quality robot segmentation in dynamic environments.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents RobotSeg, the first foundation model specifically designed for robot segmentation across both images and videos. The development of the Video Robot Segmentation (VRS) dataset, which is 38 times larger than previous image-level datasets and features hierarchical labeling and a label-efficient training strategy, addresses a significant gap in the availability of annotated data for robotic perception research.

The significance of RobotSeg lies in its ability to achieve structure-aware, automatic, and training-label-efficient robot segmentation. By introducing innovations such as the Structure-Enhanced Memory Associator, Robot Prompt Generator, and Label-Efficient Training strategy, RobotSeg overcomes limitations of general foundation models (like SAM 2) and existing robot-specific methods when confronted with the unique challenges of robot embodiment diversity, appearance ambiguity, structural complexity, and rapid shape changes in dynamic environments.

The potential impacts of this work are manifold:
- **Enhanced Robot Perception:** By providing robust and accurate robot segmentation, RobotSeg can significantly improve the foundational visual perception capabilities of robotic systems.
- **Improved Downstream Robotic Applications:**
 - **Visual Servoing and Control:** Precise robot masks can enable more accurate localization of effectors, facilitating fine-grained control for VLA systems.
 - **Scalable Data Augmentation:** High-fidelity robot masks allow for more realistic and structurally consistent robot-centric data augmentation, improving the generalization and robustness of robot policies across diverse scenes and embodiments.
 - **Accurate Real-to-Sim Transfer:** Cleaner robot geometry and appearance derived from precise segmentation can lead to more accurate simulation assets, enhancing the effectiveness of real-to-simulation transfer techniques.
 - **Reliable Safety Monitoring:** Continuous and accurate tracking of robots from third-person views can aid in detecting unsafe motions and preventing collisions in human-robot collaborative environments.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The introduction of the VRS dataset provides a new benchmark for developing and evaluating models for mask propagation, temporal modeling, and structure-aware segmentation in robotics. RobotSeg's architectural innovations also lay a foundation for further advancements in robot perception, potentially inspiring research into more lightweight architectures, multi-modal integration (e.g., depth, motion cues), and deployment on resource-constrained platforms. Its integration into closed-loop robotic systems for tasks like 3D robot reconstruction, policy learning, and navigation represents a promising avenue for future exploration.
