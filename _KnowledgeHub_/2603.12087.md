# Cross-Domain Policy Optimization via Bellman Consistency and Hybrid Critics

> [!summary] Summary
> QAvatar is a cross-domain reinforcement learning framework developed by researchers from National Yang Ming Chiao Tung University and Meta AI that enables reliable policy transfer between domains with distinct state and action spaces. It adaptively weighs contributions from mapped source and learned target Q-functions based on a measure of transferability, achieving 27-56% better data efficiency than learning from scratch across various control tasks.

## Problem

- Transferring knowledge between Reinforcement Learning (RL) domains with distinct state and/or action spaces (e.g., robots with different morphologies).
- The inherent difficulty in determining the transferability of a pre-trained source-domain model, which can lead to negative transfer if source knowledge is unsuitable.
- The high cost and time required for data collection in real-world target domains, necessitating improved sample efficiency in RL.

## Method

- Introduces **QAvatar**, a framework that employs a ==hybrid critic==, combining a target-domain Q-function learned from scratch with a pre-trained source Q-function mapped to the target domain.
- Develops a cross-domain Bellman consistency metric to quantitatively measure source-model transferability and an adaptive weighting function (α(t)) to dynamically adjust the influence of the source Q-function.
- Integrates with Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) using normalizing-flow-based functions for robust inter-domain state and action mapping, ensuring outputs remain in feasible regions.

## Results

- QAvatar consistently achieved superior data efficiency, requiring **27%** to **56%** fewer environment steps to reach target performance thresholds compared to SAC (learning from scratch) across diverse tasks.
- The adaptive weighting (α(t)) successfully prevented negative transfer by disengaging detrimental source Q-functions and gradually reducing reliance on low-quality source models.
- Demonstrated strong performance in complex transfer scenarios, including locomotion with morphological differences, robot arm manipulation between different platforms, and image-based control tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Cross-domain Bellman consistency provides a quantitative and dynamic measure of a source model's transferability, enabling adaptive control over its influence in the target domain.
> - A hybrid critic, adaptively combining a mapped source Q-function with a target-learned Q-function, allows for reliable knowledge transfer while mitigating negative transfer risks.
> - Theoretical analysis supports that QAvatar's average sub-optimality is bounded, guaranteeing performance convergence even when source models are suboptimal or transferability is low.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research contributes to the field of Cross-Domain Reinforcement Learning (CDRL), a sub-area of transfer learning focused on improving the data efficiency of Reinforcement Learning (RL) by leveraging knowledge from a pre-trained model in a source domain to facilitate learning in a related target domain. The premise of CDRL is that data collection and model training are often more feasible or less costly in the source domain, such as in simulated environments.

The existing landscape of CDRL methods can be broadly categorized by whether the source and target domains share identical or distinct state-action spaces. A substantial portion of prior work addresses scenarios where domains share the same state-action spaces but differ in transition dynamics. These methods often involve techniques such as reward augmentation, data filtering, or the use of latent representations to bridge the dynamics mismatch.

For scenarios involving distinct state and action spaces, which present a more complex challenge, existing approaches primarily focus on learning inter-domain mapping functions. These mappings can be either manually designed to project trajectories into a common latent space or learned through techniques like dynamics alignment or cycle consistency, sometimes augmented with additional loss functions like domain cycle consistency, effect cycle consistency, or mutual information maximization. Adversarial learning has also been employed to constrain the output ranges of these mapping functions.

The paper identifies two fundamental and interconnected challenges that prior work has not comprehensively addressed:
1. **Distinct state and/or action spaces**: This necessitates sophisticated inter-domain mappings to account for differing representations, as seen in robot control tasks involving agents with varying morphologies.
2. **Unknown transferability of a source-domain model**: It is often difficult to ascertain *a priori* whether a pre-trained source model will effectively transfer to a target domain, particularly with limited target-domain data. This uncertainty can lead to "negative transfer," where the application of source knowledge actually hinders rather than helps target-domain learning.

This work positions itself as an advancement in CDRL by proposing a framework that jointly tackles these two challenges. It aims to provide a method for reliable knowledge transfer between domains with distinct state-action representations, coupled with theoretical guarantees, regardless of the initial quality or known transferability of the source-domain model. The proposed approach contrasts with previous methods by explicitly incorporating a measure of transferability and adaptively controlling the influence of the source model.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust and reliable CDRL framework, named QAvatar, that can effectively transfer knowledge between a source domain and a target domain even when their state and action spaces are distinct. A central motivation is to overcome the prevalent challenges of (i) representation mismatch between domains and (ii) the inherent uncertainty regarding the transferability of pre-trained source models, which can otherwise lead to negative transfer.

The motivation for addressing these challenges is rooted in practical considerations for improving the sample efficiency of RL. In many real-world applications, such as robotics, collecting sufficient data in the target environment can be costly, time-consuming, or unsafe. Leveraging knowledge from a source domain (e.g., a simulator or a different robot) offers a pathway to accelerate learning in the target domain. However, the identified challenges significantly impede the practical realization of this potential:

1. **Addressing Distinct State and Action Spaces**: Many real-world transfer scenarios involve agents or environments with differing physical characteristics, leading to distinct state and action space representations. For example, transferring a policy learned for a four-legged robot to a five-legged variant, or from one robot arm (e.g., Panda) to another with different kinematics (e.g., UR5e), requires a mechanism to bridge these representational gaps. Without such a mechanism, direct application of source policies or value functions is infeasible. The paper motivates the need for "sophisticated inter-domain mappings" that can learn these correspondences.

2. **Mitigating Negative Transfer Due to Unknown Transferability**: A critical issue in transfer learning is negative transfer, where the source knowledge, instead of assisting, degrades the performance in the target domain. This can occur if the source model is sub-optimal, or if the source and target domains are too dissimilar for the transferred knowledge to be beneficial. Existing methods often assume sufficient similarity or optimal source models, lacking mechanisms to evaluate or adapt to varying transferability. The paper highlights that it is "rather difficult to determine a priori the transferability of a source-domain model," especially with limited target-domain data. This uncertainty necessitates a framework that can reliably leverage source knowledge in favorable conditions while gracefully falling back on target-domain learning when source knowledge is detrimental or low-quality.

To jointly tackle these challenges, the authors propose a dual approach:
- **Cross-Domain Bellman Consistency**: This concept is introduced to quantitatively measure the transferability of a source-domain model by evaluating how well its Q-function, when mapped to the target domain, adheres to the target domain's Bellman equation.
- **Hybrid Critic**: By combining a target-domain Q-function learned from scratch with the mapped source-domain Q-function, QAvatar aims to achieve reliable transfer. This hybrid approach, guided by the measured transferability, allows for adaptive weighting between the two sources of value information.

The specific objectives outlined in the paper's contributions include:
1. Proposing the QAvatar framework to improve sample efficiency in CDRL with distinct state and action spaces, alongside establishing its convergence properties in a prototypical (tabular) setting.
2. Developing a practical deep RL implementation of QAvatar that incorporates a normalizing-flow-based state-action mapping, demonstrating its compatibility with existing techniques for learning domain correspondences.
3. Empirically validating QAvatar's performance against existing CDRL benchmarks across a range of RL tasks, showing superior transferability and robustness.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology of this research addresses the identified challenges in CDRL through the introduction of a novel framework called QAvatar. The approach combines theoretical grounding in cross-domain Bellman consistency with a practical, deep RL implementation using a hybrid critic and normalizing flow-based mapping.

#### 3.1. Cross-Domain Bellman Consistency (CDBC)

The paper first establishes the concept of cross-domain Bellman consistency as a metric for quantifying source-model transferability. It starts by analyzing Direct Q Transfer (DQT), a baseline method where a target-domain policy is updated directly using a pre-trained source Q-function Q_src, mapped to the target domain via inter-domain functions ϕ (for states) and ψ (for actions).

- **Definition of Cross-Domain Bellman Error (ϵ_cd)**: This error quantifies the discrepancy between the mapped source Q-function, `Q_src(ϕ(s), ψ(a))`, and the Bellman expectation for the target policy `π` in the target domain `M_tar`. Specifically, it measures `Q_src(ϕ(s), ψ(a)) - (r_tar(s, a) + γE[Q_src(ϕ(s'), ψ(a'))])`.
- **Definition of δ-Bellman-Consistency**: A source critic `Q_src` is considered δ-Bellman-consistent under a target policy `π` if there exist mappings `(ϕ, ψ)` such that the L1-norm of `ϵ_cd` (weighted by the policy's state-action visitation distribution) is less than or equal to δ.
- **Motivation for QAvatar**: Proposition 2 demonstrates that the average sub-optimality of DQT is bounded by two terms: a term related to NPG learning progress and a term directly proportional to `||ϵ_cd||`. This theoretical analysis highlights that a large cross-domain Bellman error (i.e., poor source-model transferability) significantly limits DQT's performance, underscoring the need for a more robust approach.

#### 3.2. The QAvatar Algorithm (Tabular Prototype)

To address the limitations of DQT, QAvatar introduces a hybrid critic that combines a learned target-domain Q-function (`Q_tar`) with the pre-trained, mapped source Q-function (`Q_src(ϕ, ψ)`).

- **Inter-domain Mapping Learning**: The mappings `ϕ: S_tar → S_src` and `ψ: A_tar → A_src` are learned by minimizing a **Cross-Domain Bellman Loss (L_CD)**. This loss is computed as the squared error between `Q_src(ϕ(s), ψ(a))` and the target Bellman target (`r_tar(s, a) + γE[Q_src(ϕ(s'), ψ(a'))]`), evaluated over target-domain samples. This intuitively aligns `Q_src` with the target-domain dynamics and rewards through the mappings.
- **Target-Domain Q-function Learning**: A standard target-domain Q-function `Q_tar` is learned by minimizing the Temporal Difference (TD) loss (`L_TD`) using target-domain samples, approximating the value function of the current target policy `π_tar`.
- **NPG-like Policy Update with Hybrid Critic**: The target-domain policy `π` is updated using a Natural Policy Gradient (NPG) style update rule. The key innovation is the use of a hybrid action-value function `f(t)`: `f(t)(s, a) := (1 - α(t))Q_tar(t)(s, a) + α(t)Q_src(ϕ(t)(s), ψ(t)(a))`. This function adaptively weights the contributions of the target and source critics.
- **Adaptive Weight Function α(t)**: This function dynamically adjusts the influence of `Q_src` versus `Q_tar`. Theoretically, `α(t)` could be an indicator function, switching to `Q_src` if its error is smaller than `Q_tar`'s TD error. Practically, a smoother, hyperparameter-free variant is proposed: `α(t) = ||ϵ_td(t)|| / (||ϵ_cd(Q_src, ϕ(t), ψ(t))|| + ||ϵ_td(t)||)`. This formulation ensures that `α(t)` is high when the source model transfers well (low `ϵ_cd`) and low when it transfers poorly (high `ϵ_cd`), thus preventing negative transfer.

#### 3.3. Theoretical Justification (Proposition 3)

Proposition 3 provides an upper bound on the average sub-optimality of QAvatar. This bound consists of an NPG learning term (converging at `O(1/√T)`) and a term that is a weighted average of `||ϵ_cd||` and `||ϵ_td||`, where the weights are `α(t)` and `(1-α(t))`, respectively. The design of `α(t)` ensures that:
- If `Q_src` transfers perfectly (e.g., `||ϵ_cd|| = 0`), `α(t)` approaches 1, and the bound effectively reduces to the NPG learning term.
- If `Q_src` transfers poorly (e.g., `||ϵ_cd||` is large), `α(t)` approaches 0, and the bound is dominated by `||ϵ_td||`, similar to learning from scratch, thereby avoiding negative transfer.

#### 3.4. Practical Deep RL Implementation

The theoretical framework is extended to a practical deep RL setting:
- **Integration with SAC**: QAvatar is integrated with Soft Actor-Critic (SAC). The target Q-function (`Q_tar`) is updated using SAC's critic loss, and the target policy (`π`) is updated using SAC's policy loss, but with the hybrid Q-function `f(t)` replacing the standard Q-value in the policy objective.
- **Normalizing Flow-Based Mapping**: To ensure that the outputs of `ϕ` and `ψ` (which map to `S_src` and `A_src` respectively) remain within feasible regions, the mapping functions are augmented with a normalizing flow model. This approach is adopted over adversarial learning methods (common in prior work) to improve training stability. For image-based observations, the flow model operates on learned feature representations from an image encoder, rather than raw pixels.
- **Off-Policy Adaptation**: The theoretical results are extended to off-policy settings by adapting the bounds to account for data collected by a behavior policy, affirming QAvatar's compatibility with off-policy algorithms like SAC.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of QAvatar involved a comparative analysis against several contemporary CDRL benchmarks and standard RL baselines across a variety of continuous control tasks. The experiments assessed QAvatar's ability to improve data efficiency, its robustness to varying source model quality, and its performance in diverse transfer scenarios.

#### 4.1. Experimental Setup

- **Environments**:
 - **Locomotion (MuJoCo)**: HalfCheetah-v3 and Ant-v3 as source domains, with modified morphologies (e.g., three-legged HalfCheetah, five-legged Ant) as target domains, introducing distinct state and action spaces.
 - **Robot Arm Manipulation (Robosuite)**: Panda robot arm as source to UR5e robot arm as target for "Door Opening" and "Table Wiping" tasks, representing significant physical differences.
 - **Goal Navigation (Safety-Gym)**: CarGoal0 (source) to DoggoGoal0 (target), involving different robot types with a shared goal.
 - **Image-based Control (DeepMind Control Suite)**: Walker_walk (source) to Walker_run and Cheetah_run (targets) to test visual transfer.
- **Baselines**:
 - **SAC (Soft Actor-Critic)**: Learning from scratch in the target domain.
 - **FT (Fine-Tuning)**: SAC with source model parameter initialization.
 - **CMD (Cross-Morphology-Domain Policy Adaptation)**: A method based on dynamics cycle consistency.
 - **CAT (Cross-domain Adaptive Transfer)**: PPO-based and SAC-based versions, which adaptively combine policy layers.
 - **PAR (Policy Adaptation by Representation mismatch)**: A method focusing on representation alignment.
- **Metrics**: Learning curves (return vs. environment steps), time to threshold (steps to reach a certain performance level), and Interquartile Mean (IQM) for aggregated performance.

#### 4.2. Key Experimental Outcomes

1. **Improved Data Efficiency**: QAvatar consistently demonstrated superior data efficiency compared to SAC (learning from scratch) across all evaluated tasks (Figure 1). For instance, QAvatar required significantly fewer environment steps to reach target performance thresholds, with improvements ranging from 27% to 56% compared to SAC (Table 1). The aggregated IQM also indicated QAvatar's performance advantage over all baselines (Figure 2).

2. **Comparative Performance with Baselines**:
 - **CAT-SAC and FT**: Showed moderate improvements on MuJoCo tasks but exhibited slower transfer or limited gains in more diverse tasks like Robosuite, suggesting limitations in scenarios with substantial state-action space discrepancies. This was attributed to their reliance on shared feature representations or direct parameter transfer.
 - **CMD**: Generally performed poorly and often exhibited unstable training (e.g., in Ant). The authors attribute this to its unsupervised adversarial mapping approach, which does not leverage target-domain reward signals.
 - **PAR**: Demonstrated varied performance, sometimes surpassing SAC but generally falling behind QAvatar.

3. **Adaptive Transferability (α(t) behavior)**:
 - **Strong Positive/Negative Transfer Scenario**: In a specially designed Ant environment where the target robot moved backward, QAvatar's adaptive weight `α(t)` accurately reflected the transferability. When mappings were learned, enabling positive transfer, `α(t)` remained high, indicating reliance on the source Q-function. Conversely, when forced into a negative transfer scenario (identity mapping), `α(t)` dropped to near zero, effectively disengaging the detrimental source Q-function and preventing performance degradation (Figure 3).
 - **Low-Quality Source Domain**: When tested with a low-quality source model in the HalfCheetah environment, `α(t)` gradually decreased over training. This adaptive behavior allowed QAvatar to reduce reliance on the sub-optimal source model, mitigating negative transfer and enabling the agent to learn effectively from its own target-domain experience (Figure 4).
 - **Unrelated Transfer Scenario**: Even between structurally dissimilar domains (Hopper-v3 to Table Wiping), QAvatar performed reliably, avoiding negative transfer (Figure 9a). This demonstrates the robustness of the `α(t)` mechanism in preventing interference from irrelevant source knowledge.

4. **Robustness in Non-Stationary Environments**: In an Ant environment with stochastic rewards and actions, QAvatar successfully learned inter-domain mappings and achieved positive transfer, indicating its robustness to environmental non-stationarity (Figure 9b).

5. **Image-Based Control Tasks**: QAvatar achieved substantially higher performance than SAC trained from scratch on DeepMind Control Suite tasks (e.g., `walker_run` and `cheetah_run` from `walker_walk` source). This demonstrates QAvatar's applicability and effectiveness in complex visual control settings, even where SAC struggled (Figure 5).

6. **Sensitivity to Hyperparameters**: A sensitivity test on the update frequency of `α(t)` (`N_α`) revealed that QAvatar's performance and the learned `α(t)` trajectories were largely insensitive to `N_α` within a reasonable range (Figures 6, 7), suggesting robustness to this parameter.

7. **Extension to Multiple Source Models**: QAvatar was shown to be extendable to scenarios with multiple source models, leveraging knowledge from two specialized Ant source models (front legs disabled, back legs disabled) to achieve higher transferability in a complete Ant target domain (Figure 9c).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant advancement in the field of Cross-Domain Reinforcement Learning (CDRL) by addressing two fundamental challenges: enabling knowledge transfer across distinct state-action spaces and reliably managing unknown source-model transferability.

The primary significance of this work lies in its theoretical framework and practical algorithm, QAvatar.
- **Theoretical Foundation for Transferability**: The introduction of **cross-domain Bellman consistency** offers a novel, quantifiable measure for the transferability of a source-domain model. This theoretical contribution provides a basis for understanding why and how source models can be effectively utilized in target domains, moving beyond heuristic approaches. The established convergence guarantees for QAvatar, irrespective of source model quality, provide a strong theoretical underpinning for reliable CDRL.
- **Robust and Adaptive Transfer Mechanism**: QAvatar's **hybrid critic** and the **hyperparameter-free adaptive weighting scheme (α(t))** represent a key innovation. This mechanism allows the algorithm to dynamically adjust its reliance on the source model based on its real-time transferability to the target domain. This capability is critical for preventing "negative transfer," a common pitfall in transfer learning where poor or irrelevant source knowledge degrades target-domain performance. The empirical results demonstrate that QAvatar can effectively leverage high-quality source knowledge while gracefully falling back to target-domain learning when the source model is sub-optimal or the domains are significantly dissimilar.
- **Practical Applicability to Diverse Domains**: The successful implementation and evaluation across a variety of complex RL tasks—including locomotion with morphological differences, robot arm manipulation between different robot platforms, goal navigation with different agents, and image-based control—demonstrate QAvatar's broad applicability. The use of a normalizing-flow-based approach for inter-domain mapping addresses the practical challenge of ensuring mapping outputs remain within feasible regions, offering a more stable alternative to adversarial methods.

The potential impact of this research is substantial for advancing the deployment of RL agents in real-world scenarios:
- **Enhanced Sample Efficiency**: By facilitating reliable knowledge transfer, QAvatar can significantly reduce the amount of expensive target-domain data required for training. This is particularly valuable in domains like robotics, where data collection is costly, time-consuming, or even hazardous.
- **Broader Generalization of RL Agents**: The ability to transfer policies across domains with different state and action spaces could enable more flexible and versatile RL systems. For example, a policy learned in a simulated environment or on one type of robot could be more readily adapted to new, physically distinct robots or environments.
- **Accelerated Research and Development**: The framework could accelerate the development and evaluation of new RL agents by providing a robust method for leveraging existing models and data, reducing the need to train every agent from scratch for every new task or platform variant.

The authors acknowledge limitations, notably that QAvatar currently incurs about twice the training time of SAC due to the overhead of inter-domain mappings and the flow model. This suggests that future work on computational acceleration could further enhance its practical utility, especially in scenarios where training efficiency is paramount. Additionally, the general principles of Bellman consistency and hybrid critics could be extended to other learning paradigms, such as preference-based RL and imitation learning, potentially broadening the impact of these core ideas across the machine learning landscape.
