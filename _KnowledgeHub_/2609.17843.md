# RoboVAD: A Large Cross-Domain Evaluation Benchmark for Anomaly Detection in Robotic Arm Manipulation Videos

> [!summary] Summary
> RoboVAD is a real-world benchmark for frame-level video anomaly detection in robotic arm manipulation, with 620,979 frames, 1,570 temporally localized failures, two camera views, five tasks and five anomaly categories. Its protocols hold out tasks, anomaly types, or both. The authors also propose optical-flow FlowJigsaw models; FlowJigsaw-SSL leads cross-anomaly, yet every method stays below 70% micro AUC when tasks and anomalies are both unseen.

## Problem

- Existing robotic failure datasets cover few tasks, few failure categories, or rely on depth, force-torque, or proprioception, and most hold under 150 anomaly instances.
- Several larger benchmarks target failure classification, semantic diagnosis, or episode-level success prediction, not frame-level temporal localization of when a visual failure begins and ends.
- Supervised and weakly supervised detectors are evaluated closed-set, with test failure types seen in training, so no benchmark measures generalization to unseen manipulation tasks or anomaly types.

## Method

- **RoboVAD** dataset: 1,078 teleoperated ==SO-ARM101== episodes via ==LeRobot==, two synchronized 30 FPS cameras, frame-level annotations across five anomaly categories with severity and per-view visibility labels, plus joint and gripper states.
- Cross-domain protocol: trains on three cube tasks with object-drop and failed-grasp anomalies, then tests in-domain, cross-anomaly (placement failure, collision), cross-task (rice pouring, ring insertion), and cross-task+anomaly splits.
- **FlowJigsaw-SSL**: shared 3D CNN over two-view ==Farneback optical flow== with ==arrow-of-time==, ==motion-irregularity==, and ==masked-inpainting== heads, combining normalized scores; **FlowJigsaw-Supervised** swaps heads for one ==focal-loss== binary classifier.

## Results

- In-domain, FlowJigsaw-Supervised reaches **94.6%** micro AUC, **6.1** points above UR-DMU at **88.5%**, but falls to **69.0%** cross-anomaly, where FlowJigsaw-SSL leads with **79.6%** micro and **82.5%** macro.
- In cross-task+anomaly, best micro AUC is FlowJigsaw-SSL's **67.4%**, all methods below **70%**; UR-DMU leads macro at **71.9%**, and CLIP-based VadCLIP, DSANet, LaGoVAD stay **45-60%** micro everywhere.
- Optical-flow ablation: swapping to RGB drops FlowJigsaw-Supervised from **94.6%** to **49.9%** in-domain and **66.7%** to **35.1%** cross-task+anomaly; FlowJigsaw-SSL falls from **79.6%** to **43.1%** cross-anomaly.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Anomaly supervision trades generality for in-domain accuracy: training on known failures wins on familiar categories, but normal-only motion modeling generalizes better to unseen failure categories.
> - Manipulation failures are largely motion events; explicit optical-flow input beats raw RGB by wide margins, and frozen-CLIP detectors sit near chance, so appearance features miss slips and drops.
> - In-domain failure-detection scores overstate deployment readiness; a robot monitor should be validated on held-out tasks and failure types, since task context decides what counts as anomalous.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A robot arm working at a table releases an object. That release is normal when it places the object in a container, but it is abnormal if the object falls short of its target. What counts as failure depends on context, so collecting examples of every possible mistake is impractical. RoboVAD asks whether a detector trained on familiar tasks and known mistakes can still spot a failure when both the task and the failure type are unfamiliar. In that combined test, even the best micro-AUC score is below 70%.

Task familiarity (seen above, unseen below) crossed with anomaly familiarity (seen left, unseen right) gives four cases: in-domain, cross-anomaly, cross-task, and cross-task+anomaly. The diagram defines evaluation categories. It does not show model performance.

### 2. A benchmark for unfamiliar failures

RoboVAD contains **620,979 frames from 5.75 hours of video**, including **34,900 anomalous frames** and **1,570 temporally localized events**, recorded from two camera views. An episode is one task execution. Events are labeled over frames, and an episode may contain multiple anomalies or still reach its task goal.

The benchmark covers five tabletop tasks: single- and multiple-cube pick-and-place, cube stacking, rice pouring, and ring insertion. It also covers five failure categories: failed grasp, collision, object drop, placement failure, and object not dropped.

The three source tasks are the two cube pick-and-place tasks and stacking. Rice pouring and ring insertion are held out as unseen tasks. The benchmark then defines three held-out settings:

- **Cross-anomaly** tests held-out failure categories on source tasks.
- **Cross-task** tests seen failure categories on held-out tasks.
- **Cross-task+anomaly** holds out both.

"Seen" and "unseen" refer to the training partition. Frames with other anomaly categories are excluded from each evaluation.

Frame-level AUC measures how well anomaly scores rank anomalous versus normal frames across score thresholds. Micro AUC pools evaluated frames across episodes. Macro AUC computes AUC per episode and averages the results, which gives each episode equal weight.

### 3. Learning what normal motion looks like

The authors also propose FlowJigsaw models that use optical flow, a representation of how image content moves between frames. FlowJigsaw-SSL feeds optical-flow clips from both camera views into a shared 3D convolutional network. The network is trained with three heads:

- One head distinguishes forward from reversed clips.
- One distinguishes regular motion from artificially disturbed motion.
- One reconstructs masked patches from surrounding context.

At test time, the model turns the two classification confidences and the reconstruction error into normalized anomaly scores and combines them. It then averages scores from overlapping clips to assign a score to each frame. FlowJigsaw-Supervised uses the same backbone with a normal/anomalous clip classifier, trained on normal episodes and the failure categories available in training.

A controlled comparison tests the motion input. On cross-anomaly evaluation, which uses unseen failure categories on the source tasks, FlowJigsaw-SSL reaches **79.6% micro AUC** with optical flow and **43.1%** with RGB. Optical flow improves both FlowJigsaw variants in all four settings. This supports the choice of input representation. It does not show that optical flow alone explains all performance differences.

Micro frame-level AUC (%) for FlowJigsaw-Supervised and FlowJigsaw-SSL with RGB and optical-flow inputs, across in-domain, cross-anomaly, cross-task, and cross-task+anomaly. Flow is higher in every setting for both model variants.

### 4. The hardest setting

When both tasks and anomalies are unseen, FlowJigsaw-SSL has the highest micro frame-level AUC, 67.4%, while UR-DMU leads macro AUC at 71.9%. The two metrics weight data differently, so their leaders need not match. All evaluated methods remain below 70% on micro AUC in this setting. These results are frame-level rankings under the benchmark protocol. They are neither demonstrated online recovery nor deployment success.

Micro and macro frame-level AUC (%) by method across four evaluation settings; higher is better. In the combined setting, every listed micro score is below 70%, with FlowJigsaw-SSL at 67.4. The best macro result is UR-DMU at 71.9.

### 5. Keep the axes apart

RoboVAD's contribution is primarily a benchmark that makes this generalization problem measurable. Holding out task types and failure categories separately, then together, exposes a substantial remaining gap. The main boundary is that the current dataset is limited to one robotic platform and a tabletop environment.
