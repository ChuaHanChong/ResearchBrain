# RuN: Residual Policy for Natural Humanoid Locomotion

> [!summary] Summary
> The RuN framework introduces a decoupled residual learning approach for natural humanoid locomotion, enabling a Unitree G1 robot to achieve stable, human-like gaits and smooth walk-run transitions across a continuous velocity range of 0-2.5 m/s in both simulation and physical deployment. This method combines an autoregressive Conditional Motion Generator (CMG) with a reinforcement learning-based residual policy for dynamic corrections.

## Problem

- Current DRL methods often struggle to produce anthropomorphically natural humanoid locomotion, with generated motions appearing stiff or mechanical, due to the complexity of reward function design.
- Existing DRL approaches integrating motion priors typically use a direct-tracking strategy, forcing a single policy to simultaneously learn motion imitation, dynamic stability, and task execution, leading to complex learning problems, large action spaces, and long training times.
- Achieving versatile and dynamic gaits (e.g., seamless walk-run transitions across a broad speed range) while maintaining both naturalness and stability remains a significant challenge for humanoid robots.

## Method

- A decoupled residual learning framework (**RuN**) is introduced, separating kinematic style generation from dynamic corrections to simplify the control task.
- An autoregressive ==Conditional Motion Generator (CMG)== is trained offline on human motion data to produce natural kinematic reference trajectories conditioned on user commands (e.g., target velocities).
- A lightweight reinforcement learning policy learns online to output small residual actions that provide dynamic corrections to the CMG's output, ensuring stability and precise task completion.

## Results

- The RuN framework achieved superior motion naturalness (FID of **0.8753**) and imitation accuracy compared to state-of-the-art baselines like AMP and GMP, while maintaining competitive velocity tracking.
- Training efficiency improved significantly, with RuN converging faster and achieving higher final rewards than baseline methods due to its residual learning approach.
- The simulation-trained policy successfully deployed on a physical Unitree G1 robot, demonstrating stable, natural gaits and smooth walk-run transitions across a continuous velocity range of 0-**2.5** m/s.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decoupling the control problem into kinematic style generation and dynamic correction significantly reduces learning complexity, leading to faster training convergence and improved overall performance.
> - An autoregressive Conditional Motion Generator (CMG) with a Mixture-of-Experts architecture effectively generates high-quality, diverse, and natural kinematic priors, surpassing direct-tracking generative models in naturalness and accuracy.
> - Leveraging an asymmetric actor-critic architecture with privileged information for the critic during training enhances the robustness of the residual policy for real-world deployment while maintaining limited sensory input for the actor.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of advanced locomotion capabilities for humanoid robots, particularly achieving natural and dynamic movement across a wide range of speeds and gait transitions, remains a significant challenge in robotics. Historically, classical control methods such as Zero-Moment Point (ZMP) control and Model Predictive Control (MPC) have been employed. While these methods can ensure stability, they often result in rigid gaits and face limitations in agility and robustness due to simplified dynamics and real-time computation constraints.

Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) has emerged as a promising paradigm for teaching complex motor skills to robots without relying on explicit dynamic models. DRL-based approaches have demonstrated capabilities in various dynamic tasks, including robust bipedal locomotion, stair traversal, and complex maneuvers like parkour. However, a common limitation of many DRL methods is that the generated motions often prioritize task accomplishment and dynamic performance over anthropomorphic naturalness, resulting in movements that appear stiff or mechanical. This issue often stems from reward function design, where even minor misspecifications can lead to non-humanlike behaviors.

To address the lack of naturalness, researchers have integrated data-driven motion priors into DRL frameworks. Imitation learning, where policies are trained to mimic human reference motions, is one such approach. Advanced methods like Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP) use discriminators to guide policies towards human-like styles, while Generative Motion Priors (GMP) utilize pre-trained generative models to synthesize natural reference trajectories. These generative priors aim to provide dense, fine-grained supervision to the policy, enhancing naturalness and stability.

Despite these advancements, existing DRL methods that incorporate motion priors typically adopt a direct-tracking strategy. In this approach, a single policy is tasked with simultaneously learning to imitate the kinematic style of a reference motion, maintain dynamic stability in a physical environment, and execute specific task commands (e.g., velocity tracking). This tight coupling of objectives creates a complex learning problem, leading to large action spaces, extended training times, and inherent trade-offs among motion quality, stability, and task accuracy. The presented work, RuN, aims to address these limitations by decoupling the control task to simplify learning and improve overall performance in natural humanoid locomotion.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this work stems from the inherent complexities and limitations observed in current DRL-based methods for humanoid locomotion, particularly when attempting to achieve natural, dynamic, and versatile gaits across a continuous spectrum of speeds.

The specific challenges and motivations addressed are:

1. **Complexity of Unified Locomotion Control:** Developing a single controller that can reliably manage a wide range of locomotion behaviors, from slow walking to fast running, including seamless transitions between them, is a significant hurdle. Existing methods often struggle to generalize across such a broad velocity spectrum while maintaining both stability and naturalness.
2. **Overburdening of Single Policies in DRL:** Current DRL approaches that incorporate motion priors typically require the control policy to perform multiple, often competing, objectives simultaneously. These objectives include:
 - **Motion Imitation:** Adhering to the kinematic style and trajectory of a human-like motion prior.
 - **Dynamic Stability:** Maintaining balance and robustness against physical perturbations in a complex environment.
 - **Task Execution:** Fulfilling high-level commands, such as tracking desired linear and angular velocities.
 This tight coupling of objectives increases the policy's learning complexity, requiring it to navigate a large and intricate action space, which consequently prolongs training times and can lead to sub-optimal trade-offs between different performance aspects.
3. **Compromised Naturalness and Stability:** While data-driven motion priors are introduced to enhance motion naturalness, the simultaneous demands of stability and task completion can dilute the imitation objective. This can result in motions that are dynamic and task-efficient but still lack the fluid, anthropomorphic qualities desired for human-centric environments. Conversely, a strong focus on imitation might compromise stability or task accuracy.
4. **Inefficiency in Training:** The high dimensionality and multi-objective nature of the control problem in direct-tracking DRL methods contribute to slower convergence rates and higher computational resource requirements during training.

To address these motivations, the key objectives of the RuN framework are:

1. **Decouple the Control Task:** Introduce a novel residual learning framework to decompose the complex end-to-end control problem into more manageable sub-modules, thereby simplifying the learning process for each component.
2. **Generate Natural Kinematic Priors:** Develop a Conditional Motion Generator (CMG) that can autoregressively produce high-quality, kinematically natural reference trajectories conditioned on user commands (e.g., target velocities), serving as an effective motion prior.
3. **Learn Dynamic Corrections:** Train a lightweight reinforcement learning policy to learn small, dynamic residual corrections to the motion prior, specifically focusing on handling physical interactions, maintaining stability, and achieving precise task completion.
4. **Achieve Versatile and Natural Locomotion:** Enable humanoid robots to exhibit stable, natural gaits and smooth walk-run transitions across a broad, continuous velocity range (0–2.5 m/s) in both simulation and real-world environments.
5. **Improve Training Efficiency and Performance:** Demonstrate superior training efficiency and final performance in metrics like motion naturalness, imitation accuracy, and velocity tracking compared to existing state-of-the-art methods.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The RuN framework introduces a decoupled residual learning approach to address natural and dynamic humanoid locomotion. It consists of two primary modules: an autoregressive Conditional Motion Generator (CMG) and a residual policy trained with reinforcement learning.

**Overall Framework (RuN)**
The core idea is to first train the CMG offline using a human motion dataset to capture natural kinematic patterns. Subsequently, a lightweight residual policy is trained online within a simulation environment. The residual policy learns to provide dynamic corrections to the CMG's output, with the final command for the robot being the summation of the CMG's reference motion and the policy's residual action. This design aims to preserve motion naturalness through the prior while allowing the residual policy to focus on physical dynamics and task completion.

**A. Motion Retargeting**
To acquire high-quality training data for the CMG, the AMASS human motion dataset, parameterized by the Skinned Multi-Person Linear (SMPL) model, is utilized. Due to kinematic differences between humans and robots, raw motion data cannot be directly applied. An optimization-based motion retargeting approach, similar to HOVER, is used to adjust SMPL parameters to fit the robot's kinematic constraints.
Following retargeting, the dataset is further curated:
- **Data Filtering:** Heuristics are applied to select physically plausible and kinematically feasible walking and running sequences for the robot.
- **Data Augmentation:** Horizontal mirroring is performed on selected sequences to increase diversity and mitigate biases.
This process yields a curated dataset of 400 high-quality motion sequences, primarily concentrated in the 0.5 m/s to 1.5 m/s velocity range, which informs subsequent training.

**B. Conditional Motion Generator (CMG)**
The CMG acts as an autoregressive motion prior, predicting the next-frame motion feature ($\hat{m}_{t+1}$) based on a history of past motion frames ($m_{t-K+1:t}$) and a conditional command ($c_t$), typically a target linear velocity. The motion feature $m_t$ concatenates joint positions and velocities. The model is represented as $f_\theta (m_{t-K+1:t}, c_t)$, minimizing the Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss between predicted and ground-truth motion.
- **Architecture:** Inspired by the decoder in Motion VAE (MVAE), the CMG uses a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architecture with a gating network and a 3-layer MLP backbone. Crucially, it discards the stochastic latent variable and encoder structure of VAEs, reducing parameter count and avoiding VAE-specific training instabilities.
- **Training Techniques:**
 - **Scheduled Sampling:** To mitigate distribution mismatch between training and inference, the training regime gradually shifts from using ground-truth data (teacher mode) to the model's own predictions (student mode).
 - **Weighted Data Sampling:** To address data sparsity in less common scenarios (e.g., high-speed locomotion), a velocity distribution histogram is used to up-sample infrequent examples.
 - **Preprocessing:** Z-score normalization for motion features, min-max normalization for commands, and slight Gaussian noise injection for robustness.
- **History Length:** The history length $K$ is set to 1, meaning the input is $(m_t, c_t)$. This is based on the observation that the motion feature vector itself (joint positions and velocities) contains sufficient dynamical information for effective first-order autoregressive prediction.
Once trained, the CMG generates diverse, natural gaits in real-time based on velocity commands.

**C. Residual Policy for Locomotion**
The CMG provides kinematically plausible motions but lacks dynamic feedback. A residual policy, trained with reinforcement learning (Proximal Policy Optimization, PPO), provides real-time dynamic corrections for stability and task completion.
- **Formalization:** The task is modeled as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP). The policy $\pi_\theta(a_{res,t}|o_t)$ aims to maximize expected future discounted reward.
- **Decoupled Control via Residual Actions:** The policy outputs a residual action $a_{res,t}$. The final position target for the robot's low-level PD controller, $q_{target,t}$, is the sum of the CMG's reference pose $q_{ref,t}$ and the residual action: $q_{target,t} = q_{ref,t} + a_{res,t}$. This decouples kinematic style from dynamics and narrows the policy's exploration space.
- **Asymmetric State and Observation Spaces:** An asymmetric actor-critic architecture is employed:
 - **Actor (Policy) Observation ($o_t$):** Includes proprioceptive and task-related information: base angular velocity, gravity vector, task command, joint positions, joint velocities, and the previous action.
 - **Critic (Value Function) State ($s_t$):** Includes the actor's observations plus privileged information about the true system state and CMG's intention: base linear velocity and motion features ($q_{ref,t}$, $\dot{q}_{ref,t}$) from the CMG. This forces the policy to learn robust control using only onboard sensory feedback.
- **Reward Design:** The reward function $R(s_t, a_t)$ is a weighted sum of three components to balance motion naturalness, task fulfillment, and physical stability:
 - **Motion Imitation Reward ($r_{imitation,t}$):** Encourages tracking the CMG's reference motion for both joint positions ($r_{qpos,t}$) and velocities ($r_{qvel,t}$), formulated with exponential kernels.
 - **Task Reward ($r_{task,t}$):** Drives the robot to track target linear ($r_{lin,t}$) and angular velocities ($r_{ang,t}$), also using exponential kernels.
 - **Regularization Penalties ($r_{reg,t}$):** A set of penalties designed to promote stable, smooth, and physically plausible motions while discouraging undesirable behaviors (e.g., high torques, foot slippage, joint limit violations). Examples include penalties for termination, orientation deviation, angular velocity, energy consumption, action rate, action smoothness, joint acceleration, joint limits, joint deviation, feet slide, and feet force.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The effectiveness of RuN was evaluated through extensive experiments on the Unitree G1 humanoid robot in both simulation and real-world environments.

**A. Experimental Setup**
All policies were trained in the NVIDIA Isaac Lab simulation at 50 Hz control frequency with a 200 Hz physics step, utilizing the PPO algorithm. The policy was trained to follow commands for longitudinal velocity ($v_x \in [0, 2.5]$ m/s), lateral velocity ($v_y \in [-0.3, 0.3]$ m/s), and yaw rate ($\omega_z \in [-0.5, 0.5]$ rad/s). Training converged in approximately 8 hours on a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU. Extensive domain randomization was applied to bridge the sim-to-real gap, varying parameters such as static/dynamic friction, robot mass, PD controller gains, motor friction, and control latency.
Performance was measured using:
- **Fréchet Inception Distance (FID):** A lower FID indicates higher motion naturalness compared to human reference data.
- **Reconstruction Loss (Lrec):** Per-joint mean-squared error between generated and ground-truth motion for matched commands; lower is better.
- **Imitation Errors (Eqpos, Eqvel):** Per-joint mean errors in joint positions and velocities compared to the motion prior; lower is better.
- **Velocity Tracking Error (Evel):** Mean absolute error between the robot’s linear velocity and commanded velocity; lower is better.

**B. Comparative Analysis (Q1: Effectiveness & Efficiency)**
RuN was compared against three baseline methods: Humanoid-Gym (DRL from scratch), AMP (adversarial motion prior), and GMP (generative motion prior).

- **Motion Generator Comparison:** The standalone CMG was compared to GMP's generative model. The CMG achieved a lower FID (0.5283 vs. 0.6637) and Lrec (0.1556 vs. 0.2514), indicating that RuN's CMG generated more natural and accurate kinematic motions.
- **Policy Comparison:**
 - RuN demonstrated superior performance in motion naturalness (FID: 0.8753) and imitation accuracy (Eqpos: 3.8321, Eqvel: 36.7820) compared to all baselines (Humanoid-Gym FID: 3.8654; AMP FID: 2.8204; GMP FID: 1.1874).
 - While Humanoid-Gym achieved a slightly lower velocity tracking error (0.2732 vs. RuN's 0.2873), this came at the cost of significantly less natural motion, as indicated by its high FID and imitation errors. RuN balanced task performance with naturalness.
- **Training Efficiency:** Learning curves showed that RuN converged significantly faster and achieved a higher final reward compared to Humanoid-Gym, AMP, and GMP. This efficiency was attributed to the residual learning mechanism, which constrains the policy's exploration space.

**C. Ablation Studies (Q2: Component Analysis)**
Ablation studies were conducted to assess the contribution of RuN's key components:
- **RuN w/o CMG:** Removing the Conditional Motion Generator resulted in a significant increase in FID (2.6571) and imitation errors, indicating a substantial loss of motion naturalness. The velocity tracking error was marginally lower (0.2759), suggesting the policy over-optimized for the task without a natural motion prior.
- **RuN w/o Residual:** Replacing the residual learning with a direct-tracking policy (similar to GMP) led to worse performance in FID (1.0845), Eqpos (4.7356), and Eqvel (43.2875) compared to the full RuN, confirming the benefits of the residual formulation.
- **RuN w/o AAC:** Removing the Asymmetric Actor-Critic mechanism resulted in higher FID (1.2347) and imitation errors, demonstrating the importance of privileged information for the critic in improving policy robustness.
- **Reward Ablations:**
 - **RuN w/o rqpos:** Removing the joint position reward led to a catastrophic failure in motion quality (FID: 4.0586, highest among all variants) and high imitation errors, highlighting its critical role in maintaining human-like posture.
 - **RuN w/o rqvel:** Removing the joint velocity reward also increased FID (1.0274) and imitation errors, though less severely than removing rqpos.
 - **RuN w/o rtask:** Removing the task reward significantly impaired velocity tracking (Evel: 1.6871) while slightly improving FID, showing the trade-off between task fulfillment and pure imitation.
These results collectively affirmed the necessity and synergistic roles of each component, particularly the CMG, residual learning, and asymmetric actor-critic, in achieving both naturalness and task execution.

**D. Real-World Deployment (Q3: Generalization)**
The policy trained in simulation was directly deployed on a physical Unitree G1 robot. The deployment was successful, with the robot demonstrating stable and natural locomotion across a continuous velocity range from standstill up to 2.5 m/s. It performed smooth walk-run transitions without noticeable pauses or jerky motions, and its posture, cadence, and coordination closely resembled human movements. This real-world validation confirms the framework's effectiveness and its capability for robust sim-to-real transfer.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The RuN framework represents a notable advancement in the field of humanoid robot locomotion control, primarily through its novel decoupled residual learning approach. By separating the generation of kinematic style from the learning of dynamic corrections, the work addresses a fundamental challenge in current reinforcement learning methodologies for complex robotic tasks.

The significance of this research lies in several key aspects:
- **Enhanced Naturalness and Dynamics:** RuN enables humanoid robots to achieve highly natural, fluid, and dynamic gaits, including smooth transitions between walking and running, across a broad speed range (0–2.5 m/s). This is a crucial step towards making humanoids more effective and acceptable in human-centric environments, where rigid or unnatural movements can hinder interaction and utility.
- **Improved Training Efficiency:** The decoupled architecture and residual learning mechanism simplify the policy's learning task, significantly reducing training time and computational resources compared to end-to-end approaches. This efficiency is vital for accelerating research and development in complex robotic skills.
- **Robustness and Stability:** By focusing the residual policy on dynamic interactions and stability, the framework produces controllers that are robust to physical perturbations, a critical requirement for real-world deployment. The asymmetric actor-critic design further contributes to this robustness by leveraging privileged information during training.
- **Successful Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The demonstrated ability to directly deploy a simulation-trained policy onto a physical robot without further real-world fine-tuning highlights the effectiveness of the training methodology, including extensive domain randomization. This reduces the substantial effort and safety concerns associated with real-world robot experimentation.
- **Principled Decoupling:** The framework offers a principled way to combine data-driven kinematic priors with learned dynamic control, providing a blueprint for tackling other complex, multi-objective robot control problems where both aesthetic quality and physical performance are crucial.

The potential impact of RuN is substantial, contributing to the broader goal of developing versatile and agile humanoid robots. This work could facilitate the deployment of humanoids in various applications, from assistance in homes and workplaces to exploration in unstructured environments, by making their movements more intuitive, safer, and adaptable. Future work could extend this approach to more challenging scenarios such as uneven terrain or incorporate tighter perception-control coupling, further enhancing the capabilities of humanoid robots.
