# MVP-Tac: A Miniaturized Dual-Modal Vision and Photoelastic Tactile Sensor for Robot-Assisted Minimally Invasive Surgery

> [!summary] Summary
> A miniaturized dual-modal vision and photoelastic tactile sensor, MVP-Tac, integrates co-located vision and tactile sensing for robot-assisted minimally invasive surgery. This sensor achieved a Mean Absolute Error of 0.0511 N in force estimation and accurately classified the hardness of exposed (97.37%) and subdermal (92.11%) artificial tumors, showcasing a vision-guided "search-then-palpate" workflow in a simulated colonoscopy.

## Problem

- Robot-assisted minimally invasive surgery (RMIS) and flexible endoscopy lack direct tactile feedback for palpation, a critical capability for tumor detection in open surgery.
- Current intraoperative tumor detection methods in RMIS (e.g., visual inspection, ultrasound) are often insufficient, lacking spatial resolution, intuitiveness, or the ability to detect subsurface lesions reliably.
- Existing vision-based tactile sensors struggle with miniaturization for endoscopic applications, often obstruct camera vision during tactile sensing, or require bulky setups.

## Method

- Developed **MVP-Tac**, a miniaturized ==dual-modal sensor== that integrates co-located vision and tactile sensing within a single compact package.
- Utilized a ==reflective photoelastic imaging== system for tactile sensing, which optically encodes internal stress fields, to achieve compactness without needing structured lighting.
- Designed a ==dual-mode elastomer module== with a semi-transparent film and controllable illumination, allowing seamless switching between visual navigation and tactile palpation while preserving continuous visual feedback.

## Results

- Achieved accurate force estimation with a Mean Absolute Error (MAE) of **0.0511 N** (RMSE **0.0685 N**, R² **0.9874**) within a 0-2 N force range.
- Demonstrated high accuracy in classifying the hardness of artificial tumor phantoms: **97.37%** for exposed tumors and **92.11%** for subdermal tumors embedded 2 mm deep.
- Successfully performed a vision-guided "search-then-palpate" workflow in a simulated colonoscopy, including 3D luminal photomapping and **100%** accurate in-situ hardness classification for tested nodules.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Reflective photoelastic imaging, combined with a circular polariscope and semi-transparent film, enables compact, sensitive tactile sensing by effectively doubling optical retardation.
> - The integration of a semi-transparent membrane allows for a unique dual-mode operation, providing co-located vision and tactile feedback without obstructing the camera's view during palpation.
> - A dual-branch neural network, fusing raw image features with a photoelastic gradient metric (G²), robustly enhances force estimation and hardness classification from complex optical patterns.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robot-assisted minimally invasive surgery (RMIS) and flexible endoscopy offer advantages over traditional open surgical techniques, but they typically lack tactile feedback. In open surgery, surgeons frequently rely on manual palpation to detect masses and assess tissue stiffness, which is a critical capability for localizing tumors, especially those that are small or not visually apparent. The absence of this haptic feedback in RMIS can lead to challenges such as missed lesions, incomplete resections, or excessive tissue manipulation.

Current intraoperative tumor detection methods in RMIS include visual inspection, correlation with preoperative imaging, intraoperative ultrasound, and optical enhancement techniques. However, these methods have limitations. Visual-only endoscopy may not reliably identify small, stiff inclusions beneath compliant tissue if the surface morphology appears normal. Endoscopic ultrasound requires additional instrumentation, specialized training, and interpretation, and may not provide the intuitive mechanical feedback of direct palpation or high spatial resolution in all contexts.

The field of vision-based tactile sensors has developed various approaches, predominantly categorized into the GelSight and Marker families. GelSight sensors use photometric stereo to reconstruct 3D contact geometry, enabling applications like texture recognition and dexterous manipulation. However, these sensors often require precisely controlled internal illumination, complex fabrication, and can be difficult to miniaturize for applications in constrained environments like RMIS. Marker-family sensors estimate depth using binocular vision and optical flow from an array of markers, but dense marker distributions can interfere with feature extraction and tracking. Other miniaturized sensors for RMIS have been developed, some using darkness mapping or mechanoresponsive materials, but these typically employ opaque contact layers that obstruct camera vision, making them unsuitable for scenarios where continuous visual navigation is paramount. Photoelasticity offers an alternative by optically encoding stress fields, but traditional photoelastic systems often involve bulky polariscope setups or provide only global, rather than spatially resolved, force information.

This work addresses the identified gap by developing a miniaturized tactile sensor that integrates co-located vision and tactile sensing capabilities within a single, compact package suitable for endoscopic deployment. It aims to overcome the limitations of previous vision-based tactile sensors by employing reflective photoelastic imaging, which does not require structured lighting and supports reflective coatings for dual-mode operation, while maintaining visual feedback essential for RMIS.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the clinical need to restore tactile feedback for palpation in robot-assisted minimally invasive surgery (RMIS) and flexible endoscopy, without compromising the essential visual feedback required for navigation and safety. Surgeons in open procedures routinely use manual palpation to detect mechanical differences in tissues, which is crucial for identifying tumors, particularly those that are small, deeply embedded, or otherwise difficult to distinguish visually. This capability is largely absent in current RMIS platforms.

The key objectives of this work were to:

1. **Develop a miniaturized dual-modal sensor:** Design and fabricate a compact, vision-based tactile sensor capable of providing both visual and tactile information within a single unit suitable for the size constraints of RMIS instruments. This involved creating a design that supports co-located sensing.
2. **Utilize reflective photoelastic imaging:** Implement a sensing mechanism based on reflective photoelastic imaging. This approach was chosen to optically encode internal stress fields through stress-induced birefringence, thereby providing tactile signals. A specific objective was to make this method compact by using a reflective polariscope setup, which eliminates the need for structured lighting and contributes to miniaturization.
3. **Enable dual-mode operation:** Integrate a mechanism that allows the sensor to seamlessly switch between a visual mode (for navigation and observation) and a tactile mode (for palpation). This was achieved through a semi-transparent membrane and controllable internal illumination, ensuring that tactile sensing does not obstruct the camera's visual field when vision is needed.
4. **Validate force estimation capabilities:** Calibrate the sensor to accurately measure applied normal forces within a clinically relevant range (0 to 2 N) and assess its performance using a machine learning model that fuses image features with a photoelastic gradient metric.
5. **Demonstrate tumor hardness classification:** Evaluate the sensor's ability to differentiate between tissues of varying stiffness, particularly in the context of tumor detection. This included testing its performance on both exposed and subdermal artificial tumor phantoms of different hardness levels and sizes.
6. **Validate in a simulated clinical environment:** Conduct experiments in a simulated colonoscopy setting to demonstrate the sensor's practical utility. This involved validating its capabilities for vision-guided navigation, 3D luminal photomapping, localization of abnormalities, and in-situ palpation-based hardness classification within a constrained lumen.

The overarching goal was to bridge the gap between high-resolution vision-based tactile sensing and clinically viable tumor palpation, thereby providing a pathway toward restoring intuitive mechanical perception in minimally invasive surgical oncology procedures.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The development of MVP-Tac involved the integration of reflective photoelastic imaging with a dual-modal optical design, miniaturization, and machine learning for data interpretation.

**Sensing Principle:**
MVP-Tac leverages photoelasticity, where a transparent, compliant material exhibits stress-dependent birefringence when mechanically loaded. Polarized light passing through the stressed material undergoes a relative phase delay (retardation), which can be imaged as a tactile signal. The sensor employs a reflective circular polariscope configuration to achieve compactness and increased sensitivity. Light from an internal source passes through a linear polarizer and a quarter-wave plate, then through the photoelastic material, reflects off a semi-transparent film, and finally traverses the stack again before being captured by the camera. This reflective path doubles the effective optical retardation, enhancing the tactile signal. The resulting interference pattern recorded by the camera serves as the tactile measurement.

**Sensor Design and Fabrication:**
The sensor integrates three main modules:
1. **Dual-mode Elastomer Module:**
 - **Photoelastic Elastomer Layer:** Fabricated using VytaFlex™ 20, an amber polyurethane rubber, mixed and degassed, then cast into a 3D-printed mold (5 mm diameter, 3 mm thickness) and cured for 16 hours.
 - **Semi-transparent Layer:** A mirror-silver coating (Rust-Oleum spray) is applied in 2-3 coats to the cured VytaFlex layer, creating a half-silvered membrane. This membrane allows dual-mode operation: with internal LEDs off, external light passes through for visual mode; with LEDs on, the internal light reflects off the membrane for tactile mode. A protective VytaFlex overlayer (7 mm diameter, 3.5 mm thickness) is cast over the coating to maintain integrity and tactile fidelity.
2. **Polariscope Optical Module:**
 - A left-handed circular polarizing film (Edmund Optics CP42HE), which combines a linear polarizer and quarter-wave retarder, is mounted above the camera and LED array. The linear polarizer side faces the camera.
 - A 10 mm-thick acrylic spacer is included to establish the camera's working distance, maintain focus, and provide mechanical support for the elastomer.
3. **Perception Module:**
 - An off-the-shelf miniaturized endoscopic CMOS camera module (1280 × 720 pixels, 76° Field of View, 10–100 mm Depth of Field) is used for imaging.
 - Integrated LEDs provide illumination for the tactile mode.
 - The entire assembly is designed to be compact, with components being either off-the-shelf or 3D-printed. Bonding the cured VytaFlex to the acrylic spacer was achieved using Ure-Bond™ II adhesive due to material incompatibility.

**Sensor Calibration:**
- **Force Measurement Setup:** The MVP-Tac sensor was mounted on a base plate, and a Mark-10 force gauge with a linear stage was used to apply controlled normal forces via indenters of various shapes and sizes.
- **Data Collection:** Approximately 2500 pairs of (image, force) data were collected synchronously within a 0 to 2 N force range.
- **Force Estimation Model:** A dual-branch neural network was developed.
 - One branch processes the raw grayscale tactile images through a convolutional feature extractor to capture spatial deformation characteristics.
 - The second branch processes the photoelastic gradient metric (G²), which is the average of the squared intensity-gradient magnitude from the green channel of the image, representing global stress distribution.
 - The feature embeddings from both branches are concatenated and fed into fully connected layers to predict the applied force.

**Sensor Characterization:**
- **Vision Spatial Resolution:** Evaluated using a USAF 1951 resolution target, yielding a spatial resolution of approximately 198 μm on the tactile surface.
- **Repeatability and Hysteresis:** Five repeated loading-unloading trials were performed up to 2 N.
 - **Repeatability (r)** was calculated as the maximum measurement spread at the same force step across trials, expressed as a percentage of the maximum applied force (F_M).
 - **Hysteresis (h)** was quantified as the maximum discrepancy between the smoothed trial-averaged loading and unloading curves at matched force steps, also expressed as a percentage of F_M.

**Experiments for Artificial Tumor Detection:**
- **Phantom Preparation:** Silicone inclusions of Shore hardness 20, 30, 40, 50, and 60A (representing varying tissue stiffness) were cast using 3D-printed molds with diameters of 4, 8, and 12 mm.
 - **Exposed Tumors:** Nodules were placed on rectangular bases of the same material.
 - **Subdermal Tumors:** Hemispherical nodules were embedded 2 mm beneath an Ecoflex 00-30 silicone layer.
- **Palpation Procedure:** Samples were mounted on a CNC bedplate, and MVP-Tac, attached to a Mark-10 force gauge, performed repeated normal palpations (under 3 N). Force and image sequences were recorded synchronously.
- **Hardness Classification Model:** A video-based classifier was trained to predict hardness from 16-frame palpation sequences. The model employed a ResNet-18 (ImageNet-pretrained) for per-frame feature extraction, a GRU for temporal dynamics, and a linear head for classification, trained using cross-entropy loss.

**Simulated Colonoscopy:**
- **Setup:** MVP-Tac was mounted on a UR5e robot end-effector and inserted into an intestine-mimicking phantom (compliant cylindrical lumen, ~60 mm inner diameter) containing artificial tumor nodules.
- **Procedure:**
 - **Vision Mode:** The robot performed circular scans (52 mm diameter, 10 mm layer spacing) to survey the luminal wall. Robot states and sensor images were recorded.
 - **3D Mapping:** Acquired images were downsampled and fused into a 3D model of the lumen to localize candidate nodules.
 - **Tactile Mode:** The sensor was then switched to tactile mode to palpate identified nodules (50A and 60A) within the constrained lumen to confirm hardness.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of MVP-Tac yielded several key findings regarding its performance in force estimation, tactile characterization, tumor hardness classification, and simulated surgical application.

**Force Estimation Accuracy:**
The dual-branch neural network model, fusing raw image data with the G² photoelastic metric, demonstrated accurate force prediction within the 0-2 N range.
- Mean Absolute Error (MAE): 0.0511 N
- Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE): 0.0685 N
- Coefficient of Determination (R²): 0.9874
These results indicate that the average force error of approximately 0.05 N is below the 0.1–0.2 N threshold often considered acceptable for force sensing in RMIS, suggesting reliable image-to-force calibration.

**Sensor Characterization:**
- **Vision Spatial Resolution:** The visual modality of MVP-Tac was characterized using a USAF 1951 resolution target, showing a spatial resolution of approximately 198 μm on the tactile sensing surface. This is derived from the ability to resolve Group 1, Element 3, corresponding to 2.52 line pairs/mm.
- **Repeatability:** During repeated loading–unloading trials up to 2 N, the sensor exhibited a repeatability of 33.34%. This figure represents the maximum spread of predicted force across five trials at matched force levels relative to the maximum applied force.
- **Hysteresis:** The hysteresis, quantifying the maximum discrepancy between loading and unloading predicted responses at the same force steps, was found to be 9.46%. This is attributed to the viscoelastic properties of the elastomer and rate-dependent effects during cyclic loading. The deviation from ground truth near the maximum load (∼2 N) was noted, attributed to the elastomer approaching its compression limit, resulting in geometric stiffening and partial saturation of optical features, which reduces sensitivity at higher forces. Additionally, the slow recovery of VytaFlex after deformation contributed to underprediction during rapid loading and overprediction during early unloading.

**Artificial Tumor Detection:**
A video-based classifier was trained to classify the hardness of artificial tumor phantoms using 16-frame palpation sequences. Force measurements were used for reference but excluded from classifier training.
- **Exposed Tumors:** The model achieved an accuracy of 97.37% in classifying the hardness of exposed synthetic tumor nodules of varying stiffness (Shore 20, 30, 40, 50, 60A).
- **Subdermal Tumors:** For subdermal tumor phantoms, where nodules were embedded 2 mm beneath a compliant silicone layer, the model achieved an accuracy of 92.11%. These results indicate MVP-Tac's capability to differentiate inclusions of varying hardness in both superficial and subsurface scenarios.

**Simulated Colonoscopy:**
MVP-Tac was evaluated in a clinically inspired scenario using an intestine-mimicking phantom.
- The sensor successfully operated in visual mode for navigation and surveying the internal surface of the lumen.
- Visual data captured during robotic scans enabled the reconstruction of a 3D photomap of the luminal wall, facilitating the localization of candidate nodules.
- After localization, the sensor was switched to tactile mode, and it successfully palpated 50A and 60A nodules within the lumen's constrained space. Hardness classification during this in-situ palpation achieved 100% accuracy for the tested nodules.
This experiment demonstrated MVP-Tac's ability to support a vision-guided "search-then-palpate" workflow in a constrained environment, integrating both visual and tactile modalities effectively.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work introduces MVP-Tac, a dual-modal vision and photoelastic tactile sensor that addresses a notable limitation in current robot-assisted minimally invasive surgery (RMIS) and flexible endoscopy: the absence of integrated, high-resolution tactile feedback without compromising essential visual cues. The sensor's compact design and dual-mode functionality represent a step toward restoring a critical sensory capability in these procedures.

**Clinical Significance:**
- **Enhanced Tumor Detection:** The ability to accurately classify the hardness of both exposed and subdermal lesions, as demonstrated on tissue phantoms, suggests a potential for earlier and more precise detection of tumors. This could lead to more complete resections and reduce the incidence of missed lesions, ultimately improving patient outcomes in oncology.
- **Augmented Surgical Perception:** By providing intuitive mechanical feedback through palpation, MVP-Tac could augment the surgeon's perception in complex, constrained environments. This restoration of tactile sense, traditionally relied upon in open surgery, may enhance decision-making and reduce reliance solely on visual interpretation or other less intuitive modalities.
- **Integrated Workflow:** The successful demonstration of a vision-guided "search-then-palpate" workflow in a simulated colonoscopy highlights its potential for practical integration into RMIS procedures. Surgeons could use the visual mode for broad navigation and anomaly detection, then switch to tactile mode for localized, detailed assessment.

**Technical Advancements:**
- **Miniaturization and Dual Modality:** MVP-Tac is presented as the first miniaturized, endoscope-compatible tactile sensor to integrate co-located vision and tactile sensing within a single compact package at its scale. This co-location and dual-mode switching mechanism addresses a significant challenge in combining disparate sensing modalities in confined spaces.
- **Simplified Tactile Sensing:** The use of reflective photoelastic imaging eliminates the need for structured lighting, which simplifies the design and fabrication process and contributes to the sensor's compactness and robustness, differentiating it from many GelSight-type sensors.
- **Robust Data Interpretation:** The dual-branch neural network, combining raw image features with a global photoelastic gradient metric (G²), demonstrates a robust approach to force estimation and hardness classification from complex optical patterns.

**Future Directions and Broader Impact:**
The authors acknowledge ongoing development areas, which highlight the potential for further impact:
- **Material Improvement:** Replacing the current VytaFlex elastomer with biocompatible, softer photoelastic hydrogels is proposed to extend the detectable stiffness range, improve elastic recovery, and ensure compatibility with sterilization and physiological conditions in vivo. This is critical for clinical translation.
- **Algorithmic Enhancement:** Moving beyond a single normal force value, the estimation of dense force/stress fields directly from the interferogram images could provide richer and more localized tactile feedback, enabling a deeper understanding of tissue mechanics.
- **Further Miniaturization:** Continued efforts to reduce the form factor, particularly of the camera module, are aimed at achieving millimeter-scale dimensions for broader in vivo deployment.
- **Open-Source Contribution:** The open-sourcing of the design, fabrication protocols, and firmware provides a foundation for the broader research community to build upon and accelerate advancements in surgical tactile sensing.

In summary, MVP-Tac presents a viable path toward integrating clinically useful tactile palpation back into RMIS while preserving essential visual feedback. Its design principles and demonstrated capabilities offer a foundation for developing next-generation surgical instruments that could improve diagnostic accuracy and surgical precision.
