# Mind the Sim-to-Real Gap & Think Like a Scientist

> [!summary] Summary
> A decision-theoretic framework for planners holding a pre-trained simulator confounded by hidden state. An extended simulation lemma splits simulator value error into a randomization-identifiable calibration-deployment shift and an irreducible misspecification residual, and separates the value gap into local and reachability parts. Fisher-SEP allocates a randomized pilot by minimizing posterior predictive variance of a target policy's value, gaining +27.1 points over passive updating in an HIV mobile-testing study.

## Problem

- Offline RL and confounded-MDP methods learn from logged data alone, never jointly optimizing that data's use against future real-world field experiments.
- Bayes-adaptive MDPs and posterior sampling update within the simulator's own parametric family and assume positivity, so they cannot diagnose when unvisited states yield no evidence.
- Sim-to-real, hybrid RL, and multi-fidelity Bayesian optimization deploy the simulator as a warm start, never separating states the deployed policy visits from those it never reaches.

## Method

- An ==extended simulation lemma== decomposes per-pair reward and transition error into a calibration-deployment shift, identifiable by a randomized state-measurable pilot, and an irreducible parametric misspecification residual.
- A ==local-reachability gap decomposition== splits the simulator-optimal policy's value gap into a local part on visited states and a reachability part that passive posterior updating provably leaves open.
- **Fisher-SEP** minimizes posterior predictive value variance, a delta-method form weighting ==Fisher information== by target-policy value gradients, with reward-only and transition-only specializations and an Exploration Priority Index diagnostic.

## Results

- Vending supply chain: the simulator-optimal policy decays from **89.0%** to **37.7%** of oracle cash by T=1600, while Fisher-SEP-R reaches **75.3%**, beating adaptive updating by **4.83 points**.
- Fisher-SEP-R pays a short-horizon cost, **76.6%** of oracle at T=100, and crosses over between T=400 and T=800, matching the post-pilot residual-ratio prediction of roughly 400-600.
- HIV mobile testing: Fisher-SEP-T finds **85.2%** of oracle cases at T=400 versus **58.1%** for passive updating and **76.9%** for Thompson sampling, containing the outbreak within 30 days.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Simulator error at states a policy never visits is structural, not slow-converging; no horizon of passive online data closes an out-of-support gap.
> - Use a simulator to choose where to experiment rather than what to deploy; its connectivity pattern stays informative even when its parameter values are 15x off.
> - Target-policy-aware designs beat uniform or all-parameter exploration by skipping parameters that never move deployed value, but front-loaded pilots need long horizons to amortize.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research situates itself at the intersection of reinforcement learning (RL), Bayesian experimental design, causal inference, and sim-to-real transfer. It addresses a specific challenge for decision-makers equipped with a pre-trained simulator and the option for real-world experimentation.

The paper distinguishes its contribution from several existing areas:
- **Offline Reinforcement Learning (RL) and Confounded MDPs**: While these fields offer tools for learning from logged data and diagnosing latent confounders (e.g., Levine et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2021; Zhang and Bareinboim, 2016; Kallus and Zhou, 2018), they primarily treat the historical dataset as the sole signal. This work, in contrast, jointly optimizes the use of historical data with future field experiments.
- **Bayes-adaptive MDPs (BAMDPs) and Posterior Sampling**: These frameworks formalize how beliefs about unknown MDP parameters adapt during deployment (e.g., Duff, 2002; Osband et al., 2013; Russo et al., 2018). However, they typically assume a specific parametric family for updates and often implicitly assume "positivity" (all state-action pairs have a non-zero probability of being observed), which this paper actively diagnoses as a potential failure point.
- **Sim-to-Real and Hybrid RL**: This area combines simulated and real interaction, sometimes including simulator-directed exploration (e.g., Tobin et al., 2017; Wagenmaker and Jamieson, 2024; Ball et al., 2023; Memmel et al., 2024). These approaches generally use the simulator as a warm start for training or policy transfer. This paper deviates by focusing on using the simulator specifically to *choose* where to experiment in the real world, rather than to train a deployable policy directly.
- **Causal Transportability**: This field characterizes when effects identified in one population can be transferred to another (e.g., Bareinboim and Pearl, 2016; Lanners et al., 2025). This paper extends this by prescribing *which* experiments to run when such transfer is likely to fail.
- **Multi-fidelity Bayesian Optimization**: This treats simulators as cheap surrogates for black-box objectives (e.g., Poloczek et et al., 2017; Kandasamy et al., 2017). This work introduces a distinction between states visited by a deployed policy and those not visited, which is not typically addressed in multi-fidelity optimization.

The core contribution of this work is to address a logically prior question: given a simulator and a finite planning horizon, which portions of the value gap between the simulator's predicted optimum and the true optimum can be closed by passive online updating, and which require deliberate experimentation?

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from a common scenario: a planner possesses a pre-trained simulator of a sequential decision problem and the capacity to conduct real-world experiments. The simulator, derived from historical data, offers cheap queries but may suffer from biases. Real-world experimentation, while unbiased, consumes valuable resources per trial. The fundamental question is to determine *when* and *how* the planner should supplement the simulator's guidance with real experiments.

The paper highlights specific problems inherent in using pre-trained simulators:
- **Confounding Bias**: Simulators are often trained on historical, observational data. If the historical operator's actions were correlated with unobserved (hidden) states that also influence outcomes, the simulator learns the observational expectation E[R | S, A] rather than the causal interventional effect E[R | S, do(A)]. This "confounding" bias does not diminish with more observational data of the same type. For example, if vans were historically sent to a zone only when prevalence was high, the simulator might incorrectly attribute high yield solely to sending a van, confounding the action's effect with the underlying high prevalence.
- **Data Drift**: The underlying conditions or hidden state distributions (P(H|S)) may have shifted between the time the simulator's calibration data was collected and the present deployment. This "drift" can render the simulator's predictions inaccurate even if they were causally valid at calibration time.
- **Positivity Violations (Reachability Issues)**: A significant portion of the state-action space may remain unvisited by any policy derived purely from the simulator or via passive learning. If the optimal action or most valuable states lie in these unvisited regions, no amount of passive online data collection will provide evidence for them, as they are never experienced.

These issues lead to two core questions the research aims to answer:
1. **When should a planner run real-world experiments?** This involves understanding the nature of the simulator's error and determining if it is reducible through experimentation.
2. **How is the simulator best used?** Should it be used as a tool to directly suggest a deployable policy, or as a tool to guide the design of real-world experiments?

The overarching objective is to develop a framework that decomposes the simulator's value error into components that respond differently to various types of data and interventions, thereby guiding strategic experimentation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research establishes a finite-latent decision-theoretic framework where the real world is modeled as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP) over observed (S) and hidden (H) states, while the simulator is treated as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) operating solely on observed states (S).

**Approximation Layers and Error Decomposition**:
The framework introduces a chain of approximations that characterizes the gap between the true POMDP (M*) and the simulator (M̂_sim):
`M* (true POMDP) -> M*obs (deployment-time Markov projection) -> M_calib (calibration-time Markov projection) -> M̂_sim (simulator)`
This chain facilitates the decomposition of the simulator's error into three distinct sources:
- **ϵ_hist (Markov-projection error)**: The cost of simplifying the history-dependent POMDP to a history-independent observed-state MDP. This error is assumed negligible under contracting latent dynamics.
- **ϵ_h (Calibration–deployment regime shift)**: The error arising from the difference between the hidden-state distributions during calibration (P_calib(H|S,A)) and deployment (P_t(H|S)). This component is identifiable and reducible by randomized, S-measurable real-world experimentation.
- **ϵ_m (Functional misspecification)**: The residual error due to finite-sample noise or inherent limitations of the simulator's parametric family. This component is irreducible by further real-world interaction, as it requires retraining or redesign of the simulator itself.

**Assumptions**:
The analysis is supported by standard assumptions: finite tabular state, bounded rewards, and policies dependent only on observed states. A crucial substantive assumption (Assumption 3) is that the simulator was trained under a behavior policy where actions were correlated with hidden states (A ̸⊥ H | S), introducing confounding. A technical assumption of conjugate independent priors (Assumption 4) simplifies posterior updates.

**Policy Classes**:
The paper defines three classes of policies for the planner:
- **Simulator-Optimal Policy (SOP, Π_na)**: A non-adaptive policy that directly deploys the policy optimal under the pre-trained simulator, without any real-world data updates.
- **Adaptive Simulator-Optimal Policy (A-SOP, Π_passive)**: A passive-learning policy that treats the simulator as a Bayesian prior and updates beliefs using real-world observations. It always chooses the action that is optimal according to the current posterior mean MDP, without deliberate exploration.
- **Simulation-Aided Experimental Policy (SEP, Π_adapt)**: An adaptive policy that strategically uses the simulator's structure to design and allocate resources for real-world experiments, potentially deviating from the current posterior-mean-optimal action to gather information.

**Main Theoretical Results**:
1. **Extended Simulation Lemma (Lemma 1)**: This extends the classical simulation lemma by explicitly decomposing the simulator's error (ϵ_r, ϵ_p) into identifiable (ϵ_h) and irreducible (ϵ_m) components. It quantitatively shows how reward and transition errors propagate through the value function, with transition errors having a quadratically larger impact with increasing effective horizon. Corollary 1 provides a finite-sample form for this bound after a pilot experiment, demonstrating that ϵ_h shrinks with more pilot data, while ϵ_m persists.
2. **Value Gap Decomposition (Proposition 1)**: The total "design gap" (G), representing the value gain achievable by an SEP over an SOP, is rigorously decomposed into two parts:
 - **G_local**: The value attainable by an explorer whose visitation support is restricted to the states already visited by the SOP. Passive online updating (A-SOP) can asymptotically close this gap.
 - **G_reach**: The additional value attainable *only* by visiting states outside the SOP's visitation support. This "reachability gap" is provably not closed by purely passive learning and remains bounded away from zero at any horizon in certain MDPs (Theorem 6). This highlights the need for deliberate exploration.
3. **Fisher-SEP (Definition 5)**: This is the core proposed methodology for designing experiments. Fisher-SEP is an instance of SEP that aims to minimize the Posterior Predictive Value Variance (PVV) of a chosen target policy's value. The PVV criterion is a sum over state-action pairs, weighted by the square of the value gradient (how much the target policy's value changes with a parameter perturbation at that pair) and the inverse posterior precision of the parameter.
 - **Value Gradient**: The value gradient, computed via the Bellman resolvent, quantifies how parameter errors propagate through the MDP dynamics to affect the target policy's value. This allows Fisher-SEP to prioritize exploration in areas where uncertainty, even if small in absolute terms, has a large impact on the policy's performance.
 - **Explore under Ignorance (Remark 4)**: A key property is that Fisher-SEP can allocate pilot budget to state-action pairs that are relevant to the target policy's value but have not been visited by the current policy (and thus have high prior variance), even if the simulator's mean prediction for these pairs is confident.
 - **Identification (Lemma 2)**: A randomized, S-measurable pilot policy is crucial for identifying the true interventional reward variance (τ_int_s,a) needed for the PVV calculation, as the observational variance can be biased under confounding.
 - **Specializations**: Fisher-SEP-R (reward-dominates) and Fisher-SEP-T (transition-dominates) are provided for scenarios where one type of error is more prominent.
 - **Exploration Priority Index (EPI, Remark 5)**: A diagnostic tool that provides a per-state-action score indicating how much pilot effort a pair deserves, useful for debugging and understanding the allocation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents both theoretical findings regarding error decomposition and value gap, and empirical results from two case studies illustrating different regimes.

**Theoretical Findings**:
- **Error Decomposition**: Lemma 1, the extended simulation lemma, formally decomposes the simulator's value error into `ϵ_h` (calibration-deployment shift) and `ϵ_m` (functional misspecification). It clarifies that only `ϵ_h` can be reduced by real-world experimentation (specifically, randomized S-measurable pilots), while `ϵ_m` is an irreducible residual. The classical result that transition errors affect value quadratically (`(1-γ)^-2`) while reward errors are linear (`(1-γ)^-1`) is retained, highlighting the greater challenge of mitigating transition model inaccuracies.
- **Value Gap Decomposition**: Proposition 1 establishes that the total design gap, representing the potential value gain from simulation-aided experimentation, splits into a `G_local` component and a `G_reach` component. `G_local` pertains to value improvements within the visitation support of the already deployed (or simulator-optimal) policy, which passive online updating (A-SOP) can asymptotically close. `G_reach`, however, represents value attainable *only* by exploring states outside the current policy's visitation support. Theorem 6, supported by a deterministic combination-lock MDP, demonstrates that `G_reach` can be bounded away from zero at any horizon under purely passive learning, necessitating deliberate exploration.
- **Fisher-SEP Mechanism**: Fisher-SEP is designed to minimize the posterior predictive variance of a target policy's value. Its effectiveness stems from incorporating the Bellman resolvent in the value gradient, which allows it to identify state-action pairs whose uncertainty, if reduced, would most significantly improve the target policy's performance. This enables "explore under ignorance" (Remark 4), where Fisher-SEP prioritizes exploration in regions of the state space that are unvisited and poorly modeled by the simulator but are critical for optimal policy value, even if the simulator's prior is superficially confident.

**Empirical Findings (Case Studies)**:

1. **Vending-Machine Supply Chain (Local Regime)**:
 - **Scenario**: Five vending machines, each stocking three products across three segments. The simulator's error is primarily a miscalibration of demand (reward error) at *visited* machines, exacerbated by censored observations (stock-outs hide true demand).
 - **SOP Performance**: The SOP's value degrades substantially with increasing horizon (from 89.0% of oracle at T=100 days to 37.7% at T=1600 days), consistent with Lemma 1's prediction of compounding errors.
 - **Passive Learning (A-SOP, Thompson Sampling)**: Both passively updating policies (A-SOP and Thompson sampling) close a significant portion of the value gap by adapting within the SOP's visitation support. At T=1600, they reach approximately 70-71% of oracle value.
 - **Fisher-SEP-R Performance**: Fisher-SEP-R, a reward-dominates specialization, initially incurs a cost from front-loaded randomization (76.6% at T=100) but overtakes A-SOP by T=1600 (75.3% vs. 70.7%, a +4.83 percentage point (pp) lead, p=0.005). This demonstrates that for local-regime errors, front-loaded experimentation can amortize its cost over sufficiently long horizons.

2. **HIV Mobile-Testing Program (Reachability Regime)**:
 - **Scenario**: A 5x8 grid with two regions separated by a wall, connected by a single corridor. Region A is well-surveilled; Region B is peri-urban and historically un-sampled by the simulator's calibration data. Region B contains a hidden cluster with high true prevalence (30%), which the simulator underestimates significantly (2%). This is a `G_reach` regime scenario, where the error is concentrated in unvisited states.
 - **SOP and A-SOP Performance**: Both SOP and A-SOP policies fail to contain the HIV outbreak and show plateauing cumulative cases found (A-SOP at 58.1% of oracle by T=400). Their visitation remains largely concentrated in Region A, failing to cross the corridor to Region B.
 - **Thompson Sampling**: Thompson sampling achieves 76.9% of oracle at T=400, partly closing the gap (an +18.8 pp lift over A-SOP, p < 10^-3). This is attributed to occasional high posterior draws for Region B prevalence, which implicitly encourage exploration across the corridor.
 - **Navigation-restricted Fisher-SEP-T Performance**: Fisher-SEP-T, a transition-dominates specialization with navigation constraints, demonstrates superior performance. It contains the outbreak within 30 days and reaches 85.2% of oracle at T=400. This represents a +27.1 pp lead over A-SOP (p < 10^-3) and a +8.3 pp lead over Thompson sampling (p=0.003). Visualization (Figure 2c) shows Fisher-SEP-T's visitation successfully crosses the corridor early, directly addressing the reachability gap. This outcome is explained by Fisher-SEP's value gradient, which identifies the strong coupling between Region B prevalence and Region A's infection forecast via SIS dynamics, motivating exploration despite the simulator's confident but inaccurate low-prevalence prior for Region B. The reachability gap in this setting grows linearly with the horizon, consistent with the ability of a fixed number of explorer units to cover the hidden cluster.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research provides a framework for understanding and strategically addressing the sim-to-real gap, particularly in sequential decision problems with pre-trained simulators.

**Key Contributions**:
- **Gap Decomposition**: The central insight is the decomposition of the simulator's value gap into `G_local` and `G_reach` components. This distinction is critical because `G_local` can be closed by passive online learning, while `G_reach` necessitates deliberate, structured exploration. This framework suggests that the decision to experiment should depend not just on the magnitude of the simulator's error, but crucially on *where* that error sits relative to the deployed policy's visitation support.
- **Fisher-SEP as a Design Tool**: Fisher-SEP offers a principled, Bayesian approach to designing real-world experiments. Unlike traditional sim-to-real methods that aim to improve simulator accuracy, Fisher-SEP repurposes an imperfect simulator to identify where real-world data collection would be most informative for improving policy value. Its reliance on the Bellman resolvent's connectivity pattern (rather than precise parameter values) allows it to guide exploration effectively even when the simulator's parameter estimates are highly inaccurate, as demonstrated in the HIV case study.
- **Amortization and Horizon**: The findings highlight that the horizon for policy deployment dictates whether passive learning (for `G_local`) or front-loaded experimentation (for `G_reach` and faster `G_local` closure) is more effective. For reachability-dominated scenarios, increasing the horizon does not amortize passive learning, as the relevant states remain unvisited.
- **Connection to Causal Inference**: The local-reachability decomposition mirrors the within-positivity versus out-of-positivity distinction in causal inference, implying that simply gathering more observational data cannot identify effects in state-action pairs outside the deployed policy's support.

**Potential Impact**:
- **Improved Decision-Making**: The framework provides decision-makers with a structured method for evaluating when to trust a simulator, when to collect more data, and how to optimally design experiments, balancing opportunity cost with information gain.
- **Resource Allocation**: By identifying the most "valuable" experiments, Fisher-SEP can lead to more efficient allocation of costly real-world resources in fields like public health (e.g., mobile testing campaigns), supply chain management (e.g., inventory optimization), and robotics.
- **Diagnostic Tools**: The Exploration Priority Index (EPI) provides a diagnostic score to assess the urgency and location of experimental effort, facilitating clearer communication and justification for interventions.

**Limitations and Future Work**:
- **Tabular State Assumption**: The current framework relies on finite tabular state and action spaces. Extending it to function approximation (e.g., Gaussian processes, Bayesian neural networks) would be a significant next step, particularly for computing value gradients in high-dimensional continuous spaces.
- **Simulator Validity Diagnosis**: Developing robust methods to diagnose simulator misspecification *before* running expensive real-world pilots (e.g., via residual structure in calibration data) is an open area.
- **Hierarchical Priors**: The assumption of independent priors across state-action pairs is conservative. Incorporating hierarchical priors (e.g., spatial correlations) could allow passive learning to close parts of the reachability gap through generalization, requiring a quantification of this "kernel-transported" information.
- **Generalizability of Reachability Gap**: While the deterministic combination-lock and HIV case studies provide strong evidence, fully generalizing the conditions under which passive learning fundamentally fails to close the reachability gap for all passive policy classes (e.g., Thompson sampling) remains an open theoretical question.
- **Non-Contracting Dynamics**: The current analysis assumes latent dynamics are contracting; extending the framework to non-contracting or slowly mixing latent states would require more complex belief-state policy generalizations.

**Ethical Considerations**:
The paper explicitly addresses the ethical implications of its proposed methodology. Directing exploration to "hidden clusters" or underserved regions, while statistically efficient for maximizing value, can temporarily shift testing access away from already-served communities. Any operational deployment would necessitate thorough domain-specific ethical review, community engagement, and potentially the incorporation of quantitative equity constraints to ensure fairness alongside efficiency. The framework offers tools for understanding trade-offs but does not resolve the ethical decisions.
