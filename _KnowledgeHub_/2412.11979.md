# AlphaZero Neural Scaling and Zipf's Law: a Tale of Board Games and Power Laws

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Goethe University Frankfurt demonstrated that AlphaZero agents produce state-frequency distributions adhering to Zipf's law, with the Zipf exponent non-linearly correlating with neural scaling law exponents. This work also mechanistically explains inverse scaling in AlphaZero by showing that larger models selectively overfit numerous, less strategically important late-game states, causing performance degradation on crucial early-game situations.

## Problem

- A universal understanding of neural scaling laws (NSLs) remains elusive, especially their mechanisms in reinforcement learning (RL) like AlphaZero, compared to supervised learning (LLMs).
- The connection between Zipf's law, observed in natural language and other domains, and NSLs in RL, particularly AlphaZero, was not thoroughly investigated.
- The phenomenon of inverse scaling, where larger RL models perform worse, lacked a mechanistic explanation rooted in learning dynamics and data distribution.

## Method

- AlphaZero agents were trained and analyzed on four board games (Connect Four, Pentago, Oware, Checkers) to measure state-frequency distributions and their adherence to ==Zipf's law==.
- The policy temperature of the Monte-Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) was varied during inference to systematically alter state distributions and correlate changes in Zipf exponents with AlphaZero's performance scaling exponents.
- Loss optimization order on states was analyzed, and inverse scaling was investigated by examining state-turn distributions, split-loss between early and late game, and learning dynamics to propose a mechanism involving selective forgetting.

## Results

- AlphaZero agents consistently produced state-frequency distributions following Zipf's law, with exponents between **0.8** and 1.0, and optimized loss on more frequent (though computationally harder) states first.
- Varying MCTS policy temperature revealed a non-linear correlation between the Zipf tail exponent and AlphaZero's size-scaling exponent, while policy quality remained constant.
- Inverse scaling in Checkers and Oware correlated with 'forked' turn distributions and a 'bump' in the Zipf curve from frequent late-game states; larger models showed improved late-game loss but degraded early-game loss due to selective forgetting.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - AlphaZero agents' training and inference data exhibit Zipf's law for state frequencies, whose smooth power-law arises from the interaction of agent policies with the inherent game-tree structure.
> - A causal, non-linear relationship exists between the Zipf tail exponent of the state-frequency distribution and the size-scaling exponent of AlphaZero's Elo score.
> - Inverse scaling in AlphaZero is linked to unusual state-frequency distributions dominated by high-frequency late-game states, where larger models selectively overfit these states, leading to 'selective forgetting' of crucial early-game information due to non-stationary targets.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The research addresses the observed phenomenon of neural scaling laws (NSLs), where the performance of machine learning models follows power laws with respect to training resources such as compute, model size, or training data. While NSLs are well-documented across various architectures and applications, particularly in large language models (LLMs) and other supervised learning contexts, a universal understanding of their underlying mechanisms remains elusive. Existing theories frequently connect performance power laws to Zipf’s law, an empirical power law observed in natural language and other domains. Notably, the "quantization model" proposes that LLM scaling laws arise from learning discrete, Zipf-distributed task quanta in descending order of frequency.

In contrast to supervised learning, scaling laws in reinforcement learning (RL) are less extensively studied. AlphaZero, a prominent multi-agent RL algorithm successful in games like chess and Go, has been shown to exhibit power-law scaling for its Elo score with training resources and a power-law relationship between compute and optimal model size. This paper bridges a gap by exploring the mechanisms behind these known RL scaling laws in AlphaZero, specifically examining whether the connections between Zipf’s law and scaling laws, as theorized for LLMs, extend to the RL domain. It investigates the nature of data distributions in AlphaZero and how these relate to model performance and the specific phenomenon of inverse scaling, thereby contributing to a more comprehensive understanding of scaling laws across different machine learning paradigms.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to explore the underlying mechanism of neural scaling laws in reinforcement learning, specifically within the AlphaZero algorithm, by comparing it to the quantization model of LLM scaling. This overarching goal is motivated by the need for a universal understanding of NSLs, especially given the relative scarcity of explanatory models for RL settings.

The key objectives are detailed as follows:
1. **Investigate Zipf’s Law in AlphaZero Data:** The researchers aimed to determine if AlphaZero agents, known to exhibit scaling laws, generate training and inference data whose state-frequency distribution follows Zipf’s law. This would suggest that the "learned tasks" in AlphaZero also conform to a Zipf distribution.
2. **Elucidate the Origin of Zipf’s Law in Board Games:** A specific objective was to demonstrate how smooth Zipf distributions emerge when AlphaZero agent policies interact with the inherent tree structure of board games, building upon prior suggestions in literature that linked Zipf's law to game tree structures.
3. **Correlate Zipf and Scaling Exponents:** The study sought to examine the relationship between the exponent of the Zipf distribution and the observed scaling exponents in AlphaZero. This involved a novel approach of modulating policy temperature during inference to systematically alter the state distribution and observe its impact on the scaling laws.
4. **Analyze Loss Optimization Order:** A core objective derived from the quantization model was to ascertain if AlphaZero agents minimize loss on game states in descending order of frequency, as predicted for LLMs, and to understand the implications of this learning order for task complexity.
5. **Understand Inverse Scaling:** The paper aimed to investigate the phenomenon of inverse scaling in AlphaZero—where model performance decreases with increasing size—by correlating it with unusual state-frequency distributions. Specifically, the researchers sought to explore if larger models shift their focus to less strategically important but high-frequency late-game states, thereby potentially compromising understanding of crucial early-game states. The motivation was to propose an explanation for inverse scaling in AlphaZero by integrating the quantization model with the concept of selective non-stationarity of training targets.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The researchers employed AlphaZero agents trained on four distinct board games: Connect Four, Pentago, Oware, and Checkers. These agents utilized open-source models (Connect Four, Pentago) or newly trained models with identical hyperparameters (Oware, Checkers) from the OpenSpiel framework. All models exhibited power-law scaling for Bradley-Terry playing strength ($\gamma$), which relates to Elo score, with respect to neural network parameters ($N$) and training compute ($C$).

The methodology involved several key analytical steps:

1. **AlphaZero Agent Configuration:** The neural networks used were Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs) with three hidden layers of exponentially increasing widths. A fixed number of 300 Monte-Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) steps were performed per move during both training and inference. Game states were identified by their observation tensors (board positions), which serve as input to the neural network, without considering move history.

2. **Zipf's Law Analysis:**
- **State Frequency Measurement:** The frequency of each unique board state visited by AlphaZero agents during training was recorded. States were then sorted in descending order of frequency to generate rank-frequency distributions.
- **Origin Visualization:** To understand the genesis of Zipf's law, toy-model games with constant branching factors and no loops were used to illustrate how an ideal game tree generates a series of plateaus around a power-law trend. This was then extended to random policies in actual games (Connect Four, Pentago) to demonstrate how game rules smooth these plateaus into continuous Zipf curves. The effect of non-uniform policies was also examined.
- **Temperature Modulation for Correlation:** To investigate the correlation between Zipf and scaling exponents, the policy temperature ($T$) of the MCTS algorithm (Eq. 5) was varied during inference. This parameter controls the exploration-exploitation balance, thereby altering the state-frequency distribution. The resulting changes in the Zipf exponent (specifically the tail exponent) were then correlated with changes in the Elo score scaling exponent for Connect Four. The impact of temperature on policy quality was also assessed to ensure causality.

3. **Loss Scaling Analysis:**
- **Value-Head Loss:** The value-head loss ($L_{\text{value}} = (z-v)^2$) of AlphaZero agents was measured. This involved comparing the network's predicted value ($v$) with the ground-truth game outcome ($z$).
- **Ground-Truth Labels:** For Connect Four, ground-truth labels for states were obtained using an alpha-beta pruning solver. This allowed for an objective assessment of how well models predicted optimal play.
- **Loss vs. Rank and Complexity:** Loss was analyzed as a function of state rank (frequency). The perceived complexity of states was assessed by measuring the CPU time required for an alpha-beta pruning solver to evaluate them, correlating this with state rank.

4. **Inverse Scaling Investigation:**
- **Identification of Inverse Scaling:** Games (Checkers, Oware) where AlphaZero agents exhibited inverse scaling (decreasing Elo with increasing model size) were identified.
- **State Turn Distribution Analysis:** The average turn number at which each state was encountered was analyzed to distinguish between early-game and late-game states. This revealed "forked" turn distributions in games with inverse scaling, indicating high-frequency late-game states.
- **Anomaly Characterization:** The unusual Zipf curve in Oware (a "bump" caused by frequent late-game states) was linked to the converging game-tree structure of these games, where pieces are removed, reducing the number of possible board permutations towards the end.
- **Split-Loss Analysis:** Loss was separately analyzed for early-game and late-game states in Oware.
- **Learning Dynamics Over Time:** Training loss for the largest Oware model was tracked over time, differentiating between early-game and late-game states, to identify "selective forgetting."
- **Hypothesized Mechanism:** A mechanism involving a combination of the quantization model and selective non-stationarity of training targets (value labels) was proposed to explain inverse scaling.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

1. **Zipf’s Law in AlphaZero Data:** The study found that AlphaZero agents exhibiting scaling laws produce training and inference data where the frequency distribution of board states follows Zipf’s law. The Zipf exponent ($\alpha$) was observed to range approximately between 0.8 and 1.0 across different games and model sizes, which aligns with exponents found in human-played Go games and other natural language datasets. This finding suggests that learned tasks in AlphaZero also scale with Zipf's law.

2. **Origin of Zipf’s Law:** The research provides evidence that the smooth Zipf power laws in AlphaZero emerge from the interaction of agent policies with the inherent tree structure of board games. A toy model of a constant-branching, non-looping game demonstrated that an ideal game tree generates a frequency distribution characterized by plateaus centered around a power law with an exponent of 1. Adding realistic game rules, even with a uniformly random policy, smooths these plateaus into a continuous power law. Learned AlphaZero policies further smooth the distribution while maintaining the power-law shape.

3. **Correlation Between Zipf and Scaling Exponents:** By modulating the MCTS policy temperature ($T$) during inference, the researchers were able to systematically alter both the state-frequency Zipf curve and the AlphaZero size-scaling law. A non-linear correlation was observed between the Zipf tail exponent and the size-scaling exponent. This correlation was determined to be causal, as the policy quality (probability of optimal moves) remained constant within the temperature range where the exponents showed significant correlation.

4. **Loss Optimization Order and Complexity:** In agreement with the quantization model, AlphaZero agents were found to minimize loss on game states in descending order of frequency.
- Average value loss on training data steadily increased with state rank (i.e., decreased frequency), indicating that more frequent states were better optimized.
- Larger models consistently achieved lower absolute ground-truth value loss across all ranks compared to smaller models, confirming that increased model size improves performance.
- Counter-intuitively, the most frequent states (low rank) were identified as the most difficult to model using conventional search algorithms (alpha-beta pruning), requiring significantly more CPU time for evaluation. This suggests that AlphaZero agents achieve better loss on tasks that are inherently harder to solve by traditional methods.

5. **Inverse Scaling and Game Structure:** Inverse scaling, characterized by a decrease in Elo score with increasing neural network size beyond a certain threshold, was observed in AlphaZero agents trained on Checkers and Oware.
- **Frequency Distribution Anomaly:** This inverse scaling correlated with an unusual state-frequency distribution in these games, particularly a "bump" or increased prevalence of high-frequency late-game states in the Zipf curve.
- **Turn Distribution Anomaly:** Unlike Connect Four and Pentago, where state turn number strongly correlates with state rank, Oware and Checkers exhibited "forked" turn distributions. A substantial fraction of the most frequent states in these games appeared late in the game, close to its conclusion. This anomaly is attributed to the game rules, where pieces are successively removed, leading to game trees that contract towards a smaller set of end-game configurations.
- **Selective Overfitting on Late-Game States:** Analysis of loss for Oware revealed that larger models optimized late-game states progressively better, achieving lower loss on them. However, this came at the expense of performance on early-game states, where smaller models surprisingly achieved lower loss than larger ones.
- **Selective Forgetting:** Tracking the training dynamics of large Oware models showed that loss on early-game states initially improved but then degraded towards the end of training, while late-game loss consistently improved.
- **Proposed Mechanism:** Inverse scaling is hypothesized to result from large models over-fitting on numerous but strategically less important late-game states. This "selective forgetting" of crucial early-game information is linked to target non-stationarity during RL training, where value labels for early-game states shift more significantly than those for late-game states, potentially leading to dormant neurons and local optima that prioritize the fixed values of late-game states.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes significantly to the understanding of neural scaling laws (NSLs) by extending the theoretical framework, previously predominantly applied to supervised learning, to the domain of reinforcement learning (RL) with AlphaZero.

1. **Advancing Universal NSL Understanding:** By demonstrating the presence of Zipf's law in AlphaZero's state distributions and connecting it to scaling laws, the paper provides empirical evidence that theories like the quantization model might have broader applicability across different machine learning paradigms. This moves towards a more unified, universal understanding of why neural models exhibit power-law scaling.

2. **Insight into RL Learning Mechanisms:** The findings offer detailed insights into how AlphaZero agents learn and prioritize information within complex game environments. The observation that agents optimize for more frequent states first, even when those states are computationally harder for traditional search algorithms, provides a nuanced view of "task complexity" from an RL agent's perspective.

3. **Explaining and Mitigating Inverse Scaling:** The study provides a detailed, mechanistic explanation for inverse scaling in RL, linking it to the intrinsic game-tree structure, the resulting state-frequency distributions, and a phenomenon akin to "selective forgetting." Understanding that inverse scaling can arise from models prioritizing numerous, trivial late-game states over strategically critical early-game ones has direct implications for developing more robust and scalable RL algorithms. This knowledge could inform future design choices to prevent or mitigate performance degradation in larger models.

4. **Implications for Curriculum Learning:** The results hint at the potential for improved RL algorithms through curriculum design. By understanding how state frequency and structural properties influence learning, researchers could devise training curricula that strategically sample or prioritize certain types of states to optimize learning and avoid pitfalls like inverse scaling. For instance, modifying the training distribution to de-emphasize frequently occurring, but strategically unimportant, late-game states could lead to more effective training.

5. **Guiding Future Research:** The paper identifies open challenges, such as the difficulty in precisely identifying "task quanta" in RL (and LLMs) and developing a complete theoretical model that quantitatively links state distribution characteristics to high-level performance metrics like Elo scores. This work lays a foundation for future research into RL interpretability, algorithm design, and the general theory of neural scaling.
