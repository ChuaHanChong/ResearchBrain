# FoAR: Force-Aware Reactive Policy for Contact-Rich Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> FoAR is a force-aware reactive imitation policy for contact-rich manipulation. Built on RISE, it encodes 100Hz force/torque history alongside 3D point clouds and fuses them through a future contact predictor that gates the force feature against a learnable embedding, then uses the same contact probability to reactively correct actions under plain position control. With 50 demonstrations it beats all baselines on wiping, peeling and chopping.

## Problem

- Vision-only manipulation policies lack contact feedback, so RGB or point-cloud observations barely change across contact transitions, making precise contact-rich skills unreliable.
- Alternative modalities are weak substitutes: audio is noise-prone and indirect, while tactile sensors are heterogeneous across designs and inconsistent after replacement.
- Existing force/torque policies fuse force throughout the episode or assume the object is pre-grasped, so sensor noise in non-contact phases degrades performance.

## Method

- **FoAR**'s ==future contact predictor==: a separate ==ResNet18== image encoder plus force MLP outputs contact probability, supervised by ==binary cross-entropy== against thresholded force in a surrounding time window.
- ==Contact-gated feature fusion==: the scene feature is concatenated with a convex blend of the transformer-encoded force feature and a learnable neutral embedding, weighted by the predicted contact probability.
- ==Reactive control== at deployment: when contact probability exceeds a threshold but measured force is insufficient, actions are nudged a small step along the predicted motion direction, with separate ==temporal-ensemble== buffers per phase.

## Results

- FoAR scores **0.875** wiping, **0.850** general wiping and **0.756** peeling with **100%** action success, versus RISE at **0.500**, **0.500** and **0.377**.
- On chopping, FoAR yields **3.9** segments versus RISE's **1.8**, with normalized-length mean **0.353** versus **0.727** and standard deviation **0.094** versus **0.411**.
- Ablations: removing reactive control drops wiping to **0.650**, dropping force rate to **2Hz** gives **0.625**, and sharing the 3D encoder for prediction collapses scores to **0.175**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Gate an auxiliary noisy modality on a learned phase signal rather than fusing it constantly; naive token or concatenation fusion can hurt the non-contact phases.
> - A learned contact prior can substitute for compliance or admittance control, making simple position control sufficient for sustained-contact tasks.
> - Auxiliary predictors need their own encoder: sharing a backbone with the policy caused conflicting feature demands and performed worse than no force at all.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation has seen significant advancements, with a particular focus on enabling robots to perform complex tasks in unstructured environments. Within this field, **contact-rich manipulation** represents a distinct and highly challenging sub-domain. Tasks like assembly, wiping, peeling, and chopping inherently involve sustained or intricate physical interactions between the robot, its end-effector, and objects in the environment. These tasks demand not only precise control but also real-time adaptation to varying contact forces and object properties, making them fundamentally different from simpler pick-and-place operations.

Historically, vision-based robotic manipulation policies have made substantial progress, leveraging high-resolution visual inputs (RGB, depth, point clouds) to guide robot actions. Policies such as ACT, Diffusion Policy, and RISE have demonstrated success in a variety of manipulation scenarios. However, a critical limitation of purely vision-based approaches, as highlighted by the authors, is their inherent struggle with tasks requiring fine-grained physical interaction. Vision alone often fails to provide sufficient feedback on contact states, force magnitudes, or torque dynamics, which are crucial for tasks where physical contact defines success. For example, it's difficult for a robot to determine the exact moment of contact or the pressure applied solely through visual cues.

To address this gap, the broader research landscape has increasingly explored **multi-modal policies**, incorporating additional sensory inputs beyond vision. Audio, tactile, and force/torque sensing modalities have been integrated to provide richer feedback. While audio can be vulnerable to noise, and tactile sensors face challenges related to heterogeneity and consistency, force/torque sensing offers a direct and intuitive measure of physical interaction. Previous works have attempted to combine force/torque data with vision, either for low-level control (e.g., compliant or hybrid force/position control) or for higher-level decision-making in learning-based policies.

However, the authors identify a critical oversight in many existing force/torque-integrated policies: they often treat force/torque data as continuously relevant, combining it with vision throughout the entire manipulation process. This overlooks the fact that force/torque information is **sparsely activated**—it is only truly meaningful during contact phases. During non-contact phases (e.g., reaching, grasping, or retracting), real-world force/torque sensors are susceptible to inherent noise, which can degrade policy performance if not properly managed. Some prior works also simplify the problem by assuming the object is already grasped or fixed, thereby bypassing the non-contact challenges.

FoAR directly addresses this identified gap. It positions itself as an advancement in multi-modal robotic manipulation by proposing a novel mechanism to *dynamically* integrate force/torque data based on predicted contact states. By leveraging a "future contact predictor," FoAR aims to intelligently balance the contribution of force/torque information, emphasizing it during contact phases while minimizing its interference during non-contact phases. This nuanced approach, combined with a reactive control strategy, seeks to overcome the limitations of both purely vision-based policies and existing naive multi-modal fusion methods, thereby enabling more precise, robust, and adaptable performance in challenging contact-rich tasks using simple position control, a departure from more complex force-control schemes.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind FoAR is to overcome the limitations of existing robotic manipulation policies in handling **contact-rich tasks**, which are essential for practical robot applications but remain challenging. The core problem identified is that:
1. **Vision-only policies lack critical contact feedback:** They struggle to accurately perceive contact states and physical interactions, making it difficult to achieve the dexterity required for nuanced contact.
2. **Existing multi-modal policies have drawbacks:** While incorporating modalities like audio, tactile, or force/torque helps, methods like audio are noisy, tactile sensors are heterogeneous, and critically, many force/torque integration methods fail to account for the "sparsely activated" nature of force/torque data. Naive fusion can lead to noisy force/torque readings degrading performance during non-contact phases.

Given these challenges, the **key objectives** of the FoAR policy are:
- To **effectively integrate high-frequency force/torque sensing with visual inputs** to enhance performance in contact-rich manipulation.
- To develop a **multimodal feature fusion mechanism that dynamically adjusts the usage of force/torque data** between non-contact and contact phases, guided by a future contact predictor. This aims to leverage force/torque information when relevant (during contact) and mitigate noise when irrelevant (during non-contact).
- To enable **accurate accomplishment of contact-rich tasks through simple position control** by employing a reactive control strategy informed by contact predictions and real-time force/torque feedback, thereby avoiding the need for complex, explicitly tuned force/impedance control schemes.
- To demonstrate **superior performance and robustness** across a variety of challenging real-world contact-rich tasks, including those involving surface force control (wiping, peeling) and instantaneous force impact (chopping), and under unexpected dynamic disturbances.

In essence, the authors aim to build a policy that intelligently "knows" when to rely on force feedback, making it more precise and robust for interactions requiring physical contact, while maintaining strong performance during non-contact movements, all without resorting to complex low-level force controllers.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

FoAR (Force-Aware Reactive Policy) is designed as an imitation learning approach that builds upon the RISE (Real-world Robot Imitation Simple and Effective) policy, a state-of-the-art vision-based policy known for its effectiveness in real-world scenarios. FoAR enhances RISE by incorporating force/torque sensing and a novel fusion and control strategy.

**Inputs:**
- **Visual Inputs:** RGB-D image converted into a point cloud ($p_t$) at the current timestep $t$. A separate RGB image ($I_t$) is used specifically for the future contact predictor.
- **Force/Torque Inputs:** High-frequency force/torque observations ($f_{t-T_o:t}$) over a historical horizon ($T_o$). This captures the temporal evolution of forces and torques. (e.g., 200 steps at 100Hz, covering 2 seconds).

**Architecture:**
FoAR consists of four main components:
1. **Point Cloud Encoder:** This component processes the input point cloud ($p_t$). It uses a sparse 3D encoder (based on MinkowskiEngine, similar to RISE) followed by a Transformer to generate a compact **scene feature** ($h_s^t$). This captures the visual understanding of the robot's environment.
2. **Force/Torque Encoder:** This component processes the historical force/torque data ($f_{t-T_o:t}$). Each force/torque reading is first passed through a 3-layer MLP to generate a force token. These tokens, over the historical horizon, are then fed into a Transformer with sinusoidal positional encodings to generate a comprehensive **force feature** ($h_f^t$). This captures the temporal dynamics of physical interactions.
3. **Future Contact Predictor:** This is a crucial novel component. It takes the current RGB image ($I_t$) and the historical force/torque data ($f_{t-T_o:t}$) as inputs. It uses a ResNet18 for image encoding and an MLP for force encoding. These features are concatenated and passed through a linear layer to output a **future contact probability ($\phi(t) \in$)**. This probability indicates whether contact is likely to occur in upcoming steps. Notably, this predictor uses a *separate* visual encoder to avoid conflicting attention with the main policy's scene understanding. Force/torque data aids in correcting predictions for unexpected contact.
4. **Feature Fusion:** The core of FoAR's multimodal integration. Unlike naive concatenation or tokenization, FoAR employs a dynamic fusion strategy guided by the future contact predictor. The fused feature ($h_t$) is calculated as:
 $h_t = [h_s^t; \phi(t) \cdot h_f^t + (1 - \phi(t)) \cdot h^*]$
 where $h^*$ is a learnable embedding (neutral embedding). This formulation ensures that:
 - During contact phases (high $\phi(t)$), the force feature ($h_f^t$) strongly influences the fused representation.
 - During non-contact phases (low $\phi(t)$), the force feature's contribution is minimized, and the neutral embedding ($h^*$) effectively replaces it, preventing noise from interfering.
5. **Action Head:** The fused feature ($h_t$) serves as the conditioning input for a CNN-based diffusion action head. This head generates future robot end-effector actions ($a_{t:t+T_a}$) by progressively denoising action trajectories, predicting up to $T_a$ future steps.

**Supervision and Training:**
- **Action Loss ($L_{action}$):** The predicted actions are supervised by ground-truth actions from expert demonstrations using an L2 loss within the diffusion process.
- **Predictor Loss ($L_{predictor}$):** The future contact predictor is supervised by a binary cross-entropy loss. Ground-truth future contact states are automatically extracted from demonstrations by checking if force/torque data exceeds a predefined threshold within a surrounding time window.
- **Overall Loss:** A linear combination: $L = L_{action} + \alpha L_{predictor}$, where $\alpha$ is a weighting factor.
- **Data Efficiency:** The policy is trained with a relatively small number of demonstrations (50 per task for Wiping/Peeling, 40 for Chopping) collected via haptic teleoperation.

**Reactive Control in Deployment:**
A critical aspect of FoAR is its reactive control strategy, which allows the robot to perform contact-rich tasks accurately using **simple end-effector position control**, avoiding complex force/impedance control. This strategy operates during deployment based on the predicted future contact probability ($\phi$) and real-time force/torque readings:
- **Phase Determination:** The predicted $\phi$ is thresholded ($\delta_\phi$) to determine if the robot is in a contact phase (or will soon be).
- **Action Correction:** If a contact phase is detected ($\phi > \delta_\phi$) AND the current force/torque readings ($f_t$) are below predefined thresholds ($\delta_f, \delta_t$), indicating insufficient contact force, the predicted robot actions are dynamically adjusted. The adjustment involves moving the end-effector by a small step ($\epsilon$) towards the estimated future action direction. This effectively pushes the robot to maintain or increase contact force.
- **Temporal Ensembles:** Separate temporal ensemble buffers are used for contact and non-contact phases to ensure smooth trajectory execution and prevent interference.

This co-design of the policy architecture with the reactive control mechanism allows FoAR to intelligently adapt to contact dynamics in real-time, leveraging force/torque information precisely when it's most needed.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were designed to answer four key research questions (Q1-Q4) across three challenging real-world contact-rich tasks: Wiping (surface force control, including a generalized variant), Peeling (precision surface force control), and Chopping (instantaneous force impact).

**Q1: Does integrating force/torque information improve policy performance and manipulation accuracy in contact-rich tasks?**
- **Wiping & Peeling (Surface Force Control):** FoAR consistently achieved the highest scores (0.875 for Wiping, 0.850 for Wiping (General), 0.756 for Peeling) and a 100% action success rate (ASR) for both grasping and the contact-rich operation itself (wiping/peeling). This significantly outperformed all baselines (ACT, Diffusion Policy, RISE) which struggled with maintaining consistent and precise contact due to the lack of force/torque feedback. Qualitative results for Peeling showed FoAR consistently achieved full peeling, unlike baselines that often resulted in partial or failed peels.
- **Chopping (Instantaneous Force Impact):** FoAR demonstrated superior controlled performance, achieving more than double the number of segments (3.9 vs. 1.8 for RISE) with significantly lower average normalized length (0.353 vs. 0.727) and reduced segment variability (Std. 0.094 vs. 0.411). This highlights FoAR's ability to handle dynamic force impacts, which vision-only policies like RISE struggle with.

**Q2: Is the feature fusion module of the FoAR policy more effective than other variants in terms of integrating force/torque information?**
- **Comparison with RISE (force-token) and RISE (force-concat):** While these ablation variants, which simply integrated force/torque data as additional tokens or by direct concatenation, showed slight improvements over pure vision-based RISE, their performance was still significantly inferior to FoAR. Crucially, they often suffered from lower grasp ASRs, indicating that the constant presence of noisy force/torque data during non-contact phases interfered with policy decision-making. FoAR's dynamic fusion mechanism, guided by the contact predictor, successfully leveraged force data during contact while mitigating noise during non-contact phases, leading to superior overall performance.
- **Importance of Separate Predictor Encoder (FoAR (3D-cls)):** The FoAR (3D-cls) variant, which shared the sparse 3D encoder for both the contact predictor and the policy backbone, performed poorly, even worse than the RISE baseline. This finding strongly supports the design choice of using a separate, lightweight image encoder for the future contact predictor, preventing conflicting visual feature requirements from disrupting the main policy's performance.

**Q3: Can contact predictor, reactive control, and high-frequency force/torque sensing enhance the policy’s ability to perform contact-rich operations?**
- **Future Contact Predictor vs. Detection:** Ablations showed that using a *future* contact predictor was crucial; a simple *contact detection* mechanism often led to delayed or failed contact, emphasizing the predictive capability.
- **Reactive Control:** The results clearly demonstrated that reactive control (guided by the predicted future contact probability) was essential for optimal policy performance. It allowed for real-time action adjustments based on insufficient contact force, enabling precise manipulation even with simple position control.
- **High-Frequency Sensing:** Higher frequency (100Hz) force/torque sensing yielded better performance than lower frequencies (2Hz, 10Hz), indicating that richer temporal data enhances the policy's understanding and control of contact dynamics.

**Q4: Can FoAR maintain consistent task performance under unexpected environmental disturbances?**
- **Robustness Evaluation (Wiping (General)):** FoAR maintained consistent high performance across all three dynamic disturbance scenarios (Rewrite, Move, Rewrite + Move), achieving 100% grasp and wipe ASRs and consistently high scores (0.800-0.850). In contrast, RISE (force-token) struggled in these complex scenarios, likely due to noisy force/torque data interference during action re-generation caused by disturbances pushing the policy into new non-contact states. While baseline RISE showed some generalization, its overall performance was still limited by the lack of force/torque feedback. FoAR's robust performance highlights its adaptability and resilience in real-world, unpredictable environments.

In summary, FoAR's main findings underscore the critical role of intelligently integrated force/torque feedback for contact-rich manipulation. Its dynamic feature fusion, guided by a future contact predictor, combined with a reactive control strategy, allows it to achieve state-of-the-art performance and remarkable robustness, even with simple position control and limited demonstrations.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of FoAR represents a significant step forward in the field of robotic manipulation, particularly for tasks requiring intricate physical interaction. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Enhanced Dexterity in Contact-Rich Tasks:** FoAR significantly improves robot capabilities in challenging contact-rich tasks like wiping, peeling, and chopping. These tasks are prevalent in domestic, industrial, and service robotics applications. By enabling robots to perform such operations with high precision and consistency, FoAR paves the way for more versatile and autonomous robot systems.

2. **Intelligent Multi-Modal Fusion:** The core innovation of dynamically balancing the contribution of force/torque data through a future contact predictor is highly impactful. It solves a long-standing problem in multi-modal learning—how to effectively use sparsely activated or noisy sensor data. This approach prevents irrelevant or noisy information from degrading performance during non-contact phases while maximizing the utility of critical contact feedback. This methodology could be transferable to other multi-modal systems where specific sensor modalities are only relevant during certain task phases.

3. **Efficiency with Simple Control:** Demonstrating superior performance using only simple end-effector position control, without the need for complex, explicitly tuned force/impedance control strategies, is a major breakthrough. This simplifies the control stack, reduces implementation complexity, and makes it easier for researchers and practitioners to deploy advanced contact-rich manipulation policies on standard robotic platforms. It also indicates that high-level policy learning can effectively abstract away the need for low-level, traditionally complex force control.

4. **Robustness to Dynamic Disturbances:** FoAR's proven robustness under unexpected environmental changes (e.g., rewriting on a wiped surface, moving the object) is crucial for real-world deployment. Robots operating outside controlled lab environments must contend with uncertainties. FoAR's ability to adapt and maintain performance under such conditions increases its practical applicability.

5. **Data Efficiency:** Achieving high performance with only 50 demonstrations per task highlights FoAR's data efficiency. This is a critical factor in real-world robot learning, where collecting large datasets can be time-consuming and expensive. This makes the approach more accessible and scalable.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
Despite its advancements, FoAR has identified limitations that also suggest promising future research directions:
- **Static Force/Torque Thresholds:** The reliance on static thresholds for force/torque detection, while effective in the evaluated tasks, might struggle in more complex or varied environments where optimal contact forces could dynamically change. Future work could explore learning adaptive thresholds or integrating them more deeply into the policy.
- **Simple Position Control:** While a strength, the current reliance on simple position control might limit adaptability in scenarios requiring highly nuanced compliance or impedance behaviors. Future research will explore leveraging more advanced control strategies, such as learning compliance control or hybrid force/position control, to further enhance performance and versatility.
- **Scaling to More Complex Robots:** Extending FoAR to dual-arm robots, humanoid robots, or dexterous hands is a natural next step. This would unlock capabilities for even more complex and human-like contact-rich manipulations, though it would also introduce new challenges in coordination and control.

In conclusion, FoAR significantly advances the state-of-the-art in contact-rich robotic manipulation by intelligently integrating force/torque perception with visual inputs. Its novel dynamic fusion mechanism and reactive control strategy offer a robust, efficient, and highly effective solution for tasks that demand precise physical interaction, paving the way for more dexterous and autonomous robots in diverse real-world applications.
