# Model-Based Runtime Monitoring with Interactive Imitation Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at The University of Texas at Austin developed a model-based runtime monitoring algorithm for interactive imitation learning that leverages human interventions as implicit risk cues to anticipate robot failures. This approach achieved 32% higher task success in simulation and 40% higher on hardware compared to baselines, while also enabling 16% more efficient use of human supervision.

## Problem

- Learning-based robotic systems often lack generalization and robustness, hindering their deployment in real-world, high-stakes applications.
- Interactive imitation learning (IIL) demands continuous human supervision, leading to a prohibitive workload for human operators.
- Current runtime monitoring methods either fail to anticipate future errors (Out-of-Distribution detection) or require explicit, often unsafe, failure data for training (supervised methods).

## Method

- A **Sirius-Runtime** model-based ==runtime monitoring== algorithm is integrated into an ==interactive imitation learning== (IIL) framework to continuously learn and predict failures.
- A ==Conditional Variational Autoencoder== (cVAE) dynamics model simulates future robot trajectories in a latent space to proactively anticipate undesirable states.
- A ==failure classifier== is trained using ==human interventions as implicit labels== for risky or undesirable states, bypassing the need for explicit failure examples.

## Results

- The proposed method achieved an average of **32%** higher task success rates in simulation and **40%** higher on physical hardware compared to existing baselines.
- It demonstrated **16%** more efficient utilization of human workload (Normalized Return On Human Effort) compared to state-of-the-art interactive imitation learning baselines.
- Unit tests showed the error predictor had higher **IOU** and lower **DCI** values, indicating its predictions aligned more closely with human-labeled failure regions and intervention timings.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Human interventions, even without explicit failure labels, provide sufficient implicit supervisory signals to train a failure predictor aligned with human risk assessment.
> - Utilizing a learned dynamics model for future latent state prediction allows for proactive detection of potential failures, enabling timely human intervention.
> - The combination of out-of-distribution (OOD) detection and an intervention-informed failure classifier enhances robustness by addressing both unfamiliar situations and subtle, fine-grained error patterns.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of learning-based robotics systems has advanced significantly in recent years, demonstrating capabilities in various research settings. However, these systems frequently encounter challenges related to generalization and robustness, which impede their widespread deployment in practical, high-stakes applications. Addressing potential failures during operation is a critical requirement for rendering these systems suitable for real-world tasks.

A shift in the conventional "train-then-deploy" paradigm is observed, moving towards "learning on the job" models involving human-robot teams. Interactive imitation learning (IIL) is a framework that supports this shift, where human operators supervise robot execution, provide interventions in challenging situations, and robots continuously learn from the collected deployment data. While IIL holds promise for continuous model improvement, existing approaches often demand constant human supervision and immediate feedback, which can lead to prohibitive human workloads in realistic application domains.

Within this landscape, runtime monitoring mechanisms have emerged as a method to mitigate the human supervision burden by enabling robots to autonomously monitor their performance and predict potential errors. Current runtime monitoring approaches primarily fall into two categories:

1. **Unsupervised Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Detection:** These methods identify errors by assessing deviations from known data distributions. They may not accurately capture complex error patterns and typically only detect issues based on current system states, lacking the capability to anticipate future failures.
2. **Supervised Failure Detection:** These approaches train models to classify normal versus failure states or to estimate risk values, often derived from actual failure experiences. While more accurate in identifying error patterns and preempting failures, they critically depend on the availability of explicit failure data. Acquiring such data in deployed systems can be unsafe and impractical, particularly in safety-critical environments where failures can have severe consequences.

This research addresses the limitations of existing interactive imitation learning by introducing a runtime monitoring algorithm designed to detect anomalies and anticipate failures proactively. Unlike prior OOD methods, it aims to foresee future failures. Distinct from supervised failure detection methods, it avoids the necessity of explicit failure experiences for training by leveraging human interventions as implicit cues of risk or undesirable states. By integrating this monitoring capability within an IIL framework, the work seeks to reduce human workload over time while ensuring reliable task execution, thereby contributing to the development of more practical and trustworthy robotic systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable a robot to self-monitor and detect errors during task execution, thereby reducing the dependency on constant human supervision and facilitating reliable operation within interactive imitation learning (IIL) frameworks.

This overarching goal is driven by several specific motivations and aims:

1. **Addressing Generalization and Robustness Challenges:** Current learning-based robotic systems often struggle with performing robustly in varied, real-world conditions. This work aims to enhance their reliability by providing a mechanism to detect and mitigate potential failures during deployment.
2. **Reducing Human Workload in IIL:** Interactive imitation learning requires continuous human oversight and intervention, leading to significant human effort. A core motivation is to develop a system that can proactively identify situations requiring human attention, thus minimizing the need for constant monitoring and making IIL more practical for long-term deployments.
3. **Anticipating Future Failures:** Existing unsupervised out-of-distribution (OOD) detection methods are limited to identifying deviations in current states and typically cannot predict future failures. A key objective is to develop a model-based monitoring algorithm that can simulate future action outcomes and detect high-risk states preemptively, allowing for earlier intervention.
4. **Learning Without Explicit Failure Data:** Supervised failure detection methods often require explicit examples of failures for training, which can be dangerous or costly to collect in deployed robotic systems. This research aims to develop an intervention-informed approach that leverages implicit cues from human interventions (i.e., moments when humans perceive risk or take corrective action) to train an error predictor, avoiding the need for direct failure experiences.
5. **Continuous Improvement and Trustworthy Deployment:** The system is designed to continually update its monitoring capabilities and policy from deployment data, integrating seamlessly into a human-robot team framework. This enables the robot to adapt to changing behaviors and ensure trustworthy operation over extended periods.
6. **Unifying OOD and Failure Detection:** The work aims to integrate OOD detection and failure classification within a single, model-based framework to enhance data efficiency and overall performance, addressing the limitations of prior work that often uses isolated detection systems.

In summary, the motivation stems from the recognized gap between the capabilities of advanced robot learning methods and their practical deployability, specifically targeting the challenges of continuous human supervision and the difficulty of acquiring safe failure data for training robust error detection systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology introduces a model-based runtime monitoring algorithm integrated into an interactive imitation learning (IIL) framework. This approach is designed to continually learn from human-robot team experiences, detect system anomalies, and anticipate failures proactively. The core design principles involve a model-based predictive approach and an intervention-informed learning mechanism.

**System Overview:**
The system operates within an iterative human-in-the-loop learning and deployment framework. An initial policy and monitoring model are bootstrapped from human demonstrations. In subsequent deployment rounds, the robot executes its policy, while the runtime monitor actively detects errors. If an out-of-distribution (OOD) state or potential failure is predicted, the system solicits human monitoring. Humans can then intervene if they deem the robot's state undesirable or risky. The data collected from these deployments, including human interventions, is used to continuously retrain and update the models for the next round.

**Model Components:**
The system comprises four main learnable components, collectively denoted as W = (Eγ, Tψ, πθ, Cλ):

1. **Observation Encoder (Eγ):** This component processes raw sensor inputs, such as image observations and robot proprioceptive states, encoding them into feature vectors that represent latent states. This creates a shared latent space for the other model components. The policy, dynamics model, and failure classifier all operate within this common latent space.
2. **Dynamics Model (Tψ):** Implemented as a Conditional Variational Autoencoder (cVAE), this model predicts the next latent state (z') given the current latent state (z) and the action (a). By operating in the latent space, it enables faster simulation of future trajectories compared to predicting raw observations. The stochastic nature of the cVAE allows for sampling multiple possible future predictions, which helps in modeling potential outcomes more accurately.
3. **Policy (πθ):** This component is jointly trained with the dynamics model and also operates within the shared latent space. It is implemented as a behavioral cloning (BC) policy, specifically a BC-RNN with ResNet encoders, to map latent states to actions. Sharing the latent space enables the policy to be rolled out within the dynamics model for future state prediction.
4. **Failure Classifier (Cλ):** After the policy and dynamics model are trained, this classifier is trained on the *frozen* latent space. Instead of relying on explicit failure examples, it leverages human interventions as supervisory signals. The research observes that pre-intervention states (states immediately preceding a human intervention) often reflect the human's real-time assessment of an undesirable or risky situation. The classifier is designed for a three-class classification: failure state, normal rollout, and intervention state. This distinction aims to provide a more informative learning signal, recognizing that human interventions and system failures often occur in similar contexts. It is implemented with an LSTM, taking a history of states and actions as input, and trained using a cross-entropy loss with balanced sampling.

**Training Objectives:**
The training process involves a combined objective:
- The policy (πθ) and dynamics model (Tψ) are trained jointly. The objective includes a maximum likelihood behavior cloning (BC) loss for the policy and VAE loss terms for the dynamics model. The VAE loss incorporates Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence and a reconstruction loss for the next state latent embedding, conditional on the current state and action.
- The failure classifier (Cλ) is trained separately on the frozen latent space using human intervention data as labels, optimizing a cross-entropy loss function.

**Runtime Monitoring Operation:**
During task execution, the runtime monitor operates as follows:
1. **Future Rollouts:** The system uses the cVAE dynamics model to generate N (set to 200) future policy rollouts, each extending l (set to 10) steps into the future within the latent space. Multiple future predictions are generated due to the stochasticity of the cVAE.
2. **Two-Level Evaluation:** Each predicted future state is evaluated by two complementary modules:
 - **Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Detection:** This module assesses whether a predicted latent state is OOD by calculating its nearest neighbor distance to the latent embeddings of in-distribution data (expert demonstrations). If the mean distance across multiple imaginary futures exceeds a threshold α (set to 0.06), the state is flagged as OOD.
 - **Failure Detection:** If a state is not flagged as OOD, the failure classifier (Cλ) is employed to predict the probability of a future failure. A state is classified as a failure if the averaged failure classification result across multiple future rollouts exceeds a threshold β (set to 0.6). To ensure temporal consistency and reduce false positives, human supervision is solicited only if K (set to 5) of the past P (set to 10) states have been identified as failures.
3. **Human Solicitation:** If either OOD or failure is detected, the system requests human monitoring.

This model-based approach, combined with intervention-informed learning and a dual detection mechanism, allows for preemptive identification of potential issues, aiming to reduce human workload and ensure system reliability during long-term deployment.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates the proposed model-based runtime monitoring method across two simulated tasks (Nut Assembly and Threading in robosuite) and two real-world tasks (Coffee Pod Packing and Gear Assembly on a Franka Emika Panda robot arm). The evaluation focuses on system-level performance and unit tests for error predictors, comparing the method against ThriftyDAgger, MoMaRT, and PATO baselines.

**A. System-Level Performance**

The system-level evaluation assessed two aspects: collaborative policy performance (overall task success rate) and optimized use of human input (Normalized Return On Human Effort – ROHE).

1. **Collaborative Policy Performance (Success Rate):**
 - The proposed method consistently demonstrated higher success rates compared to all baselines across all tasks and deployment rounds (Table II).
 - For example, in the Nut Assembly task, the method achieved 98.5% success in Round 2 and 100.0% in Round 3, while ThriftyDAgger achieved 86.6%, 90.0%, and 88.1% across the rounds.
 - In the real-world Coffee Pod Packing task, the method reached 83.5% (R2) and 92.5% (R3) success, significantly outperforming ThriftyDAgger's 23.1% (R1), 50.9% (R2), and 57.5% (R3).
 - Overall, the method achieved an average of 32% higher success rates in simulation and 40% higher success rates on physical hardware compared to the baselines.

2. **Optimized Use of Human Input (Normalized ROHE):**
 - Normalized ROHE measures task outcomes per unit of human effort. The proposed method generally exhibited lower ROHE in Round 1 because it initiated with full human monitoring to collect intervention labels for warm-starting the failure classifier. However, in subsequent rounds (Round 2 and 3), its ROHE improved considerably, often surpassing the baselines (Figure 3).
 - This improvement indicates that the method became more effective at identifying critical errors and soliciting human assistance precisely when needed, leading to more efficient human workload utilization. The paper reports an average of 16% more efficient use of human workload utilization than state-of-the-art interactive imitation learning baselines.
 - The superior performance is attributed to the model-based design, which allows for timely interventions through future latent state prediction, and the intervention-informed failure classifier.
 - In contrast, baselines like ThriftyDAgger and PATO, which use ensemble policies for OOD detection, sometimes generated false positives due to task uncertainty (e.g., gripper actions). ThriftyDAgger's risk Q-function was observed to sometimes increase with task progress, leading to false positives early and false negatives late in tasks. MoMaRT, relying on VAE reconstruction error, struggled with fine-grained manipulation tasks where subtle visual changes indicate failure, leading to false negatives.

**B. Unit Testing Error Predictors**

To systematically evaluate the accuracy of error predictors in capturing human risk assessment, unit tests were conducted using two metrics: IOU (Intersection over Union) and DCI (Distance to Closest Intervention).

1. **IOU (Intersection over Union):** This metric quantifies the overlap between predicted failure regions and human-labeled failure regions.
 - The proposed method consistently yielded higher IOU values across all tasks (Table III), indicating a greater correspondence between its predictions and human judgment. For instance, in Nut Assembly, the method achieved an IOU of 0.279 compared to ThriftyDAgger's 0.153. In Threading, the IOU was 0.317, significantly higher than ThriftyDAgger's 0.044.

2. **DCI (Distance to Closest Intervention):** This metric measures the temporal distance between predicted failures and actual human interventions, penalizing predictions further away from true interventions.
 - The proposed method achieved lower DCI values across all tasks (Table III), signifying that its predicted failure events were temporally closer to human interventions. For example, Nut Assembly showed a DCI of 89.1 for the method versus 94.7 for ThriftyDAgger, and Threading recorded 30.1 versus 60.7.
 - This superior performance in unit tests is primarily attributed to the intervention-informed approach, where human interventions are directly used as supervisory signals. This allows the model to learn and align with human risk assessment, a capability that other methods struggle to capture with metrics less directly related to human judgment.

**C. Ablation Study**

An ablation study investigated the individual contributions of the OOD detection and failure detection modules.
- The full proposed method (Ours Full) outperformed configurations using OOD detection only (Ours OOD) or failure detection only (Ours Failure) in terms of overall collaborative policy performance (Figure 4).
- This suggests that the two modules play complementary roles: OOD detection is effective at identifying unfamiliar states in novel scenarios at a coarser level, while failure detection is adept at identifying subtle, fine-grained issues in critical bottleneck states that OOD detection might miss (Figure 5). Their coordinated function contributes to reliable runtime monitoring.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a model-based runtime monitoring algorithm that significantly contributes to the field of robotic learning by addressing critical limitations in current interactive imitation learning (IIL) frameworks. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Enhanced Trustworthiness and Reliability:** By enabling robots to proactively detect anomalies and anticipate failures, the method improves the overall trustworthiness and reliability of learning-based robotic systems. This is particularly important for high-stakes or safety-critical applications where undetected errors can have severe consequences.
2. **Reduced Human Workload in Human-Robot Teaming:** A key contribution is the reduction of human workload associated with continuous supervision in IIL. By allowing robots to self-monitor and request human assistance only when necessary, the method makes long-term human-robot deployments more practical and sustainable, moving beyond the need for constant human oversight.
3. **Safer Data Collection and Learning:** The intervention-informed approach, which leverages human interventions as implicit cues for risk rather than requiring explicit failure experiences, provides a safer and more practical way to train error predictors. This bypasses the need for collecting potentially catastrophic failure data, which is often infeasible or undesirable in real-world settings.
4. **Proactive Failure Preemption:** The model-based design, utilizing a learned dynamics model to simulate future action outcomes in a latent space, allows for the prediction of failures *before* they manifest. This preemptive capability distinguishes it from many existing OOD detection methods that only react to current state deviations, enabling more timely human intervention.
5. **Adaptive Learning from Human Expertise:** By continuously updating its error predictor and policy based on deployment data and human interventions, the system can adapt to changing operational conditions and policy behaviors. It effectively learns from human risk assessment, aligning its failure detection mechanisms with human judgment.
6. **Broader Applicability:** While demonstrated in quasi-static manipulation tasks, the underlying principles of model-based predictive monitoring and intervention-informed learning could be extended to a wider range of robotic platforms, more dynamic environments, and more complex tasks, facilitating the deployment of intelligent robots in diverse domains.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The authors acknowledge certain limitations that also indicate directions for future research. The experiments were conducted with a single human subject per task, which may not fully represent the behavior of a broader human population. Future work could involve large-scale studies with diverse human participants to better understand human intervention behavior and its impact on the method's ability to capture failure modes. Additionally, the current work primarily focuses on quasi-static manipulation tasks where recovery through teleoperation is relatively straightforward. Extending the system's applicability to more dynamic environments, a wider variety of robot platforms, and more complex task structures represents a significant area for further development.
