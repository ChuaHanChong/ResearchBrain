# Faster-WAM: Efficient Inference-Time Future Conditioning for Robust World Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Faster-WAM introduces an efficient architecture for Vision-Language-Action models that explicitly integrates future visual information during inference for robust robot manipulation. It achieves a 2.21x speedup over prior robust World Action Models (WAMs) while improving average success rates to 73.6% on LIBERO-Plus out-of-distribution tasks and 71.1% in real-world OOD scenarios.

## Problem

- Existing World Action Models (WAMs) face a dilemma: robust models (Joint-WAMs) are computationally expensive at inference, while efficient alternatives (Fast-WAMs) sacrifice robustness by removing future representations during inference.
- The role of inference-time future representations in WAM generalization, especially under distribution shifts, was an open question.
- The high computational cost of robust WAMs limits their practical deployment in real-time robotic control.

## Method

- **Faster-WAM** generates a reusable future visual context in a single, one-pass evaluation of a video expert before action generation begins, contrasting with iterative joint video-action denoising.
- It employs ==SparseMoT==, a sparse ==Mixture-of-Transformers== architecture, to restrict video-action interaction to a compact subset of network stages, enabling lightweight action-only refinement in between.
- ==Interval KV-Fusion== aggregates multi-depth future representations within each interaction interval, providing richer temporal information without increasing attention complexity for the action pathway.

## Results

- Faster-WAM achieved an inference latency of **252.95 ms**, representing a **2.21x** speedup over Joint-WAM (559.84 ms) and outperforming Fast-WAM (320.97 ms).
- It demonstrated enhanced out-of-distribution (OOD) robustness, achieving **73.6%** average success on LIBERO-Plus and **71.1%** in real-world OOD tasks, consistently outperforming both Joint-WAM and Fast-WAM.
- The model maintained strong in-distribution performance with **99.0%** average success on LIBERO and **92.6%** on the large-scale RoboTwin 2.0 bimanual manipulation benchmark.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Inference-time future conditioning is critical for the robustness and generalization of World Action Models, particularly under out-of-distribution settings, rather than merely serving as a training signal.
> - Efficient integration of future visual context into action policies can be achieved by generating it once and reusing it sparsely, avoiding the computational cost of dense, iterative video-action denoising.
> - Sparse interaction and multi-depth information fusion mechanisms (SparseMoT and Interval KV-Fusion) enable a superior balance between robustness and inference efficiency in WAMs.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot intelligence has seen progress with Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, which enable robots to perform tasks based on visual observations and language instructions. However, many existing VLA approaches primarily predict actions from current observations without explicitly modeling how the environment will evolve over time.

World Action Models (WAMs) emerged to address this limitation by augmenting action prediction with future visual modeling. These models learn scene dynamics under robot interaction, providing policies with temporal representations that extend beyond the immediate observation. Within WAMs, two primary approaches have been explored:

- **Joint-WAMs:** These models couple future video generation and action prediction, often through shared denoising processes. This allows the action prediction branch to access evolving future representations during inference, which can lead to robust performance. However, this dense interaction and repeated execution of the video generation branch results in substantial computational overhead, making them resource-intensive at inference time. Examples include work by Li et al. (2026b) and Bi et al. (2025).

- **Efficient Alternatives (e.g., Fast-WAM, Light-WAM):** These approaches prioritize efficiency by utilizing future modeling primarily as a training signal and removing explicit future representations during inference. While this design significantly reduces computational costs and can achieve competitive performance in environments similar to the training data, a fundamental limitation has been identified: policies may lose critical temporal information needed to generalize to unseen or perturbed environments, potentially compromising robustness under distribution shifts. Fast-WAM (Yuan et al. 2026) and Light-WAM (Li et al. 2026c) represent this direction.

This paper addresses an unresolved question within the WAM landscape: whether future predictions are merely useful as a training signal or if future representations provide essential information during inference. The authors identify a dilemma where current WAMs either sacrifice efficiency for robustness (Joint-WAMs) or efficiency for potential loss of temporal reasoning benefits (Fast-WAMs).

The presented work, Faster-WAM, positions itself as a development that navigates this dilemma. It aims to demonstrate that inference-time future conditioning is indeed critical for generalization, particularly under distribution shifts, while simultaneously achieving efficiency. It differentiates from concurrent work like Efficient-WAM (Li et al. 2026a), which focuses on compressing future generation, by instead concentrating on redesigning the interaction between pre-generated future representations and action generation. Faster-WAM's contribution is to establish a framework that efficiently preserves these future representations through sparse and selective interaction, thereby aiming for both robustness and reduced computational cost.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The motivation for this research stems from an observed dilemma in existing World Action Models (WAMs) regarding the role and computational cost of future representations during inference. The authors initiate their investigation by posing a core question: Are future predictions merely useful as a training signal, or do future representations provide essential information during inference?

Through empirical evaluation, the authors observed that removing future representations at inference time substantially compromises the robustness of WAMs, particularly under distribution shifts. Specifically, they note that Fast-WAM, which omits future conditioning during inference for efficiency, exhibits a significant performance degradation in out-of-distribution (OOD) settings compared to Joint-WAM, which retains these representations. This observation indicates that future representations are not merely an auxiliary training objective but serve as a source of generalizable temporal knowledge, critical for handling novel environments.

This finding motivated a new design principle for WAMs: future representations should be preserved during inference, but their interaction with action prediction must be selective and efficient to avoid prohibitive computational costs.

Based on this principle, the key objectives for developing Faster-WAM were formulated:

1. **Validate the criticality of inference-time future conditioning:** To empirically demonstrate that explicitly retaining future-aware representations during the inference phase significantly improves WAM generalization and robustness, especially under distribution shifts, beyond their role as a training objective.
2. **Develop an efficient future-conditioning framework:** To create a WAM architecture that maintains access to future representations during inference without incurring the high computational costs associated with dense, iterative video-action interaction found in Joint-WAMs.
3. **Introduce mechanisms for sparse and efficient video-action interaction:**
 - **SparseMoT:** To reduce redundant computation by concentrating video-action interaction at a compact subset of network stages, allowing for lightweight action-only refinement in between these interactions. This aims to avoid the expense of repeatedly executing the video branch and performing dense interactions at every action denoising step.
 - **Interval KV-Fusion:** To aggregate multi-depth future representations within each interaction interval. This mechanism aims to provide richer temporal information to the action pathway without increasing the attention complexity, which would otherwise occur if all intermediate representations were concatenated.
4. **Achieve a superior performance-efficiency trade-off:** The overarching objective was to develop a WAM that achieves state-of-the-art performance on various manipulation benchmarks, including those with distribution shifts, while simultaneously offering improved inference efficiency compared to existing WAM architectures.

By addressing these objectives, the research aims to resolve the dilemma between robustness and efficiency in WAMs, thereby advancing the capabilities of robotic manipulation in diverse and challenging real-world scenarios.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Faster-WAM is designed for language-conditioned visuomotor control, where a policy predicts an action chunk (A_t) based on a current image (o_t), a language instruction (l), and a proprioceptive state (s_t). The methodology focuses on efficiently incorporating future visual information into the action policy through a novel interaction framework.

**Problem Formulation Context:**
Existing WAMs are characterized by how they derive the internal visual representation (R_v_t). Joint-WAMs iteratively update future-video and action states, leading to an evolving R_v,joint_t,k that is recomputed at each step. Fast-WAM, in contrast, constructs R_v,fast_t from only the current observation and language, without explicit future temporal slots at inference. Faster-WAM introduces a fixed future-aware interface R_v_t, constructed in one video-expert pass, which is then reused sparsely during action generation.

**Overall Framework:**
Faster-WAM integrates a video expert, initialized from a pretrained video generator (e.g., Wan2.2-5B), with an action expert. This coupling is achieved through a Mixture-of-Transformers (MoT) architecture comprising `L` aligned stages. Language and proprioceptive signals condition both experts.

**One-pass Future Conditioning:**
The core of Faster-WAM's efficiency lies in generating a reusable future context.
1. **Future Latent Generation:** Unlike Joint-WAMs that iteratively denoise future videos, Faster-WAM processes noisy future slots. Both branches are formulated with flow matching, where τ=0 represents clean data and τ=1 represents Gaussian noise. The video expert processes future latents (Z_t,τ_v), initialized from Gaussian noise (ϵ_v_t) at the noisy endpoint (τ_v=1), alongside the clean current-frame anchor (z_0_t) and language. This process is designed to encode future-aware cues by reasoning about plausible scene dynamics without reconstructing a complete video rollout.
2. **K/V Hierarchy Creation:** From each video attention block at flow time τ_v, the key (K_v_t,τ_v,j) and value (V_v_t,τ_v,j) projections are retained. These form a raw K/V hierarchy, C_v_t,τ_v, where token layout and shape are consistent across depths.
3. **Asymmetric Attention:** Crucially, asymmetric attention is employed: future video slots can attend to the current frame anchor and other future slots, but the anchor cannot attend to future slots. In cross-expert interaction, action queries can read video features, but video queries cannot read action tokens. This ensures that the video hierarchy remains independent of the evolving action trajectory, allowing it to be constructed once and cached before action integration begins. At inference, setting Z_t,1 to Gaussian noise yields the fixed raw hierarchy C_v_t,1 in a single video pass.

**SparseMoT (Sparse Mixture-of-Transformers):**
To reduce the computational cost of dense cross-branch attention, SparseMoT restricts interaction.
1. **Interaction Set:** Instead of interaction at every aligned stage (`L` stages), interaction is limited to a compact subset of stages, `J = {j_1, ..., j_M}`, selected, for example, at a fixed layer stride (e.g., every 4 stages, resulting in `M=8` interactions for `L=30` action layers). This set `J` is reused at every action flow step.
2. **Selective Attention:** At each selected stage `j_m ∈ J`, the action-token query (Q_a_jm) attends to a combined key/value pair consisting of the action's own K/V projections (K_a_jm, V_a_jm) and a fused video K/V pair (`bK_v_jm, bV_v_jm`), which summarizes the preceding video depth interval.
3. **Action-Only Refinement:** For stages not in `J` (`j ∉ J`), only action-only self-attention and residual/feed-forward updates are performed. This mechanism propagates previously injected future information through the action state without incurring the cost of re-reading video K/V at every stage.

**Interval KV-Fusion:**
This mechanism addresses how to consolidate multi-depth video information for SparseMoT.
1. **Interval Assignment:** Each selected interaction stage `j_m` is assigned a preceding interval `I_m = {j_m-1 + 1, ..., j_m}`.
2. **Weighted Summation:** Within each interval `I_m`, a softmax-normalized weighted sum (`W_fuse_m,j`) of the stage-wise video K/V pairs (`K_v_j, V_v_j`) is computed. This produces a single fused K/V pair (`bK_v_jm, bV_v_jm`) for `j_m`.
3. **Preservation of Structure:** This weighted summation combines information from multiple depths without increasing the token sequence length or attention complexity for the action pathway, which is critical for efficiency.
4. **Reusable Interface:** The `M` fused pairs form the compact action-facing interface `bC_v_t,τ_v`. At inference, `bC_v_t,1` is generated once and supplied to the action expert as a reusable future-aware context throughout action integration.

**Joint Training:**
Faster-WAM utilizes a flow-matching objective to jointly learn the video and action flow fields.
1. **Independent Sampling:** Video (τ_v) and action (τ_a) flow times are sampled independently, along with Gaussian noise (ϵ_v_t, ϵ_a_t).
2. **Flow Prediction:** The video expert predicts the video flow `bu_v_t` for future slots and generates `C_v_t,τ_v`. Interval KV-Fusion converts this into `bC_v_t,τ_v`, which then conditions the action-flow predictor `F_a` via SparseMoT to predict `bu_a_t`.
3. **Loss Function:** A joint objective `L` comprises weighted mean-squared-error losses for both video and action flow predictions, encouraging the fused hierarchy to retain control-relevant information while supervising future-video dynamics.

**Efficient Inference:**
The inference process is designed for maximum efficiency:
1. **Initialization:** Future slots and the action state are initialized from Gaussian noise.
2. **One-time Video Expert Evaluation:** The video expert is evaluated only *once* at `τ_v=1` to generate the raw K/V hierarchy.
3. **Cache Construction:** Interval KV-Fusion is applied to this raw hierarchy to produce the cached, action-facing interface `bC_v_t,1`.
4. **Action Denoising with Reuse:** For all subsequent action-flow steps, the action expert reuses this cached context via SparseMoT without further updating or decoding the future-video latents. This effectively replaces `N` dense joint video-action evaluations with a single video-side pass followed by `N` sparse, cache-conditioned action evaluations.

**Implementation Details:**
The model is built on Wan2.2-5B, utilizing its pretrained video DiT, text encoder, and VAE. The action Transformer has 30 layers and a hidden width of 1024. Policies predict 32 actions per step, with visual sequences of nine frames sampled at a temporal stride of four. Observations from different cameras are spatially assembled. Interaction occurs every 4 stages, resulting in `M=8` selected stages. AdamW optimizer is used for training.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of Faster-WAM was conducted across various benchmarks, encompassing in-distribution tasks, out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios, and real-world robot manipulation, with a focus on success rate and inference latency. Controlled comparisons were made against Joint-WAM and Fast-WAM, implemented with identical underlying components and training protocols to isolate the impact of inference-time future conditioning and video-action interaction.

**In-Distribution Performance:**
- **LIBERO:** Faster-WAM achieved an average success rate of 99.0%, outperforming both Joint-WAM (98.5%) and Fast-WAM (97.6%). This indicates strong performance in standard, in-distribution language-conditioned manipulation tasks.
- **RoboTwin 2.0:** On this large-scale bimanual manipulation benchmark, Faster-WAM achieved an average success rate of 92.6% (92.8% in clean settings, 92.3% in randomized settings). This performance surpassed the controlled WAM baselines (Joint-WAM at 90.6%, Fast-WAM at 91.9%) and several pretrained VLA models (e.g., LingBot-VA at 92.2%).

**Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Robustness:**
- **LIBERO-Plus:** This benchmark specifically tests robustness under seven types of distribution shifts.
 - Fast-WAM, despite its high in-distribution performance on standard LIBERO (97.6%), showed a significant drop to 49.1% average success rate on LIBERO-Plus.
 - Joint-WAM, by preserving future representations, maintained a higher average success rate of 66.3%.
 - Faster-WAM further improved this, achieving an average success rate of 73.6%. It consistently outperformed Fast-WAM across all seven distribution shifts and surpassed Joint-WAM on six of the seven shifts, demonstrating enhanced OOD generalization.
- **Real-World Evaluation:**
 - Under standard conditions, Faster-WAM achieved a 95.8% average success rate across four dual-arm manipulation tasks, exceeding Joint-WAM (90.8%) and Fast-WAM (88.3%).
 - In OOD real-world conditions (e.g., novel backgrounds, altered lighting, unseen distractor objects for "Pick Strawberries"), Faster-WAM achieved an average OOD success rate of 71.1%. This was substantially higher than Fast-WAM (45.6%) and Joint-WAM (55.6%), confirming the robustness observed in simulation extends to real-world scenarios.

**Inference Latency:**
- Measurements on an NVIDIA L20 GPU showed that Joint-WAM had an inference latency of 559.84 ms due to repeated video-action denoising.
- Fast-WAM, which removes future temporal slots, achieved 320.97 ms.
- Faster-WAM achieved a latency of 252.95 ms, representing a 2.21x speedup over Joint-WAM. Notably, Faster-WAM was also faster than Fast-WAM, despite maintaining future representations.
- A latency breakdown revealed that while Faster-WAM's one-time visual context preparation was slightly higher (43.04 ms vs. 27.67 ms for Fast-WAM), its SparseMoT mechanism significantly reduced action-denoising latency (192.11 ms vs. 276.56 ms), which compensated for the initial overhead and led to an overall faster inference.

**Ablation Studies:**
- **Future Conditioning Ablation:** A "Current-Only Faster-WAM" variant, which retained SparseMoT and Interval KV-Fusion but lacked explicit future temporal slots, achieved 51.00% OOD success, comparable to Fast-WAM. The restoration of future temporal slots in the full Faster-WAM model increased the average success rate to 73.57%, confirming the contribution of inference-time future-aware representations to OOD robustness.
- **Component Ablation:**
 - Removing Interval KV-Fusion from Faster-WAM reduced the average OOD success rate from 73.57% to 69.99%.
 - Further replacing SparseMoT with dense interaction resulted in 69.78% OOD success.
 - Reverting to iterative joint denoising (equivalent to Joint-WAM) yielded 66.27%. These results support the complementary roles of multi-depth KV fusion, sparse video-action interaction, and reusable future context.
- **Interaction Sparsity Ablation:** Varying the interaction stride (how often video-action interaction occurs) showed an optimal performance at a stride of 4 (73.57%). Both denser interaction (stride 1, 69.78%) and sparser interaction (stride 7, 71.05%; stride 14, 70.05%) yielded lower average OOD success rates. This suggests a balance is required for effective future context integration.
- **Effect of Video Denoising Steps (Appendix):** Investigating the number of video denoising steps for generating the future context showed that a single pass at the noisy endpoint yielded the highest OOD success (73.57%). Increasing the number of video denoising steps generally led to a decline in performance, suggesting that explicit, full reconstruction of future video is not necessary for providing control-relevant future cues.

These findings collectively demonstrate that Faster-WAM achieves a superior balance between robustness and efficiency, establishing its effectiveness across diverse robotic manipulation tasks and challenging distribution shifts.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work presented in "Faster-WAM" introduces a shift in the understanding and design of World Action Models (WAMs) for robot manipulation, with implications for both academic research and practical applications.

**Reframing the Role of Future Representations:** The primary significance of this work lies in its empirically supported claim that inference-time future conditioning is critical for the generalization and robustness of WAMs, particularly under distribution shifts. This reframes future modeling from being merely an auxiliary training objective to an essential component of the inference process. This finding challenges the efficiency-driven paradigm of removing future representations at inference and advocates for their selective and optimized inclusion.

**Enhanced Robustness and Generalization:** By enabling robust performance under various distribution shifts (e.g., camera viewpoints, robot initial states, lighting, backgrounds, noise, object layouts) in both simulated (LIBERO-Plus) and real-world environments, Faster-WAM addresses a key challenge in deploying robots in dynamic and unstructured settings. The ability of a robot policy to generalize to unseen conditions is crucial for increasing its practical utility and reducing the need for extensive, domain-specific retraining.

**Improved Efficiency for Real-Time Applications:** Faster-WAM demonstrates that enhanced robustness does not necessarily come at the expense of computational efficiency. By achieving a 2.21x speedup over Joint-WAM while simultaneously improving performance, Faster-WAM makes future-conditioned policies more viable for real-time robotic control, where low latency is often a critical requirement. This balance of performance and efficiency has the potential to accelerate the adoption of advanced WAMs in real-world robotics.

**Novel Architectural Contributions:** The introduction of SparseMoT and Interval KV-Fusion provides concrete architectural patterns for efficiently integrating future context into action generation. SparseMoT allows for targeted interaction, reducing redundant computation, while Interval KV-Fusion effectively aggregates multi-depth information without increasing attention complexity. These mechanisms offer a blueprint for designing more efficient and context-aware models beyond the specific WAM application.

**Inspiration for Future Research:** The paper opens several avenues for future work. The exploration of learning-based strategies for dynamically determining video-action interaction stages (instead of fixed strides) could lead to more adaptive and optimized systems. Furthermore, the mention of graph compilation and custom CUDA kernels highlights opportunities for system-level acceleration, which could further improve the efficiency of Faster-WAM and similar architectures.

**Broader Applicability:** The core principle of efficient, selective inference-time temporal conditioning extends beyond WAMs. It could influence the design of other temporal models in fields requiring anticipatory reasoning and robust decision-making under constrained computational resources, such as autonomous driving, predictive maintenance, or even medical diagnostics.

In summary, Faster-WAM contributes to the field by demonstrating a method to achieve both robust generalization and high inference efficiency in robot manipulation. Its findings and proposed architecture offer valuable insights for the development of more capable and deployable robotic systems.
