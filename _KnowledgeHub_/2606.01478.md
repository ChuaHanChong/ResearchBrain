# Crazyflow: An Accurate, GPU-Accelerated, Differentiable Drone Simulator in JAX

> [!summary] Summary
> Crazyflow is a JAX-based drone simulator that fuses full-fidelity quadrotor physics, motor dynamics, and an emulated onboard controller stack into a single JIT-compiled, differentiable computation graph. It scales to millions of parallel worlds and thousand-drone swarms, exposes analytic gradients, and reaches centimeter sim-to-real accuracy, training deployable policies in seconds, including a mid-air recovery policy learned in 0.38 s.

## Problem

- CPU-based high-fidelity drone simulators such as RotorS, CrazyS, and gym_pybullet_drones model accurately but lack batched throughput, making large-scale learning impractical.
- GPU-parallel drone simulators trade differentiability away: tensorized or lightweight C++ engines deliver throughput but expose no analytical gradients for gradient-based policy optimization.
- Existing differentiable drone simulators use point-mass or idealized rigid-body models under eager PyTorch execution, offloading sim-to-real complexity and paying host-accelerator synchronization overhead.

## Method

- A monolithic ==JAX== PyTree simulation state passed through composable transforms, fused by ==XLA JIT== into one differentiable graph with scan-based multi-step rollouts, skipping general-purpose contact solvers.
- First-principles physics down to the motors: polynomial thrust and torque curves, linear body drag, an asymmetric rotor model with separate acceleration/deceleration coefficients, plus the Crazyflie onboard controller stack reimplemented in JAX.
- A data-driven abstracted model with a ==system-identification== pipeline fitting new platforms from under four minutes of flight data, plus ==CasADi== symbolic twins and ==MJX== raycasting for collisions and depth.

## Results

- Throughput reaches about **700 million** steps per second at **one million** parallel worlds, two orders of magnitude over gym_pybullet_drones and **38x** over GPU baselines; gradients hit **nine million** per second.
- Sim-to-real position gap is **19.0 mm** on a **9 s** circle, up to **61.3%** below gym_pybullet_drones and **82.8%** below the CrazySim software-in-the-loop simulator.
- BPTT trains a Lissajous tracker to **8.7 mm** in **1.56 s** with no domain randomization; motor-level training runs **14x** faster than specialized C implementations; throw-recovery converges in **0.38 s**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Specializing a simulator to one domain, dropping the general contact solver, buys speed and gradient stability that general-purpose engines cannot match.
> - Modeling actuator asymmetry down to rotor acceleration versus deceleration closes the sim-to-real gap more than elaborate aerodynamic drag models do.
> - Sub-second training breaks the train-then-deploy assumption: policies can be learned during a physical maneuver, but only when fidelity removes the need for randomization.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of advanced robot algorithms, particularly in aerial robotics, increasingly relies on high-quality, large-scale synthetic data generated from simulations. Simulators are essential tools for rapid prototyping, systematic parameter tuning, and stress-testing algorithms in a risk-free environment. Recent advancements in deep reinforcement learning (RL) have emphasized the need for massive parallelization in simulation to address complex control problems that are impractical to solve directly on hardware. Concurrently, differentiable simulation has emerged as a method to exploit analytical gradients for efficient, gradient-based policy optimization.

Existing drone simulators in the research landscape often prioritize specific aspects, leading to a fragmented ecosystem:
- **High-fidelity simulators** (ee.g., RotorS, CrazyS, sim\_cf) offer detailed models for controller development and system-level testing, including software-in-the-loop or hardware-in-the-loop evaluations. However, their CPU-based architectures are not designed for high-throughput parallel simulation, limiting their utility for large-scale learning.
- **Multi-agent frameworks** (e.g., Aerostack2, CrazySwarm, CrazyChoir) provide features for multi-agent deployment but similarly lack native support for high-throughput batched simulation required by large-scale learning methods.
- **Massively parallel simulators** (e.g., Aerial Gym, PRL4AirSim, gym\_pybullet\_drones, QuadSwarm, Flightmare, Agilicious) leverage GPUs or distributed computing for substantial speedups and scalability. A common limitation of these approaches is the general exclusion of analytical gradients, which are beneficial for advanced learning techniques.
- **Differentiable simulators** (e.g., DiffPhysDrone, DiffAero) implement dynamics using frameworks like PyTorch to enable end-to-end learning. However, some rely on simplified models (e.g., point-mass models in DiffPhysDrone), potentially shifting the complexity of sim-to-real transfer. PyTorch's eager execution model can also introduce inefficiencies due to frequent synchronization between hardware accelerators and the Python interpreter.

A significant trend in deep learning and optimization is the adoption of lazy execution with just-in-time (JIT) compilation, exemplified by frameworks like JAX, MJX, NVIDIA Warp, and Newton. This paradigm aims to bridge the performance gap between high-level languages and native C++ implementations by tracing computations and fusing them into optimized functions.

Crazyflow positions itself within this landscape by aiming to unify execution speed, physical accuracy, and differentiability into a single platform. It addresses the identified gaps by leveraging JAX's JIT compilation and automatic differentiation capabilities to create a high-throughput, accurate, and differentiable drone simulator suitable for a broad spectrum of aerial robotics algorithm development.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The motivation behind developing Crazyflow stems from the evolving demands of aerial robotics research, particularly the need for robust and efficient simulation tools to drive algorithmic advancements. The broad adoption of drones in diverse applications, from safety-critical tasks to recreational domains, necessitates high-performance tools for rapid algorithmic iteration.

The paper identifies three critical properties for modern simulators: execution speed, physical accuracy, and differentiability. These factors are crucial for accelerating algorithm design cycles and generating cost-effective, high-quality synthetic data, which is foundational for reliable real-world deployment. While existing simulators often excel in one or two of these properties, a unified platform encompassing all three was identified as a missing component.

The key objectives of the Crazyflow project are:
- **Unify critical simulation properties**: To develop a simulator that simultaneously achieves high execution speed, physical accuracy, and differentiability, thereby supporting a wide range of aerial robotics algorithm development, from model-based to data-driven methods, and from single-agent to multi-agent systems.
- **Achieve high computational throughput**: To enable simulation speeds that significantly surpass existing state-of-the-art drone simulators, facilitating large-scale learning and extensive algorithmic parameter optimization. Specifically, the goal was to achieve speedups of an order of magnitude or more for single drones and to support the simulation of thousands of swarms, each containing thousands of drones.
- **Ensure high physical accuracy and reliable sim-to-real transfer**: To provide detailed physics models that allow for the training of deployable policies with high accuracy (e.g., sub-centimeter trajectory tracking) directly in simulation, often without the need for extensive domain randomization.
- **Support gradient-based optimization**: To leverage automatic differentiation for efficient, analytical-gradient-based policy learning and optimization, which is a powerful tool for advanced learning methods.
- **Enable novel learning paradigms**: To facilitate "on-the-fly" or "in-execution" learning and optimization by achieving unprecedented simulation speeds, moving beyond the traditional "train-then-deploy" paradigm. This includes demonstrating rapid policy training for real-time applications like in-flight drone recovery.
- **Offer modularity and adaptability**: To provide a flexible architecture that supports multiple levels of simulation abstraction, is compatible with popular open-source drone platforms (e.g., Crazyflie), and allows for rapid reconfiguration and integration of custom drone platforms through a streamlined system identification pipeline.
- **Provide an open-source resource**: To serve as an open-source tool for the research community, contributing to the broader scientific ecosystem and fostering collaborative advancements in aerial robotics.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Crazyflow's methodology is centered on leveraging JAX for its core architecture, which facilitates high performance, differentiability, and flexibility.

**Core Architecture:**
The simulator is built on JAX, utilizing its automatic differentiation and just-in-time (JIT) compilation via the Accelerated Linear Algebra (XLA) compiler. This design adheres to a strictly functional programming paradigm, where stateless functions operate on immutable input data.
- **Data Structure**: The simulation data is managed through a monolithic "PyTree" compatible with JAX, holding all necessary buffers, including drone and controller states and parameters.
- **Simulation Pipeline**: The simulation is composed as a pipeline of functions that transform the simulation data. JAX's tracing capabilities fuse these transforms into a single, optimized computation graph, which can then be efficiently executed over multiple successive steps using an XLA scan operation.
- **Performance Optimization**: Unlike general-purpose JAX engines that may use iterative methods for contact constraints, Crazyflow directly numerically integrates the drone's equations of motion. This approach aims to enhance execution speed and numerical stability of gradients during backpropagation by avoiding costly solver loops. Data between simulation steps is structured as `M_worlds x N_drones`-sized tensors, facilitating massive parallelization.

**Physics Modeling:**
Crazyflow provides two primary physics modeling approaches to balance fidelity and computational efficiency:
- **First-Principles Model**: This detailed, analytical model characterizes the drone system with high fidelity, incorporating dynamics down to the motor level.
 - **Rigid Body Dynamics**: The drone is modeled as a rigid body with six degrees of freedom (position, velocity, quaternion orientation, and angular velocity in the body frame). Equations describe linear and angular motion influenced by forces and torques.
 - **Rotor Dynamics**: Individual rotor speeds are mapped to forces and torques via second-order polynomial thrust curves. Aerodynamic counter-torques, gyroscopic, and reaction torques are also included. A specialized rotor dynamics model (Eq. 6 in the paper) captures different acceleration and deceleration behaviors, as well as quadratic drag effects, to improve accuracy over conventional approaches.
 - **Aerodynamic Drag**: Modeled as a linear term in the body frame.
 - **Parameter Identification**: Parameters such as mass are directly measured. Propeller inertia is approximated from CAD. Thrust and torque curves are fitted using static and dynamic data from load cell experiments. Rigid body inertia and aerodynamic drag coefficients are identified from aggressive flight data.
- **Abstracted Physics Model**: Designed for mid-level control interfaces (attitude control), this model simplifies complexity by abstracting the low-level control loop.
 - **Assumptions**: Assumes a stable and well-tuned low-level control loop.
 - **Dynamics**: The drone's attitude (using Euler angles) is characterized as a second-order linear system. Rotor effects are abstracted to a single collective thrust state (`f_Σ`) subject to a first-order delay. The drone is treated as a point-mass subject to linear drag.
 - **Input**: Desired attitude and collective thrust.
 - **Parameter Identification**: Parameters are derived from minutes of flight data (e.g., Lissajous, melon, Rhodonea trajectories). The system identification process is decoupled into independent stages for rotational and translational dynamics, using JAX's analytical gradients with Trust Region Reflective optimization for rotational dynamics and open-loop rollouts for translational dynamics.

**Control Interfaces:**
Crazyflow supports four levels of control abstraction:
1. **Motor Commands**: Direct rotor speed setpoints (requiring >=500 Hz update rates).
2. **Torque Setpoints**: Collective thrust and body torques (>=250 Hz).
3. **Attitude Setpoints**: Collective thrust and attitude (>=50 Hz).
4. **Position Setpoints**: High-level position references.
The low-level onboard controller stack (e.g., Mellinger controller) for the Crazyflie platform has been reimplemented in JAX to ensure full differentiability and JIT compilation.

**Customization and Compatibility:**
- **Modularity**: The simulation step is designed as a sequence of customizable transforms. Users can dynamically select and integrate their own transforms for controllers, physics, and reset logic. JAX's kernel fusion ensures that user-defined modifications (e.g., custom disturbances, domain randomization) are integrated into the optimized simulation step without performance penalties.
- **Backend Agnosticism**: Core physics models and controller implementations are built on Python's array API standard, allowing compatibility not only with JAX but also PyTorch, NumPy, CuPy, and other standard-compliant frameworks.
- **Symbolic Models**: Crazyflow provides symbolic dynamics models implemented in CasADi, a toolbox for model-based control, for users who prefer optimization-based control methods.
- **Drone Platform Support**: Directly compatible with open-source Crazyflie models and offers a streamlined system identification pipeline for adapting models to new custom drone platforms.

**Visualization and Collision Checking:**
Crazyflow integrates with MJX, a JAX-compatible reimplementation of the MuJoCo physics engine, for geometric queries. The simulation environment is defined using MuJoCo's XML descriptions. Crazyflow agents' states are synchronized with MJX structures, enabling efficient ray casting and distance measurements within the JAX computation graph. This hybrid approach ensures end-to-end differentiability and parallel scaling for sensing and collision detection.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of Crazyflow demonstrates its capabilities across computational performance, sim-to-real accuracy, and diverse control and learning applications.

**Computational Performance:**
- **Throughput**: On a consumer GPU, Crazyflow achieves approximately 700 million simulation steps per second (MPS) when simulating 1 million parallel worlds.
- **Scaling (Single Drone)**: It demonstrates throughput improvements of two orders of magnitude compared to `gym_pybullet_drones` (GPD) at equivalent parallelization levels, and supports five orders of magnitude more environments while maintaining higher fidelity.
- **Scaling (GPU-Accelerated)**: Compared to other GPU-accelerated, high-fidelity simulations like Aerial Gym and DiffAero, Crazyflow achieves consistent speedups of an order of magnitude or more.
- **Swarm Simulation**: The simulator supports full-fidelity vectorized swarm simulations, scaling to thousands of drones within thousands of parallel worlds. It outperforms other GPU-accelerated simulations like DiffAero (which supports swarms only with simplified point-mass models) by enabling a tenfold increase in the number of drones.
- **Gradient Throughput**: Crazyflow computes 9 million gradients per second for 1 million environments, each differentiating through 10 consecutive simulation steps. This surpasses the gradient throughput of DiffAero by more than 10 times across most parallelization levels.
- **Rendering**: For depth image rendering, Crazyflow achieves 350,000 frames per second at a 64x64 ray resolution across 1,024 environments, representing a fivefold increase over DiffAero, albeit with higher memory consumption.

**Sim-to-Real Accuracy:**
- **First-Principles Model**: Using its first-principles model, Crazyflow achieves low, centimeter-level sim-to-real gaps for the Crazyflie 2.1 quadrotor.
 - For the Crazyflie 2.1, the sim-to-real gap is up to 61.3% lower than `gym_pybullet_drones` and up to 82.8% lower than `CrazySim` (a software-in-the-loop simulator running the latest firmware and onboard controls), when tracking various circle and Lissajous trajectories.
 - Similar low gaps are reported for the Crazyflie Thrust Upgrade Kit (CFT) and Crazyflie Brushless (CFB) models.
- **Abstracted Physics Model**: This data-driven model, adaptable with only minutes of flight data, achieves competitive sim-to-real gaps compared to the first-principles model. It also scales to larger custom drones (e.g., a 660g drone), demonstrating a 3 cm gap, which is considered within expected error for larger platforms.

**Trajectory Tracking Benchmark:**
The simulator was validated on trajectory-tracking tasks using a Crazyflie 2.1 Brushless drone.
- **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)**: Policies trained in Crazyflow using first-principles physics, without domain randomization, achieved tracking errors of 15.9 ± 0.2 mm for a 10s Lissajous trajectory (training time: 1.61s) and 14.7 ± 0.7 mm for a 5.5s Lissajous trajectory (training time: 17.58s). This represents an improvement of up to 73.8% over a geometric controller baseline.
- **Backpropagation Through Time (BPTT)**: Leveraging analytical gradients, BPTT policies achieved tracking errors of 8.7 ± 0.1 mm for the 10s trajectory (training time: 1.56s) and 11.8 ± 0.6 mm for the 5.5s trajectory (training time: 12.64s). These training times are reported as the fastest to date for learning-based quadrotor control on these tasks.
- **Nonlinear Model Predictive Control (NMPC)**: An NMPC controller utilizing Crazyflow’s abstracted symbolic models (CasADi) achieved competitive tracking errors of 14.2 ± 0.4 mm and 19.0 ± 0.9 mm for the slow and fast Lissajous tasks, respectively.

**Control in Cluttered Environments:**
- Crazyflow facilitates Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) control for obstacle avoidance. By sampling 500,000 parallel trajectories at 50 Hz with a 25-step horizon (exceeding half a billion simulation steps per second), the simulator enables real-time, derivative-free optimization with full non-linear dynamics. This approach demonstrated robustness against local minima, the ability to generate multi-modal trajectories, and successful deployment on physical hardware for reactive obstacle avoidance.

**High-Speed Training at Motor Level:**
- The simulator allows end-to-end training of policies that directly output rotor speed commands. By fusing the entire RL loop and simulation into a single JIT-compiled kernel, stable position control from raw rotor commands was learned in 12.16 seconds, representing a 14-fold speedup over specialized C implementations. This policy achieved a root mean square position error of 16.6 cm on a 5.5s Lissajous task.
- **In-Flight Reinforcement Learning**: Leveraging BPTT, Crazyflow enabled an "on-the-fly" learning paradigm. A recovery policy for a physical drone thrown into the air was trained from scratch in 0.38 seconds (after initial state prediction), with policy transmission taking 48ms, successfully stabilizing the drone before it landed.

**Applications:**
Crazyflow has been applied in various research, competition, and educational contexts:
- **Swarm Robotics**: Used in SwarmGPT for generating complex aerial swarm performances.
- **RL Benchmarking**: Instrumental in hyperparameter tuning and extensive experiments for fundamental questions in RL (e.g., SO(3) action representations).
- **Education and Competitions**: Powers the Autonomous Drone Racing challenge at the Technical University of Munich and is used in advanced control lectures to demonstrate trade-offs between different modeling and control approaches.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

Crazyflow represents a contribution to the field of aerial robotics by unifying high physical accuracy, extreme execution speed, and end-to-end differentiability within a single simulator. This addresses a long-standing gap in the existing landscape of drone simulation tools, which often prioritize one or two of these attributes at the expense of others.

The significance and potential impact of Crazyflow are multifaceted:
- **Enabling New Algorithmic Paradigms**: By delivering high accuracy without extensive domain randomization and achieving rapid training times (down to sub-second), Crazyflow facilitates the transition from a traditional "train-then-deploy" approach to "in-execution" or "on-the-fly" learning and optimization. This capability, demonstrated by the in-flight stabilization of a physical drone, could open avenues for adaptive control, rapid recovery from unforeseen disturbances, and real-time decision-making in highly dynamic environments.
- **Accelerating Research and Development**: The combination of speed and differentiability significantly shortens algorithm design and iteration cycles. Researchers can rapidly prototype, test, and optimize complex control and learning algorithms across single-agent and multi-agent systems. This is particularly valuable for fields like deep reinforcement learning, model-based planning, optimal control, and differentiable programming.
- **High-Throughput Synthetic Data Generation**: Crazyflow's capacity to simulate millions of drones across parallel worlds at high fidelity provides a scalable platform for generating vast amounts of high-quality synthetic data. This data can be crucial for training robust and generalizable robotic policies, reducing the reliance on costly and time-consuming real-world data collection.
- **Bridging Control Theory and Machine Learning**: The simulator's support for both analytical-gradient-based learning (BPTT) and symbolic models for optimal control (CasADi for NMPC) bridges the gap between control-theoretic and data-driven decision-making frameworks. This fosters interdisciplinary research and the development of hybrid control architectures.
- **Community Resource and Extensibility**: As an open-source, modular, and well-documented platform, Crazyflow aims to serve as a valuable resource for the global research community. Its architecture, built on the array API standard, allows for backend-agnostic code, increasing its accessibility and interoperability with other popular frameworks. This design encourages community contributions, such as expanding the collection of drone models or integrating other open-source controller pipelines (e.g., PX4, BetaFlight).
- **Practical Applications**: The successful deployment of learned policies on physical Crazyflie hardware for tasks like trajectory tracking and obstacle avoidance, with centimeter-level accuracy, indicates direct practical utility. Its adoption in academic competitions and educational courses further underscores its value in fostering practical robotics skills.

Potential future developments include integrating photorealistic rendering for image-based sensors, leveraging techniques like Neural Radiance Fields or 3D Gaussian splatting for differentiable rendering pipelines. Such advancements would further enhance the simulator's utility for advanced vision-based flight research in unstructured environments, broadening its impact on autonomous navigation and perception.
