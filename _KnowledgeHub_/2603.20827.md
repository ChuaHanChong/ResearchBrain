# Swim2Real: VLM-Guided System Identification for Sim-to-Real Transfer

> [!summary] Summary
> Swim2Real calibrates a 16-parameter tendon-driven robotic fish simulator in MuJoCo directly from swimming videos. A vision-language model compares simulated and real skeleton overlays, proposes parameter updates, and a backtracking line search validates each step size, tripling the accept rate from 14% to 42%. It matches real fish velocities within 7.4 mm/s MAE, and downstream RL policies swim 12% farther than BayesOpt-calibrated ones, transferring to hardware at 50 Hz.

## Problem

- Black-box optimizers like Bayesian optimization and CMA-ES rely on scalar error signals, discarding the visual and physical structure available in simulation-versus-real comparisons.
- Prior calibration on this fish platform needed three manually decomposed stages, forcing practitioners to choose parameter groupings, per-group methods, and ordering by hand.
- Earlier video-driven VLM system identification tuned at most 7 parameters and never validated whether calibration accuracy translated into downstream policy or real-robot performance.

## Method

- ==VLM-guided calibration==: Gemini 2.5 Pro receives side-by-side simulated/real skeleton overlay videos plus per-marker errors, bounds, and proposal history, then proposes a full 16-parameter update vector.
- ==Backtracking line search== treats the VLM proposal as a search direction, evaluating geometrically decayed step sizes (decay 0.5, up to 3 tries) and accepting the first that lowers marker error.
- Joint 16-dimensional parameter space spans five ellipsoid fluid coefficients, motor arm length, and per-joint hinge stiffness and damping, searched simultaneously with no staged decomposition.

## Results

- Calibration reaches **51.3 ± 1.2 mm** marker error with all **five** seeds inside a **3 mm** range, versus **52.4 ± 2.1 mm** for BayesOpt and **112.7 mm** for CMA-ES with two catastrophic seeds.
- Held-out forward velocity matching gives **7.4 mm/s** MAE, **43%** below BayesOpt's **12.9 mm/s**, and RL policies swim **7.6 m** versus **6.8 m** (BayesOpt) and **4.0 m** (CMA-ES).
- Ablations: line search lifts accepts from **14%** to **42%**, cutting VLM calls from **39** to **15.6**; single-call warm start degrades to **91.1 ± 49.5 mm**, and swapping to Gemini 3.1 changes error only **2.2 mm**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Language models supply reliable update directions but poor magnitudes; pairing their proposals with a classical line search mirrors gradient descent and recovers most oversized steps.
> - Aggregate fitting error can hide fidelity differences that matter downstream; an out-of-objective metric like forward velocity better predicts policy transfer quality.
> - Calibration blind to global heading cannot fix hardware asymmetries such as steering bias, and rigid-segment discretization imposes an irreducible error floor concentrated at the tail.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a fundamental challenge in robotics: achieving reliable sim-to-real transfer, particularly for complex systems like soft and aquatic robots. Sim-to-real transfer necessitates accurate simulators that can reproduce real-world dynamics. While rigid-body systems often benefit from analytical models and domain randomization, soft and aquatic robots present unique difficulties due to their nonlinear material responses, coupled parameter spaces, and intricate fluid-structure interactions. These complexities make manual tuning or standard optimization techniques challenging.

Traditional system identification aims to fit simulator parameters to match observed behavior, often framing it as a black-box optimization problem using methods like Bayesian optimization or evolutionary strategies (e.g., CMA-ES). However, these approaches typically rely on scalar error signals, which discard the rich visual and physical information available from comparing simulated and real robot behaviors.

A recent trend in system identification has explored using video comparisons, with Vision-Language Models (VLMs) providing structured diagnostic signals. This approach moves beyond scalar metrics by allowing VLMs to reason about physical discrepancies from video, such as a simulated robot bending too sharply or moving too slowly. The current work builds upon this foundation, specifically extending prior VLM-guided calibration efforts like Vid2Sid, which demonstrated the potential of iterative VLM feedback for system identification across multiple platforms but was limited to a smaller number of parameters and lacked downstream policy validation.

Furthermore, this research integrates with the broader application of foundation models (LLMs and VLMs) in robotics. While LLMs and VLMs have been used for higher-level tasks such as task planning, visuomotor control, and robot co-design, Swim2Real utilizes a VLM for low-level parameter estimation. It observes simulation-reality discrepancies in video to propose quantitative parameter adjustments in a continuous, multi-dimensional space, contrasting with approaches that use LLMs as black-box optimizers on scalar or text feedback in lower-dimensional spaces.

The specific platform used, a tendon-driven robotic fish, has also been the subject of prior work, which required a manually tailored, multi-stage calibration process involving different methods (FFT analysis, grid search, Bayesian optimization) for a subset of parameters. Swim2Real aims to overcome these manual intervention requirements by providing an automated, end-to-end pipeline that calibrates all relevant parameters simultaneously.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and validate Swim2Real, an automated pipeline that can calibrate a 16-parameter robotic fish simulator directly from swimming videos, utilizing Vision-Language Model (VLM) feedback without requiring any hand-designed search stages. The overarching motivation is to enable automated, general-purpose simulator tuning for underwater robotics, thereby facilitating zero-shot Reinforcement Learning (RL) transfer from simulation to physical swimmers.

The specific motivations driving this work include:

- **Addressing Challenges of Soft Aquatic Robot Calibration:** Calibrating simulators for soft aquatic robots is inherently difficult. This is due to nonlinear material responses, tightly coupled parameter spaces, and complex fluid-structure interactions. These factors make manual tuning cumbersome, and standard optimization techniques often struggle to navigate the chaotic parameter landscape. Simplified fluid models commonly used in simulation also introduce a persistent sim-to-real gap, which requires robust calibration.
- **Reducing Manual Intervention:** Prior work on the tendon-driven robotic fish platform required practitioners to manually decompose the calibration problem into three distinct stages, each with a tailored method (e.g., FFT analysis for stiffness, grid search for motor geometry, Bayesian optimization for fluid coefficients). This process demanded significant domain expertise to select parameters, methods, and their order, representing a substantial burden. The authors seek to eliminate this need, allowing calibration to proceed automatically from raw video data.
- **Leveraging Visual Information for System Identification:** Existing black-box optimization methods for system identification, such as Bayesian optimization or evolutionary strategies, typically treat the simulator as an opaque function and rely solely on scalar error signals. This approach discards the rich visual and physical structure inherent in comparing simulated and real robot behaviors. The motivation is to exploit this visual information by using VLMs, which can reason about physical discrepancies (e.g., "tail bends too sharply," "moves too slowly") from video, thereby providing more interpretable and physically grounded guidance for parameter updates.
- **Improving Downstream Policy Performance:** Inaccurate simulator calibration directly degrades the performance of downstream RL policies trained within that simulator. A key motivation is to produce a more accurate simulator calibration that translates into higher-performing policies when transferred to the physical robot.
- **Scaling VLM-Guided Calibration:** While previous VLM-guided approaches (e.g., Vid2Sid) demonstrated potential, they were evaluated on a limited number of parameters (up to 7) and often lacked comprehensive downstream policy validation. This work aims to scale VLM-guided calibration to a higher-dimensional parameter space (16 parameters) and to validate the entire pipeline, including real-world deployment of RL policies.
- **Enhancing Reliability and Efficiency:** VLM proposals, while directionally informative, can often overestimate the required step size. The motivation behind incorporating a backtracking line search is to validate VLM proposals, recover updates that would otherwise be rejected, and improve the overall accept rate and sample efficiency of the calibration process.

In summary, the objectives are to create an automated, reliable, and effective VLM-guided system identification pipeline for aquatic robots, reducing expert intervention, improving simulator fidelity through visual reasoning, and ultimately enhancing the performance and transferability of learned policies to physical hardware.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Swim2Real is an end-to-end pipeline designed for simulator calibration using VLM feedback, described in Algorithm 1 and illustrated in Figure 1.

**A. Problem Formulation**
The core problem is formulated as finding a set of simulator parameters θ* that minimizes the discrepancy between simulated and real marker trajectories. This discrepancy is quantified as the mean squared Euclidean distance between corresponding markers (p_s_m and p_r_m) across a set of actuation frequencies (F), multiple body markers (M), and time steps (T_f) for each frequency. This objective is denoted L(θ). Each evaluation of L(θ) involves running multiple MuJoCo simulations, which is identified as the dominant computational cost.

**B. VLM-Guided Calibration**
Instead of treating the objective function L(θ) as a black box, Swim2Real employs a VLM (specifically, Gemini 2.5 Pro) to provide structured feedback. At each iteration, the VLM receives:
- **Video:** Side-by-side skeleton overlays comparing simulated (blue) and real (red) marker trajectories across all eight actuation frequencies. Additionally, the real video corresponding to the worst-matching frequency is provided.
- **Numerical Data:** Per-marker and per-frequency L2 errors, forward velocities, the current parameter vector (θ_best), defined parameter bounds, and a historical log of previous VLM proposals and their outcomes.
The VLM is prompted to perform three main tasks: (1) identify physical discrepancies from the videos (e.g., "excess tail amplitude," "insufficient thrust," "incorrect body curvature"), (2) reason about which simulator parameters are responsible for these discrepancies, and (3) propose an updated parameter vector (θ'). The prompt also includes semantic descriptions of each parameter (e.g., "hingeStiffness 3 controls the restoring torque at joint 3") to enable the VLM to map visual observations to specific parameter adjustments.

**C. Backtracking Line Search**
To address the VLM's tendency to sometimes propose correct directions but excessive step magnitudes, a backtracking line search mechanism is integrated. Given the current best parameters θ_best and the VLM's proposed direction (∆ = θ' - θ_best), the method evaluates candidate parameter vectors θ_k = θ_best + β^k · ∆. Here, β is a decay factor (set to 0.5) and K is the maximum number of step sizes to try (set to 3, meaning full, half, and quarter steps). The first step size k that yields an improvement (L(θ_k) < L(θ_best)) is accepted. If no step improves the objective, the proposal is rejected, and this feedback is incorporated into the VLM's input for the next iteration. This mechanism ensures that VLM proposals are validated and refined, increasing the likelihood of successful updates.

**D. Parameter Space**
The fish simulator in MuJoCo exposes a 16-dimensional parameter space, which Swim2Real searches jointly without hand-designed search stages or domain-specific strategies. The parameters cover three physical subsystems:
- **Fluid coefficients (5):** These parameterize a stateless ellipsoid fluid model, capturing blunt drag, slender drag, angular drag, Kutta lift, and Magnus lift.
- **Motor arm length (1):** This controls the lever arm of the crank-slider tendon mechanism.
- **Hinge stiffness (5, per joint):** These govern the mechanical restoring torque of each of the five hinge joints in the discretized tail.
- **Hinge damping (5, per joint):** These control the mechanical damping of each of the five hinge joints.
Parameter bounds are inherited from the simulator design.

**E. Experimental Setup**
- **Hardware Platform:** A 0.6 m, 1.5 kg tendon-driven robot fish with a passive front-end and an active compliant tail. It uses a Raspberry Pi Zero 2 W, a Dynamixel motor, super-elastic nitinol rods, and antagonistic tendons to produce an S-bend swimming motion.
- **Data Collection:** Eleven body markers on the fish are tracked from overhead camera footage (2160x3840, 60 fps) in a 2m x 3m pool. Nine markers (M0-M8, head to tail) are used for error computation, with trajectories rotated into the fish's local frame. Data is collected across eight actuation frequencies (0.50 Hz to 2.25 Hz).
- **Simulation:** The MuJoCo simulator uses an implicit integrator at 1 kHz. The continuously bending tail is discretized as five rigid segments connected by hinge joints. Fluid forces are modeled using a stateless ellipsoid model. Each simulation evaluation runs all eight frequencies to compute the L2 marker error. Forward swimming velocity is used as an out-of-distribution metric. A single 8-frequency evaluation takes approximately 13 seconds. Each VLM call (rendering, upload, inference, parsing) takes about 42 seconds.
- **Baselines:** Swim2Real is compared against three black-box optimization methods: Bayesian Optimization (using a GP surrogate with Matérn 5/2 kernel and gp_hedge acquisition), CMA-ES (population size 12), and Random Search.
- **Ablation Studies:** To assess specific design choices, ablations include: "w/o line search" (evaluating only the full VLM proposal), "w/ Gemini 3.1" (using an alternative VLM in the no-line-search configuration), and "Warm start" (one VLM proposal followed by random search).
- **Protocol:** All experiments use a budget of 40 simulation evaluations across five random seeds, with identical initializations where applicable.

**F. Downstream RL Evaluation**
To assess the practical impact of calibration, RL agents are trained in simulators configured with parameters obtained from each method. Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) is used with consistent hyperparameters across all conditions, only varying the simulator parameters. Two tasks are evaluated (3 seeds per condition, 5M steps):
- **Forward Swimming:** Reward based on forward displacement. Observations include marker positions/velocities, head pose, and motor state (52-dim).
- **Target Reaching:** Reward combines goal distance, action cost, and a success bonus. Observations include motor state, tail joint angles/velocities, and goal vector (22-dim). This task employs a three-stage curriculum.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings regarding calibration accuracy, sample efficiency, VLM efficacy, and downstream RL transfer.

**A. Calibration Accuracy**
Swim2Real achieved the lowest mean L2 marker error of 51.3 ± 1.2 mm (mean ± standard deviation over 5 seeds), exhibiting the tightest variance among all methods. All five seeds for Swim2Real fell within a narrow range of 50.2–53.2 mm, indicating consistent performance without outliers. Bayesian Optimization (BayesOpt) achieved a comparable mean error of 52.4 ± 2.1 mm, but with a slightly higher maximum error of 55.6 mm. CMA-ES showed significantly higher average error (112.7 ± 83.9 mm) and catastrophic failures on 2 out of 5 seeds, reaching errors of 123.4 mm and 254.2 mm, attributed to insufficient generations within the allocated budget for the 16-dimensional space. Random search served as a performance reference with the highest error of 82.0 ± 34.3 mm. Qualitatively, Swim2Real reproduced the body shape and forward progression of the real fish, while CMA-ES exhibited incorrect posture (Fig. 4).

**B. Sample Efficiency**
Swim2Real demonstrated the lowest Area Under the Convergence Curve (AUC) of 85.9 mm (Table II, Fig. 5a), indicating better overall convergence speed and final accuracy compared to BayesOpt (94.2 mm), random search (129.3 mm), and CMA-ES (156.4 mm). While Swim2Real’s AUC variance was higher, this reflected seed-dependent VLM convergence speed rather than final accuracy, as all seeds ultimately reached similar low errors.

**C. VLM Call Efficiency and Ablations**
The backtracking line search component was found to significantly improve efficiency. Across 78 VLM proposals over 5 seeds, the line search accepted 33 proposals (42% accept rate). In comparison, the "w/o line search" ablation (evaluating only the full step, β=1.0) achieved only a 14% accept rate. The line search recovered 61% of successful updates that would have been rejected without it (33.3% at half-step, 27.3% at quarter-step). This translates to Swim2Real using a mean of 15.6 VLM calls per run to reach approximately 51 mm error, whereas the "w/o line search" ablation required 39 VLM calls to reach a similar error (52.4 ± 2.8 mm). The average cost per VLM call was 2.5 simulation evaluations due to line search overhead, but this investment reduced the cost per accepted update from 7.2 to 5.9 evaluations.

An ablation replacing Gemini 2.5 Pro with Gemini 3.1 Pro (in the no-line-search configuration) yielded a similar error of 54.6 ± 4.2 mm versus 52.4 ± 2.8 mm, suggesting robustness to the specific VLM version within the Gemini family. The "Warm start" ablation (one VLM proposal followed by random search) resulted in a much higher error of 91.1 ± 49.5 mm, confirming that iterative VLM feedback is essential for effective calibration.

**D. Interpretable Reasoning**
The VLM provided interpretable and physically grounded diagnostic feedback. An example quote from an early accepted round illustrates this: the VLM diagnosed "critical failure at high frequencies [...] where the tail motion is severely attenuated," attributing it to "excessive damping forces," and also noted "at low frequencies, the simulated tail appears too flexible." Based on this, it proposed decreasing fluid drag and increasing hinge stiffness, leading to a substantial error reduction from 168.8 mm to 56.5 mm. This contrasts with scalar objective functions that do not provide such actionable insights.

**E. Forward Swimming Velocity**
As an out-of-distribution metric not directly optimized during calibration, forward swimming velocity provides an independent measure of simulator fidelity. Swim2Real-calibrated parameters resulted in the closest match to real fish velocities across all motor frequencies (MAE = 7.4 mm/s). This was approximately half the error of BayesOpt (12.9 mm/s), followed by CMA-ES (14.5 mm/s) and random search (21.3 mm/s). The difference in velocity matching was more pronounced at higher frequencies, where calibration quality is critical (Fig. 6).

**F. Downstream RL Transfer**
- **Forward Swimming Task:** Policies trained in Swim2Real-calibrated simulators swam 7.6 ± 0.0 m, outperforming BayesOpt-calibrated policies (6.8 ± 0.1 m), random-calibrated policies (6.0 ± 0.0 m), and CMA-ES policies (4.0 ± 0.6 m). This monotonic ranking with calibration accuracy suggests that the L2 marker error metric effectively captures simulator fidelity relevant for locomotion. Swim2Real's superior velocity match (Fig. 6) likely contributed to its lead over BayesOpt, as it better captured thrust-producing dynamics.
- **Real-world Deployment (Target Reaching):** A target-reaching policy trained in the Swim2Real-calibrated simulator was successfully deployed on the physical fish. Motor commands generated in simulation were transferred open-loop at 50 Hz. While the physical fish exhibited a consistent leftward arc absent in simulation (hypothesized to be from open-loop execution and a hardware steering bias not captured by local-frame calibration), it produced directed swimming towards the targets (Fig. 7b). This confirmed that the calibrated simulator provided sufficient body-dynamic fidelity for motor-command transfer.

**G. The 50 mm Error Floor**
Analysis indicated that the approximately 50 mm L2 marker error floor was primarily due to the approximation of the continuously bending tail by a discretized kinematic chain of five rigid hinge joints. Head markers (M0-M2) averaged 23 mm error, while tail markers (M6-M8) averaged 80 mm, with the tail tip (M8) reaching 101 mm. This suggests that reducing this floor would require a more complex tail model or higher-fidelity fluid dynamics.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Swim2Real pipeline introduces several significant contributions to the field of robotics, particularly for sim-to-real transfer and the calibration of complex robotic systems:

- **Automation of System Identification:** Swim2Real demonstrates a significant step towards fully automated system identification. By replacing manually tailored, multi-stage calibration processes with a VLM-guided pipeline, it eliminates the need for extensive human expertise in selecting parameters, optimization methods, and their application order. This automation can substantially reduce the development time and effort required for deploying robotic systems.

- **Enhanced Simulator Fidelity and Reliability:** The pipeline achieves high calibration accuracy, demonstrated by the lowest L2 marker error and the closest match to real fish velocities across various motor frequencies. Its reliability is evidenced by the tight variance across multiple seeds and the absence of outlier results, outperforming traditional black-box optimizers like CMA-ES which exhibited catastrophic failures in some runs. This improved fidelity is crucial for building trust in simulation models for downstream tasks.

- **Improved Sim-to-Real Transfer for Complex Robots:** The successful deployment of RL policies trained in the Swim2Real-calibrated simulator to the physical fish highlights the practical utility of the approach. Policies trained with Swim2Real parameters consistently outperformed those from other calibration methods in terms of distance swam and achieved directed movement on hardware, even under open-loop control. This demonstrates that VLM-guided calibration can effectively bridge the sim-to-real gap for soft aquatic robots, enabling zero-shot transfer without manual tuning on the physical system.

- **Leveraging Visual-Physical Reasoning:** The use of VLMs allows the system to extract rich, physically grounded diagnostic feedback from video comparisons. Unlike scalar objective functions, the VLM can identify specific physical discrepancies (e.g., "excessive damping forces," "tail too flexible") and suggest interpretable parameter adjustments. This interpretability provides insights into simulator inaccuracies that can be valuable for model refinement and debugging, beyond simply improving a numerical score.

- **Scalability and Robustness:** Swim2Real successfully scales VLM-guided calibration to a 16-dimensional parameter space, an increase from previous VLM-based methods which handled fewer parameters. The integration of a backtracking line search significantly enhances sample efficiency by validating VLM proposals and tripling the accept rate, making the process more robust to VLM's overestimation of step sizes. Furthermore, the demonstrated robustness across different VLM versions (Gemini 2.5 Pro vs. 3.1 Pro) suggests that the underlying methodology is generalizable within the VLM paradigm.

- **Path Towards General-Purpose Simulator Tuning:** The approach's reliance on visual observations and general-purpose VLMs suggests its potential applicability to a wider range of robotic platforms where simulation-reality discrepancies are visually observable. This could lead to a more generalized framework for simulator tuning across different robot morphologies and actuation mechanisms.

- **Future Directions and Challenges:** The research also outlines avenues for future work, including closed-loop policy execution with onboard state estimation to mitigate issues like hardware steering bias. The method's potential to extend to even higher-dimensional parameter spaces, where traditional GP surrogates may struggle, is also noted. While the VLM API call overhead currently triples wall-clock time compared to simulation-only baselines, this is considered minor relative to real-world data collection costs. The reliance on proprietary VLMs is also acknowledged, with the suggestion that the approach's general requirements point towards portability to open-source models.

In conclusion, Swim2Real represents a significant advancement in automated sim-to-real transfer for complex robots, particularly in underwater robotics, by effectively leveraging VLM-guided physical reasoning to calibrate simulators with high accuracy and reliability, directly from visual data, and without extensive human intervention.
