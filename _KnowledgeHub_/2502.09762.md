# AT-Drone: Benchmarking Adaptive Teaming in Multi-Drone Pursuit

> [!summary] Summary
> AT-Drone establishes the first dedicated benchmark for evaluating adaptive teaming strategies within multi-drone pursuit scenarios, bridging the gap between theoretical machine learning advancements and practical robotic deployments. The platform provides customizable simulations, a real-world deployment pipeline, and an algorithm zoo, showing HOLA-Drone (V2) generally performs well and that explicit teammate modeling can sometimes hinder performance.

## Problem

- Multi-robot collaboration often relies on pre-defined coordination mechanisms, limiting adaptability and robustness in dynamic, unpredictable environments with unfamiliar teammates.
- Adaptive teaming research, enabling agents to collaborate with unseen partners, has largely been confined to simulated, discrete-action video game environments.
- There was no dedicated benchmark for studying adaptive teaming in complex, real-world multi-drone scenarios involving continuous action spaces and multiple learning agents.

## Method

- Introduce **AT-Drone**, a unified benchmark providing a highly customizable simulation environment configurator for diverse multi-drone pursuit tasks.
- Establish a streamlined real-world deployment pipeline to enable seamless translation of simulation-trained policies to physical Crazyflie drones and edge devices.
- Develop a novel algorithm zoo with diverse ==adaptive teaming== algorithms (including **HOLA-Drone (V2)** and **NAHT-D**) integrated with a distributed training framework, paired with standardized evaluation protocols using newly designed 'unseen drone zoos.'

## Results

- HOLA-Drone (V2) achieved higher success rates, fewer collisions, and reduced average timesteps compared to Self-Play and Population-Based Training across most adaptive teaming scenarios.
- An ablation study of NAHT-D indicated that removing the explicit teammate modeling decoder network sometimes led to better or comparable performance metrics, suggesting potential inefficiencies in complex modeling.
- Real-world drone experiments successfully validated the practical applicability of the adaptive teaming approach, demonstrating sim-to-real transferability on Crazyflie drones executing multi-stage capture strategies.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - HOLA-Drone (V2) consistently demonstrated superior performance in adaptive teaming scenarios without explicit teammate modeling, particularly in more complex environments.
> - The hypergraphic game mechanism in HOLA-Drone (V2) significantly benefits coordination in challenging environments, but may add unnecessary complexity in simpler conditions.
> - Explicit teammate modeling, as implemented in NAHT-D (NAHT for Drones), sometimes hindered coordination efficiency compared to simpler variants without the decoder, especially in complex, continuous-action drone pursuit tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper addresses a critical, under-explored intersection between multi-robot collaboration, adaptive teaming (AT), and real-world drone applications. Multi-drone collaboration is a well-established and rapidly growing field, with significant applications in areas like disaster response, border surveillance, and search-and-rescue missions. Traditionally, these applications have relied on pre-defined coordination mechanisms or extensive prior interactions among robots, which limits their adaptability and robustness in dynamic, unpredictable environments or with unfamiliar teammates.

Adaptive teaming, a concept enabling agents to collaborate effectively with previously unseen partners without prior coordination, has been widely explored within the machine learning community. However, this exploration has predominantly been confined to simulated environments, primarily in virtual video games with discrete action spaces, such as Overcooked, Hanabi, and Predator-Prey. These environments, while valuable for developing theoretical adaptive strategies (like zero-shot coordination (ZSC) and ad-hoc teamwork (AHT)), often lack the complexity, continuous action spaces, and physical constraints inherent in real-world robotic systems. Methods like NAHT have extended these paradigms to multiple learners but remain within discrete-action simulated domains.

The authors identify a significant gap: the absence of a dedicated benchmark for studying adaptive teaming in complex, real-world multi-drone scenarios that involve continuous action spaces and multiple learning agents. AT-Drone directly positions itself to fill this void. By introducing the first unified benchmark to integrate machine learning-based adaptive teaming methods into practical multi-drone robotics, this work aims to bridge the current disconnect between theoretical advancements in adaptive teaming (largely in games) and the practical demands of real-world multi-robot deployments. It provides a standardized framework that is crucial for validating the scalability, robustness, and applicability of advanced adaptive teaming algorithms in robotic contexts, thereby significantly advancing the state of the art in both multi-agent reinforcement learning and multi-robot systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind AT-Drone stems from the pressing need for highly adaptable and robust multi-drone systems in real-world applications where team compositions are dynamic and environmental conditions are unpredictable. Current multi-drone collaboration methods are often brittle when faced with unexpected scenarios, such as drone damage requiring integration of backup units or encountering unfamiliar partners. This limitation hinders their effectiveness in critical operations like border surveillance, search-and-rescue, and counter-terrorism, where seamless, on-the-fly collaboration is paramount. The existing adaptive teaming research, while promising, has largely remained in the realm of simplified, discrete-action simulated environments, failing to address the complexities of continuous control and physical interactions intrinsic to real robotics.

To address this critical gap, the key objective of AT-Drone is to introduce the *first dedicated benchmark* explicitly designed for comprehensive training and evaluation of adaptive teaming strategies in multi-drone pursuit scenarios. This objective is realized through four specific contributions:
1. **To provide an adaptable simulation environment configurator:** This aims to allow intuitive and rapid setup of diverse multi-drone pursuit tasks, enabling rigorous testing across varied obstacle complexities, evader numbers, and task difficulties.
2. **To establish a streamlined real-world deployment pipeline:** The goal here is to enable seamless translation of insights gained in simulation into practical drone evaluations using physical hardware and edge devices, ensuring the practical feasibility of learned policies.
3. **To develop a novel algorithm zoo integrated with a distributed training framework:** This component is designed to feature diverse adaptive teaming algorithms, specifically tailored for the first time to multi-pursuer and multi-evader drone pursuit settings, fostering systematic exploration of these strategies.
4. **To define standardized evaluation protocols with newly designed unseen drone zoos:** The objective is to rigorously assess the performance and robustness of adaptive teaming algorithms against varied and challenging teammate behaviors, providing a clear measure of their adaptability.

By achieving these objectives, AT-Drone aims to significantly enhance the operational robustness and flexibility of drone fleets, bridging the gap between theoretical adaptive teaming innovations and their practical utility in realistic robotic operations.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

AT-Drone employs a comprehensive methodology structured around four interconnected components to enable the training and evaluation of adaptive teaming in multi-drone pursuit.

**1. Customizable Simulation Environment:**
The benchmark provides a highly customizable simulation environment built on a Gymnasium-based interface. An intuitive configurator allows users to rapidly set up scenarios by adjusting parameters across three categories:
- **Players:** Defines the number of pursuers, evaders, controlled agents, and unseen teammates, along with their velocities, reception ranges, and respawn behaviors. It also integrates an "unseen drone zoo" to simulate diverse adaptive teaming conditions.
- **Site:** Configures physical properties like map dimensions and obstacle arrangements (e.g., number, type, and distribution of obstacles), allowing for a wide range of scenario complexities.
- **Task:** Specifies task-specific rules, such as capture range, safety radius, task horizon, and frame rate.
The authors design four progressively challenging environments: 4p2e3o (easy), 4p2e1o (slightly harder), 4p2e5o (hard), and 4p3e5o (superhard), varying in the number of pursuers (p), evaders (e), and obstacles (o). Each agent's observation space is a normalized continuous vector including relative positions and bearings to evaders, distance and angle to the nearest obstacle, and relative positions and bearings of nearby teammates, with masking for occluded entities. The action space is continuous, representing angular steering adjustments. The reward function incentivizes evader capture and proximity improvement while penalizing collisions.

**2. Streamlined Real-World Deployment Pipeline:**
AT-Drone includes a practical deployment pipeline to validate simulation-trained policies on physical hardware. This setup utilizes Crazyflie drones, edge computing nodes (Nvidia Jetson Orin Nano, laptops), and a motion capture system (FZMotion). The FZMotion system provides real-time position tracking data to Crazyswarm, which then feeds into the decision-making policies. These policies for both adaptive learners and unseen drone partners (sampled from the unseen drone pool) run on edge nodes, transmitting control commands via Crazyradio PA to the Crazyflie drones, which execute maneuvers using an onboard Mellinger controller. This seamless integration bridges the sim-to-real gap.

**3. Novel Algorithm Zoo with Distributed Training Framework:**
The benchmark features a distributed training architecture, leveraging parallel environments to efficiently scale learning processes. The algorithm zoo includes diverse adaptive teaming algorithms, categorized into two main paradigms:
- **Adaptive Teaming without Teammate Modeling (AT w/o TM, i.e., Zero-Shot Coordination - ZSC):**
 - **Self-Play (SP):** Drones refine policies through competitive interactions against updated versions of themselves, enhancing adaptability. Implemented with Independent PPO (IPPO).
 - **Population-Based Training (PBT):** Facilitates extensive exploration and knowledge sharing among diverse model populations, improving generalization. Also IPPO-based.
 - **HOLA-Drone (V2):** An enhanced version of a previous method, it constructs a preference hypergraph to identify optimal teammate interactions and employs a novel max-min preference oracle. This oracle systematically identifies challenging teammate subsets and iteratively optimizes drone strategies to robustly handle these scenarios by dynamically adjusting the learner strategy set.
- **Adaptive Teaming with Teammate Modeling (AT w/ TM, i.e., Ad-Hoc Teamwork - AHT):**
 - **NAHT-D (NAHT for Drones):** An adaptation of the NAHT framework for continuous-action drone scenarios. It integrates a specialized teammate-modeling network into MAPPO. This network, an autoencoder, takes past observations and actions to reconstruct teammates’ current action distributions using KL divergence as reconstruction loss. The encoder's output embedding (capturing teammate behaviors) is combined with the agent's current observations as input to the policy network.

**4. Standardized Evaluation Protocols with Unseen Drone Zoos:**
A rigorous evaluation protocol is designed with distinct "unseen drone zoos" and specific metrics:
- **Unseen Drone Zoos:**
 - **Unseen Zoo 1: Greedy Drones:** Rule-based, focused on nearest evader, with dynamic collision avoidance.
 - **Unseen Zoo 2: IPPO Self-Play Drones:** Learning-based, generated with different random seeds to produce diverse behaviors (two skill levels: 70% and 54% success rates).
 - **Unseen Zoo 3: Combined Zoo:** Randomly selects partners from all previous zoos each episode, maximizing behavioral diversity and unpredictability.
- **Evaluation Metrics:**
 - **Success Rate (SUC):** Percentage of episodes where all evaders are captured within 0.2 meters.
 - **Collision Rate (COL):** Frequency of collisions between drones or drones and obstacles.
 - **Average Success Timesteps (AST):** Mean time for successful task completion.
 - **Average Reward (REW):** Overall quality and efficiency of agent performance.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluations conducted across four progressively challenging multi-drone pursuit scenarios (4p2e3o, 4p2e1o, 4p2e5o, 4p3e5o) confirmed AT-Drone’s effectiveness in advancing adaptive teaming research. Furthermore, real-world drone experiments successfully validated its practical feasibility and utility for realistic robotic operations.

**Adaptive Teaming without Teammate Modeling:**
- **Performance Comparison:** HOLA-Drone (V2) consistently demonstrated superior performance across most adaptive teaming scenarios. It achieved higher success rates (SUC), fewer collisions (COL), and reduced average steps (AST) compared to Self-Play (SP) and Population-Based Training (PBT).
- **Impact of Complexity:** As environmental complexity escalated from easier (4p2e1o) to superhard (4p3e5o) scenarios, the success rates across all methods decreased, and the performance gap compared to best-response (BR) policies widened, underscoring the inherent challenge of coordinating with unfamiliar teammates in complex settings.
- **Ablation Study (HOLA-Drone (V2) w/o g):** Interestingly, the simplified HOLA-Drone (V2) variant (without the hypergraphic game mechanism) achieved comparable or even superior performance in simpler scenarios like 4p2e1o. This suggests that while the hypergraphic module significantly benefits coordination in challenging environments, it might introduce unnecessary complexity under relatively straightforward conditions.

**Adaptive Teaming with Teammate Modeling:**
- **Performance Comparison:** The NAHT-D w/o Dec variant (an ablation study removing the teammate modeling decoder network and its loss function) generally achieved better or comparable performance metrics (SUC, COL, AST, REW) compared to the full NAHT-D method, particularly in more complex scenarios (4p2e5o and 4p3e5o).
- **Counter-intuitive Observation:** This finding is noteworthy as it suggests that the explicit teammate modeling (decoder) in NAHT-D, intended to improve collaboration, might paradoxically hinder coordination efficiency or robustness under highly challenging, continuous-action conditions. Simpler implicit modeling, or representations without an explicit decoder for action reconstruction, appeared more effective in these complex drone pursuit tasks.

**Case Study and Real-World Validation:**
A detailed case study (Appendix E) showcased the practical applicability of the adaptive teaming approach. NAHT-D learners, paired with unseen drone partners from Unseen Zoo 3 (diverse self-play policies) in the superhard 4p3e5o environment, successfully executed a multi-stage capture strategy. This demonstration of real-world deployment on Crazyflie drones corroborated the benchmark's ability to bridge simulation research with practical robotic applications.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

AT-Drone represents a significant advancement in the fields of multi-robot systems and adaptive artificial intelligence, with profound implications for both research and practical applications.

**1. Bridging the Sim-to-Real Gap for Adaptive Teaming:** The most significant impact of AT-Drone is its pioneering role as the first dedicated benchmark explicitly designed to bridge the gap between theoretical adaptive teaming research (often conducted in simplified, discrete-action games) and its practical application in real-world multi-drone robotics. By providing a streamlined deployment pipeline to physical Crazyflie drones, AT-Drone validates the transferability and utility of simulation-trained policies, which is crucial for real-world deployment.

**2. Driving Advancements in Adaptive Teaming Research:** The benchmark provides a standardized, customizable, and rigorous framework for researchers. This includes progressively challenging environments, a diverse algorithm zoo, and robust evaluation protocols (including novel "unseen drone zoos"). This infrastructure will undoubtedly accelerate the development and rigorous testing of new adaptive teaming strategies for multi-agent, continuous-action, real-world scenarios, moving beyond the current limitations of game-based research. The distributed training framework and comprehensive evaluation metrics further support high-quality, reproducible research.

**3. Enhancing Robustness and Flexibility of Robotic Systems:** By enabling drones to dynamically coordinate with previously unseen teammates without prior coordination, AT-Drone directly addresses a critical challenge in multi-robot operations: the need for robustness and flexibility in dynamic, unpredictable environments. This capability is vital for mission success in various real-world applications where team compositions can change, or unforeseen partners may be introduced (e.g., during disaster response, military operations, or large-scale surveillance). The benchmark contributes to making drone fleets more resilient and adaptable to operational exigencies.

**4. Informing Future Algorithm Design:** The experimental findings, particularly the superior performance of HOLA-Drone (V2) in complex scenarios and the counter-intuitive results regarding explicit teammate modeling in NAHT-D, offer critical insights for future algorithm development. The observation that simpler teammate modeling strategies can sometimes outperform more complex ones in demanding real-world-like conditions is a valuable lesson, suggesting the need for careful consideration of model complexity in continuous-action, multi-agent contexts.

**5. Guiding Future Research Directions:** The explicitly stated limitations of AT-Drone, such as the simplicity of the current real-world system, scalability constraints, challenges in complex perception, and simulation fidelity, serve as a clear roadmap for future research. This transparency will encourage the community to address these issues, pushing the boundaries towards more complex scenarios, larger drone fleets, enhanced perception capabilities, and ever more realistic simulations.

In conclusion, AT-Drone is not just a collection of tools; it is a foundational step towards enabling truly intelligent and adaptive multi-robot systems, promising significant impact on both the theoretical understanding of multi-agent collaboration and the practical capabilities of autonomous drone fleets in critical real-world applications.
