# Unified Prediction and Planning via Conflict-Aware Disjoint Parameter Training

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from DGIST and KAIST introduced a framework that addresses "Skill Conflict" in compact unified prediction-planning models by proposing Disjoint Parameter Training (DPT) and Sparse Merging. This approach enables high-performance, compact multi-task models suitable for deployment on edge devices, achieving superior prediction accuracy and planning safety on benchmarks like JRDB.

## Problem

- Autonomous systems require compact and efficient models for joint motion prediction and planning on resource-constrained edge devices, where traditional sequential pipelines incur high latency and scaled-up unified models are infeasible.
- Compact unified models suffer from "Skill Conflict," where shared encoders face conflicting gradient updates from distinct prediction (diverse behaviors) and planning (ego-centric safety) objectives, degrading learned representations.
- Existing model merging techniques, primarily designed for large-scale models with sparse parameter subspaces, are ineffective for compact models due to dense parameter overlap and intensified interference.

## Method

- A novel **Disjoint Parameter Training (DPT)** strategy fine-tunes task-specific models by alternating between prediction and planning and progressively allocating strictly non-overlapping core parameter regions to each task via ==binary masks==.
- **Sparse Merging** combines DPT-trained ==task vectors== by selectively integrating only a highly restrictive fraction (e.g., 1%) of the largest-magnitude coordinates, preserving core skills while mitigating residual interference from adjacent features.
- The framework integrates **DPT** and **Sparse Merging** within a unified architecture comprising a ==shared encoder== and task-specific decoders, enabling efficient joint reasoning suitable for resource-constrained environments.

## Results

- The DPT + Sparse Merging framework achieved superior performance across all planning (ADE, Collision Rate, FDE, Miss Rate) and prediction (ADE, FDE) metrics on the JRDB dataset, outperforming existing unified architectures and various model merging baselines.
- DPT effectively mitigated Skill Conflict, enabling models to maintain high performance for both prediction and planning tasks by efficiently localizing and encoding each task's core skill within compact parameter areas, unlike methods without DPT.
- The proposed framework is robust, adaptable to different backbone architectures and merging methods, extends to end-to-end autonomous driving, and introduces no additional inference-time cost, ensuring computational efficiency for edge device deployment.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The research empirically identifies and defines "Skill Conflict" in compact unified models, demonstrating that distinct tasks (prediction and planning) compete for overlapping parameters, leading to performance degradation, especially in resource-limited settings.
> - Separating learnable parameter regions for each task during fine-tuning (via DPT) is crucial for preparing task-specialized models that can be effectively merged without severe interference, significantly enhancing multi-task performance.
> - Even after disjoint training, sparse integration of only the most influential parameters (Sparse Merging) is essential for compact models to mitigate residual functional interactions and preserve core task skills, concentrating representational capacity.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Motion prediction and motion planning are fundamental components for the safe and socially compliant navigation of autonomous systems, such as mobile robots and autonomous vehicles, particularly in complex and crowded environments. Traditionally, these tasks have been addressed through sequential pipelines, where motion prediction of surrounding agents occurs first, followed by motion planning for the ego agent. While effective, this sequential approach often introduces inference latency and limits the system's ability to learn interdependent relationships between prediction and planning tasks.

To overcome these limitations, recent research has explored unified frameworks that perform prediction and planning jointly within a single model. These unified models typically leverage a shared encoder architecture to process environmental inputs and generate representations that inform both tasks. While these unified approaches have demonstrated strong joint reasoning capabilities, a significant challenge arises when deploying them on resource-constrained edge devices. In such settings, compact models are necessary, yet they often face representational conflicts within the shared encoder due to the distinct objectives of prediction (understanding varied behaviors of surrounding agents) and planning (prioritizing ego-centric safety and feasibility). Prior unified models have generally overlooked these conflicts or addressed them by scaling up model parameters, which is not suitable for edge deployment.

Concurrently, model merging techniques have emerged as a method to combine parameters from models fine-tuned for individual tasks into a single, unified model capable of performing multiple functions simultaneously. These methods offer an alternative to ensemble learning or mixture-of-experts, producing a single, compact model efficient for deployment where inference latency is critical. However, existing model merging research has primarily focused on large-scale models, such as Large Language Models (LLMs), with massive parameter counts and has largely been applied to language-related tasks. In these large models, distinct task skills can often be allocated to separate, sparse parameter subspaces naturally. This differs fundamentally from the requirements of resource-constrained robot navigation, where compact models necessitate a dense utilization of parameters, leading to increased overlap and interference among task-critical parameter regions.

This work contributes to the landscape by explicitly identifying and addressing the "Skill Conflict" problem within compact, unified prediction-planning models for robot navigation. It bridges the gap between the need for efficient unified models and the limitations of existing model merging techniques in resource-constrained environments. By proposing a framework that actively mitigates parameter collision during training and selectively merges parameters, the research aims to enable high-performance, compact multi-task models for autonomous systems on edge devices.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to enable accurate motion prediction of surrounding agents and safe motion planning for an ego agent simultaneously and efficiently, particularly for deployment on resource-constrained edge devices in crowded environments.

The primary motivation stems from the limitations observed in existing approaches:

1. **Computational Constraints on Edge Devices:** Deploying advanced robot navigation systems that integrate both motion prediction and planning on resource-constrained edge devices necessitates compact and efficient computational models. Traditional sequential pipelines for prediction and planning often incur high latency, while simply increasing the size of unified models to improve performance is not feasible for edge deployment.
2. **Representational Conflicts in Unified Models:** Recent unified frameworks aim to jointly perform prediction and planning using shared encoders. However, the authors identify a critical issue: these shared encoders, especially in compact models, often suffer from "representational conflicts." Prediction tasks require features sensitive to diverse agent behaviors, while planning tasks demand ego-centric features prioritizing safety and feasibility. Attempting to optimize both objectives on the same compact set of parameters leads to conflicting gradient updates, which can degrade the quality of learned representations for both tasks. This phenomenon is termed "Skill Conflict"—where overlapping parameter assignments cause distinct tasks to compete for the same weights, hindering task specialization.
3. **Limitations of Existing Model Merging Techniques:** While model merging offers a solution for combining task-specific capabilities into a single model, existing methods are predominantly designed for large-scale models (e.g., LLMs). In these models, distinct skills can naturally reside in separate, sparse parameter subspaces. For compact models, however, parameters critical to different tasks tend to densely overlap, intensifying interference when naively merged. This discrepancy renders current merging approaches less effective for the specific context of compact, unified robot navigation models.

To address these motivations, the key objectives of this work are:

- **Identify and Analyze Skill Conflict:** Empirically demonstrate and define the "Skill Conflict" problem within compact, unified prediction-planning models, showing how it arises from shared encoder representations.
- **Develop a Conflict-Aware Training Framework:** Propose a novel training methodology, Disjoint Parameter Training (DPT), that explicitly mitigates task interference by separating and localizing each task's core skills within distinct parameter regions during the fine-tuning process.
- **Introduce an Efficient Merging Strategy:** Develop "Sparse Merging" to effectively combine the task-specialized models created by DPT. This strategy selectively integrates only the most influential parameters from each task to prevent interference from adjacent features and concentrate representational capacity.
- **Validate Effectiveness and Versatility:** Evaluate the proposed framework on standard crowd navigation benchmarks (JRDB and JTA) to demonstrate its superior performance in maintaining both accurate prediction and safe planning, thereby validating its effectiveness and versatility for resource-efficient mobile robots.
- **Demonstrate Broader Applicability:** Show that the identified Skill Conflict is a pervasive issue across different architectures and tasks, and that DPT can enhance various existing merging methods and extend to related domains like end-to-end autonomous driving.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology addresses the identified "Skill Conflict" in compact unified models through a two-stage process: a specialized training strategy (Disjoint Parameter Training) and a selective model integration technique (Sparse Merging).

#### 3.1. Problem Definition

The work considers a unified architecture comprising a shared encoder and two task-specific decoders: a prediction decoder for neighbor agents and a planning decoder for the ego agent. Inputs include multi-modal data such as human trajectories, poses, and bounding boxes. The objective is to train the shared parameters to produce safe ego-agent plans ($Y_{plan}$) and accurate neighbor-agent predictions ($Y_{pred}$), specifically focusing on mitigating representational interference within the shared encoder.

#### 3.2. Preliminaries: Model Merging and Task Vectors

Model merging typically involves fine-tuning individual "material" models from a pretrained backbone ($\Theta_0$) for each task ($t$), yielding task-specific models ($\Theta_t$). The difference ($\tau_t = \Theta_t - \Theta_0$) defines the task vector. These task vectors are then combined using a merging operator ($M$) and coefficients ($\phi$) to form a merged model ($\Theta_{merge}$). The authors highlight that while this approach works for large foundation models where tasks can naturally utilize sparse, distinct parameter subspaces, it leads to severe interference in compact models due to dense parameter overlap.

#### 3.3. Empirical Evidence of Skill Conflict in a Shared Encoder

To diagnose Skill Conflict, the authors conduct an empirical study using the Social-Transmotion backbone (extended with a planning decoder) on the JRDB dataset. They define a binary activation mask ($M_t^{(K)}$) for each task vector ($\tau_t$), selecting the top-K coordinates by magnitude, where K is the mask ratio (utilization percentage).

- **Observation 1 (Performance Saturation):** In single-task merges, performance improves with increasing K. However, in two-task merges (Both Merged), performance gains saturate quickly and at a lower ceiling when one task's K is increased while the other is fixed. This suggests that greedy parameter utilization by one task degrades the performance of the other.
- **Observation 2 (Parameter Overlap):** Analyzing the parameter distribution, the authors categorize parameters into "Plan Skill," "Pred Skill," "Skill Conflict" (activated by both), and "Not Activated." At K=10%, the overlap is minimal (8.35%), but at K=50%, it surges to 39.25%. This indicates that as tasks try to leverage more parameters, their active regions heavily overlap, causing the conflict.
- **Generalizability:** Supplementary analysis confirms similar findings on the DTPP architecture and shows that Skill Conflict is more pronounced in compact models (lower model dimensions) compared to larger ones, validating its pervasive nature in resource-constrained settings. Furthermore, controlled experiments demonstrate a causal link between increased overlap and performance degradation.

#### 3.4. Disjoint Parameter Training (DPT): Preparing Task-Specialized Material Models

To address Skill Conflict, DPT is proposed as a fine-tuning strategy to generate task-specialized models with strictly non-overlapping core regions.

- **Alternating Training:** DPT alternates training between prediction and planning tasks.
- **Progressive Mask Expansion:** At each alternating step, DPT computes task-specific loss and gradients across the entire parameter space. Instead of updating all weights, it identifies and selects the top-K unassigned parameters with the largest absolute gradients. These parameters are then permanently allocated to the current task via a binary mask.
- **Two Phases:**
 - **Allocation Phase (First half of training):** Task-specific masks incrementally grow by selecting new parameters.
 - **Consolidation Phase (Second half of training):** Masks are frozen, and the model refines weights within these fixed, allocated regions without further spatial expansion.
- **Outcome:** This process constructs fine-tuned models ($f_{plan}, f_{pred}$) whose core parameter spaces within the shared encoder are mutually exclusive by design, reducing interference during merging.

#### 3.5. Sparse Merging of Prepared Models

Even with DPT, simply combining the full masked coordinates of the task vectors can degrade performance, suggesting residual functional interactions from adjacent features. Sparse Merging is introduced to mitigate this.

- **Mechanism:** It integrates only a highly restrictive fraction (K%) of the largest-magnitude coordinates from each DPT-trained task vector ($\tau_{pred}, \tau_{plan}$) using the previously defined masks. The merged model is formed as $\Theta_{merged} = \Theta_0 + M_{pred}^{(K\%)} \odot \tau_{pred} + M_{plan}^{(K\%)} \odot \tau_{plan}$.
- **Goal:** By focusing on the most salient coordinates, Sparse Merging preserves each task's core skill while actively suppressing detrimental local interactions within the compact shared encoder.

#### 3.6. Experimental Setup

- **Datasets:** JRDB (real-world, pedestrian trajectories/bounding boxes) and JTA (large-scale synthetic, rich annotations). For JTA, a 15% subset is used for comparable setup with JRDB.
- **Backbone:** Social-Transmotion, extended with a planning decoder. Pretraining uses a game-theoretic loss to encourage interaction between planner and forecaster, followed by task-specific fine-tuning.
- **Baselines:**
 - **Unified Models:** DIPP, DTPP (architectures for joint prediction/planning).
 - **Model Merging Methods:** Ensemble, Task Arithmetic, Ties Merging, Localize-and-Stitch, T-Switch.
 - **Multi-Task Learning Baselines (Suppl.):** PCGrad, CAGrad.
- **Metrics:**
 - **Planning:** Average Displacement Error (ADE↓), Collision Rate (Col. rate↓, threshold 0.6m), Final Displacement Error (FDE↓), Miss Rate (MR↓, threshold 0.5m).
 - **Prediction:** ADE↓, FDE↓.
- **Implementation:** NVIDIA RTX 3090/V100 GPUs, Adam optimizer. Specific hyperparameters (batch sizes, epochs, learning rates) are detailed in the supplementary materials for pretraining, fine-tuning, and DPT. A mask ratio of K=1% is primarily used for Sparse Merging unless otherwise specified.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides empirical evidence for the existence of "Skill Conflict" and demonstrates the effectiveness of Disjoint Parameter Training (DPT) and Sparse Merging in mitigating it.

- **Empirical Validation of Skill Conflict:** The initial analysis (Fig. 2, 3) confirmed that in compact unified models, increasing parameter utilization for individual tasks during merging leads to performance saturation and a significant increase in parameter overlap (Skill Conflict). This finding was consistent across different backbones, including DTPP (Suppl. Fig. 4, 5), and more pronounced in models with limited capacity (Suppl. Fig. 6). Controlled experiments demonstrated a causal link between parameter overlap and performance degradation (Suppl. Table 5).
- **Applicability of DPT to Various Merging Methods:** DPT's strategy of separating training regions for task-specific models improved performance when applied in conjunction with existing model merging methods like Task Arithmetic, Ties Merging, and T-Switch, as well as the proposed Sparse Merging (Table 1, Suppl. Table 7, 9). This suggests that DPT is a broadly applicable enhancement for preparing models for merging, regardless of the specific merging algorithm.
- **Effectiveness of Sparse Merging:** Even after DPT ensures disjoint parameter regions during training, sparse integration during merging proved crucial. Experiments showed that a high sparsity (K=1% or K=2%) yielded optimal performance (Table 3). This indicates that selectively integrating only the most influential parameters mitigates residual interference that can arise from adjacent feature interactions, even if the primary parameter regions are separated.
- **DPT Mitigates Skill Conflict:** DPT enabled models to maintain high performance for both prediction and planning tasks, even when a larger proportion of parameters was allocated to each (Fig. 5). This contrasts with the performance degradation and saturation observed without DPT (Fig. 2), demonstrating DPT's ability to localize and efficiently encode each task's core skill within compact parameter areas. Analysis of DPT's parameter allocation ratios indicated that smaller learnable regions encourage more compact skill representation (Table 2, Suppl. Table 3).
- **Superior Performance against Baselines:** The proposed DPT combined with Sparse Merging (DPT + SM) achieved superior performance across all planning and prediction metrics on the JRDB dataset compared to other unified architectures (DIPP, DTPP) and various model merging baselines (Table 4). Notably, the Ensemble method, which averages inference results of independently fine-tuned models, performed better than other merging strategies without DPT, supporting the notion that parameter-level conflict is a primary challenge. MTL baselines like PCGrad and CAGrad showed skewed capacity towards one task, further supporting the need for parameter-level separation.
- **Importance of Joint Reasoning:** An additional fine-tuning stage with a joint task loss, applied to the remaining inactive parameter regions after DPT and Sparse Merging, further improved overall performance (DPT + SM + JR in Table 4). This indicates that while DPT isolates core task-specific skills, allowing for subsequent joint reasoning on the remaining capacity enhances the inherent advantage of unified models.
- **Qualitative Validation:** Qualitative results on the JRDB dataset (Fig. 6, Suppl. Fig. 2, 3) demonstrated that the proposed method produced more accurate predictions for surrounding agents and safer plans for the ego agent compared to baseline methods, especially in dense interaction scenarios.
- **Robustness and Generalization:** Sensitivity analyses confirmed DPT's robustness to mask allocation schedules and random seeds (Suppl. Table 2, 4). The concept of Skill Conflict was found to extend beyond just planning and prediction, occurring even between sub-tasks of prediction (e.g., based on velocity or distance), with deeper network layers exhibiting higher conflict (Suppl. Table 6, 8).
- **Efficiency:** While DPT incurred a moderately higher training cost than some baselines (due to mask management), it introduces no additional inference-time cost once deployed. The resulting model is computationally efficient, with low latency, GFLOPs, and memory usage, suitable for edge devices (Table 10, 11).
- **Adaptability to End-to-End Autonomous Driving:** The framework's scalability was demonstrated by applying DPT and Sparse Merging to an end-to-end autonomous driving model (HiP-AD). This application improved both closed-loop (Route Completion, Driving Score) and open-loop (L2 distance, collision rate, perception metrics) performance on the Bench2Drive dataset (Suppl. Table 12), suggesting broader applicability beyond robot navigation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes significantly to the field of autonomous systems, particularly in the domain of social robot navigation and end-to-end autonomous driving, with several implications:

- **Fundamental Understanding of Multi-Task Learning:** The explicit identification and detailed empirical analysis of "Skill Conflict" in compact unified models represent a foundational contribution. This phenomenon, where distinct tasks compete for overlapping parameter regions in shared encoders, was previously overlooked in the context of compact models, especially since model merging research focused on large-scale models where such conflicts are less acute. This work provides a deeper understanding of the challenges in multi-task learning for resource-constrained environments.
- **Novel Methodological Framework:** The introduction of Disjoint Parameter Training (DPT) and Sparse Merging offers a novel and effective methodological framework to address Skill Conflict. DPT's approach of separating learnable parameter regions during task-specific fine-tuning, followed by Sparse Merging's selective integration, provides a principled way to manage task interference and preserve individual task capabilities within a single, compact model.
- **Enhanced Performance for Edge Deployment:** The proposed framework enables the development of unified prediction and planning models that achieve superior performance in terms of prediction accuracy and planning safety. Crucially, it does so without increasing model size or inference latency, making these advanced capabilities deployable on resource-constrained edge devices. This can lead to more reliable, efficient, and user-friendly mobile service robots and autonomous vehicles in crowded, interactive scenarios.
- **Versatility and Generalizability:** The demonstrated applicability of DPT to various existing model merging methods, different backbone architectures (Social-Transmotion, DTPP, HiP-AD), and even within sub-tasks of prediction, highlights its versatility. Its successful extension to end-to-end autonomous driving further underscores its potential to improve multi-task learning across diverse domains in robotics and AI. This broad applicability suggests DPT could become a valuable technique for a wide range of multi-task learning problems where parameter-level interference is a concern.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** This work opens new avenues for future research. It encourages further exploration into adaptive mechanisms for DPT hyperparameters (e.g., allocation ratio, mask ratio, allocation order) and more generalized task grouping strategies for broader multi-task settings. Understanding and mitigating Skill Conflict can also inform the design of future unified architectures to be inherently more robust to task interference.

In summary, this paper provides a robust solution to a critical problem in compact multi-task learning, enabling safer and more efficient autonomous navigation systems for real-world deployment.
