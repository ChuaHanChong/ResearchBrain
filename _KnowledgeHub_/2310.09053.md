# DATT

> [!summary] Summary
> **DATT** (Deep Adaptive Trajectory Tracking) precisely tracks arbitrary, even infeasible, quadrotor trajectories under large disturbances. It uses a ==feedforward-feedback-adaptive control structure== trained in simulation with RL, augmented at deployment with an ==L1 adaptive-control== disturbance estimator in closed loop, with no fine-tuning on hardware.

## Problem

- Precise arbitrary-trajectory tracking for quadrotors is hard due to unknown nonlinear dynamics, trajectory infeasibility, and actuation limits.
- Adaptive nonlinear and model-predictive controllers fail on infeasible trajectories and in unsteady wind, and are often too slow for onboard real-time control.

## Method

- A novel ==feedforward-feedback-adaptive== control structure trained in simulation via ==reinforcement learning==.
- At deployment, augmented with a disturbance estimator using ==L1 adaptive control== in closed loop, requiring no hardware fine-tuning.

## Results

- Significantly outperforms competitive adaptive nonlinear and MPC controllers on both feasible-smooth and infeasible trajectories in unsteady wind, including scenarios where baselines completely fail.
- Runs online with inference time **< 3.2 ms**, less than **1/4** of the adaptive-nonlinear-MPC baseline.

## Takeaways

- Zero-shot sim-to-real quadrotor tracking via RL + L1 adaptive control; a common baseline for learned adaptive control and disturbance rejection on agile aerial robots.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The precise and agile control of quadrotors for trajectory tracking is a central challenge in the field of unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs), with implications for applications such as drone delivery, rescue operations, and urban air mobility. This research addresses several persistent difficulties in achieving accurate trajectory following:

1. **Nonlinear and Underactuated Dynamics:** Quadrotor dynamics are inherently nonlinear and underactuated. Furthermore, system parameters, such as motor characteristics, can be unknown, and environmental factors like wind gusts introduce significant uncertainty, making accurate modeling challenging.
2. **Actuation Limits:** Aggressive flight maneuvers require operating at the hardware's performance limits. Controllers must account for actuation constraints, particularly for quadrotors with lower thrust-to-weight ratios.
3. **Trajectory Infeasibility:** Desired trajectories are not always dynamically feasible, meaning they cannot be followed exactly. This necessitates control strategies that can reason about future states and optimize performance over a long horizon, even when exact tracking is impossible.

Traditional control strategies for quadrotors fall into two main categories:

- **Nonlinear Control based on Differential Flatness:** These methods offer proven stability and computational efficiency. However, they are typically limited to differentially flat trajectories, which are smooth and have bounded higher-order derivatives (velocity, acceleration, jerk, snap), and must satisfy actuation constraints. They struggle with non-differentiable or highly constrained trajectories.
- **Model Predictive Control (MPC):** MPC approaches can incorporate system dynamics and constraints, potentially handling non-smooth and arbitrary trajectories. Nevertheless, their performance is contingent upon the accuracy of the underlying dynamic model and the optimality of the solver for often non-convex optimization problems. MPC can also be computationally intensive for real-time applications.

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has emerged as an alternative, demonstrating flexibility and efficiency in certain trajectory tracking problems. However, many existing RL-based quadrotor controllers have focused primarily on tracking smooth trajectories in stationary environments.

This work, Deep Adaptive Trajectory Tracking (DATT), positions itself by integrating learning-based control with adaptive mechanisms to overcome the limitations of both traditional and prior RL approaches. It aims to develop a controller capable of tracking arbitrary, potentially infeasible trajectories in the presence of significant, unknown disturbances, while maintaining computational efficiency for real-world deployment. The novelty lies in its feedforward-feedback-adaptive control structure, which is trained using RL in simulation and augmented with L1 adaptive control for disturbance estimation on real hardware without fine-tuning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this research are to develop an RL-based flight controller for quadrotors that exhibits:

1. **High Accuracy on Feasible Trajectories:** The controller should be capable of tracking smooth, feasible trajectories with an accuracy comparable to or exceeding traditional nonlinear controllers and Model Predictive Control (MPC) approaches.
2. **Robust Tracking of Arbitrary and Infeasible Trajectories:** The system should accurately follow arbitrary trajectories, including those that are dynamically infeasible or non-differentiable, pushing the limits of the hardware platform's capabilities. This addresses scenarios where traditional methods often fail, such as trajectories with sharp turns or abrupt changes in direction.
3. **Online Adaptation to Unknown System Parameters and Environmental Uncertainties:** The controller must adapt to unknown or changing system parameters and uncertain environmental conditions (e.g., wind gusts, payload changes) in real-time without prior knowledge of these disturbances, thus enabling robust operation in varied real-world environments.

The motivation for these objectives stems from the identified limitations of existing quadrotor control paradigms. Applications such as drone delivery, search and rescue, and urban air mobility require not only precise flight but also the ability to handle unexpected environmental perturbations and execute maneuvers that might exceed predefined dynamic feasibility. Traditional methods, particularly differential flatness-based controllers, are restrictive regarding trajectory smoothness and constraint handling. While MPC offers some advantages in constraint incorporation, its computational cost and reliance on accurate models can hinder real-time performance and robustness to unmodeled disturbances. Existing RL approaches, while promising, have often not sufficiently addressed the combined challenges of infeasible trajectory tracking and real-world adaptation to significant, unknown disturbances. DATT aims to bridge this gap by offering a control solution that is simultaneously accurate, flexible, robust, and computationally efficient.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Deep Adaptive Trajectory Tracking (DATT) system integrates reinforcement learning with adaptive control principles to achieve robust and precise quadrotor trajectory tracking.

#### 3.1. Algorithm Overview

DATT employs a novel feedforward-feedback-adaptive control architecture. A neural network policy ($\pi$) is learned using model-free reinforcement learning. This policy is designed to track arbitrary reference trajectories, including infeasible ones, and adapt to diverse environmental disturbances. The policy's control output, $u_t$, consists of desired total thrust ($f_{\Sigma,des}$) and desired body rates ($\omega_{des}$).

The policy is conditioned on two main components:
1. **Feedforward Embedding ($h_t$):** This embedding is generated by an encoder network ($\phi$) that processes a fixed horizon of future desired reference positions ($p_d$) in the body frame. This allows the controller to incorporate future trajectory information.
2. **Disturbance Estimation ($\hat{d}_t$):** In training, the policy has access to the true translational disturbance ($d_t$). During real-world deployment, this disturbance is estimated in closed-loop using an L1 adaptive control mechanism.

The state input to the policy ($\pi$) includes the current position ($p_t$), velocity ($v_t$), and orientation ($q_t$, represented as a quaternion), with position and velocity transformed into the body frame. Additionally, the feedback error $R_t^\top (p_t - p_{d_t})$ and the estimated disturbance $\hat{d}_t$ are provided.

The system is trained by maximizing an expected reward function ($J(\pi|p_{d_{t:t+H}})$), which includes terms penalizing position error ($\|p_t - p_{d_t}\|$), yaw error ($\| \psi_t \|$), and a small velocity regularization penalty ($\|v_t\|$). Both the policy network ($\pi$) and the feedforward encoder ($\phi$) are jointly optimized using the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm.

#### 3.2. Arbitrary Trajectory Tracking

Unlike classical controllers that rely on higher-order derivatives of reference trajectories, which may not exist for arbitrary or infeasible paths, DATT's RL-based approach learns to track trajectories given only future desired positions. The feedforward encoder $\phi$ processes a sequence of desired future positions ($p_{d_t}, \ldots, p_{d_{t+H}}$), transformed into the body frame, to generate a feedforward embedding that captures the essence of the future trajectory. For simplicity, the desired yaw for all trajectories is assumed to be zero.

#### 3.3. Adaptation to Disturbance

During simulation training, DATT is exposed to randomized, time-varying force perturbations ($d$) to teach robustness. For real-world deployment, an L1 adaptive control algorithm is integrated to estimate this translational disturbance ($\hat{d}$). This L1 adaptive controller operates by:
1. Building a closed-loop estimator to predict velocity ($\hat{v}$) based on current state and estimated disturbance.
2. Computing the difference between predicted and actual velocity to infer a new disturbance estimate ($\hat{d}_{new}$).
3. Updating the disturbance estimate ($\hat{d}$) using a low-pass filter on $\hat{d}_{new}$.
The estimated disturbance $\hat{d}$ is then directly fed into the policy network. The L1 adaptive control approach is chosen for its reactive nature, robustness, and provable stability and convergence properties in closed-loop. The paper notes that DATT provides a general framework, and other disturbance estimation techniques (e.g., Rapid Motor Adaptation - RMA) could be used, though L1 adaptive control demonstrated superior robustness in real-world conditions.

#### 3.4. Simulation and Training Details

- **Simulator:** A custom quadrotor simulator is used, implementing dynamics with on-manifold integration. A first-order time delay model simulates the lower-level angular velocity and thrust controllers. The simulator runs at 50 Hz.
- **Training Trajectories:** Training includes a mix of xy-planar smooth trajectories (randomized degree-five polynomials and chained polynomials with continuous derivatives) and infeasible "zigzag" trajectories (linear segments connecting random waypoints, leading to zero or undefined acceleration).
- **Disturbance Model:** In simulation, a random time-varying force perturbation ($d$) is applied, initially randomized in direction and strength, then modeled as Brownian motion (d $\leftarrow$ d + $\epsilon$). This aims to approximate complex time- and state-dependent disturbances.
- **Training Process:** Episodes run for 10 seconds (500 steps). A feedforward horizon of 0.6 seconds (10 reference terms) is used. Stable training required an initial phase of 2.5 million steps with a fixed reference trajectory before full randomization began. Training uses PPO for 20 million steps, taking approximately 3 hours on an NVIDIA 3080 GPU.

#### 3.5. Hardware Setup

Experiments are conducted on a Bitcraze Crazyflie 2.1 quadrotor, weighing 40g with upgraded motors, resulting in a thrust-to-weight ratio slightly under 2.
- **State Estimation:** Position and velocity feedback are provided by an OptiTrack motion capture system at 50 Hz to an offboard computer. The Crazyflie provides orientation estimates.
- **Control Commands:** Control commands (desired body rates $\omega_{des}$ and total thrust $f_{\Sigma,des}$) are sent to the quadrotor at 50 Hz via a 2.4 GHz radio using the Crazyswarm API.
- **Low-level Control:** Onboard firmware handles a custom PI attitude rate controller that converts desired body rates to torque commands. This, along with desired total thrust, is then mapped to individual motor thrusts.

#### 3.6. Baselines

DATT's performance is compared against several baselines:
- **Nonlinear Tracking Controller:** A differential flatness-based controller comprising a PID position controller and a tilt-prioritized nonlinear attitude controller.
- **L1 Adaptive Control:** The nonlinear tracking controller augmented with the L1 adaptive control disturbance estimator.
- **Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI):** A sampling-based nonlinear optimal control technique, using a known dynamics model and a cost function based on position error norms over a finite horizon.
- **L1-MPC:** MPPI augmented with the L1 adaptive control disturbance estimation.
- **Rapid Motor Adaptation (RMA):** A supervised learning-based approach (denoted DATT-RMA) where an adaptation network ($\psi$) predicts the disturbance ($\hat{d}$) from a history of state-action pairs, instead of using L1 adaptive control.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments evaluated DATT's performance across various scenarios, including feasible and infeasible trajectories, both with and without environmental disturbances.

#### 4.1. Arbitrary Trajectory Tracking Without External Disturbances

Table 1 (from the paper) shows the average tracking error in meters for smooth and infeasible trajectories without any environmental disturbances.

- **Accuracy:** DATT achieved the lowest tracking errors. For smooth trajectories, DATT (0.049 m) showed approximately 34-36% smaller errors than L1 adaptive control (0.091 m) and L1-MPC (0.088 m). For infeasible trajectories, DATT (0.083 m) demonstrated a 54% smaller error compared to L1-MPC (0.181 m).
- **Robustness to Infeasible Trajectories:** The nonlinear tracking control and L1 adaptive control baselines consistently crashed when attempting to track infeasible trajectories with the tuned gains. L1-MPC could track them but with higher error. DATT successfully tracked both types of trajectories.
- **Computational Efficiency:** DATT exhibited an average inference time of 3.17 ms, which is less than a quarter of L1-MPC's 13.10 ms. DATT without adaptation (DATT w/ $\hat{d}=0$) also showed low inference time (2.41 ms).

#### 4.2. Adaptation Performance in Unknown Wind Fields with a Drag Plate

Table 2 (from the paper) presents tracking errors for scenarios involving an attached cardboard drag plate and/or unsteady wind fields, representing significant disturbances not explicitly modeled during simulation training. This evaluates zero-shot generalization capabilities.

- **Zero-Shot Generalization and Adaptation:** DATT, with adaptation enabled, successfully tracked all tested trajectories (smooth and infeasible, with plate and/or wind). This contrasts with the L1 adaptive control baseline, which crashed on all infeasible trajectories under these disturbed conditions.
- **Improved Accuracy with Disturbances:**
 - For smooth trajectories with a drag plate, DATT (0.063 m) achieved smaller errors compared to L1 adaptive control (0.163 m) and L1-MPC (0.121 m).
 - In the most challenging scenario (infeasible trajectories with both plate and wind), DATT (0.161 m) significantly outperformed L1-MPC (0.243 m) by approximately 34% error reduction, while maintaining its computational advantage.
 - For smooth trajectories with plate and wind, DATT (0.095m) achieved up to 48% smaller errors than L1 adaptive control (0.184m).
- **Comparison with RMA:** DATT-RMA, an alternative adaptation method using a learned network, showed generally higher tracking errors in real-world experiments (e.g., 0.193 m for infeasible traj. w/ plate & wind) compared to DATT (0.161 m) using L1 adaptive control. This suggested a "sim2real gap" for the RMA adaptation network, where its performance degraded significantly when transferred from simulation to reality.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies (Simulation)

Ablation studies in simulation (Table 3 in Appendix A) highlighted several architectural and training considerations:
- **Body Frame Inputs:** Transforming state inputs (position, velocity, reference positions) into the body frame was critical for accurate tracking, with the "No body frame" ablation resulting in divergence.
- **Fixed Initial Reference:** Training stability and performance improved significantly by fixing the reference trajectory for the initial 2.5 million training steps.
- **Feedforward Horizon:** An optimal feedforward horizon existed, with 0.6 seconds (10 reference terms) yielding the best performance, indicating the importance of future trajectory information.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The DATT framework represents a significant advancement in robust and agile quadrotor control, particularly for real-world deployment. Its contributions and potential impact are multifaceted:

1. **Enhanced Trajectory Tracking Capabilities:** DATT addresses the long-standing challenge of tracking arbitrary and dynamically infeasible trajectories. By outperforming established nonlinear and MPC controllers, especially for complex and non-smooth paths, DATT expands the operational envelope for quadrotors. This is crucial for applications demanding complex maneuvers or operating in environments where ideal, smooth trajectories cannot be guaranteed.

2. **Robustness to Unknown Disturbances with Zero-Shot Generalization:** The integration of L1 adaptive control for real-time disturbance estimation enables DATT to adapt effectively to unknown and time-varying environmental perturbations (e.g., wind gusts, attached payloads) without requiring retraining or fine-tuning. The demonstrated zero-shot generalization to highly dynamic and unmodeled wind conditions with a drag plate highlights its practical utility in diverse and unpredictable real-world scenarios, which is often a bottleneck for learning-based methods.

3. **Computational Efficiency:** DATT's inference time, notably less than a quarter of the L1-MPC baseline, is a critical factor for real-time control on resource-constrained embedded systems commonly found in UAVs. This efficiency allows for faster control loops and potentially more responsive flight.

4. **Novel Hybrid Control Architecture:** The combination of a reinforcement learning-trained feedforward-feedback policy with a classical, provably stable L1 adaptive control module for disturbance estimation offers a robust and effective hybrid control paradigm. This approach leverages the flexibility of RL to learn complex control policies for various trajectories while benefiting from the theoretical guarantees and real-time reactivity of adaptive control for disturbance rejection.

5. **Implications for UAV Applications:** The capabilities demonstrated by DATT directly support the "commoditization of unmanned aerial vehicles" for tasks requiring high precision and agility. This includes, but is not limited to, autonomous drone delivery in urban environments, intricate inspection tasks, rapid search and rescue operations, and complex aerial cinematography. The ability to handle unexpected conditions and challenging trajectories reduces operational risks and expands potential use cases.

6. **Framework for Future Research:** DATT provides a general framework for adaptive control in learned policies, as noted by its flexibility to potentially integrate other disturbance estimation or model learning techniques. This opens avenues for future research to explore alternative adaptive mechanisms, improve simulator fidelity, or extend to more agile 3D maneuvers, further enhancing the robustness and capabilities of autonomous quadrotor flight.

While the paper acknowledges limitations such as hardware thrust-to-weight ratio restricting extreme maneuvers, simplified simulator dynamics, and the typical variance associated with RL training, the core methodology presents a significant step towards developing resilient and high-performance quadrotor control systems for complex, real-world applications.
