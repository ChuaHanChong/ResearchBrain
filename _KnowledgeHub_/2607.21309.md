# Factorized Spatio-Temporal Convolutions for Human Pose Estimation from Planar Lidar

> [!summary] Summary
> A lightweight deep learning architecture, ST-Block, enables omnidirectional human detection and 2D relative pose estimation from planar LiDAR sequences through factorized spatio-temporal convolutions and masked cross-modal self-supervision. The system achieved a 20% reduction in position error and an 11.7% reduction in orientation error compared to a baseline, running efficiently on CPU-only processors, and demonstrated successful self-supervised adaptation to new environments.

## Problem

- Service robots need robust human perception (detection, position, orientation) for safe and socially aware interaction, especially in shared environments.
- Many mobile robots rely on cost-effective, omnidirectional planar LiDARs and modest onboard CPUs, which pose challenges due to sparse, ambiguous scans and limited computational resources.
- Existing human pose estimation solutions often depend on high-fidelity sensors (e.g., cameras, 3D LiDAR) or GPU-class compute, and traditional supervised learning for LiDAR requires extensive manual annotation.

## Method

- Developed a deep neural network leveraging ==Factorized Spatio-Temporal (**ST-Block**) convolutions== that explicitly separates spatial processing along LiDAR rays from temporal aggregation across scans.
- Implemented a ==masked cross-modal self-supervision== strategy, utilizing an RGB-D body tracker to generate pseudo-labels for LiDAR data within its field of view, removing the need for manual LiDAR annotations.
- Designed a lightweight perception pipeline capable of omnidirectional human presence, distance, and relative orientation estimation from planar LiDAR, optimized for real-time execution on CPU processors.

## Results

- The ST-Block model achieved a **20%** reduction in position error (from **13.2 cm** to **10.6 cm**) and an **11.7%** reduction in orientation error (from **37.5°** to **33.1°**), alongside a **2.7%** improvement in detection precision (P80) compared to a parameter-matched baseline.
- Demonstrated real-time inference on a robot CPU, with end-to-end times of **2.87-3.36 ms**, proving its suitability for deployment on resource-constrained mobile robotic platforms.
- Showcased rapid and substantial performance improvements during self-supervised adaptation, matching the best performance of directly trained models after only three simulated 'days' of operation in a new environment.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Factorizing spatial and temporal convolutions within the ST-Block architecture improves accuracy and parameter efficiency for processing circular planar LiDAR sequences compared to monolithic convolutional approaches.
> - Masked cross-modal self-supervision effectively mitigates label scarcity and enables rapid adaptation of the model to new robotic platforms and environments without requiring manual re-annotation.
> - Convolutional networks can generalize knowledge learned in a narrow, supervised sensor region (e.g., RGB-D camera FOV) to an entire 360-degree LiDAR field of view, providing comprehensive situational awareness.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The increasing deployment of robots in environments shared with humans, such as service robots in public spaces and household platforms, necessitates robust human perception capabilities. These capabilities include localizing nearby humans and estimating their relative pose, particularly their facing direction, which is crucial for safe navigation and socially aware Human-Robot Interaction (HRI).

Current approaches to human detection and pose estimation primarily focus on high-fidelity sensing modalities like cameras and 3D LiDAR, or assume access to GPU-class computational resources. However, many service robots are constrained by cost, power, and integration factors, often relying on omnidirectional planar (2D) LiDARs and modest onboard processors. Planar LiDARs offer 360-degree coverage and robustness to lighting conditions but provide sparse and ambiguous scans. Challenges include differentiating human-like clutter from actual people and inferring facing direction due to the approximate front-back symmetry of the human body in a 2D cross-section. Existing 2D LiDAR-based methods predominantly address people detection and 2D position estimation, with fewer solutions for the more complex problem of estimating human orientation.

This research addresses these gaps by proposing a lightweight, planar LiDAR-only pipeline for omnidirectional human detection and relative 2D pose estimation (including position and orientation) that operates efficiently on CPU-level compute. It integrates principles from spatio-temporal modeling, which have shown benefits in video understanding by factorizing spatial and temporal processing, and cross-modal self-supervision, a technique used to mitigate label scarcity and domain shift by leveraging complementary sensor data for pseudo-label generation. By focusing on planar LiDAR and resource-constrained environments, this work contributes to making advanced human perception more accessible for a broader range of mobile robotic platforms.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the increasing integration of robots into human-centric environments and the consequent requirement for enhanced human perception. Service robots, ranging from hospital assistants to robot vacuums, need to perceive people to enable behaviors such as approaching, yielding, and initiating interaction while ensuring safety. Current technical challenges hinder this capability:

- **Sensor Constraints:** Many mobile robots, including service platforms, primarily utilize omnidirectional planar LiDARs for robust, all-weather range sensing due to cost and integration considerations. However, individual 2D LiDAR scans are sparse and ambiguous, making accurate human detection and, particularly, orientation estimation challenging.
- **Computational Constraints:** Service robots are often equipped with modest onboard processors, precluding the direct application of computationally intensive vision-based or 3D LiDAR-based pose estimation pipelines that typically require GPU-class compute.
- **Annotation Burden:** Traditional supervised learning approaches for LiDAR-based perception require extensive manual annotation, which is resource-intensive and impractical for diverse deployment scenarios.

Given these motivations, the research established several key objectives:

- **Develop Factorized Spatio-Temporal Convolutions (ST-Block):** To effectively process circular 2D LiDAR sequences, the researchers aimed to design a neural network block that explicitly separates spatial processing along scan rays from temporal aggregation across scans. This architectural design was intended to improve accuracy and optimization compared to monolithic kernels that intertwine these dimensions.
- **Implement Masked Cross-Modal Self-Supervision:** To overcome the need for manual LiDAR labels and facilitate on-site adaptation, a key objective was to leverage a reliable narrow-Field of View (FOV) RGB-D body tracker. This tracker would provide supervision signals within its overlap region with the LiDAR, enabling self-supervised training with a masked loss function.
- **Achieve Omnidirectional Planar-LiDAR Pose Perception on Onboard Compute:** The overarching goal was to create a lightweight perception pipeline capable of predicting per-ray human presence, distance, and relative orientation from planar LiDAR data across a full 360-degree FOV. This system needed to run efficiently on typical onboard CPU processors of service robots, demonstrated through quantitative evaluation, real-time benchmarks, and in-field validation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology involves a deep learning model, termed Space-Time Block (ST-Block), designed for omnidirectional human detection and 2D relative pose estimation from sequences of planar LiDAR readings. The model is trained using a masked cross-modal self-supervision strategy.

#### 3.1. Input Preprocessing

The model's input consists of a temporal window of `N` LiDAR scans. These scans are reprojected to the robot's current pose using onboard odometry, which helps align static structures across scans and highlights moving objects. The robot setup utilizes two physical planar LiDARs: a front-facing Sick TiM561 and a rear-facing YDLIDAR TG15. These are combined into a single virtual omnidirectional LiDAR. Readings are time-synchronized at 10 Hz, projected onto a 2D plane, and aggregated into 1-degree angular bins, resulting in 360 evenly spaced rays covering the full 360-degree field of view. Each bin is assigned the distance of the closest point within it, or a default value of 10 meters if no points are present. This preprocessing yields an input tensor of size `360 (rays) × N (time steps)`. In experiments, `N` was set to 30, corresponding to a 3-second temporal window.

#### 3.2. Network Architecture: Space-Time Blocks

The core of the network is the ST-Block architecture, which explicitly factorizes spatial and temporal processing. Given an input tensor `X ∈ R^T_in × R × C_in` (timesteps, rays, channels):
- **Spatial Convolutions:** For each timestep `t` independently, 1D circular convolutions are applied along the spatial dimension (rays). This preserves angular continuity across the 360-degree LiDAR scan. The output is an intermediate representation `H_t` for each timestep, collected into `H ∈ R^T_in × R × C_m`.
- **Temporal Convolutions:** The `H` activations are then processed by a shallow Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN) that aggregates information across timesteps. These convolutions use a stride equal to the kernel size, compressing the sequence length (e.g., `T_out = T_in / k`). The output is `Z ∈ R^T_out × R × C_out`.
- **Residual Connections (Optional):** Each ST-Block can include a residual path where the input is downsampled temporally via average pooling and then projected with a 1D convolution to match channel dimensions before being added to the output of the temporal convolution.
- **Skip Connections (Optional):** To expose multi-scale information, skip connections from the outputs of intermediate ST-Blocks can be used. The activations from the most recent timestep of each block are projected with a 1D convolution and concatenated along the channel dimension before being fed to the prediction head.

The complete network consists of a sequence of `B` ST-Blocks. After the final block (or concatenated features from skip connections), a linear layer acts as a prediction head. For each ray, the model outputs four channels: human presence `ˆp ∈`, radial distance `ˆd`, and relative orientation encoded as `sin(ˆo)` and `cos(ˆo)`. This local, ray-centric representation for orientation promotes generalization across the omnidirectional field of view. Human detections are obtained by thresholding `ˆp` and applying non-maximum suppression.

#### 3.3. Masked Loss Function and Self-supervised Training

Training data labels are generated via cross-modal pseudo-label supervision. An off-the-shelf Microsoft Azure Kinect RGB-D body tracker is used to estimate 3D skeletons. The pelvis pose is extracted, transformed to the robot's base frame, and projected onto the ground plane to obtain 2D position and facing direction. Ray-wise labels are produced: `p` (binary presence), `d` (distance), and `o` (relative orientation). Distance and orientation labels are only assigned to rays where a person is detected.

The model is trained using a masked regression loss `L`, which is the sum of three equally weighted Mean Squared Error (MSE) terms:
`L = L_p + L_d + L_o`
Where `L_p = MSE(p, ˆp)`, `L_d = MSE(d, ˆd)`, and `L_o = 0.5 * [MSE(sin o, sin ˆo) + MSE(cos o, cos ˆo)]`. The `ˆo` is recovered from `atan2(sin ˆo, cos ˆo)`. Crucially, these error terms are enforced only within the limited FOV of the Azure Kinect camera, which serves as the supervision source. This masked loss allows the network's convolutional architecture to generalize knowledge learned in the supervised region to the entire 360-degree LiDAR coverage.

The networks are trained using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 1 × 10⁻⁴ and a batch size of 64 for 250 epochs. Data augmentation includes adding Gaussian noise and mirroring LiDAR readings and labels.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup and Dataset

Data was collected over 14 days in four diverse environments (Corridor, Break Area, Office, Lab) using a customized PAL Robotics TIAGo robot equipped with the Azure Kinect and the two planar LiDARs. A portion of the Lab environment (8k samples) included ground truth human and robot poses from an OptiTrack motion capture system. The dataset was split by separating recordings from different days to ensure realistic generalization in training, validation, and test sets.

For comparison, a baseline model, a 1D Fully Convolutional Network (FCN) from prior work, was implemented with a similar number of parameters and the same effective spatial and temporal receptive fields (43 rays and 30 timesteps). This baseline processes LiDAR history by stacking scans as additional input channels, implicitly mixing spatial and temporal information.

#### 3.5. Public Benchmark: FROG Dataset

The model was also benchmarked on the FROG dataset, which provides knee-high 2D LiDAR scans and ground-truth 2D person positions. This benchmark was restricted to presence and distance prediction due to the absence of orientation annotations in FROG. Two variants were tested: a low-resolution model (180 rays) and a high-resolution model (720 rays with an adapter layer).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of the proposed ST-Block architecture was conducted using motion-capture ground truth from the Lab environment test set and on the public FROG dataset. Performance metrics included Average Precision (AP), Precision at 80% recall (P80), various error metrics for distance, position, and orientation, and the Pearson correlation coefficient for orientation.

#### 4.1. ST-Block Performance vs. Baseline

The simplest configuration of the ST-Block model, denoted "Ours", demonstrated consistent outperformance compared to the parameter-matched 1D fully convolutional baseline. At an operating point targeting 80% recall:
- **Distance Error (E_d):** Reduced by 29.4% (from 10.7 cm to 7.55 cm).
- **Position Error (E_p):** Reduced by 20% (from 13.2 cm to 10.6 cm).
- **Detection Precision (P80):** Improved from 73.8% to 76.5%.
- **Orientation Error (E_θ):** Reduced by 11.7% (from 37.5° to 33.1°).
- **Orientation Correlation (ρ_θ):** Increased from 84.4% to 86.5%.
These improvements were achieved with a modest increase in model parameters (from 32,356 for the baseline to 40,100 for ST-Block variants), indicating more efficient use of parameters by the factorized architecture.

#### 4.2. ST-Block Ablation Study

An ablation study explored the impact of residual connections (R) within ST-Blocks and skip connections (S) from block outputs to the prediction head.
- The configuration with skip connections and no residuals, "Ours (-S)", yielded the lowest position errors (`E_p` = 9.5 cm, `E_d` = 6.6 cm).
- Adding residual connections, as in "Ours (RS)", improved orientation estimation (`E_θ` = 32.0°, `ρ_θ` = 87.2%) but resulted in slightly higher position errors.
The study indicated that different architectural choices can lead to trade-offs between position and orientation accuracy.

#### 4.3. Benchmark on FROG Dataset

On the public FROG dataset for 2D people detection:
- "Ours (Low Resolution)" (180 rays) achieved competitive accuracy (AP 65.9%, Peak F1 70.9%) compared to lightweight baselines (LFE-Peaks, LFE-PPN).
- "Ours (High Resolution)" (720 rays with an adapter) showed only a marginal improvement (AP 66.4%, Peak F1 72.0%), suggesting that for typical human awareness tasks in mobile robotics, increasing angular resolution beyond 1 ray/degree may offer diminishing returns for computational cost.
Crucially, both ST-Block variants ran in real-time on a robot CPU (2.87-3.36 ms end-to-end inference time), demonstrating their suitability for CPU-only deployments, unlike some higher-accuracy methods (DROW3, DR-SPAAM) which reported significantly longer runtimes on GPU systems.

#### 4.4. Self-supervised Adaptation

To evaluate the cross-platform generalizability and the efficacy of self-supervised adaptation, a model pretrained on FROG (without orientation labels, which were set to zero) was evaluated on the researchers' dataset. Direct transfer yielded poor performance (AP 12.4%, E_p 28.7 cm, E_θ 92.4°).
Subsequent self-supervised adaptation, by training the model on the new robot's data over three simulated "days" of operation, demonstrated rapid and substantial improvements:
- After Day 1: AP increased to 24.5%, E_p dropped to 18.1 cm, and orientation estimation began (E_θ 48.6°).
- After Day 3: AP reached 52.6%, E_p 9.5 cm, and E_θ 33.3°, closely matching the best performance achieved with direct training on the target dataset.
This validates the method's ability to adapt to new environments and learn orientation estimation from scratch without manual annotation.

#### 4.5. In-field Demonstration

A closed-loop robot demonstration illustrated the practical utility of the omnidirectional perception module. The service robot successfully detected and tracked people, approached the closest person facing it, and performed an offering gesture. This demonstration highlighted the model's ability to generalize beyond the supervised camera FOV and produce stable detections in regions without direct supervision. A limitation observed was the difficulty in detecting partially occluded individuals or multiple people falling on the same LiDAR ray.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of robotic perception by addressing the practical challenges of human pose estimation for resource-constrained service robots equipped with planar LiDARs. The significance and potential impact of this work are multi-faceted:

- **Enabling Socially Aware HRI:** By providing robust, omnidirectional human detection and relative 2D pose (position and orientation) estimates, the research directly enhances a robot's ability to engage in safe and socially appropriate Human-Robot Interaction. This includes initiating interactions, navigating around people, and understanding human intent based on facing direction, which are critical for service robots operating in shared spaces.
- **Leveraging Ubiquitous Sensors:** The focus on planar LiDAR, a common and cost-effective sensor in mobile robotics, makes the proposed solution widely applicable. This avoids the need for more expensive 3D LiDARs or dedicated RGB-D sensors for omnidirectional human perception.
- **Computational Efficiency for Onboard Deployment:** The lightweight ST-Block architecture, designed for CPU-only inference, ensures real-time performance on typical service robot processors. This is a crucial factor for practical deployment, as it eliminates reliance on external, high-performance computing resources.
- **Reduced Annotation Burden through Self-Supervision:** The masked cross-modal self-supervision strategy eliminates the need for manual annotation of LiDAR data. This significantly reduces the cost and time associated with deploying robots in new environments, enabling rapid adaptation and continuous learning on-site.
- **Architectural Innovation:** The explicit factorization of spatial and temporal convolutions within the ST-Block for circular 2D LiDAR sequences represents an architectural contribution that demonstrates improved accuracy over monolithic approaches for this specific domain.
- **Generalization Beyond Supervised Regions:** The convolutional design of the network allows knowledge acquired from the narrow-FOV camera supervision to generalize across the entire 360-degree LiDAR field of view, providing comprehensive situational awareness.
- **Public Dataset and Benchmark Contribution:** The release of the extended dataset and benchmarking on FROG provides valuable resources for further research and standardized comparison in 2D LiDAR-based human perception.

While the approach faces inherent limitations such as challenges in heavy occlusion and multi-person situations where individuals align along the same ray, and the fundamental ambiguity of inferring precise facing direction from sparse 2D geometry, the overall work represents a notable step towards making sophisticated human perception practical and scalable for the next generation of service robots. Future work on multi-target tracking and integration of richer social cues could further amplify its impact.
