# Learning Dexterous In-Hand Manipulation with Multifingered Hands via Visuomotor Diffusion

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at KTH Royal Institute of Technology developed a framework for learning dexterous in-hand manipulation, demonstrating an 85% success rate in unscrewing a bottle lid with a four-fingered robot hand using visuomotor diffusion policies and a wrist-mounted camera. Their work includes an AR-based teleoperation system for data collection and a data filtering strategy to enhance learning robustness.

## Problem

- Achieving human-like, fine-grained dexterity for in-hand manipulation with multifingered robot hands remains a significant challenge.
- Collecting high-quality expert demonstration data for complex dexterous tasks is difficult due to limitations in existing teleoperation systems, such as calibration drift or inadequate feedback.
- Suboptimal or inconsistent demonstrations within training datasets can negatively impact the performance of learned manipulation policies.

## Method

- A custom AR-based teleoperation system was developed, utilizing a ==Meta Quest== 3 for human hand tracking and sophisticated motion retargeting to the robotic Allegro Hand, for efficient collection of expert demonstrations.
- Visuomotor diffusion policies were employed, framing robot control as a conditional denoising diffusion process that learns from offline datasets of expert demonstrations.
- A two-stage outlier removal paradigm, based on HDBSCAN clustering and GLOSH scores, was introduced to filter out low-quality or anomalous demonstrations from the collected data.

## Results

- The most effective policy configuration, utilizing a wrist-mounted camera along with joint position and joint effort data, achieved an **85%** success rate in unscrewing a bottle lid.
- Removing joint effort information from the input modalities significantly reduced the policy's success rate to **30%**, underscoring its importance for maintaining a stable grasp.
- Ablation studies indicated that using only a wrist camera (**85%** success) or only a top camera (**70%** success) performed better than using both cameras simultaneously (**55%** success), suggesting potential redundancy or noise from combined visual inputs.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Proprioceptive feedback, specifically joint effort information, is crucial for maintaining stable grasps and ensuring successful task completion in dexterous in-hand manipulation.
> - A single, well-placed wrist-mounted camera, combined with proprioceptive data (joint position and effort), can provide sufficient visual information for complex in-hand tasks, outperforming configurations that use multiple cameras.
> - Judicious removal of extreme outliers from demonstration datasets can help maintain or slightly improve policy performance by eliminating highly suboptimal data, but aggressive filtering risks removing valuable learning information.
