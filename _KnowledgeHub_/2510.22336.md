# Toward Humanoid Brain-Body Co-design: Joint Optimization of Control and Morphology for Fall Recovery

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at The Chinese University of Hong Kong, Shenzhen introduced RoboCraft, an iterative framework for the brain-body co-design of humanoid robots, specifically optimizing their morphology and control policy for fall recovery. This approach achieved an average performance improvement of 44.55% in fall recovery across seven different humanoid robot models.

## Problem

- Existing co-design methods are typically limited to simpler robots, struggling with the high complexity, degrees of freedom, and rigid-body dynamics of humanoids.
- Humanoid fall recovery, a critical capability, often focuses solely on control policy optimization for a fixed morphology, overlooking potential performance gains from physical design changes.
- Evaluating novel morphologies in co-design is computationally intensive, as it often requires training a new control policy from scratch for each design, limiting scalability.

## Method

- ==The RoboCraft== framework implements an iterative bi-level optimization approach that jointly optimizes both the morphology (body) and control policy (brain) of humanoid robots for fall recovery.
- A "Control Policy Update" phase incorporates knowledge transfer through inter-design pretraining and intra-design finetuning to efficiently evaluate morphology performance.
- A "Design Update" phase systematically searches for improved morphologies within a defined parameter space using various optimization algorithms, guided by a priority buffer of top-performing designs.

## Results

- The RoboCraft framework delivered an average performance improvement of **44.55%** in fall recovery across seven different public humanoid robot models.
- Specific robots demonstrated significant gains, with Bez1 improving by **145%** and Sigmaban by **106%** in fall recovery performance.
- An ablation study confirmed the necessity of control policy finetuning, showing that finetuned policies consistently yielded higher improvements for evolved morphologies compared to using only a static pretrained policy.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Morphological optimization can contribute substantially to performance gains, with some robots (e.g., OP3-Rot, Sigmaban) showing over 90% of improvements attributed to body changes rather than solely control adjustments.
> - Knowledge transfer mechanisms, including pretraining a shared control policy and then finetuning it, are crucial for achieving computational efficiency and scalability in co-design for complex humanoids.
> - Optimized morphologies tend to evolve towards taller structures and lower passive resistance, which directly enhances fall recovery by facilitating upright balance and controller action.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in this paper operates at the intersection of embodied intelligence, robotics, and machine learning, specifically focusing on the brain-body co-design paradigm for humanoid robots.

**Embodied Intelligence and Co-design:** The foundational premise of embodied intelligence is that a robot's physical form significantly influences its capabilities and behaviors. This concept is evident in bio-inspired robot designs, such as quadrupeds for stable walking or bipeds for upright locomotion. Brain-body co-design aims to formalize this relationship by jointly optimizing a robot's control policy (the "brain") and its physical morphology (the "body"). This approach seeks to mitigate the reliance on human intuition and manual design, allowing robots to evolve physical structures inherently aligned with desired tasks.

**Existing Co-design Efforts and Limitations:** Prior research in co-design has primarily focused on systems where the design space is less complex, such as soft robots, modular robots, or robots with regular geometric shapes and simple connections. These studies have often employed bi-level optimization, where an inner loop trains a control policy for a given morphology, and an outer loop searches for improved morphologies based on performance. While these methods have demonstrated success in their respective domains, extending them to complex rigid-bodied robots, particularly humanoids, presents significant challenges. Humanoid robots possess a high number of degrees of freedom, intricate body dynamics, complex interconnections, and strict physical constraints, leading to a vast and computationally intensive design space.

**Humanoid Fall Recovery:** Within the context of humanoid robots, fall recovery is identified as a critical capability. It serves a dual role: as a fundamental developmental milestone (similar to an infant learning to stand) and as a crucial safeguard for robust autonomy against disturbances. Traditional fall recovery methods often rely on model-based optimization and handcrafted motion trajectories, which can be computationally expensive and sensitive to disturbances. More recent approaches have utilized reinforcement learning (RL) to learn robust control policies with minimal modeling assumptions. However, these RL-based methods typically remain control-centric, tailored to individual robots, and do not explicitly optimize the robot's morphology for fall recovery. They often generalize implicitly across morphologies from observations but do not provide feedback to improve the physical design itself.

**The Research Gap:** This paper addresses the gap in the existing literature by proposing a scalable and efficient co-design framework specifically tailored for complex, rigid-bodied humanoid robots, with a particular focus on the task of fall recovery. It aims to integrate morphological optimization directly into the fall recovery learning process, allowing the robot's body to adapt and become inherently more capable of this task, rather than solely relying on control policy adjustments for a fixed morphology. This work extends the capabilities of co-design to a more challenging and practically relevant class of robots, offering a pathway toward more autonomous and resilient humanoid systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a scalable and efficient framework for brain-body co-design in humanoid robots, specifically for the task of fall recovery. This overarching goal is driven by several key motivations:

1. **Advance Humanoid Autonomy and Deployment:** Humanoid robots are considered a significant frontier in embodied intelligence due to their anthropomorphic form, which facilitates natural deployment in human environments. Realizing this potential requires equipping them with advanced capabilities, with fall recovery being a fundamental one that enhances safety and resilience and integrates with locomotion systems. The authors are motivated to move beyond manually designed humanoids towards systems that can autonomously optimize their physical form to better perform tasks.

2. **Address the Complexity of Humanoid Co-design:** The intricate nature of humanoid robots presents a substantial challenge for co-design. Their high degrees of freedom, complex body dynamics, and numerous physical constraints result in an extremely large and combinatorially complex design space. Existing co-design methods, largely applied to simpler soft or modular robots, are not directly applicable or efficient enough for humanoids. The motivation here is to develop a framework capable of navigating this complex design space while respecting physical realizability.

3. **Enhance Fall Recovery Performance through Joint Optimization:** Fall recovery is identified as a critical gateway task for humanoids. Current fall recovery solutions primarily focus on control policies for a fixed morphology. The authors are motivated to explore whether jointly optimizing both the control policy and the morphology can lead to significantly improved fall recovery capabilities. They hypothesize that morphological structures conducive to recovery, combined with policies that exploit these structures, will result in more resilient performance than optimizing control alone.

4. **Improve Efficiency in Policy Evaluation:** A significant hurdle in co-design, especially for complex robots, is the computational cost of evaluating each new morphology. Learning a task-specific control policy from scratch for every proposed design is inefficient. The authors are motivated to develop mechanisms for knowledge transfer to accelerate policy learning, thereby making the co-design process more scalable. This includes leveraging shared experiences across different robot designs and progressively refining policies as morphologies evolve.

5. **Develop a Versatile and Compatible Framework:** The authors aim to create a framework, named RoboCraft, that is compatible with multiple existing humanoid robot models and various optimization algorithms for morphology search. This versatility is motivated by the desire to provide a broadly applicable tool for the robotics community, capable of being integrated into diverse research pipelines and applicable to a range of humanoid designs.

In essence, the research is motivated by the need to develop more capable, robust, and autonomously designed humanoid robots by synergistically optimizing their physical form and control strategies, with fall recovery serving as a benchmark task for this integrated approach.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research proposes **RoboCraft**, a scalable and efficient co-design framework for humanoid robots, which iteratively optimizes both control policies and morphology for fall recovery. The approach is formulated as a bi-level optimization problem.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The co-design problem is framed as a bi-level optimization: `max_ψ max_θ J(π_θ, M(ψ))`, where `ψ` represents the robot design (morphology) from the design space `Ψ`, `θ` parameterizes the control policy `π_θ`, and `J` is the expected return of discounted cumulative rewards for a given policy and robot design `M(ψ)`.

- **Humanoid Robot Learning Environment:** The environment is modeled as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) `M(ψ) = (S, A, T_ψ, r, μ_ψ0, γ)`.
 - **State Space (S):** Captures robot posture, motion, and recent control history (27-dimensional vector: joint positions, velocities, desired targets for five sagittal joints, torso orientation/angular velocity, normalized head height, and previous action). Morphology identifiers are not included.
 - **Action Space (A):** Angular-velocity increments for a fixed set of five sagittal joints (5-dimensional vector), applied symmetrically. These increments are integrated into target joint angles tracked by a PD controller. The action space is morphology-independent.
 - **Reward Function (r):** Morphology-agnostic, designed to encourage upright recovery, smooth, and safe movements. It consists of an upright reward (head height), a torso alignment reward (pitch, active after partial rise), and penalties for joint velocity, abrupt action changes, and self-collisions.
 - **Transition Function (T_ψ) and Initial State Distribution (μ_ψ0):** These are allowed to vary with morphology `ψ`, reflecting the changes in robot dynamics and initial fall configurations.
- **Conditions for Co-design:** The problem assumes bounded rewards, compact feasible sets for policy parameters and design space, and continuity of the objective function, ensuring the existence of an optimal solution.

#### 3.2. RoboCraft Framework Overview

RoboCraft operates through an iterative feedback loop between a "Control Policy Update" (forward phase) and a "Design Update" (backward phase), as illustrated in Figure 2 and summarized in Algorithm 1.

#### 3.3. Forward Phase: Control Policy Update

This phase aims to efficiently evaluate the performance of a given morphology. It incorporates a two-fold knowledge transfer mechanism to improve sample efficiency and provide effective feedback:

- **Inter-design Transfer (Pretraining):** A shared control policy (π_θ0) is initially pretrained across multiple initial humanoid designs (e.g., Bez1, Bez2, Bez3, etc.). This policy serves as a coarse, initial evaluator of morphology performance, avoiding the need to train a policy from scratch for every new design. The policy learning uses Soft Actor–Critic (SAC) augmented with the CrossQ algorithm. Pretraining is conducted for 6 × 10^5 timesteps.
- **Intra-design Transfer (Finetuning):** As morphologies evolve within each design, the shared policy from the previous iteration (π_θi) is finetuned using trajectories collected from the current top-ranking morphologies in that specific design. This recursive process generates increasingly specialized and accurate control policies (π_θi+1) for evaluating morphology-specific adaptations, thereby refining the performance assessment. Finetuning is performed for 1 × 10^5 timesteps.

#### 3.4. Backward Phase: Design Update

This phase focuses on strategically searching for improved morphologies based on the performance feedback from the control policy.

- **Design Search Space:** Morphology modifications are applied to MuJoCo XML (MJCF) models derived from URDF files of publicly available humanoids. The design space parameters allow for:
 - **Mesh Scaling:** Adjusting the X, Y, and Z dimensions of link STL files.
 - **Mass Scaling:** Modifying the mass properties of links.
 - **Joint Attributes:** Scaling joint stiffness and damping.
 - Constraints are imposed on Denavit–Hartenberg parameters to maintain feasible body interconnections and physical limits within the simulator.
- **Optimization Algorithms (Algorithm Toolbox):** RoboCraft is compatible with various optimization algorithms for searching the next morphology, including:
 - Evolutionary Search (using tournament selection, blend crossover, Gaussian mutation).
 - Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy (CMA-ES).
 - Bayesian Optimization (using a Gaussian Process with RBF kernel and Expected Improvement acquisition function).
 - Reinforcement Learning (using a policy gradient method with an MLP).
- **Priority Buffer:** At each iteration, a priority buffer `B` stores the `k` top-performing morphologies. This buffer is crucial for balancing exploration and exploitation:
 - Morphologies in the buffer are reevaluated with the newest finetuned control policy (π_θi).
 - New morphologies are sampled by the chosen optimization algorithm.
 - The buffer is updated by retaining the `k` highest-performing morphologies from the union of reevaluated buffer candidates and newly sampled morphologies.

#### 3.5. Iterative Joint Optimization

The overall RoboCraft framework iteratively alternates between these two phases:
1. **Initialize:** Pretrain the shared control policy (π_θ0) across initial designs.
2. **Iterate:** For each design and for `Ni` iterations:
 - **Design Update (inner loop):** Sample `Ns` new morphologies using an optimization algorithm (Ω). Evaluate them with the current control policy (π_θi). Reevaluate morphologies in the priority buffer. Update the priority buffer `B` with the top-`K` performing morphologies.
 - **Control Policy Update (outer loop):** Collect trajectories from the morphologies in the updated priority buffer `B`. Finetune the current control policy (π_θi) to generate a new, more specialized policy (π_θi+1).
This process yields a specialized control policy and a set of top-`k` evolved morphologies for each initial design.

#### 3.6. Experimental Setup Details

- **Robots:** Seven open-source humanoids (Bez1, Bez2, Bez3, OP3-Rot, Sigmaban, Wolfgang, NUGUS).
- **Simulation:** MuJoCo simulator, 20 Hz control frequency, randomized initial fall poses, domain randomization.
- **Policy Network:** MLP with three layers ( hidden sizes) for SAC.
- **Hyperparameters:** Learning rate 1 × 10^-3, discount factor γ = 0.99 for SAC.
- **Iterations:** 100 iterations for design updates, 3 rounds of 10 morphologies sampled per iteration, priority buffer size of 5.
- **Evaluation:** Each sampled morphology is evaluated 10 times, and the average episode reward is taken. Episodes run for up to 10s, with early termination if torso tilt exceeds 135° or angular velocity exceeds 25°/s.
- **Computational Cost:** Approximately 30 hours per optimization algorithm on specified hardware.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were designed to evaluate the effectiveness of RoboCraft, quantify the respective contributions of control policy and morphology optimization, and assess the necessity of control policy fine-tuning.

#### 4.1. RQ1: Effectiveness of RoboCraft in Co-designing Humanoid Robots for Fall Recovery

- **Overall Performance Improvement:** RoboCraft achieved an average performance improvement of 44.55% across seven public humanoid robots when optimizing their morphology and control policy for fall recovery.
- **Specific Robot Performance:**
 - For robots with initially lower baseline performance, the improvements were substantial: Bez1 showed a 145% increase (from 27.36 to 66.89), Sigmaban improved by 106% (from 52.29 to 107.92), and OP3-Rot by 32% (from 64.53 to 85.11).
 - The robot Wolfgang, which had a high initial baseline performance (134.88), exhibited less additional benefit from co-design.
- **Optimizer Performance:** Among the tested morphology optimizers, evolutionary search achieved the best performance in co-designing 4 out of 7 robots.
- **Qualitative Changes in Morphology:** Visual inspection of optimized morphologies (e.g., Bez2, Bez3, OP3-Rot, and Sigmaban) indicated a tendency for robots to evolve into taller structures. This change is attributed to the "upright reward" component in the task reward function, which incentivizes increased head height. Additionally, optimized designs exhibited lower passive resistance, which facilitates the controller in generating torques required for standing up.

#### 4.2. RQ2: Role of Control Policy vs. Morphology Optimization

To disentangle the contributions of control policy and morphology optimization, the total performance improvement (∆Jn) for each robot was decomposed into two components: one attributed to control policy optimization and another to morphology optimization. This decomposition method involves splitting the objective function changes across iterations.

- **Varying Contributions Across Robots:** The analysis revealed that the relative contributions of control policy and morphology optimization differed across humanoid robots:
 - For Bez3, control policy optimization accounted for approximately 95% of the performance gains, suggesting that the initial policy was significantly suboptimal for this specific robot.
 - Conversely, for OP3-Rot and Sigmaban, morphology optimization contributed the majority of the improvements, around 91.4% and 92.0% respectively. This indicates that their initial designs had considerable room for physical optimization that directly enhanced fall recovery.
 - For Bez1 and NUGUS, morphology optimization contributed 45.1% and 79.0%, respectively.
- **Implication:** These findings suggest that performance improvements in co-design are not always evenly distributed between control and morphology. The necessity and extent of morphological optimization are task- and robot-dependent, indicating that co-design is a valuable approach that can leverage either or both factors for performance enhancement.

#### 4.3. RQ3: Necessity of Control Policy Fine-Tuning

This ablation study investigated whether finetuning the pretrained multi-design control policy was necessary, or if the initial pretrained policy alone could provide sufficient guidance for morphology optimization. The performance of final morphologies evolved under the pretrained policy was compared to those evolved under the finetuned policy, relative to the baseline performance.

- **Benefit of Finetuning:** The results indicated that finetuning the control policy is beneficial. The ratio of (pretrained policy performance - baseline) to (finetuned policy performance - baseline) showed that, for most robots, the finetuned policy yielded significantly higher improvements.
- **Correlation with Policy Contribution:** The data showed a correlation: robots where the control policy's contribution to overall co-design improvement was weaker (e.g., OP3-Rot, Sigmaban, where morphology dominated) generally had a higher relative performance under the pretrained policy (0.81 for OP3-Rot, 0.77 for Sigmaban). Conversely, robots where the control policy contributed more (e.g., Bez3, NUGUS) showed a larger gap, with lower relative performance under the pretrained policy (0.32 for Bez3, 0.29 for NUGUS).
- **Conclusion:** This indicates that while a pretrained policy can offer a baseline evaluation, finetuning provides more accurate and specialized feedback, leading to the discovery of higher-performing morphologies. The pretrained policy alone may not fully capture the optimal performance of an evolving morphology.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The RoboCraft framework and its experimental findings represent a notable advancement in the field of robotics, particularly concerning humanoid co-design and embodied intelligence.

### 6. Advancing Humanoid Co-design

This work provides a scalable and efficient framework that bridges the gap between theoretical co-design concepts and their practical application to complex, rigid-bodied humanoid robots. By demonstrating the feasibility of jointly optimizing control and morphology for fall recovery across multiple public humanoid models, RoboCraft moves beyond simpler robotic platforms typically studied in co-design literature. This establishes a precedent for tackling the intricate design spaces and dynamic challenges inherent in humanoids.

### 7. Quantified Importance of Morphology Optimization

A key contribution is the empirical quantification of morphology's role in task performance. The finding that morphology optimization can drive at least 40% of improvements (and significantly more for certain robots like OP3-Rot and Sigmaban) underscores the critical importance of considering physical design alongside control. This challenges control-centric approaches and highlights that substantial performance gains may be latent in the robot's physical structure, awaiting co-optimization. This insight can guide future research and development efforts, indicating when and where to prioritize morphological changes.

### 8. Enhanced Efficiency in Design and Control Co-optimization

RoboCraft's innovative control policy update, incorporating both inter-design pretraining and intra-design finetuning, significantly improves the sample efficiency of the co-design process. By leveraging knowledge transfer, the framework avoids the computationally expensive retraining of control policies from scratch for every new morphology. This efficiency makes the iterative co-design process more practical for complex systems, potentially accelerating the development cycle for advanced robots.

### 9. Practical Implications for Humanoid Resilience and Autonomy

The focus on fall recovery addresses a fundamental limitation in humanoid robot deployment: their vulnerability to falls. By enabling humanoids to inherently recover from falls more effectively through optimized morphology and control, RoboCraft contributes directly to enhanced safety, robustness, and resilience. This capability is crucial for allowing humanoids to operate autonomously and reliably in unstructured or unpredictable environments, paving the way for broader real-world applications.

### 10. Versatility and Future Research Pathways

The framework's compatibility with multiple humanoid models and various optimization algorithms provides a versatile tool for the research community. This flexibility can facilitate benchmarking across diverse robot designs, enabling comparative studies and the identification of generalizable design principles. The demonstrated adaptability to fall recovery also suggests potential for extension to other complex tasks (e.g., locomotion, manipulation), laying a foundation for multi-task co-design.

### 11. Broader Impact on Embodied AI

By pushing the boundaries of brain-body co-design for humanoids, this research contributes to the broader goal of creating more intelligent and adaptable embodied AI systems. It advocates for a paradigm shift from designing robots and then programming them, to a more integrated approach where form and function co-evolve, potentially leading to robots with innate potentials better suited for specific tasks and environments. While real-world validation remains a significant challenge, this work provides a strong simulation-based foundation for such future endeavors.
