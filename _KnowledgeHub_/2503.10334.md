# Enhanced View Planning for Robotic Harvesting: Tackling Occlusions with Imitation Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the University of Groningen developed an imitation learning method using the Action Chunking with Transformer (ACT) algorithm to enable robotic harvesters to actively adjust camera viewpoints and mitigate fruit occlusion. This approach achieved an 86.7% success rate in simulated environments, identifying optimal viewpoints in an average of 3.1 seconds, and a 66.7% success rate in real-world tests, improving object detection confidence.

## Problem

- Occlusion of crops by foliage significantly hinders automated robotic harvesting, leading to high fruit detection failure rates (up to 30%) and reduced efficiency.
- Traditional view planning methods are limited by extensive manual tuning, task-specificity, and poor generalization across dynamic agricultural environments.
- Prior machine learning solutions often require complex reward function design, substantial training data, or struggle to generalize effectively to novel real-world conditions.

## Method

- An imitation learning-based viewpoint planning method was developed to guide robot cameras to optimal observation points, emulating human expert behavior.
- ==The Action Chunking== with Transformer (ACT) algorithm was employed, trained on expert demonstrations collected in a simulated environment, to predict sequences of 6-DoF camera actions.
- The system leverages a continuous 6-DoF action space for camera movements, allowing for precise and smooth adjustments to address occlusion.

## Results

- In simulated environments, the method achieved an **86.7%** success rate in finding ideal viewpoints within an average of **3.1** seconds, outperforming a traditional baseline (RVP) which averaged **53.7** seconds.
- The system demonstrated generalization across eight additional fruit types in simulation, showing improved object detection confidence, such as increasing banana detection from **0.30** (occluded) to **0.62**.
- Real-world experiments yielded a **66.7%** success rate, where the robotic arm made minimal adjustments, showcasing feasibility for practical application despite increased environmental challenges.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Imitation learning, particularly with the ACT algorithm, offers an effective way to learn complex viewpoint planning policies from expert demonstrations, circumventing the need for intricate reward function design.
> - Utilizing a continuous 6-DoF action space for camera movements enables precise and smooth adjustments, which are crucial for navigating and optimizing views in cluttered agricultural settings.
> - Predicting sequences of actions (action chunks) instead of single actions addresses the compounding error problem often found in basic behavioral cloning, improving robustness.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Automated robotic harvesting in agriculture encounters significant challenges, with occlusion being a primary impediment. The natural growth patterns of plants often result in fruits and stems being obscured by foliage, which can lead to high rates of detection failure and reduced harvesting efficiency. Previous research has quantified this issue, with reports indicating up to a 30% fruit detection failure rate in orchard environments due to occlusion.

The concept of "active vision," where an observer deliberately adjusts their position to gather more information, has been proposed as a method to address occlusions. In robotics, this is known as view planning or the next-best-view (NBV) problem. Traditional view planning methods typically involve sampling potential viewpoints and evaluating them based on manually designed metrics, such as information gain. However, these methods exhibit several limitations. They often require extensive manual tuning, are specialized for particular tasks or environments, demonstrate limited generalization capabilities, and typically assume static environments, which is not representative of dynamic agricultural settings.

More recently, machine learning-based approaches have been explored to overcome these limitations. Reinforcement learning (RL) has been applied to learn viewpoint selection policies; however, its effectiveness is often contingent on the careful design of reward functions and can suffer from generalization issues, similar to the challenges faced by traditional feature engineering. Furthermore, RL methods typically demand substantial training data and computational time. Deep learning frameworks have also been developed to infer NBVs directly from sensory data, but these approaches can struggle to generalize to novel or complex real-world environments.

Within agricultural robotics specifically, researchers have attempted to mitigate occlusion using various learning-based techniques. These include deep reinforcement learning for optimal viewpoint determination using octree-based maps, multi-camera systems for next-best-view prediction in unstructured environments, deep learning combined with active sensing for crop localization, and CNN-based models for guiding camera positioning. Some approaches have also focused on active vision strategies for specific crops, such as grapes.

This work positions itself within this evolving landscape by proposing an imitation learning (IL)-based approach for viewpoint planning. Imitation learning is presented as a method that merges advantages of supervised and reinforcement learning, potentially offering improved data efficiency, faster convergence, and reduced dependency on complex reward function design. The paper leverages the Action Chunking with Transformer (ACT) algorithm, a development in imitation learning that has shown promise in complex robotic manipulation tasks by addressing issues like distribution shift common in simpler behavioral cloning methods. By drawing policies directly from human expert demonstrations, this research aims to provide a more generalizable and efficient solution to the occlusion problem in agricultural robotics, thereby advancing autonomous harvesting system performance beyond the capabilities of current traditional and existing learning-based methodologies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core motivation for this research stems from the persistent and significant challenge of occlusion in autonomous robotic harvesting. In natural agricultural settings, crops are frequently obscured by surrounding leaves, stems, or other fruits. This biological characteristic, which protects fruits from environmental stressors, concurrently creates a practical barrier for robotic vision systems. As highlighted in the paper, occlusion can lead to substantial fruit detection failure rates (up to 30%), directly impacting harvesting efficiency, increasing the risk of crop damage, and contributing to yield loss. Current autonomous harvesting systems are therefore limited by their inability to robustly identify and localize target crops under these common conditions.

Existing solutions for viewpoint planning, which aim to actively adjust a camera's position to obtain a clearer view, face several limitations. Traditional methods rely on manually designed evaluation metrics to select optimal viewpoints. These metrics are often task-specific, challenging to generalize across diverse agricultural scenarios, and demand considerable expert effort for design and fine-tuning. Moreover, most traditional approaches presume a static environment, which is inconsistent with the dynamic nature of plant growth in real-world agriculture. Learning-based methods, such as reinforcement learning, also encounter difficulties, primarily concerning the generalization capability of their reward functions and the extensive data and time required for policy development. End-to-end deep learning frameworks, while effective in controlled datasets, similarly struggle with generalization to complex, real-world conditions.

Against this backdrop, the key objective of this study is to develop a novel and efficient viewpoint planning approach that effectively identifies optimal observation viewpoints for agricultural robotic harvesting tasks, specifically focusing on overcoming occlusion challenges. The research aims to achieve this by imitating human expert behavior in selecting viewpoints when occlusions occur, thereby providing an alternative that is expected to be more efficient and generalizable than current methods.

The specific contributions and objectives outlined in the paper are:
- **Development of a Novel Imitation Learning Method:** To introduce an imitation learning-based viewpoint planning method that emulates human expert behavior. This method is designed to adapt to complex agricultural environments, aiming to significantly improve fruit detection and harvesting success rates by effectively addressing occlusion.
- **Continuous 6-DoF Action Space Control:** To model the viewpoint planning policy within a continuous six-degree-of-freedom (6-DoF) action space. This design is intended to facilitate smoother and more precise camera movements, which are deemed critical for identifying optimal viewpoints in the intricate and variable agricultural environments.
- **Validation in Diverse Environments:** To validate the proposed method through extensive experiments in both simulated and real-world laboratory settings. The objective is to demonstrate the feasibility and effectiveness of the approach, comparing its performance against other methods.

Ultimately, the motivation is to advance robotic harvesting technology by offering a practical "learn from demonstration" (LfD) solution that directly addresses the long-standing problem of occlusion, thereby enhancing autonomous harvesting performance and productivity.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology introduces an imitation learning-based viewpoint planning approach designed to enable a robotic system to actively adjust its camera viewpoint to capture unobstructed images of target crops. The system is modeled as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) to learn policies from expert demonstrations.

**Problem Formulation:**
The objective is to train a policy function, $\pi$, which generates camera motion actions based on environmental observations. The observations, $o_t$, at each time step $t$, consist of a four-channel RGB-D image, $i_t$, and a 6-DoF camera pose change, $\Delta p_t$. The action, $a_t$, is a continuous 6-DoF command comprising three translational and three rotational components along the camera's local coordinate axes. The policy, $a_t = \pi(i_t, \Delta p_t)$, is learned using a behavior cloning algorithm that minimizes the difference between its predicted actions and those from a dataset of expert demonstrations, $D = \{\tau_1, \tau_2, \ldots, \tau_N\}$, where each demonstration $\tau$ is a sequence of observation-action pairs. The loss function is defined as $L(\pi(o), a)$.

**Expert Demonstration Collection:**
Demonstration data was collected in a simulated environment built with ROS and Gazebo, featuring a UR5e robotic arm equipped with an RGB-D camera and pepper plants. The process assumed that the target crop (e.g., a pepper) had been approximately localized. Fifty initial camera poses were uniformly generated around the target. Human experts, observing the camera's image through an RViz interface, used a custom control panel to generate continuous 6-DoF motion commands. The goal was for the expert to achieve a complete and clear view of the target crop. During each demonstration (up to 50 time steps), images, camera pose trajectories, and smoothed control commands were recorded. These smoothed observation-action sequences formed the expert dataset for training.

**Network Architecture:**
The Action Chunking with Transformer (ACT) algorithm was adopted to implement the behavior cloning. ACT addresses the compounding error problem in traditional behavior cloning by predicting sequences of actions (action chunks) rather than individual actions, thereby reducing the effective decision-making span. The ACT network comprises two main components: a Conditional Variational Autoencoder (CVAE) encoder and a CVAE decoder, both utilizing the transformer architecture.
- **CVAE Encoder:** This component includes a transformer encoder that processes camera pose changes and a sequence of target control commands. Its output is a low-dimensional latent "style variable" $z$, intended to capture high-level features or style from the observations and action sequences. The CVAE encoder is only active during the training phase.
- **CVAE Decoder:** This component consists of a transformer encoder and a transformer decoder. Prior to being fed into the transformer encoder, image features are extracted using a ResNet-18 network. The transformer encoder then receives these image features, the camera pose changes, and the style variable $z$ (or a zero vector during inference). This information is processed to generate a comprehensive representation, which is subsequently passed to the transformer decoder along with fixed positional embedding queries. The transformer decoder's output is then dimensionally reduced by a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) to generate the predicted action sequence (chunk).

**Network Training and Inference:**
- **Training Phase:**
 - Observation-action sequences $((i_t, \Delta p_t), a_t: a_{t+k})$ are sampled from the expert demonstration dataset, with an action chunk size $k=5$.
 - The CVAE encoder generates the style variable $z$.
 - The CVAE decoder predicts the action sequence $(\hat{a}_t: \hat{a}_{t+k})$.
 - The total loss function, $L_{total} = L_{reconstruction} + \beta \times L_{KL}$, is minimized using the Adam optimizer. $L_{reconstruction}$ measures the discrepancy between predicted and demonstrated actions, while $L_{KL}$ quantifies the difference between the CVAE encoder's latent variable distribution and a standard normal distribution. The weighting factor $\beta$ is set to 10.
- **Inference Phase:**
 - The CVAE encoder is discarded, and the style variable $z$ is set to a zero vector.
 - The CVAE decoder's transformer components generate an action sequence.
 - The final action $\hat{a}_t$ for execution is obtained through a temporal ensemble technique: a weighted sum of predicted actions within the chunk, where earlier actions have a decreasing influence regulated by a decay factor $m=0.01$. The robot then performs this composed 6-DoF camera motion.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluated the proposed imitation learning-based viewpoint planning method through experiments in both simulated and real-world environments, focusing on planning success rate and execution duration.

**Simulated Environments:**
A simulated environment, constructed with ROS 1 and Gazebo, featured a UR5e robotic arm with an RGB-D camera and various fruit types (pepper and eight others) under occluded conditions. The proposed method was compared against the ROI-based Viewpoint Planner (RVP), a traditional search-based method for agricultural contexts.
- **Success Rate and Efficiency:** The proposed method achieved an 86.7% success rate (13 out of 15 novel occluded scenes) in identifying an ideal viewpoint, defined as capturing the target fruit fully and unobstructed. This was achieved based on 50 expert demonstrations. In contrast, RVP, while capable of finding an ideal viewpoint, often required exaggerated robot arm movements that were considered impractical for real-world application. The average execution time for successful cases was 3.1 seconds for the proposed method, operating as a continuous closed-loop controller at 10Hz. RVP, as a discrete open-loop controller, averaged 53.7 seconds. The improved efficiency of the proposed method was attributed to eliminating the need for octree maintenance, thereby reducing computational overhead and optimizing robotic arm movements.
- **Generalization:** The method demonstrated generalization capability by achieving comparable success rates and runtimes when tested on eight additional fruit types without requiring extensive reprogramming, indicating its applicability beyond the specific crop (pepper) used for primary demonstrations.
- **Impact on Object Detection:** Integration with the YOLOv8 object detector illustrated the practical benefit of improved viewpoints. For bananas, detection confidence increased from 0.30 (occluded) to 0.62 after viewpoint adjustment. For oranges, which were initially undetected in occluded scenarios, detection confidence rose to 0.87 after the viewpoint was adjusted.

**Real-world Scenarios:**
Real-world experiments were conducted using a 6-DoF UR5e robotic arm equipped with an Intel RealSense D435 depth camera in a laboratory setting.
- **Success Rate:** With 50 expert demonstrations collected for the real-world setup, the viewpoint planner achieved a success rate of approximately 66.7% (10 out of 15 trials) in identifying ideal viewpoints.
- **Practicality:** The system made real-time adjustments, and the robotic arm required minimal movement to achieve these adjustments, which contributes to its practicality for real-world constraints.
- **Challenges:** The lower success rate in real-world tests compared to simulations (66.7% vs. 86.7%) was attributed to increased environmental complexity, cluttered backgrounds, difficulty in reliably distinguishing target fruits from background objects, and variations in lighting conditions, which were less controlled than in the simulated environment. These factors occasionally led to incorrect viewpoint adjustments or prolonged search times.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This study presents a novel approach to viewpoint planning in agricultural robotics, addressing a critical challenge that currently limits the efficacy of autonomous harvesting systems. The proposed imitation learning-based method offers several significant contributions and potential impacts.

Firstly, the research directly tackles the inherent problem of occlusion, which is a major impediment to reliable fruit detection and harvesting. By enabling robotic systems to actively adjust their camera viewpoints to obtain unobstructed images, the method can potentially reduce detection failure rates and improve the overall success rate of harvesting operations.

Secondly, the adoption of an imitation learning framework based on expert demonstrations provides a practical "learn from demonstration" (LfD) solution. This approach mitigates the need for manually designed evaluation metrics or complex reward functions, which are often difficult to generalize and labor-intensive to develop. This simplification in policy acquisition has the potential to accelerate the development and deployment of autonomous agricultural robots.

Thirdly, the implementation of a continuous 6-DoF action space for camera movement allows for smoother and more precise viewpoint adjustments compared to discrete control commands. This precision is critical for identifying optimal viewpoints in complex and confined agricultural environments, contributing to more accurate data acquisition for subsequent harvesting tasks.

Fourthly, the experimental results demonstrate superior performance in simulated environments compared to a traditional search-based method, particularly in terms of efficiency and success rate. The proposed method's ability to identify ideal viewpoints in an average of 3.1 seconds, operating at 10Hz, compared to 53.7 seconds for a baseline, indicates a notable improvement in real-time responsiveness. This efficiency, coupled with minimal robot movement, is a key factor for practical deployment in time-sensitive agricultural operations.

Fifthly, the method exhibited generalization capabilities across different crop types in simulation with minimal reprogramming. This suggests that the learned policies are not overly specific to a single fruit, potentially reducing the development effort required for adapting the system to various crops, thus broadening its applicability within diverse agricultural settings.

Finally, while real-world success rates were lower than in simulation, the method still proved effective, demonstrating its feasibility for practical application. The insights gained from the real-world trials, particularly regarding challenges posed by background clutter and lighting variations, provide clear directions for future research to enhance robustness. The framework also lays a foundation for future advancements, such as expanding expert datasets, incorporating interactive imitation learning for continuous improvement, and extending to multi-robot systems for collaborative viewpoint optimization and increased harvesting efficiency.

In summary, this work advances the field of agricultural robotics by providing an efficient, generalizable, and practical method for addressing occlusion, which can lead to enhanced autonomous harvesting performance and productivity.
