# FORGE-plus: Force-Budgeted Recovery for Contact-Rich Assembly with a Frozen LLM Supervisor

> [!summary] Summary
> FORGE-plus improves safety and robustness in contact-rich robotic assembly, particularly for fragile objects, by using a frozen Large Language Model to set per-object force limits and select recovery actions based on force signatures. The system achieved zero breakage on fragile components in various assembly tasks and successfully recovered from complex contact failures.

## Problem

- Determining appropriate, object-specific maximum allowable forces (F_max) for robotic assembly to prevent damage to fragile components, as prior methods often autotuned for success regardless of material fragility.
- Developing effective failure recovery strategies for subtle, contact-rich assembly failures where visual cues are ambiguous, and common 'press harder' tactics are destructive to fragile parts.
- Standard on-policy reinforcement learning for tight-clearance insertion is intrinsically damaging, since the exploration noise needed to discover the narrow assembly funnel is already sufficient to break fragile parts.

## Method

- A ==two-layer architecture== featuring a slow-rate frozen LLM to semantically determine a per-object maximum force budget (F_max) and select recovery actions based on force signatures, without visual input, forming **FORGE-plus**.
- A fast-rate robot control loop that employs a ==force-conditioned RL policy== for insertion and strictly enforces the F_max via a ==hard clamp==, decoupling safety from LLM decisions in **FORGE-plus**.
- A '==signature encoder==' translates robot F/T history into compact text-based force signatures (e.g., peak_axial_N, lateral_bias) to inform the LLM's ==recovery-selector== process within **FORGE-plus**.

## Results

- Achieved **100%** success and **0%** breakage on fragile objects like ABS gears and bottles across multiple assembly tasks, effectively demonstrating fragility avoidance.
- Successfully recovered from injected failures (e.g., rim wedges, in-grip slips) in bottle placement (**9/9**) and gear insertion (up to **64%** success with regrasp), using force signatures to select appropriate motion-based recovery primitives.
- An 'oracle' baseline, which set F_max just below the hidden breaking force, still resulted in **49.8%** breakage due to controller impedance overshoot, highlighting the practical limits of force control and the need for conservative budgeting.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Strict decoupling of high-level semantic decisions (LLM-set F_max and recovery choice) from low-level physical safety enforcement (hard force clamp) is essential for safely handling fragile objects.
> - Compact, text-based force signatures are effective for diagnosing subtle contact-rich assembly failures where visual cues are ambiguous, enabling more precise and less destructive recovery.
> - Controller impedance overshoots can cause actual contact forces to exceed commanded F_max, even with an 'oracle' budget, necessitating conservative force limits for truly fragile parts.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is positioned within the field of robotic manipulation, specifically focusing on contact-rich assembly tasks. It builds upon previous developments in simulated contact-rich insertion, notably the "Factory→IndustReal→AutoMate→FORGE" research line, which has progressively enhanced robustness and, in some instances, demonstrated transferability to hardware. The FORGE system, a direct predecessor, introduced policies conditioned on a maximum allowable force, allowing for a trade-off between speed and gentleness.

FORGE-plus addresses two limitations of prior work:
1. **Force Ceiling Determination:** Previous systems, such as FORGE, typically autotuned the maximum force (`F_max`) to maximize success. This approach is effective for indestructible components but is insufficient for scenarios involving fragile parts, where a universal force ceiling could lead to damage. The current research highlights the necessity for a per-object `F_max` derived from the object's identity.
2. **Failure Recovery Mechanisms:** Existing Large Language Model (LLM) and Vision-Language Model (VLM) based failure reasoners, such as REFLECT, DoReMi, and AHA, primarily rely on visual input and operate at a higher, task-planning level. However, the paper argues that critical information for distinguishing fine-grained contact failures (e.g., a wedge, cross-thread, or burr) resides in the force trace rather than pixel data, as these failures can appear visually similar. Furthermore, a common recovery strategy, such as "press harder" demonstrated in Tactile-VLA, while effective for robust parts, is inherently destructive for fragile ones.

FORGE-plus differentiates itself from related work through its architectural and evaluative principles:
- **Skill Adaptation:** It reuses the FORGE-style force conditioning but fundamentally alters the upstream decision-making for `F_max`, prioritizing part integrity over mere task success, and disallowing `F_max` relaxation during recovery.
- **Semantic Layer for Recovery:** Unlike Tactile-VLA, which often escalates force, FORGE-plus employs a frozen LLM with a force-only signature and a hard, non-negotiable per-object force ceiling. The evaluation explicitly includes fragile parts that such force escalation would damage.
- **Safety Architecture:** It shares an architectural philosophy with systems like PaCo-VLA—where a network proposes actions but a runtime shield retains authority. However, PaCo-VLA’s shield is based on passivity contracts, whereas FORGE-plus employs a hard scalar peak-force clamp directly linked to object identity.
- **Failure Detection:** In contrast to vision-centric, task-plan-level reasoners like REFLECT, DoReMi, and AHA [5–7], FORGE-plus's failure signal is a compact force/contact signature, and its recovery is constrained by a force budget, targeting contact dynamics.
- **Benchmark Extension:** The work builds on established simulation benchmarks like Factory, IndustReal, and AutoMate, all within the Isaac Lab framework, but introduces per-object fragility and force-grounded recovery. Grasping embodiments are based on GraspGen.

The authors state that while no single component is entirely new, the contribution lies in the *conjunction* of force-grounded, non-vision semantic recovery under a hard per-object ceiling, coupled with a closed-loop fragility metric that is hidden from the agent.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of FORGE-plus are to enhance the robustness and safety of contact-rich robotic assembly, particularly when dealing with objects of varying fragility. This is achieved by addressing two fundamental, unresolved questions from prior research:

1. **Who sets the maximum allowable force (`F_max`)?**
 - **Motivation:** In conventional robotic assembly, the `F_max` is often determined through autotuning or human input, primarily to maximize the success rate of the insertion task. While effective for robust, indestructible parts (e.g., a steel bolt), this approach becomes problematic when fragile components (e.g., a nylon snap clip) are involved. Using a high `F_max` suitable for a robust part can lead to the destruction of a fragile one in a mixed-material assembly. Therefore, the system needs to establish a per-object `F_max` that considers the material properties and fragility of the specific object being manipulated.
 - **Objective:** To implement a mechanism whereby a frozen, text-only Large Language Model (LLM) sets a per-object `F_max` based solely on the object's textual identity (e.g., name, material, class) *before* an assembly episode commences. This decision must be made without access to any visual information or knowledge of the hidden breaking force of the specific instance.

2. **What should the robot do when an insertion fails?**
 - **Motivation:** Existing failure reasoners in robotics, often based on Vision-Language Models (VLMs), typically analyze visual information to diagnose and replan at a higher task level (e.g., "wrong object," "missing step," "mis-grasp"). However, many subtle, contact-rich assembly failures—such as a wedge, cross-thread, or burr—may present identical visual cues but have distinct underlying physical dynamics reflected in force/torque traces. Furthermore, a common and seemingly intuitive recovery strategy, such as "press harder" (as demonstrated in some language-conditioned force control systems), poses a significant risk for fragile parts, as it directly leads to breakage.
 - **Objective:** To develop a failure recovery system that, upon detection of an insertion failure, utilizes the same frozen LLM to select an appropriate recovery maneuver from a predefined, fixed menu. This selection must be based on a *compact text representation of the force and contact signature* (e.g., peak axial force, net insertion distance, lateral bias), again without relying on visual input. A critical constraint is that the recovery menu *explicitly excludes any option to increase the force ceiling*, ensuring that fragility limits are respected throughout the recovery process.

**Overarching Safety and Evaluation Motivations:**
A central motivation is to decouple safety guarantees from the LLM's decision-making. The LLM is designed to provide high-level semantic input (budget, recovery choice), but physical safety (ensuring `F_cmd` never exceeds `F_max`) is enforced by a hard clamp within the fast control loop, operating independently of the LLM. Additionally, the evaluation methodology is designed to be non-circular, meaning the hidden breaking force (`F_break`) of each object instance is never revealed to any learned or LLM component, thereby providing an objective measure of fragility avoidance. The study is conducted entirely in simulation, focusing on the "micro" phase of manipulation where force plays a decisive role in success and potential breakage.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The FORGE-plus system employs a two-layer architecture, operating at different rates, with distinct responsibilities to ensure both intelligent decision-making and physical safety.

**System Architecture:**
1. **Slow Layer (Frozen LLM):** This layer operates at a low frequency (approximately 0.1–1 Hz) and interacts purely with text (JSON in, JSON out), without any visual input.
 - **Budget-setter:** At the start of each episode, the LLM is queried. Its input consists of the object's identity (e.g., name, material, class, nominal mass, geometry tags like `thin_wall` or `press_fit`), the task description, and a global hard force cap. The LLM outputs a numeric `F_max_N` (optionally per-axis), a confidence score, and a rationale. This output is then range-validated and clamped to a fixed global hard cap (120 N) before being passed to the controller. Budget-setter outputs are cached per object class, as the LLM reasons about general class properties rather than specific instance fragility (`F_break` is sampled per episode and hidden).
 - **Recovery-selector:** Upon detecting an insertion failure, the LLM is invoked again. Its inputs include the current `F_max` (read-only), the attempt count, and a compact text-based "force signature." This signature comprises parameters such as `peak_axial_N`, `net_insert_mm`, `axial_rising`, `lateral_bias`, `contact_persist_ms`, and `slip_events`. The LLM then selects an action from a fixed menu of recovery maneuvers: `{retract_and_reapproach, wiggle_search, rotate_align, regrasp, abort}`. Crucially, this menu *does not contain an option to increase the force ceiling*, and any `keep_F_max_N` returned by the model is overwritten to the original value. The LLM's role here is to select a motion-based recovery, not to alter force limits. The LLM backend is swappable, supporting hosted APIs, local instruct models, or deterministic heuristics.
2. **Fast Layer (Robot Control Loop):** This layer operates at a high frequency (60–120 Hz) and is responsible for real-time control and safety enforcement.
 - **RL Skill:** A force-conditioned MLP policy controls the robot. Its observations include proprioception, end-effector (EE) pose, force/torque (F/T) readings, object-up axis, and phase. A normalized force command (`F_cmd`) conditions the network, which is penalized for overshooting it.
 - **Controller:** An operational-space impedance controller generates robot motion.
 - **Force Clamp:** This component represents the ultimate authority for safety. It saturates the commanded wrench before every simulation step to ensure it never exceeds `F_max`, regardless of any LLM output. A fixed 120 N global hard cap serves as a secondary defense.
3. **Signature Encoder:** This intermediate component translates the robot's recent F/T history and contact events into the compact text force signature consumed by the Recovery-selector LLM.

**Design Invariants for Non-Circular Evaluation:**
To ensure a robust and objective evaluation of fragility avoidance, three invariants are strictly maintained:
1. `F_break` (the hidden breaking force) is never observed by any learned or LLM component. It is sampled per episode and only accessible to the evaluator for breakage checks.
2. `F_max` remains immutable during recovery attempts.
3. Force authority is exclusively vested in the fast control loop's hard clamp, not the LLM.

**Closed-Loop Operation:**
Each episode proceeds with the RL skill until success or a detected failure. Upon failure, the force signature is encoded, the LLM selects a recovery action, a fixed scripted primitive executes within the original `F_max` budget, and the RL skill then resumes. This process can repeat for a maximum of `K_max` attempts. Failure detection is conservative and force-grounded: a "jam" is defined as sustained contact without net descent, while a "contactless hover" indicates the policy correctly refusing an geometrically impossible insertion.

**Benchmark Tasks, Objects, and Embodiments:**
- **Bottle Placement:** Involves inserting a fragile bottle into a wine rack. A lateral base-aim error is injected to induce a wedge. Object classes include a fragile bottle (`F_break` ≈ 22 N, budget 8.8 N) and a robust bottle (180 N).
- **Gear Insertion:** Focuses on tight-clearance assembly (0.4 mm diametral clearance) of a gear onto a shaft. A 5 mm in-grip slip is injected, causing the gear to tilt (7–10°) and making insertion geometrically impossible without re-grasping. Object classes are `abs_gear` (fragile; `F_break` sampled from 38 ± 5 N, budget ≈10 N) and `steel_gear` (robust; 100 N budget).
- **Embodiments:** Experiments are conducted on two parallel-jaw grippers: the Robotiq 2F-140 and the Franka Panda hand.
- **Learned vs. Scripted Components:** The force-guided insertion skill and the release decision are learned. Pre-approach transport, recovery primitives, and post-release hand retracts are scripted. The final "table-pick flow" involves fully physical interactions for picking and placing objects, without teleports or kinematic attachments.

**Metrics and Baselines:**
Seven metrics are used: closed-loop multi-attempt success, breakage rate, budget appropriateness, recovery efficacy, force economy, clamp fidelity (contact overshoot), and cross-gripper transfer. Six baselines are employed: `no-ceiling`, `fixed global ceiling`, `press-harder` (escalates force per attempt, modeled after Tactile-VLA), `heuristic recovery` (hand-coded rules), `vision-LLM proxy` (random menu draw as a stand-in for vision-only reasoners), and an `oracle ceiling` (`F_break - ε`, which cheats by knowing the hidden `F_break`).

**Skill Learning Process:**
The paper details challenges in skill learning. PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization) fails for the 0.4 mm clearance task on the Robotiq 2F-140 because the exploration noise required to find the narrow funnel is already destructive to fragile parts. Fine-tuning working policies with tiny-std PPO also leads to degradation. The successful pipeline involves collecting expert demonstrations, using DAgger to relabel states, fitting a deterministic mean with Behavior Cloning (BC), and finally employing a "weight soup" (uni = 0.15·bc3+0.85·bc7) to unify policies that perform well on both fragile and robust object classes. A separate "input-skip head" (a linear layer reading raw observations) is trained for the learned release decision, as attempts to use or fine-tune the main policy trunk for this task failed due to signal discarding.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study yielded several findings regarding force-budgeted assembly and recovery strategies:

**Unified Policy Performance (Clean Gates):**
- A single, unified checkpoint (created via weight soup) successfully passed strict clean gates of 256/256 episodes for insertion on both fragile ABS gears and robust steel gears using the Robotiq 2F-140 gripper, with zero breaks. The mean peak insertion force for the fragile class was 15.9 N.
- For the Franka Panda hand, the clean gate achieved 200/200 strict seats with zero breaks, at a mean peak force of 13.8 N.
- The learned release head, when integrated, also achieved 256/256 successful releases with zero bad releases on the 2F-140.
- A fully physical "table-pick flow" (a smoke test of 64 episodes on the fragile ABS gear with the 2F-140) completed 64/64 with zero breaks and zero bad releases. Notably, this flow achieved a mean peak insertion force of 5.4 N, which was gentler than the 15.9 N observed with the initial, less physical "pinned-staging" setup, indicating that a real friction pick can improve force economy.

**Bottle Placement Recovery:**
- In the bottle placement task, with an injected rim wedge on the fragile class, the system's force signature detected the jam at 16.1 N, which was below the episode's breaking force of 23.3 N.
- The LLM's `rotate_align` decision led to successful seating of the fragile bottle in 9/9 headless recovery episodes, each requiring 2-3 attempts, with zero breaks. This demonstrated that a conservative detector combined with budget-preserving retries can make an imperfect learned insertion reliable.

**Budget Baselines and Clamp Fidelity:**
- **Ours (LLM, identity-only):** With a 10 N identity-derived budget, achieved 100% success and 0% breakage on the fragile ABS gear (Franka), with mean peak forces of 14.9 N.
- **Oracle (`F_break - ε`):** Despite knowing the hidden breaking force and setting `F_max` close to it (~33 N), this baseline achieved only 50.2% success and caused 49.8% breakage. This demonstrated a critical finding: command clamping does not perfectly bound contact force, and impedance overshoot during funnel entry could exceed `F_max` by up to 1.5 times (e.g., 45-54 N overshoot for a 33 N budget). The conservative 10 N identity-derived budget successfully absorbed this overshoot without breakage.
- **Fixed Global 60 N Ceiling:** Resulted in 0% success and 100% breakage.
- **No Ceiling:** Led to 0% success; the policy became non-functional as force conditioning was outside its training distribution.

**Recovery under In-Grip Slip (Gear Task):**
- **Ours (Force Signature):** Successfully recovered from a 5 mm in-grip slip (which caused gear tilt) in 40% of episodes on the 2F-140 and 64% on the Franka. It achieved this by correctly interpreting the "contactless hover" signature (indicating a geometrically impossible insertion) and routing to `regrasp`, the only maneuver capable of fixing an in-grip tilt. This recovery involved 12% breakage and 48% (2F-140) / 24% (Franka) timeouts, with higher peak forces (mean 23.0 N on 2F-140) compared to clean insertions, indicating the cost of recovery.
- **Vision-LLM Proxy (Random Menu Draw):** Achieved 28% success on the 2F-140 and 32% on the Franka. This performance represented how often such a proxy would randomly select the correct `regrasp` action, highlighting the value of the force signature in discriminating failures.
- **Heuristic Recovery:** Achieved 0% success on both grippers, as its hand-coded rules failed to interpret the hover signature and never selected `regrasp`.
- **Press-Harder Baseline:** Demonstrated two distinct failure modes:
 - **On 2F-140:** Futile, resulting in 0% success, 0% breaks, and 100% timeouts. The escalated ceiling never engaged because the tilted gear never took load.
 - **On Franka:** Destructive, resulting in 4% success and 96% breakage. Escalating `F_max` (to 44.8 N peak) caused most fragile gears to break.
- **No Recovery (`None`):** Led to 0% success. On the 2F-140, it resulted in 20% breakage as the policy's continued descent eventually pressed the tilted bore into the shaft.

**Negative Results (First-Class Findings):**
- **PPO Failure:** PPO proved unable to learn the 0.4 mm clearance insertion task on the Robotiq 2F-140. The exploration noise required to effectively search the funnel was already sufficient to break fragile parts, demonstrating an inherent exploration-damage trade-off that can preclude on-policy RL for such tasks. Fine-tuning a working BC policy with tiny-std PPO also destroyed it.
- **Learned Release Head Failures:** Three natural designs for the learned release head (frozen-trunk, pre-open-only labels on frozen trunk, trunk fine-tuning with arm-distillation loss) failed due to the trunk discarding necessary seat-state signals or conflicting losses. The input-skip head (a linear layer on raw observations) was the only successful design.
- **Oracle Budget Insufficiency:** The oracle budget (`F_break - ε`) breaking half the fragile parts due to impedance overshoot, as detailed above, was a significant finding.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The FORGE-plus research offers several contributions with implications for robotic manipulation, particularly in delicate and contact-rich assembly scenarios:

- **Enhanced Safety for Fragile Parts:** The work provides a robust framework for handling fragile objects in assembly. By strictly separating force authority (a hard clamp in the fast control loop) from the LLM's semantic guidance, it ensures that force limits are never violated by intelligent, but potentially misinformed, agents. The demonstration of zero breaks across both fragile and robust object classes using a unified policy represents a significant advancement in reliable robotic assembly.
- **Intelligent, Force-Grounded Failure Recovery:** The research demonstrates the effectiveness of force-signature-based recovery over both heuristic and vision-only approaches. For subtle contact failures like tilted grips that are visually ambiguous but clearly manifest in force traces, the force-signature chain's ability to diagnose and route to the correct physical maneuver (e.g., regrasp) is a key achievement. This capability is critical for reducing downtime and preventing damage in automated assembly lines.
- **Clarifying the Role of LLMs in Robotics:** FORGE-plus clearly delineates the role of LLMs in control architectures. They function as high-level decision-makers, setting semantic budgets and selecting recovery strategies based on text-only inputs, rather than directly commanding physical forces or relying on visual processing for immediate physical interactions. This structural clarity can guide future integrations of large models in robotic systems.
- **Challenges of On-Policy RL for Precision Tasks:** The negative results regarding PPO's failure at tight-clearance, fragile assembly tasks provide valuable insights. They highlight an inherent trade-off between exploration noise (necessary for learning) and potential damage to fragile parts. This suggests that for certain high-precision, low-tolerance, and damage-averse tasks, behavior cloning from expert demonstrations, potentially combined with techniques like DAgger and model ensembling, may be a more viable and safer learning paradigm than pure on-policy RL.
- **Importance of Conservative Budgeting and Clamp Fidelity:** The finding that even an "oracle" budget (set just below `F_break`) can lead to breakage due to controller impedance overshoot underscores the practical necessity for conservative force budgeting. It highlights that `F_max` must account not only for the nominal breaking force but also for the dynamics of the control system and potential transient overshoots, improving the realism of safety considerations in robotic design.
- **Rigor in Simulation and Reproducibility:** The study's commitment to simulation-only experiments with clearly defined non-circular metrics, a comprehensive set of baselines, and the explicit release of code, evaluation scripts, and even negative results (including failed PPO lineages) fosters transparency and reproducibility within the research community. This allows other researchers to build upon and validate these findings.
- **Foundation for Future Work:** The identified limitations, such as the need for gentler post-recovery descent profiles, more naturalistic failure distributions, and further development of fully physical end-to-end flows, provide clear directions for subsequent research. The work sets a foundation for developing even more sophisticated and robust autonomous assembly systems that can operate reliably in complex, real-world manufacturing environments where part fragility and diverse failure modes are common.
