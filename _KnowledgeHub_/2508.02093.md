# "Stack It Up!": 3D Stable Structure Generation from 2D Hand-drawn Sketch

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the National University of Singapore developed a system that converts rough 2D hand-drawn sketches into physically stable 3D block arrangements suitable for robotic manipulation. This system infers precise 3D object poses and automatically adds necessary hidden structural supports, allowing non-expert users to intuitively specify complex construction goals.

## Problem

- Robot manipulation systems require precise 3D object poses, which are typically generated via expert design software, creating a barrier for non-expert users.
- Hand-drawn 2D sketches are inherently imprecise, incomplete due to occlusion, and lack the detailed 3D information necessary for physically stable multi-object constructions.
- Jointly determining the presence and type of missing blocks (discrete decisions) along with their precise 3D poses (continuous decisions) results in an intractably large search space.

## Method

- The system utilizes a two-phase approach centered on an ==abstract relation graph==, which acts as an intermediate representation to translate qualitative sketch intent into precise 3D poses.
- The first phase extracts qualitative geometric relations and local stability patterns from a 2D sketch to form an initial ==abstract relation graph==.
- The second phase employs an iterative forward-backward loop: ==compositional diffusion models== ground the graph into 3D poses, and a ==physics engine-guided heuristic== updates the graph by inferring and adding hidden supports to ensure stability.

## Results

- The system achieved **79.9-84.4%** resemblance to the original sketches and **94.5-98.8%** physical stability on 30 diverse test cases, including structures requiring hidden supports.
- It significantly outperformed an end-to-end diffusion model (**16.2-18.2%** resemblance, **12.1-24.2%** stability) and a VLM-based approach (**30.2-33.1%** resemblance, **53.7-63.6%** stability).
- The stability-pattern-guided graph updates were critical, improving stability from **69.8%** to **94.5-98.8%** when hidden supports were necessary, confirming their role in recovering missing structural elements efficiently.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - An abstract relation graph effectively captures essential structural cues and user intent from noisy hand-drawn sketches, enabling zero-shot generalization to diverse inputs.
> - Pre-trained diffusion models for individual abstract relations can be compositionally combined to efficiently sample precise 3D poses that satisfy multiple complex relationships simultaneously.
> - An iterative process of grounding 3D poses and then using physics simulation to guide updates to the abstract graph is crucial for autonomously inferring hidden internal and rear supports, thereby achieving physical stability from incomplete 2D information.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics is rapidly advancing, but a significant bottleneck remains in enabling non-expert users to intuitively communicate complex goals to robots. Current robotic manipulation systems typically demand precise 3D object poses, often requiring specialized design software like CAD and rigorous structural analysis. This barrier makes robots inaccessible for everyday users, who might prefer to express their intent through simpler means, such as a hand-drawn sketch. "Stack It Up!" directly addresses this fundamental challenge.

The paper situates itself within three key areas of related research:

1. **Sketch-Based Goal Specification for Robotics:** Prior work in this domain has explored using sketches for various robotic tasks, including navigation targets, obstacle definition, or high-level scene rearrangement (e.g., RT-Sketch). However, these methods largely focus on scene-level or trajectory goals and do not delve into the intricacies of generating physically stable, multi-object 3D structures. "Stack It Up!" differentiates itself by tackling the unique challenge of inferring both precise spatial layouts and unseen structural supports from rough 2D sketches, a capability largely absent in previous systems.

2. **User-Friendly Input for 3D Structure Generation:** The broader trend towards more accessible human-computer interfaces has led to systems that convert high-level inputs, such as natural language, into object-centric 3D structures. Blox-Net and QUERY2CAD are examples where vision-language models or natural language queries are used to generate or refine 3D assemblies. While these methods demonstrate the potential of intuitive inputs, the authors argue that natural language often lacks the "fine-grained spatial control" necessary for complex geometric details and explicit "stability reasoning." "Stack It Up!" demonstrates that hand-drawn sketches offer a more effective modality for conveying precise geometric and spatial intent, overcoming the limitations of purely linguistic descriptions.

3. **3D Structure Generation from Single-View 2D Inputs:** A related research direction involves reconstructing 3D models from single 2D images. Methods like StackGen use diffusion models to generate 2D stacking layouts from silhouettes, but they typically operate in 2D (x-z plane) and prioritize visual similarity over physical stability. Other techniques, such as Part123 and its successors, reconstruct metrically accurate 3D models from photorealistic images, often producing unified meshes rather than discrete blocks, and struggle with occluded or missing structural elements. "Stack It Up!" stands apart by specifically focusing on *rough, hand-drawn sketches*, *block-based construction for robot manipulation*, and crucially, *explicitly inferring hidden internal and rear supports* to ensure physical stability, a critical aspect often overlooked or implicitly handled by other systems.

By introducing an abstract relation graph as an intermediate representation, "Stack It Up!" innovatively bridges symbolic geometric reasoning with precise 3D pose generation. This approach allows it to disentangle noisy metric details from sketches, infer missing structural elements, and leverage compositional generative models to produce stable, robot-executable structures. This work thus represents a significant advancement in making complex robotic manipulation tasks more accessible to non-experts, addressing limitations inherent in existing sketch-based, language-based, and single-view 3D generation paradigms.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching motivation behind "Stack It Up!" is to democratize robotic manipulation by making it accessible to non-expert users. The paper opens with a compelling scenario: "Imagine a child sketching the Eiffel Tower and asking a robot to bring it to life." This highlights the current disconnect between intuitive human intent and the rigid, precise requirements of modern robotic systems.

The core problem addressed is that contemporary robot manipulation systems demand fully specified 3D object poses—typically generated via expert design software and verified through force analysis—before any motion planning or execution can commence. This creates an insurmountable barrier for everyday users who lack specialized training or tools.

The authors identify several key challenges in bridging the gap between a simple 2D hand-drawn sketch and a complete, robot-executable 3D goal specification:

1. **Metric Imprecision:** Hand-drawn sketches are inherently rough, distorted, and metrically inaccurate. Objects may be drawn out of scale, misaligned, or in physically implausible configurations, making direct recovery of precise 3D poses unreliable.
2. **Incompleteness (Occlusion and Missing Information):** A front-view sketch, by its nature, omits critical information, especially concerning interior and rear supports. These hidden elements are often crucial for the physical stability of multi-level structures but are absent from a user's drawing. The robot must infer both these missing components and their accurate 3D poses.
3. **Vast Discrete–Continuous Search Space:** Jointly predicting the presence and types of missing blocks (a discrete decision) along with their precise 3D poses (a continuous decision) generates an enormous combinatorial search space, making an exhaustive search infeasible.

Given these challenges, the key objectives of "Stack It Up!" are:

- **To enable non-experts to specify complex 3D structures** for robot manipulation using only a single, rough 2D front-view hand-drawn sketch.
- **To introduce an intermediate representation** that can effectively manage the complexity arising from sketch imprecision and incompleteness. This representation should distill essential structural cues (like geometric relations and stability patterns) while discarding noisy metric details.
- **To develop a method for accurately grounding this representation into precise 3D poses**, even when the initial sketch lacks sufficient detail.
- **To infer and incorporate hidden internal and rear support blocks** automatically, ensuring that the generated 3D structures are physically stable and executable by a robot.
- **To consistently produce stable, multi-level 3D arrangements** that faithfully reflect the user's sketch intent, outperforming existing baselines in both stability and visual resemblance.

By achieving these objectives, "Stack It Up!" aims to significantly lower the barrier to entry for robotic systems, making them more intuitive, accessible, and ultimately, more useful to a wider audience.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

"Stack It Up!" tackles the challenging problem of transforming rough 2D sketches into stable 3D block structures through a sophisticated, two-phase approach centered around an **abstract relation graph (G)** as an intermediate representation. This graph bridges the symbolic, qualitative nature of sketches with the precise, quantitative requirements for robot execution.

**Phase 1: One-time Abstract Relation Graph Extraction**
The initial step involves translating the user's 2D front-view sketch (S) into an abstract relation graph (G₀). This graph encodes qualitative geometric relationships and local stability patterns among the visible blocks.

1. **Abstract Relation Graph (G):** This graph consists of nodes representing blocks (with their assigned types, T) and edges denoting abstract relations from a predefined library R. These relations abstract away exact 3D poses. The library R is divided into:
 - **Geometric Relations (R_geom):** 24 types, including 13 derivable from the 2D sketch (x-z plane, e.g., `left-of`, `touching-along-x`) and 11 involving depth (x-y plane, e.g., `front-of`, `touching-along-y`) that are inferred later. Each is detected by a rule-based classifier `h_R` operating on projected bounding boxes.
 - **Local Stability Patterns (R_stab):** 10 types (e.g., `single-block-stack`, `two-pillar-bridge`). Each pattern `h_R` describes a stable two-layer subgraph and is defined by four descriptors: admissible block counts for base and top tiers, 'supported-by' subgraphs, and permissible geometric subgraphs among base and top blocks.

2. **Extraction Process:**
 - The system first regularizes the strokes of the imperfect hand-drawn sketch using flood-fill and dilation.
 - Axis-aligned bounding boxes are fitted to each visible block, and their coarse dimensions (`ˆw_i, ˆh_i`) and centroids (`ˆx_i, ˆz_i`) are recorded. These rough metrics are used only to infer qualitative relations, not precise poses.
 - Rule-based classifiers `h_R` (for `R_geom`) operate on these bounding boxes to populate the initial geometric graph `G_geom_0`.
 - Subsequently, `h_R` (for `R_stab`) classifiers identify subgraph matches to known local stability patterns within `G_geom_0`, yielding `G_stab_0`.
 - The initial abstract relation graph `G_0` is formed by the union of `G_geom_0` and `G_stab_0`.

**Phase 2: Iterative Abstract Relation Graph Grounding (Forward–Backward Loop)**
This phase is an iterative refinement process (Figure 3) that alternates between generating 3D poses from the current graph and updating the graph with hidden supports if instability is detected.

1. **Training Individual Diffusion Models for Each Abstract Relation:**
 - A set of specialized diffusion models `{f_R}` is pre-trained, one for each abstract relation `R ∈ R_geom ∪ R_stab`.
 - Each `f_R` learns to generate valid 3D poses `p` that satisfy its corresponding rule-based classifier `h_R(g, p)`.
 - Synthetic datasets `D_R` are created by generating diverse multi-level 3D structures (composing stability patterns) and then extracting positive examples for each relation using `h_R`.
 - Standard Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPMs) are trained using an MSE loss (Equation 1) to predict the noise required to reverse the diffusion process, effectively learning the conditional distribution `q_R(p | g)`.

2. **Forward Graph Grounding via Compositional Diffusion Models:**
 - Given a graph `G_t`, the goal is to sample valid 3D poses `P_t` that satisfy *all* relations in the graph.
 - Instead of a single monolithic model, `StackItUp` composes the pre-trained individual `f_R` models. Each edge `r ∈ G_t` (representing a relation `R`) contributes its associated diffusion model `f_R`.
 - These models are combined into a product distribution `q_t_prod(p | g) := Π_(r∈G) q_t_R(p_r | g_r)`.
 - To sample from this complex distribution, the system approximates the composite score function by summing the individual score functions from each model (`∇_p_t log q_t_prod(p_t | g) ≈ Σ_(r∈G) ϵ_R(p_r_t, g_r, t)`).
 - The Unadjusted Langevin Algorithm (ULA) is then used to perform iterative reverse diffusion steps (Equation 2). Starting from random noise `p_T`, ULA gradually refines the poses to `P_0`, ensuring all relations in `G_t` are satisfied.

3. **Backward Graph Update Guided by Stability Patterns:**
 - The initially grounded poses `P_0` might be unstable because the front-view sketch `G_0` often omits crucial hidden supports.
 - The system decomposes `(G_t, P_t)` into subgraphs based on matched stability patterns and simulates their stability using a physics engine.
 - If instability is detected, the graph needs to be extended, which is challenging due to the combinatorial explosion of possible missing blocks.
 - `StackItUp` leverages the descriptors of `R_stab` (admissible counts, supported-by subgraphs, geometric subgraphs for base/top blocks) to guide a heuristic search for missing blocks and relations.
 - An unstable subgraph can be repaired by either: (i) extending it within the same stability pattern (e.g., changing `supported-by-fully` to `supported-by-partially` for a cantilever), or (ii) evolving it into a more complex pattern by adding hidden supports (e.g., transforming a single unstable stack into a `two-pillar-bridge`).
 - Hidden block types (`T_hid`) are heuristically sampled, often matching existing block types.
 - All identified updates are aggregated into an updated abstract relation graph `G_t+1 = G_t ∪ G_hid_t`, which is then re-grounded.
 - This forward-backward loop continues until a stable structure is found or a maximum number of iterations is reached.

This sophisticated iterative process, combining symbolic graph representation, compositional generative models, and stability-guided heuristics, is central to `StackItUp`'s ability to create robust and coherent 3D structures from minimal 2D input.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The authors conducted a comprehensive evaluation of "Stack It Up!" on 30 diverse hand-drawn sketches, encompassing iconic landmarks (e.g., Marina Bay Sands, Eiffel Tower) and modern house designs. These test cases varied in complexity, including those that explicitly required the inference of hidden supports for stability.

**Evaluation Metrics:**
Performance was assessed using two primary metrics:
1. **Physical Stability:** Measured as the percentage of blocks that remained in place under gravity in a physics simulation.
2. **Resemblance:** Quantified as the fraction of abstract geometric relations (derived from the 2D sketch) that were satisfied by the front-view projection of the generated 3D structure.

**Hypotheses Tested:**
The experiments were designed to validate three key hypotheses:
- **H1:** Abstract relation graphs enable zero-shot generation of stable, multi-level 3D structures from sketches.
- **H2:** Stability-pattern-guided graph updates efficiently recover missing supports.
- **H3:** Sketch-based input provides a more expressive design specification than natural language.

**Baselines and Ablation:**
"Stack It Up!" was compared against two baselines and an ablated variant:
1. **End-to-End Diffusion Model:** A single transformer-based diffusion model (inspired by StackGen) that directly predicts 3D poses from sketches, without intermediate graph representations. It was trained on the same synthetic dataset as StackItUp's compositional models.
2. **Direct VLM Prediction:** A Vision-Language Model (VLM, specifically GPT-4.1, inspired by Blox-Net) that first generates a textual description from the sketch and then uses that description to predict 3D block arrangements.
3. **Ablation: No Hidden Object Prediction:** This variant of "Stack It Up!" removed the stability-pattern-guided backward graph update. When instability was detected, it would only attempt to adjust poses using the initially specified blocks, without adding new hidden supports.

**Key Findings and Results:**

- **Overall Superiority of StackItUp:** As shown in Table 2, "Stack It Up!" consistently outperformed all baselines across both stability and resemblance metrics, regardless of whether hidden supports were required. This validates the effectiveness of its integrated approach.

- **H1 Validation: Abstract Relation Graph for Zero-Shot Generalization:** The End-to-End Diffusion Model performed poorly on both metrics (16.2-18.2% resemblance, 12.1-24.2% stability). This was attributed to the significant domain gap between synthetic training data and real-world hand-drawn sketches, leading to poor generalization. In contrast, "Stack It Up!" achieved high performance (79.9-84.4% resemblance, 94.5-98.8% stability), demonstrating strong zero-shot generalization. This confirms H1, highlighting how the abstract relation graph and compositional diffusion models effectively capture user intent and adapt to novel sketches.

- **H3 Validation: Sketches vs. Natural Language:** Direct VLM Prediction achieved reasonable stability (53.7-63.6%) but notably poor resemblance scores (30.2-33.1%). This indicates that while VLMs can infer plausible structures, natural language descriptions often fail to convey the fine-grained spatial and geometric details present in sketches, thereby supporting H3.

- **H2 Validation: Importance of Stability-Pattern-Guided Updates:** The ablation variant ("No Hidden Object Prediction") performed comparably to StackItUp when no hidden supports were needed (84.4% resemblance, 98.8% stability). However, its performance significantly degraded when hidden supports were necessary (78.8% resemblance, 69.8% stability). This drop in stability underscores the critical role and efficiency of the stability-pattern-guided backward graph updates in effectively repairing unstable configurations and supporting H2.

- **Robustness and Adaptability (Figure 6):** "Stack It Up!" demonstrated impressive robustness. Given the same sketch, it could adapt object poses to different candidate block dimensions while satisfying abstract relations (Figure 6a). Furthermore, when block sets changed, it robustly predicted different necessary hidden supports to maintain stability, showcasing its flexibility.

- **Scalability with Complexity (Figure 7):** As the number of blocks and abstract relations increased (indicating sketch complexity), "Stack It Up!" maintained consistently high resemblance and stability scores. In contrast, Direct VLM Prediction rapidly degraded, and the End-to-End Diffusion Model continued to perform poorly. The ablation also showed a decline in stability for larger, more complex structures requiring hidden supports, further emphasizing the efficacy of StackItUp's full methodology.

In summary, the experimental results conclusively demonstrate that "Stack It Up!" successfully translates rough 2D sketches into stable, accurate 3D structures, effectively addressing the challenges of imprecision, incompleteness, and stability for robot manipulation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

"Stack It Up!" represents a significant step forward in the field of human-robot interaction and robotic manipulation, offering a novel and intuitive paradigm for specifying complex 3D assembly tasks. Its core significance lies in breaking down the existing barriers that prevent non-expert users from interacting directly and effectively with robotic systems for construction.

**Key Significance:**

1. **Accessibility for Non-Experts:** The most immediate impact is the democratization of robotic control. By enabling users to simply draw a sketch, much like a child would, the system removes the need for specialized CAD knowledge, precise 3D modeling skills, or complex programming interfaces. This opens up robotic construction and assembly to a much broader audience, from educational settings to creative design and even everyday domestic tasks.
2. **Bridging Symbolic and Geometric Reasoning:** The introduction of the abstract relation graph is a pivotal contribution. It effectively bridges the gap between the symbolic, qualitative intent embedded in a rough sketch (e.g., "left-of," "supported-by") and the precise geometric poses required for physical robot execution. This intermediate representation allows the system to filter out noisy metric details while retaining crucial structural information.
3. **Automated Stability and Support Inference:** The iterative forward-backward grounding process, particularly the stability-pattern-guided graph updates, is highly significant. It addresses the inherent incompleteness of 2D sketches by intelligently inferring and adding hidden support blocks. This capability is crucial for generating physically viable multi-level structures, moving beyond mere visual resemblance to functional stability.
4. **Robustness to Imperfection and Variability:** The system's demonstrated robustness to rough sketches, varying block geometries, and increasing structural complexity showcases its practical utility. This ability to adapt and generate diverse stable structures from minimal input is a hallmark of intelligent systems.

**Potential Impact:**

- **Robotics and Automation:** "Stack It Up!" could revolutionize how assembly and construction tasks are specified for robots. Imagine robotic systems that can assist in rapid prototyping, architectural modeling, or even personal crafting, guided by simple user drawings. This could accelerate the deployment of robots in flexible manufacturing and personalized fabrication.
- **Human-Robot Collaboration:** The intuitive input modality fosters a more natural and collaborative relationship between humans and robots. It allows for a more fluid transfer of design intent, enabling robots to act as intelligent assistants that understand and complete tasks based on human creativity rather than strict instructions.
- **3D Content Creation and Design:** Beyond robotics, the methodology could inspire new tools for 3D content generation, allowing designers, artists, or even hobbyists to rapidly prototype stable 3D structures from 2D ideas, with the system handling the underlying structural integrity.
- **Education and STEM:** This technology has immense potential in educational settings, allowing students to experiment with design, physics, and robotics by drawing their ideas and watching a robot build them.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
The authors acknowledge several limitations that pave the way for exciting future research:

- **Single 2D Front-View Sketch:** Relying on a single view limits observability. Future work could extend the system to handle multi-view sketches, potentially using graph-matching techniques to establish correspondence between nodes across views, thereby improving accuracy and completeness.
- **Assumptions about Block Types and Geometries:** Currently, the system assumes labeled block types in the sketch and known candidate object geometries. Integrating 3D perception (e.g., single-view reconstruction or multi-view fusion) could allow for inferring block types and geometries from the sketch itself, making the system more autonomous in real-world scenarios.
- **Absence of Real-World Robot Feedback:** The current system operates in simulation for stability checks. Closing the loop with real-world robot feedback, incorporating visual or language-based corrections, human-in-the-loop refinement, or learning reward functions for outcome selection, would enable more adaptive and robust execution in dynamic physical environments.

In conclusion, "Stack It Up!" is a compelling demonstration of how combining symbolic reasoning with modern generative models can unlock intuitive human-robot interaction for complex physical tasks. Its significance lies in making sophisticated robotic capabilities accessible, paving the way for a future where robots can readily translate human sketches into tangible, stable creations.
