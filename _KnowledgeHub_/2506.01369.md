# Incentivizing LLMs to Self-Verify Their Answers

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Nanyang Technological University and Skywork AI developed a self-verification framework that trains Large Language Models to critically assess their own generated solutions within a unified reinforcement learning process. This approach overcomes limitations of external verifiers, enabling enhanced performance and efficiency in mathematical reasoning tasks by achieving strong greedy-decoding scores and outperforming baselines in test-time scaling.

## Problem

- Combining post-training reinforcement learning (RL) and test-time scaling methods for LLMs often yields only marginal gains in complex reasoning tasks.
- A significant distribution discrepancy exists between specialized post-trained LLMs and general-purpose external verifiers, leading to inaccurate solution assessments and hindering effective test-time scaling.
- External verifiers struggle to generalize to the narrow output distributions of LLMs fine-tuned on specific reasoning tasks, making them ineffective for guiding inference.

## Method

- A ==self-verification== framework is proposed, unifying answer generation and solution assessment within a single reinforcement learning (RL) process, using the Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) algorithm.
- The framework employs specific correctness and dynamic verification reward functions, alongside a policy-aligned buffer that stores recent model-generated solutions, to ensure the verifier is trained on relevant data.
- At inference time, the post-trained LLM acts as its own verifier, generating solutions and producing verification scores which are then used in a weighted aggregation method to intelligently combine multiple candidate answers.

## Results

- Self-verification models achieved strong greedy-decoding scores across various mathematical benchmarks (e.g., MATH500, AIME24/25), consistently outperforming their respective base models and standard GRPO.
- The trained models demonstrated verification accuracy comparable to leading commercial systems like GPT-4o and Claude-**3.7**-Sonnet when assessing their own outputs, despite having significantly fewer parameters.
- At test time, the self-verification method consistently yielded the best performance across all tested mathematical reasoning benchmarks (with N=**16** samples) and proved more efficient than external verifier-based methods, requiring only a single LLM deployment.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Training LLMs to self-verify their solutions directly addresses the distribution discrepancy issue, as the internal verifier is inherently aligned with the generator's output distribution.
> - Integrating the verification task into the RL problem-solving process intrinsically enhances the model's underlying reasoning capabilities, leading to improved greedy-decoding performance.
> - The use of a policy-aligned buffer and a dynamic verification reward function is crucial for stable training and incentivizing the model to accurately verify both correct and challenging incorrect solutions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper "Incentivizing LLMs to Self-Verify Their Answers" makes a significant contribution to the rapidly evolving field of Large Language Models (LLMs), particularly concerning their capabilities in complex reasoning tasks such as mathematics.

**LLMs and Reasoning:** The foundation of this work lies in the remarkable progress LLMs have shown in various natural language tasks, including their emerging prowess in complex reasoning. Researchers have identified "scaling laws" – principles describing how model performance improves with increased computational resources – applicable to both **post-training** (e.g., reinforcement learning for alignment) and **test-time** reasoning (e.g., using ensembles or external verifiers).

**Existing Scaling Approaches and Their Limitations:**
- **Post-training scaling** often involves fine-tuning LLMs with Reinforcement Learning (RL) using feedback signals, frequently from human preferences or ground-truth answers. Prominent examples in mathematical reasoning include DeepSeek-R1, SimpleRL-Zoo, DeepScaleR, Open R1, and Light-R1.
- **Test-time scaling** aims to boost accuracy during inference by leveraging additional computation. This includes techniques like self-consistency (majority voting from multiple generations), and more advanced methods that utilize external reward models (RMs) or verifiers to guide generation, such as best-of-N sampling, beam search, Monte-Carlo tree search, and multi-turn correction.

However, the authors identify a critical gap: combining these two scaling approaches often yields only "marginal gains." They pinpoint the core issue as **distribution discrepancies**. Specifically, LLMs post-trained on specific reasoning tasks develop a "narrow generation distribution," which external verifiers (typically trained on more general data or different distributions) struggle to accurately assess. This misalignment leads to incorrect solution verifications and consequently hinders effective test-time scaling, as highlighted by prior work on inference scaling limitations.

**Novel Contribution within the Landscape:** This paper directly addresses this distribution discrepancy by proposing a **self-verification framework**. Instead of relying on external verifiers, it unifies the processes of answer generation and verification within a *single reinforcement learning process*. This allows the LLM to learn to generate solutions and concurrently develop the ability to critically assess its *own* outputs, thereby internalizing the verification mechanism. This approach contrasts with:
- **Traditional Verifier Training:** Which often involves training discriminative classifiers with auxiliary heads or supervised fine-tuning (SFT) for generative verifiers. While concurrent work also explores SFT within RL algorithms for verifiers, this paper is presented as the first to propose a *unified RL framework for both problem-solving and verification*.
- **Existing RL for Reasoning:** Which primarily focuses on optimizing problem-solving output. This work extends these by integrating a verification objective.

By demonstrating that models can effectively self-verify and achieve superior performance in both post-training and test-time scaling without external verifiers, this research opens new avenues for creating more reliable, efficient, and self-sufficient LLMs in verifiable reasoning domains.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from an observed inefficiency and limitation in the current paradigm of scaling Large Language Models for complex reasoning tasks. While significant advancements have been made through both post-training reinforcement learning (RL) and test-time scaling techniques, the authors note that combining these methods often fails to deliver synergistic benefits.

The core problem identified is a **distribution discrepancy**. Large Language Models, when post-trained on specific reasoning datasets (e.g., mathematical problems), tend to develop a highly specialized output distribution. External reward models or verifiers, on the other hand, are typically trained on more general data. This mismatch causes external verifiers to be less effective at accurately assessing the correctness of solutions generated by these specialized post-trained models, leading to "incorrect solution verifications" and ultimately impeding performance improvements at inference time. The paper highlights this issue by showing that even advanced test-time scaling methods with external verifiers offer minimal gains compared to simpler techniques like self-consistency through majority voting.

Given this challenge, the **key objectives** of this work are multifaceted:

1. **To propose a novel framework that incentivizes LLMs to self-verify their own answers.** This involves training the model to critically assess the correctness of its generated solutions rather than relying on an external, potentially misaligned, verifier.
2. **To unify answer generation and verification within a single reinforcement learning (RL) process.** The goal is to integrate these two complementary tasks, allowing the model to learn problem-solving and solution assessment concurrently, fostering a deeper understanding of problem structures.
3. **To train models that can effectively assess the correctness of their own solutions.** This means equipping the LLM with an internal "verifier" that is inherently aligned with its own generation patterns.
4. **To enable effective test-time scaling without the need for external verifiers.** By having a built-in verification capability, the model can leverage its own assessment scores to aggregate multiple generated answers more intelligently, thereby enhancing performance at inference time using only a single LLM deployment.
5. **Ultimately, to improve post-training performance and enable robust, effective test-time scaling** across various mathematical reasoning benchmarks, showcasing the benefits of this unified self-verification approach.

By pursuing these objectives, the authors aim to overcome the limitations imposed by distribution discrepancies, thereby advancing the reliability, efficiency, and overall reasoning capabilities of LLMs.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed **self-verification framework** is designed to overcome the limitations of external verifiers by enabling an LLM to assess the correctness of its own generated solutions. The core idea is to train the model for both answer generation and verification simultaneously within a unified reinforcement learning (RL) process.

#### 3.1. RL with Self-Verification

The framework builds upon the **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)** algorithm, a variant of PPO, which is effective for training LLMs on reasoning tasks. GRPO uses group-based policy sampling to compute advantages, leading to improved efficiency and stability without requiring additional value functions.

- **Reward Functions:**
 - **Correctness Reward ($r_c$):** For the problem-solving task, a standard binary reward (1 for correct, 0 for incorrect) is used, determined by comparing the extracted answer from the generated solution against the ground truth using a rule-based verifier.
 - **Verification Reward ($r_v$):** For the verification task, a binary reward is assigned based on whether the model's predicted verification outcome (e.g., "Yes" or "No" after a generated rationale) matches the ground-truth correctness of the *specific solution being verified*. This trains the model to correctly identify accurate and inaccurate solutions.

- **Policy-Aligned Buffer:** A critical component for verification training is the **policy-aligned buffer (B)**. Since the verification task's data depends on the model's generated responses, using outdated or off-policy data can be detrimental. The buffer stores only the *most recent* solutions generated during the math reasoning training (within the last $T_b$ training steps). This ensures that the verifier is trained on data representative of the current model's capabilities, maintaining alignment between the generator and the verifier components.

- **Dynamic Verification Reward ($\hat{r}_v$):** To prevent the verifier from focusing solely on easy cases (as correct solutions might dominate the buffer over time), a dynamic reward function is introduced. This reward identifies and provides higher incentives for successfully verifying *challenging* solutions. Leveraging GRPO's group rollouts, the dynamic reward considers the proportion of correct ($G_c$) and incorrect ($G_i$) generations within a group for a given problem.
 - If the model correctly verifies a correct solution (y_v = Yes, r_c = 1), the reward is proportional to $2G_i/G$. This means a higher reward is given if many other generated solutions for that problem were incorrect, indicating it was a challenging problem to solve correctly, and thus a valuable verification.
 - If the model correctly verifies an incorrect solution (y_v = No, r_c = 0), the reward is proportional to $2G_c/G$. This incentivizes correctly identifying flaws in solutions to problems that were frequently solved correctly by the model, again highlighting a nuanced verification task.
 - The expected reward for correct verification is maintained at 1, keeping it comparable to the problem-solving reward.

The training algorithm (Algorithm 1) integrates these elements: it samples batches from both the original dataset (for problem-solving) and the policy-aligned buffer (for verification). Solutions generated for problem-solving are added to the buffer. The total reward for each sample combines $r_c$ and $\hat{r}_v$, guiding the GRPO optimization process.

#### 3.2. Self-Verification for Test-time Scaling

At inference time, the post-trained, self-verifying LLM can leverage its learned capabilities to enhance performance without any external models.

- **Generation and Scoring:** Given a generation budget of $N$ samples, the model first generates $N$ candidate responses ($y_1, \dots, y_N$). For each response, the model then acts as its own verifier, prompted with the solution, to produce a verification score $s(x, y_i)$. This score is typically the likelihood of the "Yes" token in its generative verification output.
- **Weighted Answer Aggregation:** Unique answers are extracted from the $N$ responses. The final prediction ($\hat{a}$) is determined by a weighted aggregation:
 $\hat{a} = \text{argmax}_{a \in A} \sum_{i=1}^N I[E(y_i) = a] (1 + \alpha s(x, y_i))$
 Here, $\alpha$ is a hyperparameter that controls the influence of the verification score. If $\alpha=0$, it reduces to standard majority voting (self-consistency). As $\alpha$ increases, the verifier's confidence plays a larger role.
- **Efficiency:** A key advantage is that this entire process requires only a single LLM deployment. The same model acts as both generator and verifier, making it highly efficient and compatible with modern LLM inference engines (e.g., vLLM, SGLang).

#### 3.3. Models and Data

Experiments are conducted using two base models:
- **Qwen2.5-Math-7B:** A 7B-parameter model pretrained on math data with a relatively short context length. Trained on MATH dataset levels 3-5.
- **DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B:** A 1.5B-parameter distilled version of DeepSeek-R1, capable of generating long Chain-of-Thought (CoT) responses. Trained on a combined math reasoning dataset from DeepScaleR.

This comprehensive methodology allows for robust training of self-verification capabilities and their efficient application during inference.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted by Zhang et al. demonstrate the effectiveness and efficiency of their self-verification framework across various mathematical reasoning benchmarks.

#### 4.1. Post-training Model Performance

- **Performance on Problem-Solving:**
 - The self-verification models (Self-Verification-Qwen-7B and Self-Verification-R1-1.5B) consistently achieve strong greedy-decoding scores across benchmarks like MATH500, AIME24/25, AMC23, and OlympiadBench (Table 1).
 - They significantly outperform their respective base models.
 - Crucially, integrating self-verification into the RL process yields *higher scores* than standard GRPO models trained for problem-solving only. This suggests that the act of training for verification intrinsically enhances the model's underlying problem-solving capabilities, likely by fostering a deeper understanding of logical structure.

- **Performance on Verifying Solutions:**
 - The self-verification models show substantial improvements in both accuracy and F1 score when verifying their *own* generated solutions compared to similarly sized open-source baselines (Tables 2 & 3).
 - Remarkably, their performance is "on par with leading commercial systems" such as GPT-4o, Claude-3.7-Sonnet, and DeepSeek-v3, even surpassing GPT-4o in some verification tasks despite having significantly fewer parameters.
 - Verification tasks on more complex problems like AIME24 are noted to be more challenging for all models.

#### 4.2. Self-verification on Test-time Scaling

- The framework enables highly effective test-time scaling. When using a generation budget of 16 samples, the self-verification method consistently yields the **best performance** across all tested benchmarks compared to other common scaling methods (Figure 1, Table 4, and Appendix D).
- **Validation of Hypothesis:** The results strongly support the paper's central hypothesis regarding distribution discrepancies. Methods relying on *external verifiers* (e.g., Best-of-N, Beam Search, Diverse Verifier Tree Search - DVTS) perform *worse* than even simple self-consistency (majority voting). This indicates that external verifiers indeed struggle to generalize to the specific output distribution of post-trained models, making them ineffective. Process-based methods exacerbate this by amplifying the distribution shift during step-wise generation.
- The performance gains from self-verification are notable, consistently outperforming baselines on most tasks.

#### 4.3. Analysis on the Efficiency of Self-Verification

- **Post-training Efficiency:** The training cost is comparable to other RL methods. The verification process is found to be less computationally intensive than problem-solving, requiring significantly fewer tokens (24% for Qwen-7B, 35% for R1-1.5B) on average (Figure 3).
- **Inference-time Efficiency:** Self-verification offers notable efficiency advantages at inference time. It is faster than Best-of-N sampling and significantly more efficient than process-based methods like Beam Search and DVTS (Figure 4). This is because it requires only a single LLM deployment, eliminating the overhead of managing external reward models. While slightly slower than simple majority voting, the performance gains justify this trade-off.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies

- **Aggregation Coefficient ($\alpha$):** The hyperparameter $\alpha$ (balancing majority voting with verifier confidence) shows optimal performance at $\alpha=1.0$ for Self-Verification-Qwen-7B and $\alpha=0.1$ for Self-Verification-R1-1.5B (Table 5). This suggests that smaller models might benefit from a less aggressive weighting of the verification score due to their potentially more limited verification capabilities.
- **Policy-Aligned Buffer:** The buffer's update frequency ($T_b$) is critical for stability. A moderate value ($T_b=60$) offers stable verification accuracy, while smaller values (e.g., $T_b=20$) show less stability, and larger values provide less signal at later stages (Figure 7).
- **Dynamic Verification Reward:** For the 7B model, the dynamic reward significantly improves verification accuracy compared to a simple binary reward (Figure 8). For the 1.5B model, the gain is less pronounced, possibly due to its smaller capacity limiting its ability to leverage nuanced reward signals.

#### 4.5. Comparison with Entropy-Minimization Baseline

- The Self-Verification-Qwen-7B model consistently outperforms the one-shot entropy minimization (EM 1-shot) baseline across all benchmarks (Table 9), further solidifying the efficacy of the joint problem-solving and verification framework.

In summary, the results convincingly demonstrate that the self-verification framework not only mitigates the problem of distribution discrepancies but also leads to more capable, efficient, and reliable LLMs for mathematical reasoning.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The self-verification framework proposed by Zhang et al. represents a significant advancement in the development and application of Large Language Models, particularly for verifiable reasoning tasks. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

#### 5.1. Bridging the Gap in LLM Scaling

The most profound significance of this work is its successful resolution of a critical limitation: the distribution discrepancy between highly specialized post-trained LLMs and general-purpose external reward models. By unifying answer generation and verification within a single RL process, the framework enables seamless synergy between post-training and test-time scaling. This directly addresses the inefficiencies observed when combining these two previously disparate approaches, leading to genuinely improved performance at scale.

#### 5.2. Enhanced Reliability and Trustworthiness of LLMs

Models capable of self-verification are inherently more reliable. By learning to critically assess their own solutions, LLMs can reduce the incidence of "hallucinations" and factual errors, especially in domains like mathematics where correctness is unambiguous. This increased trustworthiness is crucial for deploying LLMs in high-stakes applications such as scientific research, engineering design, financial analysis, and educational tools, where accuracy is paramount.

#### 5.3. Improved Computational Efficiency and Practicality

A major practical advantage of the self-verification framework is its efficiency. By eliminating the need for external reward models, the system requires only a single LLM deployment for both generation and verification during inference. This simplifies the deployment architecture, reduces computational overhead, and potentially lowers energy consumption associated with running complex LLM systems. The measured efficiency gains over external verifier-based methods make this approach highly practical for real-world LLM inference engines.

#### 5.4. Advancement in Multi-Task Reinforcement Learning for LLMs

The methodology introduces novel components like the policy-aligned buffer and the dynamic verification reward, which contribute to the stability and effectiveness of multi-task RL training. This demonstrates how complementary capabilities (problem-solving and self-assessment) can be synergistically learned within a unified model, inspiring future RL research in integrating diverse cognitive functions into LLMs.

#### 5.5. Potential for Broader Applicability and Generalization

While the current work focuses on mathematical reasoning, the core concept of self-verification has strong potential for generalization to other verifiable domains. This could include:
- **Code Generation:** LLMs could generate code and then self-verify its correctness, functionality, or adherence to style guidelines.
- **Scientific Reasoning:** Verifying hypotheses, experimental designs, or logical deductions in scientific contexts.
- **Agentic Tasks:** Equipping AI agents with the ability to assess the quality of their own plans or actions.
Adapting the framework to these areas would likely require domain-specific reward signals and verification strategies, but the fundamental principle remains powerful.

#### 5.6. Broader Societal Impact (as acknowledged by the authors)

- **Positive Impacts:** Contribute to more reliable AI systems for education, decision support, and scientific discovery.
- **Risks and Limitations:**
 - The framework does not guarantee 100% accuracy, and users might over-trust self-verified outputs, especially in critical domains.
 - Generalizability beyond mathematical reasoning needs further testing.
 - Potential for misuse in generating more legitimate-seeming deceptive content.
- **Mitigation Strategies:** The authors advocate for clear communication of limitations, complementary human verification in critical applications, continued research into domain boundaries, and transparent methodology.

In conclusion, "Incentivizing LLMs to Self-Verify Their Answers" is a pivotal contribution that offers a robust and efficient solution to a fundamental challenge in scaling LLM reasoning. By internalizing the verification process, this work paves the way for more autonomous, reliable, and trustworthy AI systems capable of complex reasoning.
