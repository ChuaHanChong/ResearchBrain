# GenLit: Reformulating Single-Image Relighting as Video Generation

> [!summary] Summary
> GenLit reformulates single-image relighting as a controllable video generation task, leveraging a fine-tuned video diffusion model to dynamically adjust lighting from a moving point source. This approach achieves superior quantitative performance on synthetic data (e.g., RMSE 0.0309, LPIPS 0.0209) and generalizes effectively to real-world images, generating plausible shadows, reflections, and indirect lighting effects.

## Problem

- Single-image relighting is an ill-posed problem due to ambiguities concerning light sources, object albedo, and lack of depth information.
- Traditional inverse rendering methods rely on explicit 3D asset reconstruction and computationally expensive physically based rendering (PBR) simulations.
- Existing neural relighting approaches often struggle with handling complex, spatially varying materials or require specialized light-stage setups and extensive data.
- Achieving fine-grained, controllable local illumination with realistic high-frequency details like sharp shadows and specular reflections from a single image is challenging.

## Method

- The method reformulates single-image relighting as a controllable video generation task, where a point light source moves dynamically over static objects in a video sequence.
- It fine-tunes a pre-trained ==Stable Video Diffusion== (SVD) model by introducing a trainable control branch (similar to ==ControlNet==) that takes a 5D vector as input for light control.
- A custom synthetic dataset, Objaverse-**GenLit**, featuring diverse 3D assets, varied environments, and dynamic point light trajectories, was created for training.
- The 5D control signal precisely defines the point light's 3D position, intensity, and ambient light intensity for each frame, enabling granular manipulation of local illumination.

## Results

- GenLit demonstrated superior quantitative performance on synthetic datasets, achieving lower RMSE (**0.0309**), LPIPS (**0.0209**), and higher SSIM (**0.9780**), and PSNR (**40.7174**) compared to state-of-the-art baselines.
- The model exhibited strong zero-shot generalization to real-world images, synthesizing plausible shape-appropriate shadows, reflections, and indirect lighting effects like color bleeding from unseen objects.
- A perceptual study confirmed the realism of GenLit's outputs, with participants consistently rating results on real images as "realistic" (mean/median Likert scores close to 4 out of 5).
- The system effectively diminishes existing shadows in input images and replaces them with new, consistent shadows from the inserted light source, enhancing the overall realism of the relighting process.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Video foundation models, trained on extensive image and video datasets, implicitly learn a robust understanding of 3D scenes and how light interacts with materials.
> - By modeling relighting as a temporal change in illumination, the inherent capabilities of video diffusion models to synthesize consistent changes over time can be exploited.
> - Training on a carefully constructed synthetic dataset featuring dynamic lighting allows the model to generalize effectively to real-world images without requiring explicit 3D reconstruction.
> - The dynamic change from ambient to point light source, alongside its movement, is crucial for convincing relighting and the synthesis of realistic shadow details.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The manipulation of 3D scene illumination within a single image constitutes a recognized challenge in computer vision and graphics. Traditionally, this problem has been addressed through inverse rendering techniques. These methods typically involve the explicit reconstruction of 3D assets, including geometry, material properties, and scene illumination, followed by physically based rendering (PBR) simulations, which often rely on costly ray-tracing. This approach is inherently complex and ill-posed due to ambiguities concerning light sources, object albedo, lack of depth information, and unknown material properties, leading to potential errors and high computational expense.

Recent advancements in visual foundation models, particularly image and video diffusion models, have introduced an alternative paradigm. These models, trained on extensive image and video datasets, are posited to develop an implicit understanding of 3D scenes and the interactions between light and matter. This implicit learning capability suggests a potential to bypass the need for explicit physical models and ray-tracing simulations.

GenLit positions itself within this emerging paradigm by leveraging pre-trained image-to-video diffusion models, specifically Stable Video Diffusion (SVD). The core innovation is the reformulation of single-image relighting as an image-to-video generation task. In this new framework, the scene and objects remain static, while illumination changes dynamically over time, driven by a moving light source. This approach capitalizes on the inherent ability of video diffusion models to synthesize temporally consistent changes and their hypothesized implicit grasp of 3D scene structure.

The research focuses on *near-field relighting* with a point light source, a domain that has received comparatively less attention than global scene illumination methods. Near-field relighting is critical for revealing high-frequency surface details, scene structure through cast shadows, specular reflections, and localized shading effects, making it relevant for applications such as virtual product showcases and post-production editing.

The authors contrast GenLit with existing work:
- **Inverse Rendering**: Acknowledges methods that estimate intrinsic properties (e.g., Barron and Malik, Li et al.) but highlights their limitations in handling complex, spatially varying BRDFs from single images, their reliance on assumptions like diffuse materials, and their computational demands.
- **Neural Relighting**: Distinguishes from methods that use expensive light-stage setups (e.g., Pandey et al.), GAN-based models (e.g., Deng et al.) which are often class-specific, and diffusion models applied to tasks like HDR map estimation (e.g., Lyu et al.) or object insertion (e.g., Liang et al.). It also differentiates from approaches that control lighting in text-to-image models (e.g., Bashkirova et al., Kocsis et al., Zeng et al., Jin et al.), noting that these often provide limited or discrete control, focus on large outdoor scenes, or use environment maps that may lack fine-grained detail. Image-to-image translation (e.g., Xing et al., Zhang et al.) and intrinsic image decomposition (e.g., Zeng et al.) are also contrasted, as they can sometimes struggle with identity preservation or consistency.
- **Controllable Video Synthesis**: GenLit is presented as the initial work to investigate controllable relighting, specifically focusing on local illumination, using an image-to-video generative model, building upon principles similar to ControlNet. Acknowledged concurrent work, DiffusionRenderer (Liang et al.), is noted to still rely on disentangling intrinsic properties.

This work therefore contributes to the field by proposing a data-driven, generative approach to a traditionally model-based problem, offering a pathway to overcome the challenges of explicit 3D reconstruction and physical simulation by leveraging the implicit learning capabilities of modern video foundation models.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a method for realistically relighting a single image with a point light source without requiring explicit inverse graphics techniques or 3D asset reconstruction. This encompasses synthesizing both direct and indirect lighting effects in a plausible manner.

The motivation for this work is multi-faceted:

- **Addressing the Ill-Posed Nature of Relighting**: Single-image relighting is an ill-posed problem due to inherent ambiguities (e.g., distinguishing between light and surface albedo, lack of precise 3D geometry). Traditional inverse rendering attempts to resolve these by explicitly reconstructing scene properties, which is complex, computationally intensive, and prone to errors. The authors aim to find an alternative that bypasses these difficulties.
- **Leveraging Implicit Scene Understanding in Foundation Models**: Recent advancements in large-scale image and video foundation models have demonstrated their capacity to learn intricate representations of the visual world. The researchers hypothesize that these models, particularly video diffusion models, implicitly encode substantial 3D scene understanding, including knowledge of material properties, object shapes, and how light interacts with them. This implicit understanding could potentially replace the need for explicit physics-based representations.
- **Reformulating the Problem**: The key insight is that modifying scene lighting involves a *change* over time. Video diffusion models are specifically designed to model change. By reformulating static single-image relighting as a dynamic video generation task, where a point light moves, the authors aim to exploit the temporal modeling capabilities and implicit 3D representations of these video models.
- **Achieving Fine-Grained, Controllable Local Illumination**: The focus is on *near-field relighting* with a single point light source to achieve granular control over local illumination. This enables the generation of high-frequency details such as sharp cast shadows and specular reflections, which are important for realism and various applications. The objective is to provide an interpretable control signal for precise manipulation of the light source's position and intensity, as well as ambient light.
- **Generalization to Real-World Scenarios**: A significant motivation is to develop a method that, when trained on synthetic data, can generalize effectively to real-world images and complex scenes. This would mitigate the need for extensive, specialized real-world data collection typically required for inverse rendering or light-stage setups.
- **Efficiency and Practicality**: By moving away from explicit PBR asset reconstruction and ray-tracing, the goal is to develop a more efficient and less computationally demanding approach, thereby expanding its practical applicability to creative and interactive domains.
- **Enabling New Applications**: The ability to flexibly and realistically relight single images has potential for applications in digital content creation, virtual product showcasing, post-production editing, and augmented reality, especially for scenarios where manual 3D asset design is time-consuming or challenging.

In summary, the core motivation is to harness the generative power and implicit scene understanding of video diffusion models to create a robust, controllable, and generalizable solution for single-image relighting that bypasses the limitations of traditional, explicit inverse graphics pipelines.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The GenLit framework reformulates single-image relighting as a controllable video generation task. The approach involves fine-tuning a pre-trained image-to-video diffusion model using a custom-designed control signal and a synthetic dataset.

**Core Architecture and Control Mechanism:**
The method builds upon Stable Video Diffusion (SVD) [Blattmann et al. 2023a,b], a model capable of generating a video sequence of *N* frames from a single input image. SVD employs a conditional 3D-UNet, denoted Φ, with temporal blocks to ensure temporal consistency.

GenLit extends this SVD architecture by incorporating a control mechanism similar to ControlNet [Zhang et al. 2023]:
1. **Frozen Base Model**: The pre-trained SVD model (Φ) is kept frozen during training.
2. **Trainable Control Branch**: A trainable copy of the U-Net encoder, Ψ, is introduced as a control branch.
3. **Control Signal Definition**: The control signal is a sequence of 5D vectors, [®𝑙1..., ®𝑙𝑁], where each ®𝑙𝑖 = [(𝜙𝑖, 𝜃𝑖, 𝑟𝑖, 𝐼𝑝𝑖, 𝐼𝑒𝑖)] for frame *i*.
 - (𝜙𝑖, 𝜃𝑖, 𝑟𝑖) define the 3D position of the point light source in polar coordinates (azimuth, elevation, radius).
 - 𝐼𝑝𝑖 represents the intensity of the point light.
 - 𝐼𝑒𝑖 represents the intensity of the ambient environment light.
4. **Signal Integration**: Each 5D vector ®𝑙𝑖 is broadcast onto an image L𝑖 ∈ R𝐻 ×𝑊 ×5, which is then fed as input to the trainable control branch Ψ.
5. **Feature Injection**: The conditioning features predicted by Ψ are added to the SVD model's encoder and middle blocks (Φ), directly influencing the video generation process through the U-Net's decoder.
6. **Conditional Denoising**: The conditional denoising process at each step *t* is formulated as x𝑡 = 𝑐skip(𝜎) x𝑡−1 + 𝑐out(𝜎) Φ(x𝑡−1, 𝑐, Ψ(L)), where *c* includes the CLIP embedding and VAE latents of the input image, 𝜎 is Gaussian noise standard deviation, and 𝑐skip(𝜎), 𝑐out(𝜎) are scaling coefficients.
7. **Loss Function**: The model is fine-tuned using the loss L = E𝑥0,𝜖,𝜎 [𝑤(𝜎) ∥x𝑡 − x0∥22], with 𝑤(𝜎) = 1+𝜎2/𝜎2.

**Lighting Manipulation Strategy within Video Generation:**
The method employs a specific strategy for dynamically changing the lighting over the video sequence:
- **Initial Dimming and Fade-in**: In the first four frames, the intensity of the ambient environment map (𝐼𝑒𝑖) is gradually reduced to 20% of its initial value. Concurrently, the intensity of the inserted point light (𝐼𝑝𝑖) is increased from 0 to 120 lumens, becoming visible from the second frame. This ensures the newly introduced point light becomes the dominant light source.
- **Dynamic Light Movement**: From the fifth frame (or seventh for multi-object scenes) until the end of the video sequence, the point light moves according to the trajectory defined by (𝜙𝑖, 𝜃𝑖, 𝑟𝑖) in the control signal.

**Objaverse-GenLit Dataset:**
To train the model, a custom synthetic video dataset named Objaverse-GenLit was created using Blender 3.2.2 with the Cycles path-tracing engine. The dataset captures diverse light-object interactions under dynamic lighting.

- **3D Assets**: Comprises 1436 high-quality 3D assets: 1200 objects from the Objaverse dataset (diverse LVIS categories) and 236 detailed objects from Poly Haven.
- **Scene Construction**:
 - A single textured ground plane (370 textures from Poly Haven) with random rotations.
 - Ambient lighting from 620 curated HDRI maps (Poly Haven), filtered to exclude strong direct sunlight, with random rotations for diversity.
 - A fixed camera viewpoint with slight elevation perturbations (±0.3m).
- **Scene Types**:
 - **SingleObject**: A single object placed centrally. 1200 objects used for training. Light trajectories consist of grid-based, linearly interpolated horizontal, vertical, and diagonal motions (40 sampled trajectories per object, 14 frames) with a fixed radius (r=1) and elevation (45°-80°), and full azimuth variation.
 - **MultiObject-FlyingLight**: Multiple objects (one from Poly Haven, up to ten from Objaverse) randomly scattered along a spiral path, with collision checks. 1000 random scenes for training. Light trajectories are more complex, generated using Bézier curves and spirals (40 sampled trajectories per scene, 25 frames), allowing arbitrary motion within 0.8 ≤ r ≤ 1.5, 45°-80° 𝜙, and 0°-360° 𝜃. A glow effect is applied to the moving light sphere during post-processing to make it appear "fairy-like."
- **Testing Splits**: Dedicated test sets were created, including unseen objects and distinct light trajectories. Real-world images captured with an iPhone 15 (RealData-SingleObject and RealData-MultiObject) were also used for zero-shot generalization evaluation, primarily qualitatively.

**Training Details:**
The model was fine-tuned for 20,000 iterations using the AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of 5 × 10⁻⁵ on 8 A100 GPUs with a batch size of 16. Training resolutions were 512x512 for SingleObject and 640x448 for MultiObject-FlyingLight.

**Evaluation:**
Performance was quantitatively assessed using image-space metrics: Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), perceptual loss (LPIPS), Structural Similarity Index Measure (SSIM), and PSNR against ground-truth images. Qualitative evaluation and a perceptual study (using a five-point Likert scale) were conducted on both synthetic and real-world images. Comparisons were made against various state-of-the-art inverse rendering and neural relighting baselines (e.g., WS-SIR, Neural Gaffer, IC-Light, DiLightNet, Diffusion Renderer, Latent Intrinsics, LumiNet), with careful adaptation of inputs for fair comparison.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research demonstrates that reformulating single-image relighting as a video generation task using a controllable video diffusion model is effective, yielding results that surpass existing methods in certain aspects and generalize to real-world scenarios.

**Quantitative Evaluations on Synthetic Data (SingleObject-Test):**
GenLit consistently achieved superior performance across image-space metrics when compared to state-of-the-art baselines (WS-SIR, Neural Gaffer, IC-Light, DiLightNet, Diffusion Renderer).
- **RMSE**: GenLit recorded the lowest RMSE of 0.0309, indicating closer pixel-level fidelity to ground truth, compared to the next best, Diffusion Renderer (0.0453).
- **LPIPS**: GenLit also had the lowest perceptual loss (0.0209), suggesting better perceived similarity to ground truth, with Diffusion Renderer at 0.0308.
- **SSIM**: GenLit achieved the highest SSIM (0.9780), indicating better structural similarity, surpassing Diffusion Renderer (0.9634).
- **PSNR**: GenLit yielded the highest PSNR (40.7174), signifying better signal-to-noise ratio, with Diffusion Renderer at 39.2117.
These results indicate GenLit's ability to synthesize high-quality relit images that closely resemble path-traced ground truth generated from 3D assets.

**Qualitative Evaluations on Synthetic Data (SingleObject-Test):**
GenLit demonstrated a capacity to generate plausible shadows and light fall-off, including shadows cast from the foreground object onto the background. This was observed to be a crucial element for realism, which some baselines struggled with (e.g., Neural Gaffer’s subtle lighting changes, IC-Light’s color bleeding from background, DiLightNet’s artifacts from depth estimates, Diffusion Renderer’s identity shifts).

**Generalization to Real-World Images (SingleObject and MultiObject-FlyingLight):**
A key finding is the model’s ability to generalize to real-world images captured with a phone, despite being trained exclusively on synthetic data.
- **Plausible Shadows and Reflections**: GenLit synthesized shape-appropriate shadows and reflections consistent with the materials of unseen real objects. For example, textures on an orange were retained, and metallic properties of objects like pitchers and coins were appropriately rendered.
- **Source Shadow Removal**: The method effectively diminished existing shadows in the input image and replaced them with new shadows consistent with the inserted light source's position, enhancing the realism of the relighting.
- **Indirect Lighting Effects**: In multi-object scenes, GenLit demonstrated the synthesis of indirect lighting effects such as diffuse interreflections, where light bouncing off colored objects resulted in plausible color bleeding onto adjacent surfaces.
- **Material Robustness**: The model handled materials that were out of the training distribution (e.g., linen pillows, plastic-wrapped tires), maintaining their visual realism after relighting.

**Perceptual Study:**
A perceptual study indicated that participants consistently rated GenLit's relighting results on real images as "realistic." The mean and median Likert scores were close to 4 (on a 5-point scale) across RealData-SingleObject, RealData-MultiObject, and MIT Multi-Illumination test sets, with narrow distributions, suggesting consistent positive perception.

**Quantitative and Qualitative Evaluations on MIT Multi-Illumination Dataset:**
When trained on the MIT Multi-Illumination Dataset and compared to Latent Intrinsics and LumiNet, GenLit again showed improved quantitative results and preserved scene details more effectively.
- GenLit achieved lower RMSE (0.1129 vs. 0.1513 for Latent Intrinsics, 0.1862 for LumiNet) and LPIPS (0.1302 vs. 0.1526, 0.1859), and higher SSIM (0.7623 vs. 0.7574, 0.6784) and PSNR (28.974 vs. 28.454, 28.1718).
- Qualitatively, GenLit's outputs were closer to the ground truth, demonstrating capacities like rendering bright spots from close light sources, hallucinating details in overexposed regions, and synthesizing sharp specularities.

**Ablation Study (5D Vector Analysis):**
An ablation study regarding the point light and ambient light intensities confirmed that the chosen setting (ambient environment light reduced to 20%, point light at 120 lumens) yielded the best quantitative results and generalization to real images. This setting ensured the inserted point light was dominant and created clear shadows.

Overall, the findings suggest that video diffusion models, when appropriately fine-tuned with a controlled lighting signal, can implicitly learn to perform single-image relighting with a high degree of realism and generalization, synthesizing complex direct and indirect lighting effects without explicit 3D reconstruction.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The GenLit research presents a significant contribution to the field of computer vision and graphics, particularly in the domain of image relighting, with several potential impacts:

- **Validation of a New Paradigm for Relighting**: The work validates the hypothesis that video foundation models, through their implicit understanding of 3D scenes and temporal changes, can serve as a viable and powerful alternative to traditional, explicit inverse rendering pipelines. This represents a paradigm shift from model-based physical simulations to data-driven generative synthesis for complex lighting effects.
- **Reduced Computational Complexity and Resource Demands**: By bypassing the need for explicit 3D asset reconstruction (geometry, material properties, illumination) and computationally expensive ray-tracing, GenLit offers a more efficient and less resource-intensive approach to relighting. This could democratize access to advanced relighting capabilities, previously limited by specialized expertise and hardware.
- **Enhanced Generalization Capability**: The demonstration that a model trained exclusively on synthetic data can generalize effectively to complex, unseen real-world objects and scenes is a notable achievement. This broadens the practical applicability of the method, reducing the reliance on costly real-world data collection for specific assets or environments.
- **Controllable and Realistic Lighting Effects**: GenLit provides an interpretable and fine-grained control mechanism (the 5D lighting vector) for manipulating a point light source. Its ability to synthesize plausible direct effects (shape-appropriate shadows) and complex local indirect effects (diffuse interreflections, color bleeding) from a single input image contributes to generating photorealistic results, which is a key goal in image synthesis.
- **Broadening Application Horizons**:
 - **Content Creation**: Artists, designers, and digital content creators could use GenLit to easily modify the lighting in static images or create dynamic lighting sequences for videos, without needing extensive 3D modeling or rendering skills. This could streamline workflows in film, animation, and advertising.
 - **Virtual Product Showcasing**: Businesses could generate dynamic and realistic lighting presentations of products from a single photograph, enhancing e-commerce and marketing visuals.
 - **Augmented and Virtual Reality (AR/VR)**: The method could facilitate more immersive AR/VR experiences by enabling dynamic relighting of real-world captures or virtual objects to match changing environmental illumination.
 - **Post-production Editing**: Professional photo and video editors could leverage GenLit for advanced lighting adjustments, opening new possibilities for creative manipulation of existing footage.
- **Inspiration for Future Research**: The success of GenLit encourages further exploration into applying video diffusion models to other challenging inverse graphics problems. This includes extending the approach to handle more complex global illumination via HDRI maps, or tackling intricate light-matter interactions such as caustics and subsurface scattering, potentially requiring richer synthetic datasets.
- **Community Contribution**: The authors' commitment to releasing code, model weights, and the dataset fosters reproducibility and provides a foundation for further research and development in controllable generative models for realistic image synthesis.

In conclusion, GenLit represents a significant step forward by demonstrating the efficacy of reformulating a complex static computer graphics problem into a dynamic video generation task within the generative AI framework. Its capacity for realistic, controllable, and generalizable relighting has the potential to influence various creative and technical applications, pushing the boundaries of what is achievable with single-image manipulation.
