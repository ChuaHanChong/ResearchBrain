# Adaptive Q-Chunking for Offline-to-Online Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Adaptive Q-Chunking (AQC) introduces a method to dynamically adjust action chunk sizes during offline-to-online reinforcement learning, addressing the limitations of fixed chunk sizes in long-horizon tasks. The approach achieved superior online success rates on various manipulation benchmarks, including 100% on OGBench's cube-double task and an average of 63.2% on RoboCasa-GR1 tasks, improving upon prior methods by enabling context-dependent control granularity.

## Problem

- Offline-to-online reinforcement learning struggles with long-horizon, sparse-reward tasks due to fixed chunk sizes, leading to suboptimal performance in different task phases.
- Existing action chunking methods use a single, static chunk size which is inefficient for varied demands like free-space movement (longer chunks) versus contact-rich manipulation (shorter, reactive chunks).
- Naive adaptive chunk selection suffers from discount-scale mismatch, causing a bias towards shorter chunks, and noise amplification in low-value states, leading to unreliable choices.

## Method

- **AQC** adaptively selects the optimal ==action commitment length== from a discrete set at each state using a principled ==per-scale advantage criterion==: (Q_k(s, a) − V_k(s)) / γ^k.
- The method co-trains multiple neural networks including a ==long-horizon critic== (Q_h), a ==long-horizon value== (V_h) for bootstrapping, ==partial critics== (Q_k) for shorter horizons, and corresponding per-scale baselines (V_k).
- During inference, AQC samples candidate action chunks, ==z-score normalizes== their advantages across scales, and executes the best-scoring chunk open-loop in a receding-horizon manner.

## Results

- **AQC** achieved online success rates of **100%** on OGBench's cube-double task, **90%** on cube-triple, and **88%** on cube-quadruple, outperforming all other baselines including IQL, RLPD, FQL, QC, and DQC.
- On Robomimic tasks, AQC matched or surpassed previous chunking methods, demonstrating notable gains on contact-rich tasks like 'square' through adaptive control granularity.
- The approach enhanced a large-scale Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model (**GR00T N1.6**) on RoboCasa-GR1 tasks, achieving an average success rate of **63.2%** across 24 tasks, compared to **55.5%** for QC and **46.1%** for DQC.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A single, fixed action chunk size is inherently suboptimal for tasks with diverse phases, such as predictable free-space movement versus precise, reactive contact-rich manipulation.
> - A discount-normalized advantage criterion, which subtracts a learned per-horizon baseline, effectively overcomes the issues of discount-scale mismatch and noise amplification in low-value states, enabling robust adaptive chunk selection.
> - Using a long-horizon value function (V_h) as the bootstrap target for all partial critics significantly improves training stability and performance by providing consistent long-range value signals.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has demonstrated capabilities in learning complex behaviors, but initiating learning from scratch for intricate tasks can be impractical. Offline RL attempts to address this by learning policies from pre-collected datasets, but it faces challenges such as distributional shift and value overestimation. Offline-to-online RL combines these approaches, pre-training on a fixed dataset and then fine-tuning through online interaction. While this combined strategy has proven effective for many tasks, it encounters difficulties in long-horizon settings characterized by sparse rewards, resulting in slow learning and inconsistent exploration due to single-step policies.

Action chunking is a technique proposed to mitigate these issues by enabling the policy to commit to and execute sequences of actions rather than individual steps. This approach aims to stabilize value estimation, reduce multi-step off-policy bias, and facilitate more temporally coherent exploration. Prior work in action chunking for RL includes transformer-based episodic critics, coarse-to-fine discrete Q-networks, detached value learning for offline RL, and receding-horizon exploitation. Q-chunking (QC) is a directly relevant predecessor that learns Q-values over fixed-length chunks, eliminating n-step bias. Decoupled Q-chunking (DQC) extends this by allowing policy and critic to operate at different fixed chunk sizes, but it still lacks state-dependent adaptation and often requires goal-conditioned training.

This research addresses a specific limitation within the existing action chunking paradigm: the use of a fixed chunk size across all states. It argues that a single, static chunk size is suboptimal for tasks involving diverse phases, such as free-space movement and contact-rich manipulation. The proposed Adaptive Q-Chunking (AQC) method seeks to overcome this limitation by introducing a principled mechanism for dynamically adjusting the action commitment horizon based on the current state. This positions AQC as an advancement in the field of offline-to-online RL, specifically enhancing the flexibility and effectiveness of action chunking techniques.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an offline-to-online reinforcement learning method that can adaptively select the appropriate action chunk size at each state. This objective is motivated by a critical observation regarding existing action chunking methods, which uniformly employ a fixed chunk size throughout an agent's operation.

The authors identify this fixed chunk size approach as suboptimal due to the varying demands of different task phases within a single trajectory. Specifically:
- **Free-space motion:** During phases such as a robot arm moving towards an object, long action sequences are generally effective and safe. Such predictable movements benefit from longer open-loop execution, which also accelerates credit assignment by propagating value signals over more timesteps per update.
- **Contact-rich manipulation:** In contrast, near a contact event, such as grasping or insertion, executing a long, pre-determined action sequence can be detrimental. Small errors can accumulate and propagate irreversibly through contact dynamics, necessitating frequent reactions to new observations and, thus, a shorter chunk size for reactive control.

A globally fixed chunk size represents a compromise, unable to optimally serve both types of situations. A large chunk size may lead to degraded performance near contacts by neglecting critical state feedback, while a small chunk size may fail to leverage the credit-assignment benefits of multi-step backups during free-space movement and result in incoherent online exploration. The core motivation is therefore to enable the agent to dynamically adjust its commitment horizon, or chunk size, based on the task phase, thereby achieving both efficient long-range planning and precise reactive control as needed.

The authors detail two challenges that arise from a naive approach to adaptive chunk size selection, such as directly comparing the Q-values associated with different chunk sizes:
1. **Discount-scale mismatch:** In sparse-reward environments, intermediate rewards are typically near zero. The Bellman equation for a Q-value over `k` steps often simplifies to `Q_k ≈ γ^k * V(s_t+k)`. Since the discount factor `γ` is less than one, `γ^k` strictly decreases as `k` increases. This mathematical property causes critics for shorter chunks to systematically produce higher raw Q-values, leading to an artificial bias where the agent always selects the shortest available chunk, regardless of its actual utility for the task.
2. **State-dependent baseline mismatch and noise amplification:** Even if the discount-scale issue is addressed by normalizing Q-values by `γ^k`, a subtle problem persists in states far from rewards (low-value states). Here, the discounted values `Q_k/γ^k` for all chunk sizes are expected to be close to zero. The small differences between these values become dominated by function approximation errors inherent in the neural networks, causing the chunk selection to become essentially random noise rather than a meaningful choice based on planning quality.

To resolve these fundamental issues, the research proposes a novel adaptive selection criterion based on the advantage of each chunk size relative to a per-horizon baseline, normalized by the discount factor. This approach is intended to negate the artificial dominance of short chunks and ensure that in low-value states, choices are effectively randomized rather than systematically biased by noise. Additionally, the work aims to demonstrate the applicability of this adaptive chunking strategy to enhance large-scale Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, thereby extending its utility beyond standard RL agents.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Adaptive Q-Chunking (AQC) method is designed to select an optimal commitment length `k*` from a discrete set of candidate chunk sizes `K = {k_1..., h}` at each state `s_t`. The core of its approach lies in a principled per-scale advantage criterion.

**Per-Scale Advantage Criterion:**
The central selection rule is defined as:
`score(k, a_t:t+k) := (Q_k(s_t, a_t:t+k) - V_k(s_t)) / γ^k`
- `Q_k(s_t, a_t:t+k)`: This represents the learned Q-value for executing the specific `k`-step action chunk `a_t:t+k` starting from state `s_t`.
- `V_k(s_t)`: This is a learned state-value function that estimates the expected `k`-step return if a baseline behavior policy were followed from `s_t`. By subtracting `V_k(s_t)`, the criterion removes state-dependent baseline values, addressing the state-dependent baseline mismatch problem.
- `γ^k`: This is the discount factor raised to the power of the chunk length `k`. Dividing by `γ^k` normalizes the value across different horizons, counteracting the discount-scale mismatch that would otherwise favor shorter chunks.

The authors theoretically establish the **noise immunity** of this advantage selector (Proposition 5.1). In regions where the true value `V_h(s)` is small (approximated by `ϵ`), and assuming function approximation errors are bounded by `σ`, the absolute value of the discount-normalized advantage `δ_k(s)` is bounded by `ϵ + 2σ`. This implies that in low-value states where the signal `ϵ` is much smaller than the noise `σ`, the advantage criterion will produce near-zero scores, leading to effectively random choices rather than biased deterministic ones.

**Training Components:**
AQC's training pipeline involves co-training several neural networks:
1. **Long-Horizon Critic (Q_h):** A primary critic `Q_h_ϕ(s_t, a_t:t+h)` is trained for the maximum chunk size `h` using an EMAQ (Expected-Max Q-learning) `h`-step Temporal Difference (TD) loss (Equation 12). This leverages multiple candidate action chunks sampled from the behavior policy `π_β` to approximate the best-of-N bootstrap target.
2. **Long-Horizon Value (V_h):** A state-value function `V_h_ξ(s)` is learned via expectile regression on the `Q_h` values (Equation 13). With the expectile parameter `κ_V` set close to 1, `V_h` aims to approximate the upper envelope of `h`-step returns. Crucially, `V_h` serves two roles: as the baseline for `k=h`, and as the **bootstrap target** for all shorter-horizon partial critics.
3. **Partial Critics (Q_k for k ∈ K\{h\}):** For each `k` smaller than `h` in the set `K`, a separate partial critic `Q_k_ψ(s_t, a_t:t+k)` is trained. This critic takes only the `k`-step prefix of an action chunk as input. Its training utilizes a `k`-step TD loss (Equation 14), but instead of bootstrapping from its own `k`-step value function, it directly uses `γ^k * ¯V_h_ξ(s_t+k)` (the `h`-step value function from `k` steps in the future) as its bootstrap target. This design choice is intended to allow `Q_k` to focus on fitting the `k`-step residual, leveraging the pre-learned long-range value information in `V_h`, and reducing error accumulation compared to chaining 1-step backups.
4. **Per-Scale Baselines (V_k for k ∈ K\{h\}):** For each partial critic `Q_k`, a corresponding state-value baseline `V_k_ζ(s)` is trained via expectile regression (Equation 15). These networks share the same architecture as `V_h` and aim to estimate the expected `k`-step return under the behavior policy.
5. **Behavior Policy (π_β):** The behavior policy `π_β` is parameterized as a flow-matching model and trained using the flow-matching Behavior Cloning (BC) objective (Equation 5) on full `h`-step chunk distributions from the offline dataset. This expressive policy class is utilized to generate multimodal and temporally correlated action sequences.

**Inference Procedure:**
At each state `s_t` during online execution, the AQC agent performs the following steps (illustrated in Figure 2):
1. **Candidate Chunk Generation:** `N` full-length candidate action chunks are sampled from the learned flow-matching behavior policy `π_β`.
2. **Per-Scale Advantage Evaluation:** For each sampled chunk, its prefix of length `k` is used to evaluate the advantage score (Equation 10) for all `k ∈ K` using the respective `Q_k` and `V_k` critics.
3. **Z-score Normalization:** To prevent scales with inherently higher variance in their raw advantage scores from disproportionately influencing the selection, the scores for each `k` are z-score normalized across the `N` candidates (Equation 16).
4. **Optimal Chunk Selection:** The `(k*, a*)` pair (the selected chunk size and its corresponding action sequence) that yields the highest normalized advantage score is chosen.
5. **Open-Loop Execution:** The selected action sequence `a*` is executed open-loop for `k*` steps in the environment.
6. **Receding Horizon:** After `k*` steps, the agent reaches a new state `s_t+k*`, and the process repeats in a receding-horizon manner.
During online fine-tuning, newly collected transitions are added to a replay buffer, and all networks continue training using mixed batches of offline and online data.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical evaluation of Adaptive Q-Chunking (AQC) focused on three key questions: its performance relative to prior offline-to-online RL methods, its ability to enhance large-scale Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, and the contribution of its individual components.

#### 4.1. Comparison to Prior Offline-to-Online RL Methods

- **OGBench Benchmark:** AQC demonstrated high online success rates on various domains from OGBench, particularly excelling on the more challenging `cube-double`, `cube-triple`, and `cube-quadruple` tasks. In these complex, long-horizon scenarios, AQC achieved online success rates of 100%, 90%, and 88% respectively, outperforming all other baselines including IQL, RLPD, FQL, FQL-n, QC-FQL, QC, and DQC. On simpler domains like `scene-sparse` and `puzzle-3x3`, AQC achieved 100% online success, matching other robust chunking methods. The performance gap between AQC and its predecessors (QC, DQC) widened with increasing task horizon complexity.
- **Robomimic Benchmark:** On Robomimic tasks (`lift`, `can`, `square`), AQC matched or surpassed the performance of QC. The most significant gains were observed on the `square` task, which involves a contact-rich peg-insertion. This result supports the core motivation of AQC, as it adaptively utilized longer chunks during predictable free-space reaching phases and switched to shorter chunks for reactive control during the contact-sensitive insertion phase. For `lift` and `can`, which are less contact-intensive, AQC still showed non-negative, though smaller, advantages.

#### 4.2. Enhancement of Large-Scale VLAs with Offline RL

- **RoboCasa-GR1 Benchmark:** AQC was applied to fine-tune the GR00T N1.6 VLA model on 24 RoboCasa-GR1 tabletop manipulation tasks, which are characterized by high-dimensional state-action spaces and contact-rich interactions. AQC consistently outperformed the base VLA model, Filtered BC, QC, and DQC across all 24 tasks (with an average success rate of 63.2% compared to QC's 55.5% and DQC's 46.1%). The largest performance margins were observed on tasks involving multi-stage contact transitions, such as opening cabinets and manipulating their contents. This demonstrates AQC's scalability and efficacy in enhancing large, pre-trained multi-task VLA policies, even in settings with limited online data.

#### 4.3. Analysis of AQC Components (Ablation Studies)

Ablation studies conducted on OGBench tasks (`cube-double`, `cube-triple`) provided insights into the contributions of AQC's key design elements:
- **Advantage Criterion:**
 - Direct comparison of raw `Q_k` values (labelled "Naive Raw") led to a collapse where the agent invariably selected the shortest chunk (`k=1`), confirming the discount-scale mismatch problem.
 - Discount-corrected `Q_k/γ^k` without baseline subtraction ("Naive Discount-Corrected") showed some recovery on easier tasks but degraded significantly on longer-horizon tasks, indicating susceptibility to function approximation noise.
 - AQC's principled advantage criterion, which includes both baseline subtraction and discount normalization, exhibited robustness across tasks.
- **Multi-Scale Critics and Adaptive Selection:**
 - Training multiple per-scale critics (`Q_k`) provided performance improvements even when the policy executed a fixed chunk size, suggesting that a richer value representation is beneficial.
 - Adaptive `k*` selection, built on top of multi-scale critic training, yielded further performance gains, particularly evident in longer-horizon tasks where the need to switch between different control granularities is more pronounced.
- **Bootstrap Target (Appendix G):** Using the long-horizon `V_h` as the bootstrap target for all partial critics `Q_k` was critical. This approach significantly tightened sub-optimality bounds and demonstrated better performance compared to bootstrapping from a 1-step value function (`V_1`), especially in longer-horizon tasks where `V_1` underestimates long-horizon returns.
- **Expectile Parameter (κ_V) Sensitivity (Appendix G):** The choice of `κ_V=0.9` for expectile regression (approximating the upper envelope of returns) was found to be optimal. Values too low (`0.5`) led to underestimation, while excessively high values (`0.99`) resulted in unstable Q-value estimates.
- **Z-score Normalization (Appendix G):** The z-score normalization of advantage scores within each scale during inference was demonstrated to be essential. Its removal led to significant performance degradation (e.g., 9% on `cube-double`, 45% on `cube-triple`), indicating that it successfully prevents scales with higher variance from artificially dominating the selection process.
- **Robustness of Candidate Set K (Appendix G):** The study found that adding intermediate chunk sizes to the predefined set `K = {1, 5, 10, 25}` provided no additional performance benefits, suggesting that the advantage criterion effectively interpolates between the provided extreme timescales.

Overall, the results indicate that AQC consistently outperforms previous methods on challenging offline-to-online RL benchmarks and scales effectively to large VLA models, primarily due to its principled adaptive chunk selection mechanism and robust multi-scale critic training.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Adaptive Q-Chunking (AQC) method contributes to the field of reinforcement learning by addressing a fundamental limitation in action chunking: the use of fixed commitment horizons. Its significance and potential impact stem from several aspects:

1. **Resolution of Core Problems:** AQC provides a solution to the previously identified issues of discount-scale mismatch and noise amplification that plague naive multi-chunk selection strategies. By introducing a discount-normalized advantage criterion and incorporating per-horizon baselines, AQC transforms biased and noisy selection processes into discriminative choices when a genuine signal is present, and near-random choices when no clear advantage exists. This contributes to more stable and reliable learning.

2. **State-of-the-Art Performance:** The empirical results on challenging long-horizon, sparse-reward tasks across OGBench, Robomimic, and RoboCasa-GR1 benchmarks demonstrate AQC's ability to achieve or exceed state-of-the-art success rates. This suggests that dynamic adaptation of chunk sizes is a crucial factor for performance in complex manipulation tasks that involve distinct phases requiring varying degrees of reactive control versus long-term planning.

3. **Enhanced VLA Models:** The successful application of AQC to fine-tune the GR00T N1.6 VLA model on RoboCasa-GR1 tasks highlights its practical utility beyond traditional RL agents. It demonstrates that AQC can effectively scale to large, high-dimensional policies and observations, providing a mechanism to imbue powerful pre-trained models with adaptive reactive capabilities without modifying their underlying architecture. This opens avenues for improving the flexibility and robustness of generalist robot policies.

4. **Theoretical Foundations:** The research provides theoretical analyses that formalize the conditions under which the advantage selector is sound, proving bounds on its noise immunity and establishing the value dominance of adaptive chunking over any fixed-chunk policy. These theoretical guarantees reinforce the principled nature of AQC's design.

5. **Broader Principle for RL:** Beyond its immediate results, AQC proposes a broader methodological principle for comparing and selecting among multiple learned value functions. It suggests that when faced with various value estimates (e.g., for different horizons, abstractions, or representation levels), comparing their raw values is insufficient. An advantage-based criterion, normalized and referenced to a per-scale baseline, offers a robust framework for converting these potentially incomparable quantities into a coherent decision space. This perspective could inspire future research in multi-scale decision-making and adaptive temporal abstraction across various RL applications.

6. **Practical Implications for Robotics:** For robotic manipulation, AQC's ability to seamlessly switch between long-horizon, smooth motion and short-horizon, reactive control offers practical benefits. It reduces the need for extensive manual tuning of chunk sizes for different tasks or task phases, potentially leading to more autonomous and versatile robotic systems that can adapt their planning granularity in real-time.
