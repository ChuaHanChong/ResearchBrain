# Quadrotor Navigation using Reinforcement Learning with Privileged Information

> [!summary] Summary
> This research from Carnegie Mellon University introduces a reinforcement learning framework for quadrotor navigation that combines time-of-arrival maps as privileged training information with a novel yaw alignment loss, enabling end-to-end policies to achieve 86% success in complex simulated environments and robustly transfer to physical hardware for collision-free flight up to 4 m/s in outdoor cluttered settings.

## Problem

- End-to-end learning-based quadrotor navigation methods often struggle in environments with large obstacles, sharp corners, and dead ends.
- Existing approaches either rely heavily on expert demonstrations or lack the capability to infer globally optimal paths that require significant orientation changes.
- Local obstacle avoidance mechanisms can lead to robots getting trapped in concave regions, unable to make continuous progress towards a goal.

## Method

- A ==reinforcement learning== framework is developed using differentiable simulation, incorporating a novel yaw alignment loss and time-of-arrival (ToA) maps as privileged information during training.
- The policy, based on a GRU network, predicts a mass-normalized thrust vector and a desired yaw angle from depth images and robot state, guided by a composite loss function including terms for obstacle avoidance, smoothness, and goal tracking.
- Sim-to-real transfer is enhanced through an improved body rate attitude controller and comprehensive domain randomization, notably randomizing gravity to promote adaptive thrust control.

## Results

- The proposed method achieved an **86%** success rate in 11 diverse simulated photo-realistic environments, representing a **36%** improvement over baseline strategies, with the lowest collision rate.
- Hardware experiments demonstrated 20 collision-free flights covering 589 meters in cluttered outdoor environments, including dense foliage and nighttime conditions, at speeds up to 4 m/s.
- Ablation studies confirmed that the desired body rate term in the controller reduced latency to negligible levels, and gravity randomization during training enabled stable hovering on real hardware by adapting thrust to compensate for a **15%** real-world thrust mismatch.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating time-of-arrival maps as privileged information during training effectively imbues reactive policies with global awareness, allowing them to learn to navigate around large, concave obstacles without requiring these maps at test time.
> - A dedicated yaw alignment loss is crucial for enabling quadrotors to reorient dynamically, which significantly improves navigation performance in environments necessitating turns and maneuvering around large obstructions.
> - Comprehensive domain randomization, especially of gravity, during simulation training fosters robust, adaptive feedback control policies capable of compensating for real-world modeling inaccuracies and transferring effectively to physical hardware.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Autonomous quadrotor navigation is a field that has seen advancements through both traditional and learning-based approaches. Traditional methods typically decompose the navigation problem into distinct tasks such as perception, planning, state estimation, and control. In contrast, end-to-end learning-based methods utilize neural networks to directly map raw sensor observations to control actions. These end-to-end approaches offer advantages such as reduced planning latency and lower computational overhead, making them suitable for deployment on resource-constrained platforms.

Prior learning-based methods, such as those by Loquercio et al. and Kim et al., often rely on extensive expert-labeled data. While some, like Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL) methods, can enable out-of-distribution generalization, they may lack temporal awareness, potentially leading to collisions when navigating around large obstacles. Other approaches leverage differentiable simulators, as demonstrated by Zhang et al., to train recurrent neural network control policies with lightweight dynamics models. While effective for high-speed operation in various environments, these methods typically maintain a fixed heading towards the target, limiting their ability to navigate around large obstacles or in scenarios requiring significant yaw motion. Consequently, they may struggle in complex environments characterized by large walls, sharp corners, or dead ends.

Some recent learning-based navigation methods incorporate geometric maps as privileged information during training, such as the use of Euclidean Signed Distance Fields (ESDFs) by Lu et al.. However, ESDFs primarily encode obstacle distance, providing local avoidance guidance but limited direction for globally optimal paths, and may not be sufficient for escaping large convex obstacle regions.

This work positions itself within this landscape by extending end-to-end learning methods, specifically building upon the approach of Zhang et al.. It addresses the limitations of prior art by introducing mechanisms for navigating around large obstacles and enabling the quadrotor to reorient itself, thereby enhancing navigation capabilities in challenging, complex environments. The key differentiating factor is the incorporation of time-of-arrival (ToA) maps as privileged information during training to imbue the policy with global awareness, without requiring these maps at test time. Additionally, a novel yaw alignment loss is introduced to facilitate necessary orientation changes.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the observation that while end-to-end learning-based quadrotor navigation methods offer benefits in terms of latency and computational efficiency, they frequently encounter difficulties in environments featuring large obstacles, sharp corners, and dead ends. Existing approaches either rely heavily on expert demonstrations or lack the capability to infer optimal paths that require significant changes in orientation or global awareness. Specifically, methods that maintain a fixed heading towards the target struggle to maneuver around large obstructions, leading to reduced success rates or collisions. Furthermore, local obstacle avoidance mechanisms may cause the robot to become trapped in concave regions, unable to make progress towards the goal.

To address these limitations, the paper outlines several key objectives:

1. **Develop an objective function for heading prediction:** The aim is to introduce a loss function that specifically supervises the prediction of the quadrotor's yaw angle. This is intended to improve navigation performance in environments that necessitate orientation changes, such as twisting passageways and sharp corners, thereby overcoming the fixed-heading limitation of previous methods.
2. **Integrate time-of-arrival (ToA) maps as privileged information:** A core objective is to leverage ToA maps during the training phase. These maps provide shortest-path guidance, enabling the policy to learn globally-aware navigation behavior. Crucially, this information is privileged, meaning it is available only during training and not required at inference time, allowing the deployed policy to rely solely on reactive depth observations and state estimates.
3. **Bridge the simulation-to-real-world gap:** The research aims to ensure that policies trained in simulation can be effectively deployed on physical quadrotors. This involves developing approaches such as body rate attitude control and employing domain randomization techniques during training to enhance robustness to real-world complexities and modeling inaccuracies.
4. **Extensive evaluation and open-source contribution:** The final objective is to rigorously evaluate the proposed system in photo-realistic simulation environments and through hardware experiments. This includes validating the policy's performance in diverse, cluttered outdoor settings. Additionally, the researchers committed to releasing the software as open-source to facilitate further research and development in the field.

Collectively, these objectives seek to enable reactive, end-to-end learning policies to achieve robust, globally-aware navigation around large obstacles in complex environments, making them more capable for practical autonomous flight applications.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method for quadrotor navigation utilizes a reinforcement learning framework trained with differentiable simulation, novel loss functions, and privileged information.

#### 3.1. A. Differentiable Dynamics

The navigation problem is modeled as a Markov Decision Process within a discrete-time dynamical system. The core of the training approach involves leveraging differentiable dynamics, which enables the use of Analytical Policy Gradient (APG) methods for direct policy optimization by backpropagating loss gradients through the system dynamics.
A lightweight point-mass dynamics model is employed for training efficiency, rather than a full quadrotor rigid-body model. This model tracks position ($p_k$), velocity ($v_k$), and acceleration ($a_k$) using a velocity Verlet integration scheme (Equations 1-3). The orientation of the point mass is determined by the simulator, with the body z-axis aligned with the predicted thrust vector ($t_k$) and the x-axis aligned with the predicted yaw angle ($\psi_k$).

#### 3.2. B. Network Architecture

The policy is implemented as a single neural network (Figure 3). Its inputs include a depth image, a target velocity, a goal importance value, and the robot’s current state (velocity and orientation).
Each input type is processed by a dedicated feature extractor: a convolutional network for depth images and linear layers for the other vectorial inputs. The outputs of these extractors are projected into 192-dimensional vectors, layer-normalized, and then summed. This combined feature vector serves as the input to a Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) cell, which also integrates the previous hidden state to maintain temporal context. The GRU's output, a latent representation encoding current and past observations, is then passed through a linear layer to predict the mass-normalized thrust vector (3D) and a desired yaw angle (1D). This architecture differs from previous work by incorporating a separate target extractor with a goal importance value, using normalization layers, and predicting a yaw angle in addition to thrust.

#### 3.3. C. Loss Functions

The policy is trained using a composite loss function (Equation 14) that combines several terms to encourage safe, smooth, and goal-directed navigation. Some terms are restated from prior work, while others are novel contributions.

1. **Obstacle Avoidance Loss:**
- `L_clearance` (Equation 4): A softplus penalty on the distance to the nearest obstacle, encouraging the robot to maintain a minimum safe clearance ($r$).
- `L_collision` (Equation 5): Penalizes the component of the robot's velocity directed towards the closest obstacle, encouraging velocity reduction when approaching obstructions.
2. **Smoothness Loss:**
- `L_acc` (Equation 6): Penalizes the ℓ2-norm of linear acceleration, promoting stability.
- `L_jerk` (Equation 7): Penalizes the ℓ2-norm of jerk (rate of change of acceleration), promoting smooth trajectories.
- `L_omega` (Equation 8): A novel term that penalizes the ℓ2-norm of angular velocity, computed via the Euler angle Jacobian (Equation 15), to reduce abrupt yaw changes.
3. **Target Velocity Loss:**
- `L_v` (Equation 9): Utilizes a Smooth L1 loss to encourage the policy to track a desired target velocity ($v_{set_k}$). A 2-second moving average of the actual velocity is used to smooth short-term deviations and align with the overall objective.
- `L_vmax` (Equation 10): A novel penalty term that penalizes speeds exceeding a defined maximum target speed ($v_{max}$), helping to bound predicted thrusts and prevent policy divergence.
4. **Yaw Alignment Loss (`L_yaw`, Equation 11):** This is a novel contribution defined as the negative inner product between the body x-axis ($x_{B_k}$) and an exponentially weighted moving average of the velocity ($\tilde{v_k}$). This loss encourages the quadrotor to reorient its body towards its desired direction of motion, enabling navigation around large obstacles that require significant turns.
5. **Privileged Information via Time-of-Arrival (ToA) Map:** To address the limitation of local collision losses in guiding the robot around large, concave obstacles, the method incorporates ToA maps as privileged information during training.
- A ToA map $T(x)$ defines the minimum travel time from any free-space point $x$ to the goal. It is computed as a solution to the Eikonal equation $|∇T(x)|F(x) = 1$.
- To make paths safer and bias them away from obstacle surfaces, a piecewise continuous cost function $F(x)$ (Equations 12, 13) is introduced. This function reduces wavefront speed near obstacles (within a $d_{safe}$ threshold), effectively making paths closer to obstacles appear longer.
- ToA maps are pre-computed offline using the Fast Marching Method (FMM) for each training environment. The negative gradient $∇T(x)$ then defines the velocity set-point direction ($v_{set_k}$) used in the target velocity loss ($L_v$), guiding the robot towards the goal while inherently avoiding obstacles. This map is not available at deployment.

#### 3.4. D. Training

The policy is trained exclusively in a customized GPU-accelerated simulator, VisFly, which integrates Habitat-Sim for rendering and collision detection. Training environments contain randomly generated primitive obstacles (spheres, cylinders, cuboids), arranged in a cylindrical shape to facilitate concurrent training of multiple agents. ToA maps are pre-generated for these environments. Training employs backpropagation through time (BPTT) and temporal gradient decay, with a batch size of 16 over 10,000 iterations. An initial phase of 500 iterations is performed in an empty environment without collision loss to stabilize gradients before full environment training.

#### 3.5. E. Body Rate Attitude Control

To mitigate the sim-to-real gap, a PD attitude controller is used on the real quadrotor. This controller tracks desired thrust (F), orientation ($R_d$), and body rates ($\omega_d$). The desired orientation $R_d$ aligns the body z-axis with the desired thrust and the body x-axis with the predicted yaw direction ($\psi_d$). The desired body rates $\omega_d$ are computed from estimated ZYX Euler angle rates using the Euler angle Jacobian (Equation 15). The inclusion of this derivative feedback term $\omega_d$ is highlighted as a significant improvement over proportional-only controllers, substantially reducing control response latency.

#### 3.6. F. Domain Randomization

Domain randomization is applied during training (Table II) to enhance the policy's robustness and transferability to real hardware. Key parameters randomized include gravity ($g$), initial position and height, and target speed. Noise is also injected into state inputs to simulate sensor imperfections. The policy outputs mass-normalized thrust, and randomizing gravity, in particular, encourages the policy to learn a closed-loop feedback mechanism to adapt its thrust based on velocity feedback, compensating for potential inaccuracies in thrust-to-RPM mappings or other modeling errors in the real system.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents findings from both simulation and hardware experiments, evaluating the performance of the proposed navigation system.

#### 4.1. A. Simulation Experiments

1. **Attitude Control Performance:** An ablation study was conducted to compare the attitude control performance with and without the desired body rate term ($\omega_d$). The controller incorporating $\omega_d$ exhibited negligible control response latency, achieving desired orientations quickly. In contrast, a controller without this derivative feedback term showed a latency of approximately 200 ms (Figure 6). This reduction in latency is presented as beneficial for evasive maneuvers in dynamic environments.
2. **Diverse Simulated Environments:** The trained policy was evaluated in 11 out-of-distribution photo-realistic scenarios from (Flightmare), using full rigid-body quadrotor dynamics despite being trained with point-mass dynamics. 1,350 trials were conducted.
- The proposed method achieved an 86% success rate, which represents a 36% improvement over the baseline strategies. It also demonstrated the lowest collision rate among the tested approaches (Figure 7).
- **Comparison to Baselines:**
- The **BNL (Back to Newton’s Laws)** baseline struggled with large, flat obstacles due to its fixed heading towards the target (Figure 8a,d), resulting in a lower success rate.
- An ablated version, **"Ours (Yaw w/o ToA),"** which included the yaw alignment loss but not the ToA privileged information, showed improved success rates over BNL in environments requiring significant reorientation (e.g., Industry 2). This indicates that yaw alignment aids in maneuvering around large obstacles. However, without the global guidance from ToA, this policy frequently timed out in concave regions, suggesting it could reorient but lacked direction for continuous progress (Figure 8b).
- The **full proposed model ("Ours," i.e., Yaw with ToA)** combined the benefits of yaw reorientation and global navigation cues derived from the ToA loss, yielding the highest success rate across environments (Figure 8c,f).
- All planners exhibited poor performance in the maze-like "Mine" environment.

#### 4.2. B. Hardware Experiments

A custom quadrotor platform, equipped with an Intel RealSense D456 depth camera, Lightware SF20/C range finder, Matrix Vision BlueFox2 camera, and an NVIDIA Orin NX module, was used for hardware validation. State estimation was provided by a monocular visual-inertial navigation system.

1. **Domain Randomization Experiments:** An ablation study on hardware investigated the effect of gravity randomization during training.
- A policy trained **without gravity randomization** failed to maintain altitude during a hover task, initially outputting approximately 1g of thrust but then losing altitude (Figure 9a).
- A policy trained **with gravity randomization** successfully achieved stable hover at the goal setpoint, demonstrating an adaptive behavior where it initially increased thrust to approximately 1.3g to compensate for an estimated 15% thrust mismatch due to real-world factors like battery voltage drop and airflow disturbances (Figure 9b). These results support the hypothesis that domain randomization, specifically over gravity, induces robust feedback control that compensates for modeling inaccuracies.
2. **Outdoor Navigation Experiments:** The policies were deployed in an outdoor flight arena and under a stand of trees with dense foliage and underbrush (Figure 10, 11).
- A total of 20 hardware flights were conducted, covering a cumulative path length of 589 meters.
- The quadrotor achieved speeds up to 4 m/s.
- No collisions were reported during these tests (Table III).
- The policy demonstrated the ability to predict yaw changes of up to 30 degrees to navigate through dense underbrush. These experiments were conducted both during the day and at night using LED illumination.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of autonomous quadrotor navigation by presenting an end-to-end reinforcement learning framework that addresses limitations of prior methods in complex, cluttered environments. The integration of time-of-arrival (ToA) maps as privileged information during training is a notable aspect, enabling the policy to acquire globally-aware navigation capabilities without requiring explicit maps during deployment. This approach allows the reactive policy, relying solely on depth observations and state estimates, to infer optimal paths around large obstacles and through challenging terrain. The introduction of a yaw alignment loss further enhances maneuverability, allowing the quadrotor to reorient itself as needed, which is critical for navigating twisting passages and sharp corners.

The systematic bridging of the sim-to-real gap, through methods such as an enhanced body rate attitude controller and comprehensive domain randomization (particularly gravity randomization), is significant. The hardware experiments demonstrate that policies trained entirely in simulation can transfer effectively to real quadrotors, compensating for inherent modeling inaccuracies and platform discrepancies. This robust transferability broadens the practical applicability of learning-based control policies.

The quantitative results, including an 86% success rate in diverse simulated environments and 20 collision-free hardware flights covering a substantial distance at speeds up to 4 m/s in cluttered outdoor settings, underscore the method's efficacy and robustness. The ability to operate in challenging conditions, including dense foliage and at night, indicates a level of resilience that can be valuable for various applications.

The potential impact of this work includes enabling the deployment of more capable, agile, and efficient autonomous quadrotors for tasks such as inspection, surveillance, search and rescue, and reconnaissance in complex, GPS-denied, or unstructured environments. The framework could facilitate the development of lightweight, low-cost autonomous platforms that can navigate environments where traditional, map-dependent approaches are impractical or computationally intensive. The open-source release of the software may also accelerate further research and development in vision-based navigation for robotics.

Future research directions suggested by the authors include exploring more expressive learned-memory architectures to improve spatial reasoning and long-horizon planning, potentially addressing current limitations in maze-like environments and reducing initial yaw oscillations. Additionally, incorporating task-specific inputs and new objective functions could extend the method's utility to a wider range of specialized applications.
