# Active Noise Floor Estimation for Reliability-Optimal POMDPs: A Value-of-Noise-Information Approach

> [!summary] Summary
> A framework called Value of Noise Information (VoNI) is introduced for managing uncertainty in physical noise parameters within Partially Observable Markov Decision Processes (POMDPs). This approach enables autonomous systems to actively disambiguate latent sensing and execution noise, leading to more efficient diagnostic probing and improved reliability certificate maintenance compared to entropy-based methods.

## Problem

- Existing theoretical frameworks for reliable decision-making in autonomous systems, such as the Finite Reliability Representation (FRR), assume known and fixed physical noise parameters (e.g., sensor and actuator noise).
- In real-world scenarios, the effective physical noise floor is rarely static or perfectly known, often being latent and dynamically varying with operational context.
- Traditional active perception methods focus on generic uncertainty reduction (e.g., posterior entropy) rather than specifically on uncertainty that threatens a system's reliability certificate, potentially leading to inefficient or unnecessary diagnostic probing.

## Method

- The paper defines the **Value of Noise Information (VoNI)** as a decision criterion, quantifying the expected excess ==FRR certificate gap== incurred when a policy is calibrated to an estimated noise parameter rather than the true one.
- A ==bi-level decision maker== is proposed: a lower level executes a ==Finite Reliability Representation (FRR)== policy based on the current best noise estimate, while an upper level uses **VoNI** to trigger ==diagnostic probing== actions only when uncertainty in noise parameters threatens the FRR certificate.
- An ==analytical upper bound== for VoNI is derived, and VoNI is interpreted as a tractable, certificate-aware approximation to a higher-level finite ==POMDP== for disambiguating latent noise conditions.

## Results

- In a simulated abrupt sensing-noise jump, the VoNI policy detected the change an average of **69% faster** while using **85% fewer** diagnostic probing actions compared to an entropy-based policy.
- For gradual sensing-noise drift, VoNI achieved a **41% lower** mean absolute error in tracking the drifting noise floor and utilized **59% fewer** probing actions.
- The VoNI-guided approach consistently demonstrated earlier detection of significant noise changes and more accurate tracking of gradual noise drift, with substantially fewer task-perturbing diagnostic actions.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The value of information regarding physical noise parameters is directly tied to its impact on the system's reliability certificate, not just generic statistical uncertainty.
> - The reliability certificate gap is most sensitive to noise uncertainty when the true noise condition is degraded but the system's policy is still calibrated to nominal conditions.
> - Guiding diagnostic probing with a certificate-aware metric like VoNI allows for significantly earlier detection of critical noise changes and more efficient resource utilization by reducing unnecessary probing.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated at the intersection of decision-making under uncertainty, model adaptation, and certification in autonomous systems. It addresses a specific challenge within the domain of Partially Observable Markov Decision Processes (POMDPs), which provide a formal framework for sequential decision-making when the system state is hidden.

The paper builds upon the established theory of POMDPs and belief-space planning, which typically assumes a known system model. However, exact POMDP planning is often computationally intractable for complex or continuous domains, leading to extensive research in approximation methods (e.g., value-function approximation, point-based value iteration). This work does not propose a new POMDP approximation method itself but rather focuses on a different aspect: model uncertainty.

A more direct lineage traces to Bayes-adaptive POMDPs, which augment the state with a posterior distribution over unknown model parameters. While principled, these formulations often lead to significantly higher computational complexity. This paper distinguishes itself by focusing on a specific type of model uncertainty – the physical noise floor – and by providing a certificate-aware approximation rather than a full Bayes-adaptive solution.

Furthermore, the research relates to adaptive noise and covariance estimation, which are statistical methods (ee.g., adaptive Kalman filtering) used to infer noise parameters from data (e.g., innovation sequences). The paper explicitly states that it does not develop a new estimator but rather assumes the availability of a posterior over noise parameters. It differentiates its contribution by addressing a *decision-theoretic* question: *when* to reduce uncertainty in noise parameters, rather than *how* to estimate them.

The work also connects with active perception and information-gain exploration, where actions are chosen to reduce uncertainty or improve observations. Unlike generic information-theoretic criteria (e.g., entropy reduction, mutual information), this paper proposes a criterion, Value of Noise Information (VoNI), that is explicitly tied to the impact of uncertainty on a reliability certificate, rather than on information gain alone.

Finally, the most direct predecessor is the Finite Reliability Representation (FRR) framework. FRR establishes a connection between belief-space resolution, physical noise floors, and action-value variation, providing a policy-sufficiency theorem for systems with a *known* physical noise floor. This paper extends FRR by removing the assumption of a known noise parameter, thereby providing a mechanism for active disambiguation of latent noise conditions within the FRR framework. It integrates these various concepts into a unified framework for reliability-optimal control under dynamic noise conditions.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from a practical limitation of existing theoretical frameworks for reliable decision-making in autonomous systems. While partially observed autonomous systems inherently deal with uncertainty about their current state (represented by a belief), they also face a second, often unaddressed, layer of uncertainty: the physical channels through which state information is acquired and commands are executed. This includes sensing uncertainty (e.g., due to varying illumination, reflectivity, occlusion) and execution uncertainty (e.g., due to terrain, actuator degradation, wheel-ground interaction).

The Finite Reliability Representation (FRR) framework, a foundation for this work, provides a certificate for reliable decision-making in partially observed systems. It states that if a belief space is covered by "reliability cells" where optimal action-value variation is bounded, a simple cell-constant policy can achieve a quantifiable level of suboptimality. Crucially, FRR, in its original form, assumes that the physical noise floor parameters (e.g., sensor noise standard deviation, actuator noise covariance) are **known** and fixed.

The key objectives and motivations of this paper are therefore to address this gap by:

- **Acknowledging Latent and Context-Dependent Noise:** Recognizing that in real-world scenarios, the effective physical noise floor is rarely static or perfectly known. It is often latent and dynamically varies with operational context.
- **Developing a Certificate-Aware Active Disambiguation Framework:** The central objective is to create a framework that can actively manage and disambiguate an unknown physical noise parameter, represented as `θ = (σ_y, σ_u)` (sensing noise, execution noise). This framework is designed to be "certificate-aware," meaning its actions are driven by the impact of noise uncertainty on the FRR policy certificate, not just by generic statistical uncertainty.
- **Shifting Focus from "How to Estimate" to "When to Reduce Uncertainty":** The paper explicitly states that its aim is not to simply develop another noise estimator. Instead, it asks a decision-theoretic question: "When does reducing posterior uncertainty in `θ` improve the certified reliability representation enough to justify additional information-gathering cost?" This is a key departure from traditional adaptive filtering or active perception methods.
- **Defining the Value of Noise Information (VoNI):** To quantify this decision-theoretic value, the paper introduces VoNI. This metric is defined as the expected excess FRR certificate gap incurred when the reliability cover, which dictates the policy, is calibrated to the *current estimate* of `θ` rather than to the *realized physical noise parameter*. VoNI serves as the core motivation for active probing: information about `θ` is valuable only when posterior uncertainty can invalidate the current reliability cover.
- **Guiding Bi-Level Decision-Making:** The framework motivates a bi-level decision maker. A lower level executes the task using an FRR policy calibrated to the *current best estimate* of `θ`. An upper level, informed by VoNI, decides *when* to trigger diagnostic probing actions (which might perturb the task trajectory, consume resources, or increase risk) to refine the estimate of `θ`. Probing is triggered only if uncertainty in `θ` threatens the FRR certificate.
- **Providing a Tractable Approximation:** The research aims to interpret VoNI as a tractable, certificate-aware approximation to a more complex, high-level finite POMDP that would fully disambiguate latent sensing-execution regimes. This avoids the computational burden of a full Bayes-adaptive POMDP solution.

In essence, the motivation is to bridge the gap between theoretical guarantees for reliable control (FRR) and the practical realities of dynamic, unknown physical noise, by providing a principled, decision-theoretic mechanism to manage model uncertainty in a way that directly supports the system's reliability certificate.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology developed in this paper extends the Finite Reliability Representation (FRR) framework to systems with latent and context-dependent physical noise parameters. The core approach is to define and utilize the Value of Noise Information (VoNI) as a decision criterion for active disambiguation.

**System Model and Assumptions:**
The system is modeled as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP), where the physical noise parameter `θ = (σ_y, σ_u)` (sensing noise `σ_y`, action-execution noise `σ_u`) is considered latent. The system dynamics, observations, and reward function are dependent on this `θ`.
Three key assumptions underpin the approach:
1. **Slow Variation and Diagnostic Excitation (Assumption 1):** The physical noise parameter `θ` is assumed to be constant, piecewise constant, or slowly varying. Crucially, it assumes that diagnostic data (e.g., observation innovations, execution residuals) provide sufficient information to identify `θ` when informative actions are taken, satisfying a persistent-excitation condition.
2. **Posterior Interface and Calibration (Assumption 2):** The decision maker has access to a posterior distribution `q_t(θ)` over the noise parameter, generated from diagnostic data. This posterior is assumed to be well-specified, assign positive prior mass to the true parameter, and be locally identifiable. VoNI acts as a decision layer on top of this posterior, not as a noise estimator itself.
3. **Uniform FRR Regularity (Assumption 3):** For all plausible `θ` values within the posterior support, the induced fixed-parameter POMDPs are assumed to satisfy the FRR regularity conditions, particularly that the Lipschitz constant `L_V(θ)` of the optimal action-value function is finite and uniformly bounded. This ensures that FRR certificates are comparable across different `θ` values.

**Value of Noise Information (VoNI) Definition:**
The paper defines VoNI to quantify the certificate-relevant value of reducing uncertainty in `θ`. It is not concerned with the total suboptimality, but specifically the *excess* suboptimality beyond the baseline FRR approximation floor (`2ε / (1 - γ)`).
- `G_ε(b, θ̂; θ)`: This is the "excess FRR certificate gap," defined as `max(V*θ(b) - V_(π̂_θ̂,ε)^θ(b) - 2ε / (1 - γ), 0)`. It measures the additional loss incurred when the policy is based on an estimated `θ̂` while the true noise is `θ`, after accounting for the inherent FRR approximation error.
- `VoNI_ε(b, θ̂; q)`: This is the expectation of `G_ε(b, θ̂; θ)` over the current posterior `q(θ)`. If `q` concentrates on `θ̂`, VoNI is zero, as the policy is correctly calibrated.

**Analytical Bound for VoNI (Theorem 1):**
A key contribution is an analytical upper bound for VoNI, which helps in its computation and interpretation. The bound decomposes VoNI into two mismatch terms:
1. `∆_Q(θ, θ̂)`: Represents the uniform action-value model mismatch, measuring the difference between optimal action-value functions under `θ` and `θ̂`.
2. `ρ(θ, θ̂)`: The FRR radius inflation factor, defined as `L_V(θ) / L_V(θ̂)`. If `ρ > 1`, the reliability cover calibrated at `θ̂` is "under-resolved" for the true noise `θ`, leading to a larger certificate gap.
The bound shows that VoNI is small when the FRR certificate is insensitive to `θ` (sub-crossover regimes) but becomes significant when `θ` uncertainty can invalidate the current reliability cover.

**Exploration Threshold (Corollary 1):**
This corollary specializes the VoNI bound to "sub-crossover" credible regions where `θ` variations induce only slow changes in the value model and FRR sensitivity. In such regimes, VoNI is directly proportional to the expected posterior uncertainty (`E[||θ - θ̂||]`), implying that active probing has low decision value.

**VoNI as a Myopic High-Level POMDP Approximation:**
The paper interprets VoNI as a tractable, one-step approximation to a meta-POMDP for disambiguating the true noise regime `θ`. The low-level planner executes the FRR policy, while a high-level decision maker uses `VoNI_ε(b, θ̂; q)` as the certificate-aware regret for an "exploit" action. It defines a "myopic VoNI reduction proxy" (`I^H_ε`) that compares the immediate VoNI with the expected VoNI after a diagnostic action (minus its cost). Probing is deemed valuable if `I^H_ε` is positive.

**Bi-Level Decision Maker (Algorithm 1):**
The overall architecture is a bi-level decision maker:
- **Outer Level:** Maintains or receives `q_t(θ)` from diagnostic data. It calculates VoNI (or its bound) and the `I^H_ε` for potential diagnostic actions.
- **Inner Level:** Given the current estimate `θ̂_t`, it executes the FRR cell-constant policy `π̂_(θ̂_t,ε)`. The FRR cover is recomputed only if `L_V(θ̂_t)` changes significantly, reflecting a slower timescale for noise-parameter adaptation compared to state belief updates.
- **High-Level Action Rule:** A diagnostic probe (`a^H_t,probe`) is chosen if it maximizes `I^H_ε` and this maximum is positive; otherwise, the "exploit" action (executing the current FRR policy) is selected.

**Convergence Analysis (Theorem 2):**
For stationary noise regimes, under persistent excitation and identifiability, the estimated noise parameter `θ̂_t` is shown to converge almost surely to the true `θ_true`. Consequently, the induced policy loss converges to the FRR approximation floor `2ε / (1 - γ)`, demonstrating that the VoNI-guided approach achieves the certified reliability in the long run.

**Numerical Example:**
The methodology is instantiated on a closed-loop unicycle-model Unmanned Ground Vehicle (UGV) simulation with noisy position observations and an Extended Kalman Filter (EKF).
- **Sensor-only case:** `θ = σ_y`.
- **Noise estimation:** `q_t(σ_y)` is approximated by a truncated Gaussian, updated from EKF innovation statistics after accounting for state-estimation uncertainty.
- **FRR Proxy:** The action-value sensitivity `L_V(σ_y)` and the certificate-gap proxy `b_G_ε` are calibrated functions mirroring the analytical bound.
- **VoNI computation:** Approximated via posterior sampling.
- **Comparison baseline:** A posterior-entropy probing policy that triggers actions based on raw uncertainty in `σ_y`, without considering its impact on the FRR certificate.
- **Diagnostic action:** Perturbs the nominal goal-directed command with reduced speed and periodic yaw excitation to gather informative innovations.

This comprehensive methodology, ranging from theoretical definitions and bounds to a bi-level decision architecture and convergence analysis, provides a certificate-aware framework for managing noise uncertainty.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The numerical experiments, conducted on a closed-loop unicycle-model UGV with an EKF, focused on a sensor-only (`θ = σ_y`) specialization to evaluate the performance of VoNI-guided probing against a posterior-entropy baseline. The experiments explored two scenarios: an abrupt sensing-noise jump and gradual sensing-noise drift.

**1. Certificate-Gap Landscape Visualization:**
Figure 1 illustrates the "certificate-gap proxy" as a function of the estimated sensing noise (`σ̂_y`) for different true sensing noise levels. The main finding from this visualization is that the certificate gap is:
- Small when both the candidate true noise and the estimated noise are within the nominal, "sub-crossover" regime (where FRR constants are relatively insensitive to `σ_y`).
- Large when the true noise is degraded (e.g., `σ_y,true = 0.80 m`) but the estimated noise remains nominal (e.g., `σ̂_y = 0.05 m`). This signifies that an under-resolved FRR cover, calibrated to nominal conditions, becomes unreliable under degraded actual conditions.
This graphically supports the analytical prediction of VoNI: information is valuable only when uncertainty can invalidate the current FRR certificate.

**2. Scenario 1: Abrupt Sensing-Noise Jump:**
In this scenario, the true sensing noise abruptly increased from 0.05 m to 0.80 m at step 100.
- **Detection Time:** VoNI detected the jump significantly earlier, at an average of **126.48 ± 2.58 steps**, compared to the posterior-entropy policy at **185.08 ± 7.32 steps**. This represents a reduction in mean post-jump detection delay of approximately 69% for VoNI.
- **Probing Frequency:** Despite earlier detection, VoNI used substantially fewer diagnostic probing actions, probing during only **13.93 ± 1.16%** of the experiment steps. In contrast, the posterior-entropy policy probed during **95.21 ± 1.09%** of the steps. This indicates an 85% reduction in probing frequency with VoNI.
- **Certificate Gap Reduction:** Figure 2(b) shows that the VoNI policy, by accelerating adaptation, reduced the mismatch-induced certificate gap more rapidly after the noise jump. The entropy policy, despite frequent probing, did not specifically target certificate-relevant mismatch.

**3. Scenario 2: Gradual Sensing-Noise Drift:**
Here, the true sensing noise drifted linearly from 0.05 m to 0.80 m between steps 100 and 350.
- **Tracking Error:** VoNI achieved a lower mean absolute error (MAE) in tracking the drifting noise floor, with an MAE of **0.1407 ± 0.0150 m**, compared to **0.2394 ± 0.0172 m** for the entropy policy. This is an approximately 41% reduction in tracking error.
- **Probing Frequency:** Similar to the jump scenario, VoNI used significantly fewer probing actions, with a probing rate of **39.96 ± 1.87%** of the steps, versus **96.77 ± 0.75%** for the entropy policy. This translates to approximately 59% fewer probing actions.

**4. Closed-Loop Trajectory and Diagnostic Probing:**
Representative UGV trajectories (Figure 4) visually demonstrated the difference in diagnostic behavior. The VoNI policy inserted probes selectively, only when the certificate was at risk, resulting in fewer task trajectory perturbations. The entropy policy, conversely, probed much more persistently due to its generalized uncertainty-driven approach.

In summary, the experimental results consistently demonstrate that the VoNI-guided approach provides earlier detection of significant noise changes, more accurate tracking of gradual noise drift, and significantly reduces the number of diagnostic probing actions compared to a non-certificate-aware, entropy-based probing strategy. These improvements are attributed to VoNI's focus on information that directly impacts the reliability certificate.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework that holds significance for the design and operation of autonomous systems operating in dynamic and uncertain environments. Its potential impact spans several areas:

**1. Enhanced Reliability in Dynamic Environments:**
Autonomous systems frequently encounter varying environmental conditions that affect their sensing and actuation capabilities (e.g., changing illumination for cameras, different terrains for mobile robots, wear and tear on actuators). By actively estimating and adapting to these changing noise floors in a certificate-aware manner, the proposed VoNI framework can maintain the reliability guarantees of the FRR policy even under previously unmodeled or unforeseen degradation. This is crucial for applications where system failure due to environmental uncertainty is unacceptable.

**2. Efficient and Targeted Information Gathering:**
Traditional active perception or information-theoretic exploration methods often prioritize reducing generic uncertainty (e.g., posterior entropy). However, not all uncertainty is equally critical for decision-making. VoNI introduces a criterion that focuses diagnostic probing actions specifically on reducing uncertainty that could invalidate the system's reliability certificate. This targeted approach leads to:
- **Reduced Resource Consumption:** Fewer unnecessary probing actions translate to savings in energy, computation, and time, which are critical constraints in resource-limited robotic platforms.
- **Minimizing Task Perturbation:** Diagnostic probes often involve temporary deviations from the primary task (e.g., executing specific maneuvers to gain better observations). By probing only when genuinely necessary, VoNI minimizes these perturbations, leading to smoother and more efficient task execution.
- **Lower Risk Exposure:** Certain probing actions might expose the system to higher risk (e.g., navigating into uncertain areas). VoNI ensures such actions are taken only when justified by a potential threat to the system's certified performance, contributing to safer operation.

**3. Bridging Theory and Practice:**
The work bridges a gap between theoretical guarantees (like FRR, which often assume known system parameters) and the practical reality of unknown, time-varying parameters. It provides a principled mechanism to integrate online model adaptation within a framework that offers performance certificates, thereby making theoretical guarantees more applicable to real-world deployment.

**4. Foundation for Adaptive Control and Learning:**
The VoNI framework can serve as a component in more complex adaptive control and reinforcement learning systems. By providing a decision layer for model parameter uncertainty, it enables the system to learn and adapt its understanding of the operational environment, leading to more robust and intelligent behavior over time. The established convergence properties provide theoretical assurances for this adaptation.

**5. Applicability Across Domains:**
While demonstrated on a UGV, the underlying principles of VoNI – managing certificate-relevant noise uncertainty – are generalizable. They could be applied to various autonomous systems including aerial vehicles, underwater robots, industrial manipulators, and even medical robotics, where reliable operation under varying physical conditions is paramount.

**6. Future Research Directions:**
The paper also outlines natural extensions, such as finite-sample convergence rates (to bound detection delays), joint sensing-execution noise estimation (to handle both `σ_y` and `σ_u`), and integration with more complex models of context-dependent noise (e.g., multi-modal posteriors for categorical terrain types). These directions point to a fertile area for continued research building on the VoNI foundation.

In conclusion, the Value of Noise Information framework offers a significant advancement in managing latent noise parameters in autonomous systems. By aligning information gathering with the direct impact on system reliability certificates, it promotes more efficient, safer, and ultimately more dependable operation in complex and uncertain real-world environments.
