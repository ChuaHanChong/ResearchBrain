# Adaptive Action Chunking via Multi-Chunk Q Value Estimation

> [!summary] Summary
> Adaptive Action CHunking (ACH) is an offline-to-online RL algorithm that varies action-chunk length per state instead of fixing it. A causal Transformer critic scores every prefix chunk from length 1 to h in one forward pass, and a softmax over those Q-values samples the executed length during training and inference. Across 34 OGBench and Robomimic tasks ACH beats fixed-length baselines, e.g. 90 vs 38 on puzzle-4x4.

## Problem

- Offline and offline-to-online RL methods that use action chunking fix the chunk length, so a single hyperparameter must serve every state and task.
- Optimal length trades future-state uncertainty against policy stochasticity jitter, and that balance shifts within one episode, so any static choice is suboptimal somewhere.
- Prior adaptive-length work in imitation learning adjusts only at inference using entropy heuristics, never folding length adaptation into value learning or training.

## Method

- ==Value-based optimality==: build prefix-consistent chunk sets from one state so Q differences isolate length effects, then use chunk Q-values directly as the selection criterion.
- ==Causal Transformer critic== outputs V(s) plus Q for all h+1 prefix chunks in one pass, trained with ==n-step targets== bootstrapped from state value rather than next-chunk action value.
- ==Adaptive sampling==: a ==softmax chunk-length policy== over Q-values supplies exploration online and reweights both critic and one-step flow policy losses; built on ==Q-Chunking== with h up to 9.

## Results

- On six OGBench environments after **1M** offline plus **1M** online steps, ACH leads on five: puzzle-4x4 **90** vs **64** best baseline, cube-quadruple **52** vs **33**, antsoccer **84** vs **74**.
- Against fixed lengths **1/3/5/10**, no static setting is uniformly good, e.g. H10 reaches **41** on cube-quadruple but **0** on humanoidmaze, while ACH stays strong everywhere.
- Ablations degrade performance: random length selection, uniform-weighted training, and greedy selection all lose ground; compute overhead is **1.3-1.4x** baselines on an RTX 3090.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Chunk length is a state-dependent decision variable, not a hyperparameter; a fixed value only wins when it happens to match a task's average optimum.
> - Prefix structure is the trick that makes lengths comparable, since sharing a common action prefix isolates length from action-identity differences in Q comparisons.
> - Soft value-based sampling beats greedy selection online, and average chunk length grows during fine-tuning as value-based trajectory stitching lengthens reliable behavior segments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research contributes to the fields of reinforcement learning (RL) and imitation learning (IL), specifically within the area of action chunking and offline-to-online (Off2On) RL.

Action chunking originated in imitation learning as a method to enable policies to predict cohesive sequences of actions rather than individual actions. This approach was shown to improve inference efficiency and promote behavioral consistency by reducing redundant or inconsistent actions. Its advantages led to its expansion into reinforcement learning, particularly in offline RL, where it aids in value function training and mitigates bootstrapping errors by aligning value estimation with action sequences. More recently, action chunking has been integrated into offline-to-online (Off2On) RL, a framework designed to overcome the limitations of purely offline RL by allowing policies to refine their behavior through online interaction after offline pre-training. In Off2On RL, action chunking's behavioral consistency further enhances exploration efficiency.

Existing action chunking methods in both offline and Off2On RL typically rely on a fixed chunk length. This static approach presents a performance bottleneck, as the optimal chunk length for an agent can vary significantly depending on the current state and the specific task at hand. For instance, in dynamic environments, a shorter chunk might be required for precise control, while in more predictable segments, a longer chunk could enhance momentum and efficiency.

This paper proposes Adaptive Action CHunking (ACH) as a novel Off2On RL algorithm to address this limitation. ACH introduces a mechanism for policies to dynamically adjust their chunk length during both training and inference. By doing so, this work aims to improve upon previous fixed-length action chunking strategies, which are considered suboptimal, and enhance the generalization and learning efficiency of RL agents in complex environments. The work distinguishes itself from prior research that considered adaptive chunking only during inference by incorporating adaptation into both training and inference phases.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an Off2On RL algorithm, termed Adaptive Action CHunking (ACH), that can dynamically adjust its action chunk length based on the current state during both the training and inference phases of learning.

This objective is motivated by several key considerations:
- **Suboptimality of Fixed Chunk Lengths:** Current action chunking methods in offline and Off2On RL environments universally employ a fixed chunk length. This approach is inherently suboptimal because the ideal duration of an action chunk is not constant; it fluctuates depending on the agent's current state within the environment and the specific requirements of the task.
- **Varying Optimal Lengths in Dynamic Environments:** The paper illustrates this point using a maze navigation task (e.g., antmaze). When an agent is in a rapidly changing segment, such as a sharp turn, an excessively long chunk length can lead to frequent collisions or overshooting, thereby degrading performance. In these scenarios, a shorter chunk length would be more effective, allowing for more responsive control. Conversely, in straight or open corridors where the trajectory is predictable, a longer chunk length would be advantageous for maintaining momentum and executing efficient, continuous movements towards the goal. The inability of fixed-length methods to adapt to these contextual variations creates a performance bottleneck.
- **Trade-off between Future State Uncertainty and Policy Stochasticity Jittering:** The selection of an optimal chunk length involves balancing two competing factors:
 - **Future State Uncertainty:** When a policy samples an action sequence (chunk) based solely on the current state and executes it in an open-loop manner, there is an inherent risk that the actual trajectory might deviate significantly from the states implicitly predicted by the policy. If this occurs, the executed actions become suboptimal. In environments with high uncertainty, a shorter chunk length is preferable to allow for more frequent re-evaluation and adaptation.
 - **Policy Stochasticity Jittering Loss:** In contrast, frequently sampling single actions, especially with stochastic policies (e.g., flow-based models), can introduce random components or "jitter." This interrupts the continuity of behaviors that a longer, cohesive action chunk would provide, potentially hindering an agent's progress. Longer chunks can mitigate this effect by promoting smoother, more consistent execution.
 The true optimal chunk length is therefore determined by the specific trade-off between these two factors, aiming to maximize the expected return.
- **Limitations of Heuristic-Based Optimality:** Previous approaches have explored using heuristics, such as estimated cumulative entropy of future states or action jittering loss, to guide chunk length selection. However, these methods are considered indirect, as they do not directly optimize for overall performance (return maximization). Furthermore, they often require the design of multiple heuristic functions and extensive hyperparameter tuning, adding complexity.
- **Direct Value-Based Optimality:** To circumvent these issues, the paper proposes to directly utilize the action-values (Q-values) of different candidate action chunk lengths as the optimality criterion. This aligns with the fundamental goal of reinforcement learning, which is to maximize cumulative return. By directly assessing which chunk length yields the highest expected future reward, the method aims to achieve a more direct and effective optimization of chunk length.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Adaptive Action CHunking (ACH) method is an Off2On RL algorithm built upon the Q-Chunking (QC) framework. It incorporates three fundamental components: Value-based Optimality, Transformer-based Multi-Chunk Value Estimation, and an Adaptive Sampling Mechanism.

#### 3.1. Value-based Optimality

ACH determines the most appropriate chunk length for a given state by directly employing the action-values of all candidate chunk lengths. For a given state $s_t$, the method constructs a prefix-consistent set of action chunks, $A_h$, which includes chunks of increasing lengths up to a maximum horizon $h$. For example, $A_h = \{(s_t, a_t), (s_t, a_t, a_{t+1}), \ldots, (s_t, a_t, \ldots, a_{t+h})\}$. This structure ensures that each longer chunk extends a shorter one, allowing the Q-value comparison to isolate the effect of chunk length rather than initial action mismatches. The core idea is that the chunk length which maximizes its associated Q-value represents the optimal trade-off between future state uncertainty (where shorter chunks might be better) and policy stochasticity jittering (where longer chunks might be better).

#### 3.2. Transformer-based Multi-Chunk Value Estimation

To efficiently estimate the Q-values for multiple chunk lengths simultaneously, ACH adopts a causal Transformer architecture for its value function. This Transformer takes the current state $s_t$ and a full action chunk $a_{t:t+h}$ (up to the maximum length $h$) as input. In a single forward pass, it outputs estimates for the state value $V(s_t)$ and the Q-values for all possible prefix chunks, i.e., $Q(s_t, a_{t:t}), Q(s_t, a_{t:t+1}), \ldots, Q(s_t, a_{t:t+h})$. Causal masking is implemented within the Transformer to ensure that action chunks do not attend to information from future steps.

The learning objective for the Q-function, $L_Q(\phi)$, is composed of two terms:
1. **N-step Q-value Estimation:** This term trains the Q-value for each chunk length $n$ using an $n$-step target, calculated as $R_t^n + \gamma^{n+1}V_{\bar{\phi}}(s_{t+n+1})$. This approach uses the cumulative reward for the chunk and bootstraps from the *state value* of the terminal state of the chunk, rather than the action value of a subsequent chunk. This design aims to provide less biased value estimates and reduce variance by avoiding action-based bootstrapping for the next chunk.
2. **State Value Learning:** This term trains the state value function $V_{\phi}(s_t)$ by averaging the Q-values of action chunks sampled from the current policy, weighted by the chunk length selection distribution $\pi_L$. This ensures that the state value function provides accurate estimates aligned with the agent's current chunking behavior.

The policy $\pi_{\theta}$ is trained by optimizing a loss function, $L_{\pi}(\theta)$, which combines:
1. **Action-Value Maximization:** The policy is guided to produce action chunks that, according to the chunk length selection policy $\pi_L$, yield high Q-values.
2. **Behavior Cloning (Distillation):** A regularization term ensures that the learned policy stays close to a pre-trained flow-based behavior policy $\beta_{\psi}$, which is trained via flow matching on the offline dataset. This balances exploration of high-value actions with adherence to effective behaviors observed in the dataset.

#### 3.3. Adaptive Chunk Length Sampling

During interaction with the environment, ACH employs an adaptive sampling mechanism to select the chunk length. First, the policy $\pi_{\theta}$ generates a full action chunk $a'_{t:t+h}$ for the current state $s_t$. This full chunk is then passed to the Transformer-based value function to obtain Q-values for all its prefix chunks. Instead of deterministically selecting the length with the highest Q-value (greedy approach), ACH uses a soft policy, $\pi_L(\ell)$, defined as a softmax over the estimated Q-values: $\pi_L(\ell) = \frac{\exp(\beta \cdot Q_{\phi}(s_t, a'_{t:t+\ell}))}{\sum_{n=0}^h \exp(\beta \cdot Q_{\phi}(s_t, a'_{t:t+n}))}$. The parameter $\beta$ is an inverse temperature, controlling the degree of exploration. This probabilistic sampling encourages the agent to explore different chunk lengths, while still favoring those with higher estimated values. For consistent evaluation during inference, the length corresponding to the maximum Q-value is selected, making the policy deterministic.

#### 3.4. Practical Implementation

The implementation uses QC as its backbone, specifically the variant that learns a one-step policy for inference efficiency. The Transformer model employs an embedding dimension of 128, 4 heads, and 2 attention layers. The maximum chunk length ($h$) is set to 10 for most environments, but reduced to 5 for `humanoidmaze` due to its high action dimensionality (21D). The inverse temperature $\beta$ is fixed at 1. The overall process involves an offline phase for pre-training and an online phase where the policy interacts with the environment, adaptively samples chunk lengths, executes actions, and refines its parameters.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical evaluation of ACH was conducted across 34 challenging tasks from OGBench and Robomimic benchmarks, with experiments typically involving 1M steps of offline learning followed by 1M steps of online learning, evaluated over 10 random seeds.

#### 4.1. Overall Performance

- **Superiority over Baselines:** ACH consistently demonstrated superior performance across the diverse OGBench environments (e.g., antmaze-giant, antsoccer-arena, cube-quadruple, puzzle-4x4) and Robomimic tasks (lift, can, square, transport).
- **Comparison with Fixed-Length Baselines:** A dedicated experiment compared ACH against fixed-length baselines (with chunk lengths of 1, 3, 5, and 10). The results showed that fixed-length approaches exhibited significant performance fluctuations across environments; a length optimal for one task might be suboptimal for another. In contrast, ACH consistently achieved high performance across all environments, empirically validating the benefit of adaptive chunk length modulation. For example, while QC (fixed length 5) achieved 47% in `antmaze-giant-navigate`, ACH reached 72%. In `cube-quadruple-play-10M`, the best fixed-length baseline (H10) reached 41%, while ACH achieved 52%.
- **Comparison with Other Off2On Methods:**
 - RLPD, which lacks an offline pre-training phase, showed clear limitations.
 - FQL and FINO, while effective offline RL methods, showed insufficient performance when directly extended to online fine-tuning, especially in manipulation tasks where single-action prediction proved limiting.
 - BFN, despite employing a sampling strategy, performed poorly due to single-action prediction.
 - QC, the backbone algorithm using fixed action chunking, was generally competitive but still outperformed by ACH, which suggests that the adaptive chunk length mechanism facilitates more efficient learning. The performance gains were particularly pronounced in Robomimic tasks like `square` and `transport`, where QC showed only marginal improvements.

#### 4.2. Behavior Analysis

Behavioral comparisons in the `antmaze-giant` environment illustrated the efficacy of adaptive chunking.
- **Single-Action Prediction (Chunk Length = 1):** Policies without action chunking exhibited redundant and oscillatory (jittering) behaviors near the initial state, indicative of inconsistencies in decision-making when predicting individual actions.
- **Fixed Action Chunking (Chunk Length = 5):** While demonstrating improved continuity compared to single-action prediction, this baseline still suffered from suboptimal efficiency. It struggled with precise maneuvers, such as navigating corners, and often failed to reach the goal within time limits, sometimes taking significant detours. This suggests a lack of flexibility to recover from suboptimal paths.
- **ACH (Adaptive Chunking):** By dynamically selecting the most appropriate chunk length for each state, ACH generated efficient and smooth trajectories that successfully reached the goal. These high-quality, efficient behaviors observed during online interaction were cited as a factor in its sample-efficient training and enhanced performance.

#### 4.3. Ablation Study

An ablation study confirmed the importance of ACH's core components:
- **Random Chunk Length Selection:** Replacing value-based sampling with random selection led to a significant performance decline, highlighting the necessity of considering the expected return of action chunks.
- **Uniform Training Distribution:** Training the value function and policy by uniformly weighting all chunk length candidates (instead of prioritizing the $\pi_L$ distribution) resulted in substantial performance degradation. This was attributed to a misalignment between the training objective and the online action selection policy.
- **Greedy Sampling (vs. Soft Sampling):** A greedy approach (selecting the chunk length with the highest estimated value) achieved competitive results in some environments but exhibited suboptimal sample efficiency compared to ACH's soft sampling. This indicates that the exploratory component of soft sampling is beneficial for discovering more effective chunking strategies during online learning.
These findings collectively indicate that the synergistic interaction between ACH's value-based selection criterion, Transformer-based training, and adaptive soft sampling mechanism is crucial for its sample-efficient learning.

#### 4.4. Evolution of Chunk Length

Analysis of the average chunk length during the online learning phase on the `puzzle-4x4` task showed that ACH tends to gradually increase the average chunk length as training progresses. This phenomenon is attributed to the value function's ability to "stitch" trajectories. As the policy refines its behavior based on these values, it learns to execute actions as longer, more cohesive sequences that previously might have required multiple shorter segments. This dynamic adjustment of temporal granularity is presented as a mechanism for sample-efficient learning.

#### 4.5. Computational Cost

ACH incurs a higher computational cost during both training and inference due to the use of a Transformer architecture and the requirement to estimate values for multiple chunk lengths simultaneously. Quantitatively, the processing time for ACH was approximately 1.3 to 1.4 times that of the baselines. However, the authors considered this overhead justifiable given the significant performance improvements achieved by the method.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of reinforcement learning by addressing a practical limitation in action chunking methods and advancing the capabilities of offline-to-online (Off2On) RL.

The primary significance of ACH lies in its ability to introduce dynamic temporal abstraction into RL policies. By enabling agents to adaptively modulate their action chunk length based on the current state, the method overcomes the inherent suboptimality of fixed-length chunking strategies. This adaptability allows policies to exhibit more responsive behavior in rapidly changing environments and more efficient, continuous actions in predictable contexts, thereby enhancing both control precision and overall task efficiency.

The demonstrated consistent outperformance of ACH across a diverse set of 34 challenging tasks, including complex navigation and manipulation environments, indicates improved generalization and learning efficiency. This is particularly impactful for Off2On RL, where the goal is to leverage offline data for pre-training and then efficiently refine policies through limited online interaction. The ability to learn and exploit optimal temporal granularities can lead to more robust policies that can better transfer to real-world scenarios where online data collection is costly or unsafe.

The methodology, integrating a causal Transformer for multi-chunk Q-value estimation and an adaptive soft sampling mechanism for chunk length selection, represents a novel architectural and algorithmic contribution. The empirical validation through ablation studies confirms that the synergy of these components is critical for achieving sample-efficient learning.

Furthermore, the observation that the average chunk length increases during online learning provides an insight into how adaptive chunking facilitates the learning of more cohesive and extended behaviors. This suggests that the value function learns to 'stitch' longer, high-value trajectories, enabling the policy to execute behaviors that were previously fragmented.

Potential impacts of this work include:
- **Enhanced Robotic Control:** In robotic applications, where precise control and efficient long-horizon planning are crucial, adaptive action chunking could lead to more agile and capable robots that can seamlessly switch between fine-grained movements and broad strokes.
- **More Generalizable RL Agents:** By allowing policies to learn and adapt their temporal resolution, ACH could contribute to the development of more generalizable RL agents capable of tackling a wider array of complex, multi-modal tasks without requiring extensive hyperparameter tuning for chunk lengths.
- **Improved Sample Efficiency in Real-World RL:** For real-world deployments of RL, where online interaction is expensive or time-consuming, the enhanced sample efficiency offered by adaptive chunking could reduce the resources required to fine-tune pre-trained policies.
- **Further Research in Temporal Abstraction:** This work opens avenues for future research into adaptive temporal abstraction in RL, including exploring more computationally efficient architectures than Transformers for multi-chunk value estimation and investigating dynamic replanning capabilities during chunk execution for highly dynamic and uncertain environments.
