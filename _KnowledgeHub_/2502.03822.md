# Dynamic Rank Adjustment in Diffusion Policies for Efficient and Flexible Training

> [!summary] Summary
> The DRIFT framework allows for efficient and flexible training of large diffusion policies for robotic motion generation by dynamically adjusting trainable ranks. It reduces batch training time by 11% overall and 18% during online adaptation, making interactive imitation learning with these models practical while maintaining performance.

## Problem

- Overparameterized diffusion policies for robotics incur high computational costs and long training times, limiting practical deployment.
- Existing low-rank parameter-efficient methods (e.g., LoRA) are designed for fine-tuning pre-trained models and are unsuitable for training diffusion policies from scratch.
- The high training time of large diffusion policies renders online interactive imitation learning impractical, despite its potential for improved sample efficiency.

## Method

- Introduces DRIFT, a framework for dynamic rank adjustment in diffusion policies during training, consisting of Rank Modulation and a Rank Scheduler.
- Rank Modulation uses Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) to dynamically partition weight matrices into trainable and frozen components, operating directly on original weights without introducing new parameters.
- A Rank Scheduler coordinates the decay of trainable ranks based on the training epoch, with functions like sigmoid controlling the adjustment, and DRIFT is instantiated within a DAgger-like interactive imitation learning pipeline.

## Results

- The DRIFT-DAgger framework reduced overall batch training time by **11%** and online training time during adaptation by **18%** compared to full-rank baselines.
- DRIFT-DAgger, utilizing Rank Modulation with a sigmoid decay schedule and a terminal rank of 256, achieved comparable performance and sample efficiency to full-rank methods while improving training efficiency.
- Real-world experiments confirmed the simulation findings, demonstrating reduced cumulative training time and robust performance across various robotic tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Dynamically adjusting trainable ranks via SVD (Rank Modulation) directly on existing weights offers stability and efficiency for training from scratch, unlike LoRA which adds parameters and is unstable with dynamic changes.
> - A sigmoid-based rank decay schedule effectively balances early exploration with high ranks and later refinement with reduced ranks, optimizing performance and training time.
> - Training diffusion policies from scratch with rank adjustment requires a higher minimum trainable rank (e.g., 256) than typical fine-tuning scenarios (e.g., 4) to retain representational power.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research paper addresses a critical challenge at the intersection of modern machine learning and robotics: the computational burden of training large, overparameterized models like diffusion policies for real-world robotic tasks.

**Diffusion Policies in Robotics:** Diffusion models have recently emerged as a powerful paradigm for robotic motion generation, exhibiting impressive performance due to their capacity for complex representation and generalization, often achieved through overparameterization. They have gained traction as an effective method for visuomotor control by modeling action distributions. However, this success comes at a high computational cost during training, making them less suitable for scenarios requiring rapid policy updates or interactive learning.

**Overparameterization vs. Efficiency:** The broader machine learning community has long recognized that overparameterized models, while powerful, are computationally expensive. Efforts to address this inefficiency often leverage the "intrinsic low-rank structure" of neural networks, suggesting that effective learning often occurs within a lower-dimensional subspace of the full parameter space. Existing methods, such as Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) and its variants (DyLoRA, QLoRA), primarily focus on **fine-tuning pre-trained models**. This is a significant distinction because, in robotics, policies are frequently trained **from scratch** for specific, often unique, tasks, rather than fine-tuning a generic foundation model. This makes pre-existing fine-tuning methods largely unsuitable for the *initial training* phase in robotics.

**Interactive Imitation Learning (IL):** Prior to the advent of large diffusion models, interactive IL methods (e.g., DAgger and its derivatives like HG-DAgger, ThriftyDAgger, LazyDAgger) were developed to improve the sample efficiency of offline Behavioral Cloning (BC). These methods typically involve an offline bootstrapping phase followed by an online adaptation phase where human experts provide corrective feedback. Historically, these methods relied on simpler, more compact network architectures (MLPs, LSTMs) that allowed for quick policy updates in an interactive loop. The much larger size and training time of diffusion policies makes direct application of these interactive IL methods impractical, confining most diffusion policy research to offline training.

**Bridging the Gap:** This paper aims to bridge these two major gaps:
1. **Overparameterization vs. Training from Scratch:** It seeks to apply the efficiency benefits of exploiting low-rank structures not just for fine-tuning, but for training large diffusion policies *from scratch*. This requires a dynamic approach to adjust trainable ranks throughout the entire learning process.
2. **Large Models vs. Interactive Learning:** By making training more efficient, the work seeks to enable the integration of diffusion policies with online interactive imitation learning, allowing for better sample efficiency and more intuitive expert feedback, which is currently hampered by long training times.

The paper explicitly positions itself as a solution to "the critical issue of reducing batch training time, which is essential for making online interactive learning with large models more practical and usable," distinguishing itself from prior work that focused on inference acceleration or specific forms of data augmentation in diffusion models for robotics.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a framework that enables the dynamic adjustment of trainable parameters in large diffusion policies, thereby balancing representational power with computational efficiency during the training process, especially when training from scratch.

The core motivations are:
- **Computational Inefficiency of Overparameterized Diffusion Policies:** While diffusion policies are powerful for robotic motion generation due to overparameterization, their large model size leads to high computational costs and significantly longer training times. This limits their practical deployment, particularly in scenarios requiring frequent policy updates.
- **Unsuitability of Existing Low-Rank Methods for Training from Scratch:** Current parameter-efficient methods, like LoRA, are designed for fine-tuning pre-trained models. Robotics often requires training policies from scratch, rendering these methods less effective or even impractical due to their fixed low-rank structure, instability upon dynamic rank changes, and increased forward pass computational overhead if dynamically re-injected.
- **Impracticality of Online Interactive Imitation Learning with Large Models:** Interactive IL methods offer superior sample efficiency and intuitive expert feedback compared to purely offline training. However, the substantially larger number of parameters in diffusion policies (often an order of magnitude more than traditional compact networks) makes online adaptation phases, which require rapid policy updates, unfeasible due to prohibitive training times. This forces experts into inefficient offline cycles of data collection and retraining.
- **Need for Dynamic Rank Adjustment:** The ideal solution would be to dynamically adjust the trainable portion of the policy. This means starting with a higher number of trainable ranks to capture general patterns (leveraging overparameterization) and then gradually reducing them as training progresses and the policy shifts to refinement, improving efficiency without sacrificing performance.

In essence, the paper aims to develop a system that allows large, powerful diffusion policies to be trained effectively and efficiently from scratch, making interactive and online learning practical for complex robotic tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors propose **Dynamic Rank-adjustable DIFfusion Policy Training (DRIFT)**, a novel framework designed to address the challenges of training large diffusion policies efficiently and flexibly from scratch. The framework consists of two core components: Rank Modulation and Rank Scheduler. To demonstrate its efficacy, DRIFT is instantiated within an interactive imitation learning algorithm called **DRIFT-DAgger**.

**A. Rank Modulation:**
This component is designed to dynamically adjust the proportion of trainable and frozen ranks within the policy's weight matrices while maintaining the total rank constant. It achieves this by leveraging **Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)**.

1. **Mechanism:** For each weight matrix `W` (derived from a convolutional weight tensor `W_conv` in the U-Net backbone of the diffusion policy), SVD is applied: `W = U Σ V^T`.
2. **Partitioning:** The `U`, `Σ`, and `V` matrices are then split into trainable and frozen components based on a specified rank `r`. Only `U_train`, `Σ_train`, and `V_train` (representing the rank-`r` subspace) receive gradient updates during backpropagation, while `U_frozen`, `Σ_frozen`, and `V_frozen` remain fixed.
3. **Contrast with LoRA:** The paper highlights key advantages of Rank Modulation over LoRA for training from scratch:
 - **No New Parameters:** Unlike LoRA, which injects additional low-rank adapter matrices (`W_up`, `W_down`), Rank Modulation operates directly on the original weight matrix. This means no new parameters are introduced, and the overall parameter count does not increase.
 - **Stable Dynamic Adjustment:** LoRA suffers from instability when its rank changes dynamically because new adapters must be merged and reinjected, introducing freshly initialized parameters. Rank Modulation avoids this by recomputing the SVD and partitioning the *existing* weight matrix, ensuring stable updates even when ranks change.
 - **Constant Forward Pass Cost:** LoRA's additional convolution operation for its adapter blocks increases the computational cost of the forward pass, proportional to the adapter rank `r`. Rank Modulation reshapes the recombined `W = W_train + W_frozen` back into `W_conv` for a single, standard convolution, maintaining the same forward pass complexity as the original full-rank convolution. This is crucial when higher trainable ranks are needed for training from scratch.
 - **Suitability for Training from Scratch:** LoRA relies on the main `W_conv` being thoroughly pre-trained for its low-rank approximation to be effective. Rank Modulation, by directly decomposing and updating components of the full weight matrix, is well-suited for training models from scratch.

The SVD recomputation and reorthonormalization of U and V matrices are performed at the start of each epoch only if the number of trainable ranks has changed, balancing stability with computational overhead.

**B. Rank Scheduler:**
This component coordinates the dynamic adjustment of trainable ranks throughout the training process. Inspired by noise schedulers in diffusion models, it determines the current number of trainable ranks `r_i` based on the training epoch.

1. **Decay Functions:** The paper implements and evaluates four common decay functions:
 - **Linear:** Decreases ranks uniformly over epochs.
 - **Cosine:** Follows a cosine curve, often showing a smoother decay.
 - **Sigmoid:** Starts high, then rapidly decreases around a midpoint, and flattens out.
 - **Exponential:** Rapidly decreases initially, then slows down.
 These functions take into account the current epoch, total epochs, maximum trainable rank (`r_max`), and desired minimum/terminal rank (`r_min`).

**C. DRIFT-DAgger (Instantiation):**
DRIFT is integrated into a DAgger-like interactive imitation learning pipeline.

1. **Offline Bootstrapping Phase:** The process begins with an initial policy trained on an offline dataset using behavioral cloning. During this phase, a rank scheduler *optionally* reduces the number of trainable ranks. If no scheduler is used, the rank is fixed at `r_min` before transitioning to the online phase.
2. **Online Adaptation Phase:** The learner policy interacts with the environment. When the expert policy detects deviation (e.g., via cosine similarity threshold between learner and expert actions), the human expert intervenes to provide corrective demonstrations. These new demonstrations are added to a global dataset, and the policy is updated with the expanded dataset. The reduced trainable ranks (either fixed `r_min` or dynamically adjusted by a scheduler) significantly accelerate the policy update step during this crucial interactive phase.

This methodology combines the representational power of diffusion policies with the efficiency of low-rank training and the sample efficiency of interactive learning, aiming for a more practical and effective robot learning pipeline.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The authors conducted extensive experiments in both simulated and real-world robotic environments to validate the DRIFT framework and its instantiation in DRIFT-DAgger.

**A. Ablation Study on Decay Functions (Simulation):**
- **Performance vs. Efficiency Trade-off:** All decay functions successfully reduced batch training time compared to full-rank training (HG-DAgger baseline). However, aggressive decay functions (e.g., Exponential) led to a noticeable decrease in policy performance (success rate), despite the fastest training.
- **Optimal Scheduler:** The **sigmoid decay function with a steepness parameter τ=0.5** demonstrated the best balance, achieving perfect or near-perfect success rates while significantly reducing training time. This is attributed to maintaining a high number of trainable ranks early on to capture general behaviors, then efficiently reducing ranks for refinement, thereby minimizing approximation error and preserving flexibility for online adaptation. Linear decay also performed well but was less efficient.

**B. Ablation Study on Terminal Rank (r_min) (Simulation):**
- **Impact of r_min:** Decreasing the terminal rank (`r_min`) generally reduced training time but could negatively impact policy performance, particularly when `r_min` was too low (e.g., `r_min=64` showed a noticeable performance drop).
- **Higher Ranks for Training from Scratch:** The study found that training diffusion policies from scratch with reduced ranks requires a significantly higher `r_min` (e.g., `r_min=256`) compared to fine-tuning methods (which might use `r=4`). This indicates the need for greater representational power when learning from scratch to leverage overparameterization effectively.
- **Optimal r_min:** `r_min=256` consistently provided the best balance of efficiency and performance across different tasks, with higher values like `r_min=512` offering no additional performance benefit but increasing training time.

**C. Benchmark Comparison (Simulation):**
- **Training Time Reduction:** All DRIFT-DAgger variants (LoRA-fixed, LoRA-scheduled, Rank Modulation-scheduled) demonstrated a pronounced reduction in cumulative training time compared to full-rank BC and HG-DAgger.
- **Sample Efficiency:** Interactive IL methods (HG-DAgger and most DRIFT-DAgger variants) showed superior expert sample efficiency (higher success rates with fewer expert labels) compared to BC. This advantage was more significant in longer-duration tasks.
- **LoRA Instability:** The DRIFT-DAgger variant using LoRA with a rank scheduler (dynamically merging/re-injecting adapters) suffered from significant performance degradation due to training instability, as reflected by a higher step loss and lower success rate. This validates the design choice of Rank Modulation over LoRA for dynamic rank adjustment from scratch.
- **DRIFT-DAgger (Rank Modulation + Scheduler) Performance:** This variant achieved performance and sample efficiency comparable to HG-DAgger (full-rank training), while substantially reducing training time. It also exhibited good stability and better convergence, indicated by lower standard deviation in task duration.

**D. Batch Training Time Analysis (Simulation):**
- The DRIFT-DAgger variant with **Rank Modulation and Rank Scheduler** reduced the mean batch training time across all stages by **11%** compared to the full-rank HG-DAgger baseline.
- During the critical **online training stage**, this variant achieved an **18% reduction** in batch training time, making interactive learning significantly more practical.
- The LoRA-based dynamic variant showed reduced training time but failed to maintain performance due to instability.

**E. Real-World Evaluation:**
- **Consistency with Simulation:** The real-world experiments on block stacking, drawer assembling, and drawer interaction tasks confirmed the trends observed in simulation.
- **Real-World Benefits:** DRIFT-DAgger variants (particularly with Rank Modulation) provided noticeably reduced cumulative training time, improved sample efficiency, and robust performance on par with full-rank methods, even with reduced-rank training.
- **Interactive Advantage for Longer Tasks:** The benefits of interactive methods and DRIFT's efficiency were more pronounced for longer-duration tasks, where cumulative training time and expert feedback become more critical.

**F. Additional Baselines (Appendix):**
- **QLoRA:** Performed poorly, further supporting the idea that fine-tuning methods are not directly suitable for training from scratch.
- **QR Decomposition per step:** Introduced significant computational overhead without measurable performance gains, justifying the epoch-based SVD recomputation in DRIFT.
- **DSR (Diffusion Step Reduction):** Caused model collapse, suggesting that techniques from image generation (like focusing on later denoising steps) might not directly translate to robotic policy learning without careful adaptation.

In summary, the main findings consistently demonstrate that the DRIFT framework, particularly when utilizing Rank Modulation with a sigmoid decay-based Rank Scheduler, effectively enables efficient and flexible training of diffusion policies from scratch. It significantly reduces training time, especially during online adaptation, without compromising performance, thereby making interactive imitation learning with large models a practical reality.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research paper makes several significant contributions and has substantial potential impact on the field of robot learning:

1. **Enabling Practical Interactive Learning for Large Models:** The most immediate and significant impact is making online interactive imitation learning practical for large, overparameterized diffusion policies. Historically, the high computational cost of training these models has largely confined them to offline settings. By substantially reducing batch training time (11% overall, 18% during online phases), DRIFT-DAgger allows for quicker policy updates, directly addressing the barrier that prevented the adoption of interactive learning paradigms with modern, powerful models. This means human experts can provide feedback more efficiently, leading to faster policy refinement and improved sample efficiency (fewer expert demonstrations needed for comparable performance).

2. **Bridging the Gap: Overparameterization and Training from Scratch in Robotics:** The work uniquely addresses the challenge of leveraging the intrinsic low-rank structure for *training models from scratch*, which is a common requirement in robotics. Unlike existing parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods (e.g., LoRA) that assume a well-pretrained base model, DRIFT's Rank Modulation is specifically designed to dynamically partition and update weight matrices from their initial state. This allows roboticists to benefit from the representational power of overparameterization during early learning while simultaneously gaining efficiency as training progresses. This is a crucial distinction for a field where task-specific policies are often developed without access to large, general-purpose foundation models.

3. **Novel Approach to Dynamic Rank Adjustment:** The introduction of Rank Modulation via SVD decomposition offers a stable and efficient alternative to LoRA for dynamic rank adjustment. By avoiding the injection of new parameters and maintaining constant forward pass computational cost, Rank Modulation ensures training stability and efficiency, even when the number of trainable ranks changes frequently. This technical innovation could be adopted by other large model training paradigms beyond diffusion policies in robotics.

4. **Improved Sample Efficiency:** By successfully integrating diffusion policies with interactive learning, DRIFT-DAgger demonstrates improved sample efficiency compared to traditional Behavior Cloning. This means less human effort is required to collect high-quality demonstration data, a major bottleneck in real-world robot learning applications.

5. **Robust Real-World Validation:** The extensive validation in diverse real-world robotic tasks (block stacking, drawer assembly, drawer interaction) underscores the practicality and robustness of the DRIFT framework. This moves beyond theoretical discussions and simulation results, demonstrating tangible benefits in complex physical environments.

6. **Raising Awareness and Future Research Directions:** The paper highlights the relatively underexplored concepts of overparameterization and intrinsic ranks within the robotics community. By demonstrating their benefits, the authors aim to encourage further research into more efficient learning methods for robot control in the era of large models. The identified limitations, such as exploring DRIFT's applicability to online reinforcement learning, developing task-dependent or non-uniform rank adjustment strategies, also provide clear pathways for future work, fostering continued innovation in the field.

In essence, DRIFT is a significant step towards making the power of large generative models, specifically diffusion policies, accessible and practical for real-world interactive robot learning, enabling more efficient and adaptable robotic systems.
