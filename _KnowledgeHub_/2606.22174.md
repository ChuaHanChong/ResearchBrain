# OpenHLM: An Empirical Recipe for Whole-Body Humanoid Loco-Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Tsinghua University and Spirit AI developed OpenHLM, an empirical recipe for whole-body humanoid loco-manipulation, addressing the challenge of integrating language-conditioned control across all humanoid degrees of freedom. The system enables a VLA model to achieve 87.5% task progress on complex long-horizon tasks with less than half the demonstration time compared to prior state-of-the-art systems.

## Problem

- Existing humanoid control often relies on decoupled upper/lower body control, limiting whole-body coordination and functional use of the lower body for manipulation.
- The design space for integrated whole-body Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models for high-degree-of-freedom humanoids remains largely unexplored.
- Collecting high-quality whole-body teleoperation data for humanoids is resource-intensive, hindering scalability and data efficiency for learning diverse tasks.

## Method

- Developed **OpenHLM**, an open-source empirical recipe, through a systematic roadmap of experiments across teleoperation, VLA policy design, and data co-training.
- Designed a joint-based ==whole-body teleoperation interface== with real-time retargeting to collect high-quality, coordinated demonstrations for all humanoid degrees of freedom.
- Adapted existing VLA models (initialized with "π0.5") and integrated heterogeneous data sources, including stationary teleoperation and robot-free human demonstrations, to extend capabilities efficiently.

## Results

- Joint-based whole-body teleoperation achieved 80-**87%** task progress on diverse tasks, significantly outperforming decoupled and VR 3-point teleoperation methods.
- OpenHLM, initialized with "π0.5" and trained with 40 whole-body demonstrations per task, achieved **89%** average task progress on 8 training tasks, demonstrating generalization.
- On a challenging long-horizon task (Fruit Arrangement), OpenHLM achieved **87.5%** task progress, outperforming Ψ0 (**48.8%**) and GR00T N1.6 (**57.5%**) with less than half the demonstration time (**1.14** hours vs. **2.70**–**2.73** hours).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Joint-based whole-body teleoperation with a 0.2-second preview latency for online retargeting is crucial for collecting high-quality, coordinated humanoid loco-manipulation data.
> - Pretraining on diverse robot data (like "π0.5") provides robust manipulation priors that transfer effectively, outperforming models without robot-specific pretraining even across embodiment gaps.
> - Heterogeneous co-training effectively extends policy capabilities: stationary teleoperation provides new semantics and motion patterns, while robot-free human data primarily enhances semantic understanding at lower collection costs.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of humanoid robotics has progressively advanced towards complex loco-manipulation tasks. Historically, many existing humanoid systems have relied on a decoupled control paradigm, separating the control of the upper and lower bodies. This approach often treats the lower body as a mobile base for a dual-arm manipulator, limiting the robot's ability to achieve fluid, coordinated whole-body motions. Such decoupling restricts the functional use of the lower body in manipulation tasks and results in visually mechanical behaviors.

A recent trend in humanoid robotics aims to develop integrated whole-body control stacks that reason over the robot's entire kinematic chain. This shift acknowledges the potential for humanoids to leverage their full embodiment for diverse tasks, mirroring human capabilities. Within this evolving landscape, a common architectural approach involves a two-level hierarchy: a high-level Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model that generates whole-body commands from language and visual inputs, and a low-level controller that tracks these commands.

Despite the recognition of this architectural direction, the design space for such whole-body native VLA systems for humanoids remains largely underexplored. Existing VLA models, such as RT-2, OpenVLA, and the π series, have demonstrated proficiency in manipulation tasks but primarily target static or wheeled dual-arm platforms, with action distributions dominated by arm and wrist movements. While some recent efforts, including GR00T N1.6 and Ψ0, have attempted to extend VLA capabilities to humanoids by incorporating humanoid demonstrations into pretraining, they often still rely on decoupled control or exhibit limitations in achieving robust whole-body coordination.

This research addresses a gap in the literature by systematically investigating the empirical design choices necessary to build a whole-body native VLA model for humanoids. It explores fundamental questions regarding teleoperation interfaces for data collection, adaptations required for existing VLAs to handle high-degree-of-freedom humanoids, and the effective integration of cheaper, heterogeneous data sources to enhance scalability. The work distinguishes itself by providing an empirical "recipe" derived from controlled experiments, moving beyond simply scaling existing models or data.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to develop a whole-body native Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model capable of mapping language instructions and visual observations directly to all degrees of freedom of a humanoid robot, thereby enabling coordinated loco-manipulation. The authors explicitly pose the question: "what it takes to build a whole-body native vision-language-action (VLA) model that maps language and pixels directly to all of the humanoid’s degrees of freedom."

This overarching goal is motivated by three specific design desiderata for a physical humanoid loco-manipulation system:

1. **G1: Whole-body native control:** The system should employ a single policy to simultaneously command every joint of the humanoid. This approach aims to treat the arms, knees, and feet as potential actuators for manipulation, moving beyond conventional decoupled control methods where the lower body often functions merely as a mobile base. The motivation here is to enable complex behaviors that require direct recruitment of the lower body, such as squatting to reach low objects or using a foot to manipulate an object, which are otherwise outside the expressible space of decoupled controllers.

2. **G2: Language-steerable and data-efficient per task:** The system should feature a single policy capable of driving the humanoid across a diverse range of tasks, guided by natural language prompts rather than requiring distinct models for each skill. Furthermore, each new skill should be learnable from a modest number of demonstrations, addressing the practical challenge of collecting extensive, high-quality robot data. This objective emphasizes generalization and practical deployment efficiency.

3. **G3: Extensible through cheap data:** Recognizing that collecting whole-body humanoid teleoperation data is resource-intensive, the system is motivated to leverage cheaper, heterogeneous data sources. The aim is to reduce the reliance on expensive whole-body teleop, thereby enabling faster and more scalable skill extension to new objects and instructions. This includes exploring data from stationary manipulation or robot-free human demonstrations.

To achieve these objectives, the research is structured as an empirical study to address three specific questions that arose from adopting a two-level hierarchical control framework for whole-body humanoids:
- How should the low-level controller and its associated teleoperation interface be designed to facilitate the collection of high-quality whole-body demonstrations?
- Given that many existing VLAs are designed for static or wheeled dual-arm platforms, what specific adaptations are critical for these models to effectively control a humanoid's full degrees of freedom?
- Once a robust pipeline is established, how can cheaper data sources complement expensive whole-body teleoperation to extend policy capabilities to new tasks, objects, and instructions?

These questions guide a systematic roadmap of controlled experiments across three phases, each aimed at quantifying the impact of specific design choices on task performance.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a systematic empirical study, structured as a roadmap of one-variable-at-a-time experiments, to develop a recipe for whole-body humanoid loco-manipulation. The entire system operates within a two-level hierarchical control framework: a high-level policy (either a human operator during data collection or the learned VLA during deployment) issues reference whole-body commands at a low frequency (10 Hz), which are then tracked by a lightweight low-level controller that outputs target joint positions at a higher frequency (50 Hz) for PD control.

**The HLM-12 Benchmark:**
A custom benchmark, HLM-12, was developed to evaluate the system's capabilities. It comprises 12 language-conditioned tasks categorized into four families:
1. **Pick-and-place with locomotion:** Basic tasks involving walking, grasping, and placing objects (e.g., Cola Placement).
2. **Whole-body workspace extension:** Tasks requiring coordination of hip flexion, knee bend, and torso pitch with arm movements to reach objects outside typical arm-only workspace (e.g., Shelf Cube Transfer).
3. **Using body parts as manipulators:** Tasks where non-arm body parts act as end-effectors (e.g., using a foot to press a pedal in Bottle Disposal).
4. **Loco-manipulation under environmental constraint:** Tasks involving complex object geometry or contact constraints (e.g., Sword Extraction, Cart Pushing).

**Evaluation Protocol:**
Every (policy, task) pair is evaluated in the real world over five independent rollouts. To ensure fair comparison, target objects are placed in different positions, and distractor object layouts vary across rollouts, but the same five initial scene configurations are used for all policies per task. Task progress is scored as a fraction in \, providing partial credit for sub-stages, which offers a more nuanced measure than binary success rates.

**Three Phases of the Roadmap:**

**I. Low-Level Controller & Teleoperation (§3.1):**
This phase focuses on designing the low-level controller and its teleoperation interface for collecting high-quality whole-body demonstrations.
- **Teleoperation Methods Comparison:** Three representative methods were compared:
 - **Decoupled control teleoperation:** Upper and lower bodies are controlled separately (e.g., inverse kinematics for arms, RL-trained controller for legs driven by base-velocity/root-height commands). Action space: 21 dimensions (14 arm joints + 3 waist joints + 1 root height + 3 navigation).
 - **VR 3-point teleoperation:** Operator supplies head and wrist poses via VR, with joystick for navigation. A learned kinematic motion planner generates lower-body motion. Action space: 24 dimensions (7 for each wrist pose + 7 for head pose + 3 navigation).
 - **Joint-based whole-body teleoperation:** A motion-capture rig (PICO VR with body trackers) captures operator's full-body motion, retargeted in real-time to every humanoid joint via GMR. Action space: 32 dimensions (14 arm joints + 12 leg joints + 3 waist joints + 3 root roll/pitch/yaw rate).
- **SMPL-based whole-body teleoperation:** An alternative to joint-based, using the SMPL human-body representation as the action space. Action space: 81 dimensions (72 SMPL joint positions + 6 wrist refinement + 3 root roll/pitch/yaw rate).
- **Future-Frame Preview Latency:** The effect of tunable preview latency (∆t ∈ {0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6} s) on teleoperation experience and data quality was studied.

**II. Whole-Body VLA Policy Design (§3.2):**
This phase adapts a VLA, typically pretrained on static/wheeled dual-arm platforms, to control the humanoid's full action space. The default VLA backbone was π0.5 \.
- **Action and Proprioception Interface:**
 - **Action projection initialization:** Random re-initialization vs. weight surgery (preserving pretrained weights for shared dimensions).
 - **Action ordering:** Pretrained bimanual ordering with humanoid joints appended vs. humanoid-native ordering.
 - **Absolute vs. relative action targets:** Predicting absolute joint positions vs. relative deltas.
 - **Proprioceptive input:** Including the full joint-position vector vs. inferring body pose from vision only.
- **Role of Pretraining:** Comparison of three backbone initializations:
 - π0.5 (robot-pretrained, non-humanoid data).
 - PaliGemma (same VLM architecture as π0.5, but no robot data pretraining).
 - Random initialization (no pretraining).
- **Faster Action Generation:** Evaluation of single-step inference methods vs. the default multi-step flow matching:
 - One-step flow matching (1 integration step).
 - Drifting model \ (a generative model naturally admitting one-step inference).
- **Whole-Body Teleop Data Scaling:** Investigating the impact of the number of demonstrations per task (5, 10, 20, 40) on task progress.

**III. Heterogeneous Co-Training (§3.3):**
This phase explores using cheaper data sources to extend the policy to new objects and instructions without additional whole-body teleoperation.
- **Stationary Same-Embodiment Teleoperation:** Collecting manipulation-only demonstrations with the robot's feet planted, avoiding locomotion portions.
- **HuMI-collected Robot-Free Demonstrations:** Using the HuMI system \ (handheld grippers and body trackers) to capture human task-space trajectories, which are then lifted to full-body joint positions via an IK pipeline.
- Evaluation on 4 held-out tasks, comparing performance with an 8-task baseline (whole-body teleop only) and a 12-task oracle (whole-body teleop on all tasks).

**System-Level Comparison (§4):**
OpenHLM was benchmarked against two state-of-the-art humanoid VLAs, GR00T N1.6 \ and Ψ0 \, on a challenging long-horizon task (Fruit Arrangement) spanning a wide vertical workspace. This comparison assessed task progress and total demonstration time.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical study yielded several findings across the three phases of its roadmap, culminating in the OpenHLM recipe.

**I. Low-Level Controller & Teleoperation:**
- **Teleoperation Method Comparison:** Joint-based whole-body teleoperation demonstrated superior performance, achieving 80%–87% task progress on diverse tasks (Cola Placement, Shelf Cup Transfer, Bottle Disposal) while alternatives degraded. Decoupled control exhibited inefficient locomotion (e.g., 42.3 footsteps on Cola Placement vs. 12.0 for joint-based) and could not execute foot-based manipulation. VR 3-point teleoperation led to indecisive behavior and lower task progress (40% on Cola Placement), failing entirely on tasks requiring squatting or foot manipulation.
- **Retargeting Method:** Joint-space retargeting during data collection resulted in 88% average task progress, outperforming SMPL-based retargeting (75%). The higher dimensionality (81D) of SMPL was hypothesized to present a harder learning problem for the VLA, leading to specific failure modes such as inadequate foot clearance or imprecise walking.
- **Future-Frame Preview Latency:** A preview latency of 0.2 seconds in the low-level controller provided an optimal balance. It yielded 67% task progress (on a single-task model) with an average demonstration duration of 35.2 seconds, similar to 0-second latency, but without the stuttering locomotion observed at 0 seconds. Longer latencies (e.g., 0.6 seconds) caused excessive delay and task progress collapse (13%).
 - **Conclusion for Phase I:** Joint-based whole-body teleoperation, retargeted online to robot joint space with a 0.2-second preview latency, was adopted as the primary data collection method.

**II. Whole-Body VLA Policy Design:**
- **Action and Proprioception Interface:** Individual ablations of interface choices (random re-initialization of action projection, humanoid-native action ordering, relative action targets, or removal of proprioceptive input) resulted in minor decreases in average task progress (typically a few percentage points), suggesting no single choice was a bottleneck in isolation. However, simultaneously removing proprioception and using relative actions led to catastrophic failure, indicating interactions between these choices. The default settings (weight surgery, pretrained bimanual ordering, absolute joint targets, and proprioception as input) were maintained.
- **Role of Pretraining:** Pretraining on non-humanoid robot data proved substantial. A π0.5-initialized VLA achieved 91% average task progress, significantly higher than PaliGemma-initialized (60%) or randomly initialized (42%) models. Notably, action Mean Squared Error (MSE) on held-out validation data was not a reliable indicator of on-robot performance, as π0.5 and PaliGemma models had similar MSE but diverged sharply in grasping robustness on the robot. This suggests that π0.5's manipulation prior, including error correction behaviors, transferred effectively despite the embodiment gap.
- **Faster Action Generation:** One-step alternatives for action generation (one-step flow matching and drifting model) underperformed the 10-step multi-step flow matching baseline by approximately 20 percentage points in task progress, despite exhibiting lower validation action MSE. This was attributed to single-step inference producing less temporally smooth or jittery actions on the robot. Multi-step flow matching was thus retained.
- **Whole-Body Teleop Data Scaling:** Increasing the number of whole-body demonstrations per task from 5 to 40 improved average task progress from 52% to 91%. The most significant gains occurred between 10 and 20 demonstrations per task, with returns flattening thereafter. 40 demonstrations per task was identified as a data-efficient budget.
 - **Conclusion for Phase II:** The humanoid-adapted VLA, initialized with π0.5, utilizing specific interface settings, and employing multi-step flow matching inference, achieved 89% average task progress on the 8 training tasks, demonstrating generalization of design choices.

**III. Heterogeneous Co-Training:**
- **Stationary Co-Training:** Co-training the VLA with stationary teleoperation data for 4 held-out tasks (40 demonstrations each) effectively extended the policy. It lifted average task progress on these held-out tasks from 33% (8-task baseline) to 87%, closing most of the gap to a 12-task oracle (94%). This approach successfully supplied both new semantic understanding (for new objects and instructions in motion-reuse tasks) and new motion patterns (e.g., vessel tilt for the Pouring task). Stationary teleop was also found to be more time-efficient to collect than full loco-manipulation teleop.
- **HuMI Co-Training:** Co-training with robot-free HuMI demonstrations for the 4 held-out tasks (40 demonstrations each) also improved average task progress to 87%. HuMI data effectively provided new semantic understanding for motion-reuse tasks, matching the performance of stationary co-training. However, it failed to teach new motion patterns (e.g., Pouring task). This limitation was attributed to visual and action domain gaps between robot-free human demonstrations and on-robot teleoperation. HuMI data offered the fastest collection time (7 minutes per task vs. 13 minutes for stationary teleop).
 - **Conclusion for Phase III:** Heterogeneous co-training effectively extends policy capabilities. Stationary co-training provides both new semantics and new motions, while HuMI co-training primarily delivers new semantic understanding at a lower data collection cost.

**System-Level Comparison on Long-Horizon Tasks:**
On a challenging long-horizon, language-conditioned task (Fruit Arrangement) spanning a wide vertical workspace, OpenHLM (HuMI co-training) achieved 87.5% task progress. This significantly outperformed two state-of-the-art humanoid VLAs: Ψ0 (48.8%) and GR00T N1.6 (57.5%). Critically, OpenHLM accomplished this with less than half the total demonstration time (1.14 hours vs. 2.70–2.73 hours). The baseline systems exhibited issues such as weak grasping and inadequate locomotion, despite their pretraining including Unitree G1 humanoid data, suggesting the importance of specific design details.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents OpenHLM, an open-source empirical recipe for whole-body humanoid loco-manipulation, which integrates specific teleoperation practices, VLA design choices, and data co-training strategies. The work's significance lies in several contributions to the field of humanoid robotics:

Firstly, **a systematic empirical roadmap** provides clarity in a previously underexplored design space. By conducting controlled, one-variable-at-a-time experiments, the study systematically quantifies the impact of various design choices across controller/teleoperation, VLA architecture, and data strategies. This methodical approach offers practical insights into effective system construction for whole-body humanoid control.

Secondly, the development of **OpenHLM as a concrete, open-source recipe** directly addresses the need for validated methodologies in this domain. The release of code, training data, and model checkpoints will enable researchers to build upon established findings, accelerate future developments, and ensure reproducibility. This open access facilitates broader community engagement and collaborative progress towards reliable everyday humanoids.

Thirdly, the demonstration of **heterogeneous co-training for data efficiency** represents a notable advancement. The research shows that leveraging cheaper data sources, such as stationary teleoperation and robot-free HuMI demonstrations, can effectively extend robot policy capabilities to new objects and instructions without incurring the high costs of additional whole-body teleoperation. This finding addresses a critical bottleneck in scaling robot learning to a wide range of tasks and environments, making the development of versatile humanoids more feasible. The characterization of what each data stream contributes (e.g., HuMI for semantic understanding, stationary teleop for new motions) further refines strategies for data collection.

Finally, the **benchmark performance of OpenHLM** against established state-of-the-art humanoid VLAs (GR00T N1.6 and Ψ0) on a challenging long-horizon task highlights its effectiveness. Achieving higher task progress with significantly less demonstration time suggests that the empirical design details explored in this paper are more impactful than solely scaling pretraining data with humanoid demonstrations, as seen in some prior works. This shifts the focus towards thoughtful system design and integration.

The potential impact of OpenHLM is to accelerate research and development in humanoid robotics by providing a robust and data-efficient foundation. It offers a practical pathway towards developing humanoids that can perform complex, coordinated tasks in unstructured environments, contributing to advances in areas such as assistive robotics, logistics, and exploration. The insights regarding teleoperation interfaces, VLA adaptation, and data efficiency can guide future architectural designs and training protocols for next-generation humanoid systems.
