# UMI-3D: Extending Universal Manipulation Interface from Vision-Limited to 3D Spatial Perception

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at The University of Hong Kong and the University of Science and Technology of China developed UMI-3D, an enhanced robot demonstration interface that incorporates a lightweight LiDAR sensor for robust 3D spatial perception, overcoming vision-only limitations. This system allows for the reliable collection of data for complex manipulation tasks like curtain pulling and door opening, and policies trained on it show strong generalization across unseen object combinations and maintain compatibility with prior UMI systems.

## Problem

- Monocular visual SLAM in prior robot learning systems, like UMI, is vulnerable to occlusions, textureless environments, and dynamic scene changes, leading to unreliable pose estimation.
- Limitations in data collection robustness hinder the scalability and generalizability of learned robot manipulation policies for diverse real-world tasks.
- Difficulty in acquiring high-quality, metric-consistent, and temporally aligned perception-action data under varied environmental conditions.

## Method

- Integrating a ==lightweight LiDAR sensor== and an ==industrial CMOS camera== into a wrist-mounted, hardware-synchronized multimodal sensor suite on the robot gripper for the **UMI-3D** system.
- Developing a pipeline for ==temporal synchronization==, ==spatial calibration==, and ==LiDAR-inertial odometry== to generate metric-consistent and temporally aligned 3D data.
- Adopting a ==diffusion policy== framework, enhanced with ==low-latency sensing== and ==relative end-effector pose representation==, for learning manipulation skills from the improved data.

## Results

- The LiDAR-centric SLAM system consistently provided stable and drift-resistant pose estimation and 3D map reconstruction even in visually challenging environments (e.g., textureless surfaces, dynamic deformable objects).
- Policies trained with UMI-3D data achieved strong performance on previously difficult tasks, such as curtain pulling (normalized scores **0.88-0.96**) and demonstrated generalization in cup arrangement with unseen object combinations (**0.736** normalized score).
- UMI-3D showed compatibility with policies trained on the original UMI, enabling direct transfer and robust execution for tasks like mouse placement (normalized scores **0.73-1.00**) without retraining.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Replacing monocular visual SLAM with LiDAR-centric SLAM on the robot's gripper profoundly enhances pose estimation robustness and 3D scene reconstruction reliability in challenging environments.
> - Hardware-level synchronization and a unified spatiotemporal calibration framework are critical for fusing multimodal sensor data (LiDAR, camera, IMU) into consistent perception-action pairs.
> - Representing actions as relative end-effector pose trajectories improves policy robustness by accounting for tracking errors and aligning with incremental SLAM state estimation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The research presented in this paper is positioned within the broader field of data-driven robot learning, particularly focusing on the acquisition of high-quality, scalable demonstration data for training visuomotor policies. Recent advancements in this area have emphasized the critical role of extensive and diverse datasets in achieving generalizable robot manipulation skills, drawing parallels to scaling laws observed in large language and vision models. Systems like the Universal Manipulation Interface (UMI) represent a significant step towards enabling low-cost, portable, and infrastructure-free data collection by bridging the embodiment gap between human demonstrations and robot execution.

UMI-3D specifically addresses fundamental limitations inherent in the original UMI framework. The original UMI, while effective, relies on monocular visual SLAM for pose estimation. This reliance makes it vulnerable to issues such as occlusions, dynamic scene changes, textureless environments, and poor lighting, which are common in real-world scenarios. These vulnerabilities limit the diversity and reliability of collectable data, thus constraining the scalability of embodied learning systems. Prior UMI variants have explored external tracking systems (e.g., motion capture) or off-the-shelf visual SLAM modules (e.g., Intel RealSense T265, smartphone ARKit). However, these solutions often compromise portability, introduce external infrastructure dependencies, or suffer from temporal inconsistencies and fundamental limitations of visual SLAM itself.

UMI-3D proposes a paradigm shift by elevating SLAM from an auxiliary component to a core mechanism for ensuring metric-consistent, temporally aligned perception-action data. By integrating a lightweight LiDAR sensor, the work moves from vision-centric to geometry-aware perception, aiming for superior robustness and reliability in pose estimation and 3D scene reconstruction. This approach directly tackles the bottleneck of scalable embodied data collection, enabling the acquisition of data for tasks previously challenging or infeasible for vision-only systems, and thus contributing to the development of more generalist manipulation policies and embodied foundation models. The work also aims to maintain the portability and accessibility that were central to the original UMI design.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of UMI-3D is to enhance the robustness and scalability of data collection for embodied manipulation, building upon the foundation of the Universal Manipulation Interface (UMI). This is motivated by several critical limitations identified in the original UMI system and other vision-centric data acquisition methods:

1. **Vulnerability of Monocular Visual SLAM**: The original UMI's reliance on monocular visual SLAM for pose estimation introduces significant vulnerabilities. It requires environments with rich visual features, performs poorly in textureless or poorly lit regions, and is highly sensitive to occlusions and dynamic scene changes. For instance, tasks involving large objects that block the camera view (e.g., opening doors) often lead to tracking failures or misinterpretations of motion.
2. **Constraints on Data Collection and Scalability**: These limitations impose implicit constraints on the environments, tasks, and interactions that can be reliably captured. This biases data collection towards scenarios favorable for visual SLAM, hindering the robustness and scalability required for learning generalizable policies. It also increases the cost of data processing and curation by necessitating filtering of unreliable data. The paper argues that the bottleneck for scalable embodied data collection lies fundamentally in the reliability of pose estimation.
3. **Need for Metric-Consistent, Temporally Aligned Data**: To truly scale robot learning, the system must provide high-quality, metric-consistent, and temporally aligned perception-action data. The original UMI's visual SLAM often struggles to maintain this consistency under diverse real-world conditions.

To address these motivations, UMI-3D outlines specific key objectives guided by three principles for a robust UMI system:

- **Self-contained Pose Estimation**: The system should rely exclusively on gripper-mounted sensors, eliminating the need for external infrastructure and ensuring full portability.
- **Spatiotemporal Consistency in Multimodal Sensing**: Multimodal perception must maintain strict alignment through accurate time synchronization and precise spatial calibration, ensuring coherent cross-sensor observations.
- **Consistency of Localization Accuracy**: The pose estimation system should maintain stable and consistent accuracy across diverse environments, tasks, and interaction conditions, rather than exhibiting strong performance only under favorable or constrained scenarios.

Based on these, the key objectives of UMI-3D are:

- **Integrate a Lightweight LiDAR Sensor**: To introduce a LiDAR sensor into the wrist-mounted interface for robust, metric-scale pose estimation under challenging conditions (occlusions, textureless regions, illumination variations).
- **Develop a Hardware-Synchronized Multimodal Sensing Pipeline**: To ensure accurate temporal alignment between visual and LiDAR observations, overcoming latency and drift issues of software-based synchronization.
- **Establish a Unified Spatiotemporal Calibration Framework**: To precisely align visual observations with LiDAR point clouds, producing consistent 3D representations of demonstrations.
- **Improve Data Quality and Reliability**: To generate higher-quality and more reliable collected data, reducing the need for environment curation and post-hoc filtering.
- **Expand Feasible Task Distribution**: To enable the learning of manipulation tasks that were previously challenging or infeasible for vision-only UMI, such as large deformable object manipulation and articulated object operation.
- **Maintain Portability and Accessibility**: To preserve the core advantages of the original UMI, including its low cost, portability, and ease of use, while integrating advanced sensing capabilities.
- **Open-Source Hardware and Software**: To facilitate large-scale data collection and accelerate research in embodied intelligence by making the entire system publicly available.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

UMI-3D's methodology involves a comprehensive design across hardware, data acquisition and processing, and policy learning, centered around incorporating LiDAR for robust 3D spatial perception while maintaining the core principles of the UMI framework.

#### 3.1. A. Demonstration Interface Design

UMI-3D retains the original UMI's mechanical design for the trigger-activated, handheld 3D-printed parallel-jaw gripper with soft fingers, emphasizing portability and low cost. The key innovation is a wrist-mounted, hardware-synchronized multimodal sensor suite, replacing the monocular RGB camera with a LiDAR and an industrial CMOS camera.

- **Wrist-mounted Multimodal Sensors**: All sensing modalities (camera, LiDAR, IMU) are co-located and rigidly attached to the gripper. This ensures consistent observation geometry and tight coupling of perception with manipulation, facilitating transfer between human demonstrations and robot execution.
- **Complementary Sensor Roles**: The camera provides rich visual context and appearance information for policy learning, while the LiDAR, combined with LiDAR-based SLAM, offers accurate 3D geometric structure and consistent SE(3) motion estimation.
- **Industrial CMOS Camera**: An industrial Hikrobot MV-CB013-A0UC-S camera is used instead of a consumer-grade GoPro, offering improved integration, lower latency, and greater flexibility for synchronization.
- **Wide-FoV Sensing**: A wider 185° FoV fisheye camera lens is used, consistent with UMI, to capture sufficient visual context during rapid motion. Raw fisheye images are used without undistortion to preserve central resolution and compact peripheral context. This also ensures substantial overlap with the LiDAR FoV.
- **Direct Geometric Sensing**: UMI-3D replaces UMI's implicit depth approximation (side mirrors) with direct metric-scale depth and spatial structure from LiDAR. This enables robust, drift-resistant state estimation through LiDAR-based SLAM.
- **Continuous Gripper Control**: Gripper width is continuously tracked via ArUco markers, enabling implicit recording of grasp forces.
- **Hardware Specifications**: The gripper weighs 1120g, with dimensions L285mm × W175mm × H300mm. Bill of materials (BoM) cost for the 3D-printed gripper is $50, with the Livox MID-360 LiDAR and Hikrobot camera totaling $650.

#### 3.2. B. SLAM and Data Processing Pipeline

This pipeline transforms raw sensor streams into temporally aligned, spatially calibrated, and geometrically consistent representations for policy training.

1. **Temporal Synchronization (SP1)**: A hardware-level synchronization mechanism is implemented using an STM32 microcontroller as a central clock source. It generates a 1 Hz pulse-per-second (PPS) signal and a pseudo-GPRMC message for LiDAR synchronization, and a 20 Hz trigger signal for the camera via frequency division. LiDAR streams 10 Hz point clouds over Ethernet, and the camera captures 20 Hz RGB images. Timestamps are shared between ROS drivers and recorded in a unified rosbag format, ensuring accurate temporal correspondence.
2. **Sensors Calibration (SP2)**:
 - **Fisheye Camera Intrinsic Calibration (SP2.1)**: The camera is modeled using the equidistant projection model for ultra-wide-angle lenses. Intrinsic parameters (focal length, principal point, distortion coefficients) are estimated by minimizing reprojection error of a planar checkerboard target. This enables accurate pixel-to-ray mapping, undistortion, and ArUco marker detection.
 - **LiDAR-Camera Extrinsic Calibration (SP2.2)**: A tailored calibration module, `livox2cam`, is developed. It uses a structured calibration target with circular holes and fiducial markers. 3D positions of hole centers are estimated in the LiDAR frame (via edge-based geometric fitting) and the camera frame (via marker-based pose recovery). The rigid transformation between LiDAR and camera (LTC) is found by solving a 3D-3D alignment problem via SVD. The module incorporates scan-pattern-agnostic edge extraction, ellipse-based fitting, and multi-frame joint optimization for robustness. LTC enables camera trajectory recovery from LiDAR-SLAM and accurate projection of LiDAR points onto the image plane for fusion.
3. **LiDAR-Inertial Odometry (SP3)**:
 - **ESIKF on Differentiable Manifolds (SP3.1)**: An iterated error-state Kalman filter (ESIKF) on differentiable manifolds is used for consistent and drift-resistant state estimation. The system state is defined on a manifold including IMU pose, velocity, biases, gravity, and IMU-to-LiDAR extrinsic transformation. A continuous-time kinematic model driven by IMU measurements performs high-frequency motion prediction.
 - **LiDAR-centric Measurement Model (SP3.2)**: To correct the predicted state, a measurement model based on voxelized probabilistic plane features is used. LiDAR points are registered against the map, and point-to-plane residuals are computed. Residual covariance accounts for both point-wise and plane-wise uncertainties, and Mahalanobis distance gating ensures robust data association. This approach supports consistent 3D map construction.
 - **Coordinate System Convention (SP3.3)**: The first IMU frame is set as the global reference. All states are estimated incrementally. Fixed transformations between IMU and LiDAR (ITL, factory calibrated), LiDAR and camera (LTC, calibrated), and LiDAR and tool center point (LTCP, mechanical design) define a consistent chain connecting perception and action spaces.
4. **Data Representation and Packaging for Policy Learning (SP4)**: Raw ROS bag recordings are transformed into a compressed Zarr-based replay buffer for diffusion policy training.
 - **Multi-sensor synchronization and video alignment (SP4.1)**: Camera frames are extracted as MP4 videos with timestamps. Each LiDAR frame is associated with its nearest camera observations within a temporal window.
 - **LiDAR-inertial trajectory association (SP4.2)**: The LiDAR-inertial odometry estimates the sensor trajectory (camera trajectory.csv), which is temporally consistent with video frames.
 - **Frame-level alignment and state extraction (SP4.3)**: SLAM poses and auxiliary signals are aligned with video timestamps via nearest-neighbor matching. ArUco markers detect object and gripper states (e.g., end-effector pose, gripper width). All aligned signals form episodic trajectories.
 - **Replay buffer construction (SP4.4)**: The aligned dataset is converted into a Zarr-based replay buffer containing synchronized RGB images, end-effector poses, rotations, and gripper states.

#### 3.3. C. Policy Learning

UMI-3D adopts a diffusion policy framework similar to the original UMI, with modifications for LiDAR-enhanced data and a low-latency pipeline.

- **Observation and Action Latency Alignment (PD1)**:
 - **Low-latency visual sensing (PD1.1)**: Industrial CMOS camera with direct USB interface reduces sensing and transmission latency.
 - **Action latency matching (PD1.2)**: Robot arm and gripper are controlled via a unified CAN bus, ensuring consistent actuation latency across all degrees of freedom.
- **Relative End-Effector Pose Representation (PD2)**:
 - **Relative EE trajectory as action representation (PD2.1)**: Action sequences are represented as relative SE(3) transformations to the current EE pose, improving robustness to tracking errors.
 - **Relative EE trajectory as proprioception (PD2.2)**: Historical EE poses are represented as a relative trajectory, implicitly providing velocity information. This aligns with the incremental SLAM state estimation.
- **Policy Training and Deployment (PD3)**: A diffusion policy is trained on the Zarr-based replay buffer, taking RGB observations and proprioceptive states to predict future EE poses. During deployment, a receding horizon control strategy with temporal ensembling executes predicted trajectories.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of UMI-3D encompassed three aspects: SLAM robustness and data quality, capability/generalization, and cross-embodiment compatibility, across four real-world manipulation tasks.

#### 4.1. A. Robust SLAM for Reliable Data Collection

- **Observation**: UMI-3D's LiDAR-centric SLAM system demonstrated stable and drift-resistant pose estimation and consistent 3D map reconstruction in challenging real-world scenarios. These conditions included a textureless white wall, rapid curtain pulling with a large deformable object under strong illumination changes, and a long-horizon task involving articulated objects and dynamic occlusions. These conditions are typically problematic for vision-only SLAM due to lack of texture, non-rigid motion, and drastic illumination shifts.
- **Implication**: The robustness of SLAM ensures the collection of consistently high-quality, well-aligned image-action pairs, which is critical for learning reliable manipulation policies. The resulting metric-consistent 3D maps also facilitate quality inspection and cross-sequence data alignment.

#### 4.2. B.1. Cup Arrangement

- **Task**: Grasping a cup and placing it onto a saucer. This served as a pick-and-place benchmark to evaluate manipulation and generalization.
- **Setup**: 3,500 UMI-3D demonstrations. Diffusion policy with CLIP ViT-L/14 encoder. Evaluation in unseen environments across 64 object combinations (8 cups, 8 saucers), with 10 trials per combination (640 total runs).
- **Metric**: Two-stage score (grasping 0-3, placement 0-3), max 6 per trial, reported as normalized scores.
- **Results**:
 - **Seen object pairs**: Average normalized score of 0.863.
 - **Partially unseen combinations**: Performance moderately decreased to 0.788.
 - **Fully unseen combinations**: Score of 0.736.
 - The policy demonstrated strong performance and graceful degradation under distribution shift, suggesting generalization beyond memorizing specific object instances.
- **Failure Analysis**: A brown saucer consistently yielded the lowest scores. The policy often grasped the cup and reached the correct region but hesitated to release or placed it incorrectly. This was attributed to visual ambiguity, low contrast, and deviation from training data distribution, leading to uncertainty in fine-grained placement.

#### 4.3. B.2. Curtain Pulling

- **Task**: Grasping the edge of a curtain and pulling it open, involving large deformable objects and thin, low-texture structures under varying lighting.
- **Setup**: 769 UMI-3D demonstrations. Diffusion policy with DINOv2 ViT-L/14 encoder. Three curtain types in training. Evaluation under diverse lighting (strong illumination, backlit), 120 trials.
- **Metric**: Two-stage score (grasping 0-2, pulling 0-2), max 4 per trial, reported as normalized scores.
- **Results**: Achieved strong performance across all curtain types, with normalized scores of 0.88, 0.90, and 0.96. The policy was robust to significant variations in lighting and appearance.
- **Failure Analysis**: Failures occurred under strong backlighting or low contrast, where distinguishing the curtain edge was difficult, leading to unstable or suboptimal grasping.
- **Implication**: This task highlights UMI-3D's advantage in handling deformable object manipulation under challenging visual conditions. The LiDAR-based pose estimation during data collection enables reliable image-action pair alignment even when visual-only SLAM would fail, allowing a purely visual policy at inference time to succeed.

#### 4.4. B.3. Door Opening and Cup Placement

- **Task**: Sequentially opening a sliding door, grasping a cup inside, and placing it onto a nearby saucer. This is a long-horizon task involving fine-grained interaction with an articulated object, object transfer, and precise placement.
- **Setup**: 340 UMI-3D demonstrations. Diffusion policy with DINOv2 ViT-L/14 encoder. Evaluation in the same environment, 40 trials.
- **Metric**: Three-stage score (door opening 0-2, cup grasping 0-2, placement 0-2), max 6 per trial, reported as normalized scores.
- **Results**:
 - **Door opening**: High success rate of 97.5%.
 - **Cup grasping**: Success rate of 47.5%.
 - **Final placement**: Success rate of 5.0%.
 - A Sankey diagram illustrated clear failure propagation, where early-stage errors compounded and prevented downstream success.
- **Failure Analysis**:
 - 32.5% of trials failed during placement due to violations of the robot’s inverse kinematics constraints, suggesting a mismatch between demonstrated motions and the robot's feasible configuration space.
 - Lower grasping success was attributed to limited and less diverse training data compared to the cup arrangement task.

#### 4.5. B.4. Cross-Embodiment Transfer: Mouse Placement

- **Task**: Grasping a mouse and placing it onto a mouse pad. This assessed whether policies trained with the original UMI could be deployed on UMI-3D hardware without retraining.
- **Setup**: Pretrained policy from prior UMI work. Direct deployment on UMI-3D. Evaluation in unseen environments with 16 object combinations (4 mice, 4 pads), 5 trials per combination (80 total trials).
- **Metric**: Two-stage score (grasping 0-3, placement 0-3), max 6 per trial, reported as normalized scores.
- **Results**: Consistently strong performance, with normalized scores ranging from 0.73 to 1.00 across all object combinations.
- **Implication**: UMI-3D maintains compatibility with policies trained using the original UMI, indicating a closely aligned visual observation space despite hardware differences. This suggests potential for joint training across datasets.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The UMI-3D system presents several significant contributions and potential impacts on the field of embodied intelligence and robot learning:

1. **Enhanced Data Quality and Reliability**: By integrating LiDAR-centric SLAM, UMI-3D overcomes critical limitations of vision-only systems, such as vulnerability to occlusions, dynamic scenes, and textureless environments. This results in the collection of higher-quality, more reliable, and metric-consistent demonstration data, which is crucial for the performance and generalization of visuomotor policies.
2. **Expanded Task Capabilities**: The improved robustness of data collection directly translates into the ability to learn policies for tasks previously challenging or infeasible with the original UMI. Examples include manipulation of large deformable objects (curtains) and interactions with large articulated objects (doors), which involve conditions that typically cause visual SLAM failures. This broadens the scope of real-world manipulation tasks that can be addressed through data-driven approaches.
3. **Foundation for Scalable Embodied Learning**: UMI-3D positions SLAM as a core mechanism for aligning perception and action in a unified metric space. This principled approach addresses a fundamental bottleneck in scaling embodied data collection, providing key infrastructure for advancing next-generation embodied foundation models, which rely on large-scale, diverse, and well-aligned interaction data.
4. **Preservation of UMI's Strengths**: Despite significant hardware and algorithmic enhancements, UMI-3D maintains the core advantages of the original UMI, including portability, low cost, and infrastructure-free deployment. This ensures that the advanced capabilities are accessible for widespread use in various environments.
5. **Cross-Embodiment Compatibility**: The demonstrated ability for policies trained on the original UMI to transfer effectively to UMI-3D hardware without retraining suggests a consistent and compatible observation space. This could enable leveraging existing UMI datasets and potentially combining data from different UMI variants, further accelerating data scaling efforts.
6. **Open-Source Contribution**: The commitment to open-sourcing all hardware and software components significantly facilitates broader adoption and accelerates research in embodied intelligence. This lowers the barrier for other researchers to replicate, extend, and contribute to the system, fostering community-driven advancements.

**Potential Future Impact**:
- **More Robust Robot Skills**: By enabling the collection of data in more diverse and challenging real-world conditions, UMI-3D can lead to the development of robot policies that are inherently more robust and generalizable to unstructured environments.
- **Advancements in Large-Scale Datasets**: The system's scalability and reliability support the creation of larger, higher-fidelity datasets essential for training generalist manipulation policies, mirroring the success seen in other AI domains.
- **Integration of 3D Perception**: While current policies primarily use visual input during inference, UMI-3D's capacity to capture synchronized 3D geometric information opens avenues for directly incorporating this rich data into policy learning, leading to more geometry-aware and precise manipulation.
- **Foundation for Mobile Manipulation**: The high-fidelity data collection capabilities of UMI-3D could extend to mobile manipulation scenarios, integrating locomotion and manipulation into unified pipelines for robots operating in larger, less structured environments.

In summary, UMI-3D represents a significant methodological and practical advancement in robot learning data collection, addressing a critical bottleneck for scaling embodied intelligence in real-world settings.
