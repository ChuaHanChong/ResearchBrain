# Checklists Are Better Than Reward Models For Aligning Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Reinforcement Learning from Checklist Feedback (RLCF) proposes automatically generating instruction-specific checklists to align large language models. This method consistently improved instruction-following capabilities across five diverse benchmarks, achieving a 4-point absolute boost on FollowBench and a 6-point absolute increase on InFoBench, while requiring no human annotation.

## Problem

- Existing LLM alignment methods, particularly RLHF with fixed reward models, struggle to handle subjective and open-ended instruction-following tasks effectively.
- Fixed reward models often lead to 'reward hacking,' where models optimize for an arbitrary reward signal instead of the true underlying objective.
- Current automatic evaluation approaches, even with prompted AI judges, typically rely on global criteria that fail to capture the nuanced, instruction-specific requirements of diverse user requests.

## Method

- Introduced Reinforcement Learning from ==Checklist Feedback== (**RLCF**), an approach that automatically generates flexible, instruction-specific checklists as granular feedback for LLMs.
- Developed a novel 'candidate-based' method for checklist generation, which first samples diverse responses and then prompts a powerful LLM to extract all possible failure modes (checklist items) from these candidates.
- Utilized a hybrid scoring system combining LLM-based numerical scores and program verification for individual checklist items, then applied Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) with filtered preference pairs for model fine-tuning.

## Results

- RLCF demonstrated consistent performance gains across all five evaluated benchmarks (IFEval, InFoBench, FollowBench, AlpacaEval, Arena-Hard), unlike other baselines that showed mixed improvements and regressions.
- Achieved a 4-point absolute increase in hard satisfaction rate on FollowBench (**5.4%** relative improvement) and a 6-point absolute increase in overall requirement following ratio on InFoBench (**6.9%** relative improvement).
- Showcased off-policy effectiveness, improving instruction-following capabilities for other model families (Llama 3.1 **8B** Instruct and OLMo 2 **7B** Instruct) using Qwen2.5-generated checklists.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Instruction-specific and granular checklist feedback provides a more robust and uniformly effective learning signal for LLMs compared to generic reward models or broadly prompted AI judges.
> - The candidate-based checklist generation method produces higher quality (more objective, atomic, and comprehensive) checklists than direct extraction, leading to improved downstream RL performance.
> - RLCF particularly enhances model performance on 'content' constraints, indicating it encourages models to attend to the full instruction rather than just keywords, fostering more thorough instruction following.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper addresses a critical challenge in the development of useful and reliable large language models: ensuring they accurately understand and follow diverse user instructions. Currently, the universal approach for aligning LLMs to user instructions involves a two-step process: instruction finetuning (IFT), where models learn to mimic human-generated responses, followed by reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). While IFT imbues models with initial instruction-following capabilities, RLHF is typically employed as a refinement step, primarily to mitigate undesirable behaviors like harmfulness, using predefined criteria.

However, the authors identify a significant limitation in the current application of RLHF: its difficulty in handling "ambiguous" or "non-verifiable" tasks, such as complex instruction following, where criteria for success are subjective and instruction-specific. Traditional RLHF often relies on fixed reward models (RMs) that score responses based on broad, predefined attributes like "helpfulness" or "harmfulness." This approach can lead to several issues:
1. **Limited Scope:** Fixed criteria cannot cover the multitude of nuanced requirements found in diverse user instructions.
2. **Arbitrary Rewards and Reward Hacking:** Reward models can develop an arbitrary notion of "good" responses, leading to reward hacking where models optimize for the learned reward signal rather than the true underlying objective.
3. **Generator-Verifier Gap:** When a larger model is prompted to act as a judge (AI feedback), it must infer what aspects to grade, which might not be comprehensive or aligned with the specific requirements of a given instruction. Even with multiple criteria-specific prompts (e.g., UltraFeedback), these criteria remain global rather than instruction-specific.

This paper positions itself as a solution to this problem by proposing "Reinforcement Learning from Checklist Feedback" (RLCF). RLCF seeks to make RL a general-purpose solution for eliciting desirable behaviors in subjective and open-ended settings by generating *flexible, instruction-specific* criteria in the form of checklists. This contrasts with approaches that use only verifiable answers, fixed reward models, or broadly prompted AI judges. The authors frame checklist feedback as an "extreme mixture-of-evaluators," where a unique subset of granular, prompted evaluators is chosen for each instruction, allowing for unprecedented flexibility and specificity in the reward signal.

The work also relates to a nascent line of research exploring the use of rubrics for LLM evaluation and alignment. While previous work has used model-generated checklists for inference-time evaluation or to improve constrained reasoning, this paper is among the first to apply dynamic, instruction-specific checklist generation directly to reinforcement learning for fine-tuning. It differentiates itself from contemporaneous works by generating instruction-specific rubrics for DPO training, unlike systems that use global, fixed sets of criteria.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to **broaden the impact of reinforcement learning in eliciting instruction following by moving beyond fixed evaluation criteria to flexible, instruction-specific feedback.** The authors aim to transform RL into a more versatile tool capable of handling the nuances of subjective and open-ended instruction-following tasks.

This primary goal is driven by a central research question: "how can we grade responses to instructions in a manner that is automatic (requires no human annotation), flexible (considers all aspects of response quality), intuitive (aligned with perceptible differences in responses), and applicable to any instruction or response, to enable more better language model alignment?"

To address this, the authors are motivated to develop an approach that offers several advantages over existing methods:
- **Automaticity:** Eliminate the need for costly and time-consuming human annotation for feedback.
- **Flexibility:** Adapt evaluation criteria dynamically to the unique demands of each instruction.
- **Intuitiveness:** Ensure the evaluation criteria are transparent and clearly aligned with human perception of response quality, reducing "black box" reward signals.
- **Applicability:** Create a method that works for any instruction and corresponding response, regardless of complexity or domain.

The proposed solution, RLCF, is motivated by the idea that reducing the task of grading responses to answering a series of "yes/no" questions (via a checklist) allows for objective and automatic evaluation by either AI judges or specialized verification programs. This granular, instruction-specific approach is hypothesized to provide a stronger, more targeted learning signal for LLMs, ultimately leading to superior instruction-following capabilities.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the RLCF methodology lies in automatically generating instruction-specific checklists and using these detailed rubrics to compute reward signals for reinforcement learning.

#### 3.1. Checklist Generation

The authors define a checklist as a sequence of requirements paired with an instruction, where each requirement is a yes/no question, answered relative to a candidate response, and collectively comprehensive enough to determine response acceptability. They emphasize desiderata for these checklists: comprehensiveness, naturalness, objectivity, and atomicity, prioritizing objectivity and atomicity to facilitate automatic verification and reduce false positive rewards.

Two methods for checklist extraction were explored:
- **Direct:** Prompting an LLM (Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct) directly to extract criteria from an instruction. This method was found to be more natural but less objective, atomic, and comprehensive.
- **Candidate-based:** This novel two-stage approach involves:
 1. Generating diverse responses of varying quality (using Qwen2.5 models of different sizes) to an instruction.
 2. Prompting a powerful LLM (Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct) to identify all possible failure modes (i.e., checklist items) from these responses. For each item, an "importance" weight (0-100) is also generated.
 This candidate-based method proved superior in intrinsic evaluations for objectivity, atomicity, and overall quality, and was adopted for downstream RL training.

To prevent reward hacking (e.g., models generating long preambles to satisfy criteria), the authors introduced **regularization via universal criteria**, adding two general requirements to all checklists: "The response directly address the request without excessive or off-topic information not necessary..." and "The response should match the context and the instruction, whether it requires professionalism, friendliness, formality, or neutrality."

The chosen candidate-based method was used to generate **WildChecklists**, a dataset of 130,000 instructions from WildChat, each with its corresponding synthetically generated checklist. Where applicable, verification programs were also generated for checklist items.

#### 3.2. Reinforcement Learning from Checklist Feedback (RLCF) Pipeline

RLCF involves a four-step process to generate high-quality preference data for DPO:
1. **Sampling Candidate Responses:** The base policy (e.g., Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct) samples pairs of responses using a temperature of 1.3 and top-p of 0.9.
2. **Flexible Scoring:** For each prompt, response, and individual checklist item:
 - An LLM judge (Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct) is prompted to generate a numerical score (0-100) for the item. This score is averaged over 25 samples from the model to increase robustness.
 - For hard, discrete criteria (e.g., "does the response contain 3 commas?"), a program verifier is generated by the LM (using a specialized prompt, Appendix B) and executed. The program's boolean result (0 or 100) is then averaged with the AI judge's score. The program generator abstains if not highly confident in exact verification.
3. **Preference Tuning:**
 - Per-item scores for each response are combined into a single weighted checklist score, using the importance weights.
 - From the sampled response pairs, only the 40% with the greatest difference along *at least one criterion* of their checklist are selected. This filtering step aims to provide a stronger pairwise signal for DPO by discarding overly similar responses.
 - **Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)** is then applied, using these filtered preference pairs to finetune the base language model.

#### 3.3. Experimental Details

- **Training Data:** WildChat, filtered for English, non-toxic, and two-turn conversations.
- **Models:** Qwen2.5-7B and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct were finetuned. Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct served as the teacher model for AI judgments and checklist generation.
- **Training:** 2 epochs of DPO, batch size 1024, max sequence length 2048, cosine learning rate schedule (3e-6 to 2e-6). Training on one 8xH100 node took approximately 3 hours per model.
- **Benchmarks:** Evaluation was performed on five widely-studied benchmarks: IFEval, InFoBench, and FollowBench (for fine-grained constrained instruction following), and AlpacaEval and Arena-Hard (for general conversational assistance).

#### 3.4. Baselines

The RLCF approach was compared against several state-of-the-art baselines:
- **Instruction Finetuning (SFT):** Distilling knowledge from Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct.
- **Reward Models (RMs):** Using leading reward models like Skywork/Skywork-Reward-Gemma-2-27B and ArmoRM-Llama3-8B-v0.1 for preference labeling.
- **Prompted AI Judges:** Using the same teacher model as a judge, either via "Ultrafeedback" (rating across four global aspects) or a single "AI Judge" prompt (similar to RLCF but without rubrics).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments yielded several key findings that strongly support the efficacy of RLCF:

- **Consistent Performance Gains Across All Benchmarks:** RLCF was the *only method* among the baselines to show consistent improvements across *all five benchmarks* (IFEval, InFoBench, FollowBench, AlpacaEval, Arena-Hard).
 - It achieved a 4-point absolute boost in hard satisfaction rate on FollowBench (5.4% relative improvement), a 6-point absolute increase in overall requirement following ratio on InFoBench (6.9% relative improvement), and a 3-point absolute rise in win rate on Arena-Hard (6.4% relative improvement) for Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct.
 - These results are highlighted in Tables 2, 3, and 4, where RLCF consistently shows positive (blue) improvements, unlike the mixed (blue, orange, gray) results of other methods.

- **Superiority Over Other Automatic Evaluators:** While off-the-shelf reward models like Skywork and ArmoRM showed strong gains on *some* benchmarks (e.g., InFoBench, AlpacaEval), they often led to notable regressions on others (e.g., IFEval, FollowBench). Prompted AI judges also demonstrated mixed results. This underscores RLCF's advantage in providing a more reliable and uniformly effective feedback signal. The paper also notes that high reward model accuracy (on RewardBench, Table 5) does not necessarily correlate with RLHF efficacy, reinforcing the value of RLCF's approach.

- **Importance of Candidate-Based Checklist Generation:** The internal evaluation of checklist generation methods showed that candidate-based checklists were superior in objectivity, atomicity, and comprehensiveness. This translated directly to improved downstream RL performance, with RLCF using candidate-based checklists consistently outperforming those generated directly (Table 6).

- **RLCF Excels at "Content" Constraints:** Analysis on FollowBench revealed that RLCF particularly helped with "content" constraints, which are open-ended qualifiers limiting the valid answer space (Table 7). This suggests that RLCF incentivizes models to attend to the *full instruction* rather than just influential keywords, leading to more thorough instruction following. Qualitative examples (Table 8) further demonstrated how RLCF's interpretable, stable, and fine-grained scores surpassed the overly sensitive or granular evaluations of other methods.

- **Modest Trade-offs in Generality but Off-Policy Efficacy:** While RLCF's training on WildChat (focused on daily assistance) led to minor performance reductions on out-of-domain tasks like safety (XSTest), math (GSM8K), and hallucination prevention (TruthfulQA) (Table 9), the authors argue this is a manageable trade-off. Crucially, RLCF demonstrated **off-policy effectiveness**, improving instruction-following capabilities for other model families (Llama 3.1 8B Instruct and OLMo 2 7B Instruct) using Qwen2.5-generated checklists (Table 10). This indicates that the generated checklists capture universal, transferable criteria for instruction following.

- **Computational Cost Considerations:** The scoring process, involving 25 samples per criterion from a 72B-parameter judge, is computationally intensive. However, experiments showed that reducing the number of samples to 5 (a 55% reduction in clock time) largely retained efficacy, especially for simpler criteria (Figure 4), suggesting pathways for optimization.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Checklists Are Better Than Reward Models For Aligning Language Models" paper introduces a highly significant and potentially transformative approach to LLM alignment.

- **Paradigm Shift in Alignment:** RLCF proposes a fundamental shift from generic, fixed evaluation criteria to dynamic, instruction-specific rubrics. This represents a more nuanced and powerful way to guide LLM behavior, moving beyond the limitations of traditional RLHF and broad AI judges. It highlights that *how* feedback is structured and provided is as crucial as the feedback itself.

- **Improved Instruction Following:** The consistent and substantial performance gains across a diverse set of instruction-following benchmarks are a major impact. This directly addresses one of the most critical aspects of LLM utility: their ability to reliably and precisely execute user commands. As LLMs are increasingly integrated into complex tasks, their capacity for robust instruction following becomes paramount.

- **Enhanced Interpretability and Control:** Checklists offer an inherently more interpretable reward signal. Researchers and developers can understand *why* a response was rated positively or negatively (which specific criteria were met or missed), enabling more targeted model debugging and iterative improvement. This contrasts with the often opaque nature of aggregate reward model scores.

- **Scalability and Adaptability:** By eliminating the need for human annotation for feedback, RLCF is inherently more scalable and adaptable to diverse languages, domains, and evolving user needs. While the teacher model inference is costly, the lack of reliance on human-labeled preference data makes it a more flexible solution in the long run, especially as teacher models become more efficient. The demonstrated off-policy efficacy further expands its reach, suggesting a generalizable method not tied to a specific model family.

- **Catalyst for Future Research:** The paper's findings directly challenge the conventional wisdom regarding reward model design and effectiveness. It opens exciting avenues for future research into combining checklist-style feedback with trainable judges, motivating the development of "better reward models" that incorporate structured, granular criteria. The release of **WildChecklists**, models, and code is a crucial contribution, providing a valuable resource for the research community to replicate, extend, and innovate upon RLCF.

In summary, RLCF offers a practical, effective, and principled method for enhancing LLM alignment. Its ability to generate granular, interpretable, and instruction-specific feedback marks a significant step forward in making language models more reliable, controllable, and truly useful across a wide array of applications.
