# Spectral Distillation: From Nonlinear Dynamics to Linear State-Space Models

> [!summary] Summary
> The "Nonlinear-to-LDS Distillation" pipeline learns compact linear state-space models from nonlinear dynamical systems by first leveraging convex optimization for implicit spectral prediction and then provably distilling these into explicit recurrent Linear Dynamical Systems. This approach achieves higher prediction accuracy and efficiency compared to direct training of LDS baselines on long-memory systems and complex MuJoCo control tasks.

## Problem

- Directly learning nonlinear state-space models or their optimal linear approximations involves non-convex system identification, which is challenging and lacks strong theoretical guarantees.
- Traditional system identification methods for Linear Dynamical Systems (LDS) typically assume data originates from a linear system, leaving a gap for robustly identifying LDS representations from nonlinear data.
- Existing spectral filtering methods (e.g., OSF) learn implicit predictors but do not directly yield explicit, recurrent LDS models, while recent sequence models prioritize architectural innovations over provable guarantees for nonlinear data.

## Method

- A two-stage "**Nonlinear-to-LDS Distillation**" pipeline is introduced, comprising an initial spectral learning phase followed by a structured distillation step.
- The first stage uses ==Observation Spectral Filtering (OSF)==, a convex method, to learn an implicit convolutional predictor by fitting readout parameters in a fixed temporal feature space.
- The second stage, **SpectraLDS**, distills the learned implicit OSF predictor into an explicit recurrent ==Linear Dynamical System (LDS)== by approximating spectral features with simple recurrent units.

## Results

- The distilled LDS achieved prediction errors **three orders of magnitude** lower than Adam-trained baselines on challenging long-memory linear systems with slow impulse response decay.
- On MuJoCo behavior cloning tasks, the "train-then-distill" model attained the highest mean reward on **four out of six** environments, outperforming directly trained Diagonal LDS baselines (e.g., by **1,700 reward points** on Walker2d).
- The SpectraLDS distillation process introduced negligible error, with a maximum relative error of less than **1.5%** between the raw spectral predictor and its distilled LDS representation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decoupling the learning of an implicit predictor (via convex OSF) from its explicit state-space representation allows for theoretically stronger guarantees and avoids non-convex optimization in the primary learning phase.
> - The prediction error of the distilled LDS has a 'dimension-free' guarantee, depending on the Luenberger complexity (Q∗Q^*Q∗) of the best linear observer rather than the potentially large latent dimension of the nonlinear system.
> - Implicit convolutional spectral features, learned through convex optimization, can be provably and faithfully converted into compact, explicit recurrent LDS models with negligible distillation error.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The modeling and learning of dynamical systems are foundational to areas such as sequence modeling and control. Linear Dynamical Systems (LDS) are widely recognized for their compact state-space representations, computational efficiency in simulation, and well-developed spectral theory, which aids in analysis and algorithm design. However, many real-world systems exhibit nonlinear dynamics. Learning either a direct nonlinear state-space model or even an optimal linear approximation often involves non-convex system identification problems, which are challenging to solve reliably.

Traditional system identification methods typically aim to fit state-space parameters (A, B, C) directly, a process known to be non-convex. While recent advancements have yielded non-asymptotic guarantees for LDS estimation under specific conditions, these results typically assume that the data originates from a linear system, leaving the problem of identifying LDS representations from nonlinear data open.

Alternative approaches include spectral filtering methods, such as Observation Spectral Filtering (OSF), which learn implicit predictors in a fixed convolutional feature space, thereby bypassing direct non-convex state-space identification. Another line of research involves Koopman operator methods, which approximate nonlinear dynamics with a linear operator in an infinite-dimensional observable space. Techniques like Extended Dynamic Mode Decomposition (EDMD) and deep Koopman embeddings operate within this framework, often relying on chosen observable dictionaries and providing largely asymptotic or empirical guarantees.

More recent developments in sequence modeling have introduced structured state-space models like S4, S5, LRU, and Mamba, demonstrating that linear recurrences can achieve performance comparable to or exceeding Transformer architectures on long-sequence tasks. These works primarily focus on architectural innovations and empirical performance rather than providing provable system identification guarantees for nonlinear data-generating processes.

This work addresses the challenge of extracting a compact linear state-space representation from nonlinear dynamical systems without directly engaging in non-convex system identification. It bridges the gap between convex learning methods for implicit predictors and the generation of explicit, recurrent LDS models, offering a provable end-to-end pipeline. The approach integrates OSF for initial convex learning with Spectral-to-LDS distillation (SpectraLDS) for conversion to an explicit recurrent form, aiming to combine the benefits of convex optimization with the interpretability and efficiency of LDS models.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to ascertain whether a simple linear dynamical system can reliably represent a nonlinear one, without the necessity of solving a non-convex system identification problem. This inquiry is motivated by several factors:

1. **Computational Tractability and Provable Guarantees:** Directly learning nonlinear state-space models or even their optimal linear approximations is inherently a non-convex optimization problem. Such problems are often susceptible to local optima and lack strong theoretical guarantees for convergence or solution quality. The researchers seek a methodology that relies predominantly on convex optimization, which offers stronger theoretical guarantees and is generally more computationally stable.
2. **Compact and Interpretable Representations:** Linear Dynamical Systems (LDS) provide compact, interpretable state-space representations. These models are amenable to efficient simulation, analysis through spectral theory, and integration into control systems. The goal is to retain these advantages even when the underlying system is nonlinear.
3. **Separation of Learning and Representation:** The authors propose to decouple the learning process from the explicit state-space representation. Instead of attempting to fit the parameters of a state-space model directly, the initial phase focuses on learning an implicit predictor that performs well against observable data. This separation allows for the use of convex learning techniques in the prediction phase, deferring the structural representation challenge to a subsequent distillation step.
4. **Dimension-Free Guarantees:** Traditional system identification methods often exhibit sample complexity that scales polynomially with the latent state dimension of the system. For complex nonlinear systems, this latent dimension can be prohibitively large. A key motivation is to develop a method where performance guarantees are independent of the potentially high-dimensional latent state of the nonlinear system, instead depending on an intrinsic measure of observability complexity (Luenberger complexity).
5. **End-to-End Provable Pipeline:** The ambition is to construct a complete pipeline, from observations of an unknown nonlinear system to a compact linear state-space model, with theoretical guarantees at each stage. This includes guarantees for the initial learning phase and for the subsequent distillation step.

In summary, the core motivation is to provide a theoretically sound, computationally feasible, and empirically effective method for approximating complex nonlinear dynamics with compact, explicit linear models, leveraging the strengths of convex optimization and spectral methods.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology involves a two-stage pipeline termed "Nonlinear-to-LDS Distillation," designed to convert observations from an unknown nonlinear system into a compact linear dynamical system. This process relies on convex optimization followed by a structured distillation step.

**Stage 1: Spectral Learning using Observation Spectral Filtering (OSF)**

The initial stage focuses on learning an implicit predictor without attempting to recover latent states directly. This is achieved through Observation Spectral Filtering (OSF), a convex method introduced in prior work.

1. **Linear Systems as Convolutional Predictors:** The foundation is the understanding that a Linear Dynamical System (LDS) can be described by its impulse response, which is a sequence of matrices (CAℓB). Directly learning these impulse responses is challenging due to the non-convex mapping from the state matrix A to its powers. Spectral filtering addresses this by learning in a fixed convolutional feature space.
2. **Spectral Filtering Basis:** The method utilizes a fixed temporal feature space derived from the Hankel matrix. Specifically, a set of `k` spectral filters ($\phi_1, \dots, \phi_k$) are obtained from the top eigenvectors of a Hankel spectral basis matrix, which approximates length-L geometric responses. These filters are fixed in advance.
3. **OSF Predictor Structure:** For a given time `t`, OSF predicts the output $\hat{y}_{OSF_t}$ using both the control history ($u_{t-1:t-L}$) and the observation history ($y_{t-1:t-L}$). The predictor takes the form of a linear combination of:
 - Finite-memory autoregressive (AR) terms over recent inputs ($u_{t-r}$) and observations ($y_{t-r}$).
 - Spectral features derived from convolutions of the fixed spectral filters with the input history ($U^{(u)}_{t,j} = \langle \phi_j, u_{t-1:t-L} \rangle$) and observation history ($U^{(y)}_{t,j} = \langle \phi_j, y_{t-1:t-L} \rangle$).
 The readout matrices (J, M, P, N) for these terms are the trainable parameters.
4. **Convex Learning:** Since the spectral features are fixed, the prediction is linear in these trainable parameters. Consequently, learning these parameters using a squared loss function remains a convex optimization problem. This is a critical aspect, as it avoids the non-convexities typically encountered in direct system identification.
5. **Competition with Best Linear Observer:** OSF acts as an "improper learner" for nonlinear dynamics. It does not explicitly model the nonlinear system but learns a predictor that competes with the best finite-dimensional linear observer capable of explaining the data over a given horizon. Its regret bounds depend on the Luenberger complexity ($Q^*$) of this best observer, which is an intrinsic measure of observability, rather than the (potentially high) latent dimension of the nonlinear system or its linear lift.

**Stage 2: Spectral-to-LDS Distillation**

The learned OSF predictor is an implicit convolutional model. The second stage converts this implicit predictor into an explicit recurrent Linear Dynamical System (LDS).

1. **Geometric Filters and Recurrence:** SpectraLDS leverages the observation that convolutional features from spectral filters can be approximated by linear combinations of states from simple recurrent units. For chosen modes $\alpha_i \in [-1, 1]$, a recurrence $z^{(s,i)}_{t+1} = \alpha_i z^{(s,i)}_t + s_t$ implicitly generates a geometric filter $(1, \alpha_i, \alpha_i^2, \dots, \alpha_i^{L-1})$.
2. **Approximation of Spectral Filters:** SpectraLDS selects `h` such modes ($\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_h$) and computes a mixing matrix `F` that approximates the stack of spectral filters ($\Phi_{1:k}$) as a linear combination of these geometric responses ($\Phi_{1:k} \approx F \mu_L(\alpha_{1:h})$). This means each spectral feature $\langle \phi_j, s_{t-1:t-L} \rangle$ can be approximated by a linear combination of recurrent states $\sum_{i=1}^h F(j,i) z^{(s,i)}_t$.
3. **Recurrent Realization:** By substituting these recurrent state approximations for the spectral features in the OSF predictor (Equation 2.7), and implementing the finite-memory AR terms with shift registers, the entire OSF predictor is transformed into a standard recurrent LDS.
4. **Algorithm 1 (Nonlinear-to-LDS Distillation):**
 - **Spectral learning:** Run OSF with `k` spectral filters and AR order `n` on the observed trajectory ($u_{1:T}, y_{1:T}$) to learn readout parameters (J, M, P, N).
 - **Distillation:** Apply SpectraLDS to the learned spectral filters to obtain `h` modes ($\alpha_i$) and a mixing matrix `F`.
 - **Recurrent Realization:** Initialize recurrent states $z^{(u,i)}_1$ and $z^{(y,i)}_1$. At each time step `t`, form approximated spectral features $\tilde{U}^{(u)}_{t,j}$ and $\tilde{U}^{(y)}_{t,j}$ using the current recurrent states and the mixing matrix `F`.
 - **Output Prediction:** Compute the distilled prediction $\hat{y}_{LDS_t}$ using the learned OSF readout parameters (J, M, P, N) and the approximated spectral features, along with the finite-memory AR terms.
 - **State Update:** Update the recurrent states $z^{(u,i)}_{t+1}$ and $z^{(y,i)}_{t+1}$ using the current input $u_t$ and output $y_t$ (or predicted output for closed-loop, though the bound is for teacher-forcing).

The resulting LDS operates with a state composed of `O((h + n)(d_u + d_y))` scalar variables, offering a compact representation whose per-step cost depends only on its effective dimension `h` and the input/output dimensions. This entire pipeline provides a principled route from nonlinear observations to compact linear state-space models by first learning in a convex spectral space and then distilling into an interpretable recurrent LDS.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents both theoretical guarantees for its proposed pipeline and empirical validation on synthetic and complex control tasks.

**Theoretical Findings:**

The central theoretical contribution is Theorem 3.1, which provides a prediction guarantee for the distilled Linear Dynamical System (LDS) when learning from an unknown nonlinear dynamical system.

1. **Prediction Error Decomposition:** The theorem demonstrates that the average prediction error of the distilled LDS, $ \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T \|y_t - \hat{y}_{LDS_t}\|^2 $, decomposes into two main terms:
 - **OSF Learning Term:** This term, $ \tilde{O}(Q^2_* \log Q_* \log^7 T / \sqrt{T}) $, reflects the error incurred by the Observation Spectral Filtering (OSF) algorithm in learning an implicit predictor from the nonlinear dynamics. It depends on the Luenberger complexity ($Q^*$) of the best linear observer, which quantifies the system's observability and spectral properties. The $ \tilde{O}(\cdot) $ notation suppresses logarithmic factors in `T` and other fixed problem-dependent constants.
 - **Distillation Term:** This term, $ \lambda_{\max}(F)^2 \exp \left(- \frac{2k}{\log T} \right) $, represents the error introduced by converting the spectral predictor into an explicit recurrent LDS via SpectraLDS. It decays exponentially with the number of spectral filters `k` (chosen as $ \Theta(\log^2 T) $) and depends on $ \lambda_{\max}(F) $, the largest singular value of the mixing matrix `F` used in the distillation.
2. **Dimension-Free Guarantee:** A notable aspect of the guarantee is its "dimension-free" nature. The error bound depends on the observer complexity $Q^*$ rather than on the potentially very large latent dimension required to represent the original nonlinear system or its high-dimensional linear lifting. This differentiates it from many classical system identification results that scale polynomially with state dimension.
3. **Explicit Compact Representation:** The output of the pipeline is a standard LDS with $O((d_{in} + d_{out})(h + m))$ memory and per-step computation cost, where `h` is the number of SpectraLDS modes and `m` is the AR order. This contrasts with OSF, which is an implicit convolutional predictor.

**Empirical Findings:**

The empirical validation assessed the performance of the "train-then-distill" pipeline against directly trained baselines on two types of tasks:

1. **Learning Linear Dynamical Systems (LDS Benchmarks):**
 - **Long-Memory Systems (ρ(A) = 0.999):** In a challenging regime with slow impulse response decay, the STU (OSF) $\rightarrow$ SpectraLDS pipeline, when optimized with Adam, significantly outperformed directly trained Diagonal LDS and General LDS baselines with comparable parameter counts. When optimized with Online Newton Step (ONS), the STU achieved errors three orders of magnitude lower than Adam-trained baselines. The General LDS, despite being more expressive than Diagonal LDS, did not close the performance gap, suggesting optimization advantages of the spectral parameterization. The spectral model also demonstrated faster training times than the General LDS.
 - **Shorter-Memory Systems (ρ(A) = 0.95):** At both small and large scales, the STU $\rightarrow$ SpectraLDS pipeline remained a strong predictor for both symmetric and asymmetric dynamics. It was competitive with or superior to Diagonal LDS models, particularly on asymmetric systems where diagonal recurrences struggle with complex modes. Linear FIR was competitive only in the smallest symmetric setting where its high parameter count was not a bottleneck.
 - **Distillation Fidelity:** A verification step confirmed that the SpectraLDS distillation introduced negligible error, with a maximum relative error of less than 1.5% between the raw STU predictor and its distilled LDS representation.

2. **MuJoCo Behavior Cloning (Nonlinear Control Data):**
 - **Performance on Nonlinear Tasks:** The pipeline was tested on six MuJoCo environments by training a student model to clone a Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) expert's actions. The trained student, after distillation into a recurrent LDS, was evaluated by closed-loop episode reward.
 - **Outperformance of Baselines:** The STU $\rightarrow$ SpectraLDS model achieved the highest mean reward on four of the six environments (Walker2d, Hopper, HalfCheetah, Humanoid). On the remaining two (Ant, Pusher), it remained competitive with the best directly trained Diagonal LDS baselines. For instance, on Walker2d, the spectral pipeline exceeded the parameter-matched Diagonal LDS by approximately 1,700 reward points.
 - **Efficiency:** The student models, including the distilled LDS, were intentionally compact, utilizing fewer parameters than the SAC actor deployed by the teacher on every environment. This suggests the method can yield efficient models.

In summary, the main findings establish a theoretically grounded and empirically competitive approach for extracting linear state-space models from nonlinear observations. The method leverages convex optimization for initial learning and a provable distillation step, resulting in compact and effective recurrent predictors that often surpass directly trained LDS baselines, particularly in challenging long-memory or nonlinear scenarios.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces a novel, end-to-end provable pipeline for extracting compact linear state-space representations from observations of unknown nonlinear dynamical systems. This work holds several significant implications and potential impacts:

1. **Bridging Nonlinearity and Linearity with Provable Guarantees:** A key achievement is providing a provable method for learning an LDS representation of a nonlinear system without directly tackling a non-convex system-identification problem. By decoupling learning (via convex OSF) from representation (via SpectraLDS distillation), the pipeline offers theoretical guarantees on prediction error. This contrasts with many existing methods that either lack such guarantees for nonlinear systems or rely on asymptotic analyses.
2. **Addressing Computational Challenges of Nonlinear System Identification:** The reliance on convex optimization for the primary learning phase significantly mitigates the computational and theoretical challenges associated with non-convex optimization inherent in direct nonlinear system identification. This can lead to more robust and reliable learning algorithms.
3. **Dimension-Free Performance Bounds:** The guarantee's dependence on the Luenberger complexity ($Q^*$) rather than the potentially enormous latent dimension of a nonlinear system or its linear lift is a substantial theoretical advancement. This makes the approach potentially applicable to high-dimensional nonlinear systems where traditional dimension-dependent guarantees would be prohibitive.
4. **Compact and Efficient Model Representations:** The output of the pipeline is a standard recurrent LDS. Such models offer compact state-space representations, efficient simulation capabilities, and are well-suited for long-horizon forecasting and control tasks. The empirical results demonstrate that these distilled LDS models can match or outperform directly trained LDS baselines with similar parameter budgets, suggesting practical utility in resource-constrained environments or for deployment in real-time systems.
5. **Implications for Sequence Modeling and Control:** The success in behavior cloning on MuJoCo tasks indicates the method's potential for real-world applications in control and reinforcement learning. By generating accurate and compact linear models from nonlinear expert trajectories, it could serve as a building block for model-predictive control, policy distillation, or efficient simulation. Its integration with modern sequence models could enhance capabilities for long-horizon forecasting and control, especially where interpretability and computational efficiency are critical.
6. **Foundation for Future Research:** The work opens several avenues for further theoretical and empirical investigation. Refining sampling distributions for SpectraLDS modes to improve distillation guarantees (e.g., on $ \lambda_{\max}(F) $) is one theoretical direction. Extending the prediction error bounds from average one-step prediction under teacher forcing to closed-loop rollout error, which is crucial for control and long-horizon simulation, is another significant area. Empirically, scaling the approach to larger, more complex nonlinear systems and integrating it with other advanced sequence models could further establish its practical utility.

In conclusion, this research presents a methodologically sound and empirically validated approach that advances the capability to approximate complex nonlinear dynamics with interpretable, efficient, and provably robust linear models. Its significance lies in offering a principled route from nonlinear observations to compact LDS models, potentially impacting various fields reliant on dynamical system modeling.
