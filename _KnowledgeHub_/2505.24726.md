# Reflect, Retry, Reward: Self-Improving LLMs via Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Writer, Inc. researchers develop a reinforcement learning framework that trains LLMs to generate more effective self-reflections by rewarding only the reflection tokens when a model's second attempt succeeds after initial failure, achieving substantial performance gains where trained 7B parameter models outperform vanilla 72B models on function calling (APIGen) and math equation tasks (Countdown) using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) with binary success/failure feedback, while demonstrating minimal catastrophic forgetting (<1% degradation on MMLU-Pro, GSM8K benchmarks) and producing qualitatively superior reflections that are shorter, clearer, and more actionable compared to verbose vanilla model outputs.

## Problem

- LLMs exhibit unpredictable failures and inconsistent performance across similar tasks, creating reliability issues for real-world deployment
- Direct retraining on failed tasks is often impractical due to data scarcity and inability of even large models to generate reliable synthetic training data
- Existing self-reflection methods rely heavily on prompt quality and external supervision rather than learned capabilities

## Method

- ==Two-stage RL framework== where models attempt a task, generate self-reflection upon failure, retry the task, and receive rewards only for reflection tokens when the retry succeeds
- Uses ==Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)== with binary feedback to train models on "datasets of failures" extracted from initial unsuccessful attempts
- ==Advantage masking== for all non-reflection tokens to ensure learning focuses on reflection quality rather than task-specific answers

## Results

- Trained smaller models consistently outperform much larger vanilla models (Qwen2-7B beats Qwen2-72B on both tasks when given two attempts)
- Average performance improvements of **4.7%** on APIGen function calling and **8.6%** on Countdown math after GRPO training with self-reflection
- Minimal catastrophic forgetting with less than **1%** degradation on general benchmarks while maintaining specialized self-correction abilities

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Effective self-reflections are shorter and more focused rather than verbose, contradicting assumptions about chain-of-thought reasoning
> - Training on reflection process generalizes to improve first-attempt performance even when no explicit reflection is provided
> - Binary success/failure feedback is sufficient for meaningful self-improvement without requiring detailed human annotations or teacher models

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper "Reflect, Retry, Reward" addresses a critical challenge in the current landscape of Large Language Models (LLMs): their inherent "blind spots" and lack of guaranteed consistency across tasks, even those of a similar nature. While LLMs have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in natural language processing, mathematics, coding, and reasoning, their performance can be brittle, and direct retraining on failed tasks is often infeasible due to data scarcity or the inability of even state-of-the-art models to generate reliable synthetic data.

This work builds upon the growing body of research into **self-reflection** and **chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting** as methods for improving LLM performance. CoT (Wei et al., 2022) showed that explicit reasoning steps enhance performance. Similarly, self-reflection (Ji et al., 2023; Renze and Guven, 2024; Shinn et al., 2023) involves a model analyzing its own incorrect responses to identify flaws and attempt corrections. These meta-prompting strategies are attractive because they do not require additional training data. However, their effectiveness is heavily dependent on the quality of the reasoning/reflection prompt and can suffer from limitations such as unreliable error identification, diminishing returns, and potential performance degradation for simpler prompts or highly capable base models. Existing self-reflection methods often rely on external human feedback, scalar scores, or even larger "teacher" models for supervision, which can be seen as a form of knowledge distillation.

"Reflect, Retry, Reward" distinguishes itself by moving beyond mere prompting or knowledge distillation to a **training-based approach focused specifically on optimizing the *process* of self-reflection itself**, rather than directly the task performance. By leveraging Reinforcement Learning (RL), the authors aim to make self-reflection an *endogenous learned capability* of the model. This addresses a key limitation in prior self-reflection work: how to systematically improve the quality of reflections without extensive human annotation or reliance on superior external models.

The paper utilizes **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**, an outcome-based RL method (Shao et al., 2024), which is particularly well-suited for fine-tuning LLMs in settings with sparse, end-of-sequence feedback, such as verifying the correctness of a mathematical solution or an API call. Unlike conventional RL methods like PPO, GRPO does not require a separate critic network, making it potentially more stable and efficient for these specific scenarios. The work also aligns with recent trends in RL for LLMs that encourage persistence through failure or optimize for complex, outcome-supervised behaviors like tool use.

In summary, this research is positioned at the intersection of self-improving LLMs and reinforcement learning. It seeks to bridge the gap between prompt-based self-correction and data-intensive fine-tuning by providing a novel, efficient, and task-agnostic method to teach LLMs *how to reflect effectively* on their mistakes, thereby enhancing their overall reasoning and problem-solving abilities, especially in verifiable tasks with limited explicit supervision.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core objective of the "Reflect, Retry, Reward" paper is to develop and demonstrate a novel methodology for enhancing the performance of Large Language Models (LLMs) by training them to generate more effective self-reflections. This approach aims to achieve self-improvement on complex, verifiable tasks in a task-agnostic manner, even when standard methods of data augmentation or retraining are impractical.

The key motivations driving this research include:

- **Addressing LLM "Blind Spots" and Inconsistency:** Despite their broad capabilities, LLMs exhibit unpredictable failures and a lack of consistent performance across seemingly similar tasks. This hinders their reliability and applicability in critical real-world scenarios.
- **Overcoming Data Scarcity and Synthetic Data Limitations:** The most direct way to fix LLM failures is to retrain them on relevant data. However, for many complex or niche tasks, dedicated datasets may not exist. Furthermore, if even the largest state-of-the-art models struggle, they cannot reliably generate high-quality synthetic training data. This paper seeks a solution that bypasses the need for large, task-specific datasets.
- **Improving Self-Correction Beyond Prompt Engineering:** While prompting strategies like Chain-of-Thought and self-reflection have shown promise, their effectiveness is highly dependent on the quality of the prompt. The authors are motivated to move beyond mere prompting to a learned capability, where the model intrinsically understands how to reflect effectively.
- **Leveraging Binary Feedback for Learning:** The research focuses on tasks where a simple binary signal (success/failure) is available, rather than requiring detailed human annotations or continuous reward signals. This makes the method applicable to a wide range of tasks where outcomes can be easily verified (e.g., correct API calls, executable code, valid mathematical equations).
- **Achieving Task-Agnostic Self-Improvement:** Instead of training models to improve at a specific task, the objective is to teach them *how to self-reflect* more generally. The hypothesis is that by optimizing the reflection process, the model's underlying reasoning abilities will improve, leading to better performance across various tasks without task-specific fine-tuning.
- **Enhancing Efficiency and Accessibility:** By demonstrating that smaller, fine-tuned models can outperform significantly larger general-purpose models after self-reflection training, the authors aim to show a pathway to more computationally efficient and deployable LLMs, reducing the need for massive models in certain applications.

In essence, the paper proposes a pragmatic solution for LLM self-improvement that navigates the common obstacles of data availability and computational cost, leading to more robust and reliable AI systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The "Reflect, Retry, Reward" methodology is a two-stage reinforcement learning framework designed to train LLMs to generate more effective self-reflections, thereby improving their performance on verifiable tasks. The core idea is to reward the model specifically for reflections that lead to a successful retry after an initial failure.

#### 3.1. Reflect, Retry, Reward Framework

The process unfolds as follows, based on an initial user query and a task:

1. **Initial Attempt:** The LLM first attempts to complete the given task.
2. **Binary Validation:** An external verifier evaluates the model's initial response, providing binary feedback (success or failure). This verifier is task-dependent (e.g., checking if an API call is valid, or if a mathematical equation evaluates correctly).
3. **Self-Reflection Generation (upon failure):** If the initial attempt fails, the model is prompted to generate a self-reflection. This reflection is a natural language commentary analyzing what went wrong and how to improve.
4. **Second Attempt (with reflection):** The model then makes a second attempt to complete the *same task*, with its generated self-reflection included in the conversation history as context.
5. **Reward (upon successful retry):** If the second attempt *succeeds*, the tokens generated during the self-reflection phase are rewarded using Reinforcement Learning. Critically, only the self-reflection tokens are rewarded, with advantage terms for all other generated tokens (e.g., the final correct answer) set to zero. This ensures the model learns *how to reflect effectively* rather than specializing in generating correct answers for a specific task. If the second attempt also fails, no reward is given.

#### 3.2. Reinforcement Learning Mechanism

The authors employ **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)** (Shao et al., 2024) as the sole mechanism for reinforcement learning. GRPO is an outcome-based RL method that estimates advantages by comparing outcomes from a group of sampled completions, eliminating the need for a separate value (critic) network. This makes it well-suited for settings where supervision is sparse and only available at the conclusion of a sequence generation (e.g., a final answer being correct or incorrect). The TRL framework (von Werra et al., 2020) was used as a base, with modifications to the `GRPOTrainer` to accommodate the multi-step generation and reward masking.

#### 3.3. Data Preparation: Dataset of Failures

To improve training efficiency and facilitate analysis, the models were *not* trained on the full datasets. Instead, a "dataset of failures" was created for each task. This involved:

- Prompting each base model with user queries.
- Generating multiple responses (up to 64) per query.
- Preserving *only* those queries where the model's initial attempt failed (based on the task-dependent verifier).
This approach saved significant training time as there was no need to train on already successful queries. It also made the data more robust by capturing failures even for queries where the base model might sometimes succeed (due to non-deterministic responses), and allowed for precise determination of convergence. The authors emphasize that this method is functionally equivalent to learning from real-world scenarios where both successes and failures occur.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

- **Tasks:** Experiments were conducted on two distinct tasks:
 - **Function Calling:** Using the APIGen dataset (Liu et al., 2024b), which involves generating correctly formatted JSON function calls based on user queries and available tools. The validator required an exact match to ground-truth JSON.
 - **Countdown Math Equations:** Using the Countdown dataset (Pan et al., 2025b), where the goal is to form an equation evaluating to a target number using a given set of numbers and basic arithmetic operations. The validator checked if the equation used all numbers once and evaluated to the target, without requiring ground-truth labels.
- **Models:** Experiments focused on smaller fine-tuned models (1.5B to 8B parameters) from the Qwen2, Llama3.1/3.2, and Phi3.5-mini families, as well as Writer's Palmyra 1.7B. Larger models (e.g., Qwen-72B, Llama-70B, Palmyra X4) were used as vanilla baselines. All models were pre-APIGen/Countdown release/knowledge cutoff to ensure no prior exposure.
- **Training Parameters:** Standard GRPO parameters were used, with a KL divergence coefficient of 0.001 and a learning rate of 5e-7 with a cosine annealing schedule. Training was conducted on 4-8 H100 GPUs.
- **Prompting:** Task-specific prompt formats were carefully selected for each model family to achieve the strongest baselines, and a dedicated prompt was used to elicit self-reflections upon failure.

The methodology is meticulously designed to isolate the impact of self-reflection training and demonstrate its practical utility, particularly for models operating in environments with verifiable outcomes.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted on function calling (APIGen) and math equation solving (Countdown) demonstrate significant performance improvements using the "Reflect, Retry, Reward" framework.

#### 4.1. Performance Gains on APIGen (Function Calling)

- **Vanilla Performance:** Across various models, simply allowing a second attempt with self-reflection improved accuracy by an average of 4.5% compared to the first attempt. This aligns with prior work on self-correction.
- **Impact of GRPO Training:** After GRPO self-reflection training, models showed substantial gains. Most notably, nearly all trained models were able to outperform even the two-attempt vanilla models *on their first attempt*. This suggests that the training process improves the model's general reasoning, even when an explicit self-reflection is not required.
- **Further Gains with Self-Reflection:** When trained models were allowed a second attempt with self-reflection, performance increased by an additional 4.7% on average.
- **Smaller Models Outperforming Larger Ones:** A highly striking result was that the GRPO-trained Qwen-2-7B model outperformed the vanilla Qwen-2-72B model (which is 10 times larger) when both were given two attempts. This highlights the potential for significantly more efficient deployment of highly capable models.

#### 4.2. Performance Gains on Countdown (Math Equations)

- **Lower Baselines, Higher Impact:** Performance on the Countdown task was generally lower across all models, especially for vanilla Llama models. However, this provided a greater opportunity for learning.
- **Magnitude of Improvements:** Self-reflection improved performance by an average of 5.3% in vanilla models and a notable 8.6% after GRPO training, indicating even larger gains in tasks with lower initial accuracy.
- **Smaller Models Competing with Larger:** Similar to APIGen, the trained Qwen2.5-7B Instruct model outperformed the vanilla Qwen2.5-72B Instruct model on two attempts, despite the considerable size difference.

#### 4.3. Qualitative Improvements in Self-Reflections

- A qualitative analysis (Figure 2) revealed that self-reflections generated by vanilla models tended to be lengthy, confusing, and redundant. In contrast, reflections from GRPO fine-tuned models were significantly shorter, clearer, and more generalizable. This finding contrasts with the common belief that verbose "chain-of-thought" outputs are always superior, suggesting that for self-correction, conciseness might be more effective.

#### 4.4. Low Catastrophic Forgetting

- A critical concern with fine-tuning is "catastrophic forgetting," where a model specializes in one task at the expense of general capabilities. Evaluations on diverse benchmarks (MMLU-Pro, GSM8K, HellaSwag, MATH) showed that the self-reflection trained models experienced minimal degradation (<1%) in most cases compared to their base counterparts. Some models even showed slight improvements, demonstrating the robustness of the approach and its ability to improve reasoning generally without compromising existing knowledge.

#### 4.5. Error Analysis (Appendix Summary)

- The error analysis in the appendix provides further insight. For function calling, smaller vanilla models struggled significantly with tool choice. Training helped reduce tool choice errors for these models and parameter errors for larger models. In math equations, all models primarily struggled with using only the allowed numbers. Training generally decreased this "wrong numbers" error, indicating that the models learned to adhere to the constraints of the problem better.

In summary, the "Reflect, Retry, Reward" framework, powered by GRPO, effectively teaches LLMs to generate superior self-reflections, leading to substantial and robust performance gains on complex, verifiable tasks. Crucially, these gains enable smaller, fine-tuned models to achieve performance comparable to, or even exceeding, much larger general-purpose models, opening new avenues for efficient LLM deployment.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Reflect, Retry, Reward" framework represents a significant advancement in the field of Large Language Model (LLM) self-improvement, offering several profound implications and potential impacts:

- **Efficient and Data-Scarce Learning:** The most critical significance is its ability to significantly improve LLM performance on complex, verifiable tasks with only *binary success/failure feedback* and without requiring extensive, task-specific labeled training data or costly synthetic data generation from even larger models. This addresses a major bottleneck in deploying LLMs to specialized or niche domains where high-quality datasets are scarce.
- **Empowering Smaller Models:** The demonstration that smaller, fine-tuned models (e.g., 7B parameter models) can outperform much larger vanilla models (e.g., 72B parameter models) is highly impactful. This breakthrough could drastically reduce the computational resources (GPUs, energy) required for inference and training, making advanced LLM capabilities more accessible and economically viable for a wider range of businesses and applications. For companies like Writer, Inc., this translates directly into more competitive and cost-effective product offerings.
- **Enhancing LLM Reliability and Robustness:** By teaching models *how to reflect* and improve upon their mistakes, the framework inherently makes LLMs more reliable. This is particularly crucial for tasks where correctness is paramount, such as code generation, API interaction, factual query answering, and mathematical reasoning, where automated verification is feasible. The method ensures that corrections are only applied to initially incorrect examples, guaranteeing performance improvement or maintenance.
- **Generalization of Reasoning Skills:** The hypothesis that optimizing self-reflection leads to a general improvement in reasoning skills, even for first attempts where no explicit reflection occurs, is a powerful concept. If further research confirms this generalization across tasks, it suggests a path toward more intelligent and adaptable LLMs that can learn meta-cognitive skills.
- **Robustness to Catastrophic Forgetting:** The demonstrated minimal catastrophic forgetting across diverse benchmarks means that models fine-tuned with this method retain their general language understanding and reasoning abilities while gaining specialized self-correction skills. This is vital for practical deployment, as it ensures that improving performance on one task does not degrade performance on others.
- **Novel Reinforcement Learning Application:** The successful application of GRPO to explicitly reward self-reflection tokens in a multi-step generation process showcases a novel and effective use of reinforcement learning for LLM optimization, particularly for scenarios with sparse rewards and complex reasoning chains.

In conclusion, "Reflect, Retry, Reward" offers an exciting pathway toward creating more useful, reliable, and resource-efficient language models. By focusing on teaching LLMs the crucial meta-skill of self-reflection, this work contributes significantly to the ongoing effort to make AI systems more autonomous, intelligent, and capable of continual self-improvement in real-world settings.
