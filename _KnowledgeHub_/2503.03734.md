# OTTER: A Vision-Language-Action Model with Text-Aware Visual Feature Extraction

> [!summary] Summary
> OTTER, a Vision-Language-Action model, employs text-aware visual feature extraction from frozen pre-trained vision-language models to achieve robust zero-shot generalization in robotic manipulation. The model significantly improves success rates on novel objects and environments, outperforming fine-tuned baselines by substantial margins across various tasks.

## Problem

- Fine-tuning pre-trained Vision-Language Models (VLMs) on relatively small and less diverse robot-specific datasets degrades their inherent semantic alignments.
- This degradation impairs the VLA model's ability to generalize effectively to unseen objects, environments, or task variations, which is critical for real-world robotics.
- Existing VLA approaches often fail to leverage the full zero-shot potential of robust pre-trained VLMs like CLIP due to this fine-tuning interference.

## Method

- **OTTER** freezes the pre-trained vision and language encoders of a VLM (e.g., CLIP) to preserve their rich semantic understanding, preventing degradation during training.
- It introduces a novel text-aware visual feature extraction mechanism that uses temperature-weighted attention to explicitly select and weight visual patches based on their semantic similarity to individual language tokens.
- A causal transformer-based policy network then integrates these text-aware visual features, compressed text features, and proprioceptive states to autoregressively predict robot actions.

## Results

- OTTER achieved **62%** success on unseen real-world pick-and-place tasks, substantially outperforming fine-tuned Octo (**12%**) and OpenVLA (**9%**) and showing strong generalization.
- Across four manipulation primitives, OTTER-OXE-L achieved a mean success rate of **77%** on unseen tasks, while baselines struggled significantly, particularly with complex tasks like pouring (**0%** success).
- Ablation studies confirmed that fine-tuning the CLIP encoder reduced unseen task performance by over **40%** (from **62%** to **15%**) compared to keeping it frozen, validating OTTER's core hypothesis.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Preserving the pre-trained semantic alignments of VLMs by keeping their encoders frozen is crucial for achieving superior zero-shot generalization in robot learning, as fine-tuning can compromise these capabilities.
> - Explicitly extracting text-aware visual features, which selectively highlight task-relevant visual information based on language instructions, is more effective than simply passing separate vision and language features.
> - The performance of VLA models can scale positively with the size of the underlying pre-trained VLM and benefit from pre-training on diverse human video datasets, suggesting strong compatibility with foundation models.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in this paper is situated within the rapidly evolving field of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models for robotics. This domain aims to enable robots to perform tasks by interpreting language instructions in conjunction with visual observations. The development of VLA models has been significantly influenced by the success of large language models (LLMs) and vision-language models (VLMs), prompting researchers to explore scaling datasets and computational resources for robotic control.

Traditional VLA approaches typically involve encoding multimodal inputs—such as multi-view images, tokenized language instructions, and robot proprioceptive states—into separate feature tokens. These tokens are then fed into a transformer-based policy network to predict actions. Models like RT-2 and OpenVLA exemplify this strategy, often fine-tuning pre-trained VLMs on robotic datasets to establish mappings from vision and language to control commands. While these methods benefit from the rich features learned during VLM pre-training, the paper identifies a key challenge: fine-tuning on robot-specific datasets can degrade the pre-trained semantic alignments within the VLMs. This degradation occurs because robot datasets are generally less semantically diverse than the large-scale datasets used for initial VLM pre-training (e.g., LAION). Consequently, models that undergo such fine-tuning often exhibit a noticeable drop in performance when generalizing to unseen objects or environments compared to tasks encountered during training.

This observation is consistent with broader findings in vision-language research, where fine-tuning language-aligned vision encoders like CLIP has been shown to lead to overfitting and a reduction in generalization capabilities. Given that models like CLIP and SigLIP already demonstrate strong zero-shot performance across various vision-language tasks, including fine-grained ones like open-vocabulary segmentation, the research questions the efficacy of fine-tuning these robust encoders for robotic tasks. Instead, it proposes that a more effective strategy might be to leverage and preserve the existing, powerful vision-language alignments already learned by these pre-trained models, rather than risking their degradation through task-specific fine-tuning. OTTER positions itself as an advancement that addresses this specific limitation by introducing a mechanism for explicit, text-aware visual feature extraction from frozen pre-trained VLMs, thereby preserving their semantic understanding for enhanced zero-shot generalization in robotic control.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model named OTTER that can predict robotic actions based on visual observations and natural language instructions, while exhibiting robust zero-shot generalization capabilities to novel objects and environments.

The motivation for OTTER stems from an identified limitation in existing VLA models. Current approaches often involve fine-tuning large pre-trained Vision-Language Models (VLMs), such as CLIP, on robot-specific datasets. While this strategy aims to leverage the extensive knowledge embedded within these VLMs, the authors highlight a critical drawback:

1. **Degradation of Pre-trained Alignments:** Fine-tuning VLMs on relatively smaller and less semantically diverse robot datasets can disrupt the carefully learned vision-language semantic alignments established during large-scale pre-training. These pre-trained alignments enable VLMs to understand the relationship between visual concepts and linguistic descriptions.
2. **Impaired Generalization:** The interference with these semantic alignments during fine-tuning often leads to a decrease in the model's ability to generalize to unseen objects, environments, or task variations. This is a significant challenge in robotics, where deploying agents in novel scenarios is often required.
3. **Inconsistency with VLM Properties:** The authors note that VLMs like CLIP already demonstrate impressive zero-shot capabilities and strong semantic alignment across various vision-language tasks. The practice of fine-tuning, which can weaken these inherent capabilities, appears counterintuitive.

Based on these observations, the core motivation for OTTER is to preserve and actively utilize the rich semantic understanding and zero-shot generalization abilities of pre-trained VLMs. Instead of allowing fine-tuning to potentially degrade these capabilities, OTTER aims to:

- **Freeze Pre-trained Encoders:** Maintain the pre-trained vision and language encoders of a VLM (specifically CLIP) in a frozen state, preventing any alteration of their semantic representations.
- **Extract Task-Relevant Features:** Develop a novel mechanism to explicitly extract only the visual features that are semantically aligned with the given language instruction, thereby focusing the policy on task-relevant visual information. This process is termed "text-aware visual feature extraction."
- **Decouple Planning:** Effectively decouple the higher-level "task planning" (identifying relevant visual features based on language) from the lower-level "robot action planning" (predicting specific motor commands).
- **Enhance Zero-Shot Generalization:** Through this approach, OTTER seeks to harness the full potential of pre-trained VLM semantic understanding to achieve strong zero-shot generalization to novel objects and environments in robotic manipulation tasks, with minimal performance degradation.

In summary, the detailed objective is to create a VLA model that bypasses the limitations of fine-tuning pre-trained VLMs by implementing a method that selectively extracts and utilizes their inherent vision-language alignment, thereby fostering superior zero-shot generalization for robotic control.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

OTTER proposes a novel architecture for Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models that focuses on leveraging pre-trained vision-language models (VLMs) by explicitly extracting text-aware visual features, rather than fine-tuning the VLM encoders. The methodology can be broken down into several key components:

#### 3.1. Text-Aware Visual Feature Extraction

The core of OTTER's approach lies in its method for integrating vision and language. It utilizes a pre-trained CLIP model (specifically a ViT-based vision encoder) for extracting both visual and language features.
- **Feature Source Selection:** Instead of using the final output feature map (`X_out`) from CLIP's vision encoder, OTTER extracts the attention output (`X_attn`) from the *last vision attention layer*. This choice is based on prior work (ClearCLIP) suggesting that `X_attn` contains cleaner semantic information and better visual-language alignment.
- **Feature Normalization:** Text per-token features (`f_l`) from CLIP's language encoder and visual attention features (`f_v`) are first normalized. `f_l` is normalized using CLIP's final layer normalization and language projection matrix, while `f_v` is normalized using the post-attention layer normalization and vision projection matrix. Both are then L2-normalized.
- **Temperature-Weighted Attention:** Normalized text features (`f_l`) and visual features (`f_v`) are fused using a temperature-weighted attention mechanism:
 `f_vl = softmax(f_l * f_v^T / τ) * (f_v + PE)`
 Here, `τ` is a learnable temperature parameter (clipped between 0 and 100), and `PE` represents 2D sin-cos position embeddings for visual patches. This softmax operation effectively selects and weights visual patches based on their semantic similarity to individual language tokens, generating `f_vl` (text-aware visual tokens). This process yields a compact representation that highlights task-relevant visual information.
- **Frozen Encoders:** A critical aspect is that the entire pre-trained CLIP model (both vision and language encoders) remains *frozen* during training. Only the temperature parameter `τ` is learnable. This preserves the VLM's original semantic understanding.

#### 3.2. Model Architecture (Policy Network)

The policy network receives processed features and generates actions.
- **Input Compression:**
 - `f_vl` (text-aware visual features) for each camera are further compressed into a single token (`f'_vl`) using a learnable cross-attention pooling layer with four queries.
 - Text features `f_l` are also compressed into a single token (`f'_l`) via another learnable cross-attention pooling layer to improve instruction following.
 - The robot's proprioceptive state is encoded into an embodiment feature (`f_e`) using a Feed-Forward Network (FFN).
- **Concatenated Input:** At each timestep `t`, `f'_l`, `f'_vl`, and `f_e` are concatenated to form a single token (`f_t`), which serves as the input to the policy network.
- **Policy Transformer:** The policy network is a causal transformer, configured with 4 layers, 8 attention heads, and a hidden dimension of 512. It operates with a context length of `T` steps (e.g., 12 for real-world experiments).
- **Action Prediction:** For each output token at a given timestep, an FFN predicts the next 12 actions autoregressively.

#### 3.3. Proprioception and Action Parametrization

- **Proprioception:** Represented by a 10-dimensional vector, including absolute end-effector translation (x, y, z), a 6DoF rotation vector (flattened from the first two rows of the SO(3) matrix), and a continuous end-effector gripper state.
- **Action:** Delta end-effector poses are used for action parametrization. The model predicts relative transforms (`T_ee^-1 * T_i`) from the current end-effector pose. A continuous absolute gripper position is appended to each delta action, resulting in a 10-dimensional action representation.
- **Execution:** During execution, temporal ensembling is combined with receding horizon control, with an action horizon of 8 steps found to be optimal.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

- **Simulation Environment:** Evaluated on the LIBERO benchmark (LIBERO-Spatial, LIBERO-Object, LIBERO-Goal, LIBERO-90). This included 30 in-distribution tasks and 10 novel unseen tasks with modified objects and language instructions to assess generalization.
- **Real Robot Environment:** Utilized a Franka robot for four manipulation primitives: pick and place, poking, pouring, and opening/closing a drawer.
 - Datasets: `DS-PnP` (724 demonstrations for pick-and-place) and `DS-ALL` (1185 demonstrations across all four primitives).
 - Evaluation: Conducted on 19 in-distribution training tasks and 15 out-of-distribution unseen tasks. For each task, 10 trials were performed, varying object locations and introducing distractors, to assess instruction-following and generalization to novel objects or scene configurations.
- **Baselines:** Comparisons were made against state-of-the-art VLA models: Octo, OpenVLA, and π0-Fast-Droid. A key ablation, DFP-OTTER (Direct Feature Passing OTTER), was also used to isolate the impact of text-aware visual feature extraction, where visual and text tokens are processed separately before concatenation, lacking the explicit fusion mechanism.
- **Ablation Studies:** Further ablations examined the contribution of embodiment features (`f_e`), separate text tokens (`f'_l`), using a vision encoder trained from scratch (`OTTER w.o. CLIP vision`), and fine-tuning CLIP (`OTTER (Finetune CLIP)`).
- **Scaling Experiments:** Investigated the effect of using larger CLIP models (ViT-B/32, ViT-B/16, ViT-L/14) and pre-training on human video datasets (DexYCB, HOI4D) to assess scalability and transfer learning benefits.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted both in simulation and on a physical robot demonstrate OTTER's performance in comparison to existing VLA models and its generalization capabilities.

#### 4.1. Real-world Experiments

- **Single Primitive (Pick and Place):** On pick-and-place tasks, OTTER (68% success on training, 62% on unseen) significantly outperformed finetuned Octo (15% training, 12% unseen), finetuned OpenVLA (30% training, 9% unseen), and DFP-OTTER (29% training, 4% unseen). The π0-Fast-Droid baseline achieved 61% on unseen tasks. OTTER's strong performance on unseen tasks highlights its zero-shot generalization, attributed to the preservation of pre-trained VLM semantic understanding. OTTER-OXE, pre-trained on the larger Open X-Embodiment (OXE) dataset, further improved performance (72% training, 73% unseen), indicating scalability with more robotic data.
- **Multiple Primitives (Pouring, Drawer, Poking, Pick and Place):** When evaluated across all four manipulation primitives on unseen tasks, OTTER demonstrated robust generalization. Baselines like π0-Fast-Droid, finetuned Octo, and finetuned OpenVLA struggled significantly, particularly with complex primitives like pouring (0% success rate). OTTER, OTTER-L (a larger variant), and their OXE-pretrained counterparts (OTTER-OXE, OTTER-OXE-L) achieved substantially higher success rates across all primitives (e.g., OTTER-OXE-L achieved a mean of 77% success). This outcome suggests that OTTER's text-aware visual feature extraction from frozen VLMs significantly enhances data efficiency and generalization across diverse manipulation tasks, even with a relatively small number of demonstrations per primitive.

#### 4.2. Simulation Experiments

In the LIBERO simulation benchmark, models showed comparable performance on in-distribution training tasks due to sufficient data and limited task variations. However, on the 10 novel unseen tasks constructed by the authors, OTTER outperformed other baselines by a notable margin (61% success rate vs. 26% for finetuned Octo, 48% for finetuned OpenVLA, and 45% for DFP-OTTER). This reinforces the real-world findings regarding OTTER's generalization to novel scenarios.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies

Ablation studies were conducted to assess the contribution of different components:
- **Absence of Embodiment Features (OTTER w.o. f_e):** This resulted in a significant performance drop (28% on training tasks and 33% on unseen tasks in real-world experiments), indicating the importance of proprioceptive information for grounding and generalization.
- **Absence of Text Tokens (OTTER w.o. f'_l):** Performance decreased by approximately 10% on both training and unseen tasks in real-world settings, suggesting that explicit language features provide complementary information for nuanced task understanding, especially when visual features might be ambiguous.
- **Training Vision Encoder from Scratch (OTTER w.o. CLIP vision):** Performance dropped by over 50% on both training and unseen tasks in real-world experiments, underscoring the necessity of pre-trained VLMs for robust and transferable visual representations.
- **Finetuning CLIP (OTTER (Finetune CLIP)):** Finetuning the CLIP encoder led to a large discrepancy between training (26%) and unseen (15%) task performance compared to frozen CLIP (68% training, 62% unseen). This result supports the hypothesis that fine-tuning compromises the VLM's generalization ability, validating OTTER's approach of keeping the VLM frozen.
- **Direct Feature Passing (DFP-OTTER):** This variant performed significantly worse on unseen tasks (4% in real-world), demonstrating that merely passing vision and language features separately, even from a frozen VLM, is insufficient; the explicit text-aware feature extraction is crucial. Other variants like DFP-OTTER (CLS) and OTTER (xattn), which lack the proposed text-aware fusion, also failed to generalize (6% and 2% respectively on unseen tasks).

#### 4.4. Scaling Capabilities

- **Scaling VLM Size:** OTTER's task success rate improved as larger CLIP models were used (from ViT-B/32 to ViT-L/14). This resulted in substantial gains of +27.5% on training and +39.3% on unseen pick-and-place tasks, indicating that OTTER effectively leverages the enhanced semantic understanding of more powerful VLMs.
- **Pre-training with Human Videos:** Pre-training OTTER on human video datasets (DexYCB and HOI4D) before fine-tuning on robot data improved performance on unseen robot tasks (from 62% to 70% success). This suggests that even with domain discrepancies, pre-training on diverse human interaction data can contribute to better generalization.

The aggregate results indicate that OTTER's mechanism of freezing pre-trained vision-language encoders and explicitly extracting text-aware visual features significantly contributes to its superior zero-shot generalization across various robotic manipulation tasks and environments.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of OTTER represents a notable contribution to the field of robotic learning, particularly in the domain of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. Its significance and potential impact can be understood through several key aspects:

1. **Enhanced Zero-Shot Generalization:** OTTER demonstrates a substantial improvement in zero-shot generalization capabilities for robotic tasks. By consistently outperforming baselines on unseen objects, environments, and across multiple manipulation primitives, the model advances the ability of robots to adapt to novel situations without requiring extensive re-training. This is a crucial step towards more autonomous and versatile robotic systems.

2. **Preservation of VLM Semantic Alignments:** The core insight of OTTER—to freeze pre-trained vision-language encoders and leverage their inherent semantic alignments through explicit text-aware feature extraction—addresses a significant limitation of prior VLA approaches. It demonstrates that weakening these alignments through fine-tuning on limited robotic data is detrimental to generalization. This approach provides a blueprint for effectively integrating the vast knowledge encoded in large-scale VLMs into robotic control.

3. **Data Efficiency in Robotics:** By strategically utilizing frozen, pre-trained VLMs, OTTER can achieve high performance with comparatively smaller robotic datasets. This increased data efficiency is highly significant for robot learning, where data collection is often expensive, time-consuming, and hazardous. The ability to generalize from fewer demonstrations reduces the barriers to deploying robots in new scenarios.

4. **Decoupling of Perception and Action Planning:** The architecture of OTTER implicitly decouples the task of identifying relevant visual information (guided by language) from the task of generating precise robot actions. This modularity could lead to more interpretable and robust robot learning systems, as specific failures can potentially be traced back to either the perception-language grounding or the action planning component.

5. **Scalability with Foundation Models:** The empirical evidence that OTTER's performance scales positively with larger pre-trained vision-language encoders (e.g., larger CLIP models) highlights its compatibility with the ongoing trend of developing increasingly powerful foundation models. This suggests that as VLMs continue to advance, OTTER-like architectures can readily benefit from these improvements, leading to even more capable robotic agents. Furthermore, its ability to benefit from pre-training on human video datasets indicates potential for leveraging diverse data sources to further enhance generalization.

6. **Guidance for Future Research:** OTTER's findings provide a strong argument for re-evaluating traditional fine-tuning paradigms in VLA research. The success of its feature extraction mechanism encourages further exploration into how existing powerful pre-trained models can be utilized more effectively without compromising their broad generalization capabilities. This could inspire new architectures and training strategies that prioritize the preservation of semantic understanding.

Despite its advancements, the paper acknowledges limitations, such as scaling across diverse robot morphologies (e.g., multi-finger hands) and extending to long-horizon, complex tasks. Addressing these areas represents important avenues for future research. Nevertheless, OTTER establishes a principled and effective method for building generalizable robot learning systems by strategically harnessing the power of pre-trained vision-language encoders, thereby pushing the boundaries of what is achievable in language-conditioned robotic manipulation.
