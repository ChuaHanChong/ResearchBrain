# Beyond Binary Rewards: Training LMs to Reason About Their Uncertainty

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at MIT developed Reinforcement Learning with Calibration Rewards (RLCR), an approach that trains large language models to concurrently improve answer accuracy and calibrate their confidence. RLCR integrates a Brier score into the RL reward function, enabling models to generate accurate answers alongside well-calibrated uncertainty estimates, reducing Expected Calibration Error on HotpotQA from 0.37 to 0.03 while maintaining task accuracy.

## Problem

- Standard reinforcement learning with binary rewards for large language models (LMs) often leads to overconfidence and increased hallucination, degrading calibration.
- Existing RL-based calibration methods frequently optimize solely for calibration, potentially sacrificing task accuracy.
- LMs struggle to reliably communicate uncertainty, undermining their trustworthiness in critical applications.

## Method

- Introduces ==RLCR (Reinforcement Learning with Calibration Rewards)==, which trains LMs to generate reasoning chains, answers, and explicit numerical confidence scores.
- Employs an augmented reward function combining a binary correctness reward with a ==negative Brier score== to incentivize both accuracy and calibration.
- Theoretically proves that this ==bounded proper scoring rule== encourages both accurate predictions and calibrated confidence matching the true probability of correctness.

## Results

- RLCR matches the task accuracy of standard binary-reward RL (RLVR) on in-domain datasets (e.g., **62.1%** vs. **63.0%** on HotpotQA), while dramatically improving calibration.
- On HotpotQA, RLCR reduces Expected Calibration Error (ECE) from **0.37** (RLVR) to **0.03**, and on math datasets from **0.26** to **0.10**.
- RLCR consistently and substantially improves calibration on out-of-distribution tasks, outperforming all baselines without sacrificing accuracy.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - It is possible to jointly optimize large language models for both task correctness and calibrated confidence without compromising accuracy.
> - Integrating explicit, textual uncertainty analysis within Chain-of-Thought reasoning can contribute to better calibration.
> - Training LMs to reason about and express uncertainty improves their generalization and robustness, particularly on out-of-distribution tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of large language models (LMs) has seen significant advancements through reinforcement learning (RL), particularly in training reasoning models to generate natural language reasoning chains. This approach, often referred to as reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR), has led to state-of-the-art performance on various complex question-answering tasks, including mathematics and programming.

However, a prevalent limitation in many successful RL applications for reasoning is the reliance on binary reward functions. These functions typically evaluate only the correctness of an LM's final output, assigning a reward of 1 for a correct answer and 0 for an incorrect one. While effective for improving accuracy, this binary approach does not differentiate between confidently correct answers and lucky guesses, nor does it penalize low-confidence incorrect outputs. This characteristic can inadvertently incentivize overconfident behavior and lead to a degradation in the model's calibration, often resulting in increased rates of "hallucination" or the generation of incorrect responses with high confidence. Prior research has indicated that models, even if initially well-calibrated, tend to become overconfident following standard RL training. This issue is particularly critical in high-stakes domains such as healthcare or legal applications, where not only accuracy but also the reliable communication of uncertainty is paramount.

Existing approaches to addressing confidence estimation in LMs include post-hoc verbalizations, where models are prompted to state their confidence after answering; sampling-based methods, which infer confidence from the consistency of multiple responses; and internal probing, which extracts confidence signals from the model's hidden layers or token probabilities. While some RL-based methods have also been proposed to train models for calibrated verbal confidences, these often focus solely on calibration, which can sometimes compromise task accuracy or lead to degenerate model behavior by incentivizing models to output incorrect answers with zero confidence for an optimal calibration score.

This paper positions itself within this landscape by introducing a method designed to jointly optimize both correctness and calibrated confidence estimation within reasoning LMs. It addresses the shortcomings of binary reward functions by integrating a proper scoring rule into the RL objective, thereby aiming to produce models that are not only accurate but also capable of reasoning about and expressing their uncertainty in a well-calibrated manner. The work differentiates itself by explicitly targeting reasoning models, proving the theoretical properties of its combined reward function, and demonstrating empirical improvements across both in-domain and out-of-domain evaluations without sacrificing task accuracy, unlike some prior RL-based calibration methods.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this research are articulated through two central questions:
1. **Can reasoning models be optimized for both correctness and calibration?** This question probes the feasibility of developing a training paradigm that simultaneously enhances the accuracy of reasoning models while ensuring their stated confidence levels accurately reflect the probability of their answers being correct.
2. **Can the contents of reasoning chains themselves improve calibration?** This objective investigates whether the explicit process of generating natural language reasoning steps and reflecting on uncertainty within these chains can inherently contribute to better confidence estimation.

The motivation for these objectives stems from several critical limitations identified in current approaches to training reasoning LMs:

- **Degraded Calibration from Binary Rewards:** The standard practice of using binary correctness rewards (e.g., `R_correctness(y, y*) = 1_{y≡y*}`) for RL training incentivizes models to maximize the number of correct answers. However, it fails to differentiate between a confidently correct answer and a lucky guess. Consequently, models are not penalized for high-confidence incorrect predictions, leading to a tendency for overconfidence.
- **Increased Hallucination:** Studies have indicated that LMs, particularly after RL training, often become overconfident and exhibit increased rates of "hallucination," where they generate incorrect information with high certainty. This behavior is exacerbated when reward signals primarily emphasize correctness.
- **Risks in High-Stakes Applications:** The lack of calibrated uncertainty in LM outputs poses a significant challenge for their deployment in sensitive domains such as healthcare, legal services, or financial forecasting. In these fields, it is imperative for models to not only provide accurate information but also to transparently communicate their level of uncertainty, allowing human users to make informed decisions. An overconfident model in such contexts can lead to erroneous human judgments with potentially severe consequences.
- **Leveraging Statistical Decision Theory:** The authors motivate their solution by drawing upon the theory of proper scoring rules, such as the Brier score. These rules are well-established in forecasting for incentivizing predictors to report probabilities that truly reflect the likelihood of outcomes. By applying these rules to LM training, the aim is to create a more robust and reliable mechanism for uncertainty quantification than what binary rewards currently offer.

By addressing these motivations, the research seeks to develop more generally reliable reasoning models that can not only achieve high task performance but also provide trustworthy indications of their own confidence, thus bridging a gap between accuracy and transparency in advanced LM applications.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, **Reinforcement Learning with Calibration Rewards (RLCR)**, modifies existing reasoning training approaches to jointly optimize for both task correctness and calibrated confidence estimation.

**Core Idea:**
RLCR trains LMs to generate not only predictions (answers) but also numerical confidence estimates following a natural language reasoning process. The model's outputs are structured to include explicit sections for thinking, the answer, an analysis of uncertainty, and a final confidence score.

**Reward Function Design:**
The central component of RLCR is a novel combined reward function:
`R_RLCR(y, q, y*) = R_correctness(y, y*) + R_Brier(y, q, y*) = 1_{y≡y*} - (q - 1_{y≡y*})^2`

- **Correctness Term (`R_correctness(y, y*)`):** This is a standard binary reward, equal to 1 if the model's output `y` matches the ground-truth `y*`, and 0 otherwise. This term directly incentivizes accuracy.
- **Brier Score Term (`R_Brier(y, q, y*)`):** This term is derived from the Brier score, a proper scoring rule. It penalizes the model based on the squared difference between its predicted confidence `q` (a numerical estimate between 0 and 1) and the actual outcome (`1_{y≡y*}`). A smaller difference (i.e., `(q - 1_{y≡y*})^2` closer to 0) leads to a higher (less negative) reward. This term incentivizes `q` to be a calibrated estimate of `p(y ≡ y*)`.

**Theoretical Foundation:**
A key theoretical contribution is the proof that this combined reward function (or any analogous reward function using a *bounded*, proper scoring rule) inherently incentivizes both accuracy and calibration. Specifically, `R_RLCR` is maximized when the LM outputs the answer most likely to be correct (`y = argmax_{y'} p(y' ≡ y*)`) and provides a confidence estimate `q` that matches the true probability of success (`q = p(y ≡ y*)`). The paper notes that unbounded proper scoring rules, such as log-likelihood, might not offer the same correctness incentive and could potentially motivate models to output incorrect answers.

**Training Details:**
- **Base RL Algorithm:** Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) is used as the foundational RL algorithm, with some modifications.
- **Base Model:** Qwen2.5-7B is selected as the base LM.
- **Initialization:** RL training is initialized directly from the pre-trained base model, without KL regularization.
- **Output Format:** Models are prompted to generate structured outputs using specific tags: `<think>` for reasoning, `<answer>` for the final prediction, `<analysis>` for uncertainty reasoning, and `<confidence>` for the numerical confidence score. A format reward is applied to encourage adherence to this structure.
- **SFT Warmup (for Math tasks):** A variant for mathematical reasoning tasks incorporates a lightweight Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) warmup phase. This involves generating solutions and using an expert model (Deepseek-R1) to produce uncertainty analyses, which are then used to fine-tune the model for higher-quality uncertainty reasoning before RL.

**Evaluation Metrics:**
The performance of models is assessed using a combination of accuracy and calibration metrics:
- **Accuracy (↑):** Standard measure of correct predictions.
- **Area Under ROC Curve (AUROC) (↑):** Measures the ability of confidence scores to distinguish correct from incorrect answers.
- **Brier Score (↓):** Quantifies the mean squared difference between predicted confidences and actual outcomes.
- **Expected Calibration Error (ECE) (↓):** Measures the average difference between accuracy and confidence across different confidence bins.

**Evaluation Datasets:**
The methodology involves evaluation on diverse datasets chosen to highlight various sources of uncertainty:
- **In-domain:**
 - **HotpotQA-Modified:** A multi-hop factual QA dataset where relevant paragraphs are systematically removed (0, 1, or both) to introduce varying levels of information completeness.
 - **Big-Math, GSM8K, Math-500:** Mathematical reasoning tasks.
- **Out-of-domain (OOD):**
 - **Factual QA:** TriviaQA, SimpleQA.
 - **Complex Reasoning:** GPQA (graduate-level QA).
 - **Commonsense Reasoning:** CommonsenseQA.
 - Other math datasets for math-trained models.

**Comparison Methods:**
RLCR is benchmarked against several baselines:
- **Base Model:** The pre-trained Qwen2.5-7B model.
- **RLVR:** Standard RL training using only the binary correctness reward.
- **RLVR + Classifiers/Probe:** Methods that train a separate classifier (BCE, Brier, or linear probe) on top of an RLVR model's outputs to predict confidence post-hoc.
- **Answer Probability:** Uses the average token probability of the generated answer as a confidence proxy.
- **RLCR without Analysis / RLVR with Analysis:** Ablation studies to disentangle the impact of the reward function from explicit uncertainty reasoning.
- **RL-Calibration Baselines:** Comparisons to prior RL methods that optimize solely for calibration or use abstention rewards.

This comprehensive methodology allows for a thorough assessment of RLCR's effectiveness in achieving both accurate and well-calibrated reasoning.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings regarding RLCR's effectiveness in improving the calibration of reasoning models while maintaining or improving accuracy.

#### 4.1. Theoretical Guarantees for Accuracy and Calibration

The paper provides a theoretical proof demonstrating that the proposed `R_RLCR` reward function inherently incentivizes both properties. It establishes that the expected `R_RLCR` is maximized when the model outputs the most likely correct answer and provides a confidence estimate that precisely matches the true probability of that answer being correct. A critical aspect of this proof is the requirement for the calibration term to be derived from a *bounded* proper scoring rule (e.g., Brier score), contrasting with unbounded rules like log-likelihood, which could potentially incentivize models to produce incorrect answers for optimal calibration rewards.

#### 4.2. Empirical Performance on In-domain and Out-of-domain Tasks

- **HotpotQA (Factual QA):**
 - **In-domain:** RLCR achieves accuracy comparable to standard RLVR (e.g., 62.1% vs. 63.0%) but substantially improves calibration metrics. For instance, Expected Calibration Error (ECE) is reduced significantly (from 0.37 for RLVR to 0.03 for RLCR), and AUROC is improved (0.50 for RLVR to 0.69 for RLCR). Post-hoc classifiers also show calibration improvements over RLVR but are marginally outperformed by RLCR.
 - **Out-of-domain (OOD):** On an average across six diverse OOD datasets, RLVR worsens calibration compared to the base model. In contrast, RLCR substantially improves OOD calibration (ECE 0.46 for RLVR to 0.21 for RLCR) while maintaining or slightly improving accuracy (53.9% for RLVR to 56.2% for RLCR). This suggests better generalization of calibrated uncertainty.

- **Big-Math (Mathematical Reasoning):**
 - **In-domain:** All RL methods, including RLCR, significantly improve accuracy over the base model. RLCR matches RLVR accuracy (72.7% vs. 72.9%) and substantially improves calibration (ECE 0.26 for RLVR to 0.10 for RLCR).
 - **SFT+RLCR Variant:** This variant, incorporating an SFT warmup, achieves the best in-domain calibration (ECE 0.08) but incurs a significant drop in OOD accuracy (from 50.9% for vanilla RLCR to 43.8%), attributed in part to catastrophic forgetting and formatting biases. A slight prompt adjustment during evaluation partially mitigates this OOD accuracy drop.
 - **OOD:** RLCR maintains competitive accuracy while offering strong calibration compared to baselines.

#### 4.3. Test-Time Scaling with Verbalized Confidences

RLCR's verbalized confidence scores are shown to be leverageable for test-time scaling methods:
- **Accuracy Improvement:** Confidence-weighted majority vote, utilizing RLCR's verbalized confidences, consistently outperforms vanilla majority vote, max-confidence selection, and likelihood-based baselines in improving accuracy with increased sample size (N).
- **Calibration Improvement:** Ensembling multiple uncertainty analysis chains for a fixed answer (sampling K analyses) modestly improves the overall Brier score, indicating a reduction in residual noise in confidence estimates.

#### 4.4. Self-Consistency of Verbalized Confidences

- **Intra-solution Coherence:** When generating multiple uncertainty reasoning chains for the same answer, RLCR models exhibit low standard deviation in their confidence scores, suggesting that the model's confidence estimates are generally consistent for a given solution.
- **Inter-solution Consistency:** For tasks with mutually exclusive answers, RLCR models distribute confidence across distinct answers such that the sum of confidences clusters closer to 1 (the ideal sum for exhaustive and exclusive outcomes) compared to RLVR, reflecting improved calibration in belief distribution, though some OOD overconfidence persists.

#### 4.5. Drivers of Calibration Gains (Ablation Study)

An ablation study investigates the individual contributions of explicit uncertainty reasoning (via `<analysis>` tags) and the calibration-aware reward function:
- **Explicit Uncertainty Reasoning:** Adding uncertainty analysis (`<analysis>` tags) improves calibration for both RLCR and RLVR variants.
- **Reward-based Calibration:** The calibration-aware reward learning in RLCR is substantially more effective than prompting for uncertainty reasoning alone. RLCR without explicit analysis (`RLCR w/o Analysis`) still significantly outperforms `RLVR w/ Analysis` in calibration, while maintaining comparable accuracy and token efficiency to vanilla RLVR.
- **Combined Effect:** The strongest performance is achieved when both explicit analysis and calibration-aware reward learning are used together (vanilla RLCR).

#### 4.6. Generalization to Other Models and Comparison to Baselines

- **Other Models:** Experiments on OlMo-2-7B-Instruct and Qwen3-8B confirm similar trends, with RLCR achieving comparable accuracy to RLVR while significantly improving calibration.
- **RL-Calibration Baselines:** Prior RL methods primarily optimizing for calibration (e.g., "Calibration RL" variants or "Abstention-RL") are outperformed by RLCR. Methods optimizing *only* for calibration can lead to degenerate solutions (e.g., outputting invalid answers with zero confidence), while RLCR's joint optimization prevents this.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of RLCR represents a notable development in the training of reasoning-capable language models, addressing a critical limitation of existing reinforcement learning paradigms that often prioritize accuracy over calibrated uncertainty.

**Key Contributions and Impact:**

1. **Joint Optimization of Accuracy and Calibration:** RLCR demonstrates that it is feasible to train LMs to simultaneously improve both their task performance and the reliability of their confidence estimates. This moves beyond the traditional trade-off observed with binary reward functions, which tend to degrade calibration and foster overconfidence.
2. **Theoretical Foundation:** The theoretical proof that `R_RLCR` (or any analogous reward using a *bounded*, proper scoring rule) incentivizes both accuracy and calibration provides a strong grounding for the method. This helps in understanding why methods based on unbounded scoring rules might exhibit less desirable behavior.
3. **Improved Model Trustworthiness:** By producing models that are not only accurate but also capable of reasoning about and communicating their uncertainty in a calibrated manner, RLCR enhances the trustworthiness of LMs. This is particularly impactful for high-stakes applications in domains such as healthcare, legal analysis, or engineering, where miscalibrated confidence can lead to significant errors and adverse consequences.
4. **Enhanced Generalization of Uncertainty:** The finding that RLCR improves calibration not only on in-domain tasks but also on out-of-domain evaluations suggests that the models learn a more robust and transferable understanding of their own uncertainty. This improves the adaptability and reliability of LMs in novel or unseen scenarios.
5. **Leveraging Verbalized Confidence:** The demonstration that explicit, verbalized confidence scores from RLCR can be effectively integrated into test-time scaling methods (e.g., confidence-weighted majority vote) offers practical avenues for boosting performance and reliability without additional external reward models or supervision. This provides a direct utility for the calibrated outputs.
6. **Insights into Uncertainty Reasoning:** The ablation studies highlight that both the explicit process of uncertainty analysis within the reasoning chain and the calibration-aware reward function independently contribute to improved calibration. This provides valuable insights for future research on the mechanisms through which LMs learn and express uncertainty.
7. **Path Towards More Reliable AI Systems:** This work contributes to the broader goal of developing more reliable, robust, and transparent AI systems. By enabling LMs to communicate "what they don't know," RLCR moves towards a more sophisticated form of intelligence that is essential for complex decision-making environments.

While the paper acknowledges that there remains significant room for improvement, particularly regarding out-of-domain calibration and occasional overconfidence in contradictory answers, the results suggest a promising direction. RLCR offers a practical modification to existing reasoning training methods that could lead to more generally dependable and accountable language models.
