# Learning Native Continuation for Action Chunking Flow Policies

> [!summary] Summary
> Legato introduces a training-time continuation mechanism for action chunking flow policies, reshaping the learned dynamics to achieve inherent chunk-to-chunk continuity. This approach improves trajectory smoothness, reduces robot hesitation, and shortens task completion times by approximately 10% on diverse real-world manipulation tasks.

## Problem

- Action chunking in Vision Language Action (VLA) models suffers from discontinuities at chunk boundaries, leading to hesitations and abrupt robot motions.
- Existing continuation methods like Real-Time Chunking (RTC) act as external, inference-time interventions, failing to integrate continuity natively into the learned policy dynamics.
- One-shot guidance applied at the beginning of denoising is insufficient, as initial constraints drift over subsequent denoising steps in multi-step flow-based policies.

## Method

- **Legato** defines an ==action-noise mixture== and a schedule-shaped target velocity field using a horizon-wise continuation vector (ω) to modulate transport magnitude.
- The method derives and trains the policy on an exact ==continuous-time ODE== that strictly aligns the learned dynamics with the effective dynamics of per-step, schedule-shaped guidance during inference.
- It randomizes inference delay (d) and ramp length (r) parameters during training and conditions the policy on the ==ω schedule==, enabling a single model to adapt to varying delays and desired smoothness without retraining.

## Results

- Legato achieved approximately **10%** shorter task completion times on average across five diverse real-world manipulation tasks compared to the Real-Time Chunking (RTC) baseline.
- The method consistently produced smoother trajectories, demonstrated by lower Negative SPARC (NSPARC) values and significantly reduced Chunk-Overlap RMSE compared to RTC.
- Legato exhibited robustness and adaptability to varying inference delays and ramp lengths, maintaining high performance and control over smoothness without requiring policy retraining for different conditions.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Effective chunk continuation in flow policies requires per-step guidance throughout the multi-step denoising process, as one-shot guidance is insufficient to maintain continuity.
> - Strict training-inference consistency is crucial, necessitating a reshaping of the policy's learned velocity field to inherently account for per-step, schedule-shaped guidance dynamics.
> - Schedule-shaped velocity reweighting suppresses spurious multimodal oscillations at chunk boundaries, leading to more decisive action generation and smoother trajectories.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Broader Research Landscape

**Context of VLA and Action Chunking**
Vision Language Action (VLA) models have emerged as a significant development in robotic control, enabling end-to-end visuomotor policies. A common strategy for deploying these large models in real-world robotic systems is action chunking. This technique involves predicting sequences of action vectors (chunks) per inference step, which amortizes the inference cost and allows for high-frequency control. Action chunking has been successfully integrated with various generative policy formulations, including diffusion-based and flow-based methods.

**Limitations of Existing Methods**
While action chunking improves inference efficiency, it introduces a fundamental challenge: discontinuities at chunk boundaries. These discontinuities arise from inference delays and the inherent multimodality of flow-based policies. Naive chunked execution often results in visible hesitations and abrupt changes in robot motion.

To address this, several methods have been proposed:
- **Real-Time Chunking (RTC)**: This approach attempts to mitigate discontinuities by applying inference-time inpainting. It constrains newly generated action chunks based on previously executed actions in their overlapping regions. While RTC enhances continuity compared to naive execution, its continuation mechanism is external to the policy's learning process. Consequently, policies trained without intrinsic continuity are prone to spurious multimodal switching across chunk boundaries, producing trajectories that are not inherently smooth. This can manifest as hesitation and prolonged task completion times.
- **Other Trajectory Continuation Methods**: Approaches like Bidirectional Decoding (BID) employ rejection sampling to maintain continuity. Diffusion- and flow-based policies have explored conditioning mechanisms, such as Diffusion Forcing and Rolling Diffusion Policy, often focusing on timestep-level generation or soft alignments using methods like classifier-free guidance (e.g., SAIL). These methods may not provide robust control over continuation strength or fully integrate continuity into the learned dynamics.
- **Training-time RTC**: More recently, training-time RTC has been introduced to incorporate continuation during training by conditioning on a hard action prefix. However, this still treats continuation as an external constraint and does not account for the effective denoising dynamics induced by repeated, schedule-shaped guidance during inference, leaving a potential mismatch between training and inference conditions.

**The Identified Gap**
The authors identify a critical gap: stable chunked execution requires action continuation to be a *native, learned property* of the policy, rather than an external, inference-time intervention or a hard constraint. Achieving this necessitates two core requirements: (i) **per-step guidance**, where guidance is applied iteratively across denoising steps, and (ii) **training-inference consistency**, ensuring that the policy's learned dynamics align with the guided inference process. Existing methods either rely on external mechanisms or fail to fully satisfy both requirements, leading to suboptimal smoothness and task performance. This paper aims to directly address this gap by proposing a method that internalizes continuation into the policy's learned denoising dynamics.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this work stems from the limitations of current action chunking strategies in VLA models, specifically the issue of discontinuities and multimodal switching at chunk boundaries. The authors observe that existing solutions, particularly Real-Time Chunking (RTC), operate as inference-time interventions, which do not intrinsically integrate continuation into the learned policy. This leads to trajectories that lack inherent smoothness, causing observable hesitation and increased task completion times.

The key objectives of this research are:

1. **Develop a Native Continuation Mechanism**: The central goal is to create a training-time continuation method, named Legato, that makes chunk-to-chunk continuation an intrinsic, learned property of action-chunked flow-based VLA policies. This contrasts with external, inference-time corrections.
2. **Ensure Per-Step Guidance**: To effectively maintain continuity throughout the multi-step denoising process characteristic of flow-based models, the policy needs to be guided repeatedly at each denoising step. The authors empirically demonstrate that one-shot guidance at initialization is insufficient, as the initially constrained action prefixes tend to drift away from the reference during subsequent denoising steps.
3. **Achieve Training-Inference Consistency**: A critical objective is to ensure that the dynamics learned during training are strictly consistent with the dynamics experienced during inference, especially when per-step guidance is applied. This alignment is intended to prevent mismatches that can degrade performance and make the policy less robust.
4. **Introduce Schedule-Shaped Guidance**: The method aims to use a smooth, schedule-shaped guidance rather than a hard clamp. This schedule is designed to anchor the beginning of a chunk to known actions and gradually reduce guidance strength, providing fine-grained control over continuity.
5. **Support Varying Inference Delays and Smoothness Control**: Real-world deployment often involves variations in inference latency and requires adjustable control over the strength of continuation. A key objective is to enable a single trained policy to adapt to different inference delays and desired ramp lengths without requiring retraining.
6. **Improve Trajectory Smoothness and Task Efficiency**: Ultimately, the work aims to produce robot trajectories that are smoother, exhibit reduced spurious multimodal switching, lead to less hesitation, and consequently result in shorter task completion times in real-world manipulation scenarios.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Legato proposes a training-time continuation mechanism designed to internalize chunk-to-chunk continuity into the learned denoising dynamics of flow-based VLA policies. This approach is structured around three main components: an action-noise mixture, the derivation of effective dynamics for repeated guidance, and a method for achieving training-inference consistency, augmented by schedule randomization and conditioning.

**Overview and Problem Statement**
The authors argue that existing inference-time continuation methods like RTC, while helpful, do not train the policy to intrinsically generate smooth transitions. Moreover, they identify that merely applying one-shot guidance at the beginning of the denoising process is insufficient, as the initial constraints tend to drift over subsequent denoising steps (illustrated in Figure 4). This necessitates a policy that learns to maintain continuity under per-step guidance, while ensuring training and inference dynamics remain consistent.

**Flow Matching and RTC Preliminaries**
- **Flow Matching (FM)**: Flow-based generative models train a neural velocity field to transport samples from an initial noise distribution to a target data distribution (e.g., ground-truth action `A`). The standard interpolation path is `X_t = (1 - t)ϵ + tA`, and the target velocity field is `u_FM(X_t, t) = A - ϵ`.
- **Real-Time Chunking (RTC)**: RTC introduced an asynchronous execution scheme for VLA models, overlapping inference and execution. It defines a structured guidance schedule over an action chunk of horizon `H`, characterized by inference delay `d`, executed length `s`, and ramp length `r` (where `r + s + d = H`). Guidance is applied at inference time to encourage agreement with previously executed actions, especially in the `d` and `r` regions.

**The Necessity of Per-Step Guidance**
The paper empirically demonstrates that a flow policy trained with one-shot prefix guidance (where noise `ϵ` is partially replaced by reference actions `A_ref` in the overlap region `ϵ' = (1 - m) ⊙ ϵ + m ⊙ A_ref`) fails to preserve continuity throughout the multi-step denoising process. The overlap actions tend to drift away from the desired reference as denoising progresses. This observation underscores the requirement for per-step guidance during inference to maintain effective continuation.

**Legato's Native Continuation Mechanism**

1. **Action-Noise Mixture and Schedule-Shaped Velocity**:
 - Legato introduces a horizon-wise continuation vector `ω ∈^H` that encodes the guidance schedule. This `ω` vector provides fine-grained control over guidance strength, from full guidance (`ω_i = 1`) near the chunk's beginning to zero guidance (`ω_i = 0`) at the end.
 - An "effective noise initialization" `ϵ_eff` is defined as a mixture of pure noise `ϵ` and the ground-truth action `A`: `ϵ_eff = (1 - ω) ⊙ ϵ + ω ⊙ A`.
 - This `ϵ_eff` then defines the interpolation path during training: `Y_t = (1 - t)ϵ_eff + tA`.
 - The corresponding target flow-matching velocity for this path is `u_FM(Y_t, t) = A - ϵ_eff = (1 - ω) ⊙ (A - ϵ)`. This `(1 - ω)` term introduces a "schedule-shaped velocity" where the target transport magnitude is modulated by `ω`. In regions with strong continuation (large `ω_i`), the velocity `u_FM_i` is suppressed, making these regions less mutable and discouraging multimodal switching. As `ω` gradually decreases, the effect of continuation relaxes, promoting smooth transitions.

2. **Dynamics of Repeated Guidance**:
 - At inference time, effective continuation requires applying guidance `Y_k = (1 - ω) ⊙ X_k + ω ⊙ A_ref` before *every* denoising step `X_{k+1} = Y_k + Δt f_θ(Y_k, t_k)`.
 - The authors derive the exact continuous-time ordinary differential equation (ODE) that corresponds to this repeated guidance and denoising process: `˙Y(t) = (1 - ω) ⊙ f_θ(Y(t), t) - κ ⊙ (Y(t) - A)`, where `κ = ω/Δt`. This ODE describes the effective dynamics of per-step guided inference.

3. **Training-Inference Consistency**:
 - To ensure consistency, Legato requires that the policy's learned velocity field `f_θ` generates dynamics during inference (as described by the derived ODE) that match the target flow-matching velocity `u_FM` from the action-noise mixture.
 - By equating `(1 - ω) ⊙ f_θ(Y, t) - κ ⊙ (Y - A) = u_FM(Y, t)` and solving for `f_θ(Y, t)`, the authors obtain the Legato target velocity field: `f_θ(Y, t) = (1 - ω)^(-1) ⊙ [u_FM(Y, t) + κ ⊙ (Y - A)]`.
 - Substituting the definitions for `u_FM` and `Y_t`, the closed-form target velocity `v_target(t, A, ϵ, ω) = [1 - κ ⊙ (1 - t)] ⊙ (A - ϵ)` is derived.
 - During training, the neural network `f_θ(Y_t, o, t, ω)` is supervised to predict this `v_target`. This means the network learns a reshaped velocity field that inherently accounts for the per-step, schedule-shaped guidance dynamics, thereby achieving strict training-inference consistency.
 - **Inference**: At inference, the previously generated chunk (specifically, its non-executed portion) serves as the reference action `A_ref`. The process initializes with an action-noise mixture `Y_0 = ω ⊙ A_ref + (1 - ω) ⊙ ϵ` and then integrates the learned `f_θ` from `t=0` to `t=1` using the derived per-step guidance dynamics, with the same number of denoising steps as training.

**Schedule Randomization and Conditioning**
- To make Legato robust to varying inference latencies (`d`) and to allow flexible control over smoothness (`r`), the authors randomize the `(d, r)` parameters during training. This exposes the policy to a diverse range of guidance schedules.
- The policy is explicitly conditioned on the resulting schedule `ω` by appending it as an additional feature dimension to the noisy action `Y_t`. This allows a single trained model to adapt to different runtime delays and desired smoothness strengths without requiring retraining.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The authors conducted extensive real-world experiments and ablation studies to evaluate Legato's performance against baselines across various manipulation tasks and conditions.

**Experimental Setup**
- **Tasks**: Five diverse real-world robotic manipulation tasks were used: Stack Bowls, Pour Things, Pick and Place, Fold Towel, and Open Drawer. These tasks encompass different motion patterns (e.g., rotation/translation dominant) and multimodal action selections (e.g., multiple grasp goals, arm selection).
- **Evaluation Metrics**:
 - **Task Completion Score**: A graded, task-specific score reflecting progress and handling of failure cases, with higher scores indicating better completion.
 - **Task Completion Time**: Total time to complete a task, measuring execution efficiency and reflecting hesitation. Lower times are better.
 - **Trajectory Smoothness Metrics**:
 - **Negative SPARC (NSPARC)**: Measures smoothness of the velocity profile in the frequency domain. Lower values indicate smoother global speed modulation with reduced high-frequency fluctuations.
 - **Negative LDLJ (NLDLJ)**: Quantifies high-order geometric smoothness (jerk). Lower values indicate reduced overall jerk energy.
 - **Chunk-Overlap RMSE**: Measures local trajectory continuity between consecutive chunks in the overlapping delay segment. Lower values indicate better alignment.
- **Baselines**: The primary baseline was Real-Time Chunking (RTC). Comparisons were also made with Training-time RTC and Temporal Ensembling (TE).
- **Models and Training**: Both Legato and baselines were initialized from the same `π_0.5` pretrained VLA model checkpoint and trained under identical conditions (datasets, hyperparameters, training steps).

**Main Results (Legato vs. RTC)**
Across all five manipulation tasks, Legato consistently outperformed RTC:

- **Task Efficiency**: Legato achieved significantly shorter task completion times across all tasks (approximately 10% average improvement). This was attributed to Legato's schedule-shaped velocity reweighting, which suppresses spurious multimodal oscillations, leading to more decisive action generation and less hesitation. For example, in the bowl-stacking task, RTC often alternated between competing grasp goals or execution arms, while Legato maintained consistent mode selection (Figure 6), leading to faster completion.
- **Trajectory Smoothness**: Legato demonstrated clear advantages in smoothness:
 - **NSPARC**: Consistently lower NSPARC values across all tasks, indicating reduced high-frequency velocity fluctuations and more regular speed modulation, corresponding to visually smoother motions (Figure 1).
 - **Chunk-Overlap RMSE**: Substantially reduced across tasks, signifying improved continuity at action boundaries and more coherent chunk-to-chunk transitions.
 - **NLDLJ**: Improvements were observed, though not always statistically significant across all tasks. Importantly, NLDLJ did not degrade, suggesting that while local continuity improved, global high-order smoothness was maintained.
- **Task Success**: Legato achieved higher task completion scores than RTC, indicating that the improvements in efficiency and smoothness translated into more reliable and effective real-world manipulation performance.

**Comparative Analysis with Other Baselines**

- **Comparison with Training-Time RTC**: On the pour task, Legato achieved higher task scores, shorter completion times, and improved NSPARC and NLDLJ compared to Training-time RTC (Table II). This suggests that reshaping the policy dynamics (as Legato does) is more effective for chunk continuation than merely enforcing hard overlap constraints.
- **Comparison with Temporal Ensembling (TE)**: Legato outperformed TE (a trajectory smoothing baseline) in both execution time and continuation quality (Table VI), benefiting from its native continuation mechanism.

**Ablation Studies**

- **Varying Execution Stride (s)**: Across different `(d, s, r)` schedule configurations, Legato consistently outperformed RTC. The study revealed a controllable trade-off: decreasing `s` (which implies a larger ramp length `r` given fixed `d`) improved chunk-to-chunk consistency (lower overlap RMSE) but could degrade global smoothness (higher NSPARC) due to more frequent overlap regions accumulating high-frequency components. Shortening `r` with a small `s` improved frequency-domain smoothness (NSPARC) at the cost of weaker overlap consistency (higher overlap RMSE).
- **Varying Inference Delay (d)**: Legato consistently outperformed RTC across different `d` values (Table III). Reducing `d` (and increasing `r` proportionally) improved both chunk-to-chunk continuity and frequency-domain smoothness. These results demonstrate Legato's robustness to varying inference latency.
- **Role of Schedule Conditioning**: Removing the explicit conditioning of the policy on the schedule `ω` (the "condition row") led to degraded performance, particularly in trajectory smoothness and execution stability (Table IV). This suggests that `ω` helps the model disambiguate and adapt to different continuation regimes.
- **Model Generality**: Legato's benefits extended to other VLA models, specifically `π_0` and `Gr00t N1.6` (Table V). It consistently outperformed RTC on these different backbones for the pour task, demonstrating its robustness and broad applicability across flow-based VLA architectures.
- **Sensitivity to Denoising Steps (N)**: Legato, especially with a heuristic rescaling applied at inference time (to address training-inference mismatch when `N_infer ≠ N_train`), showed robustness to varying `N_infer`. Without the condition row, it even remained effective under single-step denoising (Table VII), although results with the condition row showed instability at `N=1`.

**Simulation Results on Kinetix**
- Legato outperformed RTC, Training-time RTC, and BID in Kinetix simulations, particularly when inference delay `d > 0` (Figure 8, left). This highlighted Legato's generalization across different flow-based policies (Kinetix uses a UNet-based policy).
- Ablation on schedule randomization in Kinetix (Figure 8, right) showed that fixing `d` during training degraded performance at other `d` values during evaluation, emphasizing the importance of delay randomization for robustness.

**Preliminary Study**
A preliminary study (Table A.2) empirically confirmed that one-shot guidance is insufficient. Compared to Legato's per-step guidance, one-shot guidance resulted in significantly worse overlap RMSE and completion times, demonstrating that the overlap region progressively deviates from the desired constraint without repeated guidance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presented in this paper, through the introduction of Legato, offers a notable advancement in the field of robotic control with Vision Language Action (VLA) models, particularly concerning the practical deployment of action chunking policies.

**Key Contribution**
Legato's primary contribution is the development of a training-time continuation framework that fundamentally redefines how action chunking policies handle transitions between action segments. By reshaping the learned flow dynamics to align training and inference under schedule-shaped, per-step continuation, Legato effectively makes chunk continuation a *native property* of the policy, rather than an external, inference-time intervention. This addresses a core limitation of previous methods, which often struggled with discontinuities and spurious multimodal switching at chunk boundaries.

**Advances in Robotics**
This work advances the capabilities of VLA models in real-world robotic systems by offering a robust solution to a pervasive problem in chunked execution. By improving trajectory smoothness and reducing hesitation, Legato enables robots to perform manipulation tasks with greater fluidity and naturalness, which is crucial for safety, human-robot interaction, and overall task reliability. The ability to control smoothness through randomized schedules also adds a layer of adaptability that is highly desirable in dynamic environments.

**Practical Benefits and Impact**
- **Enhanced Trajectory Smoothness**: Legato consistently produces smoother robot trajectories, evidenced by improved NSPARC and reduced Chunk-overlap RMSE. This translates to more aesthetically pleasing, predictable, and robust robot motions, which can enhance user confidence and system reliability.
- **Increased Task Efficiency**: By suppressing spurious multimodal switching and reducing hesitation, Legato leads to significantly shorter task completion times. This is a direct practical benefit for applications requiring high throughput or efficiency.
- **Improved Robustness and Adaptability**: The introduction of randomized schedule conditioning allows a single Legato policy to adapt to varying inference delays and desired levels of continuation strength without retraining. This flexibility makes Legato highly adaptable to different hardware platforms and runtime optimization strategies, simplifying deployment and increasing the practical utility of VLA models.
- **Generalizability Across Models**: The empirical results demonstrate that Legato's benefits extend to different flow-based VLA architectures (e.g., `π_0`, `Gr00t N1.6`), indicating its broad applicability and model generality.
- **Reduced Training-Inference Mismatch**: By ensuring strict training-inference consistency, Legato builds policies that are inherently more stable and reliable under guided execution, addressing a fundamental challenge in the development of iterative generative models for control.

**Future Work**
The authors identify an area for future research, specifically investigating more flexible native continuation schemes that can adjust the denoising step count (N) during inference while rigorously maintaining full training-inference consistency. This suggests a continued effort towards even greater adaptability and robustness in real-world robotic deployments.
