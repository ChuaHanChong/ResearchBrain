# SPiDR: A Simple Approach for Zero-Shot Safety in Sim-to-Real Transfer

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from ETH Zurich, Caltech, and Johns Hopkins University developed SPiDR, a method for zero-shot safe sim-to-real transfer in reinforcement learning, which enhances domain randomization by adding an uncertainty-based cost penalty. This approach ensures policies trained exclusively in simulation satisfy safety constraints upon first deployment on real-world robotic platforms like race cars and quadruped robots, outperforming standard domain randomization without significantly compromising task performance.

## Problem

- Ensuring policies trained solely in simulators operate safely upon zero-shot deployment in real-world, safety-critical applications.
- The 'sim-to-real gap' leads to unsafe behaviors or degraded performance when policies trained in simulation are transferred to physical systems.
- Existing robust RL methods often lack scalability for complex tasks, while popular methods like Domain Randomization (DR) provide no formal safety guarantees and can underestimate real-world costs.

## Method

- **SPiDR** extends ==domain randomization== by introducing a state-action dependent 'pessimistic' cost penalty, derived from an ensemble of dynamics models.
- This penalty quantifies uncertainty regarding the sim-to-real gap by approximating the L1-Wasserstein distance between simulated and real dynamics.
- The algorithm trains a policy using a standard Constrained Markov Decision Process (CMDP) solver on this penalized cost, making it modular and compatible with existing RL pipelines.

## Results

- SPiDR achieved zero-shot safe transfer to real-world robotic platforms (race car, Unitree Go1 quadruped robot), where standard domain randomization failed by violating safety constraints.
- The method maintained task performance comparable to or exceeding baselines while consistently satisfying safety constraints across various simulated benchmarks (RWRL, Safety Gym, RaceCar).
- SPiDR demonstrated scalability to vision-based control tasks and robustness to its pessimism parameter choice, with minimal computational overhead for practical ensemble sizes.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The true safety cost on a real system can be robustly upper-bounded by the average simulated cost plus a term that quantifies the model discrepancy (uncertainty about the sim-to-real gap).
> - Uncertainty about model discrepancy can be reliably approximated using the sum of component-wise empirical variances from an ensemble of domain-randomized dynamics models.
> - Incorporating this uncertainty as a cost penalty during training effectively steers the policy away from potentially unsafe regions, thereby guaranteeing safety upon zero-shot real-world deployment.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has demonstrated capabilities in various domains, from games to robotics and autonomous driving. A critical challenge for its real-world deployment, particularly in safety-critical applications, is ensuring that policies trained in simulators can be safely transferred to physical systems. This challenge is referred to as the "sim-to-real gap," where discrepancies between simulated and real-world dynamics can lead to unsafe behaviors when policies are deployed without real-world interaction.

Existing approaches to address this issue fall into several categories:
- **Robust Optimization-based Methods:** These techniques provide theoretical safety guarantees by formulating the problem as a robust Constrained Markov Decision Process (CMDP). While theoretically sound, they often require significant modifications to existing RL training pipelines, limiting their practical adoption and scalability to complex continuous control tasks.
- **Domain Randomization (DR):** This widely adopted method enhances policy robustness by training agents across a diverse range of randomized simulator environments. Its simplicity and compatibility with standard RL algorithms make it practical and scalable. However, DR lacks formal safety guarantees and has been shown to fail in satisfying safety constraints in real-world scenarios, particularly when the sim-to-real gap is substantial or when worst-case costs are underestimated.
- **Practical Methods with Online Data/Fine-tuning:** Some approaches leverage limited real-world data for fine-tuning or utilize high-fidelity simulators before deployment. These methods can improve transfer but often require some interaction with the real system or computationally expensive intermediate steps, which may not align with "zero-shot" deployment requirements where safety must be guaranteed on first contact.

This work positions itself at the intersection of provable safety and practical scalability. It aims to bridge the gap between theoretically grounded robust safe RL and the practical applicability of domain randomization. The authors identify a need for a method that offers formal safety guarantees for zero-shot sim-to-real transfer while being modular and compatible with existing, scalable RL training pipelines that predominantly rely on domain randomization. SPiDR attempts to fulfill this role by integrating uncertainty quantification directly into the safety constraints of domain-randomized training.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a scalable and practical algorithm that ensures zero-shot safe sim-to-real transfer for reinforcement learning policies. This means that an agent, trained exclusively in a simulator, must be able to operate safely when deployed in the real world without any prior interaction or fine-tuning on the physical system.

The motivation for this objective stems from several identified challenges:
1. **Safety as a Prerequisite:** For RL to achieve widespread adoption in real-world, safety-critical applications (e.g., robotics, autonomous systems), guaranteeing safe behavior and preventing catastrophic failures is paramount. Unsafe interactions during initial deployment are unacceptable.
2. **The Sim-to-Real Gap:** Simulators, despite their advancements, are imperfect models of reality. Policies trained in simulation often exhibit degraded or unsafe performance in the real world due to this "sim-to-real gap," where dynamics, sensor noise, or actuator characteristics differ.
3. **Limitations of Existing Approaches:**
 - **Robust Optimization:** While theoretically sound for providing safety guarantees, robust RL methods often involve complex minimax formulations that are difficult to scale to high-dimensional continuous control tasks and require significant alterations to standard RL frameworks. This limits their practical adoption.
 - **Domain Randomization:** As a widely used and scalable approach for improving sim-to-real transfer, domain randomization (DR) involves training policies across varied simulated environments. However, DR, which often averages over costs or rewards across randomized domains, does not inherently provide safety guarantees for individual real-world deployments. The authors argue and demonstrate that DR can *underestimate* the true costs in the real environment, leading to constraint violations.
4. **Need for Compatibility and Scalability:** There is a demand for a safe sim-to-real method that seamlessly integrates with existing, high-performing RL algorithms and massively parallel simulation environments, rather than requiring specialized solvers or complex setup.

The key insight motivating SPiDR's design is the observation that unsafe transfer can be linked to **large uncertainty about the sim-to-real gap**. The authors theoretically posit that the true safety constraint on a real system can be *upper-bounded* by the average constraint observed in a domain-randomized simulator *plus a term quantifying the model discrepancy* (Lemma 4.1). This discrepancy is related to the uncertainty regarding how different simulated dynamics might predict future states. If the simulator's predictions are highly uncertain, it indicates a region where the model is less reliable, and thus, where unsafe behaviors are more likely in the real world. By identifying and penalizing these regions of high uncertainty, a policy can be steered away from potentially unsafe states, even if they appear safe in the averaged simulated environment. This forms the basis for SPiDR's "pessimistic" approach to domain randomization.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology of SPiDR is built upon the framework of Constrained Markov Decision Processes (CMDPs) and extends domain randomization by incorporating a pessimism principle derived from uncertainty quantification.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation: Constrained Markov Decision Processes (CMDPs) and Safe Sim-to-Real Transfer

The problem is defined as an infinite-horizon, discounted CMDP, `M = (S, A, p, r, c, γ, ρ)`. The objective is to find a policy `π` that maximizes the expected cumulative reward `J_p(π)` while satisfying an expected cumulative cost constraint `C_p(π) ≤ d`.
In the safe sim-to-real transfer setting, the agent trains in a simulator capable of generating environments `M_ξ = (S, A, p_ξ, r, c, γ, ρ)` parameterized by `ξ`. The real environment `M* = (S, A, p*, r, c, γ, ρ)` is unknown, and the policy must guarantee `C_p*(π) ≤ d` zero-shot, without direct interaction with `M*`.

#### 3.2. Domain Randomization (DR) and its Limitations for Safety

Standard DR for CMDPs involves optimizing for average reward and average cost across a distribution `μ` of randomized environments: `max_π E_ξ∼μ [J_pξ(π)] s.t. E_ξ∼μ [C_pξ(π)] ≤ d`. While practical and scalable, this approach averages costs, which can underestimate the true cost in the real environment `M*`. The paper demonstrates that `C_p*(π)` can be significantly higher than `E_ξ∼μ [C_pξ(π)]`, leading to constraint violations upon real-world deployment.

#### 3.3. A Pessimistic Upper Bound on Real-World Cost (Lemma 4.1)

To address the underestimation of costs, the paper establishes a theoretical upper bound for the real-world cost `C_p*(π)`:
`C_p*(π) ≤ E_ξ∼μ [C_pξ(π)] + E_ξ∼μ [E_(s,a)∼dpξ,π [ (γL_C / (1-γ)) * D_W(p_ξ, p*)(s,a) ]]`
This lemma states that the true cost is bounded by the expected simulated cost plus an "uncertainty penalty" term. This penalty depends on `L_C` (Lipschitz constant of the cost function), the discount factor `γ`, and the `L_1`-Wasserstein distance `D_W(p_ξ, p*)(s,a)` between the dynamics of the simulated environment `p_ξ` and the real environment `p*` at a given state-action pair `(s,a)`. This term quantifies the model mismatch and highlights that areas with high discrepancy contribute more to the potential for underestimated real-world costs.

#### 3.4. Algorithm Design: Penalized CMDPs and Approximating the Penalty Term

The insight from Lemma 4.1 motivates the use of a surrogate, penalized cost function `˜c(s, a)` during training:
`˜c(s, a) ≜ c(s, a) + (γL_C / (1-γ)) * max_ξ∈Ξ D_W(p_ξ, p*)(s,a)`
The `max_ξ D_W(p_ξ, p*)(s,a)` term represents the worst-case discrepancy. Since `L_C` and `D_W(p_ξ, p*)` are unknown in advance and cannot be directly estimated without real-world data, SPiDR proposes approximating this penalty using only simulated data.

The approximation for the penalty term is based on an **ensemble of dynamics models**: given `n` i.i.d. samples of next states `(s_1..., s_n)` predicted by `n` different domain-randomized dynamics `p_ξ_j` (all sampled from `μ`) for a given `(s, a)`, the **sum of component-wise empirical variances** `υ(s, a)` is used:
`υ(s, a) ≜ ||Var (s_1..., s_n)||_1 = Σ_j=1^dim(S) Var (s_1,j..., s_n,j)`
This `υ(s, a)` acts as a proxy for the uncertainty about the model discrepancy. Higher variance indicates greater disagreement among ensemble predictions, suggesting regions where the simulator is less reliable and the potential for real-world mismatch is higher. The paper formally shows that for a suitable constant `λ`, `λυ(s, a)` can upper-bound the true model discrepancy with high probability (Theorem C.8 in Appendix).

#### 3.5. The SPiDR Algorithm (Algorithm 1)

SPiDR modifies standard domain randomization by incorporating this uncertainty-based penalty:
1. **Sample Dynamics:** A batch of `N` dynamics `(ξ_i)^N_i=1` is sampled from `μ` for policy rollout. For *each* `ξ_i`, an *ensemble* of `n` dynamics `(p_ξ_ij)^n_j=1` (also sampled from `μ`) is used to estimate the penalty.
2. **Rollout Policy and Compute Penalized Cost:** For each step `(s_t, a_t)` in a trajectory rolled out with `p_ξ_i`:
 - The next state `s_t+1` is simulated using `p_ξ_i`.
 - The actual reward `r_t` and cost `c_t` are obtained.
 - `n` *additional* next states `s^(j)_t+1` are simulated using the ensemble `(p_ξ_ij)^n_j=1` (these are fast, parallel executions).
 - The penalty term `υ(s_t, a_t)` is computed using these `n` ensemble next states.
 - The penalized cost `˜c_t = c(s_t, a_t) + λυ(s_t, a_t)` is calculated.
3. **Solve Penalized CMDP:** The collected trajectories `(s_t, a_t, r_t, ˜c_t)` are then used by any standard CMDP solver (e.g., CRPO or a primal-dual method) to find the policy `˜π` that solves `max_π E_ξ∼μ [J_pξ(π)] s.t. E_ξ∼μ [C_pξ(π)] ≤ d`, where `C_pξ(π)` now uses `˜c`.

This design makes SPiDR highly modular and compatible, as it only modifies how the cost signal is generated during data collection, without altering the core policy optimization algorithm.

#### 3.6. Safety Guarantee (Theorem 4.2)

The paper formally proves that if `˜π` is a solution to the penalized CMDP (Equation 5), then it satisfies the safety constraint in the real environment: `C_p*(˜π) ≤ d`. This guarantee relies on the fact that `E_ξ∼μ [C_pξ(˜π)]` (the expected penalized cost in simulation) serves as an upper bound for `C_p*(˜π)`.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrate SPiDR's effectiveness in ensuring safety during zero-shot sim-to-real transfer across various robotic tasks and simulated benchmarks, consistently outperforming baselines in safety while maintaining strong performance.

#### 4.1. Real-World Deployment

- **Zero-Shot Safety:** On two distinct real-world robotic platforms—a remote-controlled race car and a Unitree Go1 quadruped robot—SPiDR successfully ensures constraint satisfaction upon zero-shot deployment. In contrast, standard Domain Randomization (DR) dramatically violates safety constraints in both tasks, even when appearing safe in simulation (Figure 3). This empirically validates Lemma 4.1 regarding constraint underestimation.
- **Performance Maintenance:** On the Unitree Go1, SPiDR achieves task completion rates comparable to DR, demonstrating that the safety guarantee does not lead to overly conservative policies that compromise performance significantly (Figure 4). For the race car, SPiDR even *outperforms* DR in the objective, as its conservative policies avoid high-speed overshooting behaviors that DR-trained agents sometimes exhibit. RAMU, another robust safe RL algorithm, showed poor performance on the Unitree Go1, often failing (falling) in a high percentage of trials compared to SPiDR.
- **Versatility:** SPiDR's effectiveness is demonstrated across different underlying RL algorithms. When combined with PPO (instead of SAC) and a primal-dual CMDP solver, SPiDR still achieves safe transfer to the real Unitree Go1, while DR fails (Figure 5). This indicates its broad applicability.

#### 4.2. Evaluation in the Sim-to-Sim Sandbox

Extensive sim-to-sim experiments across RWRL, Safety Gym, and RaceCar environments further support SPiDR's capabilities.
- **Constraint Satisfaction:** SPiDR consistently satisfies safety constraints across all six simulated tasks (PointGoal2, CartpoleSwingup, HumanoidWalk, QuadrupedRun, WalkerWalk, RaceCar) under significant sim-to-sim gaps. Nominal training and standard DR, as expected, fail to satisfy constraints in these evaluation environments (Figure 6).
- **Safety-Performance Tradeoff:** SPiDR maintains a balance of high performance while adhering to safety constraints. It achieves competitive objective performance relative to the optimal performance on the test environments. In tasks like HumanoidWalk, QuadrupedRun, WalkerWalk, and RaceCar, SPiDR matches or surpasses the performance of baselines (including RAMU on the RaceCar task) while being the only algorithm to consistently guarantee safety (Figure 7).
- **Scalability with Ensemble Size (`n`):** The performance of SPiDR is robust to the choice of ensemble size `n`. For `n ≥ 8`, safety is consistently maintained across tasks, with minimal additional computational overhead compared to standard DR. The relative runtime overhead for `n ≤ 32` is marginal, and training can be performed efficiently using GPU-accelerated simulators like MuJoCo XLA (Figure 8).
- **Vision-Based Control:** SPiDR scales to partially observable, vision-based control tasks (CartpoleSwingup). By using privileged state information to compute the uncertainty penalty during training, SPiDR satisfies constraints even when the policy operates directly on rendered images, with a small increase in training time (Figure 9).

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies (Appendix D and E)

- **Robustness to `λ`:** SPiDR demonstrates robustness to the choice of the pessimism parameter `λ`. A single `λ` value can maintain constraint satisfaction across different magnitudes of distribution shifts, although higher `λ` might lead to slightly more conservative behavior (Figure 11). A practical procedure for picking `λ` in real-world deployments is also provided, emphasizing initial conservative choices followed by iterative refinement.
- **Limitations of DR with Larger `Ξ`:** Simply enlarging the set of randomized parameters `Ξ` for standard DR does not necessarily guarantee safety. Experiments on the RaceCar environment show that DR can still violate constraints even with a wide range of parameter variations, underscoring the need for explicit pessimism (Figure 12).
- **Quantifying Constraint Underestimation:** Empirical measurements confirm that standard DR consistently underestimates the true cost in the evaluation environment, leading to the observed constraint violations. SPiDR effectively mitigates this underestimation (Figure 13).
- **`υ(s, a)` as an Upper Bound:** The empirical results show that the uncertainty estimator `λυ(s, a)` generally provides a sufficient over-approximation of the model discrepancy required to penalize the cost, making the constraint gap positive (Figure 14).
- **State-Dependent Uncertainty:** Analysis of `υ(s, a)` in the CartpoleSwingup task reveals that uncertainty increases with action magnitude and peaks in unstable regions (e.g., pole near upright position), indicating where model predictions are less reliable. This supports the state-action dependent penalization strategy (Figure 15).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents SPiDR, a contribution to the field of safe reinforcement learning and sim-to-real transfer. Its significance and potential impact are multi-faceted:

1. **Bridging Theory and Practice:** SPiDR addresses a critical gap by providing a scalable algorithm with formal safety guarantees that is highly compatible with existing, widely adopted RL training pipelines (e.g., domain randomization with SAC or PPO). This makes it more amenable to real-world adoption than many complex robust optimization methods.
2. **Provable Zero-Shot Safety:** The theoretical guarantee (Theorem 4.2) that SPiDR-trained policies will satisfy safety constraints in the real environment upon first deployment is a significant advancement. This zero-shot capability is crucial for safety-critical applications where direct interaction with the real system during learning or fine-tuning is impractical or dangerous.
3. **Enhanced Real-World Reliability:** The empirical validation on two distinct real-world robotic platforms (RaceCar and Unitree Go1) demonstrates SPiDR's practical applicability and its ability to prevent safety constraint violations that other baselines failed to avoid. This directly contributes to making RL deployment in robotics more reliable and trustworthy.
4. **Scalability and Efficiency:** SPiDR's integration with ensemble-based uncertainty estimation and massively parallel simulation environments (e.g., MuJoCo XLA) allows for efficient training. The low computational overhead of the uncertainty penalty and its robustness to ensemble size make it practical for complex, high-dimensional control tasks, including vision-based settings.
5. **Understanding Sim-to-Real Discrepancy:** The work provides a theoretical characterization (Lemma 4.1) of how the sim-to-real gap can lead to constraint underestimation in domain randomization, offering insight into a common failure mode. The proposed uncertainty metric `υ(s,a)` serves as an interpretable proxy for this discrepancy, highlighting regions of the state-action space where model predictions are less reliable.
6. **Broader Applicability:** While the experiments focus on robotic tasks, the simplicity and modularity of SPiDR suggest its potential extension to other safety-critical domains where RL is being considered, such as autonomous driving, healthcare, or industrial control.

The paper identifies future work in exploring hybrid approaches that combine SPiDR's simulation-trained policies with safe online exploration techniques to potentially further improve performance while maintaining safety guarantees. This highlights an ongoing research direction for optimizing the balance between safety and performance in practical deployments.
