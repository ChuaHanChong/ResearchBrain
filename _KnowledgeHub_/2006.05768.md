# Deep Drone Acrobatics

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of Zurich, ETH Zurich, and Intel developed an autonomous quadrotor that executes extreme acrobatic maneuvers solely using onboard sensing and computation. The system achieves this by training a sensorimotor policy in simulation and deploying it directly onto physical hardware through an input abstraction strategy that leverages feature tracks and preprocessed inertial data, leading to successful zero-shot transfer for maneuvers involving accelerations up to 3g.

## Problem

- Enabling autonomous quadrotors to perform complex, high-acceleration acrobatic maneuvers with only onboard sensing and computation.
- Existing agile flight systems either relied on external localization (e.g., motion capture) or external computation, or were limited to less aggressive flight profiles.
- Perception and state estimation based on onboard sensors face severe challenges (e.g., motion blur, feature tracking failures) during extreme maneuvers, making end-to-end solutions difficult to train and deploy robustly.

## Method

- Developed an end-to-end sensorimotor policy using deep imitation learning, trained entirely in simulation from a privileged ==Model Predictive Control== (MPC) expert.
- Implemented a novel simulation-to-reality transfer strategy based on input abstraction, where the policy processes abstracted visual data (feature tracks) and preprocessed inertial data rather than raw sensor inputs.
- Designed a neural network architecture with independent branches for processing asynchronous inputs from abstracted visual, inertial, and reference trajectory data, which are then combined by a multi-layer perceptron to generate control commands.

## Results

- The autonomous quadrotor successfully performed Power Loops, Barrel Rolls, and Matty Flips on a physical platform with accelerations up to 3g, achieving **100%** success rates for individual maneuvers in zero-shot transfer from simulation.
- In simulation, the proposed sensorimotor controller demonstrated up to **45%** lower average root mean square position tracking error and a **100%** success rate for individual maneuvers compared to a strong VIO-MPC baseline.
- Ablation studies confirmed that the combined use of abstracted visual and inertial inputs yielded superior performance, while controllers directly using raw image inputs failed entirely when tested with previously unseen background images, underscoring the effectiveness of input abstraction for robustness.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Input abstraction, particularly using feature tracks for visual data and preprocessed inertial data, is critical for bridging the simulation-to-reality gap, enabling robust zero-shot transfer for complex, dynamic tasks.
> - End-to-end sensorimotor policies, when trained with a strong privileged expert in simulation, can achieve high agility and robustness in real-world acrobatic flight without requiring direct real-world training or fine-tuning.
> - Combining abstracted visual and inertial data significantly reduces accumulated drift and improves long-term stability and success rates for aggressive maneuvers compared to relying on a single sensory modality.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of autonomous robotics, particularly for aerial vehicles, has been progressing towards enabling more complex and dynamic behaviors. Early research in agile flight primarily focused on control algorithms, often relying on high-fidelity, external localization systems such as Vicon or OptiTrack. These systems effectively provided perfect state estimation, allowing researchers to isolate and address the control problem for aggressive maneuvers like multi-flips or navigating through windows. However, this approach circumvented the fundamental challenge of reliable perception and state estimation when only onboard sensors are available, which is a critical requirement for true autonomy.

A subsequent line of research began to integrate perception and control, attempting agile flight with only onboard sensing and computation. Initial efforts in this direction demonstrated vision-based flight, though often limited to low-acceleration trajectories. More recent work achieved aggressive flight through narrow gaps, but these maneuvers were typically short and required re-initialization of the estimation pipeline. Perception-guided optimization frameworks aimed to plan aggressive trajectories while minimizing perception issues like motion blur, but these strategies were generally too conservative for acrobatic maneuvers that inherently induce significant motion blur.

Concurrently, end-to-end visuomotor policies, where neural networks directly map sensor inputs to control commands, emerged as an alternative approach. These methods sought to abolish the classic division between perception and control, learning directly from data, often demonstrations. However, a common limitation of these deep learning approaches was their high sample complexity, often restricting their application to simulation or slow, hover-to-hover trajectories. Previous work on sim-to-real transfer for quadrotors also existed, but had not demonstrated end-to-end sensorimotor mappings for high-speed, high-acceleration acrobatic maneuvers on physical systems using only onboard sensing.

This paper addresses the gap by demonstrating, for the first time, that a vision-based autonomous quadrotor can perform extreme acrobatic maneuvers (e.g., Power Loop, Barrel Roll, Matty Flip with accelerations up to 3g) using only onboard sensing and computation. It differentiates itself by leveraging a novel simulation-to-reality transfer strategy based on input abstraction, enabling zero-shot deployment of policies trained entirely in simulation to physical hardware. This work advances the field by providing a methodology that integrates robust perception with agile control under extreme conditions, without external infrastructure or extensive real-world data collection and fine-tuning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable an autonomous quadrotor to execute extreme acrobatic maneuvers using only onboard sensing and computation. This involves learning a sensorimotor policy that can directly translate sensor inputs into low-level control commands necessary for high thrust and extreme angular accelerations.

The motivation for pursuing this objective is multi-faceted:

- **Challenging Nature of Acrobatic Flight:** Acrobatic maneuvers, such as Power Loops and Barrel Rolls, are inherently difficult, even for highly skilled human drone pilots who require extensive practice. They push quadrotors to their physical limits, demanding precise control and robust state estimation under conditions of high acceleration and rapid angular changes.
- **Limitations of Existing Autonomous Systems:** Prior autonomous systems capable of agile flight either relied on external sensing (e.g., motion capture systems) or external computation, or were limited to less aggressive maneuvers. For systems relying solely on onboard resources, the high accelerations involved in acrobatics pose significant challenges for perception systems (e.g., motion blur, large displacements, feature tracking failures) and make controller tuning on physical hardware difficult and risky.
- **Establishing a Benchmark for Autonomous Capabilities:** The ability to perform complex acrobatic maneuvers autonomously with onboard sensing and computation serves as a demanding benchmark for comparing the capabilities of autonomous systems against human pilots and for evaluating advancements in integrated perception and control.
- **Advantages of Simulation-Based Learning and Zero-Shot Transfer:**
 - **Safety:** Training such extreme maneuvers directly on physical quadrotors carries a high risk of damage to the hardware. Training entirely in simulation mitigates this risk.
 - **Efficiency and Scalability:** Simulation allows for the generation of unlimited training data and the specification of arbitrary, complex reference trajectories without requiring costly or dangerous human expert demonstrations. This enables the learning of maneuvers that might be beyond human piloting capabilities.
 - **Robustness to Reality Gap:** A core motivation is to overcome the traditional "simulation-to-reality gap" to allow for direct deployment of simulation-trained policies without real-world fine-tuning. This is addressed through the specific design of input abstractions.
- **Integrated Perception and Control:** The work aims to achieve an integrated sensorimotor control, moving beyond the classical decoupled approach of state estimation followed by control, which often fails under aggressive flight conditions due to perception limitations.

By addressing these motivations, the research seeks to advance the frontier of autonomous drone capabilities, enabling them to perform complex, dynamic tasks in real-world environments with self-contained intelligence.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on learning an end-to-end sensorimotor policy in simulation, leveraging input abstraction for zero-shot transfer to a physical quadrotor.

**A. Task Formulation and Policy Definition:**
The task is defined as a discrete-time, continuous-valued optimization problem to find a neural network policy $\pi: O \rightarrow U$ that minimizes a finite-horizon objective. This objective is a quadratic cost function comparing the quadrotor's state $s[k]$ to a reference trajectory $\tau_r[k]$. The quadrotor state includes position, orientation (quaternion), and their derivatives. The policy's output $u[k]$ consists of collective thrust and body rates. The policy does not directly observe the ground-truth state $s[k]$ but rather observations $o[k]$ comprising a camera image $I[k]$ and inertial measurements $\phi[k]$.

**B. Reference Trajectory Generation:**
Dynamically feasible reference trajectories are crucial for acrobatic maneuvers. Instead of planning in the full state space, trajectories are planned in the space of "flat outputs": position ($x, y, z$) and yaw angle ($\psi$). This simplifies planning as any smooth trajectory in this space can be tracked by an underactuated platform.
- **Motion Primitives:** The core of each maneuver is a circular motion primitive with constant tangential velocity. A constraint ensures the tangential velocity is sufficiently high to prevent free fall at the top of the loop.
- **Polynomial Trajectories:** Constrained polynomial functions (7th order for position, 2nd for yaw) are used for entry, exit, and transitions between circular primitives. Continuity is enforced for derivatives up to the 3rd order.
- **Optimization:** Execution time for polynomial trajectories is minimized while respecting maximum speed, thrust, and body rate constraints.
- **Full Trajectory:** These segments are concatenated and converted back to a full state-space representation, then sampled at 50 Hz to create the discrete reference trajectory $\tau_r[k]$.

**C. Privileged Expert (Teacher Policy):**
A privileged expert policy $\pi^*$ is used to generate demonstrations for training the sensorimotor student. This expert is an MPC (Model Predictive Control) operating on a simplified quadrotor dynamic model that neglects angular rate dynamics. The MPC has access to privileged information, specifically the full ground-truth state of the platform $s[k]$. It optimizes an open-loop control problem over a receding horizon, generating collective thrust and body rates to minimize a cost function that tracks the reference trajectory while respecting system dynamics and physical bounds.

**D. Learning Process (Sensorimotor Student):**
The sensorimotor controller (student policy $\pi$) is trained using imitation learning to mimic the actions of the privileged expert. This process is based on an iterative supervised learning approach similar to DAGGER, minimizing the discrepancy between the expert's actions (given ground-truth state) and the student's actions (given onboard observations).
- **Simulation-Exclusive Training:** All training is performed in the Gazebo simulator using the RotorS extension. This provides unlimited training data for diverse trajectories without risk to physical hardware.
- **Abstraction for Sim-to-Real Transfer:** To bridge the "simulation-to-reality gap," the sensorimotor student does not process raw sensor inputs (e.g., color images) directly. Instead, it operates on *abstractions* of the input.
 - **Formal Justification:** The paper formally demonstrates that training on abstract representations can reduce the simulation-to-reality gap, provided the abstraction mapping $f$ satisfies $DW(f(M), f(L)) \leq DW(M, L)$, where $M$ and $L$ are observation models in reality and simulation, respectively, and $DW$ is the Wasserstein metric.
 - **Visual Abstraction:** Raw camera frames are replaced with *feature tracks*. These are motions of salient keypoints extracted and tracked by a visual-inertial odometry (VIO) frontend (e.g., VINS-Mono). Feature tracks primarily depend on scene geometry, ego-motion, and image gradients, making them more invariant to appearance differences between simulation and reality than raw pixels. Each track is represented by a 5-dimensional vector (keypoint position, displacement, tracking duration).
 - **Inertial Abstraction:** Raw IMU measurements, which can be sensitive to sensor biases and environmental factors, are preprocessed. Bias subtraction and gravity alignment are applied, similar to pre-integration in VIO systems, yielding an inertial signal representing estimated platform velocity, orientation, and angular rate.

**E. Sensorimotor Controller Architecture (Neural Network):**
The policy is implemented as a neural network designed to handle multiple input modalities and asynchronous sensor update rates.
- **Input Branches:** Three independent branches process different input modalities:
 1. **Inertial Branch:** Receives a history (length L=8) of filtered inertial measurements (100 Hz). Consists of temporal convolutional layers followed by a fully connected layer.
 2. **Reference Branch:** Receives a history of reference velocities, orientations, and angular rates (50 Hz). Has the same structure as the inertial branch.
 3. **Visual Branch:** Processes a history of feature tracks (randomly sampled 40 keypoints per keyframe). Uses a reduced PointNet architecture to generate a fixed-size representation from unordered feature sets at each timestep, followed by temporal convolutional layers.
- **Asynchronous Operation:** Each input branch operates independently, generating an output only when new sensor data arrives.
- **Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP):** The outputs from the individual asynchronous branches are concatenated. A synchronous MLP (operating at 100 Hz) then processes this combined representation to produce the final collective thrust and body rate commands.
- **State Representation:** Providing a history of inputs allows the network to implicitly maintain a state representation, akin to a VIO system.

**F. Implementation Details:**
- **Simulation Environment:** Gazebo with RotorS, simulating an AscTec Hummingbird.
- **Real-World Platform:** Custom quadrotor (1.15 kg, 4:1 thrust-to-weight ratio) equipped with a Jetson TX2 for inference and an Intel RealSense T265 camera for sensing.
- **Training Schedule:** Off-policy learning with iterative collection of rollouts (30 new rollouts per iteration, 5 iterations total) and training on the accumulated dataset (40 epochs per iteration).
- **Robustness to Reality Gap (Randomization):** During simulation training, IMU biases and the platform's thrust-to-weight ratio are randomized by up to 10% to improve robustness and transferability to physical hardware. Scene geometry and appearance are not randomized during data collection.
- **Safety during Training:** During early training, student actions are only executed if their difference from the expert action is below a threshold, which is progressively relaxed. Random actions (30% probability) are also introduced to increase state space coverage.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings based on simulation experiments and real-world deployment, demonstrating the efficacy of their sensorimotor control approach and the importance of input abstraction.

**A. Simulation Experiments:**

- **Performance Comparison:**
 - The proposed sensorimotor controller ("Ours," using both abstracted visual and inertial data) consistently exhibited superior performance across all acrobatic maneuvers (Power Loop, Barrel Roll, Matty Flip, Combo) compared to a strong baseline VIO-MPC (Visual-Inertial Odometry + Model Predictive Control).
 - "Ours" achieved up to a 45% lower average root mean square tracking error in position.
 - The tracking error difference between "Ours" and VIO-MPC was more pronounced for longer and more complex maneuvers (e.g., Combo), where the VIO-MPC baseline showed higher drift.
 - "Ours" achieved a 100% success rate for individual maneuvers and a 95% success rate for the challenging "Combo" maneuver, outperforming VIO-MPC, especially for the Combo (70% success rate).

- **Ablation Studies on Input Modalities:**
 - **Open-Loop Control ("Only Ref"):** Policies receiving only the reference trajectory without sensory input performed poorly (e.g., 20% success for Power Loop, 0% for Combo), highlighting the necessity of closed-loop feedback.
 - **Visual-Only Control ("No IMU"):** Policies with visual input but no inertial data performed similarly poorly (e.g., 30% success for Power Loop), indicating that visual information alone is insufficient to determine the absolute scale of ego-motion.
 - **Inertial-Only Control ("No FT"):** Policies relying solely on inertial data could safely fly most maneuvers and had significantly lower tracking error than the VIO-MPC baseline. However, these controllers accumulated greater drift over longer maneuvers.
 - **Combined Modalities ("Ours"):** The combination of both preprocessed inertial data and abstracted visual feature tracks significantly reduced drift, improved accuracy, and increased success rates (e.g., raising Combo success from 85% to 95% compared to "No FT").

- **Robustness and Generalization:**
 - When trained for a 4-second barrel roll and tested for up to 20 seconds of continuous execution, the "Ours" controller (IMU + FT) performed without a single failure, demonstrating superior robustness and generalization over time compared to other variants.

- **Importance of Input Abstraction (Sim-to-Sim Transfer):**
 - A network directly using raw camera images as visual input ("Image") achieved only an 80% success rate and 58% higher tracking error for the Matty Flip in the training environment, indicating higher sample complexity for learning from pixels.
 - Crucially, the "Image"-based controller failed completely (0% success) when tested with previously unseen background images (randomly sampled from the COCO dataset).
 - In contrast, the "Ours" approach, utilizing feature tracks as an abstraction of visual input, maintained a 100% success rate under these conditions, validating the role of abstraction in achieving robustness to appearance variations and facilitating transfer.

**B. Deployment in the Physical World (Zero-Shot Transfer):**

- **Direct Deployment:** The sensorimotor controllers trained exclusively in simulation were directly deployed on a physical quadrotor without any real-world fine-tuning. This "zero-shot transfer" was successful despite visual differences between simulation and reality.
- **Acrobatic Performance:** The physical quadrotor autonomously performed the Power Loop, Barrel Roll, and Matty Flip maneuvers, incurring accelerations of up to 3g.
- **Real-World Success Rates:** The "Ours" controllers achieved a 100% success rate for all three individual maneuvers on the physical platform (over 10 runs), with no human safety pilot intervention required.
- **Repeated Maneuvers:** In an experiment involving back-to-back Power Loops, the controller utilizing all input modalities ("Ours") demonstrated enhanced robustness compared to the inertial-only controller ("Ours (No FT)"), indicating that abstracted visual input contributes to long-term stability in physical deployment.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant advancement in autonomous aerial robotics, particularly in the domain of agile and acrobatic flight. Its primary significance lies in demonstrating, for the first time, that a quadrotor can autonomously perform a wide range of extreme acrobatic maneuvers using **only onboard sensing and computation**, and achieve this through **zero-shot transfer** from simulation to reality.

The key contributions and potential impacts are:

- **Enabling Extreme Autonomous Flight:** The ability to perform maneuvers involving accelerations up to 3g with onboard resources pushes the boundaries of autonomous flight. This capability can be crucial for applications requiring high agility, such as inspection in complex environments, high-speed delivery, search and rescue in dynamic settings, or even competitive drone racing.
- **Validation of Simulation-to-Reality Transfer:** The successful zero-shot transfer of policies trained entirely in simulation to a physical system for such a complex and physically demanding task is a notable achievement. This methodology mitigates the safety risks and high costs associated with real-world data collection and training for aggressive maneuvers.
- **Demonstration of Input Abstraction Efficacy:** The research empirically and formally validates the importance of using appropriate input abstractions (e.g., feature tracks instead of raw images, preprocessed IMU data) to bridge the simulation-to-reality gap. This principle is generalizable and can inform the design of robust sensorimotor policies for other robotic systems where direct raw sensor input transfer is challenging.
- **Reduced Reliance on Human Expertise:** The approach eliminates the need for human expert demonstrations, which are often difficult and costly to obtain for highly complex tasks. Instead, maneuvers are defined by dynamically feasible reference trajectories in simulation, allowing for the exploration of a broader range of behaviors, including those beyond typical human piloting abilities.
- **Integrated Perception and Control for Dynamic Tasks:** By training an end-to-end sensorimotor policy, the work demonstrates a robust way to integrate perception and control, which often fail when decoupled under high-speed and high-acceleration conditions. This provides a template for developing more resilient autonomous systems.
- **Generalizability to Other Robotic Domains:** The core methodology—leveraging privileged learning in simulation with input abstraction for zero-shot transfer—is not inherently limited to quadrotors. It offers a promising avenue for advancing autonomy in other robotic platforms and tasks that demand agile control, robust perception, and operate at their physical limits.

In summary, this work provides a framework for developing autonomous systems capable of unprecedented levels of agility and robustness, with implications for practical applications and fundamental research in robotics and artificial intelligence.
