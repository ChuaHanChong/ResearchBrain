# RoboTok: An Internet-Scale Data Engine for Human Demonstration Retrieval and Dexterous Manipulation Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> RoboTok is an internet-scale data engine that retrieves human manipulation demonstrations from web video using 3D hand-trajectory motion rather than appearance or language. It reconstructs metric 3D hand poses, canonicalizes them into an estimated torso-centered egocentric frame, and trains a lightweight encoder to preserve Dynamic Time Warping similarity for fast vector search. Retrieved clips reward-guide PPO, sharply raising dexterous manipulation success.

## Problem

- Robot demonstration collection is costly and cannot cover the long tail of real tasks, objects, and environments, even with large teleoperated datasets.
- Visual or semantic similarity does not imply similar manipulation behavior, so appearance- and language-based retrieval returns clips with unrelated underlying motion.
- Prior retrieval methods target fixed robot corpora, parallel grippers, and image-space motion, leaving unstructured web video with arbitrary viewpoints and occlusions unusable.

## Method

- A filtering and grounding pipeline selects static-camera hand clips, estimates 3D keypoints with ==WiLoR==, adds metric depth via ==MoGe-2==, and infills gaps with ==HaWoR==.
- A lightweight ==torso-frame estimator== predicts an actor-centered reference frame from wrist trajectories alone, canonicalizing motion for view-invariant comparison without requiring a visible body.
- A ==cross-attention trajectory encoder== trained with combined ==set and rank losses== on ==DTW==-derived positives and boundary negatives yields normalized embeddings for ==cosine nearest-neighbor indexing==.

## Results

- On **10,000** held-out Action100M queries, RoboTok reaches mAP@20 of **0.353** and Recall@20 of **0.996**, versus the best baseline STRAP at **0.007** mAP and **0.12** recall.
- Cross-dataset transfer to AssemblyHands gives mAP@5 of **0.261** against STRAP's **0.133**, with mean DTW cost **1.095m**, only **13 percent** above the oracle neighbors.
- Guiding PPO on harder VTDexManip tasks, RoboTok hits **79.3 percent** on Lever Sliding and **44.8 percent** on Faucet Screwing, gains of **59.8** and **38.0** points over HAND.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Choosing the right invariant representation, actor-relative 3D motion, can matter more than encoder capacity; a deliberately lightweight model suffices once inputs are canonicalized.
> - An expensive alignment metric can act as a free supervision oracle, amortizing quadratic comparison into training so deployment reduces to cheap vector search.
> - Benchmarks with dense shaped rewards and constrained hands can mask retrieval quality; removing that scaffolding exposes differences baselines otherwise hide.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot learning is increasingly reliant on large and diverse datasets of demonstrations. However, the collection of robot-specific data is resource-intensive, difficult to scale, and often fails to cover the wide variety of real-world tasks, environments, and objects, particularly the "long tail" of less common scenarios (O’Neill et al., 2024; Khazatsky et al., 2024; Walke et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2023).

Existing approaches to address this data bottleneck broadly fall into several categories:
- **Large-scale Robot Demonstration Datasets:** Projects such as Open X-Embodiment (O’Neill et al., 2024), BridgeData V2, and DROID (Walke et al., 2023; Khazatsky et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2023), along with models like Octo, OpenVLA, SpatialVLA, and CLIP-RT (Ghosh et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2025; Qu et al., 2025; Kang et al., 2025), aim to provide general manipulation skills through diverse robot data. Other methods, such as MimicGen (Mandlekar et al., 2023) and GRAIL (Xie et al., 2026a), focus on data generation or retargeting to amplify limited demonstrations. While these initiatives have advanced robot learning, they typically involve significant infrastructure and effort, limiting their scalability and coverage for new tasks and embodiments.
- **Leveraging Human Video for Dexterous Manipulation Learning:** Human demonstrations are a natural source of supervision, especially for humanoid robots or anthropomorphic hands. Purpose-built datasets like HO-Cap and EgoVerse (Wang et al., 2025a; Punamiya et al., 2026) offer structured human demonstrations. Techniques such as VideoDex, EgoMimic, K-VIL, YODO, SPOT, and CHORD (Shaw et al., 2024; Kareer et al., 2025; Gao et al., 2023; Wen et al., 2022; Hsu et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2026) focus on extracting relevant motion, contact, or embodiment-aware representations from human videos. However, these purpose-built datasets also face limitations in scale and diversity due to collection infrastructure. Large-scale web videos, exemplified by HowTo100M and Action100M (Miech et al., 2019; Chen et al., 2026), offer a vast and continuously expanding source of human activity, which includes a broad range of manipulation behaviors. Recent advancements like EgoInfinity (Wang et al., 2026) and CARI4D (Xie et al., 2026b) further demonstrate the ability to recover rich 3D and 4D human-object representations from unconstrained video. A key challenge, however, is to automatically identify and extract manipulation-relevant demonstrations from this unstructured, heterogeneous data.
- **Demonstration Retrieval for Robot Learning:** To mitigate the cost of data acquisition, various methods aim to retrieve relevant experiences from existing datasets. Approaches include matching state-action pairs (Du et al., 2023), motion segments (Nasiriany et al., 2022), or visually similar object interactions (Di Palo & Johns, 2024). Some methods utilize vision-language models to retrieve behaviors from unlabeled human videos based on language commands (Papagiannis et al., 2025; Zhu & Feng, 2025), or combine semantic and motion similarity for cross-embodiment learning (Lin et al., 2026). Behavior-aware retrieval methods, such as FlowRetrieval (Lin et al., 2024), STRAP (Memmel et al., 2025), and HAND (Hong et al., 2026), incorporate temporal or motion information, often relying on optical flow, temporal alignment of visual features, or 2D hand paths. These methods typically operate on fixed robot corpora, image-space motion representations, or language-based semantics, which may not translate directly to underlying manipulation mechanics.

RoboTok addresses a specific gap in this landscape: the lack of an internet-scale data engine capable of directly mining web human videos for dexterous manipulation learning based on the underlying motion similarity, rather than relying on visual appearance, semantic labels, or fixed datasets. The core challenge in leveraging internet video is that visual or semantic similarity does not inherently guarantee similarity in the actual manipulation behavior. RoboTok aims to overcome this by focusing on canonicalized 3D hand trajectories in an actor-relative reference frame, providing a representation robust to variations in camera viewpoint and appearance. This approach positions RoboTok as a framework for continuous, scalable, motion-based retrieval from a dynamic and expanding source of human demonstration data for robot learning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the recognized bottleneck in robot learning: the high cost and limited scalability of collecting diverse, task-specific robot demonstration data. Current data collection methods are often insufficient to cover the broad spectrum of tasks, objects, and environments encountered in real-world scenarios. Internet videos represent a vast, continuously growing repository of human manipulation behaviors that could serve as a valuable, complementary source of supervision for robots. However, effectively leveraging this resource is hindered by its unstructured nature and the fact that visual or semantic similarity in videos does not consistently correlate with similarity in underlying manipulation actions.

Given this context, the key objectives of the RoboTok project are:
- **Develop an Internet-Scale Data Engine:** To create a system capable of indexing and retrieving human manipulation demonstrations from internet videos on a large scale. This system, named RoboTok, is designed to take a query human manipulation video and identify other videos exhibiting similar manipulation behaviors.
- **Focus on Egocentric 3D Hand Trajectories:** To learn a latent motion space based on 3D hand trajectories, which are expressed in an estimated actor-centered reference frame. This representation is intended to provide robustness against variations in camera viewpoint, scene appearance, and actor occlusions, allowing for consistent comparison of manipulation behaviors.
- **Ensure Compactness and Efficiency:** To design the representation and retrieval mechanism to be compact enough to support efficient nearest-neighbor search and continuous, scalable indexing of new web video collections as they become available. This contrasts with fixed datasets by enabling ongoing expansion.
- **Independence from Semantic Labels and Appearance:** To automatically identify and curate manipulation-relevant behavior across heterogeneous videos without relying on explicit task labels, visual appearance cues, or specific camera configurations. This addresses the challenge of unstructured web video content.
- **Improve Downstream Robot Policy Performance:** To demonstrate that the demonstrations retrieved by RoboTok can effectively provide supervision that leads to improved performance and task success rates in downstream dexterous robot manipulation policies.

In essence, RoboTok aims to transform unstructured, internet-scale human video into a continuously expanding, scalable, and behavior-aware source of training data for dexterous robot learning by focusing on the invariant properties of manipulation motion rather than transient visual features.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

RoboTok is designed as an internet-scale data engine that retrieves human demonstrations based on similar manipulation hand trajectories, distinct from visual appearance or semantic content. Its methodology involves learning a latent motion space from 3D hand trajectories represented in actor-centered reference frames, enabling cross-video comparison and efficient retrieval. The system operates in two main phases: an offline continuous web video indexing stage and an online query-time retrieval stage.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The objective is to retrieve a subset of *K* human demonstration clips from a collection *D* that exhibit manipulation behavior most similar to a given query clip *q*. Similarity is defined by the temporal movement of hands, rather than visual characteristics.
- **Representation:** Each clip is represented as a canonicalized 3D hand trajectory.
- **Similarity Oracle:** Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) is employed to measure similarity between two 21-joint hand-pose trajectories. DTW aligns sequences while accommodating differences in execution speed by minimizing cumulative pose distance. The similarity *s(i, j)* is defined as the length-normalized negative DTW cost.
- **Embedding Goal:** The system aims to learn an embedding function Γ that maps hand-pose trajectories *X* to a d-dimensional unit hypersphere *S^(d-1)*. This embedding is trained such that inner-product similarity in the embedding space preserves the ranking induced by the DTW oracle.
- **Retrieval:** At query time, the ideal retrieval set is approximated by performing an efficient vector nearest-neighbor search (cosine similarity) on the precomputed embeddings, bypassing the computational cost of direct DTW comparisons across the entire corpus.

#### 3.2. RoboTok Training Data (Data Processing Pipeline)

To train the retrieval model, a diverse collection of human demonstration trajectories is processed, canonicalized for cross-video comparison. The pipeline includes:
- **Source and Clip Selection:** Segments are sourced from the Action100M internet video corpus, pre-filtered for human actions. Further automatic filtering retains clips between 4-8 seconds, exhibiting a near-static camera (using the Lucas-Kanade method), and maintaining mild hand visibility with at most one left and one right hand per clip (Potamias et al., 2025). Overlapping segments are then removed.
- **Hand Pose Extraction and Metric Grounding:** For each selected clip, 3D hand keypoints are estimated at 5 frames per second using WiLoR (Potamias et al., 2025), with handedness stabilized across frames. WiLoR's weak-perspective hand reconstructions are then transformed into metric camera coordinates using MoGe-2 (Wang et al., 2025b) for depth estimation. Missing hand poses are infilled using HaWoR (Zhang et al., 2025). This results in sequences of metric 3D hand poses in the camera frame.
- **Egocentric Trajectory Representation:** To enable viewpoint-invariant motion comparison, hand poses in the camera frame are transformed into an egocentric (actor-centered) coordinate frame. A lightweight human torso-frame estimator, trained using hand wrist frames as input, predicts the demonstrator's static torso frame (based on Wang et al., 2026, adapted to SMPL-H). This method allows canonicalization even when the actor's body is not fully visible. The canonicalized trajectories are then used with DTW to compute pseudo-ground-truth motion similarities, which serve as an offline supervision signal for training the retrieval model.

#### 3.3. RoboTok Model Architecture

The core of RoboTok is a lightweight trajectory encoder designed to create an embedding space that approximates DTW-defined manipulation similarity for efficient nearest-neighbor retrieval.
- **Encoder:** The model is a lightweight cross-attention network that processes the egocentric 3D hand representation. Each frame is positionally encoded before being pooled into an ℓ2-normalized d-dimensional embedding. The encoder's primary function is to organize trajectories according to manipulation similarity, as viewpoint and appearance invariances are handled by the egocentric representation.
- **Batch Construction:** To ensure informative training, batches are constructed from anchor-centered groups. For an anchor trajectory, two positive trajectories are sampled from its top *K=20* nearest neighbors (the relevant set *R(a)*) based on the DTW oracle. One "boundary negative" is sampled immediately outside this relevant set. Each batch consists of 49 such groups, totaling 196 trajectories, providing diverse and challenging examples.
- **Objective:** The model is trained using a combined objective function *L = L_set + λ L_rank*. The *L_set* term encourages the embeddings of oracle top-K neighbors to score higher than boundary negatives. The *L_rank* term promotes the correct relative ordering of sampled positives within the embedding space, matching the DTW-induced ranking. This combined loss optimizes for both the inclusion of relevant trajectories and their relative ordering within the retrieved neighborhood.
- **Inference:** Retrieval is a two-stage process. First, all egocentric trajectories are encoded once and stored in an inner-product index offline. Second, given a new query clip, its embedding is computed, and nearest neighbors are efficiently retrieved from the index via online cosine similarity search. This design allows for scalability, as new clips can be added to the index without retraining the model, enabling continuous expansion of the data engine.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of RoboTok encompassed both retrieval quality against established baselines and the performance of downstream robot policies guided by the retrieved demonstrations.

#### 4.1. Evaluating Retrieval Quality

- **Protocol:** Two corpora were used:
 - **RoboTok Training Corpus:** 100,000 Action100M clips, with 10,000 clips held out as evaluation queries. The remaining 90,000 clips served as retrieval candidates. Pseudo-ground truth for similarity was derived from the DTW oracle. The relevant set size (R) was set to 20.
 - **AssemblyHands Corpus:** An external dataset of 831 two-hand assembly clips with sensor-grade 3D hand annotations (Ohkawa et al., 2023). This corpus assessed cross-dataset domain transfer. Ground truth was also derived from DTW. The relevant set size (R) was set to 5 due to the smaller corpus size.
 - **Baselines:** Performance was compared against Random retrieval, FlowRetrieval (Flow) (Lin et al., 2024), HAND (Hong et al., 2026), and STRAP (Memmel et al., 2025). The DTW oracle ranking served as an upper bound.
 - **Metrics:** Mean Average Precision (mAP@k), Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain (nDCG@k), CKNNA@k, Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR@k), Kendall τ, and mean DTW cost@k.

- **RoboTok Evaluation Corpus Results (Table 2, Figure 5):**
 - RoboTok demonstrated substantial performance gains over all baselines. For instance, RoboTok achieved an mAP@20 of 0.353, significantly higher than the strongest baseline, STRAP, which registered 0.007.
 - RoboTok's Recall@20 was 0.996, indicating that nearly every query successfully identified a true DTW neighbor within the top 20 retrieved clips.
 - A Kendall τ of 0.487 suggested that the retrieved neighbors were not only present but also largely correctly ordered according to DTW similarity.
 - The mean DTW cost for RoboTok's top-20 retrievals was 1.333m, which was 16% above the oracle's optimal cost of 1.145m, demonstrating a close approximation of true motion similarity, in contrast to random clips with a mean cost of 4.776m.
 - While trained to optimize local neighborhoods (k=20), RoboTok's latent motion space exhibited organization that generalized to global neighborhoods, as shown by per-k plots (Figure 5).
 - Qualitative examples (Figure 4) illustrated that RoboTok retrieved demonstrations with more relevant manipulation behaviors compared to baselines.

- **AssemblyHands Retrieval Evaluation Results (Table 3):**
 - On the AssemblyHands corpus, RoboTok consistently outperformed baselines across all metrics, even when evaluated under cross-dataset domain shift. RoboTok's mAP@5 was 0.261, compared to STRAP's 0.133.
 - The mean DTW cost for RoboTok's retrievals was 1.095m, against the oracle's 0.966m (13% above optimal) and random's 1.911m. While the margins were narrower than with the in-domain corpus, RoboTok maintained its lead in retrieving motion-aligned demonstrations.

#### 4.2. Evaluating Downstream Policies Guided by Demonstrations

- **Protocol:** Policies were evaluated on the VTDexManip (Liu et al., 2025) dexterous-manipulation simulation benchmark.
 - **Original VTDexManip Formulation:** Policies guided by retrieved clips were trained with Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) from scratch. The guidance was incorporated into the reward function, providing a standing reward based on the negative weighted k-NN distance to retargeted demonstration states and using this for potential-based reward shaping.
 - **Modified HARDER VTDexManip Formulation:** To prevent saturation observed in the original benchmark, three tasks (BottleCap Turning, Faucet Screwing, Lever Sliding) were modified to increase exploration difficulty. This involved restoring full 3D control of the robot hand and removing hand-designed dense reward shaping. Success rates were reported for seen and unseen object splits over eight seeds per method. No robot demonstrations or action labels were used, attributing performance differences to the quality of retrieved clips.

- **Original VTDexManip Benchmark Evaluation Results (Table 4):**
 - Policies guided by RoboTok retrievals exceeded the benchmark's best pretrained baseline (VT-JointPretrain) on five out of six tasks.
 - Across all six tasks, RoboTok demonstrated an average improvement of 7.45% for seen objects and 5.83% for unseen objects compared to the best reported baseline.

- **Modified HARDER VTDexManip Formulation Results (Table 5, Figure 7):**
 - On the harder tasks, policies guided by RoboTok retrieved demonstrations significantly outperformed models guided by baseline methods.
 - **BottleCap Turning:** RoboTok achieved 77.3% success on seen objects, an increase of 17.8% over the next best method (HAND).
 - **Faucet Screwing:** RoboTok achieved 44.8% success on seen objects, an increase of 38.0% over the next best method (HAND).
 - **Lever Sliding:** RoboTok achieved 79.3% success on seen objects, an increase of 59.8% over the next best method (HAND).
 - The observed method ordering in success rates was also consistent with the learning speed during training (Figure 7).

These results indicate that RoboTok effectively retrieves manipulation-relevant human demonstrations, which in turn provide useful supervision that enhances the performance and learning efficiency of downstream dexterous robot policies, particularly in more challenging task formulations.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

RoboTok presents a methodological contribution to the field of robot learning by addressing the challenge of data acquisition for dexterous manipulation. The core significance of this work lies in its demonstration that internet video can be leveraged as a scalable and continuously expanding source of manipulation data for robots.

The key aspects contributing to its significance include:
- **Mitigation of the Data Collection Bottleneck:** The research offers a viable alternative to the costly and laborious process of collecting purpose-built robot datasets. By tapping into the vast, continuously growing repository of human activity on the internet, RoboTok provides access to a broader range of tasks, objects, and environments than typically feasible with dedicated data collection infrastructure. This could accelerate the development of more general-purpose robot manipulation capabilities.
- **Novelty in Manipulation-Aware Retrieval:** RoboTok's approach of focusing retrieval on *underlying manipulation motions* through canonicalized 3D hand trajectories in an estimated actor-relative reference frame is a notable development. This representation strategy effectively reduces sensitivity to extraneous factors such as camera viewpoint, scene appearance, and semantic labels, which are common sources of variability in unstructured web videos. This focus on core kinematic similarity is essential for identifying genuinely relevant manipulation behaviors.
- **Scalability and Continuous Indexing:** The design of RoboTok's learned motion embedding space is compact, facilitating efficient indexing and retrieval at internet scale. Crucially, the system supports continuous expansion, allowing newly available web videos to be indexed without requiring retraining of the entire model. This adaptability positions RoboTok as a dynamic data engine rather than a static dataset.
- **Demonstrated Impact on Robot Performance:** The empirical evaluation confirms that the human demonstrations retrieved by RoboTok provide effective supervision for downstream robot policy learning. The substantial improvements in task success rates, particularly in the modified and more challenging VTDexManip tasks, underscore the practical utility of RoboTok's approach for enhancing robot dexterity and learning efficiency.

The potential impact of RoboTok extends to reducing the reliance on meticulously curated, fixed datasets, which often limit the scope and diversity of skills that robots can acquire. By enabling robots to learn from the diverse and expansive real-world demonstrations available online, this work offers a promising direction for developing more capable and adaptable robotic systems. Future work, as suggested by the authors, may involve extending RoboTok to handle videos from moving cameras, which could further broaden its applicability and impact on robot learning from human demonstrations.
