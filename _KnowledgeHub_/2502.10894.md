# Bridging the Sim-to-Real Gap for Athletic Loco-Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at MIT's Improbable AI Lab developed the Unsupervised Actuator Net (UAN) to bridge the sim-to-real gap for complex robotic loco-manipulation tasks. This approach enabled a Unitree B2 quadruped robot with an arm to successfully perform challenging real-world tasks such as throwing objects 20 meters, lifting 8kg weights, and dragging a 113N cart.

## Problem

- Training robots directly in the real world is expensive, time-consuming, and potentially dangerous for complex, dynamic tasks.
- The 'sim-to-real gap' causes policies trained in simulation to fail on physical hardware due to modeling inaccuracies, particularly concerning actuation discrepancies and physics approximations.
- Traditional methods for bridging the sim-to-real gap, such as domain randomization or system identification, have limitations when dealing with complex actuation and dynamic interactions.

## Method

- Introduction of the Unsupervised Actuator Net (**UAN**), an ==reinforcement learning==-based approach that predicts corrective torques to minimize simulation-to-real discrepancies without requiring physical torque sensors.
- Development of a two-stage training pipeline for a Whole-Body Controller (WBC), involving initial pre-training on diverse base velocities and end-effector poses, followed by task-specific fine-tuning.
- The training framework uses task rewards combined with auxiliary rewards and reference trajectories to balance exploration and exploitation, enabling learning of robust and efficient athletic behaviors.

## Results

- The Unsupervised Actuator Net (UAN) method demonstrated significantly closer matching of real robot joint trajectories in simulation compared to default or other calibration methods.
- The robot successfully performed real-world throws of approximately 20 meters, lifted dumbbells weighing up to 8kg, and pulled a cart against 113N of friction over 10 meters.
- Ablation studies confirmed that UAN combined with the pre-training and fine-tuning strategy consistently achieved superior real-world performance for these athletic loco-manipulation tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Simulations can be calibrated for dynamic tasks without direct torque sensing through an unsupervised, reinforcement learning-based approach (UAN).
> - A two-stage training pipeline combining pre-training on diverse motions with task-specific fine-tuning enables efficient learning of complex, athletic whole-body behaviors.
> - Accurate modeling of actuator dynamics, facilitated by UAN, is crucial for bridging the sim-to-real gap and achieving robust performance in highly dynamic robot tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Introduction

Robotics researchers have long sought to create machines capable of dynamic, athletic movements that coordinate both locomotion and manipulation. However, training robots directly in the real world is expensive, time-consuming, and potentially dangerous. Simulators offer a safer alternative, but a persistent challenge remains: the "sim-to-real gap," where policies trained in simulation fail when deployed on physical hardware due to modeling inaccuracies.

In their paper, researchers from MIT's Improbable AI Lab tackle this challenge with a novel approach to bridging the sim-to-real gap for complex robotic systems performing athletic tasks. They focus on a Unitree B2 quadruped robot with a modified Z1 Pro arm, enabling it to perform challenging whole-body coordination tasks like throwing objects, lifting weights, and pulling heavy carts.

### 2. The Sim-to-Real Challenge

The sim-to-real gap arises from several factors:

1. **Actuation discrepancies**: Real robot actuators behave differently than simplified models in simulation, especially under dynamic loading conditions.

2. **Physics inaccuracies**: Simulation engines make approximations that don't fully capture real-world physics, particularly during contact-rich interactions.

3. **Sensing limitations**: Sensor noise and delays differ between simulation and reality.

4. **Reward exploitation**: When trained with task rewards, policies often learn to exploit simulation inaccuracies rather than developing robust behaviors.

Traditional approaches to this problem include domain randomization (varying simulation parameters), system identification (measuring physical parameters), and supervised learning methods. However, these approaches have limitations when dealing with complex actuation mechanisms and dynamic tasks.

### 3. Unsupervised Actuator Net

The paper presents the Unsupervised Actuator Net (UAN), a novel approach to calibrating simulations. Unlike previous methods, UAN:

1. Does not require torque sensors on the physical robot
2. Uses reinforcement learning to optimize simulation fidelity
3. Generalizes to motions beyond the training data

The UAN works by predicting corrective torques that minimize the discrepancy between simulated and real joint positions. It's trained using RL with a reward function:

$$r_t^{UAN} = r_t^{sim-to-real} + r_t^{smoothness}$$

Where $r_t^{sim-to-real}$ encourages the simulated joint positions to match real-world data, and $r_t^{smoothness}$ encourages smooth corrections.

The researchers collected joint trajectory data from the real robot performing various motions. They compared UAN against several baselines:

1. Default simulator
2. Concurrent-learning Ensemble Method (CEM)
3. Supervised Actuator Net

The results show that UAN significantly outperforms other methods in matching real joint trajectories.

### 4. Training Pipeline

The authors developed a two-stage training pipeline:

1. **Pre-training Stage**: The Whole-Body Controller (WBC) is trained on a diverse set of base velocities and end-effector poses, establishing fundamental movement capabilities.

2. **Fine-tuning Stage**: The WBC is initialized with reference trajectories and then fine-tuned with task-specific rewards, allowing it to depart from the references when beneficial.

This approach balances exploration and exploitation by providing "hints" through references while still allowing the policy to discover optimal strategies through task rewards. The formulation can be expressed as:

$$r_t = r_t^{task} + r_t^{aux}$$

Where $r_t^{task}$ rewards task achievement (e.g., throwing distance), and $r_t^{aux}$ provides auxiliary rewards like energy efficiency.

### 5. Athletic Tasks

The researchers tested their approach on three challenging athletic tasks:

1. **Ball Throwing**: The robot must throw a ball as far as possible.

2. **Weight Lifting**: The robot must lift dumbbells of varying weights (up to 8kg).

3. **Cart Dragging**: The robot must pull a cart against a friction force of 113N over a distance of 10 meters.

These tasks require whole-body coordination, with the robot using its legs to stabilize and generate force while manipulating objects with its arm.

In simulation, the behaviors demonstrate effective strategies.

### 6. Experimental Results

The effectiveness of UAN and the training pipeline was evaluated through both simulation and real-world experiments.

**UAN Simulation Fidelity**:

The researchers found that UAN achieved the closest match to real hardware behavior. Figure 3 shows that UAN (red line) tracks the hardware joint angles (dotted black line) much more accurately than the baseline methods across all joints.

**Task Performance**:

1. **Throwing**: The UAN-enhanced policy achieved throws of approximately 20 meters on the real robot, compared to shorter distances with baseline methods.

2. **Lifting**: Successfully lifted dumbbells up to 8kg, with the policy adapting its posture based on weight.

3. **Dragging**: Successfully pulled a cart with 113N of friction force over 10 meters, demonstrating efficient loco-manipulation coordination.

The researchers also conducted ablation studies to validate their design choices:

1. Comparing different simulation calibration methods
2. Testing different training strategies (pre-training + fine-tuning vs. training from scratch)
3. Evaluating the impact of reference trajectories

In all cases, the UAN combined with pre-training and fine-tuning achieved the best real-world performance.

### 7. Significance and Impact

This research makes several important contributions to robotics:

1. **Novel approach to sim-to-real transfer**: UAN provides a powerful way to calibrate simulations without requiring torque sensing, which is often unavailable on commercial robots.

2. **Effective training strategy**: The pre-training and fine-tuning approach enables efficient learning of complex behaviors while avoiding reward exploitation.

3. **Demonstration of athletic capabilities**: The successful execution of throwing, lifting, and dragging tasks pushes the boundaries of what legged manipulators can achieve.

The impact extends beyond the specific tasks demonstrated. The approach could generalize to:

- Manufacturing robots that need to coordinate movement and manipulation
- Service robots performing physically demanding tasks
- Exploration robots navigating challenging terrain while manipulating objects
- Search and rescue robots that need to move through disaster areas while moving debris

### 8. Conclusion

The paper presents a significant advance in enabling robots to perform athletic loco-manipulation tasks with policies trained primarily in simulation. The key innovations—the Unsupervised Actuator Net for sim-to-real transfer and the pre-training/fine-tuning pipeline for learning complex behaviors—provide a framework for developing more capable robots that can coordinate their entire body to achieve dynamic tasks.

By successfully demonstrating throwing, lifting, and dragging behaviors on real hardware, the research shows that the sim-to-real gap can be effectively bridged even for highly dynamic tasks that challenge conventional approaches. The ability to train primarily in simulation while achieving successful real-world transfer opens the door to more rapid development of robot capabilities without the expense and risk of extensive real-world training.

Future work could extend this approach to other robot subsystems, incorporate structural integrity constraints, and develop generative models to automatically synthesize task references. As robots continue to move from controlled factory environments to more dynamic, unstructured settings, approaches like this will be essential for enabling them to perform useful physical work in the real world.
