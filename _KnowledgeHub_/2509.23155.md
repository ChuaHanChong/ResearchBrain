# LAGEA: Language Guided Embodied Agents for Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> The LAGEA framework allows embodied robotic agents to learn from failures by converting VLM-generated natural language reflections into temporally grounded, adaptive reward signals for reinforcement learning. This approach achieved an average success rate improvement of 5.3% to 9% on Meta-World MT10 benchmarks and 17% on Robotic Fetch tasks, alongside faster policy convergence.

## Problem

- Difficulty in converting high-level VLM outputs into consistent, granular, and stable reward signals for reinforcement learning in robotic manipulation.
- Lack of a systematic method for embodied agents to diagnose errors, understand why actions failed, and receive temporally grounded feedback in complex, visually rich environments.
- Challenges in temporal credit assignment, where current VLM-derived rewards provide overall outcomes but not precise step-wise feedback on critical moments of failure.

## Method

- Utilizes a VLM to generate structured, schema-constrained natural language feedback for failed trajectories, encoding it into feedback vectors.
- Identifies "key frames" within trajectories using saliency scores derived from image embeddings and their temporal derivatives to localize critical moments of success or failure.
- Aligns language feedback with visual states in a shared embedding space using calibrated contrastive losses and converts this into dense, failure-focused, and adaptively shaped reward signals for ==reinforcement learning==.

## Results

- Achieved an average success rate of **80%** on Meta-World MT10 (fixed goal) and **70.4%** (random goal), representing improvements of **5.3%** and **9%** over baselines, respectively.
- Outperformed state-of-the-art baselines on Robotic Fetch tasks with an average success rate of **51.67%**, an absolute improvement of **17%**.
- Demonstrated significantly faster policy convergence across multiple Meta-World tasks, reaching higher success rates in fewer environment steps compared to existing methods.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Structured VLM feedback, constrained by a schema, is essential for reducing hallucination and ensuring consistent, actionable guidance in robotic learning.
> - Localizing VLM feedback to "key frames" within a trajectory provides crucial temporal grounding, enabling more precise credit assignment for failures.
> - Dynamically shaping rewards, where VLM feedback provides dense signals during early failures and its influence recedes with increasing competence, improves learning efficiency and stability.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation has experienced advancements through the integration of multimodal foundation models. These models contribute to capabilities such as language-grounded affordance reasoning and vision-language-action transfer, enabling robots to exhibit zero-shot behaviors and semantic understanding. However, converting these large pre-trained priors into consistent and effective learning signals for reinforcement learning (RL) remains a challenge, primarily due to complexities in reward design.

A current trend involves utilizing Vision-Language Models (VLMs) as zero-shot reward models to assess progress based on natural language goals and visual observations. Despite this, such VLM-derived scores typically provide an overall outcome summary rather than granular, step-wise credit. They can also exhibit instability due to viewpoint and context variations, and may inherit biases and inconsistencies, including hallucination and noise. While approaches exist to densify these rewards into per-step signals or to use contrastive objectives like FuRL to mitigate reward misalignment, these methods can still lead to training instability, reward hacking, or compounded misdirection in early exploration, especially in long-horizon, sparse-reward tasks.

A recognized gap in the current landscape is the absence of a principled method for embodied agents to learn effectively from their own mistakes. Existing self-reflection paradigms, which often rely on textual self-critique, have largely been demonstrated in text-only environments where observation, action, and feedback share a symbolic interface, abstracting away the complexities of perception and low-level control in real-world or visually rich stochastic environments. This work addresses the need for structured, temporally grounded guidance that allows agents to recognize what went wrong, when it occurred, and its implications for subsequent decisions, particularly in the context of visual robotic manipulation where exploration can be computationally expensive. The paper positions itself as developing a mechanism for learning from failure in physically embodied, visually complex settings, differentiating it from approaches that use natural language solely for policy execution or planning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable embodied robotic agents to learn from their own failures by leveraging natural language as an error-reasoning signal. The authors hypothesize that structured and temporally grounded language can facilitate self-reflection, allowing robots to diagnose errors and improve decision-making.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges in current robotic manipulation research:

- **Learning from Mistakes:** Existing robotic agents often lack a systematic way to learn from unsuccessful attempts. While foundation models help define goals, they do not inherently provide the fine-grained, causal feedback necessary for an agent to understand *why* a particular action sequence failed.
- **Limitations of VLM-derived Rewards:** While VLMs can provide reward signals, these are often high-level, episodic summaries rather than dense, step-wise feedback. This can lead to noisy or inconsistent reward landscapes that destabilize RL training or encourage superficial reward exploitation rather than genuine task mastery. Furthermore, general-purpose VLMs can hallucinate or rationalize errors, particularly under slight distribution shifts, making their raw output unreliable for error diagnosis.
- **Temporal Credit Assignment:** Simply providing episodic feedback does not solve the problem of temporal credit assignment, where the agent needs to identify the precise moments in a trajectory that were critical to success or failure. This requires localizing decisive moments and aligning feedback with specific visual states.
- **Reducing Engineering Overhead:** The traditional reliance on hand-crafted reward functions for reinforcement learning in robotics is resource-intensive and often non-generalizable across tasks and environments. Developing a framework that automatically generates useful reward signals from high-level language aims to reduce this overhead.
- **Bridging Textual and Embodied AI:** Previous work on self-reflection largely resides in text-only environments, where the interface is symbolic. A key motivation is to translate the benefits of language-based self-critique into embodied settings where perception and low-level control are paramount and exploration is expensive.

To address these motivations, the LAGEA framework aims to achieve the following specific objectives:

- To generate causal, episodic, natural-language feedback from a Vision-Language Model (VLM) that summarizes attempted actions, violated constraints, and actionable rationales.
- To localize these reflections in time by identifying "key frames" in a trajectory that correspond to decisive moments, thereby sharpening temporal credit assignment.
- To align the generated language feedback with visual states in a shared representation space, ensuring the feedback is control-relevant and actionable.
- To convert this aligned and temporally-grounded feedback into bounded, step-wise shaping rewards for reinforcement learning, modulated by an adaptive, failure-aware coefficient to provide dense signals early in learning and recede as competence grows.
- To demonstrate improved success rates and faster convergence on complex, sparse-reward robotic manipulation tasks compared to existing state-of-the-art methods.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

LAGEA (Language Guided Embodied Agents) is a framework that integrates VLM-generated feedback into reinforcement learning for robotic manipulation. It operates through a multi-stage process encompassing feedback generation, alignment, and dynamic reward shaping.

#### 3.1. Feedback Generation

This stage involves using a VLM to generate structured natural language reflections on an agent's trajectory.
- **Structured Feedback (Section 3.1.1):** The Qwen-2.5-VL-3B VLM is prompted at the end of each episode. To ensure reliability and comparability, feedback is structured as a schema-constrained JSON object. The prompt includes the task instruction, an error taxonomy, few-shot exemplars (successful and failed attempts), and a short history from previous attempts. This structure aims to mitigate VLM hallucination and drift. The resulting natural language reflection is then encoded into a 768-dimensional feedback vector using a GPT-2 model.
- **Key Frame Generation (Section 3.1.2):** To address the issue of noisy credit assignment from broadcasting a single episodic feedback across an entire trajectory, LAGEA identifies a small set of "key frames." These frames represent causal moments (e.g., approach, contact, reversal). Key frames are determined deterministically and model-agnostically by computing a goal-similarity trajectory (`s_t`) from image embeddings, along with its temporal derivatives (`v_t`, `a_t`). These signals are combined into a per-step saliency score (`p_t`), which favors frames near the goal, undergoing rapid changes, or at sharp turns. A limited number of high-saliency indices (`M`) with minimum temporal spacing are selected as key frames (`K`). These `K` frames are then converted into per-step weights (`w_t`) using a triangular kernel and a small floor, concentrating influence near causal moments.

#### 3.2. Feedback Alignment (Section 3.1.3)

This stage makes the episodic feedback actionable by aligning it with visual states in a shared embedding space.
- Small MLP projectors (`E_i`, `E_f`) map state images (`x_t`) and feedback (`f`) into unit-norm embeddings (`z_t`, `z_f`). The goal image (`g`) is also projected to `z_g`.
- Updates are weighted by `u_t` (a product of key-frame saliency and goal proximity) to focus learning on critical, near-goal moments.
- The alignment process employs two complementary losses:
 - **Calibration Loss (`L_bce`):** This loss treats the cosine similarity (`ψ_t = ⟨z_t, z_f⟩`) between state and feedback embeddings as a logit, supervised by a per-step success label (`y_t`). Successful steps pull embeddings together, while failures push them apart.
 - **Contrastive Loss (`L_nce`):** For successful examples, this loss encourages the feedback embedding to prefer its corresponding image embedding over other image embeddings in the batch, shaping the relative geometry across the batch.
- **Symmetric Refinement (`L_sym`):** A further refinement step uses a symmetric, weighted contrastive loss, averaging cross-entropy in both feedback-to-image and image-to-feedback directions. This includes label smoothing and small regularizers for pairwise alignment and uniformity on the unit sphere.
- The trained projectors are used to compute goal and feedback-delta potentials for reward shaping and to estimate instruction text-feedback agreement for reward fusion.

#### 3.3. Reward Generation (Section 3.2)

This component translates progress towards the task and alignment with feedback into dense, directional rewards.
- Current state (`z_t`), goal image (`z_g`), episodic feedback (`z_f`), and instruction text (`z_y`) embeddings are used.
- **Goal Potential (`ϕ_t`):** This potential measures the average affinity of the current state with both the instruction text and the goal image. Its temporal difference, `r_goal_t = tanh(γ(ϕ_{t+1} - ϕ_t) / τ_goal)`, forms the goal-delta reward, which is positive when moving closer to the goal.
- **Feedback Potential (`ψ_t`):** This potential is the cosine similarity between the current state embedding (`z_t`) and the feedback embedding (`z_f`). Its `ˆw_t`-weighted temporal difference, `r_fb_t = ˆw_t tanh(γ(ψ_{t+1} - ψ_t) / τ_f)`, creates the feedback-delta reward, concentrating credit on causal moments.
- **Fused Reward (`˜r_t`):** `r_goal_t` and `r_fb_t` are combined using a confidence-aware mixture. The mixing coefficient `α` is dynamically clipped and modulated by the agreement between the instruction text and the episodic feedback (`⟨z_y, z_f⟩`). This ensures `˜r_t` remains bounded within `[-1, 1]`.

#### 3.4. Dynamic Reward Shaping (Section 3.3)

The final reward signal for the reinforcement learning agent (SAC algorithm) is `r = r_task_t + ˆρ_t ˜r_t`, where `r_task_t` is the sparse environment reward.
- The shaping coefficient `ρ` is dynamically modulated to be failure-focused, progress-aware, and smooth.
- Shaping is only applied to failures using a mask `m_t = 1[r_task_t < 0]`.
- Progress `P` is estimated by combining an exponential moving average (EMA) of episodic success with a batch-level improvement signal from the goal delta.
- `ρ_t` is set such that it is large early in training (when `P` is low, indicating little progress) and gradually recedes as the policy's competence (`P`) grows. The effective shaping coefficient becomes `ˆρ_t = m_t ρ_t`. This strategy allows VLM shaping to accelerate early exploration and credit assignment, then gradually diminish its influence as the policy develops mastery.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted on the Meta-World MT10 and Robotic Fetch benchmarks provided evidence for the effectiveness of the LAGEA framework.

#### 4.1. VLM-Guided Feedback Improves Policy Learning and Task Success (RQ1)

- **Meta-World MT10 (fixed goal):** LAGEA achieved an average success rate of 80%, demonstrating a 5.3% improvement over existing baselines, including FuRL. On specific tasks like "button-press-topdown-v2," "drawer-open-v2," "push-v2," and "window-close-v2," LAGEA reached 100% success where some baselines scored lower.
- **Meta-World MT10 (random goal):** In this more challenging setting, LAGEA attained a 70.4% average success rate, which is a 9% improvement over all baselines. For example, on "button-press-topdown-v2," LAGEA achieved 96% success compared to FuRL's 64%.
- **Robotic Fetch Tasks:** LAGEA consistently outperformed baselines, achieving an average success rate of 51.67%. This represented a 17% absolute improvement over the strongest baseline. For instance, on "Push-v2," LAGEA achieved 53.33% success compared to FuRL's 40%.

#### 4.2. Natural Language Feedback Accelerates Policy Convergence (RQ2)

- Across eight Meta-World tasks, LAGEA demonstrated significantly faster policy convergence compared to both FuRL and SAC baselines. LAGEA consistently reached high success rates in fewer environment steps.
- On challenging tasks such as "button-press-topdown-v2" and "drawer-open-v2," LAGEA was the only method to achieve meaningful, non-zero success, indicating its ability to provide actionable guidance where other methods failed to progress.
- In terms of wall-clock time to convergence, LAGEA demonstrated a slightly faster average convergence time (92.36 minutes) compared to FuRL (94.90 minutes) across Meta-World MT10 tasks. This suggests that the improved sample efficiency from LAGEA's feedback mechanism compensated for the additional computational cost of VLM inference.

#### 4.3. Robustness of LAGEA Design (RQ3)

Ablation studies were conducted to assess the contribution of LAGEA's core components:
- **Synergy of Delta Rewards and Adaptive Shaping:** The study revealed that all components of the reward generation—goal delta reward (`r_goal_t`), feedback delta reward (`r_fb_t`), and dynamic reward shaping (`ρ`)—were critical and contributed synergistically. Removing any single component resulted in a substantial performance degradation. The full LAGEA framework achieved a 19% performance gain over the best-performing ablation, underscoring the complementary nature of these elements.
- **Keyframe Extraction & Credit Assignment:** Ablation of the keyframe generation mechanism on the "Drawer Open" task showed that LAGEA with keyframing learned the task efficiently, while the variant without keyframing failed to make crucial connections and catastrophically failed. This highlights the importance of localized guidance from keyframes.
- **Impact of Structured Feedback:** A comparison between structured feedback and free-form textual feedback from the VLM indicated a significant advantage for structured feedback. On average, structured feedback led to higher success rates (98.89% vs. 78.89%). This disparity was attributed to the consistency and unambiguous nature of schema-constrained feedback, which reduced noise and misleading guidance from verbose or irrelevant free-form text.
- **Feedback-Reward Alignment:** Visualizations of internal metrics showed a direct relationship between the convergence of the feedback alignment module and policy performance. Initially, the discrimination gap between successful and unsuccessful states was low, and policy success was zero. Around the 0.5M step mark, a stable and growing discrimination gap emerged, correlating with accelerated policy success. The BCE and NCE losses increased as the policy improved and encountered more challenging negative trajectories, indicating a co-adaptive learning process where the alignment module learned fine-grained distinctions.
- **Impact of Different VLMs/Text Encoders:** Additional experiments confirmed that LAGEA's framework is robust to the choice of underlying VLM backbones (e.g., SmolVLM2, InternVL2, OpenQwen2VL) and text encoders (e.g., LIV, BGE, MPNet). While stronger models generally improved performance, LAGEA's strategy of feedback grounding and reward shaping consistently yielded high average success rates across various combinations, outperforming vanilla baselines. The Qwen2.5-VL-3B + GPT-2 combination provided the best average performance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research provides a contribution to the field of embodied AI and robotic manipulation by demonstrating a method for integrating natural language as an error-reasoning signal for reinforcement learning. The LAGEA framework shifts the utility of natural language from merely defining goals to providing structured, temporally grounded feedback that enables agents to learn from their mistakes.

The significance of this work can be summarized as follows:

- **Principled Error Learning:** LAGEA introduces a systematic way for embodied agents to diagnose what went wrong in a trajectory, when it happened, and why it matters. This contrasts with previous approaches that struggled with noisy VLM rewards or were confined to text-only environments.
- **Enhanced Sample Efficiency and Convergence:** The framework's ability to convert episodic, schema-constrained VLM reflections into dense, step-wise reward shaping signals leads to faster convergence and higher success rates on complex, sparse-reward tasks. This addresses a common challenge in reinforcement learning, where sparse rewards hinder effective exploration.
- **Reduced Reward Engineering:** By leveraging VLMs to generate actionable feedback and structuring it into reward signals, LAGEA mitigates the labor-intensive process of hand-crafting reward functions for diverse robotic manipulation tasks.
- **Robustness and Generalization:** The extensive ablation studies indicate that the core components of LAGEA—structured feedback, keyframe-centric gating, feedback-vision alignment, and adaptive reward shaping—are synergistic and contribute to its robustness across different tasks, goal configurations, and even varying VLM/encoder choices.

The potential impact of LAGEA extends to several areas:

- **Assistive Robotics and Flexible Automation:** Improved learning efficiency and reduced reliance on specialized reward design could lower the development cost and accelerate the deployment of robots in assistive roles or flexible manufacturing settings, where robots need to adapt to diverse and potentially novel manipulation tasks.
- **Faster Scientific Iteration:** Researchers could leverage such frameworks to more rapidly iterate on robotic behaviors, as the system provides intrinsic error diagnostics and corrective signals, expediting the development cycle for new manipulation skills.
- **Generalist Robots:** This work contributes to the long-term goal of developing generalist robots that can perceive, understand, and learn from their environment autonomously. By providing a mechanism for self-reflection and error recovery, LAGEA advances robots toward more intelligent, adaptive, and autonomous capabilities. It represents a step towards robots that can not only execute instructions but also understand and internalize the reasons behind failures, leading to more resilient and capable agents.

While the current work is validated in simulation, the long-term implications involve bridging the sim-to-real gap, which remains an open challenge. However, the foundational method for language-guided error learning provides a basis for future research in real-world robotic deployments.
