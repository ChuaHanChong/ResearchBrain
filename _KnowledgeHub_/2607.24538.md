# NEO: NeRF It Once, Edit It Many Times for Continuous Object Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> NEO introduces a language-guided framework for editing Neural Radiance Fields (NeRFs) to enable continuous object manipulation by robots without re-scanning. This system allows robots to predict future scene states and maintain persistent changes, demonstrating superior performance over baselines in object removal and relocation with higher photometric accuracy (e.g., PSNR up to 27.20 dB) and geometric consistency.

## Problem

- NeRFs, while offering high-fidelity 3D representations, are static, necessitating time-consuming re-scanning after each robotic manipulation action to update the scene.
- Current NeRF editing methods often lack geometric consistency, persistency of edits, and reliability across multiple viewpoints, leading to artifacts or requiring full re-optimization.
- Robots need to predict the visual and geometric outcomes of their actions *before* execution for effective planning, collision checking, and user visualization.

## Method

- Developed a ==three-stage NeRF editing pipeline== (==neural field resampling==, ==multiview-consistent progressive inpainting==, ==knowledge distillation==) that updates network weights based on language prompts and planned object motions.
- Implemented ==adaptive ray resampling== for efficient object removal, mitigating undersampling in newly revealed regions and reducing artifacts.
- Utilized ==mask-conditioned, diffusion-based inpainting== with virtual viewpoints and iterative refinement to reconstruct occluded backgrounds consistently across views, and a ==teacher-student knowledge distillation== method for persistent scene reconfiguration.

## Results

- NEO consistently surpassed baseline methods (NeRFiller, Seal-3D, DFF) in object removal, achieving higher PSNR (**27.20 dB** full, **25.43 dB** masked) and SSIM (**0.881** full, **0.845** masked), with lower RMSE and reprojection errors.
- In pick-and-place experiments, NEO yielded improved photometric scores (PSNR **21.99 dB**, SSIM **0.783**) and lower reprojection error (**0.046**) compared to Seal-3D, particularly for newly revealed regions.
- The framework demonstrated faster computational performance, completing scene inpainting in approximately **6 minutes** (**15x** faster than NeRFiller) and object manipulation in **1.5 minutes**, and successfully supported multi-stage assembly tasks by accurately predicting future scene states.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Directly updating NeRF network weights via knowledge distillation allows for persistent, geometrically consistent scene changes essential for iterative robotic manipulation without re-scanning.
> - Adaptive ray resampling effectively removes objects while reallocating samples to revealed areas, preventing undersampling and improving background reconstruction.
> - Mask-conditioned, multiview-consistent progressive inpainting, leveraging virtual views, is critical for plausibly and coherently reconstructing previously occluded backgrounds.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Neural Radiance Fields (NeRFs) have developed as an implicit neural scene representation technique, providing high-fidelity novel view synthesis and dense 3D reconstruction from a collection of images. Initially applied in computer vision, NeRF methods are increasingly being explored in robotics for tasks such as 3D scene reconstruction, navigation, grasping, segmentation, and general manipulation. A key characteristic of NeRF-based representations for grasping and manipulation is their ability to encode 3D environments as continuous functions, which allows for view-consistent geometry and appearance queries at varying resolutions. This capability supports direct continuous occupancy queries through a single network evaluation, which can be beneficial for tasks like grasp synthesis and collision checking. NeRFs typically offer accurate and globally consistent 3D reconstructions, making them suitable for fine-grained spatial reasoning in robotics. Furthermore, NeRFs can integrate additional modalities, such as language features, enabling language-guided interactions.

However, robotic manipulation is an inherently interactive process where each action can alter object poses, introduce new occlusions, or reveal previously unseen surfaces. Such changes can invalidate the initial NeRF representation. Consequently, many existing NeRF-based robotic manipulation pipelines necessitate re-observing or re-scanning the scene to update the representation before subsequent actions. This re-scanning process can be time-consuming and may not be practical for real-world robotic applications.

An alternative approach involves directly editing the NeRF representation to update the scene model after interaction without requiring re-scanning or re-optimization from scratch. Several methods have been proposed for NeRF editing, but they often present limitations when applied to real-world robotic manipulation scenarios. These limitations include a lack of persistent edits, inconsistent underlying geometry after object removal, unreliability across viewpoints and subsequent actions, and the introduction of artifacts or hallucinations. Specifically, methods like Distilled Feature Fields (DFF) can lead to undersampling and distortions in newly revealed regions. Generative inpainting approaches, such as NeRFiller, can improve cross-view consistency but often require retraining and may still introduce artifacts. Other methods focus on appearance deformation, editing derived point-cloud representations, or separating object/background representations with limited inpainting capabilities, none of which fully address the requirements for robust object removal and rigid 3D transformations necessary for robotic manipulation. Existing scene editing and prediction approaches for robotics, such as those using Gaussian Splatting, may also suffer from degradation over time under repeated modifications or limitations in reconstructing occluded geometry. This research aims to address these identified gaps by proposing a NeRF editing method specifically tailored for continuous, language-guided robotic manipulation, emphasizing geometric consistency, persistence, and efficiency.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this work arises from the challenges of integrating Neural Radiance Fields (NeRFs) into iterative robotic manipulation tasks. While NeRFs offer high-fidelity 3D scene representations, they are typically static. Robotic interactions, however, dynamically alter the scene (e.g., moving or removing objects). Current NeRF-based robotic pipelines often require the robot to re-scan the environment after each action, which is inefficient and can be impractical in real-world settings. Existing NeRF editing methods, while capable of visual scene modification, generally lack the robustness, geometric consistency, and persistence required for reliable robotic applications.

Specifically, the key challenges and motivations identified are:
- **Need for Persistent Edits:** Scene modifications must be permanent to support subsequent updates, a feature often lacking in prior methods where edits might degrade or be overwritten.
- **Consistent Underlying Geometry:** When an object is removed, the previously occluded background must be reconstructed plausibly and consistently across different viewpoints, avoiding artifacts or implausible structures.
- **Reliability Across Viewpoints and Actions:** Edited representations must maintain their reliability and consistency over various viewpoints and through multiple sequential manipulation actions.
- **Avoidance of Hallucinations:** The editing process should not introduce spurious structures or visual inconsistencies that could mislead a robotic system.
- **Language-Guided Interaction:** The ability to specify manipulation targets and desired actions using natural language prompts is crucial for intuitive human-robot collaboration.
- **Prediction of Future Scene States:** Robots need to anticipate the visual and geometric outcomes of their actions *before* execution to enable planning, collision checking, and user visualization.

To address these motivations, the "NEO" framework sets forth the following key objectives:
1. **Develop a unified NeRF editing framework for robotic manipulation:** This framework should directly update network weights to facilitate realistic, geometrically consistent, and persistent scene modifications. It should include capabilities for object removal via neural field resampling, multiview-consistent progressive inpainting for reconstructing revealed regions, and support continuous, language-guided interaction without requiring additional scene re-scanning.
2. **Introduce a scene-focused knowledge distillation method:** This method aims to reconfigure scenes by composing an originally trained NeRF with an edited NeRF. Utilizing a teacher–student model, it seeks to permanently preserve scene changes, enabling coherent modeling of future scene states.
3. **Establish a benchmark dataset (NEO-Dataset):** This dataset is intended for quantitatively evaluating NeRF scene editing methods specifically within robotic manipulation scenarios. It provides registered pre- and post-edit scenes to allow for rigorous and objective assessment.

These objectives collectively aim to bridge the gap between static NeRF representations and the dynamic requirements of interactive robotic manipulation, enabling more autonomous and efficient robotic systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

NEO is presented as a unified framework for language-guided NeRF editing, designed for robotic manipulation. The approach involves a three-stage editing pipeline that processes a single initial scene scan and language prompts to update the scene representation without requiring subsequent re-scanning.

The process begins by training a network F_Θ_ on a set of camera images and their poses, learning a joint neural representation comprising a scene field (S) and an associated language field (L). Given a user prompt describing a manipulation, the target object region is localized using the language field, based on cosine similarity between the text embedding of the prompt and language features at 3D surface points. An oriented bounding box (b) is fitted to the identified object. Conditioned on this prompt and a planned object motion (τ), NEO produces an updated scene representation (S', L') which can be iteratively reused for sequential manipulations.

The three main stages of the NEO pipeline are:

#### 3.1. Neural Field Resampling for Object Removal

This stage addresses the removal of a user-specified object from the NeRF. Unlike prior methods that simply suppress densities within the object's region (which can lead to undersampling and artifacts in previously occluded areas), NEO implements an adaptive ray resampling strategy. For each camera ray, the intersection with the object's bounding box (b) is computed. The ray segment within b is excluded from sampling. The remaining ray intervals are concatenated, and a fixed number of uniform samples are drawn from this combined domain. This reallocates samples to areas outside the object, mitigating undersampling in newly revealed regions. Subsequently, predicted densities from these samples are converted to volumetric rendering weights, which define a sampling distribution for hierarchical (PDF-based) resampling. A second fixed set of samples is drawn from this distribution over the concatenated domain, further concentrating sampling density in occupied spaces while avoiding the object region. This two-stage procedure aims for immediate object removal and reduced artifacts.

#### 3.2. Multiview-Consistent Progressive Inpainting

After object removal, regions that were previously occluded and never observed during the initial scan may contain artifacts or implausible structures. To address this, NEO employs a multiview-consistent progressive inpainting approach.
1. **View Augmentation:** Virtual camera poses are generated, focused on a hemisphere centered around the removed object's bounding box. These viewpoints (at opposing azimuths and varying elevations) are added to the training set, concentrating supervision on previously occluded surfaces.
2. **Mask-Conditioned Inpainting:** A training set (D) is constructed, combining object-centered renderings from virtual viewpoints (I_v_) and original captured images (I_o_). Binary masks are generated: M_v_ for virtual views (identifying the removed region) and M_o_ for original images (complementary mask). Four images and their corresponding masks are tiled into 2x2 composites. A diffusion-based inpainting model, conditioned on these composite masks, completes missing regions, promoting cross-view consistency.
3. **NeRF Refinement:** The inpainted virtual images (I_v_), original images (I_o_), and their masks (M_v_, M_o_) are used to train a mask-conditioned NeRF (˜F_Θ_). This training utilizes a mask-conditioned reconstruction loss (L_rec_) that compares rendered images to actual images only over pixels selected by the mask, allowing selective reconstruction of occluded areas while preserving observed content.
4. **Iterative Refinement and Regularization:** The virtual object-centered views (I_v_) are re-rendered and the inpainting process is repeated every 'n' steps, with a progressively reduced inpainting strength factor (ξ) to encourage convergence. A regularization loss (L_reg_) is introduced to penalize non-zero density predictions within the bounding box (b), preventing spurious densities and ensuring persistent object removal. The NeRF (˜F_Θ_) is optimized using a combined objective L_masked_ = λ_rec_L_rec_ + λ_reg_L_reg_.

#### 3.3. Knowledge Distillation Method for Scene Reconfiguration

This stage integrates the original NeRF (F_Θ_, encoding the complete scene) and the edited NeRF (˜F_Θ_, with the object removed and background inpainted) into a single, updated NeRF (F_ˆΘ_). This is achieved through knowledge distillation using a teacher–student model.
1. **Region-Wise Supervision:** The final scene representation (S') is constructed by selectively supervising the student NeRF from multiple "teacher" networks:
 - Regions outside the object's bounding box are supervised by F_Θ_.
 - Regions inside the *removal* bounding box are supervised by ˜F_Θ_.
 - Regions corresponding to the *relocated* object (at its new pose) are supervised by F_Θ_ under the desired motion.
2. **Distillation Process:** The student NeRF's parameters (ˆΘ), initialized from Θ, are optimized. Virtual object-centered views from the inpainting stage are re-incorporated to increase ray density in modified areas. Distillation occurs in two phases:
 - Initial phase: Direct supervision using teacher-predicted color and density values along rays.
 - Second phase: A combined volumetric RGB reconstruction loss and depth loss, matching rendered RGB and depth images from the teacher. The distillation loss (L_distill_) combines these terms.
The resulting F_ˆΘ_ encodes the reconfigured scene, supporting novel-view rendering with consistent object relocation, removal, and plausible background reconstruction.

#### 3.4. Updating Language Fields and Iterative Editing

After geometric editing, the edited NeRF (F_ˆΘ_) is fixed, and its associated language field (L') is updated to maintain consistency with the modified scene. This is done by supervising L' with CLIP and DINO features extracted from new images rendered from F_ˆΘ_ at the original camera poses. NEO supports iterative scene editing, where the output NeRF and language field from one edit can be directly used as input for subsequent edits, which is crucial for multi-step robotic assembly tasks.

#### 3.5. NEO-Dataset Benchmark

To enable quantitative evaluation, NEO-Dataset was developed. It comprises 12 registered tabletop scenes (11 single-object, 1 multi-object), captured with two different robotic setups (Franka Emika Panda and UR5). Scenes are scanned both before and after object manipulations (removal or relocation). ArUco markers are used for scene registration. This dataset provides ground truth for evaluating NeRF editing quality (object removal) and manipulation prediction (object relocation).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper presents quantitative and qualitative evaluations of NEO compared to baseline methods across object removal, robotic manipulation, and sequential manipulation tasks, supported by an ablation study.

#### 4.1. Object Removal Experiments

**Quantitative Results (Table I):** NEO consistently outperformed baseline methods—NeRFiller, Seal-3D, NeRFiller without depth supervision (NeRFiller_w/o D_), and DFF—across all metrics (PSNR, SSIM, RMSE, E_rep_) for object removal. NEO achieved the highest PSNR (27.20 dB full, 25.43 dB masked), SSIM (0.881 full, 0.845 masked), and the lowest RMSE (0.056 m full, 0.033 m masked) and E_rep_ (0.067 full, 0.071 masked). This indicates superior photometric accuracy, structural similarity, and geometric consistency in both the full image and the specific regions where objects were removed.

**Qualitative Results (Figure 8):** Visual comparisons supported the quantitative findings.
- **DFF:** Produced "floating artifacts" after object removal, indicating issues with background reconstruction.
- **NeRFiller and NeRFiller_w/o D_:** While locally plausible, introduced more background artifacts and visual inconsistencies.
- **Seal-3D:** Left noticeable artifacts on the table surface.
- **NEO:** Generated images that closely resembled the ground-truth scene after removal, with improved reconstruction of the revealed background and minimal artifacts.
**Computational Efficiency:** NEO completed scene inpainting in approximately 6 minutes, which is faster than DFF (13 minutes) and significantly faster than NeRFiller (around 1.5 hours), demonstrating an approximate 15x speed-up.

#### 4.2. Robotic Manipulation Experiments (Pick-and-Place)

**Quantitative Results (Table II):** In pick-and-place scenarios, comparing against Seal-3D, NEO achieved better photometric scores (PSNR 21.99 dB vs. 18.94 dB; SSIM 0.783 vs. 0.695) and a lower reprojection error (E_rep_ 0.046 vs. 0.102) for the "Out" region (original object location). For the "In" region (new object location), NEO showed slightly higher PSNR (19.26 dB vs. 18.56 dB) and SSIM (0.619 vs. 0.589), with comparable RMSE. This indicates NEO's improved ability to reconstruct newly revealed surfaces and render the relocated object at its new position with greater accuracy.

**Qualitative Results (Figure 9):**
- **Seal-3D:** Often left remnants of the original object at its previous location and showed degradation in the appearance of the manipulated object.
- **NEO:** Avoided these visual artifacts, producing higher-quality and more coherent reconstructions of moved objects.
**Computational Efficiency:** NEO completed object manipulation tasks in approximately 1.5 minutes, compared to 3 minutes for Seal-3D, attributed to more efficient teacher–student knowledge distillation.

#### 4.3. Ablation Study

**Quantitative Results (Table III):** The ablation study investigated the contribution of NEO's individual components in the pick-and-place task:
- **Mask-conditioned training (w/o Mask-cond. train.):** This component was identified as the most crucial, as its removal led to the most significant drops in PSNR, SSIM, RMSE, and E_rep_.
- **Virtual view distribution (w/o Virtual view dist.):** Had a smaller, but notable, impact on performance.
- **Grid inpainting (w/o Grid inp.):** Showed an intermediate level of importance.
- **Field distillation only (Field distill only):** Using only the first term of the distillation loss (density and color along rays) resulted in reduced performance compared to the full model, highlighting the value of RGB and depth supervision from student rendering in the distillation process.
These findings confirm that all proposed components contribute to the overall performance of the NEO model, with mask-conditioned training being particularly critical.

#### 4.4. Assembly Results

**Qualitative Results (Figure 10):** The multi-stage assembly experiment demonstrated NEO's capability for consecutive edits. The synthesized views, predicting the future state of the scene after each manipulation, showed minimal differences from the real images of the scene. This indicated that the NEO model supported subsequent edits effectively and accurately predicted the effects of each action over a sequence of manipulations. The ability to perform bounding box generation and grasp planning directly on the current edited NEO model, which includes updated geometry and a refined language field, supported its utility as a dynamic state estimator for manipulation planning.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The NEO framework represents a development in the integration of Neural Radiance Fields with robotic manipulation. By providing a unified approach for language-guided NeRF editing, it addresses limitations inherent in static scene representations when applied to dynamic, interactive robotic tasks.

The primary significance of this work lies in its ability to enable robots to anticipate the visual and geometric outcomes of their actions *before* actual execution. This predictive capability is fundamental for robust robot planning, allowing for proactive collision checking, improved grasp synthesis, and enhanced user visualization of planned actions. The proposed direct NeRF weight editing method, utilizing knowledge distillation, allows for persistent scene changes, a key requirement for multi-stage tasks where scene modifications accumulate over time without the need for repeated re-scanning.

The specific technical contributions, such as neural field resampling for object removal, multiview-consistent progressive inpainting, and a scene-focused knowledge distillation method, collectively aim to produce geometrically consistent and visually coherent scene edits. The quantitative and qualitative results indicate that NEO surpasses prior NeRF editing approaches in terms of visual fidelity, structural consistency, and computational efficiency in both object removal and relocation scenarios. This suggests a more reliable representation for downstream manipulation tasks compared to existing methods that may introduce artifacts or require extensive re-optimization.

Furthermore, the introduction of the NEO-Dataset benchmark is a notable contribution to the research community. By providing ground-truth, registered tabletop scenes captured before and after object manipulations, it establishes a standardized platform for rigorously evaluating and comparing future NeRF editing methods tailored for robotic applications. This dataset can facilitate advancements in the field by enabling objective assessment of model performance against real-world changes.

The potential impact of NEO extends to various robotic applications that require dynamic scene understanding and manipulation, including:
- **Advanced Assembly Tasks:** Enabling robots to perform complex multi-step assembly operations by accurately predicting intermediate scene states and maintaining a consistent semantic representation.
- **Human-Robot Collaboration:** Facilitating more intuitive interaction through language-guided manipulation and real-time visualization of predicted changes.
- **Efficient Robot Learning:** Providing a foundation for training reinforcement learning agents that can leverage accurate future scene state predictions for improved policy learning.

Future work outlined by the authors, such as extending the framework to articulated objects and leveraging NEO for training RL-based controllers, suggests continued development towards more sophisticated and autonomous robotic systems capable of navigating and manipulating complex environments with dynamic scene understanding.
