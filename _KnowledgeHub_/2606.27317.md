# OctoSense: Self-Supervised Learning for Multimodal Robot Perception

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the University of Pennsylvania and Brown University developed OctoSense, a framework comprising an open-source multi-sensor platform, a 59-hour multimodal dataset, and a "late-fusion" masked autoencoder for self-supervised learning. This system generates robust representations that improve performance and resilience for dense perception and ego-motion tasks in diverse and degraded real-world robotic environments.

## Problem

- Real-world robotic applications face challenges due to the inherent limitations and diverse failure modes of individual sensors, particularly in degraded environments like low light or adverse weather.
- Existing self-supervised learning (SSL) models for robotics primarily focus on single modalities or limited multimodal combinations, lacking a comprehensive framework for integrating a full suite of heterogeneous robotic sensors.
- The scarcity of large-scale, time-synchronized datasets with diverse, multimodal sensor data hinders the development of robust robot perception systems.

## Method

- Developed **OctoSense**, an open-source hardware platform integrating eight diverse robotic sensors (RGB, event, thermal cameras, LiDAR, IMU, RTK-GPS, and proprioception).
- Created and released the OctoSense dataset, a 59-hour, 8.4 TB collection of time-synchronized multimodal data covering varied environments and conditions, including sensor degradation.
- Designed a "late-fusion" Masked Autoencoder (MAE) architecture that uses modality-specific tokenizers and spatio-temporal tube masking to learn robust representations from heterogeneous sensor data.

## Results

- The OctoSense late-fusion MAE achieved lower RMSE for depth (4.73m vs. 6.38m for V-JEPA **2.1**) and lower EPE for optical flow (1.97px vs. 9.13px for V-JEPA **2.1**), with larger improvements observed in nighttime conditions.
- It demonstrated superior ego-motion estimation, with approximately 0.06m translation error and **0.23**-**0.24**° rotation error, leveraging the contributions of LiDAR and IMU.
- The pre-trained MAE exhibited zero-shot generalization capabilities to the M3ED dataset and could effectively reconstruct missing sensor data, highlighting its potential for robust perception despite sensor failures.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The "late-fusion" masked autoencoder design achieves performance comparable to early-fusion architectures while significantly reducing inference latency, making it practical for real-time robotic deployment.
> - Integrating a diverse suite of heterogeneous sensors through self-supervised learning leads to substantially improved perception robustness and accuracy, particularly in challenging conditions such as low light or when individual sensor data is degraded.
> - Modality-specific ablation studies confirm that LiDAR is crucial for accurate ego-motion and depth estimation, while RGB cameras remain key for semantic segmentation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics has witnessed advancements driven by vision-based foundation models, such as DINO, SigLIP, and Hiera, which leverage self-supervised learning (SSL). These models primarily operate on visual or textual data. However, real-world robotic applications frequently encounter scenarios where a single sensor is insufficient due to inherent limitations. For instance, global-shutter cameras perform suboptimally in low light, high dynamic range, or rapid motion, while LiDAR provides sparse depth data at low frame rates with limited semantic information. Different sensors possess distinct sensing modalities, resolutions, data types, and information rates, and they respond disparately to environmental degradations like weather, noise, and packet loss, leading to varied failure modes. The Global Robotics Technology Roadmap 2025–2035 identifies robust multi-sensor fusion, particularly in degraded environments, as a critical enabling technology for field robots.

Existing SSL methodologies have largely concentrated on individual sensor types, with a growing body of work extending to depth, event cameras, thermal cameras, and inertial measurement units (IMUs). Nevertheless, a comprehensive SSL framework capable of integrating data from a complete suite of typical robotic sensors remains an area of active development.

This research addresses this identified gap by extending multi-modal self-supervised learning. While previous multi-modal SSL efforts have explored techniques like contrastive alignment (e.g., CLIP, ImageBind) and masked multi-modal modeling (e.g., MultiMAE, 4M, UniM2AE, CALICO), these often focus on specific combinations like vision-language or camera-LiDAR for tasks such as bird's eye view (BEV) map segmentation. The work presented here expands masked multi-modal modeling to a broader array of robotic sensors beyond just cameras and LiDAR, and applies it to dense perception and ego-motion tasks. It contributes a new hardware platform, a large-scale dataset, and a tailored SSL architecture designed to integrate heterogeneous sensor data and generate robust representations for real-world robotic operation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the recognition that real-world robotic perception systems require robust and resilient representations that can function reliably despite the inherent limitations and potential failures of individual sensors. While self-supervised learning (SSL) has enabled progress in vision-based models, these models often rely on single modalities or limited multi-modal combinations, which are insufficient for the diverse and challenging conditions robots face. Factors such as low light, high dynamic range, rapid motion, weather degradation, and sensor noise can severely impair the performance of individual sensors. The inability of existing systems to consistently perform in such degraded conditions is highlighted in the Global Robotics Technology Roadmap, which emphasizes the critical need for multi-sensor fusion robust to environmental degradation.

To address these challenges, the research established several key objectives:

1. **To develop a comprehensive open-source multi-sensor platform and dataset**: A fundamental obstacle in multi-modal robot representation learning is the scarcity of large datasets featuring diverse, time-synchronized sensor data. The project aimed to construct an open-source hardware platform, termed OctoSense, equipped with a wide array of sensors, including stereo RGB and event cameras, a thermal camera, LiDAR, an Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU), RTK-corrected Global Positioning System (GPS), and proprioception data (CAN bus from a vehicle, joint angles from a quadruped robot). A crucial objective was to collect and release a substantial dataset (59 hours) from this platform, covering various urban, suburban, and rural environments, diverse lighting conditions (sunrise, daytime, sunset, nighttime), and situations where sensor data is degraded due to factors like sunflares or packet loss. This dataset was intended to serve as a resource for the broader research community.

2. **To propose a multi-modal self-supervised learning approach**: Given the heterogeneity of robotic sensor data—encompassing different representations, frequencies, latencies, and noise characteristics—the research aimed to design an SSL architecture capable of effectively fusing these diverse inputs. The proposed approach was a "late-fusion" masked autoencoder. This architecture was designed to incorporate modality-specific tokenizers to account for the unique spatiotemporal characteristics of each sensor type. Furthermore, it aimed to implement an efficient inference mechanism that caches modality-specific tokens, processing only new measurements as they arrive, thereby ensuring practical operational speed on robotic platforms.

3. **To demonstrate the performance and robustness of the approach on downstream robotic tasks**: A key objective was to quantitatively evaluate the efficacy of the developed multi-modal MAE on critical robotic perception tasks. These tasks included estimation of optical flow, depth, semantic segmentation, and ego-motion (translation, rotation, and steering angle). The motivation was to demonstrate that the multi-modal approach not only outperforms existing image-only foundation models but also exhibits enhanced robustness and accuracy in challenging conditions, particularly at nighttime or when individual sensory data streams are degraded. This evaluation was intended to validate the practical utility and resilience of the OctoSense framework for real-world robot perception.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research involved the development of a novel multi-sensor hardware platform, the creation of a large-scale multimodal dataset, and the design of a self-supervised learning architecture tailored for heterogeneous robotic sensor data.

#### 3.1. OctoSense Hardware and Dataset

The OctoSense platform integrates eight distinct sensors: stereo RGB cameras, stereo event cameras, a thermal camera, a LiDAR unit, an Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU), an RTK-corrected Global Positioning System (GPS), and proprioception data (CAN bus from a vehicle and joint angles from a quadruped robot). This suite was designed to capture diverse information streams with varying data rates, bandwidths, and resolutions.

- **Engineering Challenges and Solutions**: Significant effort was invested in addressing practical issues:
 - **RGB Cameras**: Configured for 100 Hz frame rate with an auto-exposure policy targeting a mean brightness of 60/255, adjusting exposure then gain.
 - **Event Cameras**: Fixed biases for consistent event generation. A 390–750 nm bandpass filter was added to block infrared interference from the LiDAR. Foam insulation mitigated timestamp corruption in cold weather.
 - **Thermal Camera**: Operated at 50 Hz in high-gain mode with digital detail enhancement. Auto-exposure set to Plateau Histogram mode for 14-bit to 8-bit mapping.
 - **LiDAR**: Ouster OS1-64 running at 10 Hz, outputting range, near-infrared, signal intensity, and reflectivity images, along with internal IMU data. Packet loss due to vibration was mitigated by soft-mounting cables.
 - **IMU**: VectorNav VN-100T-CR configured at 400 Hz for accelerations, angular velocities, magnetic fields, and barometric pressure. Soft-mounting on rubber-damped standoffs reduced vibration noise.
 - **GPS**: ZED-F9P configured at 5 Hz with a Waveshare patch antenna, magnetically mounted to the vehicle roof for improved satellite lock. RTK corrections via NTRIP were facilitated by a cellular hotspot with an internal battery and USB-to-Ethernet interface, avoiding power overdraw issues from USB dongles.
 - **CAN Bus**: Accessed directly from the vehicle’s forward-sensing camera connector via a custom splitter harness, processed by a DSD-C31G adapter, and recorded via ROS2 SocketCAN with OpenDBC definitions.
 - **Compute & Data Recording**: An AMD Ryzen 9700X 8-core processor with 64GB DDR5 RAM and Samsung 9100 NVMe SSD enabled onboard computation. ROS 2 Zenoh composable nodes provided zero-copy transfer for recording compressed sensor streams (e.g., H.265 video for RGB/thermal, LiDAR/event packets), reducing bandwidth from ~1.7 GB/s to ~78.7 MB/s.

- **Temporal Synchronization**: Custom circuits were developed to hardware-trigger sensors using a Pulse Per Second (PPS) signal, enhanced with a unique six-pulse sequence every four minutes and fifteen seconds for unambiguous realignment. Post-processing applied Kalman smoothing to fit an affine transformation between sensor clocks and the main clock.

- **Calibration**: A retro-reflective circle target centered on an AprilGrid was used. Event frames were reconstructed using a second-order IIR filter. RGB and event camera intrinsics and extrinsics were optimized with Kalibr. LiDAR-camera extrinsics were determined using correspondences from the retro-reflective circle (reprojected center error, circle radius from LiDAR points, distance to board), initialized by RANSAC with the Kabsch–Umeyama algorithm, and refined by Levenberg–Marquardt. Camera-IMU extrinsics were also obtained via Kalibr.

- **Dataset Content**: The OctoSense dataset comprises 59 hours and 8.4 TB of time-synchronized data. It covers urban, suburban, and rural environments, including highway, residential, and city driving across various lighting conditions (sunrise, daytime, sunset, nighttime), with sequences exhibiting sensor degradation (sunflares, dropped packets). It is noted as one of the largest datasets of its kind incorporating event camera data. A semantic search pipeline was implemented, captioning 5-second windows with Gemma 4, embedding with Qwen3-Embedding-8B, and indexing with FAISS for natural language queries.

- **Ground-Truth Generation (Pseudo-labels)**:
 - **Odometry**: RKO-LIO, a LiDAR-inertial odometry method, provided platform pose in SE(3) and linear/angular velocities. It also deskewed 360° LiDAR scans.
 - **Depth**: Accumulated points from 61 consecutive deskewed LiDAR scans (6-second window) formed dense ground-truth depth. Moving objects (vehicles, pedestrians) were filtered using YOLO26-medium. For overlapping depth measurements, the minimum depth was retained.
 - **Optical Flow**: Ego-motion-induced pixel displacement was computed by projecting accumulated LiDAR points into future RGB frames using odometry poses and extrinsics.
 - **Semantic Segmentation**: Pseudo-labels were generated by running EoMT, a state-of-the-art segmentation model trained on Cityscapes, on rectified RGB images.

#### 3.2. Multi-modal Masked Autoencoder (OctoSense Architecture)

The core of the methodology is a "late-fusion" Masked Autoencoder (MAE) designed to handle the diverse spatiotemporal characteristics of the sensors.

- **Input Representation**:
 - **RGB**: Undistorted and rectified images were converted into ViT-style patches.
 - **Event Cameras**: Event streams were processed by a spatiotemporal filter for noise reduction and then converted into a multi-channel image-like representation using a bank of leaky integrators with different bandwidths, normalized per channel.
 - **LiDAR**: 64x2048 range images were cropped to a 90° field of view, resulting in 64x512 range images.
 - **IMU**: Accelerometer and gyroscope data within a 1.6-second window (640 samples) were convolved and tokenized via cross-attention pooling.

- **Training Tokenizers**: Modality-specific tokenizers were trained to convert continuous sensor data into discrete tokens using Finite Scalar Quantization (FSQ). This allows the MAE to minimize discrepancy between predicted and ground-truth tokens for all patches, rather than pixel intensities.
 - **Architecture**: Each tokenizer employed a transformer encoder with RoPE (2D for image-like, 1D for IMU). The decoder was a hybrid transformer-convolutional architecture for sharper reconstructions.
 - **Losses**: ℓ1 loss, with modality-specific adjustments: ℓ1 on log-depth, depth-validity mask with Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE), and SiLog loss for LiDAR; weighted reconstruction loss for event cameras balancing active/inactive pixels; ℓ1 loss on mean-removed, normalized time series for IMU.

- **Fusing Individual Modalities in the MAE**:
 - **Input to MAE**: Tokens from each sensor over a 1.4-second window (8 frames from RGB, event, LiDAR, and a 1.6-second window of IMU data).
 - **Late-Fusion Architecture**: This architecture was chosen over early-fusion to manage GPU memory and bandwidth requirements. It consists of modality-specific spatial and temporal tubelet encoders that feed into a cross-modal encoder.
 - **Masking Strategy**: Spatio-temporal tube masking was applied to RGB, event, and LiDAR data. The masking ratio `r_s` was derived from a Dirichlet distribution. IMU tokens were kept independently with a Bernoulli parameter.
 - **Architecture Details**: Uses 4D RoPE for positional encoding across time, spatial coordinates, and modality index. The encoder applies a per-modality spatial encoder (3-layer transformer) at each time instant on visible tokens, followed by a per-modality temporal encoder (2-layer transformer) across each unmasked spatio-temporal tube (tubelet attention). A final cross-modal encoder (3-layer transformer) processes features from all modalities. The decoder is an 8-layer transformer that predicts the FSQ tokenizer's values using an ℓ1 loss.

- **Task-Specific Probes**: After pretraining, task-specific probes were trained on the frozen encoder's representations.
 - **Dense Prediction Tasks (Depth, Optical Flow, Semantic Segmentation)**: A Dense Prediction Transformer (DPT)-style fusion head utilized features from four uniformly spaced encoder layers. Features were processed by a self-attention block to incorporate temporal information and then fed into the DPT. The same DPT network, with task-specific heads (1x1 convolutions), predicted multiple tasks using SiLog loss for depth, Charbonnier loss for flow, and cross-entropy for segmentation.
 - **Ego-motion Prediction (Linear/Angular Velocity, Relative Translation/Rotation, Steering Angle, Car Speed)**: Three independent cross-attention modules queried the last layer of the encoder. Rotation prediction used the squared Frobenius norm with projection to SO(3) at inference, while other targets used Huber loss on Z-scored values.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of OctoSense involved comparing its "late-fusion" MAE architecture against several vision-only foundation models and custom baselines across five key robotic perception tasks: depth estimation, optical flow, semantic segmentation, and ego-motion (translation, rotation, steering angle). The dataset comprised 293 training sequences and 78 test sequences, including daytime, nighttime, and sensor-degraded conditions.

#### 4.1. Overall Performance

The late-fusion MAE consistently demonstrated improved performance compared to existing approaches across most tasks, particularly in challenging conditions.

- **Depth Estimation**: The late-fusion MAE achieved an RMSE of 4.73 m, outperforming the best existing vision-only method, V-JEPA 2.1, which had an RMSE of 6.38 m. This difference was more pronounced at nighttime, with the late-fusion MAE achieving 6.08 m RMSE compared to V-JEPA 2.1's 9.51 m, indicating enhanced robustness to low-light conditions.
- **Optical Flow**: The late-fusion MAE yielded an average end-point error (EPE) of 1.97 pixels, a substantial improvement over V-JEPA 2.1's 9.13 pixels. Daytime and nighttime improvements were approximately 7.16 pixels and 8.51 pixels, respectively.
- **Semantic Segmentation**: The late-fusion MAE achieved a mean Intersection-over-Union (mIoU) of 0.411, marginally exceeding the best existing model (0.410). The paper suggests this narrow margin might be influenced by the DINOv2-based pseudo-label generation used for ground truth. Analysis (Table S15) indicated the late-fusion MAE was competitive on frequent categories but exhibited deficits on rarer classes such as poles, buses, and persons compared to DINOv2.
- **Ego-motion**: The late-fusion MAE demonstrated significantly superior performance in ego-motion tasks, achieving approximately 0.06 m in translation error and 0.23–0.24° in rotation error. This capability is attributed to its access to LiDAR and inertial information, which are critical for accurate self-motion estimation. Improvements were notably larger in nighttime data and under degraded sensor conditions (Table 2).

#### 4.2. Comparison to Baselines

- **RGB-only MAE baselines**: The RGB-only image MAE, trained on OctoSense data, performed comparably to existing vision-only methods evaluated zero-shot. The RGB-only video MAE showed performance gains over the image-only variant, consistent with V-JEPA 2.1's benefits from temporal pre-training.
- **Early-fusion vs. Late-fusion MAE**: The late-fusion MAE architecture achieved performance levels comparable to the "early-fusion" MAE, which uses a monolithic transformer. However, the late-fusion design offered significant computational advantages. It demonstrated lower inference latency: 6.68 ms on an NVIDIA 5090 (1.70x faster) and 112 ms on an Orin NX (2.57x faster) compared to the early-fusion MAE. This efficiency gain, without a performance penalty, makes the late-fusion approach more suitable for real-time robotic deployment.
- **Temporal Attention**: The proposed "tubelet attention" in the late-fusion MAE performed on par with a "full attention" variant that uses full temporal self-attention across all tokens in a modality, indicating the efficiency of the tubelet approach.

#### 4.3. Modality Ablation Studies

To understand the contribution of individual sensors, modalities were selectively omitted during test time:
- **LiDAR**: Removing LiDAR led to a significant increase in error for ego-motion tasks (translation, rotation, steering angle), underscoring its dominance. For dense prediction, depth RMSE increased from 4.73 m to 6.74 m, and optical flow EPE from 1.97 px to 7.19 px, indicating its crucial role.
- **RGB**: Omitting RGB data primarily impacted semantic segmentation performance, while depth and flow estimation remained largely unchanged.
- **Event Cameras and IMU**: Removing event cameras or the IMU had a smaller effect on overall performance across most tasks. The paper posits that this marginal contribution might be due to the 5 Hz evaluation rate, which is significantly lower than the native temporal resolution of these sensors, suggesting they may be more beneficial for high-speed or low-latency applications.
- Notably, the vision-only V-JEPA 2.1 performed comparably to the late-fusion MAE when LiDAR data was explicitly removed (w/o LiDAR row), emphasizing the substantial contribution of LiDAR information.

#### 4.4. Zero-Shot Generalization

The late-fusion MAE, trained solely on the OctoSense dataset, was evaluated zero-shot on the M3ED dataset (another multi-modal dataset with event cameras). The model demonstrated competitive performance on dense perception tasks and was the second-best on ego-motion tasks compared to existing foundation models. This indicates a degree of cross-dataset generalization, despite differences in sensor configurations between OctoSense and M3ED.

#### 4.5. Masked Sensor Data Reconstruction

The pre-trained MAE exhibited the capability to reconstruct missing modalities effectively (Figure S12), suggesting potential utility in handling sensor failures in robotic systems.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents OctoSense as a foundational contribution to multi-modal robot perception, addressing critical gaps in the field through both hardware and algorithmic innovation.

#### 5.1. Demonstrated Utility and Viability

The work successfully demonstrates the utility and viability of multi-modal self-supervised learning across a diverse set of robotic perception tasks (depth, optical flow, semantic segmentation, and ego-motion) and challenging environmental conditions. The significant performance improvements, particularly in degraded scenarios like nighttime or under sensor failures, highlight the practical relevance of this approach for real-world robotic deployments.

#### 5.2. Open-Source Contributions

- **OctoSense Hardware Platform**: The development of an open-source, richly equipped multi-sensor platform provides a standardized and robust foundation for future research in integrated robotic perception. Its detailed engineering solutions for synchronization and data handling serve as a practical guide for the community.
- **OctoSense Dataset**: The release of a large-scale (59 hours, 8.4 TB), time-synchronized dataset, encompassing a wide variety of environments and sensor degradation scenarios, is a substantial contribution. It addresses the critical scarcity of such data, fostering further research in multi-modal representation learning by providing a rich, diverse, and realistic data source. The integrated semantic search capability further enhances its usability for targeted research.
- **Codebase**: The open-sourcing of the underlying software, including modified sensor drivers and the MAE architecture, promotes reproducibility and accelerates community-driven advancements.

#### 5.3. Architectural Efficiency and Robustness

The proposed "late-fusion" masked autoencoder architecture offers a computationally efficient solution for integrating heterogeneous sensor data. Its design, incorporating modality-specific tokenizers and a token-caching mechanism, achieves high inference speeds on both high-end and edge computing platforms (e.g., NVIDIA 5090 and Orin NX). This efficiency is crucial for real-time operation of autonomous systems. The demonstrated robustness in adverse conditions, such as low light, directly addresses the need for resilient perception identified in the Global Robotics Technology Roadmap.

#### 5.4. Insights into Modality Contributions

The systematic ablation studies, evaluating performance when specific sensor modalities are excluded, provide valuable empirical insights into their individual contributions. The findings confirm the dominant role of LiDAR for ego-motion and dense depth estimation, and the importance of RGB for semantic segmentation. While event cameras and IMU showed marginal impact at the chosen evaluation rate, the research suggests their potential significance for high-speed tracking and low-latency maneuvers.

#### 5.5. Potential Impact and Future Directions

- **Foundation Models for Robotics**: The work lays groundwork for building "foundation" multi-modal robot perception models. Scaling the dataset size through community-wide data pooling efforts is identified as a key avenue to enhance the generalizability of the learned representations further.
- **Enhanced Network Architectures**: Future work includes exploring larger network sizes and longer temporal windows, potentially with recurrent architectures. This could unlock new capabilities such as object tracking, 3D instance detection (with appropriate annotations), and further optimize computational complexity.
- **Understanding Information Redundancy**: The authors propose investigating the unique, synergistic, and redundant information content within multi-modal sensors. This line of inquiry could contribute to developing normative principles for multi-modal robot perception, potentially leading to more efficient and robust sensor fusion strategies.
- **Sensor Failure Handling**: The demonstrated ability of the MAE to reconstruct masked sensor data suggests a direct application in creating robust perception systems that can mitigate the impact of individual sensor failures.
