# FCOS: Fully Convolutional One-Stage Object Detection

> [!summary] Summary
> FCOS (Fully Convolutional One-Stage Object Detection) is an anchor-free, fully convolutional object detector developed by The University of Adelaide that reformulates detection as a per-pixel prediction task. This model achieves state-of-the-art performance, surpassing previous anchor-based and anchor-free methods, while simplifying the overall detection pipeline by removing the need for pre-defined anchor boxes.

## Problem

- Traditional anchor-based object detectors suffer from hyper-parameter sensitivity, requiring extensive tuning of anchor box parameters (sizes, aspect ratios, number per location).
- Anchor-based methods struggle with generalization to objects of diverse sizes and aspect ratios, especially small objects, and generate a large number of negative samples leading to computational complexity and class imbalance.
- Prior attempts at fully convolutional or anchor-free object detection (e.g., YOLOv1, DenseBox, CornerNet) often faced issues like low recall, difficulty handling overlapping boxes, or introduced complicated post-processing.

## Method

- **FCOS** reformulates object detection as a per-pixel prediction problem, where each spatial location directly predicts distances to the four sides of a bounding box and a classification score.
- It leverages a ==Feature Pyramid Network (FPN)== with a novel scale assignment strategy, limiting the regression range for each feature level to address ambiguity and improve recall.
- A =='center-ness' branch== is introduced to predict how close a location is to the center of its target object, with this score multiplied by the classification score during inference to suppress low-quality predictions.

## Results

- FCOS achieved **41.5% AP** on COCO test-dev with a ResNet-101-FPN backbone, outperforming RetinaNet (**39.1% AP**) by **2.4% AP** under the same settings.
- With further improvements and a ResNeXt-64x4d-101-FPN backbone, FCOS reached **44.7% AP**, establishing a new state-of-the-art for one-stage detectors at the time of publication.
- The center-ness branch alone boosted Average Precision (AP) from **33.5%** to **37.1%** on the COCO minival split, demonstrating its effectiveness in filtering low-quality bounding boxes.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Anchor boxes are not strictly necessary for state-of-the-art object detection, as a simpler anchor-free approach can achieve comparable or superior performance.
> - Reforming object detection as a per-pixel prediction task, similar to semantic segmentation, can simplify the detection pipeline and unify fundamental vision tasks.
> - A 'center-ness' branch effectively down-weights low-quality bounding box predictions from locations far from an object's center, significantly improving overall precision during Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS).

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Object detection, a cornerstone task in computer vision, involves simultaneously localizing objects within an image using bounding boxes and classifying them. For a considerable period, the prevailing paradigm for high-performance object detection has been **anchor-based methods**. These include seminal works like Faster R-CNN (a two-stage detector that introduced Region Proposal Networks, RPNs), as well as popular one-stage detectors like SSD, YOLOv2/v3, and RetinaNet. The core idea behind anchor-based detectors is the dense placement of pre-defined bounding box priors (anchors) across the image at multiple scales and aspect ratios. The detector then predicts offsets from these anchors and a confidence score for each class, followed by non-maximum suppression (NMS) to refine results.

Despite their widespread success and high accuracy, the authors highlight several critical **drawbacks of anchor-based detectors**:
1. **Hyper-parameter Sensitivity:** Performance is highly dependent on carefully tuned anchor box parameters (sizes, aspect ratios, number of anchors per location). This tuning is often heuristic and task-specific, leading to significant performance variations (e.g., up to 4% AP in RetinaNet).
2. **Limited Generalization:** Fixed anchor shapes struggle with objects of highly varying sizes or aspect ratios, particularly small objects, and necessitate re-design for new datasets or tasks.
3. **Computational Complexity and Imbalance:** Anchor-based methods generate an enormous number of anchor boxes (e.g., over 180K for a typical image in FPN-based detectors). The vast majority of these are negative samples, exacerbating the positive-negative sample imbalance during training and leading to complex IoU (Intersection-over-Union) computations for matching anchors to ground truth.

Parallel to anchor-based detection, the field of computer vision has witnessed the tremendous success of **fully convolutional networks (FCNs)** in dense prediction tasks like semantic segmentation. FCNs perform per-pixel predictions, unifying image understanding under a single, elegant framework. This raised a fundamental question: could object detection also be solved in a "neat per-pixel prediction fashion" similar to FCNs, thereby simplifying and unifying vision tasks?

Previous attempts at **anchor-free object detection** existed, such as YOLOv1 and DenseBox. YOLOv1 predicted bounding boxes at points near object centers but suffered from low recall. DenseBox directly predicted 4D bounding box vectors per pixel, akin to semantic segmentation. However, these methods were often considered unsuitable for generic object detection due to difficulties in handling highly overlapping bounding boxes and relatively low recall. CornerNet, a more recent anchor-free detector, opted for a keypoint-based approach, detecting pairs of corners and grouping them. While achieving strong results, CornerNet introduced complicated post-processing for corner grouping.

FCOS positions itself as a direct response to these challenges. It aims to develop a **truly fully convolutional one-stage object detector** that performs per-pixel prediction, analogous to semantic segmentation, but crucially, **overcomes the limitations of prior FCN-based and anchor-free methods**. By demonstrating that an anchor-free framework can not only match but *outperform* anchor-based counterparts in generic object detection, FCOS fundamentally challenges the established belief about the necessity of anchor boxes. It seeks to provide a simpler, more flexible, and more efficient alternative that aligns object detection with other dense prediction tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of FCOS is to propose a **fully convolutional one-stage object detector that eliminates the need for pre-defined anchor boxes**, thereby simplifying the object detection pipeline and aligning it with other dense prediction tasks like semantic segmentation.

The motivations driving this objective stem directly from the acknowledged drawbacks of conventional anchor-based detectors:

- **Simplification of Detector Design:** To remove the burden of designing and tuning sensitive hyper-parameters associated with anchor boxes (e.g., sizes, aspect ratios, number of anchors per location, IoU thresholds for positive/negative sample assignment). This aims to make detector development and deployment significantly less complex and heuristic-driven.
- **Reduced Computational Overhead:** To avoid the complicated and computationally intensive processes related to anchor boxes, such as calculating IoU scores between hundreds of thousands of anchor boxes and ground-truth boxes during training. This aims for faster training and potentially reduced memory footprint.
- **Improved Generalization:** To overcome the limitations of fixed anchor shapes that struggle with objects of varying scales and aspect ratios, especially small objects. An anchor-free approach is expected to generalize better to new datasets and tasks without re-designing anchor sets.
- **Addressing Sample Imbalance:** To mitigate the extreme imbalance between positive and negative samples inherent in anchor-based methods (where most densely placed anchor boxes are negative). By treating every location within a ground-truth box as a potential positive sample, FCOS seeks a more balanced and efficient training regime.
- **Unification of Vision Tasks:** To demonstrate that object detection can be formulated as a per-pixel prediction problem, similar to semantic segmentation, thereby unifying fundamental vision tasks under a common fully convolutional framework. This facilitates the transfer of ideas and advancements across different dense prediction problems.
- **Overcoming Prior Anchor-Free Limitations:** To specifically address the known weaknesses of earlier FCN-based/anchor-free detectors (like DenseBox or YOLOv1), particularly the issues of low recall and ambiguity arising from overlapping bounding boxes, which had previously hindered their adoption for generic object detection.

In essence, FCOS aims to prove that a simpler, anchor-free design can achieve, and even surpass, the performance of complex anchor-based systems, encouraging the community to re-evaluate the foundational design principles of modern object detectors.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

FCOS reformulates object detection as a **per-pixel prediction problem**, drawing direct analogy to semantic segmentation. Instead of using pre-defined anchor boxes, each spatial location (x, y) on a feature map directly predicts a 4D vector representing the distances from that location to the four sides of a bounding box (left, top, right, bottom), alongside a classification score for 80 COCO classes.

The methodology can be broken down into several key components:

- **Per-Pixel Bounding Box Prediction:**
 - For each location (x, y) on a feature map, mapped back to the input image, FCOS directly predicts a 4D vector `t = (l, t, r, b)`, where `l, t, r, b` are the distances from the location to the left, top, right, and bottom boundaries of the target bounding box, respectively.
 - A location is considered a positive sample if it falls inside any ground-truth bounding box. Its class label is that of the ground-truth box. If it falls into multiple ground-truth boxes, the one with the *minimal area* is chosen as the regression target to resolve ambiguity.
 - The network's final output consists of an 80D vector for classification probabilities (trained as C binary classifiers) and a 4D vector for bounding box regression. An `exp(x)` function (later `exp(s_i * x)`) is applied to regression outputs to ensure positive distances.

- **Multi-level Prediction with Feature Pyramid Network (FPN):**
 - To address two major concerns with per-pixel prediction (low best-possible recall due to large strides and ambiguity from overlapping boxes), FCOS leverages FPN.
 - Five feature levels are used: P3, P4, P5, P6, P7, with strides 8, 16, 32, 64, and 128 respectively. P3-P5 are generated from backbone features (C3-C5) using top-down connections, while P6 and P7 are derived from P5 and P6 with stride-2 convolutions.
 - **Scale Assignment:** Unlike anchor-based FPNs that assign anchors of different sizes to different levels, FCOS directly limits the *range of regression* for each feature level. For example, P3 handles objects where `max(l,t,r,b)` is between 0 and 64 pixels, P4 handles 64-128 pixels, and so on. This effectively assigns objects of different sizes to specific FPN levels, which significantly reduces the ambiguity from overlapping objects (as most overlaps occur between objects of considerably different sizes, which would be handled by different FPN levels).
 - **Shared Heads and Learnable Scalars:** Classification, regression, and center-ness heads are shared across different FPN levels to reduce parameters and improve performance. A trainable scalar `s_i` is introduced for each FPN level `P_i` in `exp(s_i * x)` for the regression branch, allowing the model to adapt the exponential function's base for different scale ranges.

- **Center-ness Branch:**
 - A novel, single-layer convolutional branch is added in parallel with the classification branch (or regression branch in later refinements).
 - This branch predicts a "center-ness" score for each location, quantifying how close the location is to the center of its target object. The target center-ness is defined as `sqrt(min(l*, r*) / max(l*, r*) * min(t*, b*) / max(t*, b*))`. This score ranges from 0 to 1 (1 at the center, 0 at the boundaries).
 - During inference, the predicted center-ness score is multiplied by the classification score. This effectively down-weights the confidence of bounding boxes predicted by locations far from the object's center, which tend to be lower quality. This mechanism helps the Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS) filter out more low-quality predictions, improving overall precision without introducing new hyper-parameters for NMS.

- **Loss Function:**
 - The total training loss is a sum of three components:
 - **Classification Loss (`L_cls`):** Focal Loss is used to handle the class imbalance and focus training on hard examples.
 - **Regression Loss (`L_reg`):** IoU Loss (later GIoU Loss in improved versions) is used for bounding box regression.
 - **Center-ness Loss:** Binary Cross Entropy (BCE) loss is used for the center-ness prediction.
 - Losses are normalized by the number of positive samples.

- **Training and Inference Details:**
 - Standard backbones like ResNet and ResNeXt with FPN are used.
 - Training utilizes SGD with a standard learning rate schedule, weight decay, and momentum.
 - Input images are resized to a shorter side of 800 pixels (and longer side up to 1333 pixels).
 - Inference is straightforward: forward pass, thresholding classification scores (e.g., > 0.05), converting predicted (l,t,r,b) to bounding box coordinates, and applying NMS with the combined (classification score * center-ness) confidence.

This methodology represents a significant departure from anchor-based approaches, offering a simplified yet powerful framework that leverages the strengths of FCNs and addresses the inherent challenges of per-pixel object detection.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

FCOS presented compelling results that demonstrated its effectiveness and superiority over traditional anchor-based methods.

- **Ablation Study on Multi-level Prediction with FPN:**
 - **Best Possible Recall (BPR):** FCOS showed a high BPR. Even without FPN (using only P4 feature level), FCOS achieved a BPR of 95.55%, significantly higher than RetinaNet's 90.92% (with low-quality matches considered). With FPN, FCOS's BPR improved to 98.40%, very close to the theoretical maximum of anchor-based detectors (99.23%). This disproved the concern about low recall in FCN-based detectors.
 - **Ambiguous Samples:** FPN drastically reduced the percentage of ambiguous samples (locations falling into multiple ground-truth boxes). Without FPN, 23.16% of positive samples were ambiguous; with FPN, this dropped to 7.14%. When considering only ambiguity between objects of *different* categories (which truly matters for correct classification), the ratio decreased from 17.84% to 3.75%. Furthermore, only 1.5% of detected bounding boxes during inference originated from these ambiguous locations, suggesting the problem is largely alleviated.

- **Ablation Study on Center-ness:**
 - The proposed "center-ness" branch proved crucial for performance. It boosted the Average Precision (AP) from 33.5% (without center-ness) to 37.1% (with center-ness) on the COCO `minival` split using ResNet-50-FPN.
 - Visualizations confirmed that center-ness effectively down-weighted low-quality bounding boxes (those far from the object center but with high classification scores), allowing them to be filtered out by NMS and thereby improving overall precision. Using center-ness computed from the *predicted* regression vector was found ineffective, highlighting the necessity of a separate learnable branch.

- **FCOS vs. Anchor-based Detectors (RetinaNet Comparison):**
 - Under exactly the same training and testing settings (including using the same hyper-parameters optimized for RetinaNet), the vanilla FCOS (with minimal differences like Group Normalization) performed favorably against RetinaNet, achieving 36.3% AP versus RetinaNet's 35.9% AP (ResNet-50-FPN on `minival`). This was a significant finding, as it showed an anchor-free detector could match or slightly surpass anchor-based performance without specific hyper-parameter tuning.
 - With additional, almost cost-free improvements (e.g., moving center-ness to regression branch, center sampling, GIoU loss, normalization), FCOS's performance on `minival` was further boosted to 38.6% AP, significantly outperforming RetinaNet's 35.9% AP.

- **Comparison with State-of-the-Art Detectors on COCO `test-dev`:**
 - Using ResNet-101-FPN, FCOS achieved 41.5% AP, outperforming RetinaNet (39.1% AP) by a substantial 2.4% AP with the same backbone. This was highlighted as the *first time* an anchor-free detector, without bells and whistles, significantly outperformed anchor-based counterparts.
 - With more powerful backbones like ResNeXt-64x4d-101-FPN, FCOS reached 43.2% AP.
 - Further incorporating the "improvements" (from the ablation study) and scaling training iterations, FCOS achieved **44.7% AP** with single-model and single-scale testing, setting a new state-of-the-art for one-stage detectors at the time of publication and surpassing previous detectors by a large margin.
 - FCOS also outperformed CornerNet (40.5% AP), a competing anchor-free method, while being significantly simpler due to its direct per-pixel prediction nature (avoiding complex corner grouping).

- **Extension to Region Proposal Networks (RPNs):**
 - FCOS demonstrated its versatility by successfully replacing RPNs in a two-stage detector framework (like Faster R-CNN).
 - FCOS, as an RPN, significantly improved proposal recall. With Group Normalization, FCOS improved AR@100 (Average Recall with 100 proposals) from 44.7% (standard RPN) to 48.0% (FCOS without center-ness) and further to 52.8% (FCOS with center-ness). AR@1k saw absolute improvements of 3.4%. This suggested that anchor-free principles could also benefit two-stage detection.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

FCOS represents a pivotal contribution to the field of object detection, carrying significant implications and potential impact:

- **Challenging the Anchor Paradigm:** The most profound significance of FCOS is its successful demonstration that **anchor boxes are not strictly necessary for achieving state-of-the-art performance in generic object detection**. For years, anchor boxes were considered a fundamental component and a key to success for modern detectors. FCOS effectively debunks this long-held belief, prompting the community to rethink the foundational design principles of object detectors.
- **Simplicity and Flexibility:** FCOS offers a significantly **simpler and more flexible detection framework**. By eliminating anchor boxes, it removes numerous sensitive hyper-parameters that require heuristic tuning, simplifying the design, training, and deployment process. This makes object detection models easier to understand, implement, and adapt to new datasets or tasks without tedious trial-and-error.
- **Unification of Vision Tasks:** By formulating object detection as a per-pixel prediction task, FCOS **unifies it with other dense prediction problems** such as semantic segmentation, depth estimation, and keypoint detection under a common fully convolutional framework. This unification allows for easier transfer of architectural insights, loss functions, and optimization strategies across different computer vision tasks, fostering more cohesive research.
- **Efficiency Gains:** The removal of anchor boxes leads to several efficiency benefits. It eliminates the complex and time-consuming computations associated with IoU matching between anchors and ground-truth boxes during training, potentially resulting in **faster training times and reduced memory footprint**.
- **New Baseline for Instance-Level Tasks:** Given its effectiveness and simplicity, FCOS is positioned as a **strong and simple alternative/new baseline** for future research in object detection and other instance-level recognition tasks, such as instance segmentation and keypoint detection. Its fully convolutional nature means it can be readily extended or adapted to these related problems with minimal modification.
- **Improved Performance:** Beyond simplicity, FCOS achieves **state-of-the-art performance among one-stage detectors**, often outperforming its anchor-based counterparts (like RetinaNet) and other anchor-free methods (like CornerNet) while being less complex. This empirical success provides concrete evidence for the viability and superiority of the anchor-free approach.
- **Open-Source Contribution:** The availability of code (tinyurl.com/FCOSv1) is crucial for its impact, enabling researchers and practitioners to easily reproduce results, build upon the framework, and integrate it into their own applications.

In summary, FCOS represents a significant step towards more elegant, efficient, and versatile object detection systems. Its impact extends beyond just improving benchmarks; it encourages a fundamental re-evaluation of common practices in computer vision and paves the way for a more unified approach to dense prediction tasks.
