# Generalizable Robotic Insertion with World Models

> [!summary] Summary
> InsertionWM is a model-based reinforcement learning system for generalizable robotic insertion. A single TD-MPC2 world model fuses wrist-camera depth images with robot proprioception, planning delta end-effector poses in latent space without CAD models or object IDs at deployment. Trained on 90 geometrically diverse assemblies, it reaches 56% zero-shot success on ten unseen objects versus 7% for the model-free AutoMate baseline, improving as training objects increase.

## Problem

- State-of-the-art assembly systems train specialist policies per object pair, requiring extensive retraining and part-specific tuning that fundamentally limits scalability in high-mixture manufacturing settings.
- Model-free policy-gradient methods scale poorly once a single high-capacity visual encoder is shared across many tasks, suffering sample inefficiency and task interference under sparse reward.
- Prior generalist insertion attempts are blind or depend on privileged CAD point-cloud embeddings and pose estimation at deployment, so genuinely unknown part geometries remain unhandled.

## Method

- Builds on **TD-MPC2**: a ==latent world model== of encoder, dynamics, reward, terminal value and policy prior, trained with ==joint-embedding prediction== plus temporal-difference losses and acting via ==MPPI== latent planning.
- A multi-modal encoder fuses a shallow depth ConvNet with a proprioception MLP through a ==SimNorm==-normalized fusion MLP, producing one latent state from 96x96 depth plus 24-dimensional proprioception.
- ==Multi-task online RL== runs 90 parallel Isaac environment instances, one unique assembly each, with a per-assembly success-rate curriculum over initial states and no object IDs, forcing purely visual disambiguation.

## Results

- The 90-assembly generalist reaches **55.0%** in-domain success versus **15.6%** for AutoMate on 80 assemblies, and **56%** zero-shot on ten held-out objects versus **7%**.
- Specialists average **79.78%** across 100 assemblies against AutoMate's **70.31%**, trained for **2M** steps instead of **50M**, though large occluding sockets regress relative to the baseline.
- Ablations show depth is critical: generalist success falls from **54.5%** to **39.6%** blind, specialists from **79.8%** to **55.2%**; curriculum adds roughly two points, expert demonstrations two more.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Changing the learning algorithm, not merely adding data or compute, unlocked generalist behavior: dense self-supervised prediction signal outperforms sparse-reward policy gradients under shared encoders.
> - Zero-shot success matched in-domain success at 90 training objects, suggesting raw depth can substitute for CAD geometry priors given sufficient geometric diversity.
> - Camera placement is a real design axis: views showing the grasped part better invite occlusion, and occlusion, not control precision, dominates failures.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

Precision insertion means guiding one part, a plug, into a matching socket despite differences in geometry. The deployment problem is that a new object pair often calls for another task-specific policy and substantial retraining or tuning. InsertionWM tests whether one learned controller can use what it sees and feels to handle multiple geometries, including objects it did not train on. The robot receives a wrist camera's depth image and information about its own joints and pose. A shared learned model predicts how actions will change the situation, and the robot uses those predictions to plan its next movement. In simulation, this generalist reaches a 56% average insertion-success rate on 10 held-out objects.

A robot and depth-camera setup shown with results on seen and unseen objects.

### 2. How one model plans across different fits

InsertionWM builds on TD-MPC2, a model-based reinforcement-learning method. A shallow visual encoder processes depth images, and a separate encoder handles robot proprioception. The system then fuses the two representations.

The learned world model predicts future latent states, rewards, and returns. Here, "world model" means a learned predictor used for planning. It is neither a CAD model nor a hand-coded geometric model. At decision time, a planner samples candidate action sequences and uses the model's predictions to choose a six-degree-of-freedom end-effector movement.

During multi-task training, one simulated environment per training assembly gives the model experience across many geometries. At deployment, the model receives no object IDs or CAD models. The authors motivate the design with a hypothesis. Predicting from each experience supplies learning signals beyond the sparse reward for completing an insertion. They suggest this may help a single visual controller learn across tasks.

Conceptual overview. Diverse training assemblies feed a model pathway through encoded latent states and actions, and a generalist then interacts with held-out test objects. Inputs are wrist-mounted raw depth and proprioception. Outputs are delta end-effector commands.

### 3. What the comparisons establish

The study uses 100 two-part assemblies. It trains on up to 90 and holds out the last 10 by asset ID. These held-out objects are unseen during training but come from the same distribution. Generalist success rates are averaged across assemblies, with 100 trials per assembly.

After training on 90 assemblies, the InsertionWM generalist averages 56% insertion success on the 10 held-out objects. The paper's model-free baseline reaches 7%. "Zero-shot" means the model gets no task-specific training or adaptation on the held-out objects before evaluation. Held-out success rises as more training assemblies are included.

A sensor ablation inside InsertionWM tests what the design depends on. The approach stays the same, and only the inputs change. Adding wrist-camera depth improves success over proprioception alone, both for a 90-object generalist and for specialists. This supports the finding that vision matters in these experiments. It does not isolate model-based learning as the sole cause of the gains.

Success rate (%) on in-domain objects. 90-object generalist: proprioception only 39.6%, default depth 54.5%, alternative depth-camera placement 46.4%. Specialists trained on 100 assemblies: 55.2%, 79.8%, and 72.7%, respectively. The remaining panels compare no curriculum, the default curriculum, and demonstrations.

The alternative camera placement scores lower than the default. The authors connect its occlusion-prone view with failures when the plug partly blocks the socket.

### 4. A reusable model, a remaining gap

InsertionWM shows one perception-and-planning model reusing experience across geometries. It transfers to the paper's unseen but in-distribution objects without object CAD or IDs at deployment. The depth ablation shows that seeing the parts is part of how the approach works. All experiments are in simulation. The authors identify visual sim-to-real transfer, depth sensing on small parts, and real-time processing as challenges for real-world deployment.
