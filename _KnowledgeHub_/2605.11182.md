# The Many Faces of On-Policy Distillation: Pitfalls, Mechanisms, and Fixes

> [!summary] Summary
> A comprehensive empirical study elucidates the conditions under which On-Policy Distillation (OPD) and On-Policy Self-Distillation (OPSD) succeed or fail for large language models. The research identifies critical failure mechanisms, including gradient bias and teacher state distortion, and introduces practical solutions that enhance training stability and performance.

## Problem

- Prior research presented conflicting empirical outcomes regarding the effectiveness and stability of On-Policy Distillation (OPD) and On-Policy Self-Distillation (OPSD) for large language models (LLMs).
- A lack of clear understanding existed regarding the specific conditions under which OP(S)D methods improve LLM capabilities versus when they lead to instability or performance degradation.
- The underlying mechanistic explanations for the observed successes and failures of OP(S)D were not well-understood, making robust application challenging.

## Method

- Conducted a comprehensive empirical investigation of OP(S)D across diverse LLM architectures, tasks (math reasoning, alignment, system prompt internalization), and distillation configurations.
- Identified and mechanistically explained specific failure modes, such as the distortion of teacher state by student prefixes and optimization instability from biased TopK reverse-KL gradients.
- Developed and evaluated practical stabilizers and fixes, including a stop-gradient TopK KL objective, RLVR-adapted teachers, and Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)-stabilized students.

## Results

- Replacing the exact TopK reverse-KL with a stop-gradient version successfully removed the biased gradient term, preventing model collapse and achieving stable performance.
- An RLVR-adapted smaller teacher model (Qwen3-1.7B) proved more effective for distillation than a larger, unadapted teacher (Qwen3-8B), leading to stronger supervision signals.
- Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) of the student on teacher-generated traces stabilized its output space and regularized response length, enabling subsequent OPD to effectively improve accuracy.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - OP(S)D's effectiveness is highly dependent on the task structure and the nature of privileged information (PI), performing well for shared latent rules (e.g., alignment) but struggling with instance-specific PI (e.g., math answers).
> - Student-generated prefixes can distort the teacher model's internal state, leading to unreliable supervision and reduced teacher accuracy in generating consistent continuations.
> - Commonly used unnormalized TopK reverse-KL loss functions introduce a problematic bias (+1 term) in their gradients, causing optimization instability and catastrophic model collapse.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated within the field of post-training algorithms for large language models (LLMs), specifically focusing on on-policy distillation (OPD) and on-policy self-distillation (OPSD). These methods are designed to provide dense, token-level supervision to a student model based on trajectories sampled from its own policy, with guidance from one or more teacher models.

Prior work has highlighted the potential of OP(S)D in several areas:
- **Integration of Capabilities:** OPD has been recognized as a mechanism for transferring capabilities from multiple stronger teachers to a student model, contributing to model consolidation.
- **Catastrophic Forgetting Mitigation:** It has shown promise in reducing the loss of previously learned knowledge during continued training.
- **Sample Efficiency:** OP(S)D has been suggested to improve the efficiency of training by leveraging on-policy rollouts.
- **Context and Knowledge Internalization:** OPSD, in particular, has demonstrated success in internalizing context, such as system prompts, and improving reasoning abilities by distilling privileged information (PI) into the model's parameters.

However, the empirical effectiveness of OP(S)D has been inconsistent. Some studies report success in areas like context internalization and reasoning, while others indicate instability and performance degradation. This divergence in reported outcomes suggests a lack of comprehensive understanding regarding the conditions under which these methods succeed or fail. This paper addresses this gap by conducting a systematic empirical investigation into the underlying mechanisms driving the mixed results, aiming to provide a clearer framework for the application and development of OP(S)D. The work relates to existing literature on context distillation and reinforcement learning with textual feedback by examining the role of additional textual information in a distillation context.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to empirically study and elucidate the factors that determine the success and failure of on-policy distillation (OPD) and on-policy self-distillation (OPSD) for large language models. The authors aim to provide a comprehensive understanding of when these methods are effective, when they are not, and the mechanisms that account for these observed differences.

The motivation for this detailed investigation stems from the contradictory findings in prior literature. While OP(S)D offers conceptual advantages—such as leveraging on-policy samples for dense supervision and converting training-time context into persistent model capabilities—its practical application has yielded mixed results. Reports of promising applications in areas like system prompt internalization, reasoning, and alignment contrast with observations of instability and performance degradation in other studies. This discrepancy highlights a critical knowledge gap: without a clear understanding of the operational principles and potential pitfalls, practitioners face challenges in reliably applying and optimizing OP(S)D techniques.

Therefore, this study is driven by the necessity to move beyond anecdotal evidence and systematically identify the conditions, mechanisms, and specific design choices that lead to OP(S)D's varied performance. By pinpointing these factors, the research seeks to inform the development of more robust, stable, and effective post-training strategies for LLMs.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a comprehensive empirical study across various tasks and OP(S)D configurations to identify success and failure mechanisms.

**A. Experimental Settings and Tasks:**
The study investigates OP(S)D across three main categories of tasks:

1. **Math Reasoning:**
 - **OPSD:** Experiments were conducted using Qwen3-1.7B on the OpenThoughts dataset, with evaluation on Math500, AIME24, and AIME25. Different types of privileged information (PI) were tested, including answer-only and full-response. The teacher was either the student's step-0 checkpoint or a Qwen3-1.7B model trained with GRPO.
 - **OPD:** A Qwen3-1.7B student was trained with a Qwen3-8B teacher on OpenThoughts. An unnormalized Top-K reverse-KL objective was used initially to highlight failure modes.
2. **Alignment & System Prompt Internalization:**
 - **Alignment:** OPSD was evaluated on CharacterBench and EmotionBench using Qwen3-4B-Instruct and Qwen3-8B students. PI in these tasks involved question-specific alignment prompts. Performance was compared against GRPO and PPO.
 - **System Prompt Internalization:**
 - **Reasoning Compression:** OPSD was applied to a Qwen3-8B student trained on DAPO-Math-17k, using a fixed system prompt for conciseness.
 - **Safety Alignment:** OPSD was tested with a Qwen3-1.7B model on the Wildguardmix dataset, using a fixed system prompt for safety. These were compared against GRPO.
 - **Persuasion Tasks:** OPSD was also tested on the Persuasion for Good dataset with Qwen-1.7B and Qwen-4B students, comparing against GRPO, using PI including persuadee background, personality, and high-level persuasion strategies.

**B. Distillation Loss Design:**
The study explores various distillation loss formulations, with a particular focus on addressing challenges related to Top-K approximations:

- **Full-vocabulary KL:** Standard reverse-KL was considered for its mode-seeking properties.
- **Sampled-token KL:** A policy-gradient objective (Equation 4) that uses the log-probability gap of sampled tokens as an advantage.
- **Top-K Reverse KL:**
 - **Unnormalized Top-K Reverse KL (Equation 6):** This formulation was identified as problematic due to a non-zero "+1" term in its gradient when truncated (Equation 8, 9), leading to biased updates.
 - **Stop-gradient Top-K KL (Equation 12):** Proposed as a fix, this objective stops gradients through the student's log-probability term, re-aligning it with a policy-gradient view where the weighting term acts as an advantage.
 - **Renormalized Top-K Reverse KL (Equation 15):** Another proposed fix where probabilities are normalized within the Top-K set, ensuring the "+1" term cancels out in the gradient (Equation 16).
- **Top-K Engineering:** Practical challenges with SGLang's API for position-dependent Top-K token lists led to a strategy of using the intersection of teacher and student Top-K sets for backpropagation.

**C. Teacher Construction and Privileged Information (PI) Design:**

- **Teacher Models:** A range of teacher strengths were employed, from fixed student checkpoints (for OPSD) to stronger external models (for OPD, e.g., Qwen3-8B for Qwen3-1.7B student).
- **Privileged Information (PI):** Varied based on task: ground-truth answers (math), full reasoning responses (math), fixed system prompts (reasoning compression, safety), or question-specific alignment prompts (style alignment). The distinction between instance-specific and shared latent rule PI was a core aspect of the analysis.
- **RLVR-adapted Teachers:** A strategy to enhance teacher effectiveness by pre-training a teacher model (Qwen3-1.7B) with GRPO (on DAPO) to improve its task performance and distribution alignment with the student.

**D. Stabilization Techniques:**

- **Stop-gradient Top-K KL:** Implemented to correct the biased gradients of Top-K reverse KL.
- **Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) adapted teachers:** Employed to improve the teacher’s task performance and reduce teacher-student distribution mismatch.
- **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) for Student Stabilization:** Fine-tuning the student on teacher-generated traces *before* distillation to regularize output format and stabilize response-length dynamics, particularly for models prone to degenerate outputs.

The experimental setup utilized Qwen3 models of various sizes (1.7B, 4B, 8B, 14B) and employed standard optimization techniques (Adam optimizer, cosine decay learning rate schedule). Evaluation involved task-specific accuracy metrics (e.g., Pass@1 for math), LLM judges for alignment, and internal metrics such as response length, repetition ratio, teacher-student log-probability gap (∆logprob), and Top-K overlap ratio to diagnose behaviors.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study yielded several findings regarding the conditions, mechanisms, and solutions for the effectiveness of OP(S)D.

**A. Empirical Behavior of OP(S)D:**

- **OPSD Failure in Math Reasoning:** OPSD consistently failed to improve the student model (Qwen3-1.7B) on math reasoning tasks (Math500, AIME24, AIME25). Providing richer PI (full response) sometimes degraded performance compared to answer-only PI, suggesting increased mismatch. Even when the PI-conditioned teacher was strengthened through RL training (GRPO), OPSD remained ineffective.
- **OPD Instability and Collapse in Math Reasoning:** OPD, when implemented with an unnormalized Top-K reverse KL objective, initially showed improvement but subsequently collapsed. This collapse was characterized by an explosion in response length, increased repetition of tokens (e.g., "maybe"), and a significant drop in evaluation accuracy.
- **OPSD Success in Alignment and System Prompt Internalization:** In contrast to math reasoning, OPSD proved effective for tasks like character and emotion alignment (CharacterBench, EmotionBench) and system prompt internalization (reasoning compression, safety alignment). OPSD improved performance and converged faster than RL baselines (GRPO, PPO) for alignment tasks. For reasoning compression, OPSD reduced response length without sacrificing accuracy, demonstrating sample efficiency. For safety alignment, OPSD showed rapid early improvement but its final performance was constrained by the teacher, while GRPO continued to make gains.
- **OPSD Failure in Persuasion Tasks:** OPSD demonstrated rapid degradation shortly after training began, leading to a quick collapse in evaluation scores and driving the truncation ratio to nearly 1.0, indicating excessively long and ineffective generations.

**B. Identified Failure Mechanisms:**

1. **Distribution Mismatch due to Student-Generated Prefixes:** Student-generated prefixes can steer the teacher into states where its token-level supervision becomes locally incompatible with the student’s trajectory. A Qwen3-14B teacher's accuracy on GPQA-Diamond dropped from 62.1% to 46.0% when continuing from Qwen3-1.7B student prefixes, indicating that student errors distort the teacher's effective reasoning. This mismatch often manifests as the teacher assigning high probabilities to revision tokens (e.g., "wait," "but"), which attempt to redirect the student's path rather than refining its current reasoning.
2. **Optimization Instability from Biased Top-K Reverse-KL Gradients:** The standard full-vocabulary reverse KL loss has a "+1" constant term in its gradient that cancels out across the entire vocabulary. However, in Top-K truncated versions (Equation 6), this term no longer cancels (Equation 8, 9), introducing a bias. This bias suppresses teacher-preferred tokens with smaller teacher-student margins, potentially pushing the student towards unstable low-probability continuations and leading to model collapse (as observed in math reasoning OPD).
3. **OPSD's PI-Free Policy Aggregation:** OPSD learns a single PI-free consensus policy by effectively taking a normalized geometric mean of PI-conditioned teacher distributions (Equation 11). This mechanism is effective when PI represents a shared latent rule (e.g., a system prompt or alignment preference), as the student can compress this consistent behavior. However, it is insufficient when PI is instance-specific (e.g., ground-truth math answers), where different instances require distinct PI, leading to incompatible teacher behaviors. In such cases, the aggregated policy becomes substantially weaker than the PI-conditioned teacher, and PI was found to *increase* teacher-student mismatch (higher KL loss).

**C. Effectiveness of Proposed Fixes:**

1. **Stop-Gradient Top-K Objectives:** Both the proposed stop-gradient Top-K reverse KL (Equation 12) and the renormalized Top-K reverse KL (Equation 15) successfully mitigated the collapse observed with the unnormalized version, achieving stable training performance. The sampled-token KL objective in a policy-gradient form (Equation 4) also demonstrated stability, further supporting the importance of correcting the biased reverse-KL gradient.
2. **RLVR-Adapted Teachers:** Adapting the teacher model (Qwen3-1.7B) using GRPO improved its task performance and, crucially, made it a more effective teacher for OPD compared to a stronger base model (Qwen3-8B). This suggests that alignment between the teacher and student distributions, rather than just raw teacher accuracy, significantly contributes to OPD effectiveness.
3. **SFT-Stabilized Students:** Supervised Fine-Tuning the student on teacher-generated traces *before* OPD proved effective. This SFT warm-up regularized the student's output space, preventing degenerate outputs (e.g., garbled text, non-English Unicode) and stabilizing response length dynamics. This stabilization enabled subsequent OPD to improve accuracy, indicating that SFT helps keep on-policy samples within well-formed regions where teacher feedback remains effective.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research provides a detailed empirical and mechanistic understanding of on-policy distillation (OPD) and on-policy self-distillation (OPSD), offering both clarity on their operational principles and practical guidance for their application.

**A. Enhanced Understanding of OP(S)D:**
The study resolves ambiguities regarding the mixed effectiveness of OP(S)D reported in previous work. By meticulously identifying distinct failure mechanisms—prefix-induced teacher distortion, biased Top-K reverse-KL gradients, and the limitation of OPSD in aggregating instance-specific privileged information—the paper offers a theoretical foundation for understanding why these methods succeed in some contexts (e.g., style alignment, system prompt internalization) but fail in others (e.g., complex mathematical reasoning with instance-specific answers). This improved understanding is crucial for moving beyond trial-and-error approaches in LLM post-training.

**B. Practical Guidelines for Robust Distillation:**
The research translates its mechanistic findings into concrete recommendations for practitioners:
- **Loss Function Design:** The identification and resolution of the biased Top-K reverse-KL gradients, through stop-gradient or renormalized variants, provide stable and effective loss objectives for distillation. This directly addresses a critical source of instability.
- **Teacher Selection and Adaptation:** The finding that RLVR-adapted teachers, even if not absolutely stronger, can be more effective due to better distribution alignment with the student, guides the strategic preparation of teacher models. This suggests that simply using the largest available teacher may not always be optimal.
- **Student Initialization and Stabilization:** The demonstration that supervised fine-tuning (SFT) can stabilize the student's output space, preventing degenerate generations and facilitating effective distillation, highlights the importance of student pre-conditioning.

**C. Potential Impact on LLM Training:**
This work contributes to the development of more robust, efficient, and reliable post-training methods for LLMs. By providing a clearer roadmap for when and how to apply OP(S)D, the findings can:
- **Improve Training Stability:** The proposed fixes for loss function instability can lead to more predictable and less prone-to-collapse distillation processes.
- **Optimize Resource Allocation:** Understanding when OPSD is effective can help researchers and developers focus its application on tasks where it provides a genuine advantage (e.g., prompt internalization) and avoid scenarios where it is likely to underperform.
- **Inform Future Research:** The identified limitations, particularly concerning instance-specific privileged information, suggest avenues for future research into more sophisticated distillation architectures or PI handling mechanisms. The authors specifically propose future work exploring iterative self-improvement pipelines that combine SFT for stable initialization, RL for task-specific improvements, and OPD for distilling improved on-policy behavior.

In conclusion, this research significantly advances the understanding and practical application of on-policy distillation techniques, offering both diagnostic tools for identifying problems and validated solutions for enhancing training stability and effectiveness in diverse LLM tasks.
