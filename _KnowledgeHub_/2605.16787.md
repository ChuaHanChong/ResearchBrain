# The Unlearnability Phenomenon in RLVR for Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> This research characterizes an "unlearnability phenomenon" in Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Reward (RLVR) for Large Language Models (LLMs), identifying that a notable subset of challenging examples consistently fails to improve even when provided with correct solution rollouts. The study demonstrates this unlearnability is primarily due to fundamental representational flaws within the model, evidenced by unlearnable examples acting as "gradient outliers" and exhibiting poor reasoning quality, rather than issues with optimization or reward scarcity.

## Problem

- Despite RLVR's effectiveness in enhancing LLM reasoning, the granular learning dynamics, particularly for difficult examples, remain underexplored.
- There is an implicit assumption that the mere presence of positive reward is sufficient for an LLM to learn a given example.
- A subset of challenging examples remains unlearned in RLVR, even with positive feedback, pointing to limitations beyond reward assignment or optimization.

## Method

- The study defined and systematically identified "unlearnable" examples as those with initial low success (<10%) that fail to improve (final pass@1 < 0.1) despite receiving correct rollouts during ==GRPO== training.
- It conducted controlled experiments to test common optimization-side hypotheses, including positive rollout scarcity, gradient regularization (e.g., PPO clipping, KL penalties), and gradient interference.
- The research utilized gradient analysis, qualitative assessment of reasoning quality, and data augmentation techniques to investigate underlying representational issues, comparing results across different LLM sizes and training stages (e.g., mid-training).

## Results

- Interventions aimed at increasing positive rollout exposure (e.g., oversampling, SFT on correct responses, larger rollout groups) did not improve the learnability of unlearnable examples.
- Ablation studies confirmed that PPO clipping, KL penalties, and gradient interference were not the primary causes, as their removal or analysis showed no disproportionate negative impact on unlearnable examples.
- Unlearnable examples consistently showed significantly lower cosine similarity with gradients from other examples and demonstrated poorer reasoning quality, indicating a lack of effective representation alignment, which was notably improved by strong mid-training stages in foundational models.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A significant portion (14.2% to 30.2%) of initially difficult examples remains consistently unlearnable across diverse models and mathematical reasoning datasets.
> - Common optimization-side factors like insufficient positive rollouts, gradient regularization, or destructive gradient interference do not account for the unlearnability phenomenon.
> - Unlearnability is strongly linked to fundamental representational flaws within the LLM, as unlearnable examples behave as "gradient outliers" and exhibit ungeneralizable reasoning patterns.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Reward (RLVR) has become a prominent technique for enhancing the complex reasoning abilities of Large Language Models (LLMs). This includes applications in mathematical problem-solving, code generation, and agentic tasks, with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) serving as a foundational algorithm. Existing research in this domain largely focuses on whether LLMs acquire new skills through RL fine-tuning, how they compose atomic skills learned during supervised fine-tuning (SFT), and their generalization capabilities post-RL. Some studies have investigated the learning dynamics of extremely difficult examples, demonstrating that models can learn them if fine-grained reward assignment is feasible.

Alongside these investigations, various training techniques have been proposed to improve RLVR, addressing aspects such as training efficiency, exploration, and credit assignment. These include approaches like clipping higher gradients, removing KL penalties to encourage exploration (DAPO), utilizing entropy-based loss adjustments, and refining credit assignment granularity. Data scheduling methods, such as dynamic sampling and curriculum learning, have also been explored to enhance training efficiency.

This paper positions itself by noting that while the effectiveness of RLVR is established, the underlying learning dynamics remain underexplored. Specifically, it challenges the implicit assumption that the mere presence of positive reward is sufficient for an LLM to learn a given example. It seeks to explore fundamental limitations in RL post-training that extend beyond reward assignment mechanisms, particularly concerning examples that models initially struggle with. The study aims to systematically characterize a phenomenon where certain examples remain unlearnable despite receiving positive feedback, suggesting a deeper representational issue rather than merely an optimization or reward sparsity problem.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to identify and characterize a "unlearnability phenomenon" in LLM training using Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Reward (RLVR). The authors observe that a significant subset of challenging examples, which the model initially fails to solve, remains unlearned even when correct rollouts—sequences of actions leading to a correct answer—are provided during training. This counterintuitive observation motivates a central question: why do certain examples remain unlearnable despite receiving positive reward signals?

The specific motivations for this investigation are multifaceted:
- **Underexplored Learning Dynamics:** Despite RLVR's proven effectiveness in improving LLM reasoning, the granular learning dynamics of how models acquire and apply reasoning skills are not fully understood.
- **Challenging Assumptions about Reward Sufficiency:** The work aims to critically examine the assumption that the presence of positive reward alone guarantees learning. The observed "unlearnability phenomenon" directly contradicts this, suggesting that other factors might impede learning.
- **Investigating Root Causes:** The research is motivated by a desire to determine whether this unlearnability stems from:
 - **Optimization-side issues:** Such as insufficient positive rollouts, gradient regularization effects (e.g., clipping, KL penalties), or gradient interference between correct and incorrect rollouts.
 - **Fundamental model limitations:** Suggesting deeper problems with how the model represents or processes certain types of examples.
- **Systematic Characterization:** The authors seek to provide the first systematic characterization of unlearnable data in RLVR training, offering insights into its prevalence and underlying mechanisms.
- **Informing Training Pipelines:** By understanding the limitations of current RL approaches, the study aims to inform the development of more effective training pipelines for LLMs, particularly for complex reasoning tasks.

Ultimately, the goal is to reveal fundamental limitations in current RL approaches for reasoning tasks, moving beyond surface-level explanations to uncover deeper representational challenges.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed a multi-stage methodology involving controlled experiments, gradient analysis, and qualitative assessments to investigate the unlearnability phenomenon.

#### 3.1. Training Algorithm and Definition of Unlearnability

The study utilized the Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) algorithm (Shao et al., 2024), a standard RLVR method, with dynamic sampling (Yu et al., 2025) to enhance training efficiency. The GRPO objective is based on a PPO-like loss, maximizing a clipped advantage term and penalizing deviation from a reference policy using a KL term. Advantages are calculated as standardized binary rewards (1 for correct, 0 for incorrect) from sampled rollouts.
- **Definition of Unlearnable Examples**: Examples with an initial success rate below 10% were classified as "hard." Among these, "unlearnable" examples were defined as those with a final pass@1 score below 0.1 (estimated with N=32 responses) after training convergence, despite observing at least one correct rollout during the training process. Examples that never generated a positive reward were excluded.

#### 3.2. Experiment Setups

Experiments were conducted using three LLMs: Qwen2.5-0.5B, Qwen2.5-3B, and Llama3.2-3B-Instruct.
- **Datasets**: Qwen2.5-0.5B was trained on MATH (Hendrycks et al., 2021) difficulty levels 1-4 (MATH Easy). Llama3.2-3B-Instruct was trained on MATH levels 3-5 (MATH Hard). Qwen2.5-3B used DeepScaleR (Luo et al., 2025), a large-scale math dataset.
- **Validation**: MATH 500 was used for validation with Qwen2.5-0.5B and Llama3.2-3B-Instruct. DeepScaleR's validation set was used for Qwen2.5-3B.
- **Robustness**: To mitigate noise from arbitrary thresholding, three independent GRPO training runs were performed for each setting, and the intersection of identified example groups (easy, learnable, unlearnable) was used for analysis.
- **Training Details**: Specific hyperparameters (sampling batch size, rollout group size k=8, gradient update batch size, learning rates, inference temperature, max length, Math-Verify for answer verification) are provided in Appendix B.1.

#### 3.3. Examining Common Explanations for Unlearnability

The study investigated three hypotheses related to optimization-side issues:
- **Positive Rollout Scarcity (Hypothesis 1)**:
 - **Oversampling with Replay**: Implemented a modified GRPO (Algorithm 1) to ensure a fixed number of positive rollouts ($k_{pos}=1$) and negative rollouts ($k-k_{pos}=7$) per example in each batch. Buffered positive rollouts were replayed if current sampling was insufficient.
 - **SFT on Filtered Correct Responses**: Fine-tuned Qwen2.5-0.5B on a supervised dataset of 800 correct responses (200 unlearnable, 200 learnable, 400 easy) generated by a larger model (Qwen2.5-7B).
 - **Larger Rollout Group**: Trained on unlearnable examples alone with an increased rollout group size ($k=64$).
- **Gradient Regularization (Hypothesis 2)**:
 - **Analysis of Reference Probability**: Measured the log-likelihood of correct rollouts under the initial policy for different example groups.
 - **Clipping Rate Analysis**: Tracked the realized clipping ratio during training for each group.
 - **Ablation Studies**: Trained models with "clip-higher" (Yu et al., 2025) and with the KL penalty term removed from the GRPO loss.
- **Gradient Interference (Hypothesis 3 - Appendix A.3)**:
 - **Within-prompt and Cross-prompt Analysis**: Measured cosine similarity between gradients from correct and incorrect rollouts, both within the same example and across the entire training batch, at different training stages. Gradients were computed using a fixed, randomly initialized LoRA adapter for efficiency.

#### 3.4. Investigating Representation Issues

- **Cross-Example Gradient Analysis**:
 - **Method**: Sampled 100 examples per group (easy, learnable, unlearnable) and 1000 rollouts per example from the initial policy. Correct rollouts were filtered, and GRPO loss was computed. Example-level gradients (with respect to LoRA parameters) were derived by averaging token-level gradients, then averaged across responses. Cosine similarity between pairs of example gradients was then calculated.
- **Reasoning Quality Analysis**:
 - **Method**: Randomly sampled 100 examples from each group, collected their correct rollouts. GPT-5-mini (Singh et al., 2025) was used to assign a quality score (0-5) to the reasoning traces. This was performed at initialization and at different training steps.
- **Data Augmentation**:
 - **Generation**: For unlearnable examples, GPT-5 was prompted to generate 5 "similar problems" ($D_{sim_u}$) solvable with the same strategy and 5 "subproblems" ($D_{sub_u}$) whose solutions would lead to the original answer. Generated answers were cross-validated by Gemini-2.5-pro.
 - **Training Settings**: Models were fine-tuned with different augmented datasets: $D_u + D_{sim_u}$, $D_u + D_{sub_u}$, and $D_u + D_{sim_u} + D_{sub_u}$.
 - **Analysis**: Training rewards, evaluation pass@k, and gradient similarity between original and augmented examples were assessed.
- **Mid-training Impact**:
 - **Models Compared**: Llama-3.2-3B-Base was compared against OctoThinker-3B-Hybrid-Base and OctoThinker-3B-Long-Base (Wang et al., 2025), which are Llama-3B models that underwent mid-training on 20B tokens with different data mixtures.
 - **Analysis**: Gradient similarity between difficult examples and the full set of 500 examples was computed for each model.
- **Curriculum Learning (Appendix A.5)**:
 - **Setup**: In stage one, unlearnable examples were excluded. In stage two, the unlearnable examples were combined with a subset of the first-stage training data.
 - **Analysis**: Training reward dynamics and reasoning quality distributions were examined after each stage.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study's investigations revealed several key findings regarding the unlearnability phenomenon in RLVR for LLMs:

#### 4.1. Prevalence of Unlearnable Examples

A substantial portion of initially difficult examples remains unlearnable across various model sizes (0.5B, 3B) and mathematical reasoning datasets (MATH Easy, MATH Hard, DeepScaleR). Specifically, after excluding examples with no positive rewards, 14.2% to 30.2% of hard examples consistently exhibited no improvement in reward over time, even with correct rollouts available. This establishes the unlearnability phenomenon as a widespread issue.

#### 4.2. Rejection of Optimization-Side Hypotheses

- **Positive Rollout Scarcity**: Experimental interventions, including oversampling positive rollouts with replay, supervised fine-tuning on distilled correct responses, and training on unlearnable examples with a significantly larger rollout group (k=64), did not resolve the unlearnability. The performance gap between learnable and unlearnable examples persisted, indicating that the mere quantity or direct supervision of correct rollouts is not sufficient for learning.
- **Gradient Regularization**: No evidence was found that PPO clipping or KL penalties disproportionately suppressed learning for unlearnable examples. The reference log-likelihood of correct rollouts was comparable across easy, learnable, and unlearnable groups, and the clipping ratios during training were similar. Ablating these regularization mechanisms did not improve the learnability of the unlearnable group.
- **Gradient Interference**: Analysis of gradient similarity between correct and incorrect rollouts, both within individual prompts and across batches, showed no significant evidence of destructive interference for unlearnable examples. For learnable examples, gradients of incorrect rollouts tended to oppose correct ones as training progressed, aiding learning. However, for unlearnable examples, gradients of correct and incorrect rollouts remained aligned, indicating a lack of effective signal differentiation rather than cancellation.

#### 4.3. Evidence for Representation Issues

The investigations strongly suggest that unlearnability stems from fundamental representation flaws within the language model.
- **Gradient Outliers**: Unlearnable examples exhibited significantly lower cosine similarity with the gradients of other training examples (both easy and learnable examples). They also showed lower intra-group gradient similarity compared to easy and learnable examples. This indicates that unlearnable examples are distinct outliers in the optimization space, and learning signals from the broader data do not effectively transfer to them. This pattern of low gradient similarity persisted midway through training.
- **Ungeneralizable Reasoning Patterns**: Qualitative analysis of correct rollouts for unlearnable examples revealed lower reasoning quality, characterized by incoherent or erroneous intermediate steps, even when the final answer was correct. This suggests that the model might be exploiting ungeneralizable shortcut solutions or heuristics rather than sound reasoning. This reasoning quality gap widened during training, with learnable examples improving significantly while unlearnable ones did not.
- **Data Augmentation Ineffectiveness**: Generating semantically similar problems or subproblems for unlearnable examples through GPT-5 did not substantially improve their learnability or evaluation performance. Crucially, the gradient similarity between original unlearnable examples and their augmented counterparts remained low. This finding indicates that semantic similarity does not necessarily translate to similarity in the model's optimization space, posing challenges for data synthesis efforts in post-training.
- **Mid-training as a Mitigator**: In contrast to RL-stage interventions, mid-training (e.g., OctoThinker models compared to Llama-Base) significantly improved the gradient similarity of difficult examples with the broader training distribution. This suggests that pre-RL reshaping of model representations can effectively align them for subsequent learning, implying a critical role for the mid-training stage in establishing suitable foundational representations.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The findings of this research carry significant implications for the understanding and development of training methodologies for Large Language Models (LLMs), particularly in complex reasoning domains.

#### 5.1. Implications for Understanding RLVR

This study fundamentally challenges the assumption that the mere presence of positive reward is sufficient for effective learning in Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Reward (RLVR). By identifying and characterizing the "unlearnability phenomenon," the research reveals that for a non-trivial subset of difficult examples, LLMs may possess inherent representational flaws that prevent them from learning, even when correct answers are demonstrated. This moves the discourse beyond issues of reward sparsity or exploration, suggesting that positive outcome-based rewards alone cannot always rectify deeply rooted model deficiencies. The finding that unlearnable examples are consistent "gradient outliers" underscores that the model's internal representations are misaligned with the broader learning landscape. This highlights the need for future research to explore and identify rollout features beyond simple outcome correctness that genuinely contribute to smooth and generalizable LLM optimization.

#### 5.2. Implications for Reasoning LLM Training Pipelines

The research provides critical insights into the stages of LLM development. It suggests that while RL is powerful, it may be limited in its ability to "repair" fundamental representational issues for certain challenging examples. The demonstration that data augmentation at the RL stage fails to improve gradient similarity for unlearnable examples further supports this, indicating a non-triviality in synthesizing data that effectively aligns with the model's optimization space. Conversely, the observed effectiveness of "mid-training" in improving gradient similarity for difficult examples emphasizes its crucial role in preparing LLMs for subsequent RL fine-tuning. Mid-training appears to reshape the model's representation space in a way that makes hard examples more amenable to learning, suggesting it is a more effective stage for addressing foundational representational challenges than the RL fine-tuning stage itself. This highlights the necessity of a holistic view of the LLM training pipeline, prioritizing the development of strong, well-aligned base representations before applying RL.

#### 5.3. Future Research Directions

The study opens several avenues for future research. It prompts investigation into:
- **Effective Mid-training Data and Algorithms**: What specific data mixtures and algorithms are most effective during the mid-training stage to cultivate robust and generalizable representations that preclude unlearnability?
- **Identifying Effective Rollout Features**: Beyond the correctness of the final answer, what characteristics of a rollout (e.g., quality of intermediate reasoning steps, diversity of thought processes) truly contribute to effective and generalizable learning in RLVR? Developing reward signals that consider these nuanced features could be impactful.
- **Diagnostic Tools**: Developing tools and metrics to identify unlearnable examples and diagnose their specific representational flaws early in the training process could enable more targeted interventions.

#### 5.4. Limitations

The authors acknowledge several limitations:
- **Scale of Experiments**: The experiments were conducted on small to mid-scale base and instruction models within the mathematical reasoning domain. The generalizability of these findings to larger, more diverse models and tasks remains to be fully explored.
- **Definition of Unlearnability**: The working definition of "unlearnable" relies on a thresholded notion of convergence (pass@1 < 0.1), which is an operational tool rather than a sharp categorical distinction. Some examples may sit near this boundary, and a different threshold might slightly alter the identified sets.

In conclusion, this study offers a detailed characterization of the "unlearnability phenomenon," attributing it to fundamental flaws in model representations rather than mere optimization challenges. It underscores the critical importance of upstream training stages, particularly mid-training, in establishing a strong representational foundation for LLMs to effectively learn from RLVR, thereby offering crucial insights for the future development of robust reasoning capabilities in language models.
