# Teach and Grow: An Agent-Centered Architecture for General Robot Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Teach-and-Grow Learning (TGL) is an agent-centered robotics architecture where a multimodal agent turns a handful of demonstrations into reusable, closed-loop Skill Blocks stored in a persistent Skill Library plus structured Experience Memory, composing and repairing them via physical feedback without retraining the base policy; it reaches state-of-the-art LIBERO success while foundation weights stay frozen.

## Problem

- End-to-end VLA and world-action models bind reliability to validated physical coverage, so an unfamiliar object, sensor, embodiment, or contact triggers a costly global retraining and regression-testing cycle, the retraining tax.
- Robotics lacks a pre-existing data substrate like text or code, so covering new factors (objects, cameras, grippers, embodiments) requires expensive robot or simulator operation that scales combinatorially with interacting factors.
- Zero-shot agentic exploration (e.g. ASPIRE-style systems) can assemble and repair behavior without policy retraining but needs many sequential trial-and-error decisions and risks unsafe physical exploration on real robots.

## Method

- **Skill Blocks**: a formal tuple of goal, applicability scope, reusable strategy, grounding function, compatible executors, success verifier, and bounded recovery, synthesized by ==cross-demonstration abstraction== over segmented demonstrations rather than trajectory replay.
- **Structured Experience Memory**: textual records of task, context, blocks used, outcome, diagnosis, and repair that guide later decisions without themselves being able to act, kept separate from the executable Skill Library.
- ==Dynamic agentic composition==: at each semantic decision step the agent retrieves/composes blocks into a working plan, runs one block, observes physical outcome evidence, and continues, replans, requests targeted teaching, or stops.

## Results

- On the standard LIBERO benchmark TGL attains state-of-the-art task success rates while foundation model parameters remain entirely fixed throughout learning.
- Fixed-executor library pilot: with a six-block library the system solves **0/6** evaluation states; after two learned blocks are added the same executor solves **4/6**, with no change to weights, runtime, or budget.
- Task-specific learning cycle: two blocks synthesized from three teacher trajectories solve **3/3** evaluation states, survive save-and-reload at **3/3**, and correctly halt at the first semantic effect outside their learned scope.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Separating task acquisition (updating an explicit Skill Library and Experience Memory) from policy parameter optimization lets local competence gaps be patched without a global retraining cycle.
> - Treating demonstrations as evidence for reusable subgoal structure, not as action targets to imitate, lets the same taught strategy be re-grounded across different scenes, objects, and executor backends.
> - Framing effective reusable experience as a scaling variable (not raw episode count) predicts diminishing future-task error and teaching demand only when experience remains retrievable, groundable, and composable.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of general-purpose robotics has increasingly focused on end-to-end vision-language-action (VLA) and world-action models. These models aim to map observations and language directly to actions, potentially consolidating numerous task-specific systems into a single, scalable policy. Research efforts such as RT-1, RT-2, Octo, and OpenVLA have demonstrated the capabilities of this approach, often relying on large-scale datasets and model architectures to achieve broader task generalization. The prevailing hypothesis in this area suggests that further enlarging models and datasets will eventually lead to general robot intelligence.

However, the authors identify a challenge unique to robotics: the lack of a pre-existing, web-scale data substrate comparable to what enabled the scaling of language models. Generating embodied interaction data—which includes synchronized observations, actions, contact, and physical outcomes—often requires operating physical robots or simulators, a process that is resource-intensive and time-consuming. When a robot encounters an unfamiliar object, sensor, embodiment, or contact scenario not covered by its training data, existing end-to-end policies may fail. Correcting such failures typically necessitates new data collection, a global policy update, and subsequent regression testing across all previously supported behaviors. This recurring cycle is termed the "retraining tax." This global update mechanism is deemed a practical bottleneck as robot deployment expands across diverse tasks, sensors, and embodiments, as a local deficiency often triggers a system-wide retraining process.

The work presented in this paper, Teach-and-Grow Learning (TGL), proposes an architectural alternative to this dominant end-to-end scaling paradigm. Instead of relying solely on monolithic policy updates, TGL aims to enable a robot to acquire and validate local competence incrementally, without requiring global retraining for every new encountered condition. It draws inspiration from modern coding agents that observe a workspace, plan, execute tools, interpret results, and revise strategies. TGL positions an AI agent as the central organizing intelligence, with pre-trained models, planners, and controllers serving as capabilities under the agent's direction. This approach addresses the "retraining tax" by allowing for explicit, local updates to reusable skills and memory, thereby distinguishing itself from methods that primarily focus on parameter optimization of a singular policy. It integrates concepts from few-shot adaptation, reusable skills, agentic tool use, and lifelong learning, but in a specific cyclical framework designed for continuous growth.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an agent-centered architecture that enables general robot learning through continuous, non-destructive growth of capabilities, rather than through repeated global policy retraining. This architecture, Teach-and-Grow Learning (TGL), aims to facilitate the acquisition of new manipulation tasks from sparse teaching, operate through dynamic capability composition and physical feedback, and expand its competence without task-specific policy updates.

The motivation for TGL stems from several challenges inherent in the current trajectory of general-purpose robotics, particularly concerning end-to-end VLA and world-action models:

1. **The Retraining Tax:** A significant motivation is to overcome the "retraining tax." When end-to-end models encounter scenarios outside their validated physical coverage (e.g., an unfamiliar object, sensor, or contact), failure correction often requires collecting new robot data, updating the entire policy, and performing extensive regression testing. This global update process for a local failure is costly and time-consuming, hindering the deployment and continuous improvement of robots in varied environments. The authors argue that robot learning lacks the abundant, pre-existing data substrate of text, necessitating costly physical interaction for data generation.
2. **Mismatch between Local Gaps and Global Updates:** The current paradigm often forces a global update to address a localized gap in competence. TGL seeks an architecture that can add and validate local competence without rewriting or destabilizing unrelated existing behaviors. This is motivated by the need for more efficient and targeted learning.
3. **Limitations of Semantic Understanding without Grounded Execution:** While web-scale semantics can help identify objects or interpret instructions, they often do not provide the metric geometry, collision checking, contact handling, or continuous control information essential for reliable physical execution. The motivation is to bridge this gap by integrating robot-native tools and short feedback cycles directly into the learning and execution process.
4. **Efficiency of Learning and Safety:** Purely trial-and-error exploration for learning on physical robots can be inefficient, unsafe, and require many sequential decisions. TGL is motivated to use sparse, successful demonstrations to seed task structure, providing a productive starting point for autonomous learning and reserving exploration for addressing remaining knowledge gaps. This approach aims to accelerate learning and enhance safety compared to unrestricted exploration.
5. **Lifelong Learning and Non-Destructive Growth:** The motivation includes enabling robots to continuously learn and grow throughout their operational life. The objective is to ensure that experience from one task can make subsequent tasks easier, without overwriting previously acquired knowledge. This involves storing validated behaviors and experience in persistent, explicit forms, allowing for local additions of new competence while preserving earlier skills.
6. **Inspiration from Agentic Systems:** The success of modern coding agents that inspect workspaces, plan, use tools, and revise strategies motivated the authors to explore whether a similar AI agent could provide organizing intelligence for a robot, observing the world, deciding on necessary capabilities, acting, inspecting consequences, and adapting.

In essence, TGL aims to shift from a paradigm where new knowledge is absorbed into model weights through global retraining to one where an AI agent orchestrates explicit, reusable capabilities and knowledge that grow incrementally and persistently.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Teach-and-Grow Learning (TGL) is an agent-centered architecture designed for general robot learning, emphasizing the acquisition of new tasks and capabilities without task-specific policy retraining. Its methodology centers on the creation and dynamic composition of "Skill Blocks," informed by sparse teaching and physical feedback, and supported by structured memory.

**A. Learning with Fixed Model Weights:**
A core principle of TGL is that it learns new tasks by expanding explicit skills and memory, while its underlying pre-trained models (e.g., vision, language, foundational policies) remain fixed. When a new task $T_n$ is presented with a small set of demonstrations $D_n$, the robot updates its Skill Library $B_n$ and Experience Memory $M_n$ based on $D_n$ and the interaction history $\Delta H_n$. The foundational parameters $\theta$ are not altered (i.e., $\theta_{n+1} = \theta_n = \theta$). Learning occurs by modifying what the robot can retrieve and execute, rather than by updating a policy's weights. This contrasts with traditional behavior cloning or reinforcement learning, which focus on changing a policy or value function.

**B. Skill Blocks:**
Skill Blocks are the fundamental units of reusable behavior in TGL. Each block $b_i$ is a closed-loop behavior for a meaningful subgoal, represented as a structured tuple: $\langle g_i, S_i, \rho_i, \gamma_i, \Pi_i, v_i, R_i \rangle$.
- $g_i$: The goal, describing the intended change (e.g., "acquire the requested container").
- $S_i$: The scope, defining when the block applies.
- $\rho_i$: The reusable strategy, which is abstracted from demonstrations.
- $\gamma_i: O \rightarrow Z_i$: A grounding function that maps current observations $O$ to scene-specific variables $Z_i$ (e.g., object poses, grasp points).
- $\Pi_i$: A list of compatible executors (e.g., learned policies, geometric planners, controllers).
- $v_i$: An outcome test, which maps pre/post-action observations and executor evidence to a "pass," "fail," or "inconclusive" result.
- $R_i$: A list of bounded recovery choices if execution fails.
Skill Blocks emphasize compositionality; a block's internal implementation (e.g., which specific executor is used) is decoupled from its semantic effect, allowing for flexible realization and replacement of backends.

**C. Structured Experience Memory:**
Experience Memory stores lessons learned from using Skill Blocks. Each record $\mu_j$ is a structured textual item: $\langle \text{task, context, blocks, outcome, diagnosis, repair, evidence} \rangle$. This memory serves as guidance for future decisions, detailing what happened during an attempt, why it succeeded or failed, and what repair actions were taken. This explicit, human-inspectable memory complements the executable behaviors in the Skill Library.

**D. The Teach-and-Grow Scaling-Law Hypothesis:**
TGL introduces a novel scaling law hypothesis focusing on "effective reusable experience" ($X$). Unlike traditional scaling laws that relate loss to model parameters or data volume, $X$ quantifies accumulated interaction that has been validated, can be retrieved, grounded, and composed in new scenes. It is defined as a sum of scores $\omega(h)$ for each useful lesson $h$ in the interaction history $H_n$ at checkpoint $n$. The hypothesis states:
$E_{\text{future}}(X) = E_{\infty} + AX^{-\alpha}$ (expected error on related future tasks)
$D_{\text{teach}}(X) = D_{\infty} + BX^{-\beta}$ (teaching demand for new tasks)
This predicts that as effective reusable experience $X$ grows, future-task error and teaching demand will decrease following power laws, approaching irreducible floors.

**E. General Agentic Robot Architecture:**
The architecture operates in a continuous learning cycle:
1. **Few-Shot Teaching and Cross-Demonstration Abstraction:** For a new task, a few successful demonstrations are provided. The AI Agent analyzes these demonstrations, looking for meaningful state changes (e.g., object acquired, drawer opened). It segments each trajectory into sequences of accomplishments and aligns segments that achieve the same semantic effect across different demonstrations. From these aligned segments, the agent synthesizes a shared, reusable strategy ($\rho_k$) that captures common roles, relations, ordering, and effects, while abstracting away scene-specific details (e.g., precise pose, path, gripper type).
2. **Skill Blocks in the Current Scene:** When executing a Skill Block, its meaning remains consistent, but its physical realization is dynamically rebuilt based on current scene observations. The grounding function $\gamma_i$ identifies current objects, their geometry, and viable interaction points. Different backends (e.g., geometric grasping, VLA policies, tactile policies) can be invoked to realize the same block's effect.
3. **Dynamic Agentic Composition and Tool Selection:** Given a task instruction, current observations, and its persistent state (Skill Library $B_n$, Experience Memory $M_n$), the Agent retrieves relevant Skill Blocks and forms an ordered working plan. This plan is dynamic, not a fixed script. The Agent selects an appropriate executor for each block.
4. **Physical Feedback, Bounded Exploration, and Targeted Teaching:** After a block executes, the Agent compares the observed physical outcome with the block's intended effect using the outcome test $v_i$. If they agree, the task proceeds. If they differ, the Agent re-evaluates. It may re-observe, select an alternative executor, revise the remaining plan, or request targeted teaching to acquire missing behaviors or clarify ambiguities. This feedback loop allows for physical grounding and real-time adaptation.
5. **Structured Growth and Fast-Policy Distillation:** Successful and failed execution attempts contribute to the robot's growth. Reusable behaviors satisfying validation checks are added to the Skill Library. Structured textual records of context, outcomes, diagnoses, and repairs are stored in Experience Memory. Over time, verified trajectories from stable Skill Blocks can be used to train "fast student" policies (e.g., VLA, WAM) for low-latency execution of familiar tasks. The Agent continues to handle novelty and capability expansion, with control returning to it if the fast path encounters uncertainty or failure.

This methodology contrasts with monolithic approaches by separating the acquisition of new, uncertain behavior (managed by the Agent) from the fast execution of familiar, verified behavior (potentially handled by distilled policies), and ensuring persistent, explicit knowledge growth.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates TGL using the LIBERO benchmark and presents controlled studies to demonstrate its architectural components.

**A. LIBERO Evaluation Performance:**
On the standard LIBERO evaluation, the TGL system achieved state-of-the-art task success rates. The paper states this as an overall system performance indicator before detailing the internal mechanisms.

**B. Controlled Studies on Architectural Elements:**

1. **From Demonstrations to Persistent Blocks:**
 - **Induction:** The system was presented with ten visual demonstrations of condiment-transfer tasks, which were decomposed into acquisition and release stages. All 20 observable effects from these demonstrations were confirmed by the system. In a separate learning cycle, three teacher trajectories were used to induce two reusable Skill Blocks: one for acquiring a requested object and another for releasing it in a required relation.
 - **Persistence and Reuse:** These two induced blocks successfully solved three out of three evaluation states. They demonstrated persistence by surviving a save-and-reload procedure, again solving the same three states.
 - **Scope Awareness:** When applied to states outside their learned range, the learned route stopped at the first instance where an expected semantic effect was not met. This indicates that the blocks are scope-aware, preventing erroneous propagation of a skill beyond its validated context.

2. **Feedback Changes the Workflow:**
 - **Dynamic Re-planning:** In a "bowl-on-plate" task, the Agent initially planned with an acquisition block followed by a release block. The acquisition succeeded, but post-grasp evidence was deemed insufficient for immediate placement. The Agent dynamically rebuilt the remaining route based on this physical feedback, subsequently completing the task.
 - **Re-observation:** For a "drawer-opening" task, an initial action resulted in an "inconclusive" outcome from the verifier. In response, the Agent initiated a fresh observation, demonstrating its ability to gather more evidence and adapt its plan when faced with uncertain physical states.
 - These traces illustrate that physical outcomes directly influence the Agent's subsequent actions, enabling real-time adaptation and plan revision.

3. **Local Growth Under a Fixed Executor:**
 - A pilot study was conducted to assess the impact of local library growth on an executor's capabilities. Initially, a six-block Skill Library was used, resulting in 0 out of 6 task evaluations being solved.
 - Subsequently, the two learned Skill Blocks (acquisition and release, as described above) were added to form an eight-block library. With this expanded library, the system solved 4 out of 6 evaluations.
 - This result was achieved without changing any model weights, runtime, or evaluation budget, demonstrating that adding reusable capability locally can alter future behavior without requiring a global policy retraining. The study reported a median of 2.5 attempts with six blocks versus 1.5 attempts with eight blocks, and a statistical significance ($p=0.061$) for the improvement in success rate.
 - A separate cohort recorded failures for specific acquisition attempts, localizing failures to pre-motion planning, path consistency/calibration, or gripper closure, indicating the system's ability to diagnose and categorize failure points.

In summary, the experimental results demonstrate TGL's capability to induce reusable skills from sparse demonstrations, persist these skills across system resets, and dynamically adapt its task execution based on real-time physical feedback. The studies also provide evidence that the architecture facilitates local, non-destructive growth of robotic competence.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Teach-and-Grow Learning (TGL) architecture offers a conceptual and practical shift in how robots can acquire and retain new capabilities, with several significant implications for the field of general robotics:

**A. Shift Towards Capability Growth and Persistent Knowledge:**
TGL proposes that a robot's learning can manifest as the growth of explicit, reusable behaviors (Skill Blocks) and structured experiential knowledge, rather than being solely encapsulated within a policy's distributed parameters. This allows for a more interpretable and manageable approach to lifelong learning. New abilities are added locally to a "Skill Library," preserving existing competence. This contrasts with approaches that necessitate global policy updates, which can be disruptive and difficult to inspect or delimit.

**B. Addressing the "Retraining Tax" and Non-Destructive Learning:**
A central contribution is its direct challenge to the "retraining tax" problem. By enabling local additions and validation of competence without requiring global policy retraining or regression testing across all behaviors, TGL offers a path towards more efficient and scalable deployment. New tasks can be acquired by adding explicit behaviors, which can be tested and linked to existing blocks, thus avoiding the cascading costs associated with modifying monolithic neural networks. This non-destructive growth ensures that previously mastered skills remain available and stable.

**C. The Agent as Organizing Intelligence:**
TGL introduces an AI agent that acts as a semantic control layer, operating above the low-level execution workflow. This agent decides which observations are needed, which Skill Blocks and executors to invoke, whether observed effects are sufficient, and how the remaining task route should be revised. This explicit reasoning and control at semantic decision points enable dynamic adaptation to physical realities. It separates high-level planning from low-level continuous control, allowing for local improvements to perception, planning, or control backends without altering the overall architectural logic or task-level reasoning. This makes failure diagnosis more informative, identifying specific missing effects or uncertain observations rather than just a task-level failure.

**D. Grounding and Reusability of Skills:**
The concept of Skill Blocks emphasizes reusability by abstracting a strategy from demonstrations and dynamically re-grounding its physical realization in the current scene. This means a skill learned in one context (e.g., for a specific object pose or robot gripper) can be adapted to new, unseen variations by recomputing scene-specific details. This approach promotes broader generalization than simple trajectory replay.

**E. Teach-and-Grow Scaling-Law Hypothesis:**
The introduction of a scaling-law hypothesis based on "effective reusable experience" ($X$) provides a new framework for quantifying robot learning progress. It suggests that as a robot accumulates validated, retrievable, and composable experience, the error on future tasks and the teaching required for new tasks should decrease predictably. This shifts the focus from raw data volume or model size to the quality and reusability of learned experience over a robot's lifetime, opening new avenues for longitudinal research and evaluation.

**F. Two-Speed Learning: Slow Teacher and Fast Student:**
TGL proposes a two-speed extension where the agentic route handles novel task acquisition and expansion, while verified trajectories from mature Skill Blocks can be distilled into "fast student" policies for low-latency execution of familiar tasks. This division allows the slow, deliberative agent to operate at the frontier of competence, generating structured, verified training data for fast, efficient policies. When the fast path encounters uncertainty or distribution shift, control can revert to the diagnostic capabilities of the agent.

**G. Integration of Diverse Robotic Capabilities:**
The architecture provides a framework where various existing robot methods—from classical geometric planners and controllers to modern VLA models and perception systems—can function as specialized "executors" or provide "evidence" within Skill Blocks. This ecology allows the system to leverage strengths from different paradigms, integrating them under the agent's organizational layer.

**H. Limitations:**
The paper acknowledges a current practical limitation: an unfamiliar task may require multiple rounds of agent reasoning, tool use, and observation, which can be time-consuming compared to feed-forward policy inference. The proposed fast-student distillation path is intended to mitigate this in the future.

In conclusion, TGL presents a vision for lifelong robot learning where the robot continuously grows its capabilities through explicit, verifiable, and reusable components. This approach has the potential to make robot deployment more robust, adaptable, and efficient by enabling local learning and growth without the prohibitive costs of global retraining.
