# DRIFT: Derailing Denoising Trajectories of Flow-Matching VLAs with Adversarial Patch Attack

> [!summary] Summary
> The DRIFT attack, a universal physical adversarial patch, exposes vulnerabilities in flow-matching Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, achieving nearly 100% attack success rate on the LIBERO benchmark by strategically perturbing the first step of the denoising trajectory.

## Problem

- Previous research suggested that flow-matching Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, like 	0, exhibited notable robustness against adversarial perturbations.
- Existing test-time adversarial attacks on VLA models often did not specifically account for the multi-step denoising ordinary differential equation (ODE) that governs action generation in flow-matching policies.
- There was a need to understand the adversarial robustness of VLA models, particularly in safety-critical real-world robotic deployments.

## Method

- Developed **DRIFT** (Denoising Redirection via Input perturbation of the Flow-matching Trajectory), a novel test-time ==adversarial patch attack== for flow-matching VLAs.
- The attack operates by optimizing a single, small (32x32 or 64x64 pixels) ==universal physical adversarial patch==, placed on the robot's gripper, to directly perturb the ==denoising velocity field==.
- The optimization process uses ==Projected Gradient Descent (PGD)== to target only the first denoising step (k=0), a strategy identified through step-wise vulnerability analysis to maximize impact and efficiency.

## Results

- DRIFT achieved an average Attack Success Rate (ASR) of **99.8%** on π₀ across all four LIBERO suites, significantly outperforming action-space (UADA, **13.2%** ASR) and embedding-space (EDPA, **25.3%** ASR) baselines.
- The attack consistently induced a 'phantom grasp' failure mode, where the robot closes its gripper prematurely without approaching any object.
- While the attack generalized to π₀.5 (**99.3%** ASR with 64px patch), JPEG compression served as a partial defense, reducing ASR for most tasks but showing limited effectiveness on long-horizon tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Flow-matching VLA models are highly susceptible to targeted test-time adversarial patches, challenging prior assumptions of their robustness when the denoising ODE is explicitly exploited.
> - An 'early injection, full-path amplification' effect ensures that perturbations introduced at the earliest denoising steps cascade and compound, leading to complete task failure.
> - A 'less-is-more' effect was observed, where attacking only the first denoising step is more effective and computationally efficient than perturbing multiple steps, due to gradient conflicts when optimizing across broader temporal windows.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models represent a paradigm for general-purpose robot control, translating visual observations and natural language instructions into robot actions. Within this field, flow-matching VLAs, such as π₀ and its successor π₀.₅, have demonstrated performance in generating continuous action chunks through an iterative denoising process. These models differ from autoregressive VLAs (e.g., OpenVLA, RT-1, RT-2) which decode discrete action tokens.

The adversarial robustness of VLA models, particularly in safety-critical real-world deployment, is an active area of research. Previous work by Guo et al. (2026) suggested that flow-matching VLAs, exemplified by π₀, exhibit notable robustness against naive adversarial perturbations, contrasting with autoregressive models. This reported robustness forms a central point of investigation for the current paper.

Existing adversarial attacks on VLA models typically focus on perturbing the final action space (e.g., UADA Wang et al., 2025) or the vision-encoder embedding space (e.g., EDPA Xu et al., 2025a). However, these approaches often do not specifically account for the multi-step denoising ordinary differential equation (ODE) that governs action generation in flow-matching policies.

Another line of research, exemplified by FlowHijack An et al. (2026), has identified the denoising trajectory as an exploitable attack surface. FlowHijack, a training-time backdoor attack, corrupts the velocity field early in the integration process by retraining the policy on poisoned data. This approach operates under a white-box threat model with access to the training pipeline, enabling a compromised model.

This work contributes to the landscape by specifically addressing the test-time adversarial robustness of deployed, frozen flow-matching VLAs. It introduces an attack that explicitly targets the multi-step denoising ODE, which previous test-time attacks have largely overlooked. By demonstrating that flow-matching VLAs can be disrupted at test time using a physically realizable adversarial patch, this paper revises the understanding of their robustness and distinguishes its threat model from training-time backdoor attacks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations for this research are centered on understanding and exploiting the adversarial vulnerabilities of flow-matching Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models.

One key objective is to challenge the reported adversarial robustness of flow-matching VLAs, such as π₀. Prior benchmarks suggested these models were resistant to common adversarial perturbations. The authors hypothesize that this perceived robustness may stem from existing attack methods overlooking the specific multi-step denoising ordinary differential equation (ODE) that characterizes how these policies generate actions. Therefore, a central motivation is to investigate if an attack specifically designed to exploit this unique structural aspect can reveal vulnerabilities in deployed policies.

A second objective is the development of a novel test-time adversarial patch attack, named DRIFT (Denoising Redirection via Input perturbation of the Flow-matching Trajectory). This attack aims to directly perturb the denoising velocity field, which the policy integrates to produce action chunks. The motivation here is to devise a practical threat model: a single, small, universal adversarial patch placed on the robot's gripper, optimized offline without requiring modifications to the policy’s weights or its training data. This approach contrasts with more powerful, but less practical, training-time backdoor attacks or attacks that require manipulating model internals during deployment.

A third objective involves identifying the specific stages within the denoising trajectory that are most susceptible to adversarial input perturbations. This includes an analysis of whether attacking early, late, or multiple denoising steps is most effective. The motivation is to pinpoint the critical vulnerabilities within the iterative action generation process to inform efficient and potent attack strategies.

Furthermore, the research aims to provide a mechanistic explanation for the observed attack effectiveness, particularly for a counterintuitive finding: that attacking only the first denoising step can be stronger and more cost-effective than perturbing multiple steps. This involves analyzing the gradient dynamics in input-space optimization and comparing them with observations from training-time backdoor regimes. The motivation is to contribute to the theoretical understanding of adversarial attacks on complex sequential generation processes.

Ultimately, the overarching motivation is driven by the increasing deployment of VLA models in real-world, safety-critical robotic applications. Understanding and characterizing their adversarial robustness is crucial to ensure their reliability and prevent malicious exploitation. By demonstrating vulnerabilities, the research aims to highlight areas where robust defenses are needed.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research focuses on attacking flow-matching VLA models, specifically π₀ and its successor π₀.₅, which generate action chunks by iteratively integrating a learned denoising velocity field over K=10 Euler steps. The policies used are publicly released LIBERO-finetuned checkpoints and remain frozen during the attack process.

**Threat Model:**
The adversary operates under a white-box patch-attack setting, restricted to test-time, input-space manipulation. The adversary has:
1. **White-box access for optimization:** Access to the frozen policy's architecture and weights is granted for offline patch optimization, allowing gradient computation through the denoising process. However, the adversary cannot modify model weights, fine-tune the model, or poison training data. This distinguishes it from training-time backdoor attacks.
2. **Physical patch constraint:** A single, small (32x32 pixels, approximately 2% of the wrist image, or 64x64 pixels for some experiments) physical adversarial patch is placed on the robot's gripper, within the camera's field of view. Only pixels within this fixed region are modified.
3. **Universal patch:** The patch is optimized once offline and then applied universally across all tasks, scenes, and episodes without further modification or per-instance optimization.
4. **Untargeted goal:** The objective is to induce task failure by driving the generated action chunk away from the policy's intended behavior, rather than steering it toward a specific attacker-chosen action.

**Attack Strategy (DRIFT):**
DRIFT's core approach is to directly attack the denoising velocity field vθ. A single universal patch δ is pasted onto a fixed region R of the wrist image, perturbing the observation o_t to o⁺_t.

1. **Step-wise Vulnerability Analysis:**
 - **Single-step ablation:** Patches were optimized to corrupt the velocity at a single denoising step k (from 0 to K-1) using the objective:
 L_k(δ) = E[ ||vθ(Aτ(k), o⁺) - vθ(Aτ(k), o)||² ], where τ(k) = k/(K-1) and o⁺ = A(o, δ). This identified early steps (k=0) as most vulnerable.
 - **First-M ablation:** Patches were optimized to corrupt the velocity across a window of M earliest steps (S = {0..., M-1}) by summing the per-step divergences:
 L_S(δ) = E[ Σ_{k∈S} ||vθ(Aτ(k), o⁺) - vθ(Aτ(k), o)||² ]. This revealed that attacking only the first step (M=1) was more effective than wider windows.

2. **Gradient Conflict Analysis:** To explain the "less-is-more" effect, the pairwise cosine similarity between per-step patch gradients (g_k) and a "survival ratio" (ratio of the norm of the sum of gradients to the sum of the norms) were computed. This demonstrated that gradients from different steps could be misaligned and cancel each other out.

3. **DRIFT Training Objective:** Based on the vulnerability analysis, DRIFT's objective is defined as L_DRIFT(δ) = L₀(δ), meaning it targets only the first denoising step (k=0). This choice avoids gradient conflict and is computationally efficient.

4. **Patch Optimization:** Projected Gradient Descent (PGD) is used to optimize the patch. At each iteration, a sign-gradient ascent step is taken on the objective L, and the patch is projected back to the valid image range. An L∞ ball constraint can be applied for stealth. The patch is optimized using a pool of LIBERO wrist-camera observations to ensure universality. Optimization requires 500 iterations with a step size α=0.01. Targeting only k=0 reduces optimization cost by a factor of K.

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Benchmark:** LIBERO benchmark Liu et al. (2023), including Spatial, Goal, Object, and Long suites (LIBERO-10, 10 manipulation tasks each).
- **Evaluation:** 10 trials per task, 100 episodes per suite. A single patch is optimized on LIBERO-Spatial observations and then evaluated across all four suites to assess generalization.
- **Metrics:**
 - Clean Task Success Rate (TSR, %).
 - Attack Success Rate (ASR, %): (TSR_clean - TSR_attack) / TSR_clean, representing the fraction of originally-solvable tasks broken.
 - Normalized Action Discrepancy (NAD, %): Measures the deviation between attacked and clean policy actions, normalized by maximum possible deviation.

**Baselines:**
DRIFT is compared against three baselines under the same threat model and patch-size budget:
1. **Random patch:** An untrained patch of uniform random pixel values, serving as an occlusion baseline.
2. **UADA Wang et al. (2025):** A universal patch optimized to maximize deviation in the final action space.
3. **EDPA Xu et al. (2025a):** An attack that disrupts the vision-encoder embedding space. EDPA was tested at 64x64 pixels as it was ineffective at 32x32 pixels.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings regarding the adversarial robustness of flow-matching VLAs and the effectiveness of the proposed DRIFT attack.

**1. Vulnerability of Flow-Matching VLAs:**
Contrary to previous reports suggesting robustness, flow-matching VLAs like π₀ and π₀.₅ were found to be susceptible to test-time adversarial patches. This vulnerability is attributed to the attacks specifically targeting the denoising ODE, a mechanism previously overlooked by most test-time attacks.

**2. Single-Step Vulnerability and Cascade Effect:**
A detailed step-wise ablation analysis revealed that the vulnerability of flow-matching VLAs to input-space perturbations is not uniformly distributed along the denoising path. Patches optimized to corrupt the velocity field at early denoising steps (k ≤ 5) achieved significantly higher velocity divergence and Attack Success Rate (ASR, near 100%). Conversely, attacking later steps (k ≥ 6) resulted in negligible ASR. This finding confirms an "early injection, full-path amplification" effect, where an error introduced at the beginning of the denoising ODE cascades and compounds throughout the remaining trajectory, similar to observations in training-time backdoor attacks.

**3. "Less-Is-More" Effect in Patch Optimization:**
A significant and counterintuitive finding is that attacking only the *first* denoising step (M=1) proved to be stronger and more cost-effective than perturbing a wider window of early steps (e.g., M=3 or M=5). This "less-is-more" effect implies that adding more early steps to the attack objective actually degrades attack performance.

**4. Mechanistic Explanation via Gradient Conflict:**
This "less-is-more" phenomenon is attributed to a gradient conflict specific to input-space optimization. Analysis showed that the per-step gradients (g_k) with respect to the adversarial patch are mutually misaligned, particularly between early and late steps within the denoising trajectory (e.g., cos(g₀, g₉) ≈ -0.35). When these conflicting gradients are summed, they partially cancel each other, resulting in a weaker overall optimization signal and a less effective patch. This contrasts with the training-time backdoor regime, where a wider early window is needed to learn a consistent malicious vector field in weight space. For a frozen model, concentrating the attack on the first step avoids this cancellation.

**5. DRIFT Performance:**
- **High Attack Success Rate (ASR):** DRIFT achieved nearly 100% ASR on π₀ across all four LIBERO suites (average 99.8%), effectively breaking almost all originally-solvable tasks.
- **Outperformance of Baselines:** DRIFT significantly outperformed action-space (UADA, 13.2% ASR) and embedding-space (EDPA, 25.3% ASR, even with a larger 64px patch) baselines under comparable patch-size budgets. This indicates that targeting the denoising velocity field is more effective than perturbing final actions or vision embeddings for flow-matching VLAs.
- **High Normalized Action Discrepancy (NAD):** DRIFT also exhibited the highest NAD, confirming that it causes substantial deviation in the generated actions at an action-level.
- **Generalization to π₀.₅:** The attack generalizes to π₀.₅, although this model appeared more robust, requiring a larger 64px patch to achieve similar ASR (average 99.3%).

**6. Optimization Efficiency:**
Because DRIFT targets only the first denoising step (k=0), its optimization cost is reduced by a factor of K (the total number of denoising steps) compared to attacks that require forward and backward passes through the full denoising trajectory.

**7. Consistent Failure Mode: "Phantom Grasp":**
Qualitative analysis revealed a consistent failure mechanism: the patched policy commands the gripper to close almost immediately (within the first 7 control steps) without approaching any object. The robot then proceeds with a clenched gripper, failing to execute the required reach-then-grasp sequence. This "phantom grasp" behavior is consistent with an untargeted attack that derails the policy's primary control sequence.

**8. Perturbation Budget and Stealthiness:**
Experiments on perturbation budget (ℓ∞ norm) showed a sharp phase transition: ASR remained near zero below a threshold (e.g., ϵ ≤ 0.05) and jumped to high values (97% at ϵ=0.1) above it. Grayscale patches were also effective but required approximately twice the perturbation budget for a similar effect, offering a path to more inconspicuous attacks.

**9. Limited Cross-Model Transfer:**
DRIFT patches showed limited transferability between π₀ and π₀.₅ (e.g., 8.9% ASR from π₀ to π₀.₅ with a 64px patch). This suggests that while the early-step vulnerability is a shared characteristic of flow-matching VLAs, the specific perturbations needed to exploit it are model-specific, necessitating white-box access for optimal patch generation.

**10. Attention Map Analysis (Appendix):**
An analysis of the policy's attention weights revealed that the adversarial patch absorbed a disproportionate share of the attention from object-referring language tokens (e.g., "bowl" token's attention on the patch region increased from 1.7% to 28-33% in early steps), redirecting the model's focus away from the task-relevant object.

**11. JPEG Compression as a Defense (Appendix):**
JPEG compression was tested as a simple, training-free defense. ASR decreased progressively with lower JPEG quality (higher compression). For most tasks (Spatial, Goal, Object), strong compression (Q ≲ 50-60) largely blocked the attack, reducing ASR to near zero. However, this defense was less effective on long-horizon tasks (Long suite), where ASR plateaued at 16-21% even under strong compression.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research has several implications for the understanding and deployment of robotic VLA models, particularly those based on flow matching.

**1. Revised Understanding of Robustness:** The study challenges the prior perception that flow-matching VLAs possess inherent robustness against adversarial attacks. By demonstrating a highly effective test-time attack that specifically exploits their internal mechanics, the research indicates that this robustness was largely an artifact of attack formulations that did not account for the denoising ODE. This finding necessitates a re-evaluation of the security posture of these advanced robotic control systems.

**2. Identification of Critical Vulnerability:** The explicit identification of the first denoising step as the most potent attack surface in flow-matching VLAs reveals a critical, previously under-exploited vulnerability. This provides a focused target for both attackers seeking to disrupt and defenders seeking to protect these models.

**3. Informing Defense Strategies:** The findings directly suggest that future defenses for flow-matching VLAs should prioritize protecting the earliest stages of the denoising process. Strategies could involve robustifying the velocity field prediction at noise-adjacent timesteps or implementing monitoring mechanisms to detect early deviations in the denoising trajectory. The identified "phantom grasp" failure mode and the shift in attention towards the patch also offer concrete targets for designing detection or mitigation mechanisms.

**4. Practical Implications for Robot Deployment:** The ability of a single, small, universal, and physically realizable adversarial patch to consistently cause task failure in advanced robot control policies underscores a practical security concern. This research highlights that deployed robotic systems are vulnerable to such low-cost, real-world manipulations, which could have significant safety and reliability implications in critical applications.

**5. Theoretical Insights into Adversarial Attacks:** The mechanistic explanation of the "less-is-more" effect via gradient conflict offers a theoretical contribution to the field of adversarial machine learning. It distinguishes input-space optimization for test-time attacks from weight-space optimization for training-time backdoors, providing insights into why different attack strategies are optimal under varying threat models. This understanding can guide the design of more efficient and effective adversarial attacks and defenses across different model architectures and attack types.

**6. Efficiency in Attack Generation:** By demonstrating that attacking only the first denoising step is sufficient and superior, the research provides a method for generating highly effective adversarial patches with significantly reduced computational cost (a factor of K cheaper than full-trajectory attacks). This efficiency can accelerate future research in both attack development and defensive countermeasures.
