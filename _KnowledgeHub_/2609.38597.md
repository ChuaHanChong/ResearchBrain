# PixelUMM: Encoder-Free Unified Image and Video Understanding and Generation

> [!summary] Summary
> PixelUMM is an 8B encoder-free unified model that understands and generates both images and videos directly from raw pixels. Images become spatial patches and videos become spatiotemporal tubelets, each linked by a single linear projection to a Qwen3-initialized Mixture-of-Transformers backbone trained jointly on autoregressive text and clean-pixel flow matching. It is broadly competitive: 70.53 MVBench, 0.83 GenEval, 83.24 VBench total.

## Problem

- Dual-encoder unified models like BAGEL represent each image with both ViT and VAE tokens, roughly doubling visual context and inflating attention and KV-cache cost in multi-turn use.
- The ViT-plus-VAE interface departs from the single visual stream used in standard vision-language pretraining, forcing costly changes to existing data pipelines and training recipes.
- Prior encoder-free pixel-space unified models handled only images, and video lacks a shared interface: generation uses causal 3D VAEs while understanding encodes sampled frames independently.

## Method

- ==Native pixel interface==: 16x16 image patches and 4x16x16 video tubelets enter the backbone through single linear projections, separate for understanding and generation, with zero-initialized linear output heads.
- ==Mixture-of-Transformers== backbone initialized from Qwen3 routes text and clean visual tokens to an understanding expert and noisy tokens to a generation expert, sharing self-attention under generalized causal masks.
- ==JiT-style clean-pixel prediction== with a ==velocity-space loss==, extended to video and used without explicit timestep conditioning, trained through a six-stage recipe that progressively adds video tasks and higher resolutions.

## Results

- Image understanding reaches **90.42** DocVQA, **82.96** ChartQA and **94.30** CountBench, but trails BAGEL on MMMU (**41.67** vs **55.30**) and AI2D (**80.12** vs **89.20**).
- Image generation scores **0.83** GenEval with prompt rewriting and **85.74** DPG-Bench; video generation reaches **83.24** VBench total, close to UniVideo's **83.48** but below Lance's **85.11**.
- Ablations: 16x16 patches reach lower generation loss than 32x32 despite fitting four times fewer images per step; convolutional heads remove the linear head's patch-boundary elevation (**1.078** spatial, **1.160** temporal).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Raw pixels passed through one linear layer can serve as the single visual interface for both understanding and generation, removing duplicated ViT and VAE tokens without collapsing performance.
> - Token compression carries a hidden cost in pixel space: packing more pixels into each token consistently raised generation loss, so shorter sequences trade off against learnability.
> - Linear pixel heads leave grid artifacts at high guidance and cheap PixelShuffle decoders mostly fix them; denser 4 FPS tubelets gave no understanding gain over 1 FPS frames.

## Detailed Report

### 1. PixelUMM: Detailed Research Report

#### 1.1. Position in the Research Landscape

PixelUMM addresses a current challenge in unified multimodal models (UMMs): how to use one system for visual understanding, language responses, and image or video generation without maintaining separate visual representations for different tasks. Many existing UMMs combine a vision encoder, such as a Vision Transformer, for semantic understanding with a variational autoencoder (VAE) for image generation. The two representations serve different purposes: semantic features support recognition and reasoning, while reconstruction-oriented latents can preserve visual detail for generation and editing. However, carrying both representations for the same image increases the number of visual tokens and complicates integration with vision-language pretraining pipelines that ordinarily use a single visual stream.

PixelUMM builds on encoder-free vision-language modeling and pixel-space image generation. Rather than replacing a VAE with another learned visual tokenizer, it maps raw pixels into a Transformer using simple linear projections. Related work has applied this approach to image understanding and generation, but extending it to video requires resolving a mismatch between common video-generation interfaces, which use temporally compressed latents, and video-understanding interfaces, which often encode sampled frames independently. PixelUMM’s main research contribution is to investigate whether images and videos can share a raw-pixel interface for both understanding and generation.

The model nevertheless does not use a single set of parameters for every operation. Its Mixture-of-Transformers (MoT) design has distinct understanding and generation experts, with task-specific parameters and shared multimodal attention. The work is therefore best understood as unifying the input representation and attention-based interaction, rather than eliminating all task-specific structure.

#### 1.2. Objectives and Motivation

The central objective is to develop and evaluate an encoder-free model that supports four core tasks: image-to-text understanding, video-to-text understanding, text-to-image generation, and text-to-video generation. The authors ask whether direct pixel-space modeling can extend from images to videos while preserving a common interface for perception and synthesis.

The motivation has both representational and practical dimensions. A dual-encoder design can provide semantic and reconstruction-oriented information, but requires extra visual tokens and more attention and memory as visual context grows. This burden matters in long conversations and settings with multiple images or video frames. It also creates a gap between UMM training and established vision-language pipelines, because adding VAE tokens entails changes to data preparation and training.

Pixel-space modeling offers a possible alternative: the same raw-pixel representation can serve as clean input for understanding and as the target space for generation. The authors focus particularly on video, where a shared representation is not straightforward. Images can be divided into two-dimensional patches, but videos have both spatial and temporal structure. The work therefore investigates patch and tubelet sizes, attention patterns, output decoders, and training dynamics, as well as whether visual conditions used in editing or reference-based generation can be processed without a separate VAE stream.

#### 1.3. Methodology and Approach

##### 1.3.1. Pixel interface and model architecture

Images are divided into non-overlapping 16 × 16 patches. Videos are divided into spatiotemporal tubelets of 4 frames × 16 × 16 pixels. Each flattened patch or tubelet is mapped to the Transformer’s hidden size by a single linear projection. Understanding and generation have separate input projections: clean visual inputs use the understanding projection, while noisy generation targets use the generation projection. Linear output heads map hidden states back to RGB pixels and are followed by deterministic unpatchifying. PixelUMM therefore has no pretrained vision encoder, VAE, or discrete visual tokenizer.

The Transformer is initialized from a decoder-only Qwen3 model. Each block routes tokens to an understanding or generation expert, with task-specific normalization, projections, and feed-forward networks. The experts share multimodal self-attention, allowing text and visual tokens from either route to interact. Text and clean visual tokens use the understanding route; noisy visual tokens use the generation route. The output projections are zero-initialized before multimodal training.

For video understanding, the model uses two input modes. In dense mode, videos sampled at 4 frames per second or more are grouped into temporal tubelets. In sparse mode, lower-rate or otherwise unsuitable videos are sampled at 1 frame per second and each frame is processed independently. Positional encoding represents time, height, and width. Visual attention is bidirectional within an image block or frame block; text attention is causal. Video frames retain temporal ordering, and generation targets attend to the prompt and to other tokens in the target block.

##### 1.3.2. Training objectives and schedule

PixelUMM combines autoregressive text prediction with pixel-space flow matching. Text is trained with cross-entropy on assistant responses. For generation, clean pixels \(x\) are interpolated with Gaussian noise \(\epsilon\) to form a corrupted input \(z_t=(1-t)x+t\epsilon\). The model predicts clean pixels, and the training loss is calculated as squared error in velocity space. The total objective combines text, image-generation, and video-generation losses, with task-specific weights adjusted by training stage. The denoiser receives no explicit timestep embedding; the timestep is used to construct targets and guide sampling.

Training proceeds through six stages. Initial joint training combines text tasks, image understanding, and 256 × 256 image generation. A generation stage adds text-to-video training, followed by stages that introduce native-resolution image understanding and video understanding at 224 × 224 and then 448 × 448 pixels per frame. Final joint training includes all tasks, with image and video generation at 512 × 512 and video understanding at 448 × 448. The reported data sources include FineVision for image understanding; VideoChat-Flash and LLaVA-Video-178K for video understanding; and public real and synthetic data for generation.

##### 1.3.3. Empirical studies

The authors conduct eight experimental families covering image and video patch size, decoder design, pixel-space versus VAE-space training dynamics, model size, distributed compute, visual conditioning, and video-understanding input modes. These studies compare training losses and, in some cases, generated outputs or benchmark scores. The decoder study also measures spatial and temporal discontinuities at patch and tubelet boundaries.

#### 1.4. Main Findings and Results

Across its benchmark evaluations, PixelUMM shows performance comparable to other unified models, although it does not lead consistently and the paper cautions that training data and evaluation protocols differ across systems. On image understanding, the 8B model scores 41.67 on MMMU, 82.96 on ChartQA, and 90.42 on DocVQA. Its performance is competitive on some measures but below several listed vision-language baselines on others, including MMMU, OCRBench, and InfoVQA. On video understanding, evaluated in sparse mode with up to 96 sampled frames, it scores 70.53 on MVBench, 57.33 on Video-MME without subtitles, 59.61 on LongVideoBench, and 40.41 on LVBench. These results establish coverage across tasks, not superiority over models trained with different data.

For image generation, the reported GenEval overall score is 0.77 for the original PixelUMM configuration and 0.83 for the version using prompt rewriting. Its DPG-Bench overall score is 85.74. On video-generation benchmarks, PixelUMM receives a VBench Part 1 quality score of 84.10 and semantic score of 79.80; its VBench Part 2 total score is 83.24. These figures place it within the range of reported unified-model results, while several competing systems score higher on particular measures.

The ablations identify several practical design patterns. In image generation, 16 × 16 patches produce lower training loss than 32 × 32 patches, even though the larger patches allow four times as many images per step under the same sequence-length budget. For video generation, the tested configurations generally show higher losses with more aggressive spatiotemporal compression. The p32/t1 configuration has the lowest loss among those tested, but the final model uses p16/t4 to align with a convention used in common video VAEs.

The linear output heads can produce grid-like artifacts, especially in smooth image and video regions and at high classifier-free guidance. Convolutional decoders reduce measured boundary artifacts. However, the Wan-style upsampling-and-convolution head has substantially higher computational cost and training loss; PixelShuffle heads offer a compromise between artifact reduction and optimization, with temporal-first PixelShuffle performing slightly better than spatial-first in the reported comparison.

Other experiments show that an 8B model reaches comparable text and generation losses in about one-third as many training steps as a 1.7B model. Increasing training resources from 8 to 128 GPUs improves step efficiency more for text cross-entropy than for image-generation loss. These are comparisons in training steps, not wall-clock speedups. Multi-task fine-tuning that adds image-to-video and editing tasks improves all four reported video-understanding benchmarks, while image-understanding results are mixed: some measures improve and others decline. Finally, dense 4-FPS video input performs comparably to sparse 1-FPS input on four video benchmarks, providing no consistent evidence that the denser tubelet interface improves understanding in this setting.

#### 1.5. Significance and Potential Impact

PixelUMM provides evidence that an encoder-free, pixel-space interface can be extended from images to videos and used for understanding, generation, and visual conditioning within one model. Its principal significance is methodological: it demonstrates a way to avoid maintaining separate semantic and reconstruction-oriented token streams, while retaining shared multimodal attention and task-specific computation where useful. This could simplify multimodal data pipelines and reduce duplicated visual context in applications involving multiple conditions or long interactions.

The study also offers practical guidance for future designs. It shows that aggressive patch compression can make generation harder to optimize, and that decoder choice affects both visual artifacts and computational cost. Its exploration of video conditioning and editing further suggests that clean visual inputs can guide generation through the understanding pathway without uniformly degrading understanding performance.

The evidence has limits. Pixel-space patches can create long sequences, and the paper does not establish that the approach is more efficient overall than VAE-based systems. Benchmark comparisons are affected by differences in data and evaluation procedures, while training losses across pixel and latent spaces are not directly comparable. The authors also acknowledge that separate understanding and generation experts remain in the architecture. Accordingly, PixelUMM is a contribution to the design space of unified multimodal systems, rather than a demonstration that encoder-free pixel modeling is universally preferable.
