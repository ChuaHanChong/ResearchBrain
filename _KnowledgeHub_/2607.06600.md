# MiLSD: A Micro Line-Segment Detector for Resource-Constrained Devices

> [!summary] Summary
> MiLSD introduces a learned line segment detector specifically designed for microcontrollers with sub-megabyte memory constraints. The system achieves a structural Average Precision (sAP10_{10}10) of 24.1 on the ShanghaiTech Wireframe dataset, demonstrating the feasibility of deploying accurate learned structural perception on highly resource-constrained edge devices.

## Problem

- Modern learned line segment detectors require substantial computational resources (GPUs, high-end mobile processors) and large memory footprints, making them unsuitable for microcontrollers (MCUs) with sub-megabyte SRAM budgets.
- Classical line detectors, while lightweight and MCU-compatible, suffer from degraded accuracy under real-world conditions (blur, low contrast, clutter) and exhibit content-dependent performance.
- The primary bottleneck for deploying deep learning models on MCUs is peak activation memory during inference, not solely the total parameter count, limiting advanced vision tasks on low-cost edge hardware.

## Method

- **MiLSD** employs a compact, ==fully-convolutional== backbone optimized for minimal activation memory, processing 256x256 grayscale inputs to produce 128x128 prediction maps.
- It utilizes the ==F-Clip==-style center-with-length-and-angle output representation, which proved most effective for encoding line segments efficiently and accurately under severe memory constraints.
- The system incorporates ==8-bit quantization-aware training==, ==sub-pixel decoding==, ==test-time augmentation==, and a learned ==Line-of-Interest verification head== to maximize accuracy within a 1 MB activation memory budget for STM32H7 microcontrollers.

## Results

- MiLSD achieved a structural Average Precision (sAP$_{10}$) of **24.1** on the ShanghaiTech Wireframe dataset, operating within a **1 MB** activation memory budget on an STM32H7 microcontroller.
- The F-Clip output representation alone, with a **25k-parameter** model, achieved sAP$_{10}$ = **10.6** with a **0.25 MB** activation footprint, demonstrating its efficiency compared to heatmap (sAP$_{10}$=**0.3**) and center-with-displacement (sAP$_{10}$=**3.6**) representations.
- Inference-time enhancements, including sub-pixel decoding, test-time augmentation, and a learned Line-of-Interest verification head, cumulatively improved sAP$_{10}$ from **17.8** (scaled backbone only) to **24.1**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The F-Clip output representation (center, length, angle) is the most effective for learned line segment detection under severe memory constraints, outperforming heatmap and center-with-displacement encodings.
> - 8-bit quantization is viable for MCU deployment with minimal accuracy loss, but 4-bit quantization causes significant performance degradation, particularly for angle regression due to its narrow dynamic range.
> - Beyond a certain point, increasing model parameter capacity offers diminishing returns for accuracy in extreme resource-constrained environments, suggesting that output representation and inference-time enhancements become more critical factors.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Line segment detection is a foundational task in computer vision, serving as a primitive structural feature for diverse applications such as Visual Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM), 3D reconstruction, vanishing-point estimation, lane and power-line detection, and industrial inspection. Over time, two main categories of line segment detectors have emerged:

1. **Classical Detectors:** Methods like LSD, EDLines, and ELSED operate on CPUs or embedded ARM processors. These detectors generally grow line-support regions from local gradients and validate them statistically. While computationally light, they tend to detect all edges, including clutter, and their accuracy can degrade under blur, low contrast, and complex scenes. Their runtime is often content-dependent, and some require memory buffers (e.g., ELSED at 1.5–2 MB) that can exceed typical microcontroller SRAM.
2. **Modern Learned Wireframe Parsers:** Recent deep learning approaches, including L-CNN, HAWP, F-Clip, ULSD, LETR, and M-LSD, have significantly advanced accuracy by reformulating line detection as a learning problem. These methods recover salient segments but typically demand substantial computational resources, such as GPUs or high-end mobile phone processors. Even optimized "tiny" versions, like M-LSD-tiny, can require runtime memory exceeding 78 MB, placing them well beyond the capacity of typical microcontrollers (MCUs) like the STM32F746 (320 KB SRAM, 1 MB flash) or even the more powerful STM32H7 (1 MB SRAM).

This work addresses a specific, largely unaddressed gap within this landscape: the deployment of learned line segment detectors on **microcontroller-level hardware with sub-megabyte memory budgets**. Prior learned methods primarily target GPU or phone platforms, leaving MCUs reliant on classical techniques. The research explicitly highlights that for MCUs, the primary bottleneck is **peak activation memory**, not merely parameter storage. By designing MiLSD, the authors aim to bridge the performance gap between accurate, resource-intensive learned methods and lightweight, less accurate classical methods, establishing the feasibility of learned line segment detection within severe memory constraints. This work also systematically characterizes the accuracy–memory trade-off across different design choices, a study not previously conducted for this specific memory regime.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to **investigate the maximum achievable accuracy for line segment detection under a sub-megabyte memory budget**, specifically targeting microcontroller-level constraints.

This overarching goal is motivated by several factors and pursued through three primary axes of investigation:

1. **Motivation for MCU-Scale Line Detection:**
 - **Growing Need for Edge AI:** There is an increasing demand for sophisticated computer vision capabilities on low-cost, real-time embedded hardware, such as MCUs, for applications in robotics, industrial inspection, IoT edge devices, and specific automotive components.
 - **Limitations of Current Solutions:** Existing classical line detectors, while lightweight, exhibit degraded accuracy under real-world conditions (blur, low contrast, clutter) and have content-dependent performance. Conversely, state-of-the-art learned detectors, despite their superior accuracy, have memory footprints that are orders of magnitude larger than what typical MCUs can support, making their direct deployment impossible.
 - **Memory as the Primary Constraint:** For MCUs, the bottleneck is often peak activation memory during inference, rather than the total number of parameters or the energy consumption, which necessitates highly optimized network designs and inference strategies.

2. **Key Objectives (Axes of Investigation):**
 - **Output Representation Comparison:** To determine the most memory-efficient and effective way to encode continuous geometric line segments into discrete network outputs under extreme memory constraints. The study compares three distinct representations: heatmap, center-with-displacement, and F-Clip-style center-with-length-and-angle.
 - **Quantization Study:** To evaluate the impact of different bit-widths (full-precision, 8-bit, and 4-bit) on accuracy and memory, and to assess the effectiveness of post-training quantization (PTQ) versus quantization-aware training (QAT) for MCU deployment. This aims to understand the limits of aggressive model compression.
 - **Inference Enhancements:** To explore how various post-processing and inference-time techniques—including sub-pixel decoding, test-time augmentation (TTA), and a lightweight learned verification head—can improve accuracy without increasing the core network's trained parameter count or peak memory footprint.

By systematically addressing these objectives, the research aims to define the accuracy–resource frontier for learned line segment detection on microcontrollers, enabling the development of performant embedded vision systems within tight hardware limitations.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research develops MiLSD, a Micro Line-Segment Detector, by systematically evaluating and integrating various architectural and inference-time strategies tailored for microcontroller-scale memory.

**A. Core Network Design (Section II)**
All tested variants share a compact, fully-convolutional backbone designed to minimize activation memory.
- **Input/Output:** Processes 256x256 grayscale input images to produce prediction maps on a 128x128 grid.
- **Backbone Architecture:** Comprises a strided encoder with five convolutional layers (channel widths 8→16→32→32→32, stride-2 downsampling to 64x64 resolution), followed by a 1x1 convolutional reduction, nearest-neighbor upsampling to 128x128, and a 3x3 output head. The smallest configuration has approximately 25k parameters.

**B. Output Representations (Section II-A)**
Three distinct methods for encoding segments on the 128x128 output grid were investigated:
1. **Heatmap (v1):** A per-pixel binary map indicating line presence. This approach requires an external linker for segment assembly and does not directly produce geometric primitives.
2. **Center-with-Displacement (v2):** Predicts a center confidence map and two displacement vectors from the center pixel to the segment endpoints. This is a single-stage method but requires regressing four continuous values.
3. **F-Clip (v6, adopted for MiLSD):** Based on Dai et al., this encodes each segment as a center confidence map, a length ($\ell$), and an orientation angle ($\theta$). The angle is robustly encoded as (cos 2$\theta$, sin 2$\theta$) to resolve the 180° ambiguity of undirected lines. This representation requires four output channels (center, length, cos 2$\theta$, sin 2$\theta$).

**C. Quantization for MCU Deployment (Section II-C)**
To enable deployment on MCUs using integer arithmetic (e.g., via CMSIS-NN), weights and activations are quantized.
- **Method:** Per-tensor symmetric quantization, with fake-quantization in the forward pass and the Straight-Through Estimator (STE), for gradient propagation during backpropagation.
- **Strategies Evaluated:**
 - **Post-training quantization (PTQ):** Quantizing a fully trained full-precision model.
 - **Quantization-aware training (QAT):** Simulating quantization during training to make the model robust to quantization error.
- **Bit-widths:** Full-precision (fp32), 8-bit (int8), and 4-bit (int4) were evaluated.

**D. Training and Deployment Pipeline (Section II-D)**
- **Training (Off-device):** Performed on a GPU workstation using PyTorch.
 - **Dataset:** ShanghaiTech Wireframe (5,000 training, 462 validation images).
 - **Hyperparameters:** 300 epochs, batch size 32, Adam optimizer, cosine annealing learning rate (1x10^-3).
 - **Loss Function:** Combined binary cross-entropy for center confidence and smooth L1 loss for geometric attributes (length, angle), masked to ground-truth segment locations.
 - **Augmentation:** Random horizontal and vertical flips.
- **Deployment (On-device):** The trained int8 model is exported via X-CUBE-AI, an STMicroelectronics tool, which generates optimized C code for the Arm Cortex-M7 core, leveraging CMSIS-NN for efficient integer arithmetic. The MCU performs int8 inference and subsequent decoding.

**E. MiLSD Enhancements for STM32H7 (Section V)**
For deployment on the STM32H7 (1 MB SRAM budget), MiLSD incorporates further enhancements:
1. **Capacity-Scaled Backbone:** The backbone width is increased to yield approximately 0.39 million parameters, with the int8 activation arena sized to approach the 1 MB SRAM limit (peak activation memory ~1 MB).
2. **Sub-Pixel Decoding:** Center peaks are refined to sub-pixel accuracy using a one-dimensional parabolic fit over their neighborhood, improving endpoint precision.
3. **Test-Time Augmentation (TTA):** Predicted maps are averaged over four views (original, horizontal flip, vertical flip, horizontal+vertical flip) to improve robustness. This increases inference latency but reuses the same activation arena, thus not increasing peak memory.
4. **Line-of-Interest (LoI) Verification Head:** A small multilayer perceptron (MLP) is added. For each candidate segment, it pools features (from the backbone and output maps) at 32 points along the line, combines them with a short geometric descriptor, and predicts a verification score (real vs. spurious). Segments are then re-ranked by this verification score multiplied by the center confidence. This head is trained with the backbone frozen.

**F. Evaluation Metric**
Accuracy is measured using structural Average Precision (sAP$_{t}$) at squared-endpoint-distance thresholds $t \in \{5, 10, 15\}$ in the 128x128 output space, with sAP$_{10}$ as the primary metric.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings regarding the design and performance of line segment detectors under strict memory constraints:

**A. Output Representation Comparison (Section IV-A)**
- **Heatmap (v1):** Performed poorly with sAP$_{10}$ = 0.3, indicating its inadequacy for directly generating discrete line segments without complex external linking.
- **Center-with-Displacement (v2):** Achieved sAP$_{10}$ = 3.6, demonstrating an improvement over the heatmap but still exhibiting limitations in recovering coherent geometry.
- **F-Clip (v6):** Demonstrated the most effective performance at small model sizes, reaching sAP$_{10}$ = 10.6 for a 25k-parameter model with a 0.25 MB activation footprint. This result indicated that the geometric encoding itself, rather than merely model size, was a dominant factor in accuracy for this memory regime. The F-Clip representation was adopted for MiLSD.

**B. Quantization Study (Section IV-B)**
- **8-bit (int8) Quantization:** Preserved full-precision performance with negligible accuracy loss. The deployed F-Clip model's sAP$_{10}$ decreased from 10.6 (fp32) to 10.0 (int8). This indicated int8 arithmetic is suitable for on-device deployment without significant degradation.
- **4-bit (int4) Quantization:**
 - **Post-training quantization (PTQ):** Resulted in catastrophic performance degradation, reducing sAP$_{10}$ to 0.7.
 - **Quantization-aware training (QAT):** Partially recovered performance to sAP$_{10}$ = 6.9, about 60% of the fp32 baseline.
- **Sensitivity Analysis:** The primary source of degradation at 4-bit quantization was identified in the (cos 2$\theta$, sin 2$\theta$) angle regression branch, attributed to its inherently narrow dynamic range being poorly represented by the coarse 4-bit grid.

**C. Resolution–Memory Trade-off (Section IV-C)**
- **Optimal Operating Point:** For the 25k-parameter model, structural accuracy (sAP$_{10}$) improved with increasing input resolution up to 256x256 pixels, where the 128x128 output grid aligned with the native label resolution. Beyond 256x256, accuracy saturated, while peak SRAM consumption continued to increase, crossing the 320 KB budget of the STM32F746. Thus, 256x256 input was determined as the optimal balance for the F746 target.

**D. Capacity and Overfitting (Section IV-D)**
- **Capacity Limit:** Increasing the backbone's parameter count from 25k to ~209k showed minimal reduction in held-out loss (from 1.74 to 1.73). This suggested that, in this extreme resource-constrained regime, parameter capacity was not the primary bottleneck, and the model was operating near the information-theoretic limits imposed by the dataset and input resolution.
- **No Overfitting:** The 25k-parameter model exhibited no overfitting, with training and held-out loss trajectories tracking closely over 300 epochs, indicating good regularization through architectural constraints.

**E. MiLSD Performance on STM32H7 (Section V)**
- **Capacity Scaling:** By leveraging the 1 MB SRAM of the STM32H7, the backbone was scaled to approximately 0.39 million parameters. This alone improved sAP$_{10}$ from 10.6 (25k params on F746) to 17.8.
- **Inference-Time Enhancements:**
 - **Sub-pixel decoding:** Increased sAP$_{10}$ from 17.8 to 18.1.
 - **Test-Time Augmentation (TTA x4):** Further improved sAP$_{10}$ from 18.1 to 21.0.
 - **Line-of-Interest (LoI) verification head:** Contributed the largest incremental gain, pushing sAP$_{10}$ from 21.0 to 24.1. The oracle (perfect verifier) achieved sAP$_{10}$ = 37.4, indicating the LoI head recovered approximately 55% of the remaining potential.
- **Final MiLSD Performance:** Achieved sAP$_{10}$ = 24.1 on ShanghaiTech Wireframe within a 1 MB activation budget on the STM32H7.

**F. Comparison with Prior Work (Section IV-E)**
- MiLSD (24.1 sAP$_{10}$, 0.39M parameters, ~1MB) and the F-Clip baseline (10.6 sAP$_{10}$, 0.025M parameters, 0.25MB) occupy a distinct region at the extreme low-resource end of the accuracy–parameter spectrum.
- State-of-the-art GPU-class parsers achieve much higher sAP$_{10}$ values (e.g., DT-LSD 71.7, HAWPv2 69.7) but utilize 24x to 1600x more parameters and significantly larger memory footprints (e.g., M-LSD-tiny at ≥78 MB runtime memory). This work represents the first learned line segment detector specifically designed and evaluated under sub-megabyte MCU SRAM budgets.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a notable advancement in the field of embedded computer vision by demonstrating the feasibility and establishing an accuracy baseline for learned line segment detection on highly resource-constrained microcontrollers. Its significance and potential impact are multifaceted:

1. **Enabling New Edge AI Applications:** By providing a learned line segment detector capable of operating within sub-megabyte SRAM budgets, MiLSD directly addresses a critical gap in TinyML. This enables the deployment of more robust and accurate structural perception capabilities on low-cost, low-power embedded hardware previously limited to simpler, classical methods. This can impact various domains, including:
 - **Industrial Inspection:** Real-time quality control and defect detection on manufacturing lines.
 - **Robotics:** Enhanced navigation, object manipulation, and environment understanding for compact robots and drones.
 - **Smart IoT Devices:** Contextual awareness for devices with limited computational resources.
 - **Automotive Sub-components:** Specific tasks within ADAS or in-cabin monitoring where highly efficient vision is required.

2. **Systematic Characterization of Accuracy–Resource Trade-offs:** The work provides a systematic empirical study of how different design choices—output representations, quantization bit-widths, and inference enhancements—impact accuracy and memory usage within the extreme constraints of MCUs. This characterization is a key contribution to the TinyML community, offering:
 - **Guidance for Future Designs:** The finding that F-Clip's center-with-length-and-angle representation is most effective at small model sizes, and the identification of angle regression's sensitivity to low-bit quantization, offer valuable insights for designing other geometric vision tasks for TinyML.
 - **Benchmarking and Frontiers:** The established accuracy–resource frontier for learned line detection on MCUs serves as a benchmark for future research aiming to further optimize performance under these constraints.

3. **Practical Deployment Demonstrated:** The development of MiLSD, with its full training and deployment pipeline (including int8 quantization via X-CUBE-AI for STM32 microcontrollers), showcases a practical and deployable solution. The sequential improvements from the capacity-scaled backbone to sub-pixel decoding, test-time augmentation, and the learned Line-of-Interest verification head demonstrate effective strategies for maximizing accuracy within a fixed memory envelope.

4. **Challenging Previous Assumptions:** The research clarifies that the "memory is the bottleneck" premise for TinyML is genuinely about peak activation memory, not just parameter count. It also indicates that, in this specific memory regime, the choice of output representation can be a more significant factor than raw model capacity for initial performance gains.

While the accuracy of MiLSD remains below that of GPU-class parsers, this is an acknowledged and expected consequence of operating within a vastly different memory and computational budget. The work does not aim to close this gap but rather to define what is achievable *within* these strict limits, opening up new avenues for efficient, learned structural perception in embedded systems.
