# In-Context Imitation Learning via Next-Token Prediction

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from UC Berkeley and Autodesk Research developed the In-Context Robot Transformer (ICRT), a framework that allows a real robot to perform novel manipulation tasks solely from human teleoperated demonstrations provided as prompts during inference. ICRT achieved an average success rate of 79.2% on previously unseen pick-and-place and poking tasks, significantly outperforming state-of-the-art robot foundation models without requiring any fine-tuning.

## Problem

- Existing robot foundation models often require additional training or fine-tuning to adapt to unseen tasks or novel environment configurations, limiting their real-world applicability.
- Current few-shot imitation learning approaches in robotics typically involve complex loss functions, prior knowledge, or specific perception modules, hindering simple, general adaptation.
- A need exists for robots to rapidly learn new skills from one or a few demonstrations without parameter updates, mirroring the in-context learning success of large language and vision models.

## Method

- The **In-Context** ==Robot Transformer (ICRT)== frames in-context imitation learning as a next-token prediction problem for visuomotor trajectories, employing a causal transformer model with a frozen, pre-trained vision encoder.
- ICRT is trained on a unique multi-task dataset (ICRT-MT) where multiple actions are possible from the same initial observation, compelling the model to rely on provided prompt demonstrations to disambiguate the intended task.
- The training loss is exclusively computed for actions predicted *after* the prompt trajectories, encouraging the model to generate conditioned responses based on the examples rather than performing unconditional generation within the prompt itself.

## Results

- ICRT achieved an overall success rate of **79.2%** across unseen pick-and-place and poking tasks, substantially outperforming baselines like Octo (**9.2%**) and OpenVLA (**7.5%**).
- Ablation studies confirmed that the custom ICRT-MT dataset and the 'no prompt loss' strategy were critical for in-context learning, as training solely on the DROID dataset yielded **0%** success.
- ICRT demonstrated robustness to the number of prompt trajectories (1, 2, or 3) and showed compositional generalization to some conceptually similar, albeit unseen, action primitives.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Real-world in-context imitation learning is feasible for robot manipulation, allowing parameter-free adaptation to novel tasks through human teleoperated demonstrations at inference time.
> - Careful design of multi-task training datasets, where prompts are essential to distinguish tasks from identical initial states, is crucial for developing robust in-context learning capabilities in robotics.
> - Computing the loss only on predicted actions following the prompt is a key training strategy that fosters genuine in-context conditioning, enabling the model to effectively learn from demonstrations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated at the intersection of **robot learning, imitation learning, and foundation models**, drawing significant inspiration from recent advancements in large language models (LLMs) and large vision models (LVMs). The broader landscape of robot learning has seen remarkable progress with learning-based policies, often leveraging techniques from vision and language domains. A key trend has been the formulation of robot learning problems as "next-token prediction" tasks, mirroring the success of transformers in LLMs and LVMs. This has led to the development of powerful multi-task robot policies, often trained on large-scale datasets, such as RT-1, RT-2, Octo, and OpenVLA.

However, a critical challenge remains: despite extensive pre-training, these state-of-the-art robot foundation models often require **additional training or fine-tuning** to adapt to *unseen tasks* or *novel environment configurations*. This "re-training" overhead limits their applicability in dynamic real-world scenarios where rapid adaptation is crucial.

This paper addresses this gap by exploring **in-context learning** in robotics. In-context learning, as pioneered by LLMs (e.g., GPT-3/4) and LVMs, allows a model to rapidly infer and execute new tasks by simply providing examples (prompts) at inference time, without modifying its underlying parameters. The authors argue that this capability, largely unexplored in real-robot settings, is crucial for developing truly generalist robots.

The work differentiates itself from existing few-shot imitation learning and meta-learning approaches, which often focus on parameter-efficient fine-tuning or require complex loss functions, prior knowledge, or specific perception modules (e.g., object segmentation). For example, previous works like Keypoint Action Tokens or Vid2Robot utilize auxiliary losses or specialized representations. Similarly, works like One-Shot Imitation Learning and Prompting Decision Transformer showed promise in simulation but often rely on full state information or known reward functions, which are hard to obtain in real-world robot manipulation.

ICRT distinguishes itself by:
- **Operating directly on raw sensorimotor trajectories for continuous control**, avoiding complex auxiliary losses or explicit perception modules.
- **Employing a significantly longer context window**, enabling it to learn from multiple demonstration trajectories.
- **Demonstrating in-context learning on a *real robot*** using image observations, overcoming the simulation-to-reality gap that many prior works faced.
- **Emphasizing the importance of specific dataset properties** (multi-task environments where the prompt is necessary to disambiguate tasks) for fostering in-context learning.

By bringing the powerful paradigm of in-context learning from language and vision to real-world robot manipulation, this work pushes the boundaries towards more adaptable and versatile robot agents that can learn new skills on the fly from human demonstrations, without the need for cumbersome re-training cycles.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to **enable in-context learning capabilities for next-token prediction models in robotics**, specifically allowing a robot to perform novel tasks by simply *prompting* it with human teleoperation demonstration examples, without requiring any fine-tuning of the model's parameters.

This objective is motivated by several critical challenges and observations in current robot learning paradigms:

- **Limitations of Existing Robot Foundation Models:** While existing robot foundation models (e.g., Octo, OpenVLA, RT-1/2) have made strides in multi-task learning and generalization, they still struggle with truly novel tasks or environment configurations without additional training or extensive fine-tuning. This necessity for re-training is a bottleneck for real-world deployment, adding complexity and slowing down the adaptation process.
- **Inspiration from LLMs and LVMs:** The groundbreaking success of large language models and large vision models in performing "in-context learning" – rapidly adapting to and recognizing tasks from prompts at inference time – presents a compelling paradigm. The authors hypothesize that this capability, inherent to next-token prediction architectures, could be extended to robotics.
- **Need for Flexible, Training-Free Adaptation:** An ideal robot system should be able to learn a new task from just one or a few demonstrations and execute it immediately. Current methods fall short of this ideal, demanding engineers to collect new data, perform re-training, or design complex reward functions for each new variation.
- **Simplification of Robot Learning Frameworks:** Many existing few-shot or in-context imitation learning approaches in robotics involve complex loss functions, prior knowledge, or the identification of key points/frames. The authors aim for a simpler framework that operates directly on raw robot trajectories, making the approach more general and less reliant on domain-specific engineering.
- **The Crucial Role of Multi-Task Data:** The paper explicitly motivates the need for specialized multi-task datasets where multiple tasks can be performed from the *same initial observation*. This dataset structure compels the model to rely on the provided prompt to disambiguate the intended task, preventing it from learning "shortcut" solutions based solely on the current visual scene.

In essence, the paper seeks to bridge the gap between the remarkable generalization capabilities of large foundation models in other domains and the current state of robotics, striving for robots that can learn and adapt with human-like efficiency through demonstration-based prompting.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed approach, **In-Context Robot Transformer (ICRT)**, frames in-context imitation learning as a next-token prediction problem for sensorimotor trajectories.

**A. Data Formulation:**
ICRT is trained on a dataset `D` of visuomotor trajectories `T`. Each trajectory is a sequence of camera images (`i_t`), proprioceptive robot states (`s_t`), and actions (`a_t`).
- **Proprioception:** Absolute end-effector pose (translation, 6DoF rotation, continuous gripper action) – 10 dimensions.
- **Actions:** Delta end-effector pose (relative translation, 6DoF rotation, continuous gripper action) – 10 dimensions.
The dataset is structured such that trajectories are grouped by task.

The authors use two datasets:
1. **DROID:** A large-scale real-world robot manipulation dataset (76k demonstrations). A subset of 10k demonstrations (filtered for length) is used for pre-training. Demonstrations are grouped by CLIP text embedding cosine similarity to identify tasks, ensuring at least 4 trajectories per task group to allow for prompting within tasks.
2. **ICRT-Multi-Task (ICRT-MT):** A manually collected dataset (1098 trajectories, 29 tasks, 6 primitives) using the DROID setup. Crucially, each environment in ICRT-MT is set up with *more than two possible tasks* for the current observation. This forces the model to learn to distinguish tasks based on the prompt, preventing shortcut learning.

**Training Data Preparation:**
- Vision augmentations (brightness, contrast, random crops/scaling) and proprioception noise are applied.
- For each epoch, trajectories from the same task are shuffled and concatenated to form long training sequences.
- A subsequence of length `L = 512` is sampled. The first `k` trajectories (at least one complete trajectory) within this subsequence are randomly designated as the **prompt**. This encourages the model to learn inter-trajectory patterns.

**B. Model Architecture (ICRT):**
ICRT consists of three main components:
1. **Vision Encoder:** A pre-trained ViT-Base (Vision Transformer) is used to process multi-view image observations. To mitigate domain shift (robot images vs. ImageNet), it's pre-trained on an equal mix of ImageNet and Open X-Embodiment data using CrossMAE. This encoder is *frozen* during ICRT training for efficiency.
2. **Modality-Specific Projectors:** MLPs project the robot's proprioceptive state and actions into a shared latent space. For observations, attention pooling is used to combine visual tokens from each camera view and the proprioception embedding into a single state token. Action tokens are similarly embedded with an MLP.
3. **Transformer Model:** A causal transformer backbone, based on the Llama2 design (12 layers, 768 latent dimension for Llama2-Base), takes the sequence of encoded state and action tokens. It autoregressively predicts the next series of tokens. MLP decoders are attached to the transformer's last layer to produce proprioceptive state and action outputs. ICRT predicts the next `h=16` actions at each time step, with temporal ensembling used for execution.

**Model Initialization:**
- **Default ICRT:** Randomly initialized Llama2-Base model, pre-trained on DROID, then fine-tuned on ICRT-MT.
- **ICRT-Llama2:** Uses a pre-trained Llama2-7B language model as the transformer backbone, fine-tuned on ICRT-MT with LoRA (rank 32) to align multimodal inputs.

**C. Loss Function:**
- The model uses an **L1-loss** for action prediction.
- Crucially, the loss is *only computed for actions after the prompt trajectories*. This design, inspired by multi-turn conversation chatbots, encourages the model to generate responses (actions) conditioned on the provided prompt rather than performing unconditional generation for the prompt itself, especially in multi-task scenarios.

**D. Inference:**
- At test time, one or more human-teleoperated demonstration trajectories (identical format to training data) are provided as prompts.
- The current image observations and robot proprioceptive state are also fed as input.
- The model predicts the next action, which the robot executes. This process iterates, using updated observations and states.
- **Efficiency:** The transformer utilizes a key-value (KV) caching mechanism, which stores previous outputs, reducing computational complexity from quadratic to linear with respect to sequence length. This enables real-time closed-loop control at 39.6 Hz for ICRT (10.7 Hz for ICRT-Llama2).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were conducted on a Franka Emika robot, focusing on evaluating ICRT's in-context learning capabilities for novel tasks.

**A. Experimental Setup:**
- **Primitives:** Two action primitives were tested: pick-and-place and poking.
- **Tasks:** Six *unseen* tasks per primitive were designed, covering both in-domain objects (e.g., yellow cube) and unseen objects (e.g., radish, blue sponge).
- **Difficulty Tiers:** Each task had five difficulty tiers, varying the number of distractor objects or placement positions, to assess generalization robustness.
- **Prompt Generation:** 3 human-teleoperated demonstrations were collected per task (with varying distractors). During testing, a random demonstration was chosen as the prompt. Crucially, the *environment setup at test time differed from the prompt's setup*, ensuring the model learned task-relevant information rather than simply copying actions.

**B. Comparison with Baselines (Table I):**
ICRT was compared against:
- **Goal-conditioned policy:** A standard imitation learning setup where a goal observation is prepended.
- **Octo:** A state-of-the-art goal-image and language-conditioned policy.
- **OpenVLA:** A state-of-the-art language-conditioned multi-task imitation learning policy (fine-tuned with action chunking).

**Key Findings:**
- **ICRT significantly outperformed all baselines.**
 - **Pick-and-place:** ICRT achieved 65.0% success (±7.3%), vastly outperforming Goal-Conditioned (33.3% ±6.5%), Octo (5.0% ±2.7%), and OpenVLA (11.7% ±4.6%).
 - **Poking:** ICRT achieved an even higher 93.3% success (±4.6%), while baselines struggled significantly (Goal-Conditioned 6.7% ±4.6%, Octo 13.3% ±6.2%, OpenVLA 3.3% ±3.3%).
 - **Overall Average:** ICRT achieved 79.2% (±4.6%) across all tasks, compared to 20.0%, 9.2%, and 7.5% for the baselines.

- **Baseline Limitations:**
 - Goal-conditioned policies struggled with distractors and when the goal state was ambiguous for the action (e.g., poking).
 - Octo and OpenVLA had difficulty identifying the correct object to interact with or the correct placement, and sometimes performed the wrong task primitive, suggesting they require more extensive demonstrations or different conditioning for generalization to unseen task variations.

- **ICRT's Strengths:** ICRT demonstrated strong ability to identify the correct task primitive and target object based on the prompt, even in environments with distractors and differing from the prompt's configuration.

**C. Ablation Studies (Table II):**
- **Model Initialization (ICRT-Llama2 vs. ICRT):** Initializing with Llama2-7B (ICRT-Llama2) resulted in lower performance (58.3%) compared to the randomly initialized Llama2-Base (ICRT) (79.2%). This was attributed to the significantly lower inference frequency of the larger model (10.7 Hz vs. 39.6 Hz), highlighting the importance of real-time control.
- **Training Dataset (ICRT (DROID) vs. ICRT (MT) vs. ICRT):**
 - Training only on DROID (ICRT (DROID)) resulted in 0% success, emphasizing that DROID's structure alone is insufficient for developing in-context learning.
 - Training only on ICRT-MT (ICRT (MT)) achieved 73.3% success, demonstrating the crucial role of the ICRT-MT dataset's multi-task, prompt-dependent structure.
 - Pre-training on DROID before fine-tuning on ICRT-MT (default ICRT) yielded the best overall performance, suggesting that diverse pre-training helps align visual features and control.
- **No Prompt Loss (ICRT +Prompt Loss vs. ICRT):** Calculating loss on the prompt trajectories (ICRT +Prompt Loss) drastically reduced performance (22.5%) compared to only computing loss on predictions *after* the prompt (ICRT) (79.2%). This supports the hypothesis that ignoring prompt loss encourages the model to pay attention to and learn from the prompt context.

**D. Supplementary Ablations:**
- **Repeatability (Table III):** ICRT showed low standard deviation in success rates (e.g., 60% ±0.5% for pick-and-place, 88% ±3.2% for poking), indicating reliable performance.
- **Prompt Trajectories (Table IV):** ICRT was largely insensitive to the type or number of prompt trajectories (1, 2, or 3 prompts), suggesting robust learning from various demonstration examples.
- **Unseen Primitives (Table V):** ICRT showed decent generalization (40-80% success) to some *unseen but similar* action primitives (e.g., "grasp and drop," "put A to right of B"), suggesting a degree of compositional generalization.
- **Co-training (Table VI):** Training on DROID and ICRT-MT simultaneously led to much worse performance (6.7%) compared to the two-stage approach, indicating challenges in balancing diverse datasets and ensuring effective prompt-conditioned learning.

**E. Failure Modes:**
Despite its strong performance, ICRT exhibited some failure modes, such as missing the grasp, grasping the wrong object (especially when colors were similar but shapes different), or placing objects incorrectly. This implies potential areas for improvement, such as further fine-tuning of the vision encoder.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "In-Context Imitation Learning via Next-Token Prediction" paper presents a significant advancement towards building more adaptable and general-purpose robots, with several key significances and potential impacts:

1. **Demonstration of Real-World In-Context Learning:** This work provides the first compelling demonstration of LLM-style "in-context learning" in a real-world robot manipulation setting. By enabling a robot to understand and execute novel tasks from human teleoperated demonstrations at inference time, without any parameter updates, it marks a critical step towards bridging the gap between powerful AI models and practical robotics.
2. **Simplified Robot Deployment and Adaptation:** The ability to teach new tasks through mere demonstrations, without the need for additional training or fine-tuning, dramatically simplifies the workflow for deploying and adapting robots to new environments and tasks. This reduces engineering effort, accelerates deployment cycles, and makes robots more accessible to non-expert users, potentially democratizing robot programming.
3. **Foundation for Generalist Robot Policies:** By significantly outperforming state-of-the-art robot foundation models (Octo, OpenVLA) in generalization to unseen tasks, ICRT provides a robust framework for developing more generalist robot policies. The in-context learning paradigm allows these policies to leverage existing knowledge and quickly acquire new skills, moving beyond task-specific or environment-specific limitations.
4. **Emphasis on Dataset Design for In-Context Learning:** The paper highlights the crucial role of specific dataset characteristics—particularly multi-task environments where multiple actions are possible from the same observation, necessitating reliance on the prompt—in fostering in-context learning. This insight provides valuable guidance for future data collection efforts aimed at developing advanced robot intelligence.
5. **Open Science Contribution:** By making the code, checkpoints, and data openly available (icrt.dev), the authors enable reproducibility, foster further research, and accelerate the development of in-context robot learning across the community. This aligns with the principles of open science and can catalyze future innovations.
6. **Bridging Modalities:** ICRT effectively unifies vision, proprioception, and action within a single transformer framework, demonstrating the power of next-token prediction for complex sensorimotor control. This reinforces the broader trend of multimodal foundation models in AI.
7. **Future Research Directions:** While significant, the work also points to clear avenues for future research. Addressing generalization to *completely unseen action primitives*, adapting to *different robot morphologies*, and optimizing the *inference speed of larger models* (like ICRT-Llama2) are crucial next steps to unlock even broader capabilities.

In summary, ICRT represents a compelling paradigm shift in robot learning, moving towards robots that can adapt and learn on the fly from human examples, similar to how large language models comprehend new instructions. This work has the potential to significantly impact fields ranging from manufacturing and logistics to assistive robotics, making robots more autonomous, flexible, and capable in dynamic, real-world scenarios.
