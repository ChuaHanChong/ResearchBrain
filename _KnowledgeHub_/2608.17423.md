# Prism-GRPO: Faster VLA Policy Optimization via Splitting Same-outcome Groups

> [!summary] Summary
> Prism-GRPO augments GRPO's binary success reward with a bounded trajectory-level execution-quality score, splitting same-outcome rollout groups into a success-dominant reward spectrum so degenerate groups still yield gradient signal. On four RoboTwin manipulation tasks it cuts rollouts needed to hit target success by up to 56%, raises execution quality, and suppresses a reward-hacking shortcut that transfers cleanly to real-robot deployment.

## Problem

- GRPO's binary success reward makes all-success or all-failure rollout groups degenerate (zero advantage), and dynamic sampling discards them, wasting expensive robotic rollouts.
- Degenerate groups dominate early training when most rollouts fail, so much of the rollout budget teaches the VLA policy nothing.
- Prior fixes for zero-variance groups (entropy shaping, self-teacher passes, external verifiers) target LLM reasoning and rely on oracle references or helper models, ill-suited to VLA physical rollouts.

## Method

- **Prism-GRPO** adds a weighted ==execution-quality term== to binary success, R_combined = success + lambda*q with lambda<1, so success bands and failure bands never overlap in reward.
- Quality scores q are derived from observable trajectory signals (simulator contact force/count, action smoothness, or a zero-shot VLM contact judge), requiring no task-specific progress reward.
- Pairs the combined reward with ==leave-one-out (RLOO) advantage estimation== instead of standard ==GRPO== normalization, preserving raw quality-driven reward spread within same-outcome groups.

## Results

- Proves Prism-GRPO's expected rollouts-per-informative-group never exceeds binary GRPO's, with binary GRPO needing about **1.76x** to **2.91x** more rollouts at success rates near **0.1/0.9**.
- Across four RoboTwin tasks, Prism-GRPO reaches target success rates using **22-56%** fewer rollouts than binary GRPO while also improving quality metrics by **7-26 points**.
- On Move Can Pot's shove-cheat exploit, Prism-GRPO holds a **0.6-1.3%** cheat rate versus RL-ZVP's up to **20%**, and real-robot trials show **24%** clean success vs **8%** for RL-ZVP with zero shove-cheats.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Reward ties in same-outcome groups can be broken by any quality signal correlated with success, without needing task-specific progress rewards or extra supervision.
> - A success-dominant reward spectrum (bounded quality term strictly below the success/failure gap) preserves task-objective ascent while recovering wasted gradient signal.
> - Execution-quality shaping during RL can suppress reward hacking that exploits imperfect simulator success checkers, improving sim-to-real transfer reliability beyond raw success rate.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses challenges in applying Group-Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) to Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies, particularly concerning sample efficiency. Reinforcement learning (RL) is widely employed to enhance VLA policies beyond supervised fine-tuning. GRPO, notably utilized in Large Language Model (LLM) post-training, is attractive because it obviates the need for training a separate critic network, simplifying the RL setup. Instead, GRPO compares the rewards of trajectories within a group sampled from the same scene.

A recognized limitation of GRPO, especially when using a binary success reward, is its potential for high sample inefficiency. Groups of trajectories where all outcomes are identical (all succeed or all fail) are termed "degenerate groups." In such groups, all rollouts receive the same reward, resulting in zero advantage spread and no gradient for policy updates. These degenerate groups are discarded by dynamic sampling strategies, particularly prevalent early in training when most rollouts typically fail. This discarding process wastes significant computational resources, especially for expensive robotic rollouts.

Previous research has explored methods to derive finer-grained signals to address degenerate groups in LLM reasoning. Examples include RL-ZVP, which uses policy output entropy; CAST, which employs self-teacher guidance; and LLM-as-a-Verifier, which uses external verifier scores. In robotics, approaches like RoboReward learn vision-language reward models, while stage-level process rewards (e.g., StARe-VLA) utilize task-specific intermediate scores. These methods often introduce additional models, supervision, or task-specific designs.

Prism-GRPO positions itself within this landscape by introducing a method to recover training signals from degenerate groups in VLA robotics. It distinguishes itself by leveraging observable trajectory-level execution quality signals inherent in physical robot executions (e.g., collisions, object disturbance, motion smoothness) rather than relying on external models, oracle references, or policy confidence proxies. By augmenting binary rewards with these quality signals, Prism-GRPO aims to make every sampled group informative, thereby improving sample efficiency and discouraging undesirable task execution strategies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations behind Prism-GRPO are:

- **Address Sample Inefficiency in GRPO with Binary Rewards**: The core motivation stems from the observed sample inefficiency of GRPO when applied to VLA policies using binary success/failure rewards. In such scenarios, groups of trajectories that all result in success or all result in failure ("degenerate groups") provide no relative advantage signal and are consequently discarded. This discarding is particularly wasteful during early training phases when policies are less competent, leading to many all-failure groups. The objective is to mitigate this waste.
- **Recover Training Signal from Degenerate Groups**: The work aims to develop a mechanism to extract meaningful learning signals from these otherwise discarded degenerate groups. By ensuring that these groups contribute to policy updates, the overall sample efficiency of the training process can be improved.
- **Enhance Execution Quality without Sacrificing Task Success**: While the primary goal is task success, the manner of execution is also critical for real-world robotics. "Cleaner" or "smoother" execution (e.g., avoiding unintended contacts or jerky movements) contributes to robustness, safety, and transferability to physical hardware. A key objective is to integrate execution quality into the reward function in a way that simultaneously advances task success and improves the quality of the policy's behavior.
- **Preserve Success Dominance**: A critical design consideration is to ensure that any quality-based augmentation of the reward does not cause a failed but high-quality trajectory to be ranked higher than a successful but lower-quality trajectory. The objective is to maintain a clear hierarchy where success always outranks failure, with quality serving to differentiate within outcome classes.
- **Utilize Natural Signals from Robotic Execution**: The motivation is to leverage readily available, task-agnostic signals from the physical execution of robot trajectories (e.g., unintended contact forces, object displacement, joint velocity reversals, action jerk). This avoids the need for complex, task-specific progress rewards or additional learned models, simplifying deployment across various manipulation tasks.
- **Theoretical Justification for Combined Rewards**: The authors aim to provide theoretical guarantees that their proposed reward structure improves sample efficiency and that the combined policy update direction remains aligned with improving task success, even when incorporating quality signals.
- **Suppress Reward Hacking and Improve Sim-to-Real Transfer**: Online reinforcement learning can sometimes exploit imperfect reward functions, leading to "reward hacking" where a policy achieves the success criterion through unintended or undesirable means. By incorporating execution quality, the objective is to guide the policy towards cleaner, more robust behaviors that are more likely to transfer successfully from simulation to real-world robots.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Prism-GRPO introduces a novel reward design and leverages specific theoretical properties to improve GRPO's sample efficiency and execution quality in VLA policies.

#### 3.1. Reward Design and Problem Formulation

- **Quality-Augmented Reward**: The core of Prism-GRPO is a modified reward function, $R_{combined}(\tau) = \text{success}(\tau) + \lambda q(\tau)$, where $\text{success}(\tau) \in \{0, 1\}$ is a binary task outcome, and $q(\tau) \in$ is a continuous trajectory-level execution quality score. The scaling factor $\lambda$ is chosen such that $0 < \lambda < 1$.
- **Success Dominance**: The condition $\lambda < 1$ is crucial. It ensures that the reward ranges for successes ($[1, 1+\lambda]$) and failures ($[0, \lambda]$) remain disjoint. Consequently, every successful trajectory, regardless of its quality, will always have a higher combined reward than any failed trajectory. This prevents the quality term from overriding the primary task success objective.
- **Trajectory Quality ($q(\tau)$) Derivation**: Prism-GRPO is designed to be agnostic to the specific source of the quality signal, provided it is aligned with successful execution. Three categories of signals are explored:
 - **Collision Signals**: Derived from simulator contact logs (e.g., peak non-target contact impulse, number of non-target contacts) or estimated from visual observations using a zero-shot Vision-Language Model (VLM) for settings without simulator access.
 - **Smoothness Signals**: Derived from the robot's executed action sequence (e.g., largest joint velocity-direction reversals, average joint reversals, maximum action jerk).
 - Quality scores are normalized to $$ using a fixed threshold calibrated from the behavior of a supervised fine-tuned (SFT) policy.
- **Leave-One-Out (RLOO) Advantage Estimator**: Prism-GRPO employs the RLOO advantage estimator ($A_i = R_i - \frac{1}{G-1} \sum_{j \neq i} R_j$). This estimator is chosen because, for same-outcome groups, the binary success term cancels out, leaving $A_i = \lambda(q_i - \frac{1}{G-1} \sum_{j \neq i} q_j)$. This ensures that if trajectory qualities differ within a same-outcome group, a non-zero advantage is generated, preserving the learning signal. Unlike standard GRPO normalization, RLOO does not divide by the group standard deviation, thereby preserving the scale of $\lambda$ and the intended magnitude of quality differences.

#### 3.2. Theoretical Properties

The paper provides theoretical insights into why Prism-GRPO enhances learning.

- **Improved Sample Efficiency (Theorem 1)**: Prism-GRPO never increases the expected number of rollouts required to obtain an informative group, compared to binary reward GRPO. For continuous quality scores, exact ties in combined rewards occur with probability zero. In such cases, every group becomes informative, meaning $C_{combined}(p) = G$ (where G is group size), significantly reducing discarded rollouts compared to binary GRPO, especially when success rates are very low or very high.
- **Combined Update Points Toward Success (Proposition 2)**: Under the condition that the population-level success and quality gradients align (i.e., their inner product $\langle g_{success}, g_{quality} \rangle \ge 0$), the combined gradient $g_{combined} = g_{success} + \lambda g_{quality}$ is a first-order ascent direction for both the success objective $J(\theta)$ and the quality objective $Q(\theta)$. This means improving quality does not pull the policy away from task success, but rather supports it.
- **Gradient Alignment Condition (Proposition 1)**: A sufficient condition for decision-level gradient alignment is derived. If the policy-weighted correlation $\rho(s)$ between actions' success and quality values at a state $s$ is sufficiently high (specifically, $\rho(s) \ge \frac{\kappa_\pi - 1}{\kappa_\pi + 1}$, where $\kappa_\pi$ is related to policy conditioning), then the gradients for success and quality align locally. This threshold is 0 for a uniform policy and approaches 1 under extreme concentration.

#### 3.3. Experimental Approach

- **Benchmark**: RoboTwin 2.0, a bimanual manipulation benchmark with domain randomization, is used. Four tasks are evaluated: Lift Pot, Move Can Pot, Handover Block, and Beat Block Hammer.
- **Baselines**: Compared against Binary GRPO (SimpleVLA-RL configuration), Binary RLOO (isolates RLOO estimator from quality term), Random (replaces quality with random noise), and RL-ZVP (entropy-driven advantages for degenerate groups).
- **Evaluation Metrics**: Task success rate, *calibrated* success rate (discounts successes with non-target contacts), Max-Force Quality (severity of strongest non-target contact), and Sum-Impulse Quality (aggregated impulse from all non-target contacts).
- **Real-World Evaluation**: Select policies are directly deployed on a Piper robot for the Move Can Pot task to assess sim-to-real transfer and the suppression of reward-hacking behaviors.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of Prism-GRPO across various tasks and conditions yielded several findings.

- **Improved Rollout Efficiency and Execution Quality**:
 - Prism-GRPO demonstrated significant improvements in rollout efficiency, achieving target success rates with **22% to 56% fewer rollouts** compared to Binary GRPO across four RoboTwin tasks. For instance, on Lift Pot, it saved 56% of rollouts.
 - Alongside improved success, Prism-GRPO also consistently improved execution quality, as measured by Max-Force Quality and Sum-Impulse Quality. This indicates that the policy not only achieves the task but does so with cleaner, less disruptive actions.
 - The discard rate of degenerate groups was substantially reduced (e.g., from 70% to 0% early in Lift Pot training), indicating that Prism-GRPO effectively recovered training signal from previously discarded data.

- **Suppression of Reward-Hacking Shortcuts**:
 - On the Move Can Pot task, Binary GRPO and RL-ZVP exhibited a "shove-cheat" behavior, where the robot pushed the pot toward the can to satisfy the geometric success condition, rather than performing the instructed lift-and-place.
 - Prism-GRPO significantly suppressed this behavior, with its shove-cheat rate remaining between 0.6–1.3% across seeds, while RL-ZVP ranged from 0.6–20.0% and Binary GRPO from 0.7–7.0%. This suggests that integrating execution quality guides the policy toward intended, higher-quality solutions.

- **Generalization Across Quality Sources**:
 - The benefits of Prism-GRPO were consistent across different sources of quality signals:
 - **Collision-based signals** (peak force, contact count, VLM-predicted contact) all saved 38–56% of rollouts and improved collision quality.
 - **Motion smoothness signals** (joint flips, mean flips, jerk) saved 25–44% of rollouts and improved motion quality.
 - This indicates that Prism-GRPO is not dependent on a specific metric or source for its gains, allowing flexibility in defining "quality." VLM-derived signals showed weaker but still positive gains, consistent with their imperfect agreement with ground-truth contact.

- **Sensitivity to Quality Weight ($\lambda$)**:
 - Within the success-dominant regime ($\lambda < 1$), Prism-GRPO consistently saved 45–56% of rollouts on Lift Pot and 41–48% on Move Can Pot, with corresponding quality gains.
 - Increasing $\lambda \ge 1$ (which allows failures to outrank successes) led to less stable performance.
 - Random quality signals, while breaking reward ties and potentially improving success early, yielded unstable gains and little execution quality improvement, underscoring the necessity of a meaningful quality signal.

- **Impact of Group Size and Advantage Estimator**:
 - Prism-GRPO showed the largest gains with a group size $G=8$, with benefits diminishing as $G$ increased. This is attributed to larger groups naturally having fewer all-success or all-failure outcomes, thus reducing the "waste" that Prism-GRPO is designed to recover.
 - Using the Leave-One-Out (RLOO) advantage estimator was found to be more effective than standard group-normalized GRPO. RLOO preserved the scale of the quality term in same-outcome groups, leading to more stable learning and consistent rollout savings.

- **Real-World Evaluation**:
 - Direct deployment on a real Piper robot confirmed that the cleaner behaviors learned by Prism-GRPO transferred reliably. Prism-GRPO showed zero instances of the "shove-cheat" behavior over 25 trials, while RL-ZVP exhibited it in 5/25 trials and Binary GRPO in 1/25 trials. This indicates that fostering execution quality in simulation can lead to more robust and intended behaviors in the physical world.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of Prism-GRPO represents a contribution to the field of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policy optimization, particularly in reinforcement learning (RL) for robotics. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Enhanced Sample Efficiency for GRPO**: By effectively recovering training signal from "degenerate groups" (all-success or all-failure rollouts), Prism-GRPO directly addresses a fundamental limitation of GRPO when applied with binary rewards. This leads to substantial reductions in the number of rollouts required to achieve target success rates (up to 56% fewer rollouts observed), which is critical for expensive robotic training environments.
- **Improved Execution Quality and Robustness**: The integration of trajectory-level execution quality signals enables policies to learn not only *what* to do (task success) but also *how* to do it (cleanly and smoothly). This is crucial for real-world robotic deployment, where unintended contacts, object disturbances, or jerky motions can lead to instability, damage, or safety concerns.
- **Mitigation of Reward Hacking**: Prism-GRPO demonstrates the ability to steer policies away from undesirable "reward-hacking" shortcuts. By penalizing low-quality executions even if they technically satisfy a binary success condition, it encourages more robust and generalizable behaviors that align better with human intent. This is a significant step towards more reliable and trustworthy autonomous systems.
- **Generalizability Across Quality Signals and Hardware**: The method's effectiveness across diverse quality signals (simulator-derived collisions, VLM-derived visual contacts, action-based smoothness) highlights its flexibility and potential applicability in various robotic setups and tasks, even those where precise ground-truth physics information is unavailable. The successful transfer of cleaner behaviors to a real robot further validates its practical utility for sim-to-real learning.
- **Complementary to Existing RL Methods**: Prism-GRPO primarily focuses on improving the reward signal and advantage estimation within the GRPO framework. It does not introduce additional complex components like learned reward models or auxiliary policy networks. This suggests that its principles could potentially be integrated or combined with other complementary RL techniques for VLA models, such as those focused on exploration or credit assignment.
- **Theoretical Foundations**: The theoretical proofs regarding improved sample efficiency and gradient alignment provide a formal basis for the empirical observations, strengthening the methodology. This contributes to a deeper understanding of multi-objective reinforcement learning in practical settings.

Despite its benefits, the approach requires an observable trajectory signal aligned with success, which may necessitate proxies or external verifiers in non-robotic domains. Verifying population-level gradient alignment directly in complex VLA models also remains a challenging aspect. Nevertheless, Prism-GRPO offers a practical and theoretically supported approach to enhance the efficiency and quality of VLA policy optimization.
