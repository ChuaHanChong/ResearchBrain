# Symphony: A Heuristic Normalized Calibrated Advantage Actor and Critic Algorithm in application for Humanoid Robots

> [!summary] Summary
> The Symphony algorithm introduces an advantage-based Actor-Critic framework designed to balance sample efficiency, action safety, and harmonious movements for training humanoid robots from scratch. It yielded more stable and coordinated locomotion in the Humanoid-v4 environment, completing training in fewer episodes compared to other configurations while mitigating jerky movements and avoiding suboptimal policies.

## Problem

- Reconciling the conflicting goals of high sample efficiency and generating safe, harmonious robot movements in Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL).
- Mitigating risks of robot hardware damage and environmental hazards due to unstable or jerky actions, especially from noise accumulation at action limits.
- Addressing learning instabilities and a tendency to converge to suboptimal policies or exhibit frequent falls with existing off-policy DRL algorithms for complex agents.

## Method

- **Symphony** is a deterministic ==Actor-Critic== algorithm utilizing tailored Rectified Huber Symmetric/Asymmetric Error (ReHSE, ReHAE) loss functions and Rectified Sine (ReSine) activation functions for stable gradient steps and harmonic exploration.
- It incorporates a Fading Replay Buffer (FRB) with dynamic priority sampling to balance recent and diverse older experiences, and a 'Swaddling' regularization mechanism to control action strength and encourage gradual, safe learning.
- The architecture uses three Critic networks with sorted, weighted outputs, AdamW optimizer with custom parameters, and gradient dropout to enhance robustness and generalization.

## Results

- The Symphony-S2 configuration demonstrated superior stability and continuous performance improvement, exhibiting more coordinated and human-like movements using both hands and legs for balance in the Humanoid-v4 environment.
- Symphony-S2 agents completed 3 million training steps in fewer episodes (**8284.1** ± **753.7**) compared to other configurations, indicating improved policy longevity and maintained health within the environment.
- The algorithm successfully traded a faster initial convergence rate for enhanced stability and prevention of suboptimal policy convergence, with the 'Swaddling' regularization effectively controlling action strength as evidenced by the scaling factor's behavior.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A controlled, gradual learning approach ('Swaddling' regularization) that initially constrains action strength can lead to more stable, robust, and ultimately higher-performing policies, even if it trades off initial convergence speed.
> - Dynamically prioritizing samples within the replay buffer, giving relevance to both recent and diverse past experiences, is crucial for better generalization and reducing sample correlation in off-policy learning.
> - Customized loss functions (ReHSE, ReHAE) and harmonic activation functions (ReSine) can provide a more stable learning landscape, reducing prediction inaccuracies and fostering smoother, more coordinated motor control.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work operates within the field of Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL), specifically focusing on continuous control tasks for complex agents like humanoid robots. Training such robots from scratch, especially in real-world environments, presents significant challenges, primarily concerning sample efficiency, the safety of actions, and the generation of harmonious, non-jerky movements.

Existing DRL algorithms address these challenges with varying trade-offs. On-policy algorithms, such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), are known for producing stable and harmonious agent movements due to their use of small, "safe" gradient steps. However, PPO exhibits low sample efficiency, making it computationally expensive and less practical for real-world robot training where data collection is costly. Its effectiveness often relies on parallelization in simulated environments.

In contrast, off-policy, sample-efficient algorithms like Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) and its derivatives are frequently employed for real robot training due to their ability to learn from past experiences. While SAC offers higher sample efficiency, it can be susceptible to prediction inaccuracies in its Temporal Difference (TD) equation and may lead to unstable learning or sub-optimal policies. Recent advancements built upon SAC, such as Randomized Ensembled Double Q-learning (RedQ) and Aggressive Q-learning with Ensembles (AQE), aim to further increase sample efficiency by performing more updates per frame and by using ensembles of Q-networks to mitigate overestimation bias. However, these methods can introduce new issues, such as stagnation at local optima (when using `min(Q1, Q2)`) or increased instability and "optimistic" predictions leading to more falls in humanoid agents (when averaging Q values, as seen in Distributional SAC). Other approaches, like Reset, refresh network weights periodically to allow higher update frequencies.

The Symphony algorithm positions itself as an integrated approach that seeks to reconcile the conflicting goals of sample efficiency and the generation of safe, harmonious movements for humanoid robots. It attempts to combine the benefits of existing algorithms while introducing novel components to mitigate their respective drawbacks, particularly focusing on stability, safety, and human-like learning progression in the context of model-free, imitation-less training.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of the Symphony algorithm is to enable the training of humanoid robots from scratch using model-free and imitation-less reinforcement learning, with a focus on achieving a balance between Sample Efficiency, Sample Proximity, and Safety of Actions.

The motivations for this work stem from several challenges inherent in training complex robotic systems:

- **Reconciling Conflicting Goals:** The paper highlights the inherent conflict between achieving high sample efficiency (learning quickly from limited interactions) and ensuring harmonious, safe agent movements. Many algorithms that achieve high sample efficiency often do so at the cost of stability, leading to jerky movements, while algorithms that prioritize smooth motion are often sample-inefficient. Symphony aims to bridge this gap.
- **Addressing Robot Safety and Longevity:** The continuous increase in Gaussian noise, common in some stochastic algorithms, can be detrimental to robot hardware (motors, gearboxes). Furthermore, action clipping can lead to noise accumulation at action limits, increasing the frequency of maximum control values. The authors are motivated to develop a method that promotes safer action exploration and execution, thereby protecting the robot's mechanisms and the surrounding environment.
- **Mitigating Learning Instabilities:** Standard off-policy algorithms can suffer from prediction inaccuracies and instability. The paper aims to develop a more robust learning process that reduces these instabilities, particularly those arising from averaging Q values in distributional methods, which can lead to frequent falls in humanoid agents.
- **Emulating Gradual Human Learning:** The authors propose that humans learn gradually over time, often with initial constraints (e.g., "swaddling" infants, physical limitations of children). This perspective suggests that rapid, unconstrained learning in robots might not be ideal. The motivation is to introduce a regularization mechanism, termed "Swaddling," that encourages a restrained but stable development, allowing the agent to learn fundamental movements before attempting more complex or powerful actions. This is intended to improve the quality of learned policies and reduce initial instability.
- **Reducing Reliance on Pre-training/Imitation:** The explicit goal of "training from scratch with Sample Efficiency, Sample Proximity and Safety of Actions in mind" implies a motivation to reduce or eliminate the need for pre-training, expert demonstrations, or sophisticated reward engineering, thereby simplifying the deployment pipeline for real robots.

In essence, the motivation is to create a reinforcement learning algorithm specifically tailored for humanoid robots that prioritizes long-term stable and safe learning, even if it means a trade-off in initial convergence speed, to produce robust and human-like locomotion without external guidance.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Symphony algorithm is characterized as an advantage-based, transitional-policy, deterministic Actor-Critic algorithm that incorporates several novel and modified components to address the challenges of humanoid robot training.

**A. Core Algorithm Structure:**
Symphony employs a deterministic Actor that outputs continuous actions `a_t` with added limited Normal Gaussian noise `ϵ ∼ a_max * 1/e * N(0.0, 1.0)`. It uses an Actor-Critic architecture where the Actor and Critic networks are combined into a single object (class) to facilitate simultaneous updates. A Target Critic is used, updated via Polyak Averaging (`θ_T ← τθ + (1−τ)θ_T`). No Target Actor is utilized.

**B. Temporal Advantage (TA) and Loss Functions:**
The algorithm increases Temporal Advantage (TA), defined as the plain difference between a currently predicted target Q-value and an exponentially weighted moving average of target Q-values (`Q_T = α ¯Q_T-1 + (1−α)Q*_target`). A heuristic `α` value, specifically `1/ϕ ≈ 0.62` (reciprocal of the golden ratio), is used for smoothing.
The objective function for the Actor's update includes a term based on the normalized TA, `−ReHAE((Q_target − Q_T) / |Q_T|)`.
The Critic's loss is based on the Temporal Difference error: `ReHSE(br_t + γQ*_target − Q_online)`.
- **Rectified Huber Symmetric/Asymmetric Error (ReHSE, ReHAE):** These are modified loss functions. ReHSE `x tanh(x/2)` is used for the Critic, while ReHAE `|x| tanh(x/2)` is used for the Actor's Advantage term. These functions behave quadratically for small errors (dampening small deterministic gradients) and transition gradually towards linearity for larger errors, offering more stable learning than a purely quadratic loss while being less strict than a purely linear one. The division by 2 in the `tanh` argument is a customization to bring them closer to the Huber representation.
- **Normalized Rewards:** Rewards `r_t` are normalized (`br_t`) using `¯r_n`, the mean of absolute exploratory rewards collected during an initial `N_exp = 10,240` exploration steps.

**C. Critic Network Design:**
Instead of a small ensemble of Critics, Symphony uses three Critic networks, each outputting 128 nodes. These outputs are concatenated to form a 384-node distribution. For the Target Critic's output, this distribution is sorted, and values are multiplied by fixed weights (`w_n = tanh((π(1−i_n))^e) / Σ w_i_n`). These weights are inversely related to the Fading Replay Buffer probabilities, emphasizing certain parts of the Q-distribution.

**D. Fading Replay Buffer (FRB):**
The FRB is designed to address issues of sample correlation and generalization.
- **Priority Sampling:** It assigns sampling priorities `p` to transitions based on their normalized index `i_n` (age). A `tanh((πi_n)^e)` formula provides a smooth transition and a plateau-like shape for the latest experience, giving higher probability to both recent and older, diverse experiences.
- **Initialization:** The buffer is initially filled by repeating `N_exp = 10,240` exploration transitions 50 times, resulting in a capacity of 512,000. This ensures thorough processing of initial exploratory data.
- **Terminal State Handling:** When a terminal transition `br_t = r_done` is at the oldest placeholder in the buffer, a "shift left 2 positions" operation is performed instead of 1, preventing terminal points from being immediately overwritten. Additionally, near the artificial termination limit (e.g., 950/1000 steps), actions are deliberately zeroed, leaving only noise, to induce falls. This is intended to make the Actor-Critic less dependent on bootstrapping.
- **Memory Type:** It is described as an intermediate step between on-policy cache-type memory and off-policy experience replay, smoothing the relevance difference between past and current policies.
- **Optimization:** Later refined to use a circular buffer with a cyclic pointer and in-place operations for memory efficiency, and transitions are stored as `float16` and converted to `float32` on retrieval for the embedded devices (ED) baseline.

**E. "Swaddling" Regularization (Ω):**
This component aims to encourage controlled, less forceful movements without directly adding a control cost to actions.
- **Action Decoupling:** The Actor network outputs two independent parameters: `a` (actions) and `s` (a scaling factor vector, dimension of actions). `s` is processed through `tanh(x/2)`, and its absolute mean `σ` is used for action scaling.
- **Regularization of `σ`:** A barrier regularization using the inverse hyperbolic tangent (ATanh) is applied to `σ` in the form `Ω(σ^(1/β))`, where `β` is a temperature parameter. This function strives for a minimum of 0.0 and approaches `+inf` as `σ` approaches 1.0, discouraging `σ` from reaching its maximum.
- **Trainable `β`:** `β` can also be made trainable, with the Actor outputting `β` in addition to `a` and `s`. `β` is clipped and regularized using `2 ATanh(β^2)`.
- **Combined `Ωω` function:** A helper function `ω(x) = x ln(x)` (which has a minimum at `1/e`) is combined with `Ω` to ensure the scaling factor `σ` floats around `1/e`, aligning it with the parametric standard deviation of the noise. The full `Ωω` function is `Ω (x^(1/κ)) + κ ω(x) + Ω (κ^2)` (where `x` is `σ` and `κ` is `β`). This regularization is applied to the Actor's loss.

**F. Activation Functions:**
- **Rectified Sine (ReSine):** An internal Sine wave function is placed between two linear layers in both Actor and Critic networks, approximating a Fourier series `Asin(wx + b) + c`. This harmonic nature introduces exploration.
- **Stabilization:** These instabilities are rectified by a Swish (SiLU) activation function with a weight factor of 1.5, and further stabilized by proportional scaling `σ` (a trainable vector squashed by sigmoid) applied to the Sine function: `f(x) = σ sin(x/σ)`. The full ReSine function is `F(x) = f(x) sigmoid(1.5/σ f(x))`.

**G. Optimization and Generalization:**
- **Optimizer:** AdamW, is used with `α_lr = 1e-4` (or `1e-5` for `~S2`), `β1, β2 ≈ (0.62, 0.995)`, and `λ = 0.01`. Adam's bias correction is removed.
- **Layer Normalization:** Applied after the first fully connected layer.
- **Hidden Layer Size:** `h_dim = N_rb / 1000 = 512`. Each Critic outputs `h_dim / 4 = 128` nodes. Batch size `B = 384`.
- **Gradient Dropout:** Implemented after the Fully Connected Output Layer in Actor and Critic with a 50% probability (or random for `~S2`). This prevents gradient calculation for some nodes, `mask * x + (1 - mask) * x*` (where `x*` is detached).
- **Update-To-Data Ratio (G):** Set to 3, meaning three gradient updates per environmental step.
- **Computational Efficiency:** Random seeds are set at each step. PyTorch's Just-In-Time (JIT) compilation is recommended. The Replay Buffer is placed on the same device as network learning (GPU) to minimize data transfer overhead.

**H. Algorithm Configurations:**
The paper presents several configurations:
- **Symphony-S3 (Default):** Balanced configuration.
- **Symphony-SE (Sample-efficient):** Decreased noise level `ϵ ∼ a_max * 1/π * N(0.0, 1.0)`.
- **Symphony-S2 (Sample-Proximity and Safety):** Increased `N_exp = 20,480`, `N_rb = 25 N_exp = 512,000`, decreased `α_lr = 0.5e-4`, added Gradient Dropout.
- **Symphony-ED (Embedded Devices/Baseline):** Smaller `N_exp = 7680`, `N_rb = 384,000`, `h_dim = 384`, 96 Critic nodes, `B = 288`, and `float16` storage for efficiency.
- **Symphony-~S2 (Modified S2):** Further refined `S2` with cyclic buffer for FRB, new Gaussian bell-shaped weights for FRB and Target Critic, random dropout probabilities, different noise `1/e^2`, `α_lr = 1e-5`, and `N_rb = 1,024,000`.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

Experiments were conducted on the Humanoid-v4 OpenAI Gym (Mujoco) environment, with the action scale limit returned to `[-1, 1]`. Ten random seed experiments were run for Symphony-S3, Symphony-SE, Symphony-S2, and Symphony-ED (Baseline) over 3 million total steps, with an episode limit of 1000 steps.

- **Symphony-ED (Baseline):** This configuration, designed for embedded devices, showed performance comparable to the Soft Actor-Critic algorithm. It developed alternating leg movements, though the upper body remained relatively underdeveloped. It exhibited relatively low variance in its return.
- **Symphony-SE (Sample-Efficient):** This version developed a better gait compared to ED. However, it suffered from high volatility, particularly as the agent's speed increased. The authors suggest that decreasing the learning rate after some training duration might improve stability.
- **Symphony-S3 (Default):** Symphony-S3 showed better overall development throughout training compared to SE. Similar to SE, it experienced higher variance at increased speeds, leading to the same recommendation for learning rate adjustment.
- **Symphony-S2 (Sample-Proximity and Safety):** This configuration demonstrated improved stability and addressed many of the problems observed in the SE and S3 algorithms. It exhibited positive dynamics in its average return and, unlike the Baseline model which might hit a plateau, showed continuous improvement. The cost for this improved stability was a reduced initial sample efficiency.
- **Scaling Factor Behavior:** The analysis of the scaling factor (σ) over episodes showed that it initially varied significantly (least for Symphony-S2), then converged closer to `1/e` (the parametric standard deviation of the noise), before slowly increasing from that value. This behavior indicates that the "Swaddling" regularization effectively controls action strength.
- **Episode Completion and Return Thresholds:**
 - Symphony-S2 reached 3 million steps in fewer episodes (8284.1 ± 753.7) compared to other versions (ED: 11530.5 ± 1132.8, SE: 12433.5 ± 1055.0, S3: 11411.5 ± 966.0). This suggests that S2 agents maintained a healthy state and completed longer episodes more frequently, indicating better overall policy stability and longevity within the environment.
 - Despite completing fewer episodes, Symphony-S2 generally required more environmental steps to reach specific average return thresholds (e.g., 285k steps for a 5000 average return, compared to 227k for ED). This finding confirms the stated trade-off: Symphony-S2 sacrifices some initial sample efficiency (takes more steps to achieve a given return) in exchange for more robust, stable, and ultimately higher-performing policies.
- **Qualitative Observations:** Visual depictions suggested that Symphony-S2 enabled the agent to utilize both hands and legs for balance during training, indicating more coordinated and human-like movements.
- **Developmental Defects:** The authors acknowledge that even with their improvements, there is no guarantee of complete absence of "developmental defects," suggesting that environmental obstacles and gradual increase in difficulty might still be beneficial for further refinement.

The authors conclude that they successfully traded off a faster convergence rate for the sake of eliminating jerky motions and preventing convergence to suboptimal policies, achieving a balance between Sample Efficiency, Sample Proximity, and Safety of Actions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Symphony algorithm presents several significant contributions and potential impacts in the field of deep reinforcement learning for robotics:

- **Enhanced Robot Safety and Longevity:** By prioritizing the "Safety of Actions" through controlled action strength and mitigating issues like noise accumulation at action limits, Symphony has the potential to reduce wear and tear on robotic hardware and minimize risks to the surrounding environment during the learning process. This is a critical factor for the practical deployment of DRL in real-world robots.
- **Improved Movement Quality:** The explicit focus on "Sample Proximity" and generating "harmonious agent motion without jerky movements" aims to produce more natural, fluid, and human-like locomotion. This can enhance the perceptual quality of robot movements and improve interaction in human-robot collaboration scenarios.
- **Robust Learning for Humanoids:** The algorithm's integrated approach, combining novel components like Fading Replay Buffer, "Swaddling" regularization, ReSine activations, tailored loss functions, and gradient dropout, offers a more robust framework for training complex humanoid robots from scratch. This can lead to more stable learning dynamics and reduce the propensity for agents to fall into suboptimal or unstable gaits.
- **Reduced Dependency on Imitation Learning:** By targeting "model-free imitation-less" training, Symphony could decrease the reliance on human demonstrations or expert data, which are often expensive and time-consuming to acquire. This could streamline the development process for new robotic behaviors.
- **Adaptability to Resource-Constrained Devices:** The inclusion of the Symphony-ED configuration demonstrates consideration for embedded systems like NVIDIA Jetson series. This broadens the applicability of the algorithm to less computationally powerful, and often more cost-effective, robotic platforms, thereby expanding the reach of advanced reinforcement learning techniques.
- **Paradigm Shift in Learning Philosophy:** The implicit argument that "humans learn fast" is a misconception and that a "swaddling" regularization can lead to more stable development challenges conventional reinforcement learning approaches that often prioritize raw speed of convergence. This might inspire new perspectives on how to design learning curricula for robots, emphasizing gradual and safe skill acquisition.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The algorithm's complex interplay of components suggests a rich area for further investigation. The planned ablation study will be crucial in isolating the contributions of each element, which could lead to more refined algorithms and a deeper understanding of the mechanisms driving stable and efficient robot learning.
