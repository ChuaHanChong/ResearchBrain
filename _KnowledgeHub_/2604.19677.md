# Learning Hybrid-Control Policies for High-Precision In-Contact Manipulation Under Uncertainty

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Oregon State University developed a reinforcement learning framework to enable robots to learn and execute hybrid position-force control policies for high-precision, in-contact manipulation under uncertainty. This approach, named MATCH, demonstrated superior robustness to state estimation noise and achieved significantly lower applied forces compared to existing methods, successfully transferring from simulation to a physical robot without fine-tuning.

## Problem

- Real-world robotic manipulation tasks often require strict force constraints, which analytical methods struggle with under sensing noise and state uncertainty.
- Current RL methods for manipulation primarily use pose control or indirect force regulation, offering limited explicit control over contact forces and potentially leading to component damage.
- Fully integrating dynamic, discrete hybrid position-force control into model-free reinforcement learning is challenging due to complex mixed discrete-continuous action spaces and inefficient training.

## Method

- A ==reinforcement learning== framework was developed to allow policies to dynamically select between ==position and force control== for each end-effector dimension.
- **Mode-Aware Training for Contact Handling (MATCH)** was introduced to improve learning efficiency by ==propagating gradients only through the active control mode== actually utilized by the low-level hybrid controller.
- A ==Supervised Selection Loss (SSL)== was implemented to guide the policy towards appropriate force/position mode selection (force control in contact, position control in free space), fostering safer exploration.

## Results

- Hybrid-control policies, particularly MATCH, achieved a **97.0%** success rate with **0.0%** peg breaks in simulated fragile peg-in-hole tasks, outperforming pose-control and variable impedance control baselines.
- MATCH demonstrated superior robustness to increasing state estimation noise in simulation, maintaining up to **~10%** higher success rates and less than half the break rates compared to pose-control policies.
- Policies trained with MATCH successfully transferred directly to a physical Franka FR3 robot, showing up to **35%** higher success rates than pose-control and applying approximately **30%** less average force than variable impedance control.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly aligning the RL policy's action distribution with the underlying hybrid controller's behavior (MATCH) significantly enhances learning efficiency in mixed discrete-continuous action spaces.
> - Guiding the policy's control mode selection using ground-truth contact information (SSL) is critical for efficient exploration and robust, context-appropriate force control.
> - Learned hybrid position-force control policies can develop physically meaningful and adaptable strategies for contact-rich tasks under uncertainty, akin to expert-designed analytical approaches.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Many robotic manipulation tasks in real-world environments involve contact with strict force constraints. Examples include industrial assembly of delicate components, underwater welding, and medical procedures, where excessive or inconsistent forces can lead to damage or failure. Traditionally, these challenges have been addressed using analytical approaches such as hybrid position-force control, which allows for explicit control of forces in some dimensions while controlling position in others. These methods often require careful definition of constraint frames, system identification, and model parameter fitting, leading to systems that can be brittle and require re-derivation for new geometries or operating conditions.

Reinforcement learning (RL) has emerged as an alternative, demonstrating effectiveness in complex tasks without requiring explicit models. RL methods typically learn observation-to-action mappings that can generalize. Common RL approaches for manipulation often utilize simple kinematic action spaces, such as directly controlling end-effector pose. While these pose-based policies have shown success, they offer limited explicit control over contact forces, relying on low-level controllers to manage force interactions, which can result in unbounded forces during collisions or unexpected contact.

More recently, there has been increasing interest in incorporating force regulation into RL action spaces. Variable Impedance Control (VIC) allows learned policies to dynamically adjust the stiffness and damping parameters of a pose controller, thereby indirectly regulating forces. VIC methods have demonstrated improved performance, safety, and energy efficiency in contact-rich tasks. However, these methods still provide indirect force control. Direct force control, as offered by hybrid position-force control, has been long recognized in classical robotics literature as essential for contact-rich problems.

The integration of direct hybrid position-force control into model-free RL, where policies dynamically select control modes per dimension, has presented challenges. These challenges include the complexity of training in a combined discrete-continuous action space, ensuring controller stability, and achieving robust sim-to-real transfer. Prior attempts have often simplified this interaction, such as by pre-defining control modes or using continuous blending weights, which can limit policy expressiveness and adaptability.

This work addresses the gap by developing a framework that fully integrates dynamic, discrete hybrid position-force control into model-free reinforcement learning. It seeks to overcome the training inefficiencies associated with mixed discrete-continuous action spaces and demonstrate the benefits of direct force regulation for high-precision manipulation under uncertainty, thereby offering an alternative to impedance control-based RL methods.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and validate a reinforcement learning framework that enables robots to learn and execute hybrid position-force control policies for high-precision, in-contact manipulation tasks, particularly those involving fragile components and significant state estimation uncertainty.

This overarching objective is driven by several motivations and broken down into specific aims:

1. **Addressing Strict Force Constraints and Uncertainty:** Many real-world manipulation tasks, such as industrial assembly of delicate connectors or medical interventions, impose strict force constraints. Violating these constraints can lead to component damage or task failure. Analytical methods for precision planning often fail due to noisy sensing and uncertain state estimation, especially during contact engagement and disengagement. The motivation is to create robotic systems that can adapt robustly during execution and comply with environmental interactions, managing forces explicitly and safely.

2. **Overcoming Limitations of Existing RL Approaches:** Current RL-based manipulation policies predominantly use pose-control, which provides limited explicit control over forces and may lead to damaging actions without carefully tuned low-level controllers. While variable impedance control (VIC) offers indirect force regulation, direct force control is considered essential for critical contact-rich problems. The motivation is to leverage the expressiveness and safety benefits of direct hybrid force-position control within an RL framework, moving beyond indirect force regulation.

3. **Integrating Hybrid Control into Model-Free RL:** Despite the recognized advantages of hybrid position-force control in classical robotics, its full integration into model-free reinforcement learning, allowing for dynamic selection of control modes in each dimension, has been challenging. These challenges stem from the complexities of training in mixed discrete-continuous action spaces, issues with controller stability, and difficulties in achieving sim-to-real transfer. A key motivation is to devise a method that can successfully learn in this complex action space without prior models or manual mode pre-definition, thus enabling adaptable and context-aware control strategies.

4. **Improving Learning Efficiency in Complex Action Spaces:** Directly applying standard RL techniques to hybrid control's mixed discrete-continuous action space can lead to reduced sample efficiency due to credit assignment problems, where policy outputs for an unselected control mode still influence gradients. A specific objective is to develop a training methodology that improves learning efficiency for these complex policies, making them competitive with simpler action spaces like pose control or VIC.

5. **Ensuring Safe and Realistic Action Selection:** Force control in free space can be risky, potentially leading to unbounded accelerations and controller instability. Policies might avoid exploring force control if early failures are penalized. The motivation is to guide the policy towards safer and more realistic mode selections, encouraging the use of force control when in contact while discouraging it in free space, without relying on task-specific safety rewards.

6. **Demonstrating Real-World Applicability:** The ultimate motivation is to validate the developed hybrid control policies in realistic conditions, including varying levels of state estimation noise and through direct sim-to-real transfer without fine-tuning. This aims to demonstrate the practical viability and superior performance of these learned policies on a physical robot, showcasing their potential impact on industrial and delicate manipulation tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research focuses on learning hybrid position-force control policies within a reinforcement learning framework, specifically for contact-rich manipulation tasks under uncertainty. The methodology can be broken down into task definition, control architectures, a novel training approach, and experimental validation.

**Task Definition: Fragile Peg-in-Hole (fPiH)**
The Fragile Peg-in-Hole (fPiH) task is used as the primary case study. In this task, a robot manipulator inserts a peg into a hole without applying forces exceeding a defined threshold ($F_{th}$), which would cause the peg to break. The task is formalized as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP).

- **States:** The true physical configuration of the environment, including poses, velocities, forces of the manipulator, peg, and hole, alongside contact forces and a binary contact indicator per axis.
- **Observations:** Noisy estimates of the relative position between the end-effector and hole, hole orientation, end-effector velocity, end-effector forces, and the previous action. Noise is normally distributed (e.g., 1 N for force, 1 mm for hole pose).
- **Transitions:** The Factory simulation environment in IsaacLab, using a Franka FR3 robot, simulates physical interactions.
- **Rewards:** A dense reward function from prior work is utilized, providing incremental rewards for moving towards insertion, engaging with the hole, and task completion.
- **Actions:** Policy actions vary based on the control method (pose, force, or hybrid).
- **Policy:** The objective is to learn a policy $\pi_{\theta}: O \rightarrow P(A)$ that maximizes the expected discounted cumulative reward. Low-level control laws execute at 120 Hz, while the policy acts at 15 Hz.

**Control Architectures Compared:**
1. **Pose Control (Baseline):** Policies output 6-dimensional end-effector pose goals ($x_d$) (xyz position, RPY rotation) that are fed to a standard proportional-derivative (PD) pose controller (Eq. 3). The desired pose components are sampled from scaled tanh-transformed Gaussians (Eq. 4). This method can lead to unbounded forces during collision.
2. **Variable Impedance Control in End-effector Space (VICES):** Similar to Pose control, but the policy also learns to dynamically vary the proportional and derivative gains ($k_p, k_d$) of the pose controller, offering indirect force regulation.
3. **Hybrid Position-Force Control (Proposed):**
 - **Controller:** A hybrid position-force controller (Eq. 5) selects between position and force control for each of the 6 end-effector dimensions using a diagonal binary selection matrix $\Lambda$. If $\Lambda_{ii}=1$, position control is applied; if $\Lambda_{ii}=0$, force control is applied.
 - **Policy Outputs:** The neural policy outputs three components for each control dimension:
 - An observation-dependent probability $\phi(o_t)$ for selecting force control, sampled from a Bernoulli distribution (Eq. 6).
 - Pose target distributions ($x_{d_i}$) (similar to Eq. 4).
 - Force target distributions ($f_{d_i}$) (scaled tanh-transformed Gaussians, Eq. 7).
 - **"Hybrid-Basic" Formulation:** Initially, the action distribution $p(a)$ is formulated as a product of independent probabilities for pose, force, and selection for each dimension (Eq. 8), following standard mixed discrete-continuous RL approaches.

**Mode-Aware Training for Contact Handling (MATCH):**
Recognizing that the Hybrid-Basic formulation leads to inefficient learning due to uninformative gradients (as force targets are irrelevant if position control is selected, and vice-versa), the authors introduce MATCH.

- **Mode-Aware Action Distribution:** MATCH redefines the action distribution to explicitly reflect the controller's behavior. The per-control dimension distribution $c(a_i)$ is defined piecewise (Eq. 9), such that gradients are only propagated through the selection variable and the *corresponding* pose or force action that was actually utilized by the controller. This ensures correct credit assignment and makes training more efficient.

**Supervised Selection Loss (SSL):**
To address the challenges of exploring force control, particularly the risks of free-space force control and the sparsity of contact events early in training, a Supervised Selection Loss is introduced.

- **Mechanism:** This loss (Eq. 10) is a binary cross-entropy between the policy's predicted selection probability $p(\Lambda_{ii})$ and the ground-truth contact state $\psi_i$ for each dimension $i$.
- **Purpose:** It encourages the policy to select force control when actual contact is detected in that dimension and position control otherwise. This provides a consistent gradient signal that guides the policy towards safer and task-appropriate mode selection, promoting exploration of force control strategies when in contact. The loss is weighted by $\beta_{SSL}$.

**Training Details:**
- **Algorithm:** Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is used with an asymmetric actor-critic architecture.
- **Parallelism:** Training is conducted across 256 parallel simulation environments.
- **Network Architecture:** Simba architecture is used for both policy (2 residual blocks, latent size 256) and critic (3 residual blocks, latent size 1024).
- **Initialization:** Hybrid policies are initialized with a bias towards position control (~93%) to stabilize early training.
- **Evaluation:** Policies are trained for 3 million steps (5 seeds per condition), and the checkpoint with the highest training success rate is selected for evaluation.

**Experimental Setup and Ablations:**
- **Simulation Environment:** IsaacSim using IsaacLab, Franka FR3 robot.
- **Peg-Hole Geometry:** Circular cross-section, ~0.5 mm clearance, default break threshold $F_{th}=10$ N.
- **Noise:** Policies are trained with Gaussian noise on hole position (σ=1mm) and force (σ=1N).
- **Methods Compared:** Pose, VICES, Hybrid-Basic, Hybrid-MATCH, and ablations for Hybrid-Basic (no-SSL) and Hybrid-MATCH (no-SSL).
- **Sim-to-Real:** Selected policies are deployed on a physical Franka FR3 robot with additional dynamics randomization during training, without fine-tuning on the real robot. Real robot break threshold is 20 N.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides a detailed analysis of the proposed hybrid control policies, specifically MATCH, against baseline methods in both simulation and real-world experiments.

**A. Effectiveness of Hybrid Control Policies (Simulation - Nominal Conditions)**
In nominal simulated fragile peg-in-hole (fPiH) tasks, hybrid-control methods (Hybrid-Basic and MATCH) demonstrated higher success rates and lower peg break rates compared to pose-control (Pose) and variable impedance control (VICES).
- MATCH achieved a 97.0% success rate with 0.0% peg breaks, outperforming Pose (95.4% success, 1.6% breaks) and VICES (95.2% success, 1.4% breaks). Hybrid-Basic also showed high success (98.2%) and low breaks (0.4%).
- Hybrid methods exhibited higher average applied forces (2.7-3.0 N) compared to Pose (1.9 N) and VICES (2.0 N). This is hypothesized to aid consistent contact during alignment, which was observed in sim-to-real trials.

**B. Learning Efficiency**
Hybrid-control introduces a more complex action space. The study found that the uninformed Hybrid-Basic formulation required considerably more training steps to converge.
- MATCH, with its mode-aware action distribution, achieved comparable learning efficiency to the simpler Pose and VICES methods, converging at similar rates. This indicates that explicitly modeling the underlying hybrid controller's action utilization improves sample efficiency.

**C. Effect of Supervised Selection Loss (SSL)**
Ablation studies demonstrated that the Supervised Selection Loss (SSL) substantially contributed to both learning efficiency and overall performance of hybrid policies.
- Hybrid-Basic (no-SSL) and MATCH (no-SSL) converged to significantly lower success rates (87.0% and 82.0% respectively) and higher break rates (2.6% and 8.2% respectively) compared to their SSL-enabled counterparts.
- Policies trained without SSL made approximately three times less contact and had a force selection probability less than 0.1%, suggesting that SSL is crucial for encouraging exploration and appropriate use of force control during training.

**D. Learned Selection Policy of MATCH**
MATCH learned a physically meaningful and semantically interpretable control strategy:
- **Approach Phase:** During free-space movement, MATCH consistently predicted a near 0% probability of selecting force control.
- **Initial Contact Phase:** Upon contact, there was a pronounced spike in force control probability, particularly in the z-direction (downward axis), which persisted until insertion began. Concurrently, xy-plane control remained largely position-based. This strategy aligns with maintaining firm contact with the workpiece while maneuvering laterally for alignment.
- **Insertion Phase:** As insertion progressed, z-axis force control decreased, with position control increasing to regulate peg descent. This mirrors common manually designed analytical approaches like spiral search.
- **Contact Correspondence:** During evaluation, MATCH used force control in 84% (±10.8%) of in-contact steps across all directions, and only 2.9% (±2.1%) of free-space steps, indicating effective learning of contact detection and appropriate mode selection.

**E. Robustness to Noise (Simulation)**
Policies were evaluated under increasing levels of uniform hole localization noise.
- All methods showed degraded performance with higher noise, but hybrid policies (MATCH and Hybrid-Basic) maintained significantly higher success rates (up to ~10% higher than Pose) and substantially lower break rates (less than half of Pose).
- No significant difference was observed between VICES and hybrid methods in success or break rates under noise.
- Pose policies trained with higher noise distributions proved difficult to stabilize and often learned overly conservative behaviors.
- Qualitatively, Pose policies exhibited a jerky, frequently making-and-breaking-contact search strategy, while hybrid methods maintained consistent force on the hole and slid the peg tip to align.

**F. Performance on Unconstrained Tasks (Unbreakable Peg)**
When evaluated with unbreakable pegs, both MATCH and VICES demonstrated increased robustness and improved success rates compared to Pose under increasing hole localization noise.
- MATCH and VICES showed 17.1% and 21.1% higher success rates, respectively, over Pose in this setting, indicating that force regulation provides generally beneficial behaviors even when strict force constraints are absent.

**G. Sim-to-Real Experiments**
Policies trained in simulation (with dynamics randomization) were deployed directly to a Franka FR3 robot without fine-tuning.
- All policies transferred well in low-noise settings, with performance degrading as noise increased, consistent with simulation.
- MATCH consistently outperformed Pose in success rate, with substantial improvements (e.g., +35% in success rate over Pose at 7.5mm noise).
- MATCH achieved similar success rates as VICES but exerted approximately 30% less average force and substantially lower maximum forces.
- Pose exhibited chaotic search behavior, resulting in the only recorded peg break (1 out of 100 trials). Hybrid methods, including MATCH, maintained sustained contact. These results suggest MATCH as an effective alternative to VICES, offering explicit force control with reduced force exertion.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework for learning hybrid position-force control policies using reinforcement learning, addressing a long-standing challenge in contact-rich robotic manipulation. The findings have several implications for the field:

1. **Advancement in RL for Contact-Rich Tasks:** The work introduces the first framework to fully integrate dynamic, discrete hybrid position-force control into model-free reinforcement learning. This expands the capabilities of RL for tasks requiring explicit force regulation, offering a method to directly control forces and positions simultaneously and adaptively in complex environments.

2. **Improved Learning Efficiency for Complex Action Spaces:** The introduction of Mode-Aware Training for Contact Handling (MATCH) is significant. By aligning the policy's action probability distribution with the actual utilization of commands by the low-level hybrid controller, MATCH mitigates the credit assignment problem inherent in mixed discrete-continuous action spaces. This improves learning efficiency, making it comparable to simpler action spaces like pose control, thus enabling the practical application of more expressive hybrid control policies in RL.

3. **Enhanced Robustness and Safety:** The learned hybrid policies, particularly MATCH, demonstrate superior robustness to state estimation uncertainty and significantly lower peg breakage rates compared to pose-only policies. This is crucial for real-world applications involving delicate components or imprecise sensing. The Supervised Selection Loss (SSL) further contributes to safety by guiding the policy to use force control appropriately when in contact, while discouraging risky free-space force control, without needing task-specific safety rewards.

4. **Benefits of Direct Force Regulation:** The study positions hybrid control as a viable alternative to variable impedance control (VIC). While MATCH achieves comparable success rates to VICES, it consistently demonstrates lower average and maximum forces applied during tasks. This ability to directly and precisely regulate forces can be critical in applications where force limits are strict, and even indirect force control might lead to transient high forces.

5. **Practical Real-World Applicability:** The successful direct sim-to-real transfer of policies to a physical Franka FR3 robot, without any fine-tuning on the real hardware, highlights the practical utility of this approach. The performance gains observed in real-world experiments, especially under high noise, suggest that MATCH can be a robust and reliable solution for industrial assembly, medical robotics, and other high-precision manipulation tasks involving contact.

6. **Interpretable and Generalizable Control Strategies:** The analysis of learned behaviors reveals that MATCH converges to a physically meaningful and interpretable control strategy that dynamically adapts across task phases (e.g., free-space pose control, z-axis force control during contact search, xy-plane position control for alignment). This similarity to manually designed analytical approaches suggests that the learned policies are not merely task-specific but may represent a generalizable approach to in-contact manipulation under uncertainty.
