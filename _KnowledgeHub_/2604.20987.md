# Co-Evolving LLM Decision and Skill Bank Agents for Long-Horizon Tasks

> [!summary] Summary
> A multi-institution team introduced COS-PLAY, a framework enabling Large Language Model agents to continuously improve performance in complex, long-horizon tasks by autonomously discovering, refining, and reusing structured skills. The system demonstrated an average reward increase exceeding 25.1% in various single-player games against GPT-5.4, while maintaining strong performance in social reasoning tasks.

## Problem

- Building autonomous agents capable of continuous improvement through discovering, retaining, and reusing structured skills in complex, long-horizon environments.
- Current LLM agents often rely on curated human datasets or extensive external feedback for skill acquisition, limiting scalability and adaptability.
- Organizing, refining, and reliably retrieving skills across diverse and future tasks poses a challenge for existing agent architectures.

## Method

- The COS-PLAY framework introduces a multi-agent co-evolution system, featuring a Decision Agent (LLM) that generates trajectories and a Skill Bank Agent that converts these into reusable, structured skills.
- Skills are defined as reusable, temporally extended behavior abstractions with protocols including summaries, pre-conditions, plans, and success/abort criteria, stored in a dynamic, evolving skill bank.
- Both agents are optimized using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) with separate LoRA adapters, fostering a closed-loop co-evolution where improved skills enhance decision-making, and better rollouts refine skill learning.

## Results

- COS-PLAY achieved an average reward improvement of over **25.1%** compared to GPT-5.4 across four single-player games (2048, Candy Crush, Tetris, Super Mario Bros.).
- In multi-player social reasoning games, COS-PLAY attained a **39.0%** win rate in Avalon against GPT-5.4 opponents and outperformed GEMINI-3.1-PRO by **8.8%** in mean supply center count in Diplomacy.
- Ablation studies confirmed that the co-evolutionary loop, dynamically aligning the skill bank with the decision agent's policy, is essential for consistent performance gains, as neither skill banks nor GRPO alone were sufficient.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The tight coupling between the skill bank and the decision agent necessitates a co-evolutionary approach, where joint learning leads to a more harmonized and effective system.
> - Unsupervised trajectory decomposition and skill-bank refinement enable the continuous extraction, refinement, and reuse of skills directly from agent-environment interaction without substantial human supervision.
> - Learned skills function as compact and adaptable abstractions rather than memorized trajectories, which contributes to robust long-horizon control and efficient adaptation in complex environments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper addresses the challenge of building autonomous agents capable of continuous improvement through interaction, particularly by discovering, retaining, and reusing structured skills in complex, long-horizon environments. This area has a history spanning early reinforcement learning in games like Atari and AlphaZero to contemporary applications of Large Language Models (LLMs) in domains such as coding, web interaction, and embodied exploration. The authors identify a shift in the research focus: from merely acquiring useful skills to efficiently organizing, refining, and retrieving them for reliable reuse across diverse and future tasks.

Current approaches often conceptualize skills as an abstraction layer for LLM agents, distinct from simple tool calls. However, many existing game-playing agents still rely on curated human datasets or extensive training with external feedback. The authors position their work as a step towards addressing this by developing a framework that learns a co-evolving skill bank directly from agent-environment interaction, without substantial human supervision or external feedback.

The work builds upon existing research in game-playing agents and benchmarks, acknowledging the utility of games as testbeds for long-horizon decision-making under complex dynamics, delayed rewards, and planning requirements. It also relates to advancements in memory and skill-augmented self-improving agents, which leverage past experience and executable skills to improve performance in complex multimodal tasks. While previous works often focus on memory augmentation, skill transfer, or runtime skill retrieval within largely fixed pipelines, this paper distinguishes itself by investigating a "co-evolution" dynamic. In this paradigm, a decision agent and a skill bank mutually improve over time by automatically extracting, refining, and reusing skills from unlabeled trajectories to enhance downstream decision-making.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to develop autonomous agents that can improve their performance in complex, long-horizon tasks by discovering, retaining, and effectively reusing structured skills. The authors highlight a specific challenge: as agents operate in increasingly intricate environments, the difficulty lies not just in acquiring skills, but in organizing, refining, and retrieving them reliably across different tasks.

To address this, the paper proposes the maintenance of an "external skill bank"—a structured library of reusable behavioral protocols that agents can acquire, refine, and compose over time. The motivation for this approach stems from the observed tight coupling between the skill bank and the decision agent: a skill bank is only beneficial if the decision agent can select and execute its skills effectively, and the decision agent's capabilities are inherently limited by the quality and relevance of the skills available in its bank. This interdependence leads to two primary research questions:
1. **Should the skill bank and decision agent be learned jointly, rather than separately?** The hypothesis is that joint learning could lead to a more harmonized and effective system.
2. **What properties make a skill bank useful for improving decision-making?** This question probes the characteristics of skills and skill bank management that contribute to robust agent performance.

Games were chosen as the experimental environment to study these questions due to their inherent characteristics that facilitate co-evolution dynamics:
- **Long-horizon requirements:** Episodes are sufficiently extended to necessitate the sequential application of multiple skills.
- **Recurring subgoals:** This allows for meaningful skill reuse across different episodes.
- **Computational efficiency:** Rollouts are sufficiently inexpensive to support iterative refinement of the skill bank.
- **Cognitive demands:** Games demand long-context understanding, memory, and adaptive decision-making.

However, games also present challenges that existing approaches often struggle with, such as delayed rewards, the need for compositional strategies, and the scarcity of high-quality demonstrations or skill annotations. Consequently, a key motivation for this work is to develop a framework capable of learning and refining a skill bank directly from interaction, without relying on extensive curated human data or prolonged external feedback. The paper aims to contribute a novel multi-agent co-evolution framework that closes the loop between decision-making and skill learning, enabling an LLM decision agent to interact with the environment and collect trajectories, while a skill bank agent converts these rollouts into reusable skills that enhance future decision-making. A secondary objective is to provide a comprehensive evaluation of this framework across diverse game environments, assessing both task performance and skill reusability.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors introduce **COS-PLAY**, a multi-agent co-evolution framework designed for long-horizon video-game decision-making. This framework integrates unsupervised trajectory decomposition and skill-bank refinement through two main components: a Decision Agent and a Skill Bank Agent. The two agents are designed to improve each other in a closed loop, where the Decision Agent generates trajectories, and the Skill Bank Agent processes these into structured, reusable skills that inform future decision-making. Both agents are optimized using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) with separate LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) adapters to manage distinct trainable functions.

#### 3.1. Skill Definition and Management

A skill is defined as a reusable, temporally extended behavior abstraction derived from trajectory segments. Each skill is stored in a dynamic "skill bank" as a structured protocol comprising:
- **Summary:** Describes the skill's purpose.
- **Pre-condition:** Specifies applicability.
- **Plan:** Outlines execution steps.
- **Success/Abort Criteria:** Define termination conditions.
- **Contract:** Summarizes expected state changes.
The skill bank is not static but continuously updated and refined as new trajectory evidence becomes available.

#### 3.2. Skill-Augmented Decision Agent

The Decision Agent is an LLM-based component responsible for interacting with the game environment. Its operation involves:
1. **Skill Retrieval and Intention Update:** The agent maintains an active skill and current intention. Based on a state summary, it either continues the active skill or retrieves a new one from the skill bank. Skill retrieval is triggered if the current skill is unavailable, exhausted, or ineffective. Skills serve as structured context prompts. After each action, a natural-language intention tag is updated; sharp shifts in this tag can signal a subtask transition and prompt skill switching.
2. **Action Execution:** The agent executes primitive environment actions conditioned on the current state summary, task context, interaction history, intention, and the active skill plan. The skill guides multi-step behavior rather than replacing low-level control.
3. **Reward-based Learning:** The agent is trained to maximize cumulative reward using a composite reward function, which includes environmental feedback, a skill-following shaping term (bonuses for satisfying contract predicates, penalties for no progress), and a cost for unnecessary skill switching. The skill retrieval component is separately rewarded for selecting skills that lead to useful, contract-satisfying outcomes.

#### 3.3. Skill Bank Agent for Skill Discovery and Maintenance

The Skill Bank Agent is an LLM-based pipeline that converts unlabeled trajectories into reusable, protocol-based skills and learns their compact effect contracts. This agent operates through a four-stage pipeline:
1. **Boundary Proposal:** Identifies plausible skill transition points in a trajectory using local signals such as predicate flips, intention-tag changes, reward spikes, and transitions between primitive-action and skill-selection steps. High-scoring candidates are retained and merged to form a compact, high-recall set.
2. **Infer Segmentation:** From the candidate boundaries, the agent selects a subset that best explains the trajectory as a sequence of skill segments. Each segment's observed effects are compared against existing skills in the bank using their learned effect contracts. Segments matching existing skills with high confidence are labeled; others are marked as "new skill" for later evaluation.
3. **Contract Learning:** For each skill, the agent aggregates added and deleted predicates across its decoded segments to learn an effect contract. Only "consensus effects" that appear consistently are kept. An LLM-based summarization module can enrich contracts. Contracts are verified against observed state changes, and only those with high pass rates are written back to the bank, also providing a compatibility prior for segmentation.
4. **Skill Bank Maintenance:** This final stage involves five operations to evolve the skill bank:
 - **Materialize:** Promising new skill candidates from a buffer are added to the bank once sufficient evidence is accumulated.
 - **Refine:** Existing skill contracts are updated based on new verified evidence.
 - **Merge:** Redundant, near-duplicate skills with overlapping contracts are removed.
 - **Split:** Overly broad or inconsistent skills are marked for potential re-segmentation.
 - **Retire:** Skills that are no longer used or supported by recent trajectories are removed to maintain a compact and relevant bank. The Skill Bank Agent can approve or reject proposed mutations.

#### 3.4. Co-Evolution Framework

The co-evolution process is iterative and self-reinforcing:
1. The Decision Agent interacts with the environment to collect rollouts.
2. The Skill Bank Agent segments these rollouts into skill candidates and updates the skill bank.
3. GRPO is applied to update both agents' LoRA adapters.
This design ensures that better skills lead to improved decision-making, and better rollouts subsequently enhance skill learning. A Qwen3-8B model, fine-tuned on 60 seed trajectories generated by GPT-5.4, serves as the cold-start initialization for both agents. For social reasoning games, GPT-5-mini acts as an opponent during training.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents quantitative and qualitative results across six game environments, encompassing single-player puzzle/control tasks (2048, Candy Crush, Tetris, Super Mario Bros.) and multi-player social reasoning games (Avalon, Diplomacy).

#### 4.1. Performance Evaluation

- **Single-Player Games**: COS-PLAY, built on an 8B base model (Qwen3-8B), achieved an average reward improvement of over 25.1% compared to GPT-5.4 across the four single-player benchmarks. Specifically, it demonstrated higher average rewards in 2048 (1589.0 vs 1126.6), Tetris (510.9 vs 458.2), Candy Crush (648.8 vs 532.6), and Super Mario Bros (948.9 vs 752.0), leading to an overall average reward of 924.4 compared to GPT-5.4's 717.4.
- **Multi-Player Social Reasoning Games**: COS-PLAY exhibited competitive performance. In Avalon, it achieved a 39.0% win rate against GPT-5.4 opponents, comparable to GEMINI-3.1-PRO (42.0%) and GPT-OSS-120B (40.0%), despite using a substantially smaller backbone model. In Diplomacy, COS-PLAY outperformed GEMINI-3.1-PRO by 8.8%, achieving a mean supply center count of 2.96 compared to 2.72 for GEMINI-3.1-PRO (GPT-5.4 achieved 4.70 in self-play). The authors attribute this to structured state tracking and reusable skills providing an effective inductive bias for long-horizon social reasoning.
- **Efficiency**: The framework demonstrated few-shot adaptation capabilities, requiring at most 25 co-evolution iterations to reach strong performance in new benchmarks, which is fewer steps than often required by prior RL-based game agents.

#### 4.2. Ablation Study

Ablation experiments showed that neither skill banks nor reinforcement learning (GRPO) alone were sufficient for consistent performance gains. Variants lacking a skill bank, or those with skill banks that were not continually co-evolved and aligned with the policy, performed inconsistently or worse. For example, a Supervised Fine-Tuned (SFT) model without a skill bank improved action formatting but lacked long-horizon structure, while GRPO without a skill bank improved behavior but remained unstable under sparse rewards. The results indicate that the primary advantage of COS-PLAY stems from the co-evolutionary loop, which dynamically aligns the skill bank with the decision agent's evolving behavior and task distribution.

#### 4.3. Skill Reusability Analysis

The learned skills were found to be compact and adaptable abstractions rather than memorized trajectories.
- **Skill Bank Evolution (Diplomacy Case Study)**: Over training, the skill bank in Diplomacy demonstrated increased strategic diversity, including notable increases in skills related to phase transitions and territory loss, indicating a broader tactical repertoire. Despite the discovery of 121 total skills, periodic curation (merging, splitting, retiring) kept the active bank compact, typically between 55–70 skills.
- **Quantitative Metrics**: Metrics such as average instances per skill, Gini coefficient for reuse concentration, and average number of contract refinement versions per skill across different games further supported the claim that the framework learns reusable and adaptable skill abstractions.

#### 4.4. Generalization to General LLM Reasoning Tasks

Evaluation on standard LLM reasoning benchmarks (MMLU-Pro and Math-500) indicated that the adaptation process preserved the base model's general reasoning ability, with only small absolute performance drops (0.8% on MMLU-Pro and 1.8% on Math-500). This suggests that the performance gains in game environments did not come at the cost of significant reasoning degradation.

#### 4.5. Qualitative Analysis (Appendix I)

Qualitative case studies for Candy Crush and Diplomacy illustrated how the co-evolved skill bank influenced trajectory-level decisions.
- In Candy Crush, COS-PLAY utilized distinct "OPTIMIZE" (board preparation) and "CLEAR" (harvesting) skills, leading to strategic board restructuring and larger cascade rewards compared to GPT-5.4's more reactive, overlapping skill usage.
- In Diplomacy, COS-PLAY's skills imposed a clear temporal pipeline (e.g., EXPLORE → SETUP → DEFEND), guiding multi-phase planning and yielding steady territorial gains, whereas GPT-5.4 often stalled after initial aggressive moves or exhibited noisy, state-independent skill transitions.
- Failure analysis revealed that COS-PLAY tended to fail by "stagnation" (getting stuck in repetitive support loops) rather than the "collapse" (losing most centers rapidly) observed in GPT-5.4. Stagnation was identified as a safer failure mode, as the agent typically retained its initial centers.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework that demonstrates the viability and advantages of co-evolving LLM decision agents with dynamic, agent-managed skill banks for navigating long-horizon tasks. The core significance lies in its ability to address a critical challenge in autonomous agent development: enabling agents to discover, structure, refine, and reuse skills from unlabeled interactions without extensive human supervision or fixed skill repositories.

The findings indicate that this co-evolutionary approach can empower relatively smaller LLMs to achieve performance competitive with, or even superior to, much larger frontier LLMs in complex interactive environments. This suggests a pathway to more data-efficient and adaptable AI agents, potentially reducing the computational burden associated with training monolithic large models for every new task. By explicitly managing a skill bank that evolves alongside the agent's decision-making policy, the framework provides a mechanism for robust long-horizon control and efficient adaptation, particularly in settings characterized by delayed rewards, compositional strategies, and partial observability. The demonstrated reusability and adaptability of automatically extracted skills represent a step beyond simply augmenting agents with memory or fixed tool-use capabilities, moving towards systems that can genuinely "learn to learn" and continuously improve their strategic repertoire.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Enhanced LLM Agency:** Provides a method for LLMs to develop more consistent and strategic long-horizon behaviors, moving beyond single-step prompting.
- **Reduced Data Dependency:** The unsupervised skill discovery from unlabeled rollouts can alleviate the need for extensive human-curated demonstrations or explicit skill annotations, making agent training more scalable.
- **Foundation for Complex Environments:** The framework could serve as a foundation for developing agents in other complex, interactive domains, including robotics, embodied AI, and scientific discovery, where tasks require chaining multiple steps and adapting to dynamic conditions.
- **Efficiency in AI Development:** By enabling smaller models to perform well, it could democratize access to advanced AI agent capabilities, making them less reliant on the largest, most expensive models.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The authors acknowledge that a main limitation is the reliance on compact text-based state summaries. This can restrict the agent's ability to process raw visual observations, miss critical evidence in multi-modal inputs, or lose long temporal dependencies due to potential summarization errors. Future work aims to extend COS-PLAY to multi-modal interactive environments, allowing learned skills to integrate both interaction dynamics and visual reasoning. Another direction is to improve cross-domain transfer, enabling skills learned in one setting to generalize across different multi-modal games, agentic environments, and broader visual reasoning tasks.
