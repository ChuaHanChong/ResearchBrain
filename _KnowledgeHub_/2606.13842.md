# Efficient Domain-Adaptive Policy Learning via Kernel Representation with Application to Quadrotor Control under Non-Stationary Disturbances

> [!summary] Summary
> A quadrotor control method that represents unknown disturbances with a differentiable random-Fourier-feature kernel whose coefficients and bandwidth are both learnable. Offline, randomized kernel parameters generate diverse disturbance profiles and a policy is trained by backpropagation through a differentiable simulator in 50 seconds; online, least-squares updates both parameters each control step, cutting tracking error up to 70% in simulation and 30% on hardware.

## Problem

- Robust control assumes worst-case disturbances and stays conservative, while classical adaptive control is capped by the expressiveness of a fixed, pre-committed set of basis functions.
- Learning-based policies freeze their environment encoder after offline training, so they cannot represent deployment disturbances beyond the diversity of training environments.
- Online-learning controllers make no parametric disturbance assumption but prove sensitive to tuning parameters, and on-hardware online policy learning takes seconds to hours.

## Method

- ==Random-Fourier-feature kernel== disturbance model with two learnable parameter sets, coefficients and Gaussian bandwidth, giving an ==RKHS==-expressive yet differentiable residual added to nominal quadrotor dynamics.
- Offline policy optimization by ==backpropagation through time== in a ==differentiable simulator==: kernel coefficients and bandwidth are randomly sampled per episode and fed into the 187-dim policy observation.
- ==Online least-squares estimation== updating both bandwidth and coefficients by ==projected gradient descent== at every 50 Hz control step, letting the basis itself adapt rather than only its projections.

## Results

- Simulation position RMSE is lowest across all four disturbance types, e.g. **4.11** cm versus **10.71** (DATT L1-E), **14.22** (DATT RMA), **12.16** (MPC L1-E), **5.44** (MPC RFF) under switching disturbance.
- Crazyflie hardware RMSE reaches **6.41** cm (ground effect), **8.80** cm (wind), **6.68** cm (20%-mass swinging payload), beating every baseline by up to **34%**.
- Ablations: dropping both updates degrades simulation error from about **4** cm to about **40** cm; dropping only the bandwidth update still costs **10-30%** in simulation and **3-10%** on hardware.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Adapting the basis itself, not just coefficients on a fixed basis, is what buys robustness to non-stationary disturbances that leave the pre-committed function space.
> - Random features trade some neural-network expressivity for data efficiency, which matters more than capacity when adaptation must happen from a single sample per control step.
> - Randomizing latent disturbance-model parameters during differentiable-simulation training yields zero-shot transfer cheaply, but out-of-distribution parameters and formal stability remain unaddressed.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research addresses a fundamental challenge in robotics: enabling mobile robots to operate effectively under uncertain, time-varying, and non-stationary environmental disturbances. Such conditions are prevalent in real-world applications like aerial transportation, search and rescue, and inspection. Achieving accurate and efficient control in these scenarios requires an environment representation that is both sufficiently expressive to capture complex environmental dynamics and computationally efficient to support rapid online adaptation.

The paper positions its contribution by contrasting it with existing control paradigms. Traditional **robust control** methods are highlighted as conservative, as they typically design controllers based on worst-case disturbance realizations. **Classical adaptive control** often assumes parametric uncertainty, where unknown coefficients multiply a fixed set of known basis functions. While these coefficients can be updated online, the disturbance compensation capability is limited by the fixed expressiveness of the chosen basis functions, hindering generalization to diverse or evolving disturbances. **Learning-based control** approaches, particularly those employing neural networks with environment encoders, train policies offline. However, the encoder becomes fixed after training, which means it cannot represent disturbances beyond the diversity encountered during the initial training phase, thereby restricting online adaptability. Lastly, **online learning methods** operate based on past information without an explicit disturbance model, making them agnostic to the disturbance form, but are often sensitive to tuning parameters in practice.

This work endeavors to bridge the gap between expressiveness and computational efficiency by proposing a kernel-based representation. It leverages Random Fourier Features (RFF) to approximate unknown disturbances, benefiting from their ability to represent functions in Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces (RKHS) while maintaining computational tractability, a characteristic sometimes likened to linearized neural networks. A key differentiation of this work is its online adaptation of *both* the kernel coefficients and the kernel bandwidth. This mechanism allows the underlying basis functions themselves to adapt to the encountered disturbances, thereby overcoming the limitations of methods that rely on pre-committed or fixed basis sets. In doing so, the research aims to provide a more flexible and robust solution for domain-adaptive policy learning in dynamic and uncertain environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an algorithm for efficient domain-adaptive policy learning that enables mobile robots, specifically quadrotors, to perform agile tasks reliably under unknown, time-varying, and non-stationary disturbances. The core motivation stems from the limitations of existing control strategies in simultaneously addressing two critical requirements for real-world robotic autonomy:

1. **Sufficient Expressiveness for Offline Training:** The environment representation must be expressive enough to accurately model complex discrepancies between simulation and reality (sim-to-real gaps) during the offline policy training phase. This includes capturing intricate aerodynamic effects, varying payload dynamics, or ground interactions. Without a sufficiently rich representation, policies trained offline may fail to generalize to the complexities of real-world deployment.
2. **Computational Efficiency for Online Adaptation:** During actual hardware deployment, the robot must be able to adapt rapidly to unforeseen and dynamically changing disturbances in real-time. This necessitates an environment representation and adaptation mechanism that is computationally light, allowing for updates at control loop frequencies.

The paper highlights practical scenarios for quadrotors that exemplify these challenges: sudden gusts of wind, shifts in payload mid-flight that alter the center of mass, or transitions between distinct flight regimes such as entering or exiting ground effect. These scenarios underscore the need for a control system that can autonomously adapt to unmodeled forces or system parameter changes.

To address these challenges, the authors are motivated to employ kernel representations, specifically a differentiable kernel approximation based on Random Fourier Features (RFF). This choice is driven by the RFF's capacity to approximate functions within a Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space, offering the requisite expressiveness to model complex disturbances. Critically, the motivation extends to adapting *both* the kernel coefficients ($\alpha$) and the kernel bandwidth ($\sigma$) online. The bandwidth parameter ($\sigma$) directly influences the expressiveness and characteristics of the function space approximated by the kernel. The authors argue that while fixed basis functions limit adaptation, allowing the bandwidth to also adapt enables the basis functions themselves to change, providing a more versatile and adaptive model for disturbances whose characteristics might evolve over time. This dual adaptation capability is central to achieving robust performance in non-stationary environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed algorithm for efficient domain-adaptive policy learning is structured into two distinct phases: an offline policy training phase and an online adaptation phase.

**Disturbance Modeling via Random Fourier Features (RFF):**
The methodology commences by modeling unknown disturbances $h(\cdot)$ using a differentiable kernel approximation based on Random Fourier Features (RFF). This approximation, $\hat{h}(\cdot; \alpha) \triangleq \frac{1}{M} \sum_{i=1}^M \Phi_{\nu}(\cdot, \theta_i) \alpha_i$, leverages the ability of RFF to approximate functions in a Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS). Here, $\Phi_{\nu}$ is the feature map, $\theta_i = (w, b)$ are random parameters (with $w$ sampled from a Gaussian distribution and $b$ from a uniform distribution for the Gaussian kernel case), and $\alpha_i$ are learnable coefficients. The theoretical basis, supported by Proposition 1, indicates that the approximation error scales as $O(1/\sqrt{M})$, given a base measure $\nu$. A crucial aspect emphasized is that for kernels like the Gaussian kernel, the base measure $\nu$ (and thus the induced function space) is determined by the kernel bandwidth parameter $\sigma$. This relationship underpins the motivation for adapting $\sigma$ online, in addition to the coefficients $\alpha$. The input features $z_t$ for the disturbance model include body-frame velocity, quaternion, normalized thrust, and angular velocity command.

**Offline Policy Optimization (Algorithm 1):**
In the offline phase, a domain-adaptive control policy $\pi_{\psi}$ is trained. This policy is parameterized by $\psi$ and is designed to leverage estimated disturbance information by taking the kernel parameters ($\alpha$ and $\sigma$) as part of its observation space ($o_t$). The quadrotor dynamics, which are assumed to be differentiable, are augmented with the RFF-based disturbance model: $x_{t+1} = f(x_t) + g(x_t)u_t + \frac{1}{M} \sum_{i=1}^M \Phi(z_t, \theta_i) \alpha_{i,k}$.
Policy optimization is performed by maximizing a cumulative reward function $R(\psi)$ over an N-step rollout, utilizing differentiable simulation and analytical gradients via Back-Propagation Through Time (BPTT). During this training, the kernel coefficients $\alpha$ are randomly sampled from a Gaussian distribution $N(0, 0.5I)$, and the bandwidth $\sigma$ is uniformly sampled from the range $[0.001, 1]$. This randomization generates a diverse set of disturbance profiles, enabling the policy to learn to adapt to various conditions. The policy network is a 3-layer Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) with 512 hidden dimensions. Its observation vector (size 187) includes standard state information, future reference trajectory points, and the current $\alpha$ and $\sigma$ values. The training involves tracking a lemniscate trajectory across 500 environments for 300 epochs, with each episode lasting 250 simulation steps (0.02s timestep). The reward function penalizes tracking errors in position, quaternion, velocity, and control deviation. The offline training is computationally efficient, completing in approximately 50 seconds on an RTX 4090 GPU.

**Online Least-Squares Estimation (Algorithm 2):**
Upon deployment, the trained policy $\pi_{\psi}$ is transferred zero-shot to hardware. In this online phase, the kernel parameters $\hat{\alpha}_t$ and $\hat{\sigma}_t$ are continuously updated at every control step to adapt to the encountered non-stationary disturbances. The actual disturbance $h(z_t)$ is estimated online by observing the mismatch between the actual state transition and the nominal dynamics: $h(z_t) = x_{t+1} - f(x_t) - g(x_t)u_t$. An online least-squares algorithm is then employed to minimize the approximation error $l_t = \|h(z_t) - \hat{h}(z_t)\|^2$, where $\hat{h}(z_t)$ is the RFF approximation with the current estimates $\hat{\alpha}_t$ and $\hat{\sigma}_t$. Gradients $\nabla_{\hat{\sigma}_t} l_t$ and $\nabla_{\hat{\alpha}_t} l_t$ are computed, and the parameters are updated via gradient descent, followed by projection onto their predefined domains $D_{\sigma}$ and $D_{\alpha}$. The initial values for $\alpha$ are set to zeros, and $\sigma$ to 0.5. Learning rates ($\eta$) are tuned for simulation ($\eta=0.1$) and hardware ($\eta=0.02$) to account for real-world noise and avoid aggressive updates. This online adaptation of both $\alpha$ and $\sigma$ allows the policy to adjust its understanding of the disturbance model's fundamental characteristics in real-time.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates the proposed method through both high-fidelity numerical simulations and hardware experiments using Crazyflie drones, comparing its performance against several benchmark adaptive control algorithms. The primary metric for performance evaluation is the Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) in position tracking.

**Benchmarking Algorithms:**
The proposed method is compared against four baselines:
- **DATT (L1-E) & DATT (RMA):** Learning-based adaptive control policies using PPO, conditioned on disturbance estimates derived from an L1-estimator or a Rapid Motor Adaptation (RMA) neural network, respectively.
- **MPC (L1-E) & MPC (RFF):** Model Predictive Control (MPC) incorporating disturbance estimates from an L1-estimator or an RFF-based residual model. Notably, MPC (RFF) only updates the kernel coefficients ($\alpha$) online, with a fixed bandwidth ($\sigma$).

**Numerical Simulations (Section 4.3):**
Simulations employed a realistic simulator with a Blade Element Momentum (BEM) model for aerodynamics and high-frequency low-level controller dynamics. Disturbances included:
1. Aerodynamics effects only.
2. Aerodynamics plus sinusoidal disturbance.
3. Aerodynamics plus switching disturbance (sinusoidal to constant).
4. Aerodynamics plus quadratic-phase sinusoidal disturbance.

Across all these diverse scenarios, the proposed method consistently exhibited the lowest position tracking error. Specifically, it achieved improvements in RMSE by up to 70% compared to DATT (L1-E) and DATT (RMA), up to 60% over MPC (L1-E), and up to 30% over MPC (RFF). An analysis of prediction errors indicated that RMA-based methods had the largest errors, attributed to their dependency on the training dataset. RFF-based models demonstrated better disturbance prediction accuracy than L1-estimators. Furthermore, the paper noted that DATT variants were constrained by fixed disturbance training ranges (e.g., [-2, 2] m/s^2), which complex disturbances could exceed, while the RFF model could represent these within its adaptive parameter ranges.

An **ablation study** in simulation further elucidated the importance of adapting both kernel parameters. Removing both $\sigma$ and $\alpha$ updates resulted in a substantial degradation of performance, with tracking errors increasing from approximately 4 cm to around 40 cm. Even removing only the $\sigma$ (bandwidth) update led to a performance drop of 10% to 30% across all scenarios, underscoring the contribution of adaptive kernel bandwidth to overall robustness and accuracy.

**Hardware Experiments (Crazyflie Drones, Section 4.4):**
The algorithm was deployed on Crazyflie drones under real-world, time-varying disturbances:
1. Ground effect (switching between conditions with and without).
2. Time-varying wind disturbance (1.5 m/s to 2.5 m/s).
3. Suspended payload (6g, equivalent to 20% of drone mass).

Consistent with simulations, the proposed method achieved the lowest tracking error in all hardware scenarios. It demonstrated improvements in RMSE by up to 10% over DATT (L1-E), 25% over DATT (RMA), 34% over MPC (L1-E), and 20% over MPC (RFF).

The **ablation study** on hardware also confirmed the benefits of dual parameter adaptation. Removing both $\sigma$ and $\alpha$ updates led to tracking errors increasing from 6-9 cm to over 10-15 cm. Removing only the $\sigma$ update still resulted in a 3% to 10% performance decrease. The smaller performance difference between variants on hardware compared to simulation was attributed to the use of a lower learning rate ($\eta=0.02$) for online adaptation, implemented to mitigate aggressive updates in the presence of noisy real-world data.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a notable advancement in the field of robotic control, particularly for operations in unpredictable environments. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Addressing a Critical Gap in Robotic Autonomy:** The work directly tackles the challenge of balancing expressive environment modeling with computationally efficient online adaptation. This is a fundamental requirement for deploying mobile robots in real-world scenarios characterized by unmodeled, time-varying, and non-stationary disturbances. By effectively handling these conditions, the proposed method enhances the autonomy and reliability of robotic systems in complex and dynamic settings.

- **Novelty in Adaptive Kernel Representation:** A key innovation is the simultaneous online adaptation of both kernel coefficients ($\alpha$) and kernel bandwidth ($\sigma$) within a Random Fourier Features (RFF) framework. This allows the underlying basis functions of the disturbance model to adapt, rather than being confined to a fixed, pre-committed set. This flexibility enables the model to capture a broader range of disturbance characteristics as they evolve, going beyond the limitations of traditional adaptive control methods that rely on fixed basis functions or learning-based methods with static encoders.

- **Enhanced Performance and Robustness:** The consistent and significant improvements in position tracking accuracy demonstrated across diverse simulated and hardware experiments highlight the practical efficacy of the approach. Outperforming several state-of-the-art learning-based and model-based adaptive control baselines by up to 70% in simulation and 30% on hardware underscores the method's robustness and superior performance under challenging conditions, including complex aerodynamics, wind, ground effects, and payload fluctuations.

- **Computational Efficiency and Real-world Feasibility:** The method's offline training time of approximately 50 seconds on a single RTX 4090 GPU, combined with its ability to adapt online at the control loop frequency (50 Hz) and enable zero-shot transfer to hardware, makes it highly practical. This efficiency reduces development cycles and facilitates rapid deployment of adaptive controllers in real robotic systems.

- **Foundation for Future Research:** The paper identifies several important avenues for future work, including handling out-of-distribution disturbances through detection and rapid online re-training, establishing formal stability guarantees for non-stationary disturbances, and mitigating bursting behavior (a common issue in adaptive control) through active learning. These outlined directions indicate that the presented work serves as a strong foundation, opening pathways for further theoretical and practical advancements in robust and adaptive control.

In summary, this research provides an effective and efficient approach to domain-adaptive policy learning, enabling robots to robustly navigate complex, non-stationary environments. Its impact lies in advancing the capabilities of autonomous systems by offering a method that is both expressive in modeling unknown disturbances and efficient in adapting to them in real-time.
