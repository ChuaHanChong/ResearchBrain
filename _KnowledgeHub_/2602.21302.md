# Learning Dynamic Rope Manipulation Using Task-Level Iterative Learning Control

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Carnegie Mellon University developed Task-Level Iterative Learning Control, a method for robots to learn dynamic manipulation of deformable objects like ropes. This approach successfully enabled an xArm 7 robot to tie an overhand "flying knot" on seven diverse rope types within 10 trials, consistently achieving a 100% success rate once learned.

## Problem

- Manipulating deformable objects such as ropes is challenging for robots due to their high unactuated degrees of freedom and complex, non-linear dynamics, making accurate physical modeling difficult.
- Traditional Iterative Learning Control (ILC) methods, which often minimize robot trajectory tracking errors uniformly, prove ineffective for dynamic deformable object tasks, as diffuse error reduction does not lead to task success.
- Many robot learning paradigms require extensive demonstration data, large-scale simulation, or complex Sim2Real transfer, which are costly and time-consuming for real-world deployment.

## Method

- A novel **Task-Level Iterative Learning Control (ILC)** algorithm was developed, which iteratively refines a robot's feedforward command trajectory based on errors observed in real-world trials.
- The learning objective was focused on a ==critical point objective==, targeting a specific desired rope state (shape and velocity) at a predefined crucial moment (e.g., self-collision) rather than across the entire trajectory.
- The system learns to correct the manipulated object's trajectory using a simplified, ==approximate physical rope model== to derive gradient information for command updates, rather than relying solely on precise robot trajectory tracking.

## Results

- The robot successfully learned to tie an overhand "flying knot" on **7** diverse rope types (varying in material, thickness, and density) within **10** trials, starting from a single human demonstration for each.
- Once a successful command trajectory was learned for a given rope and demonstration, the robot achieved a **100%** success rate over **40** repeated trials, demonstrating high repeatability and robustness.
- The method proved robust to variations in initial human demonstrations and significant inaccuracies (order-of-magnitude variations) in the rope model's stiffness and end mass parameters.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - For dynamic deformable object manipulation, concentrating learning efforts on achieving a specific, critical state at a key moment in the task is more effective than diffuse error reduction over the entire trajectory.
> - Sample-efficient learning on real hardware is feasible even with deliberately approximate physical models, as long as these models provide sufficient gradient information for iterative control updates.
> - Focusing the learning process on the manipulated object's state allows the robot to adapt its own motion to the object's complex dynamics, which is crucial for achieving desired task outcomes with deformable materials.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Broader Research Landscape

The manipulation of deformable objects, such as ropes and cloth, by robots represents a challenging area within robotics due to the high number of unactuated degrees of freedom and the difficulty in creating accurate physical models. This work is situated within the field of robot learning for dynamic manipulation, specifically focusing on flexible, one-dimensional objects.

Previous research in deformable object manipulation has explored both quasi-static scenarios, often relying on strong perception, geometric priors, or imitation learning, and dynamic manipulation, which involves faster motions for tasks like whipping or cloth flinging. Key to dynamic tasks is the repeatability of actions despite complex dynamics.

Iterative Learning Control (ILC) is a control paradigm that improves robot performance on repetitive tasks by iteratively updating a feedforward command based on observed errors from real-world trials. Model-based ILC leverages an approximate system model to convert task errors into command corrections. This approach is distinct from model-free methods that require extensive data or purely simulation-based learning. While traditional ILC typically focuses on robot trajectory tracking and often weights errors equally across the entire trajectory, variations like optimization-based ILC (Norm-Optimal ILC) incorporate constraints and event-based ILC methods focus learning on specific critical points or phases of a task. The latter aligns with the paper's concept of a "critical point objective."

Recent advancements have integrated modern learning techniques with ILC, such as using approximate simulators for policy improvements, framing ILC within regret minimization, or applying ILC-style gradient updates in reinforcement learning. Another relevant area is Sim2Real transfer, where policies are trained in simulation and then adapted to real robots, often employing domain randomization to bridge the reality gap. However, these methods typically require large amounts of simulated data or intricate model fitting, which the current work aims to mitigate.

This paper extends ILC to the domain of dynamic deformable object manipulation. It addresses limitations of standard ILC formulations that fail in this context by introducing "Task-Level ILC" with specific features: focusing on errors in the manipulated object's state rather than just the robot's, and concentrating learning on a "critical point" within the task execution. This approach contrasts with methods heavily reliant on large-scale simulation or extensive demonstration data by aiming for sample-efficient learning directly on hardware with simplified models.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable a robot to learn complex dynamic manipulation tasks involving deformable objects, specifically ropes, in a sample-efficient manner. The authors chose the "flying knot" as a representative dynamic task, which involves intricate, fast motions and self-collisions of the rope to form a knot without changing grip.

The motivation stems from several challenges inherent in dynamic deformable object manipulation and limitations of existing learning paradigms:
- **High Complexity of Deformable Objects:** Ropes and similar materials possess a large number of unactuated degrees of freedom and exhibit complex, non-linear dynamics, making them difficult and computationally expensive to model accurately.
- **Limitations of Traditional ILC:** Standard Iterative Learning Control formulations, which typically aim to minimize robot trajectory tracking errors with equal weighting throughout the task, were found to be ineffective for dynamic rope manipulation. The authors observed that diffuse error reduction across the entire trajectory did not lead to task success in this domain.
- **Avoidance of Extensive Data and Simulation:** Many contemporary robotic learning methods rely on large amounts of demonstration data, massive simulation efforts, or sophisticated Sim2Real transfer techniques. The authors aimed to develop an approach that minimizes these requirements, allowing for learning directly on physical hardware with minimal prior data.

To address these motivations, the authors established two key features for their Task-Level ILC approach:
- **Critical Point Objective:** Instead of distributing learning effort across the entire task trajectory, the robot's attention is focused on achieving a specific, single rope state at a pre-defined "critical point" in the trajectory. This concept is motivated by human instructional practices that often emphasize crucial "strike points" in dynamic tasks, and by the observation that errors at the final stages of dynamic tasks can be hard to measure or attribute. Concentrating on a critical intermediate state is hypothesized to simplify the learning problem, improve performance, and facilitate task transfer across variations.
- **Object Trajectory Learning:** The learning process is designed to correct the trajectory of the manipulated object's state variables (e.g., rope shape and velocity), rather than solely focusing on the robot's end-effector trajectory. This allows the robot to learn the required dynamic behavior of the object itself, which may necessitate deviations from the initial robot motion.

In summary, the key objectives were to develop a robust, data-efficient, and generalizable learning method for dynamic rope manipulation that could perform complex tasks like the flying knot, adapt to variations in rope properties and demonstrations, and operate effectively with approximate models and minimal real-world trials.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the methodology is a novel adaptation of Iterative Learning Control, termed Task-Level ILC, designed for dynamic rope manipulation. The system learns to refine a robot's feedforward command trajectory over repeated real-world trials.

**Task Definition and Critical Point:**
The chosen task is the overhand "flying knot." This dynamic task involves specific stages: initiating an upward and twisting hand motion, forming a rope loop, causing the rope to collide with itself, flipping the end through the loop, and tightening the knot.
A crucial element of the Task-Level ILC is the "critical point objective." For the flying knot, the authors defined the critical point as the precise moment of rope self-collision, or the "strike point." This specific event was selected because human instruction often emphasizes it, it is detectable and measurable before complex rope overlap occurs, and it is considered a strong proxy for successful knot formation. The learning objective is to match the rope's shape and velocity at this critical point to that of a demonstration.

**Demonstration and Initialization:**
The robot is provided with a single human demonstration of the flying knot performed on a specific rope (Rope 1). This demonstration includes the full hand trajectory and the rope's trajectory up to the critical collision point, which is manually annotated. This provides the target rope state at the critical point, denoted as $x_{\text{demo}}(t_c)$.
An initial robot command ($u_0(t)$) is generated by attempting to track the demonstrated human hand trajectory. This is achieved by solving a non-convex trajectory optimization problem that minimizes fingertip tracking error while respecting the robot's joint position, velocity, acceleration, and torque limits. This initial command serves as the starting point for the ILC loop.

**Iterative Learning Control Loop:**
The Task-Level ILC algorithm proceeds iteratively:
1. **Trial Execution:** The robot executes the current command trajectory ($u_k(t)$) in the real world.
2. **State Measurement:** The resulting rope trajectory ($x_k(t)$) is measured using a Vicon motion capture system, which tracks markers on the rope and the robot's end-effector. Tracking is primarily reliable up to the critical point due to occlusion challenges during self-collision.
3. **Error Calculation:** The critical-point error ($\tilde{x}_k(t_c)$) is calculated as the difference between the measured rope state at the critical point ($x_k(t_c)$) and the desired demonstrated rope state at the critical point ($x_{\text{demo}}(t_c)$).
4. **Command Update:** An "inverse model" ($M^{-1}$) is used to map this critical-point error into a command correction ($\Delta u_k(t)$).
5. **Command Refinement:** The next command ($u_{k+1}(t)$) is generated by subtracting the correction from the current command: $u_{k+1}(t) = u_k(t) - \Delta u_k(t)$.
This process repeats for a fixed number of iterations ($K$).

**Command Parameterization:**
The feedforward robot command trajectory is parameterized using 10 Bezier curves (7 robot joint angles + 3 robot base translation dimensions), each defined by 8 knot points ($\kappa$). The base translation dimensions are constrained to remain fixed during a trial, allowing the robot base location to shift only between trials if needed. The total execution time ($T$) of the command is fixed by the demonstration length.

**System Models:**
- **Robot Model:** A simplified kinematic chain model is used, mapping robot joint angles ($q$) to the fingertip's position and orientation ($K(q)$). It is assumed that the robot's internal controller accurately follows commanded joint trajectories within limits. The rope's dynamics are considered negligible on the robot due to its high gear ratio.
- **Rope Model:** The rope is modeled as a 3D serial chain of 11 point masses, connected by fixed-distance constraints. Each joint has bending stiffness ($k$) and damping ($b$). An additional mass ($m_e$) approximates the weight attached to the end of the rope. The first point of the rope model is kinematically driven by the robot's fingertip trajectory. This model is deliberately approximate, using a single, fixed set of parameters (stiffness, damping, link mass, end mass, link length) for all rope types, without fitting to individual ropes. The rope dynamics are simulated using a maximal coordinate variational integrator.

**Optimization-Based Inverse Model:**
The command update $\Delta \kappa_k$ is computed by solving a Quadratic Program (QP). This QP minimizes an objective function that primarily penalizes the error at the critical point ($t_c$) between the linearized model's predicted state correction and the measured real-system error. It also includes terms to encourage the robot's end-effector to follow the demonstrator's motion after the critical point (follow-through) and a regularization term on the command updates. Crucially, errors for times *after* $t_c$ are ignored in the primary objective, simplifying the modeling requirements by not needing to account for complex post-collision physics.
The QP includes linear constraints that ensure the updated command respects the robot's joint position, velocity, acceleration, and torque limits, and the fixed base position. The linearized system model ($M = \partial M / \partial \kappa$) is obtained by differentiating the combined robot and rope models. The QP is formulated using the Drake optimization toolbox and solved with the Clarabel Solver.

**Experimental Setup:**
Experiments are conducted on an xArm 7 robot arm. Rope markers are tracked by a Vicon Vantage 16 motion capture system. Various ropes (7 types, 7-25 mm thickness, 0.013-0.5 kg/m density, diverse materials) are used, each with a mass affixed to its end.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of Task-Level ILC involved comparisons against alternative objectives, assessment across diverse rope types and demonstration variations, and analysis of learned command transferability and robustness to model parameter changes.

**Initial Guess Performance:**
The robot's initial attempt to follow the human demonstration (prior to ILC) consistently failed to tie the flying knot. This was attributed to the inherent differences in morphology, kinematics, and dynamics between human and robot, which prevent exact replication of human motion, even for a task demonstrated with Rope 1 (Fig. 7). This highlights the necessity of a learning or adaptation mechanism.

**Effectiveness of Critical Point Objective:**
A direct comparison demonstrated the critical importance of the critical point objective. When the ILC algorithm used an "equally-weighted objective" (minimizing rope tracking errors uniformly across the pre-collision trajectory), it failed to achieve the flying knot. This failure occurred because the equally-weighted objective prioritized minor errors at earlier stages of the trajectory, leading to larger, detrimental errors at the crucial critical point. In contrast, the Task-Level ILC with its critical point objective successfully reduced the error at the collision moment, leading to successful knot formation (Fig. 4). This indicates that focusing learning on specific, impactful task states is more effective than diffuse error reduction for dynamic deformable object manipulation.

**Learning Across Rope Types:**
Task-Level ILC demonstrated successful learning of the flying knot on all 7 diverse rope types (ranging from cotton to steel chain and latex surgical tubing, with varied thicknesses and densities). Crucially, the learning process initiated from the *same single human demonstration* (performed on Rope 1) for all rope types. The robot achieved a successful flying knot command within 10 trials for all ropes (Fig. 9). The learned commands varied significantly for different rope types, indicating that the algorithm adapted the robot's motion to the specific dynamics of each rope while achieving the same critical point objective. The learning cost generally decreased over trials, though occasional increases were observed, which the authors attributed to model errors and the absence of a line search. Learning was stopped after the first success to avoid potential degradation from high-frequency error amplification.

**Learning Across Demonstration Variation:**
The method also proved robust to variations in the initial human demonstration. For Rope 1, the algorithm successfully learned the flying knot from four different demonstration styles ("Fast," "Slow," "Swipe," and "Inverse"), which varied in execution speed and total duration (Fig. 10). The number of trials required for success varied among these demonstrations, with one requiring more trials than others.

**Learned Command Success Rate:**
Once a successful command trajectory was learned for a given rope and demonstration, the robot exhibited a 100% success rate over 40 repeated trials. This indicates the repeatability and robustness of the learned feedforward commands on the real system.

**Transfer of Learned Commands:**
The research evaluated the transferability of learned commands between different rope types. A command successfully learned on Rope A was used as the initial guess for learning on Rope B. In the majority of transfers, the command required adaptation, typically succeeding within 2 to 5 additional trials (Fig. 11). This suggests that while rope dynamics are distinct enough to require some re-learning, the underlying structure of the task allows for efficient transfer. Notably, for Rope 7 (latex surgical tubing), several transfers required no additional learning, indicating its robustness to command variations. However, some transfers failed to converge within 10 trials, indicating limitations in adapting to highly dissimilar rope dynamics with the given learning budget.

**Sensitivity to Model Parameters:**
The learning process demonstrated robustness to significant inaccuracies in the rope model parameters. The robot could still learn successfully with order-of-magnitude variations in rope model stiffness and end mass (Table II). This suggests that the approximate models provided sufficiently accurate gradient information for ILC convergence. However, extreme deviations (e.g., very low stiffness or end mass) could lead to learning failures or divergence, as large command updates based on a highly inaccurate model might push the system into unrecoverable states.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a notable advancement in the field of robot manipulation, particularly for deformable objects, by introducing Task-Level Iterative Learning Control with a critical point objective. Its significance and potential impact are multi-faceted:

- **Enabling Dynamic Deformable Object Manipulation:** The work addresses a long-standing challenge in robotics: reliably manipulating objects with complex, unactuated dynamics like ropes. By demonstrating success on a difficult dynamic task like the flying knot across a wide variety of ropes, it opens avenues for robots to perform more dexterous and complex real-world tasks involving flexible materials.
- **Sample-Efficient Learning on Hardware:** A key contribution is the ability to learn directly on the real robotic system with a single human demonstration and simplified, approximate physical models. This significantly reduces the reliance on large datasets, extensive simulation, or time-consuming model fitting, which are common bottlenecks in other robot learning paradigms (e.g., deep reinforcement learning, Sim2Real). This makes the approach practical for real-world deployment where data collection or accurate modeling can be costly.
- **Advancement of Iterative Learning Control:** The introduction of the "critical point objective" and the focus on "object trajectory learning" extend the capabilities of traditional ILC. By targeting error reduction at specific, critical moments in a task's unactuated dynamics, the method overcomes limitations of standard ILC formulations that struggled with the complexities of dynamic deformable object manipulation. This conceptual contribution can inform future ILC applications in other challenging domains.
- **Robustness and Generalization:** The demonstrated robustness across diverse rope types (materials, thicknesses, densities) and variations in human demonstrations suggests a degree of generalization in the learned approach. The ability to transfer learned commands between different ropes with minimal additional trials further highlights this adaptability, indicating that the system learns generalizable strategies rather than rope-specific hardcoded motions.
- **Reduced Sim-to-Real Gap Issues:** Learning directly on the hardware inherently bypasses the challenges associated with the "Sim-to-Real gap." The method leverages the real system's repeatable dynamics to refine commands, making it robust to model inaccuracies because it primarily relies on the model for gradient information rather than precise state prediction.
- **Potential for Broader Applications:** The principles demonstrated, particularly the critical point objective and object-centric learning, could be extended to other dynamic manipulation tasks involving flexible objects such as cables, wires, fabrics, or even biological tissues. This has implications for industries like manufacturing, assembly, surgery, and service robotics.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The identified limitations (e.g., unhandled collisions, limitations of critical point for full task success, divergence with large errors) provide clear directions for future work. These include autonomous critical point selection, adaptive model structures, and learning from lower-fidelity sensor data, which could further enhance the autonomy and applicability of such systems.
