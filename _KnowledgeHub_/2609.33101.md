# Evolving Dexterous Robots from Scratch

> [!summary] Summary
> A de novo manipulator design pipeline that evolves freeform dexterous robots without presupposing fingers, palms, or joint layout. A contrastive spherical latent genome is searched by CMA-ES, decoded by an autoregressive grammar-masked developmental transformer; each design is PPO-trained and auto-CADified for printing. Evolved robots rotate, pick, hang, retrieve, flip and hold objects; a didactyl rotator turns unseen real objects at 1.39 rad/s zero-shot, nearly twice prior state-of-the-art.

## Problem

- Prior manipulator optimization deforms links within a fixed kinematic template, such as serial chains, parallel-jaw grippers, or fingers on a planar palm, so radically different anatomies cannot emerge.
- VAE-based latent design spaces can map nearly identical morphologies to distant latent codes and suffer posterior collapse, obstructing evolutionary search over body plans.
- Existing genotype-to-phenotype decoders are either too constrained, yielding stick-figure bodies, or too unconstrained, producing invalid anatomies with floating, disconnected body parts.

## Method

- ==Contrastive latent genome==: a ==3D-CNN voxel encoder== plus a ==graph network== over the kinematic tree, trained with ==NT-Xent== on perturbed positive pairs and projected onto a ==32-D hypersphere==.
- ==Autoregressive developmental decoder==: a latent-conditioned transformer emits design tokens that grow appendages one segment at a time, with a ==grammar masking== invalid tokens to guarantee valid bodies.
- Evolution-learning-fabrication loop: ==CMA-ES== searches the genome, ==PPO== trains each design in Isaac Lab, and an automatic CAD pipeline carves servo cavities and sets joint limits for printing.

## Results

- In-hand rotation in simulation: the evolved didactyl robot reaches **3.66 rad/s** versus **2.09 rad/s** for an anthropomorphic hand and **3.3 rad/s** for prior optimized manipulators.
- Zero-shot real deployment rotates unseen objects (16-687 g) at **1.39 rad/s** versus **0.74 rad/s** prior best; the evolved picker succeeds **eight in ten** attempts across 24 unseen objects.
- Latent genome reaches **100%** augmentation recall, **92%** topology 10-NN accuracy and **0.97** Spearman amputation-distance correlation; removing contrastive loss significantly lowers evolved fitness, while a VAE posterior-collapses.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Relaxing anthropomorphic and modular priors lets non-humanlike bodies, such as a two-appendage rotator, beat human-inspired hands, suggesting hand templates can constrain rather than enable dexterity.
> - For evolutionary search, latent geometry matters: contrastive embeddings that keep similar bodies genetically close improve evolvability more than reconstruction-plus-regularization VAE objectives.
> - Multitask selection exposes morphological trade-offs: gaining picking and holding cost rotation skill, so generalist bodies may underperform specialists when tasks demand conflicting anatomies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

Much robot-hand research starts from a design template, such as a human-shaped hand or a fixed arrangement of fingers. Those templates restrict which anatomies can even be tried. This paper instead searches for robot manipulators whose body shapes and controllers are developed together, which is a difficult search problem. The authors call this de novo manipulator design. Their designs are freeform within a defined parameterization: one root, up to five unbranched appendages, and at most 25 joints. In one task, a robot mounted root-up uses its appendages to rotate an object on its body. The evolved design rotated previously unseen real-world objects.

The paper's six-task overview shows evolved morphologies for rotating, retrieving, flipping, holding, picking up, and hanging, in design, simulation, and in some cases physical form. Only some examples are real robots: C, E, Q, and S were built and tested in the real world.

### 2. A searchable space for bodies

The pipeline has three steps. First, the design space encodes one root plus up to five appendage chains, with up to 25 joints. Within this parameterization, root geometry, appendage placement, segment shape, and joint articulation can all vary.

Second, an encoder combines a 3D representation of the body with its joint graph and produces a 32-dimensional genetic code on a unit hypersphere. Training pulls a design and transformed versions of it closer together in that space and pushes them away from other designs. This training method is called contrastive learning. Designs with nearby codes form a "genetic neighborhood."

Third, a transformer decodes a code one design token at a time, starting with the root and then adding appendage segments. A grammar masks invalid next tokens. Motor-fit, pose, and fabrication checks can still reject designs after decoding.

CMA-ES searches over the codes. For each candidate, PPO trains a fresh controller in simulation.

### 3. The test: representation and rotation

The closest prior work is Fay et al. ([[2512.03743|paper]]), which also co-optimized hand morphology and control. In that work, finger shape and articulation were largely presupposed, and finger positions were locked in a plane around a 2D palm. The new work used the same actuators for comparison.

Rotation is measured as net object rotation rate over a 30-second evaluation period, in radians per second. The evolved robot was essentially didactyl. On the paper's set of 15 unseen test objects, it achieved an average of **1.39 rad/s**. Fay et al.'s design is reported at **0.74 rad/s**. This is a reported cross-study comparison, not a paired test on matched objects. The didactyl robot rotated every test object, which weighed 16–687 g, and it sustained rotation for the full 30 seconds on 11 of them.

The authors call this deployment "zero-shot" because the physical robot received no real-world training. Before deployment, its policy was distilled from a simulation policy and fine-tuned in simulation under domain randomization.

The decisive test of the representation is an ablation that removes contrastive learning. All ablation models used a 200,000-body-plan training set and shared encoder and decoder architecture. They were compared on rotation evolution over three trials. The test used recall@1: the share of transformed copies whose nearest genetic neighbor is their original. On 2,000 held-out designs, recall@1 was **1.00** with contrastive loss and **0.03** without it. Without contrastive learning, reconstruction accuracy for joint counts stayed relatively high, but evolution performed substantially worse.

The authors interpret this result as showing that coherent genetic neighborhoods help evolution search. Removing the spherical projection produced no statistically significant difference, so its role remains unclear.

### 4. What body search buys, and what it does not

The search produced task-specific morphologies, including the didactyl rotator, which used two functional appendages. This result illustrates why a human-shaped template need not be the default solution. The system is simulation-led and was tested on sessile robots with PLA bodies, in-joint servos, and proprioceptive sensing. Evolution and learning used no feedback from the real environment, and the demonstrated behaviors are simple and operate on nearby objects.
