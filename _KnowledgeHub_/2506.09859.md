# Hierarchical Learning-Enhanced MPC for Safe Crowd Navigation with Heterogeneous Constraints

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Harbin Institute of Technology developed HALO, a hierarchical framework integrating deep reinforcement learning and model predictive control for safe robot navigation in crowded environments. The system, which leverages a GNN for local goal recommendations and MPC for explicit constraint enforcement, achieved a 99.4% success rate and 0.5% collision rate in simulations, demonstrating robust performance in diverse real-world scenarios.

## Problem

- Autonomous robots face challenges in safe and efficient navigation in dynamic, crowded environments, struggling to accurately estimate long-term costs and ensure safety guarantees.
- Existing navigation methods either lack explicit constraint guarantees (learning-based) or suffer from high computational complexity and limited planning horizons (optimization-based), often failing to handle diverse constraints simultaneously.
- Many hybrid learning-optimization methods contend with high training costs and difficulties in 'sim-to-real' transfer, hindering their practical deployment in complex real-world settings.

## Method

- A hierarchical framework, HALO, integrates a GNN-based Deep ==Reinforcement Learning== (DRL) frontend for recommending local goals with an **MPC** backend for explicit constraint enforcement and trajectory generation.
- The approach introduces an 'incremental action masking' mechanism to guide DRL exploration by filtering infeasible local goals and a 'privileged learning strategy' to enhance temporal consistency during training.
- A spatio-temporal path search module refines local goal recommendations into high-dimensional reference trajectories, serving as robust initializations for the MPC problem, which then handles explicit obstacle avoidance and kinematic constraints.

## Results

- Achieved a **99.4%** success rate and **0.5%** collision rate in simulation, outperforming eight baseline methods in crowded scenarios with up to 10 pedestrians or obstacles.
- Demonstrated the lowest disturbance to humans (DN = 23), indicating superior social awareness and ability to reduce pedestrian interference compared to baseline methods.
- Successfully deployed on a physical robot, confirming real-world feasibility and sim-to-real transfer with a real-world success rate of **84%**, affirming consistent performance in diverse, kinematically constrained environments.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Effectively integrating DRL's implicit reasoning for local goal recommendations with MPC's explicit constraint guarantees provides a robust solution for balancing performance and safety in complex dynamic environments.
> - Novel training mechanisms like incremental action masking are crucial for enabling efficient end-to-end training of hybrid DRL-MPC systems in low-fidelity simulations, significantly reducing training costs and improving sim-to-real transfer.
> - Explicitly modeling and simultaneously handling heterogeneous constraints (dynamic obstacles, diverse static structures, and robot kinematics) within a unified framework is vital for achieving state-of-the-art safety and social compliance in real-world crowded scenarios.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Autonomous robot navigation in dynamic and complex environments, such as crowded spaces or industrial settings, remains a paramount challenge in robotics. Historically, approaches to this problem have evolved through several paradigms, each with its strengths and limitations.

Early planning methods, like A*, RRT, and DWA, primarily focused on static environments. Their extension to dynamic scenarios often involved high-frequency replanning, which, while offering limited adaptability, suffered from issues like the "Freezing Robot Problem" and could not guarantee global optimality or handle complex interactions effectively.

The advent of improved perception technologies led to model-based methods (e.g., social force models, velocity obstacles (VO)) that could explicitly model dynamic obstacles at an agent level. While simplifying complex interactions, these methods relied heavily on hand-crafted functions, limiting their generalization across diverse scenarios and often leading to sub-optimal, locally greedy behaviors.

Optimization-based methods, such as Model Predictive Control (MPC) and dynamic control barrier functions (DCBF), gained prominence due to their ability to explicitly enforce non-linear constraints, including obstacle avoidance and robot kinematics. These methods offer local optimality and strong safety guarantees, making them a mainstream choice in industrial applications. However, their computational intensity restricts their application to shorter prediction horizons, making them reliant on higher-level planners for long-term goals and often unable to fully incorporate the costs associated with dynamic obstacles in global planning.

More recently, learning-based methods, particularly Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) with deep neural networks (DNNs) and Graph Neural Networks (GNNs), have shown promise in modeling complex agent interactions and implicitly estimating the cost-to-go. These data-driven approaches offer enhanced navigation performance in dynamic scenes but critically lack explicit constraint guarantees, posing significant challenges for safety and reliability in unforeseen "corner cases." They also often face high training costs and difficulties in "sim-to-real" transfer, especially when relying on high-fidelity simulations or raw sensor data.

The current research landscape sees a growing interest in hybrid methods that combine learning with optimization to leverage the strengths of both: the implicit reasoning and adaptability of learning, and the explicit safety and constraint guarantees of optimization. However, existing hybrid methods (e.g., GO-MPC, AC-MPC) still contend with challenges. Many do not effectively handle both complex multi-agent interactions and environmental structural constraints simultaneously, or fail to fully account for the robot's kinematic constraints. Furthermore, high training costs and dependence on high-fidelity simulations for effective sim-to-real transfer remain prevalent issues.

This paper positions itself directly at the forefront of this hybrid approach. It seeks to overcome the aforementioned limitations by proposing a novel hierarchical framework that effectively integrates DRL and MPC. Specifically, it addresses the need for robust constraint enforcement (kinematics, static, and dynamic obstacles) while enabling efficient learning and effective generalization in heterogeneous environments, all while striving to reduce the heavy reliance on high-fidelity simulation and expensive training processes that plague many DRL-based or hybrid methods. By focusing on local goal recommendations and a refined training strategy, the authors aim to push the boundaries of safe and efficient autonomous navigation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a novel, robust, and efficient hierarchical framework for autonomous robot navigation in dynamic environments characterized by **heterogeneous constraints**. These constraints include the presence of dynamic obstacles (e.g., pedestrians), diverse static obstacles (e.g., walls, circular, and polygonal obstacles), and the robot's own nonholonomic kinematic limitations.

The core motivations behind this work stem from several key challenges inherent in existing robot navigation solutions:

1. **Inaccurate Cost-to-Go Estimation in Dynamic Environments:** Traditional planning methods struggle to accurately estimate the "cost-to-go" in highly dynamic scenarios, leading to sub-optimal global trajectories and susceptibility to issues like the "Freezing Robot Problem." Learning-based methods offer implicit estimation but lack safety guarantees.
2. **Balancing Performance and Safety:** Purely learning-based methods, while adept at implicit modeling of complex interactions, typically lack explicit constraint guarantees, making them unsafe in critical "corner cases" and hindering their deployment in real-world safety-critical applications. Conversely, purely optimization-based methods, while safe, suffer from high computational complexity and limited perception range, often requiring simplified assumptions or reliance on sub-optimal reference paths.
3. **High Training Costs and Sim-to-Real Transfer Issues:** Hybrid learning-optimization methods, despite their promise, often face significant training costs due to the complexity of integrating neural networks with optimization solvers. Furthermore, they frequently struggle with "sim-to-real" transfer, as policies learned in high-fidelity simulations may not generalize well to the complexities and uncertainties of the real world. Many also rely on high-fidelity simulations or dense sensor inputs, which are expensive.
4. **Simultaneous Handling of Diverse Constraints:** Few existing methods can simultaneously and effectively account for complex multi-agent interactions, various types of static environmental structures, and the robot's specific kinematic constraints within a single coherent framework.

To address these motivations, the paper sets out to achieve the following specific objectives:

- **Develop a hybrid DRL-MPC framework:** Integrate the implicit reasoning capabilities of DRL (specifically, a GNN-based frontend) for dynamic scene understanding and local goal recommendation with the explicit constraint guarantees of an MPC-based backend for trajectory generation.
- **Improve cost-to-go estimation and local goal generation:** Design a GNN-based frontend to aggregate heterogeneous environmental information and efficiently recommend step-wise local goals that implicitly estimate the cost-to-go.
- **Ensure explicit constraint enforcement:** Utilize an MPC-based backend to formulate and solve a non-convex optimization problem that explicitly incorporates obstacle avoidance (for both dynamic and static obstacles) and the robot's kinematic constraints.
- **Enhance training efficiency and sim-to-real adaptability:** Introduce novel training mechanisms, specifically an "incremental action-masking mechanism" and a "privileged learning strategy," to enable low-cost, highly efficient, end-to-end training in low-fidelity simulation environments, thereby facilitating better sim-to-real transfer.
- **Provide high-quality initializations for optimization:** Design a spatio-temporal path-searching module that refines the neural network's local goal output into a high-dimensional reference trajectory, serving as a robust initialization for the MPC problem.

By achieving these objectives, the authors aim to develop a state-of-the-art navigation planner that is both highly safe and socially compliant, capable of operating effectively in complex, dynamic real-world scenarios.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, dubbed HALO (Hierarchical Learning-Enhanced MPC), adopts a novel hierarchical framework that intelligently combines Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) with Model Predictive Control (MPC) to enable safe and efficient robot navigation.

#### 3.1. Hierarchical Framework Overview

The core idea is a two-stage process:
1. **DRL-based Frontend:** A Graph Neural Network (GNN) acts as a high-level policy, taking heterogeneous environmental observations as input and outputting a recommended *local goal*. This implicitly estimates the cost-to-go.
2. **MPC-based Backend:** This module refines the local goal into a kinematically feasible and collision-free *trajectory*. It explicitly enforces safety constraints against dynamic and static obstacles.

#### 3.2. Problem Formulation

The motion planning problem is formulated as an optimal control problem, which is decomposed into two sub-problems:
- **Problem 1 (High-Level):** Find a local waypoint (p_ref_t) with minimal cost-to-go within the robot's perceptual range. This is solved by a learnable policy network (π_θ) through DRL.
- **Problem 2 (Low-Level):** Solve an optimization problem using p_ref_t as the terminal state, considering obstacle avoidance and kinematic constraints, to obtain a locally optimal control policy (π_L).

The robot's kinematics are explicitly modeled as a differential-drive system, incorporating velocity and acceleration limits, which is a significant departure from simplified omnidirectional models often used in RL.

#### 3.3. Scenario to Markov Decision Process (MDP)

The navigation problem is cast as an MDP. The state space (S) is represented as a heterogeneous graph, where nodes are entities in the scene (ego-robot, pedestrian, static circular obstacle, line obstacle). Each node has a feature vector (h) incorporating conventional state variables (e.g., position, velocity, distance to goal) and "velocity obstacle (VO) vector" features, including an artificial state variable (ζ) to quantify collision risk based on expected collision time. Edges represent implicit interactions.

The action space (A) is *discrete*, consisting of D candidate local goal points in the robot's neighborhood. The reward function (R) is a composite of sparse (collision, goal), step-dependent, orientation-guiding, velocity-collision-risk-aware, and dense social-aware components, balancing safety, efficiency, and social compliance.

#### 3.4. Neural Network Architecture

The DRL frontend utilizes an Actor-Critic architecture. Both the policy (actor) and value (critic) networks use GNN layers for feature extraction, followed by fully connected layers. The GNN's output is concatenated with the robot's features to form a fixed-length observation. The policy network outputs logits for the D discrete local goal candidates, which are then converted into a probability distribution via softmax.

#### 3.5. Incremental Action Masking Mechanism

A novel "incremental action masking" mechanism is introduced to address the problem of infeasible local goals during DRL exploration. Two types of invalid actions are identified: 1) spatially unreachable (e.g., inside a static obstacle), and 2) spatially reachable but rendering the backend MPC problem infeasible. Invalid actions are masked by setting their corresponding logits to a large negative number, effectively making their selection probability negligible. This mechanism provides a form of supervised learning, guiding the RL agent away from infeasible regions, significantly aiding convergence and mitigating sparse reward problems.

#### 3.6. Spatio-Temporal Path Search (G)

This module bridges the gap between the DRL frontend's local goal output and the MPC backend's need for a full reference trajectory. It leverages explicit predictions of dynamic obstacle trajectories (using a previous work, PMM-Net) and constructs a spatio-temporal (S-T) graph. The A* algorithm is then employed to search this DAG for an optimal path (P*) from the robot's current state to the recommended local goal (p_ref), considering kinematic constraints via a set of seven motion primitives. A custom cost function in the S-T graph penalizes path length, reversals, turns, and deviations from a straight-line trajectory, while prioritizing nodes with larger time values to ensure real-time performance. Velocity pruning is used to restrict node expansion, improving search efficiency.

#### 3.7. Optimization-based Trajectory Refinement (MPC)

The backend uses a Model Predictive Control (MPC) framework to generate a collision-free and kinematically feasible local trajectory (T_opt). It takes the reference path (P_ref) from the S-T search as an initialization. The MPC formulates a non-convex optimization problem (8) that minimizes a cost function over a prediction horizon (N=10 steps). Constraints include:
- **Robot Dynamics:** Discretized using the fourth-order Runge-Kutta method.
- **Dynamic Obstacle Avoidance:** Enforced using predicted trajectories of pedestrians.
- **Static Obstacle Avoidance:** For convex polygonal obstacles, signed distance is reformulated into smooth and differentiable hyperplane separation constraints, leveraging strong duality.
- **Kinematic Constraints:** Explicitly enforced on the robot's control inputs (accelerations).
The problem is solved using CasADi/Ipopt during training and Forces Pro during testing to ensure real-time performance.

#### 3.8. Privileged Reinforcement Learning

To address the temporal inconsistency and trajectory jitter that can arise when the policy network optimizes only for immediate rewards, a "privileged learning" strategy is implemented. During training, the environment is simulated forward for M steps (M ≤ N) based on the MPC-generated trajectory (T_opt). Rewards (R_k_s) related to safety (collision, social, velocity-risk) are accumulated over this M-step horizon, then back-propagated with a discount factor (λ_p) to evaluate the overall reward (R(S_tilde)) for the current rollout. Crucially, the policy network (the "student") only has access to the current observable state (S_t), while the value network (critic) uses this privileged, future-aware reward for overall value evaluation. This design prevents data leakage while enabling the policy to learn temporally consistent decisions.

#### 3.9. Curriculum Learning

The training process adopts curriculum learning, starting with simple environments (e.g., one static obstacle, one pedestrian) and gradually increasing complexity (more static obstacles, rectangular obstacles, more pedestrians) across four phases. This progressive difficulty helps stabilize the DRL training process.

This comprehensive methodology, integrating sophisticated DRL components with a robust MPC backend, novel training strategies, and explicit kinematic modeling, aims to deliver a state-of-the-art solution for safe and efficient crowd navigation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents extensive simulation and real-world experimental results to validate the effectiveness and robustness of the proposed Hierarchical Learning-Enhanced MPC (HALO) framework.

#### 4.1. Simulation Experiments

- **State-of-the-Art (SOTA) Performance:** HALO achieved superior performance across key metrics compared to eight baseline methods (A*+DWA, NH-ORCA, TEB, MPC-D-CBF, SG-D3QN, RL-RVO, HGAT-DRL, SAGE).
 - **Success Rate (SR):** HALO demonstrated the highest success rate at 99.4%, significantly outperforming all baselines (e.g., SAGE at 98%, HGAT-DRL at 92%, RL-RVO at 86%).
 - **Collision Rate (CR):** Correspondingly, it achieved the lowest collision rate at 0.5%, indicating high safety.
 - **Disturbance to Humans (DN):** HALO recorded the lowest average number of intrusions into humans' social comfortable distance (DN = 23), highlighting its strong social awareness and ability to reduce pedestrian disturbance. This is significantly lower than SAGE (110.8) and HGAT-DRL (243.4).
 - **Navigation Time (NT):** While slightly slower than the fastest baseline (16.57s vs. 13.33s for RL-RVO), this trade-off is considered acceptable given the significant improvements in safety and social compliance.
- **Ablation Study:** Critical components of HALO were ablated to demonstrate their individual contributions:
 - **Incremental Action Masking:** This module proved to be the most crucial, directly enabling the learning process to converge by preventing the sampling of infeasible local goals during RL exploration. Without it, the system failed to converge.
 - **Privileged Learning:** This significantly improved the temporal consistency of planned trajectories, reducing oscillations between planning steps and enhancing overall navigation quality.
 - **Spatio-Temporal Joint Search:** Its direct contribution to overall performance was relatively minor when the network was well-trained, as the frontend already guides the robot away from densely interactive regions. However, in an "MPC only" scenario (without the frontend network's guidance), the S-T joint search module substantially improved the success rate by 8%, validating its effectiveness in complex interactive environments when the high-level guidance is absent or insufficient.
- **Discretization Resolution:** Performance improved with increasing action space discretization, stabilizing beyond 81 discrete points, which was adopted for all other experiments.
- **Generalization Capability:** HALO exhibited superior adaptability to varying crowd sizes (2 to 10 pedestrians or static circular obstacles). Its performance advantage over baselines widened as scene complexity increased, demonstrating robust generalization, especially compared to traditional methods that degraded rapidly.

#### 4.2. Hardware Experiments

- **Real-World Feasibility and Sim-to-Real Transfer:** HALO was successfully implemented and deployed on a custom four-wheel skid-steer robot. The experimental setup used an onboard perception system (LiDAR SLAM for static map, stereo camera + LiDAR for dynamic object detection, Kalman filter for tracking, and ReID for occlusion mitigation) communicating via ROS.
- **Consistent Performance:** The real-world results largely mirrored those in simulation, demonstrating HALO's practical feasibility. While overall performance for all methods was slightly lower in real-world scenarios (due to increased spatial constraints and perception limitations), HALO still significantly outperformed SAGE and HGAT-DRL in terms of SR, CR, and DN (e.g., SR of 84% for HALO vs. 72% for SAGE and 39% for HGAT-DRL).
- **Qualitative Observations:** Visualizations of real-world scenarios showcased HALO's advanced decision-making:
 - **Anticipatory Navigation:** The robot could choose different topological paths based on pedestrian motion trends to minimize interactions.
 - **Adaptive Following:** Successfully followed slow pedestrians, maintaining social distance, and accelerating when paths cleared.
 - **Proactive Avoidance:** Executed backward motions, waited for pedestrians, and made detours to avoid collisions and dense interaction regions, demonstrating robust and safe behavior even in challenging, non-yielding crowd scenarios.

In summary, the results strongly support the claims of the paper, demonstrating that the hierarchical DRL-MPC framework with its novel training enhancements achieves SOTA safety and social compliance, good navigation efficiency, and robust generalization in both simulated and real-world dynamic, heterogeneous, and kinematically constrained environments.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presented in this paper, "Hierarchical Learning-Enhanced MPC for Safe Crowd Navigation with Heterogeneous Constraints," makes several significant contributions and has substantial potential impact on the field of autonomous robotics.

#### 5.1. Addresses Core Challenges in Robotic Navigation

The most profound significance of this work lies in its successful integration of the complementary strengths of Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) and Model Predictive Control (MPC). By leveraging DRL for implicit reasoning and high-level local goal recommendations, and MPC for explicit, hard-constraint enforcement at the low level, the framework effectively navigates the long-standing trade-off between performance (adaptability to dynamic scenes) and safety (guaranteed collision avoidance and kinematic feasibility). This hybrid approach directly tackles the limitations of purely learning-based methods (lack of safety guarantees) and purely optimization-based methods (computational burden, limited planning horizon, and difficulty with complex interactions).

#### 5.2. Enhances Safety and Social Compliance

The achieved state-of-the-art success rates (99.4%) and minimal collision rates (0.5%) in complex dynamic environments are critical for practical deployment. Furthermore, the significantly reduced disturbance to humans (lowest DN score) highlights the framework's ability to generate socially aware and compliant trajectories. This is crucial for robots operating in human-centric environments (e.g., airports, hospitals, public spaces), fostering greater acceptance and trust in autonomous systems. The explicit constraint guarantees from the MPC backend also ensure robustness in "out-of-distribution" scenarios or corner cases where learning-based methods might fail.

#### 5.3. Improves Training Efficiency and Sim-to-Real Transfer

The introduction of the "incremental action masking mechanism" and "privileged learning strategy" is a pivotal contribution for practical DRL training. These mechanisms enable the entire hybrid planner to be trained end-to-end in low-fidelity simulation environments. This significantly reduces computational costs and time associated with training, which is a major bottleneck for many complex DRL systems that often require high-fidelity simulations or extensive real-world data. The GNN-based frontend, coupled with these training strategies, also enhances the sim-to-real generalization capability, as demonstrated by the successful deployment and performance on a physical robot.

#### 5.4. Holistic Constraint Handling

The proposed method effectively integrates consideration for various types of constraints simultaneously:
- **Dynamic Obstacles:** Handled through explicit trajectory prediction and spatio-temporal path searching.
- **Heterogeneous Static Obstacles:** Addressed through a combination of traditional occupancy grid approaches and sophisticated hyperplane separation constraints for polygonal obstacles.
- **Robot Kinematics:** Explicitly modeled and enforced within the MPC framework, moving beyond idealized kinematic assumptions common in many RL works.
This comprehensive approach makes the planner applicable to a wide range of real-world scenarios with diverse environmental challenges.

#### 5.5. Potential for Broader Applications

The demonstrated feasibility and performance of HALO suggest its significant potential impact across various domains requiring autonomous navigation:
- **Logistics and Delivery:** Robots in warehouses, airports, or hotels can navigate safely and efficiently amidst human workers and varying static layouts.
- **Healthcare:** Service robots in hospitals can deliver supplies without interfering with staff or patients.
- **Autonomous Driving:** The core principles of robust, safe, and socially compliant navigation in dynamic, constrained environments are highly relevant, especially for urban last-mile delivery or public transport.
- **Exploration and Inspection:** Robots can operate more safely in dynamic, hazardous, or complex industrial settings.

#### 5.6. Future Directions and Continued Impact

The authors acknowledge limitations, such as not explicitly accounting for dynamic agent uncertainty and modal collapse in the frontend network. Their proposed future work—incorporating uncertainty into MPC, leveraging off-policy algorithms like Discrete SAC to mitigate modal collapse, and adapting the framework for robots with larger aspect ratios—points toward continued research and innovation that could further enhance the robustness and applicability of the framework. This self-critique demonstrates a healthy research outlook, indicating that the foundational work laid out here can serve as a strong basis for further advancements in safe and intelligent robot navigation.

In conclusion, this paper represents a substantial step forward in developing robust and deployable autonomous navigation systems. Its significance lies in solving complex problems at the intersection of learning and control, offering a practical and high-performing solution that addresses critical safety, efficiency, and social interaction challenges in dynamic, constrained environments.
