# 1000 Layer Networks for Self-Supervised RL: Scaling Depth Can Enable New Goal-Reaching Capabilities

> [!summary] Summary
> This research investigates scaling network depth in self-supervised reinforcement learning (RL) using Contrastive RL (CRL), showing that increasing depth up to 1000 layers leads to substantial performance gains. The approach enables the emergence of complex, qualitatively distinct behaviors across diverse locomotion, navigation, and manipulation tasks.

## Problem

- Reinforcement learning (RL) models typically remain shallow (2-5 layers), in contrast to the hundreds or thousands of layers used in other AI domains like NLP and CV.
- Prior attempts to increase network depth in RL often yielded only marginal benefits, instability, or even negative impacts.
- The lack of clear 'scaling laws' in RL limits the development of more general and powerful learning agents.

## Method

- Utilized ==Contrastive Reinforcement Learning (CRL)==, an actor-critic method, for goal-conditioned learning without explicit rewards, employing an ==InfoNCE objective== for the critic.
- Designed and trained very deep networks (up to ==1024 layers==) for both actor and critic, incorporating residual connections, Layer Normalization, and Swish activations to stabilize training.
- Conducted experiments on a diverse suite of **10** simulated environments using the GPU-accelerated ==JaxGCRL framework== to facilitate high-throughput data collection and training.

## Results

- Achieved performance improvements ranging from **2x** to over **50x** by increasing network depth from **4** to **64** layers, with continued gains observed up to **1024** layers.
- Unlocked qualitatively new capabilities and behaviors, such as a depth-256 Humanoid agent performing 'vaulting walls' or 'worming its way' over obstacles.
- Demonstrated that scaling network depth is more effective than scaling width, that joint actor and critic scaling is beneficial, and that deeper networks effectively leverage larger batch sizes.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Self-supervised learning, particularly CRL, is essential for enabling stable and effective depth scaling in RL, unlike other standard TD-based RL methods.
> - Deeper networks learn richer, more topological representations of the environment, moving beyond naive distance metrics and strategically allocating representational capacity.
> - The benefits of depth scaling stem from a synergistic enhancement of both exploration capabilities (collecting better data) and model expressivity (learning more effectively from that data).

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper addresses a critical and long-standing challenge in the field of artificial intelligence: replicating the immense success of "scaling laws" observed in other domains like Natural Language Processing (NLP) and Computer Vision (CV) within Reinforcement Learning (RL).

In NLP, models like Llama 3 (Dubey et al., 2024) and in CV, models such as Stable Diffusion 3 (Esser et al., 2024) and large Vision Transformers (Dehghani et al., 2023; Zhai et al., 2021), have demonstrated that increasing model size (including depth) can lead to breakthroughs, emergent capabilities, and performance jumps that are not merely linear improvements. These large models often rely heavily on self-supervised pre-training to leverage massive amounts of data.

In stark contrast, RL research has largely been restricted to relatively shallow network architectures, typically 2 to 5 layers deep (Raffin et al., 2021; Huang et al., 2022). Efforts to scale RL models, particularly in terms of depth, have often reported limited or even negative returns (Lee et al., 2024; Nauman et al., 2024b). The conventional wisdom attributed this to the "sparse feedback" problem in RL, where a small amount of reward signal struggles to effectively train a large number of parameters (LeCun, 2016). This has led many to believe that RL might primarily serve as a fine-tuning mechanism for models pre-trained via self-supervision in other modalities.

This paper directly challenges this prevailing view by proposing that self-supervised RL, when combined with appropriate architectural choices and computational infrastructure, *can* indeed benefit significantly from scaling network depth. It positions itself as a crucial step towards unlocking emergent phenomena in RL comparable to those seen in NLP and CV. The authors highlight that if RL is to develop its own "large models" that jointly optimize both the model and the data collection process through exploration, then effective scaling is paramount. By focusing on self-supervised goal-conditioned RL, the work directly taps into a paradigm that has shown promise for scaling in other domains and is well-suited for autonomous exploration without explicit reward functions or demonstrations. It contrasts with most prior RL scaling efforts that often target specific domains (e.g., imitation learning, multi-agent games, discrete actions) or rely on different architectural paradigms or training strategies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate whether increasing network depth can unlock substantial performance improvements and emergent capabilities in self-supervised reinforcement learning, akin to the breakthroughs observed in large-scale language and vision models.

The key motivations driving this work are:

1. **Bridging the Scaling Gap in RL:** Despite the transformative power of scaling in NLP and CV, RL has lagged, with most models remaining shallow. The authors aim to understand if this gap is inherent to RL or if the right "building blocks" can enable similar scaling benefits.
2. **Challenging Conventional Wisdom:** The long-held belief in RL is that sparse feedback makes it difficult to train very deep networks. This paper seeks to experimentally challenge this notion by demonstrating that depth can be a critical factor for scalability in a self-supervised RL setting.
3. **Harnessing Self-Supervision for RL Scalability:** Recognizing the success of self-supervised learning as a foundation for large models in other domains, the authors hypothesize that integrating self-supervision directly into RL (Self-Supervised RL) might be the key to overcoming the feedback sparsity problem and enabling deeper networks.
4. **Unlocking Emergent Behaviors:** Beyond mere performance gains, the motivation includes exploring whether increased depth can lead to the emergence of qualitatively new and more complex behaviors, suggesting implicit skill acquisition and a deeper understanding of the environment.
5. **Identifying Critical Scaling Components:** The research aims to empirically analyze which components are essential for effective depth scaling, including architectural choices (residual connections, normalization, activation functions), and the interplay with other factors like network width, batch size, and the actor-critic components.
6. **Advancing Goal-Conditioned RL:** By focusing on unsupervised goal-conditioned RL, the paper seeks to push the state-of-the-art in agents that can explore and learn to reach arbitrary goals without human intervention or dense rewards, which is a crucial aspect of building more general-purpose intelligent agents.

In essence, the overarching motivation is to lay foundational groundwork for building large, general-purpose RL systems that can autonomously learn complex skills by leveraging scale, mirroring the success of large models in other AI fields.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors propose an approach that integrates several key "building blocks" to enable unprecedented depth scaling in self-supervised reinforcement learning.

#### 3.1. Core Algorithm: Contrastive Reinforcement Learning (CRL)

The work leverages **Contrastive Reinforcement Learning (CRL)** (Eysenbach et al., 2022) as the underlying self-supervised RL algorithm. CRL is an actor-critic method designed for goal-conditioned problems without explicit reward functions or demonstrations.
- **Critic:** The critic, denoted `f_ϕ,ψ(s, a, g)`, is parameterized by two neural networks that produce state-action embeddings `ϕ(s, a)` and goal embeddings `ψ(g)`. Its output is the L2-norm between these embeddings: `∥ϕ(s, a) − ψ(g)∥²`. The critic is trained using the **InfoNCE objective** (Sohn, 2016), a contrastive loss that effectively classifies whether a given state-action pair and a goal belong to the same trajectory or not. This loss encourages similar embeddings for (state, action, future state) triples from the same trajectory and dissimilar embeddings for random goals.
- **Policy:** The policy `πθ(a | s, g)` is trained to maximize the critic's output, thereby learning actions that lead towards commanded goals by maximizing the likelihood of reaching those goals.

#### 3.2. Architectural Innovations for Depth Scaling

The central methodological innovation is the ability to train networks up to 1024 layers deep, a stark contrast to the typical 2-5 layer networks in RL. This is achieved by incorporating robust architectural elements:
- **Residual Connections:** Following the ResNet architecture (He et al., 2015), residual connections are integrated into both the actor and critic networks. Each residual block consists of four units: a Dense layer, Layer Normalization, and Swish activation. The depth of the network is defined as the total number of Dense layers across all residual blocks.
- **Layer Normalization:** (Ba et al., 2016) is used within each residual block to stabilize training by normalizing the activations across features.
- **Swish Activation:** (Ramachandran et al., 2018) is employed as the activation function, chosen for its demonstrated effectiveness in deep neural networks.

#### 3.3. Data Availability and Computational Efficiency

To facilitate large-scale experimentation, the authors build on recent GPU-accelerated RL frameworks:
- **JaxGCRL codebase:** (Bortkiewicz et al., 2024), which is based on Brax (Freeman et al., 2021) and MJX (Todorov et al., 2012) environments, enabling fast online goal-conditioned RL experiments. This framework supports efficient data collection and parallel training crucial for deep networks.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

- **Environments:** Experiments are conducted on a diverse suite of 10 challenging simulated locomotion, navigation, and robotic manipulation tasks from the JaxGCRL benchmark (e.g., Humanoid, Ant mazes, Arm Push/Binpick).
- **Reward Setting:** A sparse reward setting is used, where `r=1` only when the agent is in proximity to the goal, reflecting a challenging unsupervised learning scenario.
- **Evaluation Metric:** Performance is measured by the number of time steps (out of 1000) that the agent stays near the goal.

#### 3.5. Empirical Analysis and Ablations

The methodology includes extensive empirical studies to understand the factors contributing to scaling success:
- **Depth vs. Width:** Comparative analysis of scaling network depth against scaling network width, a common approach in prior RL work.
- **Actor vs. Critic Scaling:** Investigating the individual and combined effects of scaling the actor and critic networks.
- **Batch Size Scaling:** Examining how increased network depth interacts with larger batch sizes, addressing previous findings of limited benefits of large batches in shallow RL.
- **Qualitative Policy Analysis:** Visualizing learned behaviors at different depths to identify emergent skills.
- **Representation Analysis:** Analyzing the learned representations (Q-values and state-action embeddings) to understand how deep networks capture environment topology and allocate representational capacity.
- **Exploration-Expressivity Disentanglement:** Designing an experiment with a shared replay buffer ("collector" and "learners") to separate the effects of improved exploration (due to better policies) from increased model expressivity.
- **Generalization Studies:** Evaluating the ability of deeper networks to generalize to unseen tasks through "partial experience stitching."
- **Algorithm Specificity:** Ablations with other goal-conditioned baselines (SAC, TD3, GCBC, GCSL) to determine if depth scaling benefits are specific to CRL.
- **Offline RL:** Preliminary investigation into depth scaling for offline goal-conditioned RL.
- **Architectural Ablations:** Testing the importance of Layer Normalization and Swish activation.

This comprehensive approach allows the authors to not only demonstrate the efficacy of depth scaling but also to provide insights into *why* it works in the context of self-supervised RL.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper presents several significant findings that collectively challenge existing paradigms in Reinforcement Learning and open new avenues for research:

1. **Dramatic Performance Gains with Depth Scaling:**
 - Increasing network depth, particularly from the baseline of 4 layers up to 64, 256, and even 1024 layers, leads to **substantial performance improvements** across a diverse suite of locomotion, navigation, and manipulation tasks.
 - Performance gains range from **2× to over 50×** (e.g., 52× on Humanoid, 63× on Ant U5-Maze, 1051× on Humanoid Big Maze), far exceeding the marginal benefits typically reported for scaling in RL.
 - **State-of-the-art results:** The scaled CRL approach outperforms other standard goal-conditioned baselines, including SAC, SAC+HER, TD3+HER, GCSL, and GCBC, in 8 out of 10 environments.
 - **Continued improvements at extreme depths:** On challenging Humanoid maze tasks, performance continues to improve significantly even at depths of 256 and 1024 layers.

2. **Emergent and Qualitatively Distinct Policies:**
 - Performance gains are not gradual but often exhibit **pronounced jumps** at specific "critical depth" thresholds (e.g., 8 layers for Ant Big Maze, 64 layers for Humanoid U-Maze).
 - These jumps correlate with the **emergence of qualitatively distinct and complex behaviors**. For instance, a depth-4 Humanoid agent might collapse or throw itself, while a depth-16 agent learns to walk upright. A depth-256 agent in Humanoid U-Maze develops unique acrobatic skills like vaulting walls or shifting into a seated posture to navigate obstacles, capabilities not observed in shallower networks or prior goal-conditioned methods.

3. **Depth is More Effective than Width Scaling:**
 - While increasing network width does improve performance (consistent with prior work), **depth proves to be a more impactful axis for scaling**.
 - For example, doubling the depth from 4 to 8 layers (with fixed width) often outperforms networks with 2048 units of width (at depth 4) across environments, especially those with higher observation dimensionality (e.g., Humanoid).
 - **Computational Efficiency:** Depth scaling can be more computationally efficient, as parameter count scales linearly with width but quadratically with depth for fixed overall operations.

4. **Complementary Roles of Actor and Critic Scaling:**
 - Unlike prior work suggesting limited benefits or even degradation from actor scaling, this research finds that **scaling both the actor and critic networks can be complementary**. While critic scaling is often more impactful, actor scaling also contributes to overall performance, with some environments (e.g., Ant Big Maze) showing actor scaling to be more influential.

5. **Deeper Networks Unlock Batch Size Scaling:**
 - Traditionally, larger batch sizes have yielded limited or negative returns in RL. However, the study reveals that **as network depth increases, larger batch sizes become effective and further boost performance**. This suggests that shallow RL models might have obscured the benefits of larger batches, which are unlocked by sufficient model capacity.

6. **Mechanisms Behind Depth Scaling (Why it Works):**
 - **Enhanced Representation Learning:** Deeper networks learn richer, more informative representations that effectively capture environment topology. For instance, in U-Maze tasks, a depth-64 network accurately models the maze layout, identifying paths around walls, whereas a shallow network might rely on naive Euclidean distance.
 - **Synergistic Exploration and Expressivity:** Experiments show that depth scaling improves both exploration (by enabling better policies that gather more diverse data) and expressivity (the model's capacity to learn from that data). Crucially, these two factors work synergistically: deep networks require good data coverage to fully realize their capacity, and good exploration *is* enabled by deep networks.
 - **Targeted Representational Capacity:** Deeper networks appear to allocate greater representational capacity to critical state regions, particularly those near the goal or important for successful task completion, resulting in more "spread out" and discriminative embeddings for relevant states.
 - **Improved Generalization via Partial Experience Stitching:** Deeper networks exhibit significantly better generalization to unseen goal-conditioned tasks. In modified Ant U-Maze environments, depth-64 networks can effectively "stitch" together partial experiences to solve long-distance goals not encountered during training, indicating a capacity for combinatorial generalization.

7. **Algorithm Specificity:**
 - The benefits of depth scaling are highly dependent on the algorithm. While CRL, and to some extent other self-supervised methods like GCBC, greatly benefit from increased depth, **traditional temporal difference (TD) methods (SAC, SAC+HER, TD3+HER) show no or negative performance gains** beyond 4 layers. This highlights that the self-supervised nature of CRL is critical for leveraging deep architectures.
 - Preliminary experiments on offline goal-conditioned RL showed limited evidence of depth scaling improvements, suggesting it might be an important direction for future adaptation.

Overall, the findings demonstrate that by carefully integrating architectural best practices from deep learning into a self-supervised RL framework, it is possible to achieve substantial and qualitatively novel capabilities through model depth scaling, providing a strong counter-narrative to previous limitations in RL.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research marks a significant step forward in reinforcement learning, challenging long-held assumptions and offering a promising path toward more capable and general intelligent agents. Its potential impact spans several key areas:

1. **Revising the Paradigm of RL Model Design:** The paper fundamentally challenges the conventional wisdom that RL networks must be shallow due to sparse feedback. By demonstrating that scaling depth (up to 1024 layers) can unlock emergent capabilities and dramatically boost performance in self-supervised RL, it encourages a re-evaluation of architectural choices across the entire field. This could lead to a shift in RL research priorities, moving away from a sole focus on width scaling or algorithmic tweaks to embrace deeper, more expressive models.

2. **Unlocking "Scaling Laws" for RL:** The observed "critical depth" thresholds and emergent behaviors (e.g., complex locomotion, acrobatic maneuvers) directly parallel the "emergent abilities" seen in large language and vision models. This work suggests that RL might finally be on the verge of discovering its own set of scaling laws, paving the way for unprecedented capabilities as computational resources increase. Understanding these laws could accelerate the development of more general and intelligent RL agents.

3. **Advancing Self-Supervised RL:** The paper strongly reinforces the importance of self-supervised learning as a core component for scaling RL. By showing that depth scaling benefits are specific to CRL and other self-supervised methods, it highlights this paradigm as a key ingredient for future breakthroughs. This could lead to increased research into developing more robust and scalable self-supervised RL algorithms capable of autonomous skill acquisition without human-provided rewards or demonstrations.

4. **Toward General-Purpose AI Agents:** The ability of deeper self-supervised RL agents to explore, learn complex behaviors, and generalize to unseen tasks moves the field closer to building agents that can acquire a wide range of skills in complex, open-ended environments. Such agents would be crucial for real-world applications in robotics, autonomous navigation, and intelligent systems where explicit reward engineering or extensive demonstrations are impractical. The synergy between exploration and expressivity revealed in the paper is a vital insight for agents that must learn from scratch.

5. **New Research Directions:**
 - **Optimization Techniques:** The success of deep networks opens new avenues for research into optimization strategies, regularization, and initialization techniques specifically tailored for very deep RL architectures.
 - **Computational Efficiency:** While depth comes with a computational cost, the paper suggests it might be more parameter-efficient than width scaling in some cases. This could drive research into distributed training, model compression (pruning, distillation), and hardware-aware designs for deep RL.
 - **Offline RL Scaling:** The current limitations in offline depth scaling present a clear future research direction, crucial for leveraging large pre-collected datasets.
 - **Understanding Emergence:** Further theoretical and empirical work is needed to deeply understand the mechanisms by which increased depth leads to emergent behaviors and improved representations, including how representational capacity is allocated.

In conclusion, this paper provides compelling empirical evidence that scaling network depth, when combined with self-supervised learning and robust architectural components, is a powerful recipe for achieving substantial and qualitatively different capabilities in reinforcement learning. It challenges the status quo and offers a clear vision for how RL can pursue its own "large model" breakthroughs, potentially transforming the field into one that trains its own foundational models rather than solely relying on pre-training from other modalities.
