# From Failures to Supervision: DynamicEnvPlan for Robust Long-Horizon Embodied Planning

> [!summary] Summary
> DynamicEnvPlan is a closed-loop data-synthesis framework that turns execution failures into training supervision for long-horizon embodied household planning. A memory-enhanced planner proposes primitive actions, a perturbation engine injects environment and execution deviations, and a privileged corrector rewrites unsafe or stale proposals into recovery traces. Two-stage supervised fine-tuning of a 4B vision-language planner lifts test success from 33.3% to 76.2%.

## Problem

- Embodied benchmarks expose long-horizon failures but treat them purely as evaluation outcomes, never converting them into learnable supervision that teaches agents how to recover.
- Most embodied training data come from static demonstrations, navigation traces, or successful rollouts, giving planners almost no signal about acting under mid-execution state changes.
- Prior feedback, reflection, and state-tracking methods improve closed-loop decisions generally, yet none make dynamically induced failure states the central unit of supervision.

## Method

- **MeHLP**, a memory-enhanced high-level planner, proposes reasoning-action pairs over symbolic primitives from egocentric observation, semantic map, scene graph, and a five-step episodic memory of prior outcomes.
- **DynaPerturb** injects task-relevant, recoverable deviations: environment-level open/toggle/relocation state changes plus execution-level navigation drift, forced primitive failure, degraded execution, and placement regression.
- **DynaGuard**, a privileged corrector with simulator-state access, blocks unsafe proposals before execution and diagnoses failures after; approved or corrected traces drive two-stage SFT of **DynaPlanner**.

## Results

- Staged fine-tuning raises test success from **0.33** for the bare 4B backbone to **0.48** after stage one and **0.76** with recovery training, matching Claude Opus 4.6.
- DynaPlanner posts the best recovery score **0.69** and lowest repeated-invalid-action rate **0.16**, beating GPT-5.4 (0.33 recovery, 0.30 RIAR) despite far fewer parameters.
- Removing episodic memory collapses test success to **0.00** and dropping semantic memory costs 0.67 to 0.52; skipping the stage-one warm start yields only **0.19**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Failures are supervision, not noise: deliberately perturbing a simulator and labelling the corrected recovery step gives a data-efficient training signal.
> - Curriculum order matters: collecting perturbed trajectories before the model masters nominal procedures produces near-useless data, so warm-start adaptation comes first.
> - Recovery hinges on short-term memory of what was just attempted; caution, gains are simulator-only and safety plus affordance scores still trail frontier planners.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Embodied intelligence research aims to develop agents that can perceive, reason about, and interact with the physical world over extended periods. This often involves executing long-horizon, temporally coherent action sequences. A key challenge in this domain is the inherent dynamism of physical environments, where states can evolve due to external factors, execution uncertainty, or hidden transitions. Traditional embodied agents, including those powered by recent large language models (LLMs) and vision-language models (VLMs), have demonstrated capabilities in high-level planning by translating instructions into symbolic action sequences (e.g., Huang et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2025a). These models can generate plausible initial plans for tasks involving navigation, manipulation, and object state tracking.

However, the existing research landscape, particularly in benchmark development, has primarily focused on task completion and static reasoning abilities (e.g., Chen et al., 2026; Qiu et al., 2026; Lu et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2025b). While these benchmarks expose failures (such as a forgotten door closure or an object being moved), these failures are typically treated as terminal evaluation outcomes rather than as actionable signals for agent learning. The majority of embodied training data is collected from static demonstrations, successful rollouts, or navigation trajectories, providing limited supervision on how agents should recover from interaction failures in dynamic settings. Consequently, current embodied models often exhibit brittleness in real-world scenarios where environmental changes or execution uncertainties invalidate previously feasible plans.

This work addresses the gap in robust long-horizon embodied planning by focusing on recovery from dynamic interaction failures. It positions itself within a line of research that increasingly uses interaction traces, online feedback, and correction signals to improve sequential decision-making under closed-loop execution (e.g., Chen et al., 2025a; Wang et al., 2025). Methods involving structured feedback, clarification, and reflection (Lee et al., 2025; Park et al., 2024) and state-tracking with scene graphs or object-centric memory (Cifuentes, 2026; GX-Chen et al., 2025) also contribute to closed-loop decision-making. DynamicEnvPlan extends these efforts by making dynamically induced failure states the central unit of supervision, thereby converting task-relevant environment and execution deviations into explicit recovery-oriented training signals.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the robustness of embodied agents in dynamic environments by enabling them to recover from execution-time failures and adapt to evolving scene states. The authors identify a significant limitation in current embodied AI: while agents can generate initial plans, they often struggle when the environment deviates from expectations or when actions do not unfold as anticipated.

The motivation for DynamicEnvPlan stems from several observed deficiencies:
1. **Dynamic Environment Challenge**: Physical-world interaction is inherently non-stationary. Environments can change during execution (e.g., an object moving, a door closing), requiring agents to constantly update their world belief, detect deviations, and revise plans. Current VLM-based agents, despite their planning prowess, often lack the capability to handle these dynamic conditions, leading to unsafe or infeasible actions.
2. **Execution Uncertainty**: Beyond external changes, an agent's own actions can have uncertain outcomes (e.g., navigation drift, failed manipulation). These minor deviations can compound errors over long horizons, invalidating entire plan segments. Agents need mechanisms to monitor state consistency and synthesize recovery actions.
3. **Limitations of Existing Benchmarks and Data**: Existing embodied benchmarks expose failures, but typically treat them as metrics for evaluation rather than as learning opportunities. Furthermore, most training data for embodied agents is derived from static, successful demonstrations, which provides insufficient supervision for learning how to recover from failures in dynamic settings.
4. **Need for Recovery-Oriented Learning**: The paper argues that effective embodied agents must go beyond generating executable plans initially. They must continuously monitor for violated preconditions or execution-time failures and generate new actions to restore progress towards the task objective under updated environmental conditions. The central motivation is to convert these dynamic perturbations and failures into explainable, learnable recovery supervision.

To address these points, the key objectives include:
- Developing a closed-loop framework, DynamicEnvPlan, that can synthesize training data specifically designed to teach recovery from dynamic perturbations.
- Extending the OmniGibson simulator to support more realistic long-horizon interaction, including humanoid agents, high-level primitive skills, structured semantic memory, and controllable dynamic perturbations.
- Introducing structured environment representations (scene graphs and semantic maps) to provide agents with explicit abstractions for state tracking and spatial reasoning.
- Designing a training methodology that uses staged supervised fine-tuning, leveraging both nominal execution traces and perturbed recovery trajectories.
- Evaluating the trained agent's ability to generalize and adapt in dynamic environments across in-distribution (IID), compositional generalization (CG), and out-of-distribution (OOD) settings.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

DynamicEnvPlan is a closed-loop framework designed to generate recovery-oriented training supervision for high-level embodied planning in dynamic environments. The methodology involves an extended simulation environment, a three-component data synthesis design, and a two-stage supervised fine-tuning process for the planning agent.

**Simulation Environment and Extensions:**
The framework is built upon the OmniGibson simulator (from BEHAVIOR-1K), which has been extended to better support long-horizon embodied interaction in dynamic physical settings. Key extensions include:
- **Humanoid Robot Support**: Enables more realistic agent interaction.
- **High-Level Primitive Skills**: A finite set of parameterized symbolic commands (e.g., NAVIGATETO, GRASP, TOGGLEON, WIPE, DONE) that express task-level intents. A simulator-side Plan Execution interface validates preconditions and reports execution status (SUCCESS, DEGRADED SUCCESS, FAILURE).
- **Structured Semantic Memory**: Agents are provided with explicit intermediate abstractions for state tracking and spatial reasoning, including scene graphs and semantic maps.
- **Controllable Dynamic Perturbations**: Allows for the injection of realistic execution variability and failure modes, expanding task scenarios towards dynamic long-horizon execution.

**Problem Formulation:**
The agent, given a natural-language task instruction `g` and an initial scene `s_0`, interacts with the environment through primitive skills `A`. At each step `t`, the agent receives a decision context `x_t = (g, o_t, M_t, H_t, R_t)`, where `o_t` is egocentric observation, `M_t` is semantic memory, and `(H_t, R_t)` is episodic memory (recent observations, actions, reasoning traces, execution statuses). A high-level planner `P` proposes a reasoning-action output `y_P_t = (z_P_t, a_P_t)`. The environment state `s_t+1` can be affected by the agent's action `a_t` and a deviation `δ_t` representing execution uncertainty or exogenous changes. A "recovery decision point" is when the agent must revise its plan due to an updated observation-memory context to maintain safe and executable progress.

**Closed-Loop Data Synthesis Components:**
The data generation loop integrates three collaborative components:
1. **MeHLP (Memory-enhanced High-Level Planner)**: This is the synthesis-time planner, implemented using GPT-5.4. It conditions on the task instruction, egocentric observation, structured semantic memory (map and scene-graph relations), and recent episodic context (history of frames, reasoning, actions, outcomes) to propose a reasoning-action output `y_P_t = (z_P_t, a_P_t)`. MeHLP serves as the proposal generator for the training data.
2. **DynaPerturb (Context-Grounded Dynamic Perturbations)**: This deviation engine injects controlled, task-relevant, physically plausible, and recoverable dynamic perturbations. It covers two families of dynamics:
 - **Environment-level perturbations**: Modify the physical or semantic state *before* the next decision (e.g., flipping open/closed states of containers, changing appliance toggled states, relocating task-relevant objects).
 - **Execution-level perturbations**: Modify the outcome of an issued primitive *during or after* execution (e.g., navigation drift, forced action failure, degraded execution, placement regression).
 DynaPerturb targets stale scene belief, violated preconditions, unreliable primitive execution, and regressed task progress.
3. **DynaGuard (Privileged Correction for Recovery Traces)**: This is a privileged correction component used *only during data synthesis*. It receives the same context as MeHLP, the proposed action, simulator feedback, and synthesis-time access to the simulator's true state.
 - **Prevention mode**: Blocks high-risk or infeasible actions (e.g., accessing a closed container, unsafe placement) *before* execution.
 - **Reflection mode**: Acts *after* failed or degraded execution by diagnosing the failed precondition or changed state and suggesting a recovery action.
 If the MeHLP proposal is invalid or risky, DynaGuard produces a corrected reasoning-action output `y_C_t`. The approved (MeHLP's `y_P_t`) or corrected (`y_C_t`) output becomes the target `~y_t` for training.

**Closed-Loop Synthesis Procedure:**
At each step `t` of an episode, MeHLP proposes `y_P_t`. DynaPerturb may introduce `δ_t`. The simulator provides feedback `η_t`. DynaGuard then records the approved or corrected target `~y_t`. The environment state and agent's memory are updated. This process generates trajectories `τ = {(x_t, ~y_t, η_t)}` that capture both nominal task execution and recovery from dynamic deviations.

**DynaPlanner Training and Inference:**
The trainable high-level planner, DynaPlanner, is initialized from Qwen3VL-4B Thinking. It shares the same input-output interface as the synthesis loop but *does not* have privileged access or DynaGuard feedback during evaluation. Training is done in two stages of supervised fine-tuning (SFT):
1. **Stage 1 (Task-Execution Alignment)**: Uses synthetic task-execution traces generated by MeHLP and DynaGuard to teach the primitive interface, memory format, and long-horizon reasoning style.
2. **Stage 2 (Dynamic Recovery Alignment)**: Continues training from the Stage 1 model using successful trajectories collected under DynaPerturb-enabled execution, including execution-time failures and subsequent recovery attempts. This stage exposes the model to recovery-oriented reasoning traces from closed-loop, failure-prone states.

**Evaluation Protocol:**
Evaluation is conducted on 104 task-scene combinations, split into training, validation, and test sets. Held-out combinations are categorized into IID (seen task/scene), CG-Scene (seen task, unseen scene), CG-Task (unseen task, seen scene), and OOD (unseen task/scene) settings to assess generalization. The evaluation uses DynaPerturb to introduce dynamic states, but DynaPlanner operates without DynaGuard intervention.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings regarding the effectiveness of DynamicEnvPlan and the DynaPlanner agent.

**Overall Performance Improvement:**
The full DynaPlanner, trained with both stages of supervised fine-tuning, significantly improved task success rates. On the test set, the base planner (w/o SFT-stage1) achieved a 33.3% success rate (SR), which increased to 48% with Stage 1 SFT (w/o SFT-stage2), and further to 76.2% with the full two-stage training (DynaPlanner). Validation success rates also rose from 0% to 60%. This demonstrates that the closed-loop perturbed trajectories provide useful supervision beyond nominal task-execution traces. A variant trained directly on perturbed trajectories without the Stage 1 warm-up achieved only 0.19 test SR, indicating the importance of initial task-execution alignment.

**Performance Across Evaluation Metrics:**
DynaPlanner improved across all seven evaluation metrics critical to physical-world interaction:
- **Task Success Rate (SR)**: Increased as described above.
- **Steps**: Maintained efficiency among completed tasks (DynaPlanner 1.08 vs. base 1.22).
- **Recovery**: DynaPlanner achieved the strongest Recovery score (0.69), indicating a higher ability to resume productive execution after invalid steps.
- **Repeated Invalid Action Rate (RIAR)**: DynaPlanner had the lowest RIAR (0.16), suggesting fewer instances of getting stuck in loops of invalid actions.
- **Safety/Resource Score (SRS)**: Improved to 0.94 for DynaPlanner compared to 0.79 for the base, reflecting better adherence to safety and resource consistency.
- **Affordance Compliance Score (ACS)**: Rose to 0.90 for DynaPlanner from 0.83 for the base, indicating higher quality of executable actions.

**Comparison with Larger Models:**
DynaPlanner matched the test success rate of Claude Opus 4.6 (0.76) and outperformed other large online planners (GPT-5.4, Qwen3.5-397B-A17B) on Recovery and RIAR. However, GPT-5.4 achieved slightly higher SRS (0.98) and ACS (0.96), suggesting continued room for improvement in safety and affordance consistency for DynaPlanner.

**Generalization Capabilities:**
Evaluation across held-out generalization settings revealed:
- **Overall Generalization**: DynaPlanner exhibited the strongest overall generalization.
- **CG-Task**: Performed particularly well, demonstrating transfer of seen procedural knowledge to new task families in familiar scene templates.
- **CG-Scene**: Remained more challenging, with unseen layouts reducing recovery stability, although DynaPlanner still performed comparably to w/o SFT-stage2.
- **OOD**: DynaPlanner was the only Qwen3VL-4B variant to successfully solve any out-of-distribution episode (33% SR), indicating some ability to handle novel tasks in novel scenes.

**Behavioral Insights:**
- **Stage 1 SFT**: Primarily stabilized nominal procedures like tool acquisition, preparation, target application, and completion judgment.
- **Stage 2 SFT**: Introduced disturbance-aware recovery behaviors, such as more frequent re-acquisition after failed tool use, continuation after interrupted operations, correction for changed open/toggled states, and fewer premature task terminations.

**Memory Ablation:**
A diagnostic memory ablation indicated the critical role of different memory components:
- **Episodic Memory**: Removing episodic memory (recent observations, actions, reasoning, outcomes) reduced test SR to 0.00, highlighting its essential role in recovery.
- **Semantic Memory**: Removing semantic memory (semantic map, scene graph) lowered test SR from 0.67 to 0.52, suggesting its importance for maintaining task-scene context, particularly for generalization.

**Stronger Backbone Validation:**
Validation with a Qwen3.5-9B backbone demonstrated that the gains from DynamicEnvPlan's methodology persist, with Stage 2 SFT raising SR from 0.52 (base) to 0.85 and improving other metrics.

**Matched Component Ablation (Appendix G):**
This ablation showed that exposure to dynamic failure states (DynaPerturb alone) already improves SR and Recovery. However, adding DynaGuard's privileged correction further significantly boosts performance, indicating the value of both introducing failures and providing expert guidance for recovery.

**Recovery Cost and Horizon Scaling (Appendix I):**
Analysis of recovery cost across different task horizons showed that DynaPlanner effectively reduced invalid and repeated invalid actions. For long-horizon tasks, these costs decreased substantially. Recovery latency after perturbations was low, with progress resuming within one or two decisions (mean 1.08 steps), and perturbations added only modest primitive overhead.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The DynamicEnvPlan framework introduces a methodological shift in training embodied agents, moving beyond static success-oriented learning to embrace failures as explicit, learnable signals. This approach has several significant implications and potential impacts on the field of embodied AI:

**Advancement in Robustness and Adaptability:**
The primary significance lies in enhancing the robustness and adaptability of embodied agents in dynamic and uncertain physical environments. By actively synthesizing and learning from perturbed trajectories and recovery-oriented supervision, DynamicEnvPlan equips agents with the ability to detect deviations, diagnose failures, and synthesize corrective actions. This directly addresses a major challenge in deploying embodied agents in real-world scenarios, where ideal conditions are rarely maintained.

**Novel Training Data Paradigm:**
The work proposes a novel closed-loop data synthesis paradigm where dynamic execution failures are systematically converted into explainable recovery supervision. This moves beyond passively observing failures in benchmarks to actively creating structured learning opportunities from them. The use of MeHLP for proposal generation, DynaPerturb for controlled deviation injection, and DynaGuard for privileged correction forms a powerful system for generating high-quality, recovery-focused training data.

**Enhanced Generalization Capabilities:**
The controlled evaluation across IID, compositional generalization (CG-Scene, CG-Task), and out-of-distribution (OOD) settings demonstrates that DynamicEnvPlan can foster improved generalization. This suggests that agents trained with this framework are better equipped to handle new tasks or operate in novel environments, a crucial step towards truly general-purpose embodied intelligence.

**Contribution to Simulation Environments:**
The extensions to the OmniGibson simulator, incorporating humanoid agents, high-level primitive skills, structured semantic memory, and controllable dynamic perturbations, provide a more realistic and powerful testbed for future research. This contribution is valuable for the broader embodied AI community, enabling more sophisticated studies of closed-loop decision-making under realistic physical and semantic constraints.

**Potential for Practical Applications:**
The improved recovery behavior, reduced repeated invalid actions, and enhanced safety/affordance compliance demonstrated by DynaPlanner have direct implications for practical applications of robotics in household and other complex environments. Agents that can gracefully recover from unexpected events or execution errors are more reliable and safer for human interaction.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
Despite its contributions, the paper acknowledges several limitations, which also highlight areas for future research:
- **Simulator-Reality Gap**: The environment remains a simulator, and behaviors may be simplified compared to real robots. Direct transfer of strong results to hardware is not guaranteed. Future work could focus on bridging this gap through sim-to-real transfer learning.
- **Privileged Supervision**: DynaGuard's reliance on privileged simulator state during data generation is essential for creating high-quality supervision but implies that the agent cannot generate such "expert" feedback at test time. Future research might explore ways to learn or approximate such corrective capabilities without privileged access.
- **Simulator Artifacts and Scale**: Simulator artifacts can affect data quality, and the current corpus size, while sufficient for controlled study, is modest compared to broad web-scale or robot-scale corpora. Scaling up data collection and addressing simulator fidelity are important considerations.
- **Focus on High-Level Planning**: The claims are primarily focused on long-horizon planning and recovery under closed-loop disturbances, not low-level motor control or robust real-world manipulation. Integrating robust low-level control with high-level planning remains a challenge.

In conclusion, DynamicEnvPlan represents a substantial step towards more robust and adaptive embodied AI by reframing dynamic failures as a rich source of learning signals. Its systematic approach to generating and utilizing recovery supervision offers a promising direction for developing intelligent agents capable of operating effectively in complex, dynamic real-world environments.
