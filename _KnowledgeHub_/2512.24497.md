# What Drives Success in Physical Planning with Joint-Embedding Predictive World Models?

> [!summary] Summary
> This research provides a systematic empirical study of Joint-Embedding Predictive World Models (JEPA-WMs), identifying the specific technical choices that contribute to their success in physical planning. It proposes optimized model configurations that consistently achieve higher success rates compared to existing baselines on diverse simulated and real-world robotic manipulation and navigation tasks.

## Problem

- A lack of comprehensive understanding regarding the specific technical choices that drive success in physical planning using Joint-Embedding Predictive World Models (JEPA-WMs).
- Prior JEPA-WM implementations often rely on ad-hoc design choices, hindering principled advancement and optimization for robotic tasks.
- The challenge of developing AI agents that can efficiently solve a wide range of physical tasks and generalize to unseen environments.

## Method

- Formalize ==JEPA-WMs as a unified recipe==, systematically varying and evaluating key components across ==model architecture==, ==training objectives==, and ==planning algorithms==.
- Conduct a ==component-wise empirical study== on diverse simulated (Metaworld, Push-T, Wall, PointMaze, Robocasa) and real-world (DROID) robotic tasks to isolate the impact of each design choice.
- Propose ==optimized JEPA-WM configurations== based on the comprehensive analysis, distinguishing between optimal settings for simulated versus complex real-world dynamics.

## Results

- The proposed optimal JEPA-WM models consistently outperformed established baselines like DINO-WM and V-JEPA-2-AC across most simulated and real-world environments.
- Proprioceptive input consistently improved planning success, and a **2-step** rollout loss was generally optimal for simulated tasks, while a **6-step** rollout was best for complex real-world dynamics.
- Nevergrad, a tuning-free planner, performed comparably to CEM on real-world manipulation data (DROID), offering a practical advantage by requiring less hyperparameter tuning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - DINO-style visual encoders consistently outperform V-JEPA encoders due to their superior fine-grained object segmentation capabilities, which are essential for precise manipulation and navigation.
> - While gradient-based planners excel on tasks with smooth cost landscapes, population-based methods like CEM and Nevergrad are more robust for complex, multi-modal cost landscapes encountered in robotics.
> - The benefits of model scaling (larger encoders and deeper predictors) are primarily observed in complex real-world data, whereas they offer limited or negative gains in simpler simulated environments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of artificial intelligence agents capable of solving a diverse range of physical tasks and generalizing to novel environments is a persistent challenge in AI. A recent approach to address this involves training a world model from state-action trajectories, which is then utilized with a planning algorithm. While planning is conventionally performed in the input space, a class of methods has emerged that optimize in the learned representation space of the world model. These methods, often referred to as Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPAs) adapted for world modeling, propose to enhance planning efficiency by abstracting irrelevant details within the learned latent space.

The paper identifies this family of models as Joint-Embedding Predictive World Models (JEPA-WMs). Unlike other prominent model-based reinforcement learning (MBRL) approaches such as MuZero, PlaNet, or the Dreamer series, JEPA-WMs distinguish themselves by training the dynamics model solely through a predictive loss in the embedding space. This training objective excludes reconstruction, reward prediction, or value/policy heads. Previous work within this family, including PLDM (Sobal et al., 2025), DINO-WM (Zhou et al., 2024a), DINO-World (Baldassarre et al., 2025), and V-JEPA-2-AC (Assran et al., 2025), has demonstrated capabilities in areas such as 2D navigation, goal-conditioned planning, dense prediction, intuitive physics, and object manipulation. This research aims to provide a systematic investigation into the technical choices that contribute to the success of these JEPA-WM algorithms, thereby contributing to a more principled understanding of their design.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching motivation for this research is to advance the capabilities of physical AI agents, specifically concerning their ability to solve diverse physical tasks and generalize to unseen scenarios. While JEPA-WMs represent a promising direction in this regard, the authors identify a gap in the existing literature: a comprehensive study characterizing the technical choices that drive the success of these models.

The primary objectives of this study are:
- **Characterization of JEPA-WMs**: To formally define and characterize JEPA-WMs as a unified implementation recipe, distinguishing them from other world modeling paradigms.
- **Investigation of Key Components**: To conduct a detailed study of several critical components influencing the training and planning success of JEPA-WMs. These components include model architecture (predictor architecture, depth, size), training objectives (multistep rollout, context length, proprioception), and planning algorithms (optimizers, cost functions).
- **Identification of Optimal Approaches**: To identify the empirically optimal approaches within the JEPA-WM family by systematically varying these components.
- **Proposal of an Improved Model**: To integrate the insights gained from this study into a refined JEPA-WM design that demonstrates improved performance compared to established baselines, specifically DINO-WM and V-JEPA-2-AC, across a range of navigation and manipulation tasks.

The motivation stems from the increasing prevalence and observed performance of JEPA-WMs, necessitating a deeper understanding of the factors that contribute to their effectiveness in learning dynamics models within the embedding space of pretrained visual encoders for robotic control.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research investigates the impact of various design choices within the JEPA-WM framework, which involves a frozen visual encoder and a learned predictor. The methodology encompasses detailed analyses of both the training and planning procedures.

#### 3.1. JEPA-WM Formalization and Training

A JEPA-WM operates on a dynamical system where states, observations, and actions are represented in feature spaces. The core components are:
- **Encoders**: A frozen visual encoder ($E_{vis_\phi}$) embeds visual observations ($o_t$). An optional shallow proprioceptive encoder ($E_{prop_\theta}$) processes proprioceptive input. The combined global state encoder is denoted $E_{\phi,\theta} = (E_{vis_\phi}, E_{prop_\theta})$. An action encoder ($A_\theta$) embeds robotic actions ($a_t$).
- **Predictor**: A predictor ($P_\theta$) takes state and action embeddings as input to predict future state embeddings. $E_{prop_\theta}$, $A_\theta$, and $P_\theta$ are jointly trained, while $E_{vis_\phi}$ remains frozen.
- **Training Objective**: The primary training objective is a predictive loss, typically Mean Squared Error (MSE), computed between the predicted next state embedding and the ground-truth next state embedding (Equation 1). This is referred to as "teacher-forcing loss." The predictor is trained with a frame-causal attention mask, enabling it to predict from various context lengths up to a maximum of $W-1$. Actions can be frame-skipped, forming higher-dimensional action inputs ($f \times A$).
- **Multistep Rollout Training**: To improve robustness to compounding errors during multi-step unrolling at planning time, additional loss terms ($L_k$) are introduced. These terms train the predictor to minimize errors at horizons $k > 1$, where the input for prediction incorporates previous predictions. Truncated backpropagation over time (TBPTT) is applied, discarding gradients accumulated in earlier prediction steps.
- **Predictor Architecture**: The study primarily uses the ViT architecture for the predictor, aligning with baselines. Various action conditioning schemes are evaluated:
 - **Feature Conditioning with Sincos Positional Embedding**: Action embeddings are concatenated with visual features, increasing the embedding dimension. 3D sinusoidal positional embeddings are used.
 - **Sequence Conditioning with RoPE**: Actions are encoded as separate tokens and concatenated with visual tokens along the sequence dimension, maintaining the original embedding dimension. Rotary Position Embeddings (RoPE) are used.
 - **Feature Conditioning with RoPE**: Combines feature concatenation with RoPE.
 - **Adaptive Layer Normalization (AdaLN) with RoPE**: Action embeddings modulate Layer Normalization statistics within each transformer block, allowing action information to influence all layers. An AdaLN-zero variant, which initializes the conditioning MLP to output a zero-vector, is also tested.

#### 3.2. Planning Procedure

Planning involves optimizing a sequence of actions ($a_{t:t+H-1}$) over a horizon $H$ to minimize a planning objective ($L_{p_\alpha}$).
- **Unrolling Function**: A recursive unrolling function ($F_{\phi,\theta}$) is defined, where the predictor generates a sequence of predicted state embeddings based on an initial state and the action sequence (Equations 3, 4). A sliding context window ($W_p$) of previous predictions is used.
- **Planning Objective**: The objective function (Equation 2) calculates the dissimilarity between the predicted final state embedding and the goal state embedding ($E_{\phi,\theta}(o_g)$). This dissimilarity can be based on $L_1$ or $L_2$ distances for visual embeddings ($L_{vis}$) and an optional proprioceptive loss ($L_{prop}$), weighted by $\alpha$.
- **Planning Optimizers**: Four optimizers are compared for solving the differentiable planning problem:
 - **Cross-Entropy Method (CEM)**: A population-based method that iteratively refines a multivariate Gaussian distribution over action sequences by selecting top-performing samples.
 - **NeverGrad (NG)**: A meta-optimizer that adaptively selects optimization algorithms (e.g., a variant of CMA-ES) for black-box optimization.
 - **Adam**: A gradient-based optimizer, directly optimizing action sequences through backpropagation.
 - **Gradient Descent (GD)**: A basic gradient-based optimizer.
- **Model Predictive Control (MPC)**: For some environments, MPC is used, where the plan is re-optimized after executing a portion of the planned actions in the environment.

#### 3.3. Studied Design Choices and Evaluation

The research systematically varies individual components from a base configuration (DINO-WM without proprioception, ViT-S encoder, depth-6 predictor) to isolate their effects. These components include:
- Visual encoder type (DINOv2, DINOv3, V-JEPA, V-JEPA-2).
- Predictor architecture and depth (3, 6, 9, 12 layers).
- Multistep rollout training (1-step to 6-step losses).
- Training context length ($W$).
- Inclusion of proprioception.
- Model size scaling (ViT-S, B, L).
- Data augmentation techniques.
- Planning optimizer (CEM, NG, Adam, GD) and cost metric ($L_1$, $L_2$).

Experiments are conducted on both simulated environments (Metaworld, PointMaze, Push-T, Wall) and real-world robotic data (DROID, Robocasa), evaluating success rate and other metrics like embedding space error, proprioceptive decoding error, and visual decoding quality.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study systematically investigated various design choices, yielding several key findings:

- **Planning Optimizers**: The CEM L2 planner demonstrated the most consistent performance across all tasks. Gradient-based methods (Adam, GD) performed well on environments with smooth cost landscapes, such as Metaworld, but were susceptible to local minima in tasks requiring non-greedy planning (e.g., 2D navigation tasks like Wall, Push-T, Maze, and complex manipulation tasks like DROID). Nevergrad (NG) achieved comparable performance to CEM on real-world manipulation data (DROID, Robocasa) while requiring less hyperparameter tuning, indicating its practical utility. L2 distance consistently outperformed L1 distance as the planning cost metric.

- **Multistep Rollout Training**: Training with multistep rollout losses generally improved performance, with an optimal range observed. For simulated environments, a 2-step rollout loss was most effective, whereas for DROID, a 6-step rollout loss yielded better results. This behavior is attributed to an accuracy-robustness trade-off, where multistep training helps the model maintain predictions closer to the data manifold over multiple autoregressive steps, mitigating compounding errors.

- **Impact of Proprioception**: Models incorporating proprioceptive input consistently achieved higher success rates than those relying solely on visual input. Proprioception provides precise metric information (e.g., joint positions), which complements visual embeddings, particularly for resolving fine-grained positional discrepancies near goal states that visual features alone might not resolve effectively. This was especially noticeable in Metaworld tasks where visual-only models exhibited oscillation around the target.

- **Training Context Size (W)**: The study confirmed that the planning context window ($W_p$) should not exceed the training context window ($W$). An increase from $W=1$ to $W=2$ or $W=3$ significantly improved performance, suggesting that the predictor benefits from sufficient context to infer dynamics such as velocity. For complex, real-world dynamics (DROID), a longer context ($W=5$) was found to be optimal, likely enabling the model to capture richer temporal dependencies. However, excessively long contexts (e.g., $W=14$) could negatively impact performance on datasets with limited trajectory diversity by reducing the number of unique training slices.

- **Encoder Type**: DINO encoders (DINOv2, DINOv3) demonstrated superior performance compared to V-JEPA encoders. This is hypothesized to be due to DINO's better fine-grained object segmentation capabilities, which are crucial for tasks involving precise perception of agent and object locations. DINOv3 showed a particular advantage in photorealistic environments (Robocasa, DROID), likely because its pretraining data aligns better with such imagery, whereas DINOv2 was sufficient for simpler synthetic environments.

- **Predictor Architecture**: AdaLN with RoPE positional embeddings generally achieved the best average performance across environments. This is attributed to AdaLN's ability to modulate predictor layers throughout its depth, ensuring consistent influence of action information. While RoPE did not show a substantial improvement over sincos positional embeddings in isolation, AdaLN with RoPE offered a compute-efficient and empirically effective conditioning scheme.

- **Model Scaling**: On simulated environments, increasing encoder size (from ViT-S to ViT-L) or predictor depth (from 3 to 12 layers) did not result in performance improvements, suggesting saturation at smaller model capacities for these simpler tasks. Conversely, for real-world data (DROID, Robocasa), both larger encoders and deeper predictors correlated positively and consistently with improved planning performance, indicating that high-capacity models are beneficial for environments with complex, high-dimensional dynamics.

- **Data Scaling**: Performance for all evaluated methods consistently improved with increased training data, highlighting the importance of data quantity for world model generalization. The proposed JEPA-WM maintained its performance advantage across different data scales, particularly on DROID and Wall.

**Proposed Optimal JEPA-WMs**:
Based on these findings, the study proposes optimal model configurations tailored to task complexity:
- **For simulated environments**: A ViT-S encoder (DINOv2), a depth-6 ViT-S predictor, AdaLN conditioning with RoPE, proprioception, 2-step rollout loss, and a maximum context length of $W=3$.
- **For DROID and Robocasa**: A ViT-L encoder (DINOv3), a depth-12 ViT-L predictor, AdaLN conditioning with RoPE, no proprioception (due to dataset specificities), 6-step rollout loss, and a maximum context length of $W=5$.
- **Planning**: CEM L2 planner for all environments.

This optimized JEPA-WM consistently outperformed DINO-WM and V-JEPA-2-AC in most navigation and manipulation tasks, with qualitative examples demonstrating improved action consequence prediction.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the understanding and development of model-based physical AI agents by systematically analyzing the components of Joint-Embedding Predictive World Models (JEPA-WMs). The detailed empirical study of model architecture, training objectives, and planning algorithms provides a foundational characterization of this class of models.

**Significance**:
- **Empirical Best Practices**: The study provides practical guidelines for practitioners, identifying specific design choices that enhance planning success across different types of robotic tasks. For instance, it highlights the importance of proprioceptive input, the benefits of multi-step rollout training (with task-dependent optimal steps), the superior performance of DINO-based visual encoders for fine-grained perception, and the effectiveness of AdaLN conditioning.
- **Model Scaling Insights**: The finding that model capacity scaling (larger encoders, deeper predictors) yields benefits primarily in complex, real-world scenarios, but not necessarily in simpler simulated environments, offers critical guidance for resource allocation in model development.
- **Planner Characterization**: The comparative analysis of planning optimizers delineates their strengths and weaknesses across various cost landscape complexities, with CEM L2 emerging as a robust general-purpose choice and Nevergrad offering a hyperparameter-free alternative for real-world tasks.
- **Performance Advancement**: The proposed optimal JEPA-WM configurations demonstrate improved performance over established baselines, representing an empirical step forward in the capabilities of this class of reward-free, goal-conditioned world models.

**Potential Impact**:
The findings can inform the design of more effective and robust world models for robotic control, potentially leading to advances in autonomous physical agents capable of complex manipulation and navigation. The detailed breakdown of component effects facilitates future research by providing a clearer understanding of underlying mechanisms.

**Limitations**:
A noted limitation of JEPA-WMs, as highlighted by the authors, is their reliance on a deterministic predictor based on MSE loss, which learns the conditional mean of potentially multi-modal future dynamics. While this is mitigated in the current benchmarks by deterministic environments and the robustness of closed-loop MPC, environments with significant aleatoric uncertainty would necessitate stochastic extensions (e.g., latent variable injection or latent space diffusion).

**Broader Impact**:
The work focuses on enabling more autonomous and intelligent robots, which carries the general societal implications associated with advancements in robotics. The authors acknowledge potential risks of misuse and emphasize the importance of efficient training procedures and model sharing to mitigate the environmental impact of large model training.
