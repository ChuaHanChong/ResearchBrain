# Following the Human Thread in Social Navigation

> [!summary] Summary
> A two-stage reinforcement learning framework, Social Dynamics Adaptation (SDA), enables robots to infer and adapt to human movements for social navigation, achieving higher finding success (0.91) and following rates (0.39) compared to baseline methods in simulated human-centric environments. This allows robots to operate robustly by distilling complex human trajectory information into an adaptable latent representation without needing real-time privileged human data.

## Problem

- Robots face challenges adapting to real-time human motion and inferring human intentions in dynamic, shared environments for tasks like "find and follow".
- High-performing social navigation methods often depend on "privileged information" (e.g., exact human GPS coordinates) during deployment, which is rarely available in real-world scenarios.
- Existing approaches frequently oversimplify human social dynamics, treating humans as simple obstacles or only focusing on collision avoidance, rather than understanding nuanced interactive behaviors.

## Method

- A two-stage ==Reinforcement Learning== (RL) framework, Social Dynamics Adaptation (SDA), is introduced, leveraging the Habitat 3.0 simulator for human-robot interaction.
- Stage 1 trains a social policy to encode complex human trajectories into a latent representation of "social dynamics" using privileged information.
- Stage 2 trains an "Adapter" module to infer these learned latent social dynamics solely from the robot's egocentric state and action history, enabling deployment without real-time privileged sensors.

## Results

- The SDA-S2 model achieved higher Finding Success (**0.91**), Finding Success Weighted by Path Steps (**0.45**), and Following Rate (**0.39**) compared to Habitat **3.0** baseline (**0.76**, **0.34**, **0.29** respectively) in deployment scenarios without privileged information.
- The approach demonstrated robustness against sensor noise, reduced update frequencies, and even in multi-human environments, consistently outperforming baselines under these varied conditions.
- Ablation studies confirmed that adapting human trajectories was effective, whereas attempting to adapt more explicit privileged information like Humanoid GPS (hGPS) resulted in significant performance degradation in the adaptation stage.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Human trajectory information proves more effectively adaptable from robot's egocentric observations than direct Humanoid GPS (hGPS) for inferring social dynamics during deployment.
> - The learned latent social dynamics space organizes into discernible clusters representing key interaction phases such as "Find," "Seek," "Lost," and "Follow."
> - Encoding human trajectories over a sufficient historical window (e.g., 20 steps/0.8 seconds) is critical for extracting robust social dynamics, with shorter windows leading to significant performance degradation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research contributes to the field of Embodied Artificial Intelligence (EAI), specifically focusing on **social navigation** for robots operating in human-centric environments. Traditional navigation in EAI has largely focused on agents traversing vacant or static spaces, or treating humans as simple dynamic obstacles. However, the increasing demand for human-robot collaboration necessitates robots that can understand and adapt to complex human movements and intentions in real-time.

Prior work in social navigation often characterized the task as a variation of PointGoal Navigation, where the primary objective was to reach a destination while avoiding collisions with humans. These methods frequently relied on privileged information, such as perfect localization (e.g., GPS, compass) or explicit human position data, which are typically unavailable or impractical in real-world deployment scenarios. Furthermore, many existing approaches either did not adequately capture the nuanced "social dynamics" of human behavior or modeled them in simplified forms (e.g., as proximity tasks). Simulators like Habitat 3.0 have recently advanced to incorporate lifelike human avatars and dynamic interactions, enabling more realistic investigation of human-agent collaboration.

This paper addresses a critical gap by proposing a method that leverages privileged information (human trajectories) during training to learn social dynamics, but then transitions to inferring these dynamics solely from the robot's egocentric observations and actions during deployment. This approach aims to bridge the realism gap between simulation-based training and real-world applicability, where constant access to global, perfect human information is not feasible. By focusing on "finding and following" tasks, the work pushes beyond mere collision avoidance, requiring a more sophisticated understanding of human intent and behavior.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to enable robots to navigate effectively and safely in shared environments with humans by real-time adaptation to human motion, specifically for tasks involving finding and following a person.

The motivation behind this objective stems from several challenges and limitations in current social navigation paradigms:

- **Real-time Adaptation to Human Motion:** Successful human-robot collaboration requires robots to continuously adjust their movements based on human actions. This involves maintaining appropriate proximity (close enough for assistance, but distant enough for free movement) and avoiding collisions.
- **Partial Observability of Human Trajectories:** Human trajectories are crucial cues for social navigation, providing insights into human intentions and movement patterns. However, from a robot's egocentric perspective, these trajectories are often partially observable (e.g., humans moving out of sight) and computationally complex to process in real-time.
- **Impracticality of Privileged Information:** Many high-performing existing social navigation methods rely on "privileged information" during inference, such as accurate humanoid GPS coordinates or complete environment maps. While obtainable in simulation, these are frequently impractical or impossible to acquire reliably in real-world scenarios, limiting the practical deployment of such systems. For instance, methods using GPS or SLAM might fail when a human is not in the line of sight.
- **Inadequate Capture of Social Dynamics:** Previous attempts to model social factors often simplify human behavior, focusing on collision avoidance rather than cooperative navigation. Robots need to anticipate human intentions and maintain socially acceptable distances, which necessitates a deeper understanding of social dynamics beyond simple geometric rules.
- **Complexity of "Find and Follow" Task:** The task of finding and reliably following a human through unpredictable and dynamic environments (e.g., search-and-rescue, assistive robotics) is inherently complex. It involves challenging long-term human motion prediction, navigation in unknown or changing environments, and adherence to strict safety requirements.

To address these motivations, the key objective is to propose a novel Social Dynamics Adaptation (SDA) model. This model aims to:
1. Learn to encode complex human trajectories into a latent representation of "social dynamics" during training, utilizing privileged information.
2. Develop a mechanism (an Adapter module) to infer these learned social dynamics solely from the robot's own state and action history during deployment, eliminating the need for real-time privileged information.
3. Achieve state-of-the-art performance in finding and following humans in dynamic, human-centric simulated environments, demonstrating robustness to real-world complexities like sensor noise and varying human behavior.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology introduces the Social Dynamics Adaptation (SDA) model, a two-stage Reinforcement Learning (RL) framework designed to enable robots to infer and adapt to human social dynamics for navigation tasks. The framework operates within the Habitat 3.0 simulation platform, which facilitates realistic human-robot interaction.

**Problem Formalization:**
The task is defined as locating a humanoid in motion within an indoor environment and subsequently following it while maintaining a safe distance (1 to 2 meters) for a specified number of steps (at least *k*). The robot's perception at time *t* is denoted *x<sub>t</sub>*, comprising depth images from multiple cameras and preprocessed humanoid detection bounding boxes. These perceptions are processed by a ResNet and fed into a recurrent policy network, which selects the best action *a<sub>t</sub>* (velocity commands: linear and angular) at time *t*.

**Two-Stage Reinforcement Learning Framework:**

**Stage 1: Social Policy Training**
This stage focuses on learning a robust motion policy conditioned on observed human social dynamics, using privileged information.
- **Input to Policy:** The policy ($\pi$) takes the current processed sensor input (*x<sub>t</sub>*), the previous action (*a<sub>t-1</sub>*), and a latent vector *z<sub>t</sub>* as input.
- **Social Dynamics Encoding:** The latent vector *z<sub>t</sub>* is derived by an encoder ($\mu$) that processes privileged information about the human's trajectory. Specifically, *z<sub>t</sub>* = $\mu(e_{t-N:t-1})$, where *e<sub>t-N:t-1</sub>* represents the human's absolute x-y coordinates over a trajectory of *N* past steps (N=20, covering 0.8 seconds). This encoder ($\mu$) is implemented as a Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) with 3 layers.
- **Joint Training:** The policy ($\pi$), the perception encoder (ResNet), and the trajectory encoder ($\mu$) are jointly trained using Decentralized Distributed Proximal Policy Optimization (DD-PPO). This algorithm iteratively improves the agent's policy by maximizing rewards derived from parallel environmental interactions, using a CLIP loss formulation. The objective implicitly encourages *z<sub>t</sub>* to encode social dynamics that lead to higher rewards.
- **Privileged Information:** During this stage, the system has "privileged information" about the human's exact position trajectory (traj.) or, in some ablations, humanoid GPS (hGPS) which provides relative distance and angle. This detailed information is assumed to be available during training to establish an upper bound on performance and extract rich social cues.

**Stage 2: Social Dynamics Regression (Adaptation)**
This stage aims to enable the robot to operate without direct access to privileged human information by inferring the social dynamics from its own internal history.
- **Adapter Module:** An "Adapter" module ($\psi$), also implemented as an MLP with alternating spatial and temporal layers, is introduced. This module's purpose is to regress the latent vector *z<sub>t</sub>* learned in Stage 1.
- **Input to Adapter:** The Adapter ($\psi$) takes the robot's recent history of states (*x<sub>t-N:t-1</sub>*) and actions (*a_{t-N:t-1}*) as input.
- **Output:** It generates an estimated latent vector $\hat{z}_t$, intended to approximate *z<sub>t</sub>*.
- **Training:** The Adapter is trained using a supervised regression objective, specifically Mean Squared Error (MSE) between $\hat{z}_t$ and the ground-truth *z<sub>t</sub>* (computed from privileged trajectories as in Stage 1). During this stage, the policy ($\pi^*$) and the perception encoder learned in Stage 1 are frozen.
- **Deployment:** After Stage 2 training, the robot operates by using the Adapter to infer $\hat{z}_t$ from its own history, and then feeds this $\hat{z}_t$ along with current perception and previous action to the frozen policy $\pi^*$. This allows the agent to estimate social dynamics online, independent of external privileged sensors.

**Implementation Details:**
- The latent vector *z<sub>t</sub>* has a dimensionality of 128.
- Stage 1 training involves 250 million steps across 24 environments on 4 A100 GPUs (approximately 4 days).
- Stage 2 supervised training involves 5 million steps across the same environments (approximately 2 hours).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study evaluated the SDA model against several baselines on the Social Navigation task in Habitat 3.0, using metrics such as Finding Success (S), Finding Success Weighted by Path Steps (SPS), Following Rate (F), Collision Rate (CR), and Episode Success (ES).

**1. Performance without Privileged Information (Deployment Scenario):**
- **SDA-S2 (adapting human trajectories)** consistently outperformed the Habitat 3.0 Baseline and an adapted Proximity Tasks method (from Cancelli et al.) when no privileged information was available during inference.
 - **Finding Success (S):** SDA-S2 achieved 0.91 (Baseline: 0.76, Baseline+Proximity: 0.85), indicating it located humans more frequently.
 - **Finding Success Weighted by Path Steps (SPS):** SDA-S2 achieved 0.45 (Baseline: 0.34, Baseline+Proximity: 0.41), suggesting more optimal paths to finding the human.
 - **Following Rate (F):** SDA-S2 achieved 0.39 (Baseline: 0.29, Baseline+Proximity: 0.37), demonstrating longer periods of successful following.
 - **Episode Success (ES):** SDA-S2 obtained 0.43 (Baseline: 0.40, Baseline+Proximity: 0.41), showing a slight improvement in overall task completion.
 - **Collision Rate (CR):** SDA-S2 had a CR of 0.57 (Baseline: 0.48, Baseline+Proximity: 0.58). The higher CR for SDA-S2 was attributed to its improved ability to find and follow the human for longer durations (390 steps vs. 218 steps for Baseline). If episodes concluded after the required 400 follow steps or upon collision, CRs were comparable (0.39 for SDA, 0.38 for Baseline).

**2. Performance with Privileged Information (Stage 1 Training and Upper Bounds):**
- When privileged information was available during training (Stage 1), methods utilizing Humanoid GPS (hGPS) as input (Habitat 3.0 Baseline, Baseline+Proximity) generally achieved higher Finding Success (S) and SPS scores (e.g., S=0.97, SPS=0.65 for Baseline with hGPS GT) compared to SDA-S1 (S=0.92, SPS=0.46) which only used human trajectories. This suggests hGPS provides more direct information for efficient path planning.

**3. Adaptable Information Analysis:**
- Ablation studies showed that **human trajectories (traj.) were more adaptable than hGPS** for Stage 2 inference.
 - While hGPS as privileged input in Stage 1 led to strong performance, attempts to adapt hGPS in Stage 2 (SDA-S2 with hGPS or hGPS+traj adaptation) resulted in significantly degraded performance (e.g., ES dropping to 0.02-0.03).
 - This was attributed to hGPS containing implicit information about the robot's position, which is difficult to regress accurately from the robot's egocentric history without initial context. Human trajectories, being solely human-centric, were more amenable to adaptation from egocentric observations.

**4. Qualitative Analysis and Latent Space:**
- Qualitative examples demonstrated the agent's ability to locate and follow humans, adapt to brief disappearances, and yield space when a human moved backward.
- A t-SNE projection of the inferred latent space ($\hat{z}_t$) revealed distinct clusters corresponding to different behavioral stages: "Find," "Seek," "Lost," and "Follow." The transitions between these clusters were continuous, aligning with the fluid nature of human-robot interaction and validating that the latent vector captured meaningful social dynamics.

**5. Robustness Towards Real-World Scenarios:**
- **ORCA (Human Reciprocal Collision Avoidance):** Incorporating ORCA for human behavior in Habitat 3.0 resulted in a lower Collision Rate (CR decreased from 0.57 to 0.37) and higher Episode Success (ES increased from 0.43 to 0.48) for SDA, indicating benefits from more realistic human responses to the robot's presence.
- **Lower Frequency Updates:** Reducing the update rate of the Adapter module (e.g., once every two or hundred timesteps) caused slight degradation in finding metrics but improved overall ES and Following Rate (F). This suggested that slower updates encouraged more stable behavior and long-term strategies, avoiding unnecessary short-term adjustments.
- **Noisy Inputs:** The addition of Gaussian noise to bounding box detections and actuators, and Redwood noise to depth images, caused overall performance degradation. However, the performance gap between SDA and the Baseline remained consistent across all noise types and metrics, supporting the algorithmic novelty and robustness of SDA in varied conditions.
- **Statistical Significance:** Independent two-sample t-tests confirmed that the improvements of SDA over both the baseline and the Proximity Tasks method were statistically significant across key metrics (p-values < 0.001).
- **Multi-Human Interactions:** While performance metrics declined with an increased number of humans (due to training on single-human scenarios), SDA consistently outperformed the baseline in scenarios with two and three humans across all metrics, indicating its relative robustness to increased task complexity.

**6. Design Analysis (Appendix):**
- **Trajectory Length:** An ablation on the length of the human trajectory used for encoding in Stage 1 revealed that 20 steps (0.8 seconds) was optimal, yielding significantly better performance (ES 0.50) than shorter (1-5 steps, ES 0.03-0.06) or longer (50 steps, ES 0.08) trajectories. Encoding only a single state resulted in unsatisfactory performance.
- **Encoder Type:** An MLP performed better than a Transformer for encoding trajectories in Stage 1, highlighting the non-trivial nature of the sequence modeling mechanism.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work presents a notable contribution to the field of Embodied AI and human-robot interaction by addressing the challenge of robust social navigation in dynamic environments without relying on real-world impractical privileged information.

The significance and potential impact of the Social Dynamics Adaptation (SDA) model include:

- **Advancement in Social Navigation Capabilities:** SDA sets a new benchmark for "find and follow" tasks within the Habitat 3.0 platform, demonstrating improved performance over existing methods in finding humans, navigating efficiently, and sustaining following behavior. This represents a step beyond purely reactive collision avoidance towards a more proactive and socially aware robotic agent.
- **Bridging the Sim-to-Real Gap:** By learning social dynamics from privileged information in simulation and then inferring them from the robot's own observational history during deployment, SDA offers a practical methodology for transferring complex learned behaviors from simulated environments to real-world applications. This reduces the dependency on specialized, often unavailable, real-time external sensors.
- **Enhanced Human-Robot Collaboration:** The ability of robots to adapt to and anticipate human movements is fundamental for effective collaboration. SDA's framework, which models human behavior through inferred social dynamics, has the potential to make robots more predictable and safer partners in shared spaces.
- **Robustness to Real-World Variability:** The investigation into noisy sensor inputs, varying human motion patterns (e.g., ORCA, reduced/random speeds), and computational constraints (lower update rates) demonstrates the model's robustness. This suggests that SDA could maintain performance in less-than-ideal real-world conditions.
- **Foundation for Complex Social Interactions:** By successfully extracting and utilizing latent social dynamics, this work lays a foundation for future research into more nuanced human-robot interactions. The identified behavioral stages (Find, Seek, Lost, Follow) through latent analysis indicate that the robot is developing a meaningful internal representation of its social context.
- **Applications in Critical Domains:** The advancements in social navigation can have a direct impact on domains such as:
 - **Search and Rescue:** Robots can more effectively find and follow human responders in hazardous and unpredictable terrains, carrying equipment or relaying information.
 - **Assistive Robotics:** Robots can provide better support in elder care or other assistive roles, seamlessly following caregivers or patients across various home environments.
 - **Logistics and Manufacturing:** Robots can navigate workspaces alongside human co-workers, improving efficiency and safety.

Future research directions, such as incorporating more diverse human dynamics beyond trajectories and integrating additional sensors like microphones, indicate the potential for further expanding the robot's comprehension of human behavior and enhancing its adaptability in increasingly complex social scenarios.
