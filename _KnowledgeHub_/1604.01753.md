# Hollywood in Homes: Crowdsourcing Data Collection for Activity Understanding

> [!summary] Summary
> Charades is a dataset of everyday household activity videos built by the Hollywood in Homes method: Amazon Mechanical Turk workers crowdsource the whole pipeline, writing scripts from seeded object/action vocabularies, filming themselves acting them out at home, then verifying and annotating. It yields 9,848 videos (avg 30s), 27,847 descriptions, 66,500 localized intervals over 157 action classes, and 41,104 object labels; best baseline reaches only 18.6% mAP.

## Problem

- Internet video sources like YouTube are biased toward entertaining, atypical clips, so mundane everyday activities such as drinking from a cup are never uploaded.
- Movie-sourced and sports-heavy web datasets skew action vocabularies away from daily living, leaving no realistic training data for home robots and assistive vision.
- Lab-recorded and in-house cooking or ego-centric datasets control content well but use fixed backgrounds and few actors, lacking the diversity needed for generalization.

## Method

- **Hollywood in Homes**: a three-stage crowdsourcing pipeline on Mechanical Turk, script generation, worker self-filming at home, then peer verification and annotation, controlling scene, object and action composition.
- Script seeding from 549 movie scripts analyzed by term-frequency and TF-IDF, yielding 40 objects and 30 actions sampled into prompts across 15 indoor room types for open-ended paragraph writing.
- A worker-economics design of sign-up, recruit-a-friend, and every-15th-video performance bonuses that cut per-video cost from $3 to $1, plus exhaustive multi-pass temporal interval labeling.

## Results

- Best classification baseline is a late-fusion Combined model at **18.6%** mAP; improved dense trajectories alone reach **17.2%**, beating two-stream at **14.3%**, AlexNet **11.3%**, C3D **10.9%**, random **5.9%**.
- Confusion concentrates among actions sharing an object; the subset of actions without a specific interacted object scores **38.9%** mAP, far above average, and washing a window reaches **62.1%** AP.
- Captioning with S2VT reaches CIDEr **0.17** on scripts and **0.14** on descriptions, well under human performance of **0.51** and **0.53**; label precision of annotations measures **95.6%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Crowdsourcing can produce content, not merely labels, if worker incentives are engineered; retention bonuses raised per-worker output 109% at only 33% higher base cost.
> - Controlling the generating vocabulary while letting people write freely yields a near-uniform, low-bias label distribution instead of the long tail typical of natural data.
> - Fine-grained discrimination between actions on the same object remains the hard residual problem; scene context is weakly discriminative for most everyday activities.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of computer vision has made significant advancements, largely attributed to the availability of large-scale datasets. However, much of this success has been biased towards static images and objects found on the Internet (e.g., ImageNet), as noted in the paper. As the field progresses towards developing AI and robotics capable of operating in real-world environments, a crucial gap emerges: the lack of comprehensive datasets representing dynamic, everyday human activities and complex human-object interactions in natural settings.

Existing video datasets primarily fall into several categories, each with limitations:
- **Internet-sourced videos (e.g., YouTube datasets like UCF101, Sports1M, ActivityNet):** These datasets are abundant but inherently biased towards "exciting" or entertainment-driven content (e.g., sports, stunts). "Boring" everyday activities, such as putting away groceries or drinking from a cup, are rarely uploaded or appear in highly stylized forms, thus not reflecting typical daily life.
- **Movie-sourced videos (e.g., MPII-MD):** Similar to YouTube, movies are designed for entertainment, leading to content that is often dramatic, exaggerated, and not representative of common daily actions or object interactions.
- **Controlled lab environments (e.g., KTH, MPII Cooking, TUM Breakfast):** These datasets offer precise control over data collection, allowing for detailed annotation of specific activities. However, they suffer from a lack of diversity in backgrounds, participants, and scenarios, which limits the generalizability and scalability of models trained on them.
- **Ego-centric datasets (e.g., ADL):** While capturing first-person views of daily activities, these datasets often lack the diversity in perspectives and environments needed for robust general-purpose models.

This paper addresses these limitations by introducing a novel data collection paradigm, "Hollywood in Homes," and a resulting dataset, Charades. It positions itself as a critical effort to bridge the gap between existing biased datasets and the real-world data needed for advanced AI and robotics applications. By focusing on diverse, everyday household activities, it aims to provide the necessary data for developing models that can learn common sense, context, object states, and intricate human-object interactions in realistic scenarios, thereby overcoming the "entertainment bias" and "lab-controlled bias" prevalent in much of the current data landscape.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to create a large-scale, diverse, and realistic video dataset of everyday activities, particularly focusing on household routines, to serve as a robust training and benchmarking resource for computer vision algorithms. This objective is driven by several motivations:

1. **Addressing Data Bias in Computer Vision:** Current advancements in computer vision, particularly in image recognition, have been heavily influenced by large datasets primarily composed of static images from the Internet. This has led to models that are proficient at recognizing objects and scenes in static contexts but struggle with the dynamic, nuanced nature of real-world interactions. The authors emphasize that a shift is needed from static Internet imagery to dynamic, real-world scenes.

2. **Enabling AI and Robotics for Daily Life:** The move towards sophisticated AI and robotic systems necessitates an understanding of complex concepts such as object states (e.g., an apple being "whole" versus "cut"), how human actions affect these states, and sequences of common activities. For robots to assist in daily tasks (e.g., finding lost keys, watering plants), they require models trained on relevant data that reflects the "boring" yet common activities of human life, which are often missing from existing datasets.

3. **Limitations of Existing Video Datasets:**
 - **Entertainment Bias:** Videos found on popular platforms like YouTube or in movies are intrinsically biased towards content that is entertaining, exciting, or unique. Consequently, mundane but pervasive activities such as "getting dressed," "putting groceries in a fridge," or "cutting vegetables" are underrepresented or presented in an unrepresentative manner. This "entertainment bias" prevents models from learning typical, commonplace scenarios.
 - **Lack of Diversity and Scalability in Controlled Environments:** Datasets collected in controlled laboratory settings offer precision but inherently lack the diversity of environments, lighting, objects, and human behaviors found in real homes. Such controlled setups are also not easily scalable to the vast quantities of data required for modern deep learning techniques.
 - **Limited Scope in Specialized Datasets:** While some datasets focus on human-object interactions or specific activities like cooking, they often restrict the domain or environment, limiting generalizability. Ego-centric datasets, while capturing daily life, often lack diversity across participants and contexts.

4. **Learning Common Sense and Contextual Knowledge:** The authors argue that a dataset rich in natural action sequences and human-object interactions in typical environments is crucial for models to learn common sense and contextual knowledge. This high-level reasoning capability is essential for AI systems to interact intelligently and safely within human environments.

5. **Controlling Data Composition:** Unlike downloading untagged or pre-existing videos from the web, the proposed approach aims to control the vocabulary of scenes, objects, and actions during the data generation phase. This control allows for focused data collection on specific types of interactions and activities that are deemed most relevant for everyday AI applications.

In summary, the motivation is to overcome the data scarcity and biases in existing computer vision datasets by proposing a scalable, crowd-sourced methodology to collect realistic, diverse, and context-rich video data of everyday household activities, thereby fostering the development of AI models capable of understanding and interacting with the real world.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of this work is the "Hollywood in Homes" approach, a novel crowdsourcing methodology designed to collect a large-scale, diverse video dataset of daily activities. This method distributes the entire video creation process, from scriptwriting to recording and annotation, to workers on Amazon Mechanical Turk (AMT). The process is structured into three main steps: Script Generation, Video Generation, and Annotation.

#### 3.1. Script Generation

1. **Scene and Vocabulary Definition:** The focus was on indoor household scenes, identifying 15 common room types (e.g., Kitchen, Living Room, Bedroom). To establish a vocabulary for scripts, 549 movie scripts were analyzed using term-frequency (TF) and TF-IDF to extract frequent nouns and verbs within these room contexts. From this analysis, a curated list of 40 generic objects and 30 generic actions was selected as seed words.
2. **Crowdsourcing Script Composition:** AMT workers were presented with a randomly selected scene, 5 random objects, and 5 random actions from the curated vocabulary. Their task was to compose a short paragraph (script) describing realistic and commonplace activities involving one or two people in their home, using at least two of the provided objects and two actions. This approach aimed to balance control over the vocabulary with leveraging human creativity and natural biases in activity generation.

#### 3.2. Video Generation (Recording)

1. **Distributed Video Recording:** To maximize diversity in scenes, objects, clothing, and human behavior, the workers themselves were tasked with recording 30-second videos in their own homes, following the generated scripts.
2. **Worker Recruitment and Retention Strategies:**
 - **Cost Management:** Initial pilot studies revealed a high cost ($3 per video) for content creation. Strategies were implemented to reduce the effective base cost to approximately $1 per video.
 - **Recruitment:** Sign-up bonuses ($5 for the first submission) were offered, increasing new worker rates by 211%. "Recruit a friend" bonuses ($5 if a referred friend submitted 15 videos) were also utilized for outreach.
 - **Retention:** Performance bonuses, awarded every 15th video, significantly improved worker retention (34% increase in returning workers) and output (109% increase in output per worker).
 - **Geographic Scope:** Workers from the US, Canada, UK, and India were included, with the first three accounting for most videos.
3. **Quality Control:** Each submitted video was manually verified by other AMT workers. Verification involved confirming that the recorded video corresponded to the provided script by selecting the correct sentence from a lineup.
4. **Cost Distribution:** The final cost allocation was approximately 65% for base payment per video, 21% for performance bonuses, 11% for recruitment bonuses, and 3% for verification.

#### 3.3. Annotations

1. **Action Class Definition:** From the (verb, proposition, noun) triplets extracted from the generated scripts, 157 distinct action classes were identified (e.g., "pouring into cup," "folding towel").
2. **Video Descriptions:** After video recording, a separate set of AMT workers watched the videos and provided free-text descriptions of what they observed. These "descriptions" are distinct from the initial "scripts."
3. **Object Interaction Labels:** The original script and subsequently collected video descriptions were used to automatically generate a list of interacted objects for each video. These lists were then verified by workers.
4. **Action Presence Verification:** For each video, workers were presented with a short list of 4-5 relevant action classes (selected from the 157 classes) involving corresponding object interactions and asked to verify their presence. An exhaustive annotation process, particularly focused on the test set, was employed to minimize missing labels.
5. **Temporal Localization:** For all verified action classes within each video, another group of workers was tasked with labeling the precise start and end times of the activity, creating temporal intervals for each action.

#### 3.4. The Charades Dataset (v1.0)

The result of this process is the Charades dataset (v1.0), comprising:
- **Videos:** 9,848 annotated videos (7,985 for training, 1,863 for testing) with an average length of 30.1 seconds.
- **Participants:** Videos recorded by 267 unique individuals across three continents. Over 15% of videos feature more than one person.
- **Content:** 15 types of indoor scenes, interactions with 46 object classes, and a vocabulary of 30 verbs leading to 157 action classes.
- **Annotations:**
 - 27,847 free-text video descriptions.
 - 66,500 temporally localized intervals for 157 action classes (average 12.8 seconds per interval).
 - 41,104 labels for 46 object classes.
- **Key Characteristic:** An average of 6.8 relevant actions are present in each video, indicating a rich and complex activity context, which is significantly higher than many existing datasets.

The "Hollywood in Homes" approach is presented as the first method to demonstrate the feasibility of crowdsourcing video content creation at such a large scale, beyond just annotation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper presents an analysis of the Charades dataset and baseline evaluations for action classification and sentence prediction tasks.

#### 4.1. Charades Dataset Analysis

1. **Diversity and Realism:** The dataset successfully captures realistic, casual, and diverse everyday activities, primarily within household environments. This contrasts with datasets sourced from entertainment media (e.g., YouTube, movies), which typically lack such mundane content. The crowdsourcing of scriptwriting, recording, and diverse participants contributes to this realism.
2. **High Action Density:** Charades videos contain an average of 6.8 relevant actions per video, which is considerably higher than many existing video datasets (e.g., UCF101, ActivityNet, HMDB51 which often have 1-2 actions per video). This density provides rich contextual information and necessitates algorithms capable of understanding multiple co-occurring activities.
3. **Vocabulary Distribution:** The distributions of action classes, verbs, nouns, and scenes within the dataset are relatively even, rather than heavily skewed (long-tailed). This indicates a broad coverage of the intended domain. T-SNE embedding of co-occurrence data demonstrates that words group together in a way that reflects real-world intuition (e.g., "food" and "cooking" co-occur with "Kitchen").
4. **Action Sequences:** Analysis of preceding and succeeding actions (e.g., "Standing up" often precedes "Opening a window," which might then be followed by "Looking out the window") suggests that the crowdsourced scripts result in realistic and common-sense sequences of human behavior.

#### 4.2. Baseline Evaluations

The authors provide baseline results for two main tasks: Action Classification and Sentence Prediction, designed to benchmark future algorithms. The train/test split (7,985 vs. 1,863 videos) was carefully constructed to ensure worker separation, similar class distributions, minimum class examples, and prevent single-worker dominance in the test set.

##### 4.2.1. Action Classification (157 classes)

1. **Metrics:** Mean Average Precision (mAP) was used to evaluate performance, given that videos can contain multiple action labels.
2. **Baselines Tested:**
 - Random Baseline: 5.9% mAP
 - C3D Features (3D ConvNet): 10.9% mAP
 - AlexNet (Static CNN features): 11.3% mAP
 - Two-Stream Networks (Balanced): 11.9% mAP
 - Two-Stream Networks: 14.3% mAP
 - Improved Dense Trajectories (IDT): 17.2% mAP
 - Combined (Late fusion of all methods): 18.6% mAP
3. **Key Observation:** The mAP scores for state-of-the-art baselines are significantly lower than those typically achieved on other available benchmarks. This indicates that Charades presents a substantial challenge for current action recognition methods, highlighting its complexity and real-world diversity.
4. **Performance Variation and Confusion Analysis:**
 - While the overall mAP is low, some action classes demonstrate reasonable performance (e.g., "Washing a window" achieved 62.1% AP). There is a slight trend for larger classes to have higher accuracy, but this is not universally true.
 - Analysis of the confusion matrix reveals that most misclassifications occur between different actions involving the *same object* (e.g., various actions related to "blanket," "clothes," or "towel") or objects with similar functional properties ("couch" and "bed").
 - Actions that do not involve specific object interactions (e.g., "standing up," "sneezing") show less confusion and a higher mAP (38.9%), suggesting that fine-grained recognition of human-object interactions remains a particularly difficult challenge.

##### 4.2.2. Sentence Prediction (Video Captioning)

1. **Task:** Generate free-form sentences describing the video, evaluated against both the original "scripts" used to create the video and the "descriptions" provided by other annotators after watching the video.
2. **Metrics:** CIDEr, BLEU-1 to BLEU-4, ROUGE-L, and METEOR. Human performance was also measured for comparison.
3. **Baselines Tested:**
 - Random Words (RW)
 - Random Sentence (Random)
 - Nearest Neighbor (NN)
 - S2VT (Sequence to Sequence - Video to Text, a CNN+LSTM model)
4. **Key Observation:** S2VT emerged as the strongest baseline, achieving a CIDEr score of 0.17 for script prediction (compared to human performance of 0.51) and 0.14 for description prediction (compared to human performance of 0.53). All baselines perform significantly below human level.
5. **Challenges:** While the generated captions often demonstrate linguistic coherence, models struggle with providing relevant and specific details. They tend to overfit to frequent patterns (e.g., "drinking from a glass/cup") rather than accurately describing the unique actions in a video. The disparity between "scripts" and "descriptions" highlights the difference between an imagined scenario and observed reality.

In summary, the Charades dataset successfully provides a diverse and complex collection of everyday activities. The baseline evaluations underscore the significant challenges it poses for current computer vision models in understanding detailed actions, particularly fine-grained human-object interactions, and generating accurate video descriptions, indicating substantial room for future algorithmic development.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Hollywood in Homes" approach and the resulting Charades dataset represent a significant contribution to the computer vision community, with several potential impacts:

1. **Novel Data Collection Paradigm:** The paper introduces and validates a scalable, controlled, yet diverse crowdsourcing methodology ("Hollywood in Homes") for generating video content, not just annotating it. This approach offers a blueprint for creating large-scale datasets in various novel domains where specific types of visual data are required but are not readily available through existing sources. It demonstrates that distributed content creation can overcome the limitations of lab-controlled environments and the inherent biases of web-scraped data.

2. **Addressing Data Bias and Enhancing Realism:** Charades directly addresses a critical gap in computer vision by providing a large-scale dataset focused on "boring" yet realistic everyday household activities. This counters the entertainment bias prevalent in YouTube and movie datasets and the lack of diversity in lab-controlled environments. By offering data that genuinely reflects daily life, it enables the training of models with biases relevant to real-world scenarios, which is crucial for practical AI applications.

3. **Challenging Benchmark for Advanced Algorithms:** With 66,500 temporally localized action annotations and an average of 6.8 relevant actions per video, Charades offers a rich and complex benchmark. The observed low performance of state-of-the-art baselines in action classification and video captioning underscores the dataset's challenge. This will stimulate research into more robust and sophisticated computer vision techniques capable of handling complex, multi-action, and context-rich videos.

4. **Facilitating Research in Key Computer Vision Areas:**
 - **Human-Object Interaction:** The dataset's emphasis on intricate object-action relationships and the identified difficulty in distinguishing actions with the same object will inspire novel research into fine-grained understanding of how humans interact with their environment.
 - **Contextual Understanding and Common Sense Reasoning:** The realistic co-occurrence patterns and action sequences within Charades can aid in developing models that learn common sense and contextual knowledge, moving beyond isolated event recognition to understanding the flow and implications of human activities.
 - **Action Recognition and Temporal Localization:** The detailed temporal annotations for multiple actions per video provide an ideal testbed for algorithms that need to detect, localize, and recognize multiple concurrent or sequential activities within a single untrimmed video.
 - **Video Captioning and Description:** The task of generating accurate descriptions for the complex, multi-action videos in Charades pushes the boundaries of video-to-text models, encouraging development of systems that can generate more relevant and detailed captions.

5. **Enabling AI and Robotics in Domestic Environments:** The data collected is directly applicable to developing AI systems and robots designed to operate within human homes and assist with daily tasks. By training on realistic activities performed by diverse individuals in varied home settings, these systems can potentially learn to understand, predict, and assist with a wide array of everyday human needs.

6. **Open Science Contribution:** The public release of the Charades dataset, along with associated code and interfaces, fosters collaborative research and provides a standardized platform for benchmarking, accelerating progress in activity understanding.

In essence, Charades and the "Hollywood in Homes" methodology are poised to be foundational resources for advancing computer vision beyond biased, entertainment-focused data, paving the way for AI models with a deeper understanding of real-world human behavior and interaction.
