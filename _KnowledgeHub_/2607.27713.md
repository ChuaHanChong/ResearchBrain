# Write-Safe Flow Field Mapping under Ambiguous Onboard Sensing and Localization Drift

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Iowa State University and Brown University developed a map-reference-aware conservative fusion framework for online flow mapping, which uses a learned write-safety score to manage spatial inconsistencies arising from observation aliasing and localization drift in fluid-dominant environments. This framework reduced "ghost structures" by 42% and Normalized Root Mean Squared Error (NRMSE) from 0.356 to 0.197 in synthetic tests, and demonstrated zero-shot transferability to real hardware data.

## Problem

- Mobile robots in fluid-dominant environments struggle with sparse and spatially ambiguous local flow observations (observation aliasing), making it difficult to correctly place local estimates on a global map.
- Localization drift, particularly in GPS-denied environments, causes cumulative errors in the robot's reported pose, leading to misregistration of locally accurate flow patches on the global map.
- The combination of observation aliasing and localization drift leads to the accumulation of misregistered updates, forming persistent, non-existent "ghost structures" that corrupt the global map and propagate further errors.

## Method

- A **map-reference-aware conservative fusion framework** was developed to continuously assess the reliability of incoming local flow patches based on their alignment with the existing map and their inherent informativeness.
- A learned ==write-safety score== (κ_t) was integrated as a continuous gate, dynamically modulating the influence of new observations on the global map by tracking alignment risk without ground-truth pose.
- The system prioritizes preserving a spatially consistent and accurate map by selectively attenuating uncertain updates, even if it means leaving certain uncertain regions unsupported, rather than filling them with potentially erroneous data.

## Results

- The proposed method reduced the average "Ghost" score (normalized artifact score in low-flow regions) by **42%** and the Normalized Root Mean Squared Error (NRMSE) from **0.356 to 0.197** across synthetic scenes with simulated localization drift.
- These improvements were achieved through more conservative writing (Write Ratio of **0.451**), demonstrating that the learned write-safety score intelligently attenuated uncertain updates, performing comparably to an oracle with perfect alignment knowledge.
- The simulation-trained model successfully transferred zero-shot to real-world hardware data from a turbulent jet flow, reducing the Ghost score by **39%** and preserving the dominant wake structure without any fine-tuning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Online flow mapping in ambiguous environments should be framed as a "write-safety problem," where the robot must decide not only what flow to predict but also whether the prediction can be safely written to the map.
> - Leveraging temporal alignment evidence through a recurrent state and conditioning on existing map features are crucial for robust decision-making regarding map updates.
> - A learned reliability gate can effectively track alignment risk using only deployment-available inputs, preventing the formation of "ghost structures" and preserving map integrity without requiring perfect localization or loop closure features.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research addresses a specific challenge in mobile robotics operating within fluid-dominant environments such as oceans and rivers. While these environments are critical for observation and can offer opportunities for flow-aided navigation, most robotic systems primarily focus on compensating for flow disturbances rather than comprehensively mapping the flow fields.

The difficulty in flow mapping for mobile robots is multifaceted. Firstly, local flow observations obtained from onboard sensors are often sparse and can be spatially ambiguous or non-unique. Similar flow structures might produce identical sensor signatures at different locations, a phenomenon termed "observation aliasing." This makes it difficult for a robot to determine the correct global placement of a locally inferred flow estimate. Biological systems and artificial lateral-line systems have demonstrated the ability to extract hydrodynamic cues, but the issue of spatial non-uniqueness for global mapping persists.

Secondly, localization drift significantly exacerbates this ambiguity. Robots operating underwater frequently lack continuous global positioning system (GPS) signals, relying instead on dead reckoning, acoustic positioning, or other signal- and map-based navigation methods. These methods are susceptible to cumulative errors, causing the robot's reported pose to drift from its true position. Consequently, a locally accurate flow patch may be written into the global map at an incorrect location. Repeated instances of such misregistration can lead to the accumulation of "ghost structures"—coherent but nonexistent features—that corrupt the map and can subsequently propagate further errors by providing an unreliable reference for future observations.

Existing research has focused on various aspects related to this problem. Some methods infer local wind or marine flow fields from onboard sensing, or jointly estimate robot trajectories and parametric flow models. However, these approaches typically do not include mechanisms to assess the spatial safety of integrating these local estimates into an evolving global map. Similarly, probabilistic Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM) techniques manage pose uncertainty but often rely on persistent geometric features for loop closure, which may be sparse in open water environments. Learned and physics-informed flow models contribute to expressive field representations, but they usually target offline reconstruction or local prediction rather than real-time, online map updating with uncertainty management.

This work specifically addresses the gap where current methods fail to determine whether an incoming flow estimate is "safe" to commit to an evolving map under conditions of ambiguous alignment. By introducing a "write-safety" problem formulation, the paper seeks to enable online flow mapping that maintains spatial consistency even in the presence of observation aliasing and localization drift.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from two primary challenges encountered by mobile robots in flow-dominant environments: **observation aliasing** and **localization drift**.

**Motivation:**
1. **Observation Aliasing:** Mobile robots sense their local environment. In fluid flows, different spatial locations can exhibit similar local flow structures (e.g., the edge of a jet, a symmetric vortex street). Onboard sensors, which typically provide measurements only within a small neighborhood around the robot, may therefore capture nearly identical local flow signatures from distinct global positions. This ambiguity means that a locally plausible flow estimate does not uniquely identify its correct global location, creating uncertainty about where it should be integrated into a larger map.
2. **Localization Drift:** Robots, particularly those operating in GPS-denied environments like underwater, rely on dead reckoning or other relative positioning techniques. These methods are prone to accumulating errors over time, leading to a divergence between the robot's estimated position (reported pose) and its true physical location. When a local flow estimate, even if accurate relative to the robot, is projected onto the global map using a drifted pose, it is written at an incorrect spatial position.
3. **Consequence: Ghost Structures and Map Corruption:** The combination of observation aliasing and localization drift leads to a critical failure mode: repeated misregistered updates accumulate on the map. These repeated, slightly shifted, or incorrectly placed observations can coalesce into "ghost structures"—coherent but non-existent flow patterns that appear on the map. Unlike transient noise, these ghost structures are persistent and can corrupt the global map. Furthermore, a corrupted map can then serve as an unreliable reference for subsequent local-to-global alignments, potentially perpetuating or even amplifying future misregistrations.

**Objectives:**
The key objective of this research is to develop an online flow mapping system that can effectively manage these challenges. Specifically, the authors aim to:

1. **Formulate "Write-Safety" as a Core Problem:** To shift the focus from merely predicting local flow information to discerning whether that prediction is spatially safe and reliable enough to be committed to an evolving global map. The robot must decide not only *what* flow to predict but also *whether* the prediction can be safely written.
2. **Develop a Conservative Fusion Framework:** To introduce a "map-reference-aware conservative fusion framework" that can selectively attenuate uncertain map updates. This framework is designed to continuously assess the reliability of an incoming flow patch based on its alignment with the existing map and its inherent informativeness.
3. **Integrate a Learned Write-Safety Score:** To incorporate a learned write-safety score (κ_t) that acts as a continuous gate. This score should dynamically modulate the influence of new observations, allowing for strong updates when confidence is high (e.g., initial map regions where no reliable reference exists) and attenuating updates when alignment is ambiguous or the map reference is unreliable.
4. **Preserve Map Consistency over Full Coverage:** To prioritize the preservation of a spatially consistent and accurate map, even if this means leaving certain uncertain regions unsupported or less dense, rather than attempting to fill every grid cell with potentially misregistered data.

Ultimately, the goal is to enable robust and reliable online flow mapping for mobile robots in challenging, flow-dominant environments, specifically addressing the propagation of errors caused by localization uncertainty and perceptual ambiguities.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology introduces a map-reference-aware conservative fusion framework designed to maintain an evolving flow map while selectively writing updates based on their perceived reliability.

**Problem Formulation and Map Representation:**
The system maps a 2D horizontal domain `D` (e.g., 3m x 1m). The true robot pose at time `t` is `p*t`, while odometry provides a drifted pose `ˆp_t = p*t + η_t`. The map state `M_t` consists of two components: `Ω_t: D → R^2` storing the 2D velocity field, and `Ψ_t: D →` storing accumulated evidence or confidence for each cell.

Onboard observations `o_t` comprise four pressure measurements `π_t ∈ R^4` and a 3x3 optical-flow velocity stencil `U_of_t ∈ R^(2x3x3)`. To compare with the map, a local counterpart of the map is defined:
- **Map-reference confidence `c_map_t`**: The average evidence `Ψ_t-1` within the footprint `F_t` of the sensor stencil at the reported pose `ˆp_t`.
- **Map velocity stencil `˜U_map_t`**: A 2x3x3 velocity stencil sampled from `Ω_t-1` at `ˆp_t` using a query function `Q_v`, but only if `c_map_t` exceeds a threshold `τ_Ψ`. If `c_map_t ≤ τ_Ψ`, a learned null token `U_∅` is used, signifying an unavailable reliable map reference rather than disagreement.

**Patch Prediction and Conservative Fusion:**
The core of the method involves a neural network architecture for patch prediction and a specific fusion rule for map updates.
1. **Feature Encoding:** Pressure `π_t` and velocity stencils (`U_of_t` and `˜U_map_t`) are encoded into latent features `z_π_t`, `z_v_t`, `˜z_v_t` respectively, using 3-layer Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs) `ϕ_π` and `ϕ_v`.
2. **Discrepancy Calculation:** A discrepancy `d_t` between the observed and map-referenced velocity features is computed, weighted by `c_map_t`: `d_t = c_map_t |z_v_t - ˜z_v_t|`.
3. **Recurrent Input and Prediction:** A recurrent input `r_t` is formed by concatenating `z_π_t`, `z_v_t`, `˜z_v_t`, `d_t`, the normalized reported pose `¯p_t`, and `c_map_t`. This `r_t` is fed into a single-layer Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU). The GRU's output then feeds a decoder (fully connected projection and transposed-convolution layers) to produce the predicted local velocity patch `ˆμ_t ∈ R^(2x21x21)`.
4. **Auxiliary Predictions:** In addition to `ˆμ_t`, auxiliary heads predict:
 - `q_t`: A scalar indicating the informativeness of the predicted patch.
 - `κ_t`: The learned write-safety score.
 - Relative pose.
 - Reconstructed sensor readings.
5. **Effective Reliability and Write Mass:**
 - The predicted patch `ˆμ_t` is splatted onto the map at the reported pose `ˆp_t` to obtain `˜μ_t`.
 - An `effective reliability score κ_eff_t` is calculated: `κ_eff_t = (1 - c_map_t) + c_map_t κ_t`. This formulation is crucial: if the map reference confidence `c_map_t` is low (e.g., in unexplored regions), `κ_eff_t` is high (approaching 1), permitting map initialization. If `c_map_t` is high, `κ_t` directly controls the update.
 - The cellwise write mass `w_t(x, y)` combines the patch support `m_t(x, y)`, the informativeness `q_t`, and `κ_eff_t`: `w_t(x, y) = m_t(x, y) q_t κ_eff_t`.
6. **Map Update Rules:** Velocity `Ω_t` and evidence `Ψ_t` are updated pointwise using a weighted average:
 - `Ω_t = (Ψ_t-1 Ω_t-1 + w_t ˜μ_t) / (Ψ_t-1 + w_t + ϵ)`
 - `Ψ_t = clip_(Ψ_t-1 + w_t)` (evidence is clipped to to prevent visit-count dominance).

**Training Procedure:**
Training occurs in two stages, using simulated data where both true and drifted poses are available.
1. **Reliability Target:** An alignment reliability target `κ_align_t = exp(-e_t/σ_κ)` is defined based on the ground-truth alignment error `e_t = ||ˆp_t - p*t||_2`. This target guides the learning of `κ_t`.
2. **Stage 1 Training:** Encoders, GRU, and all prediction heads are trained jointly using a multi-component loss function `L_S1`. This loss includes terms for sensor reconstruction, relative pose prediction, write reliability (`L_κ`), velocity-patch prediction (`L_patch`), and informativeness (`L_q`). The informativeness target `q*t` combines the valid-support fraction of the target patch and a measure of local flow structure (normalized vector-patch gradient magnitude).
3. **Stage 2 Training:** Encoders, patch-decoder weights, and the sensor-reconstruction head are frozen. The GRU and the pose, reliability, and quality heads remain trainable. `L_patch` is retained to ensure the patch representation remains stable while the reliability mechanism is refined.

This methodology aims to intelligently decide *when* and *how much* to update the global flow map based on the reliability of the local-to-global alignment, rather than simply writing all observed data.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study evaluated the proposed map-reference-aware conservative fusion framework against several baselines across synthetic flow scenes and a zero-shot hardware replay.

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Flow Scenes:** Four 3m × 1m FluidX3D synthetic scenes were used: single jet, double jet, and their crossflow (CF) variants, with crossflow scenes exhibiting greater spatial ambiguity due to background advection. Scenes were split for training, validation, and evaluation.
- **Evaluation Protocol:** All methods received identical physical trajectories and sensor streams, with only the reported pose (`ˆp_t`) being corrupted by simulated localization drift. This isolated the performance of the fusion rule.
- **Drift Process:** Localization drift was simulated as a cumulative Gaussian random walk (`d` pixels/step). The main comparison used `d = 6 px/step`.
- **Compared Methods:**
 - **No-Gate:** Baseline with reliability gating disabled (`κ_eff_t ≡ 1`).
 - **EKF-Gate:** Per-cell Extended Kalman filtering with innovation gating.
 - **Hard-Gate:** Binary write control based on `κ_eff_t > 0.5`.
 - **Oracle-κ:** A privileged baseline using ground-truth alignment error for gating, representing an upper bound on what could be achieved with perfect alignment knowledge.
- **Metrics:**
 - **Ghost:** Normalized artifact score in low-flow regions (lower is better).
 - **NRMSE:** Normalized Root Mean Squared Error in supported regions (lower is better).
 - **ActCov:** Active flow coverage (fraction of active flow region captured in the map, higher is better).
 - **WR:** Write Ratio (cumulative write mass relative to No-Gate, lower implies more conservative writing).

**Main Results (Synthetic Scenes, d = 6 px/step):**
The proposed "Ours" method demonstrated consistent improvements in map quality metrics:
- **Ghost Reduction:** Ours reduced the average Ghost score by 42% (from 0.451 for No-Gate to 0.263). The most substantial absolute reductions occurred in crossflow scenes, where spatial ambiguity is higher.
- **NRMSE Improvement:** NRMSE was reduced from 0.356 (No-Gate) to 0.197.
- **Coverage and Writing:** Ours achieved an average ActCov of 0.540 and a WR of 0.451, indicating that these improvements were obtained through more conservative writing rather than simply better local prediction.
- **Baseline Comparison:**
 - **EKF-Gate** showed limited Ghost reduction while retaining high coverage and write mass.
 - **Hard-Gate** preserved more coverage than Ours but had higher Ghost and NRMSE.
 - **Oracle-κ** performed comparably to Ours in Ghost (0.265) and NRMSE (0.210) but with a higher WR (0.579), suggesting that the learned gate achieves similar safety behavior at a more conservative operating point using only deployment-available inputs.
- **Visual Evidence:** Representative reconstructions showed that Ours successfully preserved dominant flow structures by leaving uncertain regions unsupported, whereas No-Gate accumulated visible ghost artifacts.

**Component Ablation:**
At matched WR, the full model (Ours) yielded the lowest Ghost and NRMSE and the highest ActCov.
- **Recurrent State:** Removing the recurrent state led to the largest degradation in performance (Ghost 0.337 vs. 0.263), underscoring the importance of temporal alignment evidence.
- **Map Conditioning:** Removing map conditioning primarily worsened Ghost and NRMSE.
- **Informativeness Gate:** Removing the informativeness gate resulted in a larger loss in active-flow coverage, indicating its role in appropriate write allocation.

**Mechanism Analysis:**
- **Ghost Reduction vs. Drift:** The absolute Ghost reduction relative to No-Gate increased with higher injected drift levels across all flow families. This was accompanied by a progressively lower write ratio, indicating that the method becomes more conservative as alignment risk increases.
- **κ_t vs. Alignment Error:** The learned reliability score `κ_t` exhibited a systematic decrease as the realized ground-truth alignment error increased, with a Pearson correlation coefficient of r = -0.63. This confirms that `κ_t` effectively tracks alignment risk using only information available during deployment.

**Robot-Platform Validation (Zero-Shot Hardware Replay):**
- **Setup:** Experiments were conducted in a water tank using a submerged thruster to generate a turbulent jet flow. A physical sensing module (with pressure sensors and an optical-flow camera) on a linear stage followed a lawnmower path. The simulation-trained model was deployed zero-shot (frozen weights) using real sensor measurements. PIV provided the reference flow field.
- **Results:** The learned write-safety behavior successfully transferred to real-world sensing conditions.
 - Under injected drift, Ours reduced the Ghost score from 0.28 (No-Gate) to 0.17, a 39% reduction.
 - This reduction was achieved while retaining 81% map coverage (compared to 100% for No-Gate), consistent with the intended behavior of leaving uncertain regions unsupported.
 - Visual comparison confirmed that No-Gate formed a ghost artifact, while Ours attenuated these updates, preserving the dominant wake structure.
 - Under zero drift, both methods qualitatively recovered the principal wake structure, indicating that reliability gating did not suppress valid updates when alignment was accurate.

These findings collectively demonstrate that the proposed conservative fusion framework effectively suppresses drift-induced map corruption by learning to assess and attenuate uncertain updates, both in simulation and in a zero-shot hardware context.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework for online flow field mapping that addresses a fundamental challenge in robotic environmental perception: maintaining map integrity under ambiguous sensing and cumulative localization errors. Its significance and potential impact are multifold:

1. **Addressing a Critical Failure Mode:** The work directly tackles the problem of "ghost structures" and map corruption caused by the interplay of observation aliasing and localization drift. This is a prevalent issue in real-world deployments, particularly for underwater or aerial robots operating in feature-poor or dynamic environments where robust and continuous localization is difficult. By mitigating this, the framework contributes to more reliable and trustworthy environmental maps.
2. **Introducing the Concept of "Write-Safety":** The paper conceptualizes online flow mapping as a "write-safety problem." This represents a shift from merely predicting local information to explicitly deciding *whether* an observation is spatially safe to write into a global map. This distinction is crucial, as a locally accurate prediction can still be detrimental if incorrectly placed. This paradigm could be broadly applicable to other types of online environmental mapping where similar ambiguities and localization challenges exist.
3. **Enhanced Map Consistency and Accuracy:** By intelligently attenuating uncertain updates, the framework demonstrably reduces ghost contamination and NRMSE, leading to cleaner and more accurate maps. This is achieved even if it means sacrificing some map coverage in uncertain regions, prioritizing spatial consistency over completeness—a practical trade-off for downstream tasks.
4. **Robustness in Challenging Environments:** The ability to operate effectively where persistent references for loop closure are unavailable (e.g., open water, homogeneous fluid flows) is a significant advantage. This enhances the operational capabilities of autonomous underwater vehicles (AUVs) and other mobile robots in unexplored or dynamic fluid environments, such as those encountered in oceanography, aquatic monitoring, or search and rescue operations.
5. **Generalizability and Zero-Shot Transfer:** The successful zero-shot transfer of the simulation-trained model to real-world hardware data, without any fine-tuning or domain adaptation, highlights the robustness and generalizability of the learned write-safety behavior. This suggests that the principles learned in simulation can be directly applied to complex, unsteady, and nonlinear real flow conditions, which is a key step towards practical deployment.
6. **Enabling Advanced Robotic Applications:** More accurate and reliable flow maps can enable a range of advanced robotic applications:
 - **Improved Navigation:** Robots can use reliable flow maps for flow-aided navigation, potentially reducing energy consumption and increasing operational endurance.
 - **Better Sampling Strategies:** Understanding the flow field allows for more intelligent and efficient sampling trajectories for environmental monitoring.
 - **Enhanced Environmental Monitoring:** Higher fidelity maps contribute to better scientific understanding and monitoring of complex fluid phenomena.
 - **Swarm Robotics:** In multi-robot systems, consistent flow maps can facilitate coordinated behaviors and shared situational awareness.

In summary, this research provides a principled approach to a long-standing problem in robotic mapping, demonstrating that intelligent control over map updates is as critical as accurate local sensing and prediction for maintaining map integrity in challenging real-world environments.
