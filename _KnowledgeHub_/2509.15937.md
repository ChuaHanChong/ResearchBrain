# A Vision-Language-Action-Critic Model for Robotic Real-World Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Shanghai AI Lab developed the Vision-Language-Action-Critic (VLAC) model and an associated real-world reinforcement learning framework to enable robots to autonomously learn complex manipulation tasks. The system provides generalizable dense rewards and demonstrates robust performance across varied environments, achieving up to 98% success rates with improved sample efficiency.

## Problem

- Real-world robotic reinforcement learning is hindered by sparse and handcrafted reward functions, requiring extensive task-specific engineering.
- Imitation learning for Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models demands vast, costly expert demonstrations that often lack diversity and struggle with cross-scene generalization.
- Real-world data collection and exploration for robots are inefficient, expensive, and time-consuming, slowing down policy learning.

## Method

- Introduces **VLAC**, built on ==InternVL==, which unifies action generation (actor) and task progress prediction/done signals (critic) into a single ==autoregressive architecture==.
- Develops a **VLAC** critic component that generates generalizable ==dense rewards== by predicting pairwise progress and task completion from image observations and language goals, trained with specific data construction strategies.
- Integrates **VLAC** into an asynchronous real-world reinforcement learning framework using ==PPO==, enhanced with graded ==human-in-the-loop (HiL)== interventions and multi-robot scaling capabilities.

## Results

- The VLAC critic achieved robust generalization, reaching **0.95** VOC-F1 on out-of-distribution RT1 data with one-shot in-context learning, and effectively distinguished successful from failed trajectories (**0.89** vs **0.44** VOC-F1 on RoboFAC).
- The VLAC actor demonstrated strong initial capabilities, achieving an average **75%** success rate and **82.5%** task progress across five real-world manipulation tasks, and maintained **57-63%** success under extreme lighting and scene shifts.
- The integrated real-world RL framework improved robot success rates from approximately **30%** to **~90%** within **200** episodes; HiL interventions further boosted average success rates to **93-98%** and improved exploration efficiency by **50%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A unified Vision-Language-Action-Critic architecture can simultaneously provide dense, fine-grained intrinsic rewards and generate actions, simplifying the real-world RL system design and improving learning.
> - Pretraining a critic model on pair-wise progress understanding from diverse multimodal and robotic datasets enables robust, generalizable intrinsic reward generation for novel tasks and environments without manual engineering.
> - Strategic human-in-the-loop interventions, particularly targeted demonstrations and replay, significantly accelerate real-world robot learning and improve sample efficiency by stabilizing exploration and policy improvement.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The research by Zhai et al. addresses critical bottlenecks in the burgeoning field of robotic real-world reinforcement learning (RL) using Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. This area sits at the intersection of robotics, computer vision, natural language processing, and machine learning, aiming to create intelligent robots capable of understanding high-level natural language instructions and executing complex tasks in unstructured physical environments.

**Current Challenges in Robotic Real-World RL:**
Traditionally, robotic manipulation with RL has been hampered by several fundamental issues:
- **Sparse and Handcrafted Rewards:** Designing effective reward functions for real-world tasks is notoriously difficult. Rewards are often sparse (only available at task completion) or require tedious, task-specific engineering, which limits generalization and requires significant human effort for each new task.
- **Inefficient Exploration:** Real-world data collection is expensive, time-consuming, and risky. Robots often struggle with efficient exploration, especially in environments where failures can be costly or damaging.
- **Imitation Learning Limitations:** While VLA models initially showed promise through imitation learning (IL), IL demands vast quantities of high-quality expert demonstrations, which are costly to collect, often limited to laboratory settings, lack diversity, and struggle with cross-scene generalization and robustness in dynamic real-world scenarios.
- **Sim-to-Real Gap:** Training policies in simulation and transferring them to the real world remains a significant challenge due to discrepancies between simulated and physical dynamics, perception, and environment complexities.

**Existing Approaches and Their Limitations:**
The paper situates its work against various existing efforts:
- **Small Policy Models with Human-in-the-Loop (HiL):** Many "on-line" approaches for real-world RL use small policy models, often relying on self-imitation or hindsight relabeling. These typically require human intervention (e.g., expert demonstrations, human guidance) to improve sample efficiency. However, they demand considerable engineering overhead for each new task, including collecting task-specific data, hand-designing rewards, and implementing bespoke task-completion detectors.
- **Large VLA Models for Real-World RL:** More recently, researchers have explored real-world RL atop large VLA models, leveraging their strong pretrained multimodal priors to accelerate early exploration. However, the field lacks a converged design pattern due to the heterogeneity of VLA architectures (perception-language fusion, action representation, progress/value evaluation interfaces).
- **General Reward Models:** To address sparse rewards, various methods have emerged:
 - **Prompt-based VLM scoring:** Querying general VLMs to estimate completion or compare states. These suffer from limited precision, sensitivity to environmental changes, temporal instability, and weak discrimination of failures.
 - **Semantic embedding distance (e.g., CLIP-style):** Using similarity in a shared embedding space as a reward, which can also be imprecise.
 - **Goal state synthesis/editing:** Generating a target image and using the difference as a reward, which adds complexity.
 - **Learned progress embeddings (time-contrastive/temporal-difference):** Training models to learn rank-consistent progress signals from videos. While smoother, these often lack generalization capabilities to novel tasks or objects.
- **RL Post-training for VLAs:** Integration of RL with VLA models presents architectural challenges. For discrete action tokens, PPO variants are common. For continuous actions (e.g., diffusion models), techniques like value-guided filtering or offline-regularized RL are used, but they can incur high computational costs and instability due to long gradient chains. Strong zero/few-shot capabilities and offline-to-online strategies are crucial here.

**VLAC's Contribution to the Landscape:**
VLAC attempts to bridge these gaps by introducing a unified VLA-Critic model that simultaneously addresses the problems of dense reward generation and efficient policy learning in real-world settings. It builds upon a state-of-the-art multimodal foundation model (InternVL) to:
1. **Generate Generalizable Dense Rewards:** By focusing on pair-wise progress prediction, VLAC aims to create a "universal" critic that provides fine-grained, low-noise, and failure-discriminating rewards that generalize across tasks and environments without task-specific engineering.
2. **Unify Actor-Critic Roles:** Integrating both policy (action generation) and critic (progress prediction) into a single autoregressive architecture simplifies the overall system design and enables joint training benefits.
3. **Facilitate Efficient Real-World RL:** By providing dense rewards and combining strong pretrained priors with an asynchronous real-world RL loop and graded human-in-the-loop interventions, VLAC aims to significantly accelerate exploration and stabilize learning on physical robots.

In essence, this work represents a significant step towards creating more autonomous, adaptable, and general-purpose robotic agents by tackling the core challenges of reward design and sample efficiency in real-world reinforcement learning, moving beyond the limitations of purely imitation-based or heavily engineered approaches.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to **develop a general and efficient framework for robotic real-world reinforcement learning using Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, specifically addressing the challenges posed by sparse, handcrafted rewards and inefficient exploration.**

This overarching goal is driven by several key motivations stemming from the limitations of current robotic AI systems:

1. **Overcoming Imitation Learning (IL) Bottlenecks:** Current VLA models predominantly rely on IL, which is severely limited by the immense cost and time required for large-scale, high-quality data collection. This data is often specific to laboratory setups, lacks diversity, and presents significant hurdles for cross-scene generalization and real-world robustness. The authors are motivated to move beyond this reliance to enable robots to learn autonomously from their own interactions.

2. **Eliminating Task-Specific Reward Engineering:** A major barrier to scalable real-world RL is the need to hand-engineer dense reward functions for each new task. This process is time-consuming, non-generalizable, and often yields sparse rewards that impede learning. The motivation is to create a "universal" reward model that can provide dense, fine-grained progress feedback across diverse tasks and environments without manual intervention.

3. **Improving Sample Efficiency in Real-World RL:** Real-world robot interactions are costly and slow. Existing methods often struggle with inefficient exploration, leading to prohibitively long training times. The goal is to develop mechanisms that accelerate learning, allowing robots to achieve high success rates with fewer real-world interactions.

4. **Enhancing Generalization and Robustness:** Robots need to operate effectively in dynamic, unconstrained real-world environments with novel objects, scenes, and task variations. Traditional methods often exhibit poor generalization. The motivation is to imbue robotic policies with strong cross-task and cross-scene transferability, ideally with zero-shot or one-shot learning capabilities.

5. **Unifying Actor and Critic Capabilities:** Many existing systems treat the policy (actor) and reward/value function (critic) as separate components, leading to integration complexities. The authors aim to unify these roles within a single autoregressive VLA architecture to simplify the system and potentially leverage shared representations for mutual improvement.

6. **Stabilizing Real-World Learning:** Online real-world RL can be unstable, prone to policy deterioration from failed explorations. A key motivation is to incorporate robust learning strategies, including human-in-the-loop interventions, to stabilize the training process and ensure continuous improvement.

In summary, the authors are motivated to enable robots to learn effectively and autonomously in the real world by developing a VLA model that provides generalizable dense rewards, facilitates efficient exploration, demonstrates strong generalization, and integrates seamlessly into a robust real-world RL framework.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of this work lies in the **Vision-Language-Action-Critic (VLAC) model** and its integration into a **real-world reinforcement learning framework** enhanced with human-in-the-loop mechanisms.

#### 3.1. Vision-Language-Action-Critic (VLAC) Model

VLAC is built upon **InternVL**, a state-of-the-art multi-modal model, and designed as a single autoregressive architecture that unifies the roles of "actor" (action generation) and "critic" (task progress prediction and done signal).

#### 3.1.1. VLAC Critic Learning (Dense Reward Generation)

The critic component is central to addressing the sparse reward problem. It predicts a signed progress delta and a "done" signal given pairwise observations and a language goal.
- **Pair-wise Progress Understanding**: The model takes two images ($o_i, o_{i+\Delta t}$) and a task description ($l_{task}$) as input. It outputs $c_{i,i+\Delta t} = \text{VLAC}(o_i, o_{i+\Delta t}; l_{task})$, representing the relative progress (as a percentage $\Delta t / (T-i)$) from $o_i$ to $o_{i+\Delta t}$. This formulation is robust to starting points and data collection strategies.
- **Task Description Estimation**: To enhance semantic understanding, the model also learns to generate the task description from initial and final frames: $l_{task} = \text{VLAC}(o_{i_{start}}, o_{i_{end}})$.
- **Task Completion Judgment**: A binary prediction ($l_{done} = \text{VLAC}(o_i; l_{task})$) indicates whether the task is completed (1) or not (0), using specific thresholds based on trajectory duration.
- **Data Construction Strategies for Enhanced Critic Learning**:
 1. **Pair-wise Image Difference Filtering**: Filters out noisy or static segments by setting progress to 0 if pixel difference is below a threshold, making the model focus on significant changes.
 2. **Pair-wise Progress Understanding with Joint Sampling**: Inspired by contrastive learning, for each sampled pair, four related samples are constructed (forward/backward, fine-grained/global) to ensure data balance and symmetry.
 3. **Task Completion Judgment Joint Sampling**: Ensures balanced training by sampling both completed and incomplete states from the same trajectory.
 4. **Cross-sampling of Task Descriptions**: To improve robustness and semantic alignment, 5% of samples are created with a task description mismatched to the trajectory, setting progress to 0. This helps the model reject irrelevant prompts.
- **In-context Learning for Generalization**: To improve cross-scene and cross-task transferability, the model can take a reference process ($O_{ref}$) and the current trajectory's starting point ($o_0$) as additional input: $c_{i,i+\Delta t} = \text{VLAC}(o_i, o_{i+\Delta t}; l_{task}, O_{ref}, o_0)$. This allows the model to learn from a single reference example.

#### 3.1.2. VLAC Action Learning (Policy)

The actor component generates actions in a semantic space, leveraging the pretrained multimodal model's generation capabilities.
- **Action Representation**: Actions are represented as delta End-Effector (eef) poses (e.g., "x: -47mm, y: 19mm, z: 66mm, roll: 14 degrees, pitch: 10 degrees, yaw: 15 degrees, open: 0"). These are encoded as numerical strings within a structured template, allowing for autoregressive token generation.
- **Input**: The policy takes multiple viewpoints, the robot's state, task description, and recent action history to generate the next action.

#### 3.1.3. VLAC Vision-Language Perception Learning and Data Mixture

To enhance multimodal understanding, VLAC is trained on a vast and diverse dataset:
- **Multimodal Datasets**: Public VQA datasets (Llava, RoboVQA, SpatialVQA, Spot-the-diff, InstructPix2Pix) are incorporated to strengthen general conversational ability, robotic understanding, spatial reasoning, and pair-wise image difference distinction.
- **Robotic and Human Trajectory Data**: Over 3000 hours of human data, 1200 hours of public robotic manipulation data (AGIBOT, Bridge, Droid, FMB, RoboSet), and 15 hours of self-collected manipulation data are used. This data mixture provides action control, robust task progress assessment, and task completion verification, with a total of 40 million sampled data points.

#### 3.2. Real-World RL Framework with VLAC: Infrastructure of RL

- **Asynchronous, Low-Latency Design**: A single-controller architecture processes observations promptly to generate actions. A dynamic inference server allocation mechanism ensures VLA inference within 0.1 seconds to minimize real-world execution delays. Robots operate asynchronously, uploading observations and executing actions independently, with action timestamps adjusted for VLA inference time to ensure smooth movements.
- **Technology Stack**: The framework uses ZeroMQ (zmq) for communication, Ray for distributed components (inference workers, trainers, data servers, log servers, rollout workers), and supports both vllm and torch for inference (with torch used for probability recomputation during training to avoid PPO clipping issues).

#### 3.2.2. PPO Based Policy Optimization

- **Actor-Critic Roles**: A 2B VLAC model acts as the policy (actor), while an 8B VLAC model serves as the external "critic" for computing dense rewards and done signals using specific prompts. A separate, simpler deep neural network is used as the critic for Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) within PPO.
- **Reward and Done Signal Extraction**: The 8B VLAC model is prompted with "<image> The 1 means yes, the 0 means no. Check if the robot has completed its task: <task>" for the done signal, and "Image-1: <image> Image-2: <image> Compare two images and evaluate whether the second image is closer to achieving task objectives compared to the first image. + score means the second image is closer, - score means the first image is closer Response the relative progressing of target task follow <score>. The target task is: task <score>" for rewards.
- **PPO Algorithm**: Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is used to optimize the policy, employing a clipped surrogate objective and entropy regularization for stability and exploration. Logits from the autoregressively generated action tokens are used for policy updates. A linear value head is attached to the VLAC's hidden state to estimate $V(s_t)$ for PPO.

#### 3.2.3. Human-in-the-Loop (HiL) Mechanisms

Three graded HiL strategies are introduced to stabilize training and accelerate exploration, especially during early learning or when the policy struggles:
1. **Offline Demonstration Replay**: An expert demonstration buffer is pre-populated with human-collected data. The model is periodically updated using Negative Log-Likelihood (NLL) loss on this data, with tele-operators targeting scenarios where the policy has low success rates.
2. **Return and Explore**: Tele-operators manually reset the robot and objects to problematic states (e.g., frequent failure points) to facilitate targeted practice and exploration.
3. **Human Guided Explore**: For tasks where the policy consistently fails to explore correct behaviors, tele-operators provide direct demonstrations, which are then added to the human replay buffer.

#### 3.2.4. Multi-Robot Scaling

- The framework supports training with multiple real-world robots.
- A **dynamic sampling strategy** is implemented to address learning imbalances caused by heterogeneity across robots (backgrounds, lighting, etc.). Data from under-learned robots is sampled more frequently to balance overall progress.

This comprehensive methodology allows VLAC to leverage large-scale multimodal pretraining for robust task understanding and action generation, while the real-world RL framework ensures efficient, stable, and scalable learning on physical robots, augmented by strategic human intervention.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yields several significant findings across VLAC's critic performance, actor performance, real-world RL results, and multi-robot scaling.

#### 4.1. VLAC Critic Performance

The VLAC critic model demonstrates strong performance in understanding task progress and generalizing across diverse scenarios.
- **Robust Generalization**: On in-distribution datasets (Bridge, Droid), VLAC-8b achieves VOC-F1 scores of 1.00. Crucially, it shows robust generalization on out-of-distribution datasets, such as RT1 (tasks, scenes, robots vastly different from training data), achieving a VOC-F1 of 0.95 with one-shot in-context learning.
- **Impact of In-Context Learning**: One-shot learning significantly boosts performance on novel tasks. For instance, on RoboNet (lacking language annotations and smooth temporal structure), zero-shot VOC-F1 is 0 (correctly reflecting reliance on language), but one-shot performance dramatically improves to 0.76 VOC-F1, highlighting powerful contextual reasoning.
- **Distinguishing Success from Failure**: On the RoboFAC dataset, which contains both successful and failed trajectories, VLAC-8b clearly distinguishes them, achieving a VOC-F1 of 0.89 on successful videos and only 0.44 on failed ones. This demonstrates a strong ability to identify erroneous actions.
- **Benefits of Human Data**: Including Ego4D (human demonstration video data) in training significantly enhances the model's ability to understand embodied task processes and further improves the gap between successful and failed video scores on RoboFAC.

#### 4.2. VLAC Actor Performance

The VLAC actor exhibits strong action generation capabilities and impressive robustness.
- **High Initial Success Rates**: After pretraining, the VLAC model achieves an average success rate of 75% and an average task progress of 82.5% across five diverse real-world manipulation tasks (Open Cooker, Pick&Place Bowl, Unfold Mat, Desktop Sweep, Rice Transfer).
- **Robustness to Environmental Changes**: The model demonstrates robust generalization under challenging conditions:
 - **Lighting Transfer**: Maintained a 57% average success rate under extreme lighting disturbances (colored flashing lights), a notable drop from 75% but still performing reasonably.
 - **Scene Transfer**: Achieved a 63% average success rate across different workbenches with varying camera perspectives and settings, again without extra data collection.
- **Importance of Task Progress Pretraining**: A VLAC model without task progress pretraining ("VLAC w/o pretrain") shows a significant drop in success rate (average 16%), indicating that the critic's ability to interpret task progression is crucial for the actor to make correct decisions and ensure a higher success rate at each execution stage.

#### 4.3. Real-World RL Results

The integrated real-world RL framework with VLAC demonstrates rapid self-improvement, significantly enhanced by human-in-the-loop interventions.
- **Autonomous Improvement**: A single VLAC model (baseline, without HiL) autonomously improves its success rate from approximately 30% (initial) to about 90% within 200 real-world interaction episodes across four distinct manipulation tasks.
- **Impact of Human-in-the-Loop (HiL)**: HiL interventions yield substantial improvements in final success rates and sample efficiency:
 - **Final Success**: Offline Demonstration Replay and Human Guided Explore achieve 100% success rates on several tasks and average 93% and 98% respectively, significantly outperforming the Baseline (88%) and Return and Explore (95%).
 - **Sample Efficiency**: HiL interventions lead to an approximate 50% improvement in exploration efficiency. Offline Demonstration Replay typically results in faster learning curves initially.
 - **Effectiveness of Strategies**: Human Guided Explore and Offline Demonstration Replay are most effective, suggesting that high-quality human demonstrations (targeted for failures or as expert examples) are critical for robust policy learning. Return and Explore, while better than baseline, is less impactful, potentially due to the difficulty of selecting optimal return points.

#### 4.4. Multi-Robot Scaling

Experiments on a representative tabletop manipulation task (Pick-and-Place Bowl) show positive scaling with the number of robots.
- **Scaling Law**: Increasing the number of robots (from 2 to 8) decreases the data required *per robot* to reach an 80% success rate. With 8 robots, only 64 episodes per robot (24.6 minutes) were needed, compared to 325 episodes per robot for 2 robots.
- **Single-Robot Anomaly**: The single-robot case performed comparably to 4 robots (fewer episodes than 2 robots), attributed to its visually static background, implying that visual heterogeneity across multiple robots can introduce disturbances that slow learning of a more generalized policy.
- **Dynamic Sampling**: A dynamic sampling strategy that prioritizes data from under-learned robots effectively balances training progress across heterogeneous multi-robot setups.

In essence, the findings demonstrate that VLAC, leveraging strong multimodal priors and structured intrinsic rewards within an efficient real-world RL framework with strategic human guidance, can enable robots to learn complex manipulation skills quickly, robustly, and generalize well across varied environments.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work presented by Zhai et al. on the VLAC model and its real-world RL framework offers several significant contributions and holds substantial potential impact for the field of robotics and artificial intelligence.

1. **Enabling Generalizable Real-World Reinforcement Learning**: The primary significance lies in providing a practical and effective method for robots to continuously learn and improve in the physical world. By converting general multimodal understanding into dense, signed, stepwise intrinsic rewards, VLAC addresses the long-standing challenge of sparse and handcrafted rewards in real-world RL. This eliminates the need for task-specific reward engineering, making the deployment of RL to new and diverse robotic tasks far more scalable and accessible.

2. **Unified Actor-Critic Architecture for VLAs**: VLAC unifies the "actor" (action generation) and "critic" (progress prediction, done signal) within a single autoregressive Vision-Language-Action model. This architectural integration simplifies the overall system design, potentially fostering stronger coupling and mutual benefits between understanding task progress and executing actions. This is a crucial step towards more holistic and efficient VLA-based robot learning systems.

3. **Enhanced Sample Efficiency and Faster Learning**: The combination of powerful VLA priors (from large-scale pretraining), intrinsic rewards, an asynchronous real-world RL infrastructure, and strategic human-in-the-loop (HiL) protocols drastically accelerates learning. The demonstrated ability to raise success rates from ~30% to ~90% within 200 real-world episodes, and a further 50% improvement in sample efficiency with HiL, is a significant leap forward. This makes real-world experimentation more feasible and less resource-intensive, unlocking possibilities for widespread robot deployment.

4. **Robustness and Cross-Domain Generalization**: The impressive transfer capabilities of VLAC under lighting and scene shifts, as well as its accurate progress understanding for novel entities and tasks (demonstrated by high VOC-F1 scores on out-of-distribution datasets), are critical for real-world application. Robots need to operate in dynamic, unconstrained environments, and VLAC's ability to generalize without additional data collection in these varied conditions is a strong indicator of its potential for real-world robustness.

5. **Scalability to Multi-Robot Systems**: The empirical evidence demonstrating that learning performance scales positively with the number of robots (decreasing data per robot for target success) is highly impactful. This suggests a viable path for deploying and continuously improving fleets of robots, which is essential for industrial applications, logistics, and large-scale autonomous systems. The dynamic sampling strategy for heterogeneous multi-robot setups further enhances this scalability.

6. **Practical Recipe for Self-Improving Robots**: The work provides a "practical recipe" showing that large multimodal priors combined with structured intrinsic progress feedback can make real-world online RL feasible, data-efficient, and incrementally improvable. This moves beyond theoretical discussions to a tangible framework that can be adopted and built upon by other researchers and practitioners aiming to develop truly autonomous and adaptive robotic agents.

7. **Guidance for Future Research**: By explicitly outlining limitations (e.g., heuristic nature of HiL, RL recipe specificity, multi-task online instability) and suggesting future work directions, the paper also serves as a valuable roadmap for advancing the field. Formalizing intervention metrics, designing architecture-agnostic progress/value bridging layers, and developing stabilization mechanisms are crucial next steps that can build directly on VLAC's foundation.

In conclusion, the VLAC model and its accompanying real-world RL framework represent a substantial advancement towards robust, general-purpose, and continuously improving robotic intelligence. By addressing fundamental challenges in reward design, sample efficiency, and generalization, this research brings us closer to a future where robots can autonomously learn and adapt in complex, unstructured real-world environments.
