# MoWAM: Explicit Future Motion Prediction for Efficient World Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> MoWAM is a world action model that replaces dense future video generation with explicit prediction of structured end-effector motion, built from fingertip and palm keypoints. A Mixture-of-Transformer design learns video dynamics only during training while an Action-Motion Transformer jointly denoises actions and motion. A motion-aware verifier enables cheap best-of-K sampling, reaching 98.9% on LIBERO, 81.43% on LIBERO-Plus, and 80% real-world success.

## Problem

- WAMs that explicitly generate future videos at inference, such as joint or video-then-action designs, pay heavy compute and memory costs rendering backgrounds and textures irrelevant to control.
- Fast-WAM-style models drop future generation at inference, leaving dynamics implicit in observation features; they hold in-distribution but trail video-rollout WAMs by about 10 points under OOD shift.
- Compact alternatives represent the future only in latent spaces, and dense video rollouts make sampling and comparing multiple candidate futures at inference prohibitively expensive.

## Method

- ==Structured end-effector motion==: projected left/right fingertip and palm keypoints yield ==gripper center==, ==fingertip span==, and ==palm offset==; motion combines their per-step changes (dynamics) with per-step span and offset (structure).
- **Mixture-of-Transformer** on Wan2.2-5B: a **Video Transformer** denoises future video in training but runs once at inference for observation keys/values; an **Action-Motion Transformer** jointly ==flow-matches== actions and motion over them.
- ==Motion-based inference-time scaling==: sample K action-motion candidates and execute the best under a ==motion-aware task-progress verifier==, trained with ==pairwise ranking loss== against perturbed demos (shrunk, shifted, truncated motion).

## Results

- LIBERO-Plus: **81.43%** average OOD success, **+11.0** points over Fast-WAM and on par with Joint-WAM (**80.57%**) and IDM-WAM (**80.14%**), at **293.5 ms** versus **766.3** and **995.4 ms** latency.
- LIBERO **98.9%** average without embodied pretraining (**+2.4** over Fast-WAM); real Franka tasks average **80%** versus Fast-WAM **45%** and Motus **71.67%**, at **293.5 ms** versus Motus's **1621.9 ms**.
- LIBERO-Long OOD ablations: no motion **65.71%**, motion without action conditioning **70.86%**, full **76%**; frozen video DiT collapses to **33.14%**. Best-of-8 lifts Pick Banana **65%** to **80%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicit future prediction matters most under distribution shift, but the control-relevant future can be a compact end-effector trajectory rather than a full rendered video.
> - Training-time video prediction and inference-time explicit motion play different roles: video supervision shapes the representation, while motion supplies a cheap explicit future; neither substitutes for the other.
> - Compact future representations turn saved rendering compute into test-time search; commanded actions projected into images poorly predict actual execution, so learned outcome prediction is worth scoring instead.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

MoWAM addresses a current research problem in robot learning involving the relationship between **Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models**, **world models**, and efficient inference. VLA systems generally map an image, language instruction, and robot state directly to actions. They have benefited from large-scale vision-language pretraining, but many do not explicitly model how the environment will change after an action is executed. This limits their ability to reason about consequences, particularly when observations differ from the training distribution.

World Action Models (WAMs) extend VLA policies by incorporating future dynamics. Recent WAMs jointly model robot actions and future visual states, often using video diffusion or video-generation models. These approaches can provide an explicit, scene-specific prediction of how the environment may evolve. However, generating future videos is computationally expensive and can require substantial memory and latency. Moreover, complete visual futures contain information—such as textures, backgrounds, and other scene details—that may not be necessary for manipulation control.

A second line of work, represented in the paper by Fast-WAM, retains future-dynamics learning during training but removes future-video generation at inference. Actions are instead predicted directly from observation features produced by the trained video model. This improves efficiency, but the future remains encoded implicitly rather than explicitly instantiated for the current scene. The authors argue that this distinction matters under distribution shift: implicit dynamics features may be adequate for familiar inputs but less effective when objects, lighting, sensors, initial states, or language conditions change.

MoWAM is positioned between these two alternatives. It preserves future-video modeling during training, but replaces dense future-video generation during inference with explicit prediction of structured robot motion. The work therefore contributes to a broader effort to identify compact, task-relevant representations of future dynamics, while also exploring inference-time scaling through multiple candidate action predictions.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

The central objective is to determine whether a WAM can retain the benefits of explicit future reasoning without incurring the cost of generating complete future videos at inference. The authors focus on robot manipulation, where the most important future information may be how the robot’s end effector moves and changes configuration in response to the scene and task constraints.

The motivation has several components. First, action prediction based only on current observations does not explicitly represent the consequences of candidate actions. Second, full future visual rollouts are costly because they require iterative generation of multiple video frames, often through a large diffusion model. Third, visual future predictions include many details that are not directly relevant to robot control. The authors therefore seek a representation that is both explicit and compact.

MoWAM uses end-effector motion as this representation. The intended motion is not simply a geometric projection of commanded actions. Instead, it is a prediction of how the robot is expected to move when those actions are executed in the current scene. This distinction is important because the same low-level command can lead to different observed motion depending on object contact, obstacles, grasping conditions, and other interaction constraints.

The paper also aims to make inference-time scaling practical. If a model can generate several possible action plans and select the most suitable one, additional computation at test time may improve reliability. Full video-based WAMs make this expensive because every candidate may require a separate visual rollout. MoWAM instead generates multiple action-motion pairs and uses a motion-aware verifier to rank them.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Structured Motion Representation

The proposed motion representation is derived from projected end-effector keypoints in one or more camera views. For each future timestep, the model uses the image positions of the left fingertip, right fingertip, and palm. From these points, it computes:

1. The fingertip span, which represents the vector between the two fingertips.
2. The gripper center, defined as the midpoint between the fingertips.
3. The relative palm offset from the gripper center.

Temporal motion is represented by the changes in these quantities between consecutive timesteps. This produces a six-dimensional dynamic representation consisting of changes in gripper-center position, fingertip span, and palm offset. A separate structural representation retains the fingertip span and palm offset at each future timestep. The combined representation captures both overall end-effector displacement and changes in gripper geometry, including opening and closing.

The representation is collected across the action horizon and camera views. It discards dense scene appearance while retaining information intended to be relevant to manipulation execution.

#### 3.2. Dual-Branch Mixture-of-Transformer Architecture

MoWAM consists of two Transformer branches:

- A **Video Transformer**, which learns future visual dynamics during training.
- An **Action-Motion Transformer**, which jointly predicts action chunks and structured future motion.

Observed images are first encoded into latent tokens using a variational autoencoder. Future video frames are separately encoded and noised for future-video training. Actions and motion are also encoded and noised. The Video Transformer learns to denoise future visual latents conditioned on the current observation and language instruction.

The Action-Motion Transformer receives the observation representation produced by the Video Transformer. Its action and motion tokens exchange information through self-attention and access the observation representation through attention keys and values. Language instructions are incorporated using cross-attention. Action and motion are modeled jointly, allowing the predicted motion to influence action generation and allowing the two outputs to remain paired.

Because multiple future motions can be plausible for a given scene and instruction, the authors model motion probabilistically using flow matching. During training, the model jointly learns the distribution of actions and corresponding future motions while the Video Transformer learns scene evolution.

At inference, the Video Transformer processes the current observation once to produce its observation representation. It does not generate future frames. The Action-Motion Transformer then denoises action and motion from random noise, conditioned on the observation representation and language instruction. Only the resulting action chunk is decoded into robot control commands.

#### 3.3. Motion-Based Inference-Time Verification

MoWAM can sample multiple action-motion candidates for the same observation and instruction. A separate motion-aware verifier scores each candidate according to the current image, language instruction, and predicted motion.

The verifier is trained using pairwise ranking. Successful demonstration motions are treated as positive examples. Negative motions are generated by perturbing the demonstrations in several ways, including reducing motion changes, applying smooth spatial shifts, or truncating the later portion of the trajectory. These perturbations represent incomplete execution, incorrect final positions, and altered motion shapes.

The verifier is trained with a margin-based ranking loss so that the demonstrated motion receives a higher score than its perturbed counterpart. At inference, the action associated with the highest-scoring motion is executed.

#### 3.4. Experimental Design

The evaluation uses:

- **LIBERO**, consisting of four task suites with ten tasks each. The authors report 50 rollouts per task.
- **LIBERO-Plus**, which evaluates seven types of distribution shift using 700 OOD evaluation instances. Policies are trained on LIBERO and evaluated without adaptation.
- **Real-world tasks** on a Franka Research 3 robot: Pick Banana, Stack Bowls, and Close Drawer. The authors collect 100 demonstrations per task and run 20 evaluation trials per task.

The implementation uses the pretrained Wan2.2-5B video model. Action and motion horizons are both 32, and videos are temporally downsampled to nine frames per chunk. Models are trained for 20,000 steps on four NVIDIA A100 GPUs. Inference uses ten denoising steps and classifier-free guidance of 1.0. Latency is measured on an NVIDIA RTX 5090 GPU.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. In-Distribution Performance

On LIBERO, MoWAM achieves an average success rate of **98.9%**. This is 2.4 percentage points higher than Fast-WAM’s 96.5% and is comparable to Joint-WAM’s 98.75%, while avoiding dense future-video generation at inference. It also exceeds LaWAM’s 96.4% among methods using compact future representations.

The results indicate that replacing complete visual futures with structured motion does not substantially reduce in-distribution task performance. MoWAM obtains suite-level success rates of 98.8% on Spatial, 99.8% on Object, 99.0% on Goal, and 97.8% on Long.

#### 4.2. Robustness Under Distribution Shift

On LIBERO-Plus, MoWAM obtains an average success rate of **81.43%**. It improves over Fast-WAM by 11 percentage points under aligned training settings and exceeds LaWAM by 3 percentage points. It also exceeds Motus, which uses embodied pretraining, by 5.43 percentage points.

MoWAM is competitive with methods that generate full visual futures. Joint-WAM and IDM-WAM reach 80.57% and 80.14%, respectively, compared with MoWAM’s 81.43%. However, MoWAM has an action-generation latency of **293.5 ms**, compared with 766.3 ms for Joint-WAM and 995.4 ms for IDM-WAM.

The gains over Fast-WAM are particularly evident under Initial and Sensor perturbations, where MoWAM improves by 12 and 14 percentage points, respectively. It matches or exceeds full visual-future baselines under Light, Objects, and Background shifts. Nevertheless, it is not uniformly equivalent to full visual prediction: under some Initial and Camera conditions, MoWAM remains 5 and 8 percentage points below the strongest visual-future methods.

#### 4.3. Real-World Performance

Across the three real-world tasks, MoWAM achieves an average success rate of **80%**, compared with 45% for Fast-WAM and 71.67% for Motus. It achieves 65% on Pick Banana, 95% on Stack Bowls, and 80% on Close Drawer. The method matches Motus on Pick Banana, improves substantially on Stack Bowls, and performs slightly worse than Motus on Close Drawer.

Its latency is 293.5 ms, compared with 260.1 ms for Fast-WAM and 1,621.9 ms for Motus. Thus, the additional motion-prediction cost is relatively small compared with the cost of a video-generating WAM.

#### 4.4. Ablations and Motion Accuracy

Ablations show that both motion components contribute. Removing dynamic-motion supervision reduces average LIBERO-Plus success from 76.0% to 69.14%, while removing structural supervision reduces it to 73.71%. Removing motion entirely lowers it to 65.71%.

Training-time future-video modeling is also important. Freezing the video model and omitting future-video training reduces average performance to 33.14%, indicating that motion prediction alone does not replace scene-level dynamics learning. Preventing action tokens from attending to motion tokens yields 70.86%, below the full model’s 76.0%, showing that motion improves performance both through supervision and through direct conditioning.

Predicted motion is substantially closer to actual execution than motion obtained by projecting action commands into image space. Average displacement error is 5.63 in-distribution and 9.38 out-of-distribution for predicted motion, compared with 77.56 and 63.86 for action-projected motion.

Increasing the number of candidates also improves performance. On Pick Banana, success rises from 65% with one candidate to 75% with four and 80% with eight. Memory and latency grow more slowly for MoWAM than for full visual-future alternatives, making candidate exploration more feasible.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

MoWAM’s main significance is methodological: it shows that explicit future reasoning in robot policies need not require dense future-scene generation. Structured end-effector motion provides a task-relevant intermediate representation that can preserve some benefits of explicit prediction while reducing computational cost.

The approach may be useful for real-time manipulation systems, where latency and memory limit the practicality of large video world models. Its verifier-based candidate selection also provides a way to trade additional inference computation for improved reliability without proportionally increasing visual-generation costs.

The results suggest that future representations should be selected according to control relevance rather than visual completeness. However, the method is specialized toward manipulation and end-effector-centric behavior. It may be less suitable for tasks where object motion, articulated environment dynamics, contact forces, or scene-wide changes are essential. The evaluation is also limited to three real-world tasks and benchmark environments, and the paper does not establish how the representation would transfer to mobile robots, deformable objects, or long-horizon navigation. Future work could therefore examine richer structured representations that retain the efficiency of motion prediction while modeling important non-robot future states.
