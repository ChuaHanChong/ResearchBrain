# ROBOGATE: Adaptive Failure Discovery for Safe Robot Policy Deployment via Two-Stage Boundary-Focused Sampling

> [!summary] Summary
> ROBOGATE introduces an adaptive framework for validating robot manipulation policies, employing a two-stage boundary-focused sampling strategy to efficiently identify failure modes across high-dimensional operational parameter spaces. The study empirically quantifies a "cross-simulator gap," revealing that Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models performing well in academic benchmarks achieve 0% success in industrial simulation environments, underscoring the need for a dedicated validation layer in Physical AI deployment.

## Problem

- Traditional uniform random testing for robot policy validation is computationally inefficient in high-dimensional parameter spaces.
- Current evaluation frameworks often lack cross-embodiment validation, hindering the distinction between universal and robot-specific failure modes.
- Learned robot policies are often poorly characterized in edge cases or adversarial conditions, posing risks for real-world industrial deployment.

## Method

- A ==two-stage adaptive sampling== strategy is implemented in **ROBOGATE**, moving from ==Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS)== uniform exploration (Stage 1) to ==boundary-focused sampling== (Stage 2) that concentrates resources on the ==30–70% success-rate transition zone==.
- A ==logistic regression risk model== with interaction terms is developed to derive ==closed-form failure boundary equations== and quantify deployment risk with interpretable parameter thresholds and ==bootstrap confidence intervals==.
- The framework enables ==cross-embodiment validation== across multiple robot platforms (Franka Panda 7-DOF, UR3e/UR5e/UR10e 6-DOF) within an ==8-dimensional operational parameter space== using ==NVIDIA Isaac Sim== with ==Newton physics==, and produces a ==deployment-gate== leaderboard for VLA models.

## Results

- The two-stage adaptive sampling strategy improved risk prediction, achieving an AUC of **0.780** for the logistic regression model (vs **0.754** for Stage 1 alone), and effectively targeted failure regions with **63.9%** of boundary-focused samples resulting in failure.
- All **seven** state-of-the-art VLA models (GR00T N1.6, π0, OpenVLA, SmolVLA, Octo-Base, Octo-Small) achieved **0%** success on ROBOGATE's 68 industrial scenarios in Isaac Sim, with NVIDIA's GR00T N1.6 showing a **97.65 pp** cross-simulator gap (LIBERO MuJoCo **97.65%** → Isaac Sim industrial **0/68**) on a single fine-tuned checkpoint.
- A closed-form failure boundary equation $\mu^*(m) = (1.469 + 0.419m)/(3.691 - 1.400m)$ was derived for the friction-mass space, and **four universal danger zones** (e.g., object masses **0.935 kg – 2.114 kg**) were identified affecting multiple robot embodiments.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Efficient and robust robot policy validation requires actively targeting success-failure boundary regions rather than relying on uniform or exhaustive sampling.
> - A significant "cross-simulator gap" exists, where high performance in one academic simulator (e.g., MuJoCo) does not guarantee readiness for deployment in another industrial simulator (e.g., Isaac Sim), necessitating a dedicated validation layer for Physical AI.
> - Interpretable risk models that provide closed-form failure boundaries and critical parameter thresholds are essential for translating validation results into actionable operational constraints and policy improvements.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a critical challenge in robotics: the safe and reliable deployment of learned manipulation policies, particularly in industrial contexts. As advanced robot policies, including imitation learning and Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, demonstrate increasing capabilities in controlled environments, the need for rigorous pre-deployment validation intensifies. Current validation methodologies are noted to have limitations, such as the intractability of exhaustive testing in high-dimensional parameter spaces, the inefficiency of uniform random sampling, and the common practice of evaluating policies on a single robot embodiment.

ROBOGATE distinguishes itself from existing approaches in several ways. In **robot policy evaluation**, it diverges from traditional fixed benchmark suites like RL-Bench, Meta-World, and LIBERO by explicitly focusing on the success-failure transition zones through adaptive sampling. Regarding **sim-to-real transfer and domain randomization**, while complementary to domain randomization (DR) which enhances policy robustness, ROBOGATE focuses on systematically mapping the failure landscape. It differs from Bayesian optimization methods for failure discovery by aiming to map the entire boundary surface for interpretability rather than optimizing for worst-case performance.

In the area of **adaptive testing and falsification**, the two-stage sampling strategy employed by ROBOGATE shares similarities with sequential experimental design, but utilizes a simpler binning strategy for scalability and direct interpretability, contrasting with reinforcement learning-based approaches like Adaptive Stress Testing (AST) that prioritize temporal sequences. For **safety validation for robot deployment**, the framework directly addresses gaps in industrial safety standards (e.g., ISO 10218, ISO/TS 15066) by providing a metric-based validation gate with hard thresholds, adaptive failure discovery, and interpretable risk models, a concept analogous to "deployment gates" in software engineering.

A significant aspect of this work relates to **Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models**. It contributes to the systematic evaluation of these models under challenging industrial conditions, an area previously underexplored. It extends existing work on VLA robustness (e.g., LIBERO-PRO, LIBERO-Plus, vla-eval) by specifically investigating and quantifying a "cross-simulator gap," arguing that strong performance in one academic simulator does not predict deployment readiness in another. This concept is framed within a "validation layer paradigm" for Physical AI, drawing an analogy to NVIDIA's Ising for quantum computing, which acts as a calibration layer for noisy processors. This positioning implies that foundation policies trained in academic simulators require a dedicated validation layer before deployment into industrial environments with distinct physics, sensor noise, and failure modes.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the challenges associated with deploying learned robot manipulation policies in real-world industrial settings. While these policies exhibit proficiency in controlled benchmark scenarios, their behavior in edge cases or under adversarial conditions often remains poorly characterized. An undetected failure mode can lead to substantial financial losses through equipment damage, production downtime, and safety incidents.

Specifically, the paper identifies two fundamental limitations of current validation approaches:
1. **Inefficiency of Uniform Random Testing:** This method is computationally expensive as it allocates resources uniformly across the parameter space, including regions that yield consistently trivial success or failure, thus providing limited information about the critical transition zones where policy performance shifts.
2. **Lack of Cross-Embodiment Validation:** Most evaluation frameworks focus on a single robot embodiment, which hinders the ability to differentiate between policy-inherent failures and limitations specific to the robot hardware or gripper type.

In response to these motivations, ROBOGATE sets forth the following key objectives:
- **Develop a Deployment Risk Management Framework:** To create a systematic framework for pre-deployment validation of robot policies in industrial environments.
- **Efficient Failure Boundary Discovery:** To implement a two-stage adaptive sampling strategy that efficiently discovers failure boundaries within high-dimensional operational parameter spaces, moving beyond uniform exploration to focus on critical success-failure transition zones.
- **Enable Cross-Embodiment Validation:** To facilitate parallel evaluation across multiple robot embodiments to identify universal danger zones—parameter regions where multiple platforms exhibit low success rates—and distinguish embodiment-specific limitations.
- **Generate Interpretable Risk Models:** To develop a logistic regression-based risk model that produces a closed-form failure boundary equation, quantifies deployment risk, and provides interpretable parameter thresholds and confidence intervals, thereby aiding in the formulation of operational constraints.
- **Evaluate VLA Model Readiness for Industrial Deployment:** To systematically assess the performance of state-of-the-art Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models in industrial simulation scenarios, specifically to identify and quantify the "cross-simulator gap" that may exist between academic benchmark performance and industrial deployment readiness.
- **Establish a Validation Layer Paradigm:** To empirically demonstrate the necessity for a dedicated validation layer in Physical AI deployment, asserting that academic benchmark results alone are insufficient indicators of a policy's preparedness for real-world or different simulated environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

ROBOGATE operates as a deployment risk management framework, integrating physics-based simulation with an adaptive sampling strategy. The methodology is structured around four main subsystems and a two-stage sampling process.

**Operational Parameter Space and Task Definition:**
The framework defines an 8-dimensional operational parameter space ($P \subset R^d$) for pick-and-place tasks, encompassing physical, geometric, and perceptual parameters: friction coefficient ($\mu$), object mass ($m$), center-of-mass offset ($\delta_c$), object size ($s$), Inverse Kinematics (IK) noise ($\sigma_{ik}$), obstacle count ($n_o$), object geometry ($g$), and placement configuration ($p$). The outcome for a given policy and parameter configuration is binary (success or failure). Failures are classified into four modes: grasp\_miss, grip\_loss, collision, and timeout, with collisions designated as zero-tolerance safety violations. The primary objective is to estimate the failure boundary ($\partial B$), defined as the iso-surface where the expected success rate is 50%, and to develop an interpretable risk model ($\hat{P}(\text{fail}|x)$).

**Deployment Metrics and Thresholds:**
ROBOGATE enforces five key deployment metrics with predefined hard thresholds for a policy to pass validation: Grasp Success Rate ($\ge 0.92$), Cycle Time ($\le 1.1 \times$ baseline), Collision Count ($=0$), Drop Rate ($\le 0.03$), and Grasp Miss Rate ($\le 1.2 \times$ baseline). A composite Confidence Score (0-100) is calculated as a weighted sum of these metric assessments.

**System Architecture:**
1. **Simulation Backend:** Utilizes NVIDIA Isaac Sim 5.1 with the Newton physics engine for high-fidelity, GPU-accelerated manipulation simulation. The Franka Panda (7-DOF, parallel-jaw gripper) and UR5e (6-DOF, suction gripper) robots are employed. Physics computations run at 60 Hz, with control commands issued at 20 Hz. A scripted six-phase pick-and-place controller is used for baseline evaluations.
2. **Scenario Generation:** Scenarios are created by sampling from the defined 8-dimensional parameter space. Domain randomization techniques are applied, including variations in lighting, camera viewpoint, table texture, and background color.
3. **Metric Evaluation and Confidence Scoring Pipeline:** Processes episode outcomes, classifies failure modes, and computes the Confidence Score.
4. **Runtime Monitoring Agent:** Designed for post-deployment drift detection, tracking success rates over moving windows.

**Two-Stage Adaptive Sampling Strategy:**
This is a central methodological contribution designed for efficient allocation of simulation resources to maximize failure boundary resolution.
- **Stage 1: Uniform Exploration:** N$_1$ = 20,000 experiments (10,000 for Franka Panda, 10,000 for UR5e) are conducted. Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS) is used across the continuous dimensions, with log-scaling for friction and mass, to ensure space-filling coverage. This stage provides a coarse initial map of the failure landscape, categorizing regions into safe (SR $\ge 70\%$), boundary (30% $\le$ SR $< 70\%$), and danger (SR $< 30\%$) zones.
- **Stage 2: Boundary-Focused Sampling:** N$_2$ = 10,000 additional experiments (Franka only) are concentrated in identified boundary regions.
 - **Boundary Detection:** For each continuous parameter, its range is binned (10 equal-width bins). Bins with success rates between 30% and 70% define the boundary region for that parameter. The intersection of these per-parameter boundary ranges forms the Stage 2 sampling volume ($P_{boundary}$).
 - **Emphasis Region:** Within $P_{boundary}$, 30% of samples are allocated to a sub-region (defined as $\mu < 0.3$ AND $m \ge 0.5$) where preliminary analysis indicated the steepest failure gradient.
 - **Sampling:** LHS is applied within $P_{boundary}$, with obstacle count constrained to realistic deployment values (1 to 5).
- **Computational Cost:** The total 30,000 experiments (20K Stage 1, 10K Stage 2) were completed in approximately 14 GPU-hours on an NVIDIA RTX 4090.

**Risk Model Development:**
A 10-feature standardized logistic regression model (including interaction terms) is fitted to the combined Franka dataset to predict the probability of failure. This model is used to derive a closed-form failure boundary equation in the friction-mass subspace and estimate critical parameter thresholds with bootstrap confidence intervals.

**VLA Policy Evaluation:**
Seven VLA models (GR00T N1.6, PI0, OpenVLA, SmolVLA, Octo-Base, Octo-Small) were evaluated on a 68-scenario industrial suite in Isaac Sim. A two-process ZMQ pipeline decoupled the simulator from model inference. A scripted Inverse Kinematics (IK) controller served as an upper baseline (100% SR). A key case study involved fine-tuning NVIDIA’s GR00T N1.6 on the LIBERO-Spatial dataset (MuJoCo simulator) and then evaluating the same checkpoint on both LIBERO and ROBOGATE's Isaac Sim scenarios to quantify the cross-simulator gap.

**Cross-Robot Generalization:**
The framework extended its analysis to four distinct robot embodiments: Franka Panda, UR3e, UR5e, and UR10e, each evaluated with 10,000 LHS-sampled experiments using a scripted controller, to investigate the influence of robot reach and gripper type on deployment risk.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research produced several key findings derived from over 40,000 physics-based simulation experiments across multiple robot platforms and VLA models.

**Overall Sampling and Risk Model Performance:**
- The Stage 2 boundary-focused sampling achieved a 63.9% success rate, indicating effective targeting of the success-failure transition zone. This led to a combined Franka dataset (20,000 experiments) with a 48.6% overall success rate, providing a balanced dataset for risk model training.
- The logistic regression risk model, trained on the combined dataset, achieved an Area Under the Curve (AUC) of 0.780, outperforming the model trained on Stage 1 data alone (AUC = 0.754). This demonstrates the value of boundary-focused sampling in improving risk prediction accuracy.

**Cross-Embodiment Comparison and Universal Danger Zones:**
- The UR5e robot with a suction gripper exhibited a substantially higher success rate (74.3%) compared to the Franka Panda with a parallel-jaw gripper (33.3%) under uniform sampling. This difference was attributed to the suction gripper eliminating the `grip_loss` failure mode and the UR5e's evaluation using a reduced parameter space where excluded parameters are strong failure predictors for the parallel-jaw gripper.
- Despite overall performance differences, four **universal danger zones** were identified (object masses between 0.935 kg and 2.114 kg), where both Franka and UR5e exhibited success rates below 40%, indicating parameter regions that challenge multiple robot platforms.

**Parameter-Failure Correlations and Failure Boundary Mapping:**
- Univariate analysis revealed a strong positive correlation between friction and success rate and a weaker negative correlation with mass.
- Multivariate logistic regression identified friction (z=19.28) as the strongest positive predictor for success, and IK noise (z=-17.31) as a significant negative predictor. The interaction term friction $\times$ mass (z=-10.00) was the most significant interaction, indicating a compounded negative effect of low friction and high mass.
- A **closed-form failure boundary equation** in the friction-mass space was derived: $\mu^*(m) = (1.469 + 0.419m)/(3.691 - 1.400m)$. This equation defines the 50% success rate boundary, showing that the critical friction threshold increases with object mass.
- Bootstrap resampling provided **critical parameter thresholds** for a 50% success rate, with friction having the tightest confidence interval (0.492 [0.450, 0.545]) and mass having the widest (0.422 kg [0.097, 0.747]).

**Failure Mode Transitions:**
- The dominant failure mode was observed to change systematically across the parameter space. Low friction conditions predominantly led to `timeout` failures (due to gripper slippage), while higher friction conditions increased `collision` failures (due to firmer grasps increasing contact risks with obstacles). `Grip_loss` was dominant at low object masses.

**VLA Policy Evaluation and the Cross-Simulator Gap:**
- All seven evaluated VLA models (GR00T N1.6, PI0, OpenVLA, SmolVLA, Octo-Base, and Octo-Small) achieved a **0% success rate** on ROBOGATE's 68 Isaac Sim industrial scenarios, in stark contrast to the scripted IK controller's 100% success rate.
- The most significant finding was the **97.65 percentage point cross-simulator gap**: NVIDIA’s GR00T N1.6, fine-tuned on the LIBERO-Spatial dataset (MuJoCo simulator) to achieve 97.65% success, exhibited a 0% success rate on ROBOGATE’s Isaac Sim scenarios using the identical model checkpoint. This empirically demonstrates that academic benchmark performance does not predict industrial deployment readiness.
- Despite 0% success, the **Confidence Score** provided differentiation: GR00T N1.6 (fine-tuned) scored 49/100 due to zero collisions and fast cycle times, while other models scored 1/100 due to collisions. This indicates that some safety-related behaviors (e.g., collision avoidance) can be learned even without task completion.
- VLA failure analysis showed `grasp_miss` as the dominant mode for six models (suggesting perception-to-action misalignment), while SmolVLA showed `collision` dominance (suggesting action-scale mismatch).
- The gap was attributed to systematic differences between simulators in rendering pipelines, physics solvers, and object/scene distributions.

**Cross-Robot Generalization (Four Robots):**
- Evaluation across Franka Panda, UR3e, UR5e, and UR10e revealed a non-monotonic relationship between robot reach and success rate, with UR5e (0.85m reach) achieving the highest SR (74.3%) and UR3e (0.50m reach) the lowest (9.6%). This suggested an optimal workspace-to-reach ratio for dexterity.
- All three UR robots (suction grippers) exclusively exhibited `grasp_miss` failures, confirming the impact of gripper design on failure modes.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

ROBOGATE presents a significant contribution to the field of robotic safety and policy deployment, addressing a critical need for rigorous and efficient validation. Its potential impact spans several areas:

**Enhanced Safety and Reliability in Industrial Robotics:**
The framework offers a systematic pre-deployment validation methodology that can reduce the incidence of unexpected failures in industrial settings. By efficiently discovering failure boundaries and providing an interpretable risk model, ROBOGATE enables engineers to proactively identify and mitigate risks, thereby enhancing the safety and reliability of robot operations, minimizing equipment damage, and preventing production downtime.

**Efficiency in Validation:**
The two-stage adaptive sampling strategy is a key innovation that makes comprehensive validation practical. By focusing computational resources on the critical success-failure transition zones, ROBOGATE optimizes the allocation of simulation budget, allowing for more thorough testing than traditional uniform sampling methods within realistic timeframes (e.g., under 14 hours for 30,000 experiments).

**Actionable Insights and Operational Constraints:**
The use of logistic regression yields an interpretable risk model, including a closed-form failure boundary equation and quantifiable critical parameter thresholds with confidence intervals. This transparency is crucial for industrial operators, as it allows for direct translation of validation results into concrete operational guidelines and physical requirements (e.g., minimum friction coefficients for objects, maximum payload mass, or required joint encoder accuracy). This moves beyond simple pass/fail reporting to provide diagnostic information for policy improvement and environmental design.

**Clarifying the Sim-to-Real and Cross-Simulator Gaps:**
The most impactful finding is the empirical demonstration and quantification of the **cross-simulator gap**, where a highly performant VLA model in one simulator (MuJoCo) completely fails in another (Isaac Sim). This challenges the prevailing assumption that strong academic benchmark performance is a reliable indicator of deployment readiness. It highlights that systematic differences in rendering pipelines, physics solvers, and asset distributions across simulators create significant domain shifts for learned policies. This finding mandates a paradigm shift, advocating for a "validation layer" in Physical AI, similar to calibration layers for quantum computing, to ensure policies are robust to target environment specifics.

**Guidance for VLA Model Development and Evaluation:**
The universal 0% success rate of VLA models on ROBOGATE's industrial scenarios underscores their current limitations for real-world deployment. However, the Confidence Score provides a meaningful safety gradient, differentiating models based on collision avoidance and trajectory efficiency, even in the absence of task success. This offers VLA developers a new metric to track safety improvements and suggests that the generated failure dictionary can serve as targeted adversarial training data to improve robustness for industrial applications.

**Generalizability and Future Work:**
The framework's ability to identify universal danger zones across different robot embodiments (e.g., mass sensitivity) indicates its potential to inform generalized design principles and training curricula. Its open-source nature promotes collaborative research and industry adoption. Future work integrating active learning, extending to multi-task scenarios, and conducting real-world validation campaigns promises to further bridge the sim-to-real gap and enhance the framework's utility.

In conclusion, ROBOGATE provides a robust, efficient, and interpretable framework essential for managing deployment risk in modern robotics. By highlighting the critical cross-simulator gap, it advocates for a more rigorous and structured approach to validating learned policies, ultimately accelerating the safe and effective integration of advanced AI into industrial operations.
