# Open-World Object Manipulation using Pre-trained Vision-Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> An approach called MOO integrates pre-trained vision-language models with robot policies, enabling robots to understand and manipulate object categories not encountered during their first-hand training. The method demonstrates approximately 50% higher task success rates on novel objects across various manipulation skills and enhances robustness in diverse environments.

## Problem

- Robots struggle to understand and act upon human instructions that refer to a vast and continuously evolving vocabulary of objects, many of which are novel to the robot's direct experience.
- Traditional robot learning systems are limited by the scope of their direct interaction data, making it impractical to provide exhaustive first-hand experience for open-vocabulary generalization.
- Effectively integrating the extensive semantic knowledge captured in large-scale pre-trained Vision-Language Models (VLMs) into robust, end-to-end robot control policies remains an active research challenge.

## Method

- The **Manipulation of Open-World Objects (MOO)** framework employs a two-stage process: VLM-based object localization followed by policy-based action prediction.
- A frozen, pre-trained VLM (==OWL-ViT==) is used to generate a ==single-pixel object mask==, representing the target object's location from natural language instructions, which serves as a robust object representation.
- An end-to-end trained robot policy, built on ==RT-1==, conditions its action outputs on this VLM-generated object mask, current image observations, and language embeddings, learning robust manipulation through ==imitation learning==.

## Results

- MOO achieved approximately **50% higher** task success rates on both seen and unseen objects for 'pick' tasks and other manipulation skills compared to baseline methods, demonstrating robust zero-shot generalization.
- The system showed significantly enhanced robustness in visually challenging and novel environments, including new kitchen/office spaces, patterned tablecloths, and scenes with multiple distractor objects.
- Qualitative experiments confirmed MOO's flexibility in handling object specification via non-linguistic inputs, such as masks generated from human pointing, reference images, or a graphical user interface.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Leveraging frozen pre-trained VLMs for coarse object localization allows robots to generalize to entirely novel object categories (zero-shot generalization) by grounding semantic knowledge from large internet datasets.
> - Training an end-to-end policy to interpret a simple single-pixel object mask from the VLM alongside raw visual inputs enables robust manipulation, implicitly learning to track and refine VLM predictions.
> - A generic object representation (e.g., a single-pixel mask) derived from a VLM can serve as a flexible interface, supporting various input modalities like natural language, finger pointing, or reference images for human-robot interaction.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics is progressing towards enabling robots to operate in unstructured environments and interact with humans through natural language. A significant challenge in this domain is equipping robots with the ability to understand and act upon instructions that refer to a vast and continuously evolving vocabulary of objects, many of which the robot may never have encountered directly.

Traditionally, robotic systems have relied on modular pipelines for perception, planning, and control. While such pipelines can allow robots to handle a wide range of objects, their success is often dependent on precise object localization, leading to brittleness in real-world scenarios. More recently, robot learning approaches have emerged, often leveraging pre-trained visual representations or language instruction embeddings. These methods can improve efficiency and generalization within known semantic categories or for compositional variations of seen concepts. However, they typically lack a mechanism to ground and manipulate entirely novel semantic concepts, such as object categories unseen during the robot's first-hand experience.

This research addresses the gap between robust, end-to-end robot learning and the need for open-vocabulary generalization. It leverages the extensive semantic knowledge captured in large-scale pre-trained vision-language models (VLMs), which are trained on vast internet datasets. While VLMs have advanced open-vocabulary object detection in computer vision by replacing fixed category maps with shared image-language embedding spaces, integrating this capability effectively into robot control policies remains an active area of research. Previous efforts, such as CLIPort and PerAct, have incorporated VLMs into robot policies, demonstrating generalization to unseen semantic categories. This work extends these concepts by focusing on broader manipulation settings, reducing reliance on specific sensors like depth cameras, expanding input modalities, and addressing more complex 3D and mobile manipulation tasks. By interfacing policy learning with VLMs, the authors aim to combine the robustness of end-to-end trained policies with the open-world semantic understanding capabilities of large pre-trained models.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable robotic manipulators to understand and execute human instructions that refer to object categories not encountered during the robot's own first-hand training experience. This is motivated by the inherent impracticality of providing robots with exhaustive first-hand experience across the entire spectrum of human language vocabulary. For instance, a robot tasked with retrieving a "pink stuffed whale" should ideally be able to complete this task even if it has never previously interacted with any type of "stuffed whale."

The core motivations for this work are:
1. **Bridging the Semantic Gap:** Human instructions often contain rich semantic information that necessitates a robust connection between natural language (e.g., "yellow banana") and a robot's sensory observations and actions. Current robot learning systems, while effective for learned behaviors, are constrained by the limited scope of their direct interaction data.
2. **Leveraging External Knowledge:** The vast semantic information available in static datasets, such as images and text found on the internet, can be harnessed to extend a robot's understanding beyond its own experiences. Pre-trained vision-language models (VLMs) have demonstrated an ability to capture this knowledge and ground linguistic concepts visually.
3. **Enhancing Generalization to Novel Objects:** The specific objective is to allow robots to perceive and manipulate objects belonging to categories that are entirely new to their first-hand experience, an aspect termed "zero-shot object generalization." This requires a mechanism to translate novel object descriptions into actionable information for the robot's policy.
4. **Improving Robustness over Pipelined Approaches:** While traditional modular pipelines can achieve some level of object-agnostic manipulation, they are often brittle due to their reliance on precise state estimation. The motivation here is to integrate VLM capabilities into an end-to-end trained policy to maintain robustness while gaining semantic generalization. The VLM is intended for coarse localization, with the policy learning to interpret this information robustly alongside raw visual observations.
5. **Exploring Multi-Modal Interaction:** Beyond language instructions, the research aims to investigate whether the proposed approach can generalize to other non-language-based input modalities for specifying objects of interest, such as finger pointing or reference images, thereby enhancing human-robot interaction flexibility.
6. **Extending to Open-World Mobile Manipulation:** A further objective is to integrate the open-world manipulation capabilities with existing open-vocabulary navigation systems to achieve comprehensive mobile manipulation tasks in novel, unconstrained environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed approach, termed Manipulation of Open-World Objects (MOO), enables a robot policy to leverage the visually-grounded semantic information from pre-trained vision-language models (VLMs) for generalization to object types not present in the policy's training set. MOO operates in two primary stages: object localization using a VLM and action prediction by a policy conditioned on this information.

**Problem Formulation:**
The robot interacts with its environment via image observations ($o \in O$) and actions ($a \in A$). It is provided with expert demonstrations ($D_{robot}$) consisting of sequences of observation-action pairs, each annotated with a structured language instruction ($\ell$). These instructions are composed of a template (verb $v$) and object descriptions (e.g., $X$, $Y$). The training objects belong to a set $S_{robot}$. The goal is to execute instructions with seen templates but novel objects not in $S_{robot}$, which also have novel descriptions.

**Two-Stage Approach:**
1. **VLM-based Object Localization:**
 - The current image observation and the object descriptions extracted from the language instruction are input to a frozen, pre-trained VLM.
 - The VLM used is OWL-ViT, an open-vocabulary object detector. It takes a natural language prompt (e.g., "An image of an X") and an image, outputting a score map and associated bounding boxes.
 - To simplify the representation and reduce reliance on exact segmentation, a single pixel at the center of the VLM's predicted bounding box is chosen as the object representation.
 - This object location is encoded into a single-channel object mask: 1.0 at the pixel for the first object, and 0.5 for a second object if two are specified.
 - This VLM inference step is performed only once at the beginning of an episode to ensure real-time control, relying on the policy to track object movement thereafter.

2. **Policy-based Action Prediction:**
 - The generated object mask is concatenated channel-wise with the current image and its recent history.
 - The extracted verb ($v$) from the language instruction is encoded into an embedding using a language model.
 - These combined inputs (image history + object mask, and language embedding) are fed into the robot's policy.
 - The policy architecture is based on RT-1, a robotics transformer model. It uses an EfficientNet backbone conditioned on the text embedding via FiLM layers, followed by a Token Learner to produce a small set of tokens, and a Transformer to attend over these tokens.
 - The policy outputs discretized action tokens representing a 7-DoF delta end-effector pose (x, y, z, roll, pitch, yaw) and gripper opening.
 - The entire policy network is trained end-to-end using an imitation learning objective (behavioral cloning), minimizing the negative log-likelihood of expert actions. Crucially, the VLM is kept frozen during this training, forcing the policy to learn robustness to potential VLM errors and to ground the object masks within the broader visual context.

**Training Data:**
The training dataset is an extension of the RT-1 dataset, initially containing 16 unique objects. To enhance physical object diversity and generalization, an additional 90 diverse objects were added specifically for "pick" task demonstrations, bringing the total to 106 training objects. While these 90 objects only appear in "pick" episodes, other skills like "move near," "knock," "place upright," and "place into" are learned solely from the original 16 objects. This setup allows for evaluating the policy's ability to generalize physical manipulation skills to novel objects, even for tasks for which they were not explicitly trained on diverse objects.

**Practical Implementation:**
Prompt engineering is employed for the OWL-ViT detector, utilizing descriptive phrases including object color, size, and shape to obtain accurate detections. The single-pixel mask representation is designed to be robust to different object sizes and compatible with various VLM output formats.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper presents a comprehensive evaluation of MOO across 1,472 real-world robot evaluations, demonstrating its capabilities in open-world object manipulation.

1. **Generalization to Novel Objects:**
 - MOO demonstrated substantial improvement in task success compared to baseline methods (RT-1 and a VIMA-like approach) for "pick" tasks, achieving approximately 50% higher success rates on both seen and unseen objects. This indicates that MOO effectively leverages the VLM to identify and interact with novel objects.
 - For "non-pick skills" (e.g., "move near," "knock," "place upright"), MOO also exhibited generalization to unseen objects at a similar success level as for "pick" tasks, and approximately 50% better than baselines. This suggests that MOO can transfer its learned object generalization across different manipulation skills, even for objects only seen in "pick" demonstrations.
 - The definition of "unseen objects" was strict: objects not present in the robot's demonstration data for any task, thus representing a zero-shot object generalization problem.

2. **Robustness Beyond New Objects:**
 - Experiments conducted in visually challenging scenarios, including new environments (kitchen and office spaces), patterned tablecloth backgrounds, and scenes with additional open-world distractor objects, showed that MOO was significantly more robust than baseline methods.
 - This finding indicates that MOO's use of VLMs not only enables generalization to novel object categories but also substantially improves its ability to operate reliably in diverse and novel environmental contexts.

3. **Support for Non-Linguistic Input Modalities:**
 - Qualitative experiments showed MOO's flexibility in handling object-centric mask representations generated from various upstream input modalities, even in cluttered scenes with duplicate objects.
 - MOO successfully interpreted masks generated from a VLM inferring the object a human was pointing at (by first generating a text caption), from an image query of a target object (using visual features instead of text), and from direct human input via a graphical user interface (GUI). This demonstrates the utility of the single-pixel mask representation as a generic interface for human-robot intent communication.

4. **MOO Ablations (Dataset and Model Scale):**
 - **Dataset Diversity:** Ablation studies on training data revealed that generalization to unseen objects is highly sensitive to the diversity of objects in the training set. Reducing unique training objects from 106 to 16 significantly decreased unseen object performance (from 79% to 19%), while seen object performance was less affected. This highlights the importance of broad physical diversity for robust open-world generalization.
 - **Model Scale:** Performance on both seen and unseen objects correlated positively with model size. Scaling down the model from 111M parameters to 10.2M and then to 2.37M resulted in significant drops in success rates (e.g., unseen object performance decreased from 79% to 50% and then to 13%). The authors noted potential for further performance gains with larger models if real-time latency constraints could be relaxed.

5. **Open-World Navigation and Manipulation (CoW-MOO):**
 - The MOO system was successfully integrated with an open-vocabulary object navigation algorithm called Clip-on-Wheels (CoW), forming a combined system referred to as CoW-MOO.
 - CoW-MOO demonstrated the ability to first navigate to an object of interest, even if previously unencountered, and then manipulate it successfully. This integration enables truly open-world task execution, from finding an object to manipulating it.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents Manipulation of Open-World Objects (MOO), an approach that significantly advances robotic manipulation capabilities by effectively integrating pre-trained vision-language models (VLMs) with robot learning policies. The findings indicate a notable step towards robots that can adapt to the semantic richness and variability of human instructions and real-world environments.

The key significance of MOO lies in its ability to enable **zero-shot generalization to novel object categories**. By using VLMs for coarse object localization and conditioning an end-to-end trained policy on this information, robots can successfully manipulate objects they have never encountered in their first-hand training data. This addresses a fundamental limitation in robot learning, where scaling first-hand experience to cover the vastness of human vocabulary is impractical.

Furthermore, MOO demonstrates **enhanced robustness to diverse environments and visual variations**, including novel backgrounds, textures, and distractor objects. This suggests that the VLM-integrated approach not only helps with object identification but also contributes to more resilient perception and control in unstructured settings, moving beyond the brittleness often associated with traditional, highly-calibrated robotic pipelines.

The research also highlights the **flexibility of MOO to integrate various input modalities**. The ability to use finger pointing, reference images, or direct human GUI input to specify target objects, in addition to natural language, broadens the scope of human-robot interaction. This multi-modal capability has the potential to make robotic systems more intuitive and accessible for human users.

The successful integration with an open-vocabulary navigation system (CoW-MOO) underscores the potential for **truly open-world mobile manipulation**. This paves the way for robots that can operate autonomously in complex, unknown environments, identifying and interacting with objects based on high-level human commands, a crucial step towards more general-purpose intelligent robots.

While the approach has limitations, such as potential challenges with object occlusion, reliance on sufficient motion diversity in training data, templated instructions, and handling complex spatial relations, it lays a foundation for future advancements. The findings suggest that as VLMs continue to evolve, their integration with robot control policies will further improve generalization and capabilities, pushing the boundaries of what robots can achieve in real-world scenarios. This work contributes to the development of more adaptable, intelligent, and human-friendly robotic systems.
