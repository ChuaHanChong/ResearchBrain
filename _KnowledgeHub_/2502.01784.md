# VILP: Imitation Learning with Latent Video Planning

> [!summary] Summary
> VILP, a framework from Purdue University, integrates latent video diffusion models into robotic policies to enable real-time receding horizon planning, achieving inference speeds of up to 17 Hz. It robustly executes imitation learning tasks by leveraging video data for planning and mapping generated videos to actions, demonstrating superior performance against baselines in simulation and real-world environments with reduced reliance on extensive action-labeled data.

## Problem

- Existing video generation models for robotics lacked real-time performance, preventing effective receding horizon planning and leading to error accumulation in open-loop systems.
- Bridging the gap between generated video frames and precise robot actions remained challenging, especially when action-labeled data was limited or varied.
- General video generation policies struggled to adapt to unique robotic data characteristics, such as multi-modal action distributions and inputs from multiple camera views.

## Method

- VILP compresses raw image data into a lower-dimensional latent space using a VQGAN autoencoder to manage computational complexity.
- A latent video diffusion model, built on a 3D UNet and conditioned on current observations, synthesizes future video frames in this compressed space.
- A low-level policy maps these predicted future frames to robot actions, employing a receding horizon control strategy to enhance responsiveness and mitigate prediction errors.

## Results

- VILP achieved real-time inference speeds for video planning (e.g., 0.058 seconds for some tasks, approx. 17 Hz), significantly outperforming UniPi in both speed and video quality (better FID/FVD scores).
- In simulation, VILP demonstrated consistently higher success rates across tasks (e.g., 84.0% for VILP-8 on Arrange-Blocks-Hybrid vs. 22.7% for Diffusion Policy), particularly with varied action-labeled datasets.
- In a real-world 'Arrange-Blocks' task, VILP-16 successfully arranged blocks (7/15 'Two Blocks Arranged') while UniPi-4 and UniPi-16 failed, highlighting VILP's ability for real-time closed-loop control in complex environments.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Generating videos in a compressed latent space significantly accelerates the planning process, making real-time receding horizon control feasible for robot policies.
> - Decoupling video generation from action mapping allows for robust policy performance by utilizing abundant video data for planning, even when task-specific action-labeled data is scarce or varied.
> - The use of latent diffusion models combined with cross-attention conditioning effectively captures multi-modal action distributions and ensures temporal consistency across multi-view generated videos.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in this paper is situated within the intersection of generative AI, particularly video generation models, and robotics, specifically in the domain of imitation learning. The advent of sophisticated video synthesis techniques has enabled models to simulate aspects of the real world and predict future interactions, concepts that align with "model" and "planning" in robotics. This alignment has motivated increasing research into applying video generation to robotic tasks.

Traditional robot learning approaches often rely on extensive, high-quality robot action and state data, which can be resource-intensive to collect. In contrast, video data is generally more readily available and scalable. This disparity has led researchers to explore methods where video generation models can act as "planners" to produce videos of robots performing tasks, which are then translated into robot actions. Previous research, such as, has explored this paradigm by learning mappings from video frames to actions or utilizing optical flow.

However, the existing landscape of video planning in robotics has faced several challenges that limit its practical applicability. These include:
1. **Real-time performance:** Video generation traditionally requires significant computational time, often precluding real-time receding horizon planning, where short-term plans are continuously updated based on new observations. Prior works have typically resorted to open-loop planning or long replanning intervals, which can lead to error accumulation and task failure.
2. **Bridging the video-to-action gap:** While video generation models can be trained on task-specific video data to capture relevant information, effectively converting these generated videos into precise robot actions, especially with limited or varied action-labeled data, remains an open problem.
3. **Uniqueness of robotic data:** Robotic data often exhibits characteristics such as multi-modal action distributions and inputs from multiple camera views, which differ from general single-view video generation tasks. Designing video generation policies that can effectively adapt to these unique characteristics is crucial.

This paper positions VILP as an advancement that addresses these identified gaps. It aims to demonstrate an effective integration of video generation models into robot policies that allows for real-time receding horizon planning, robust performance with less reliance on extensive action-labeled data, and adaptation to the multi-modal nature of robotic data and multi-view inputs. It builds upon the progress in diffusion models and their application in robotics, such as Diffusion Policy, by extending these to latent video planning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and evaluate VILP (Imitation Learning with Latent Video Planning), a framework that integrates video generation models into robotic policies. The motivation stems from the observed limitations in current video generation applications for robotics and the desire to leverage the advancements in generative AI for more general-purpose robot agents.

Specifically, the paper aims to achieve the following detailed objectives, driven by the motivations outlined in the introduction:

1. **Enable Real-time Receding Horizon Planning:** The motivation for this objective arises from the computational intensity of traditional video generation models, which typically prevent real-time performance in robotic applications. Existing methods often employ open-loop planning or long intervals between replanning steps, leading to error accumulation and potential task failures over time. VILP seeks to accelerate video planning speed to facilitate real-time receding horizon control, where the robot continuously plans and executes short segments of actions, adapting to new observations.
2. **Optimize the Bridging of Video and Action Generation:** Current video generation models, while capable of synthesizing task-relevant videos, face a challenge in efficiently translating these videos into robot actions, especially when action-labeled data is scarce or differs from the video data used for training. The motivation is to devise a method that can effectively utilize task-relevant information inherent in videos, even when trained on task-specific videos, to generate robust actions with reduced reliance on extensive, high-quality action-labeled datasets. This involves optimizing the low-level policy that maps generated video frames to robot actions.
3. **Adapt to the Uniqueness of Robotic Data:** Robotic tasks often involve complex characteristics like multi-modal action distributions (e.g., multiple ways to achieve a goal) and data from multiple camera views (RGB and depth). General video generation models are not always designed to handle such specific data structures and complexities. The motivation is to design a video generation policy that effectively adapts to these unique attributes, ensuring that generated multi-view videos are temporally consistent and that the policy can represent diverse action possibilities.
4. **Demonstrate Practical Integration of Video Generation Models:** Beyond addressing specific technical challenges, a broader objective is to provide a practical example of how to effectively integrate advanced video generation models into robot policies. This includes showcasing the framework's ability to represent multi-modal action distributions and operate effectively with less demand for large, high-quality action-specific datasets.

These objectives collectively aim to enhance the utility of video generation in robotics, moving towards more efficient, adaptive, and scalable robot learning paradigms.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

VILP (Imitation Learning with Latent Video Planning) is designed to integrate video generation models into robot policies. The methodology consists of several interconnected components, as illustrated in Fig. 1, 2, and 3.

**A. Dataset Format**
The input data for VILP comprises video datasets $D_v = \{o_i^0, o_i^1, \dots, o_i^{T_i^d}\}_{i=1}^E$, where $T_d$ is the video length, $E$ is the number of episodes, and $o$ represents a combination of images from multiple views or RGB-D cameras. Each observation $o = \{f_j\}_{j=1}^M$ consists of $M$ image frames $f$, where $f$ can be an RGB or depth image.

**B. Image Compression to Latent Space**
To manage computational complexity and enable efficient processing, VILP first compresses raw image data into a lower-dimensional latent space. This is achieved using a VQGAN autoencoder, which is trained with a combination of perceptual loss and a patch-based adversarial objective. This training objective aims to preserve local realism, reduce blurriness, and prevent high-variance latent spaces.
- **Encoder (E):** Given an image $f \in \mathbb{R}^{\tilde{H} \times \tilde{W} \times \tilde{C}}$, the encoder $E$ maps it to a latent representation $z = E(f) \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times C}$.
- **Decoder (D):** The decoder $D$ reconstructs the image from its latent representation.
Once trained, the VQGAN autoencoder is fixed and not adjusted during the subsequent training of the diffusion model.

**C. Latent Video Diffusion Model**
The core of VILP's planning capability is a latent video diffusion model, which synthesizes videos in the compressed latent space.
- **Diffusion Process:** The model operates based on denoising diffusion implicit models (DDIM). It learns a data distribution by gradually denoising a normally distributed variable. Starting from random noise, it iteratively performs denoising steps to generate a sequence of progressively less noisy latent representations, culminating in a desired noise-free output.
- **Video Representation:** Instead of diffusing individual images, VILP samples a sequence of frames $f_t = [f_{t+\Delta t}, \dots, f_{t+N\Delta t}]$ and compresses them into a latent variable $z_t = [z_{t+\Delta t}, \dots, z_{t+N\Delta t}] \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times H \times W \times C}$. The diffusion model then generates $z_t$, and the decoder $D$ reconstructs the video frames.
- **Objective Function:** The model is trained to minimize the expected difference between the true noise $\epsilon_k$ and the noise predicted by the model $\epsilon_\theta(z_t^k, k)$, conditioned on the current observation $o_t$. This can be formalized as $L(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(o_t, z_t^0), \epsilon_k, k} [\|\epsilon_k - \epsilon_\theta(o_t, z_t^k, k)\|^2]$.
- **Architecture:** The model employs a UNet architecture, with 3D convolution layers to capture both temporal and spatial information within the video data.

**D. Observation Conditioning and Multiview Generation**
To function as a video planner, the latent video diffusion model is conditioned on the current observation $o_t$, allowing it to approximate the conditional distribution $p(z_t | o_t)$. This conditioning mechanism comprises three components:
1. **Visual Encoders:** Images within the observation $o_t$ are mapped to low-dimensional vectors using modified ResNet-18 encoders (without pretraining), similar to. Distinct encoders are used for different camera perspectives. For depth images, they are repeated three times to create a three-channel input for the encoder. The outputs from all encoders are concatenated into a low-dimensional vector $c_t \in \mathbb{R}^{D_c}$.
2. **Cross-Attention Conditioning:** The vector $c_t$ is embedded into the intermediate layers of the 3D UNet using a cross-attention mechanism, adapted from. This involves query ($Q$) tensors derived from the UNet's intermediate representations of $z_t^k$, and key ($K$) and value ($V$) tensors derived from $c_t$, using learnable transformations $W_Q, W_K, W_V$. This global conditioning mechanism provides control over the generation process.
3. **Multiview Generation:** For tasks requiring multiple camera views, VILP trains a separate diffusion model for each view's image to enable the generation of multiview videos. The conditioning vector $c_t$ integrates embeddings from all multiview observations (Fig. 2b), ensuring that generated videos from different views are temporally aligned.

**E. Imitation Learning with Video Planning (Low-level Policy)**
Once future video frames are planned, a low-level policy maps these frames to robot actions.
- **Action Mapping Module:** An action mapping module, $\pi$, takes two adjacent generated frames (e.g., $\hat{o}_{t+n\Delta t}$ and $\hat{o}_{t+(n+1)\Delta t}$) and predicts an action sequence $\hat{a}_{t+n\Delta t} = [a_{t+n\Delta t}, \dots, a_{t+(n+1)\Delta t-1}]$. This module consists of two CNN encoders and one MLP head. The same module is used to map all predicted future frames to a continuous action sequence.
- **Receding Horizon Control:** Instead of executing the entire generated action sequence, a receding horizon control strategy is employed. Only the first $N_e$ steps of the predicted action sequence are executed. This approach aims to enhance policy responsiveness and mitigate the accumulation of errors in video and action predictions over time.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates VILP across two primary aspects: video planning performance and policy rollout performance in imitation learning tasks, comparing it against existing methods like UniPi and Diffusion Policy.

#### 4.1. Video Planning Performance

**A. Video Planning**
VILP's video planning capabilities were evaluated on three tasks: Move-the-Stack, Push-T, and Towers-of-Hanoi, comparing it with UniPi using FID (Fréchet Inception Distance) and FVD (Fréchet Video Distance) metrics, and inference time (Table I).
- **Quantitative Performance:** VILP achieved better FID and FVD scores for Push-T and Towers-of-Hanoi tasks. For Move-the-Stack, UniPi-64 showed slightly better FID/FVD but with significantly higher inference time (2.5 seconds vs. VILP-4's 0.058 seconds). VILP demonstrated comparable quality with much faster inference. VILP also generated high-quality videos with fewer inference steps (e.g., 4 steps) compared to UniPi, whose quality significantly decreased under similar conditions.
- **Time Efficiency and Training Costs:** VILP exhibited superior time efficiency, achieving inference speeds as low as 0.058 seconds for some tasks (approx. 17 Hz). This capability enables real-time receding horizon planning. Furthermore, VILP demonstrated lower GPU memory occupation during training compared to UniPi (e.g., 8.9 GB for VILP vs. 35.7 GB for UniPi on Move-the-Stack, Table II).
- **Qualitative Observations:** VILP produced video plans with stronger temporal consistency and a more stable representation of robot and object dynamics compared to UniPi (Fig. 5 and supplementary video). This is attributed to VILP's use of diffusion in latent space, which reduces computational complexity and allows for better data distribution capture, and its global conditioning mechanism that integrates condition images into the U-Net more effectively than UniPi's conditional concatenation.

#### 4.2. Simulation Task Performance

**B. Imitation Learning with Video Planning (Policy Rollout)**
VILP was evaluated as a policy in simulation tasks (Nut-Assembly, Can-PickPlace, Sim Push-T, Arrange-Blocks) and a real-world task (Real-Arrange-Blocks), comparing it with UniPi, Diffusion Policy, LSTM-GMM, and IBC.

- **Performance Comparison (VILP vs. UniPi):** Across Nut-Assembly and Arrange-Blocks tasks (Table III, IV), VILP consistently outperformed UniPi in success rates, inference speed, and training costs. VILP's faster inference speeds (e.g., 0.073 seconds per inference for Arrange-Blocks-Hybrid) were crucial for real-time policy execution. The performance advantage is attributed to VILP's latent space prediction, which significantly reduces computational load (e.g., 96x160 pixels to 12x20 latent space).
- **Performance Comparison (VILP vs. Diffusion Policy):** VILP demonstrated robust performance, particularly in "Hybrid" datasets with varied action-labeled data (e.g., Nut-Assembly-Hybrid: 56.7% max success rate for VILP-4 vs. 48.0% for Diffusion Policy; Arrange-Blocks-Hybrid: 84.0% for VILP-8 vs. 22.7% for Diffusion Policy). This indicates VILP's effectiveness when extensive video data is available, but action-labeled data is scarce or varied, suggesting that video and action model training can be separated.
- **Robustness to Artifacts:** The paper noted that despite minor artifacts in generated videos, VILP's policy performance was not adversely affected, implying that the generated videos still effectively simulated robot-object interactions.
- **Multi-modal Action Distribution:** On Sim Push-T, which has a strong multi-modal action distribution, VILP (88.0% success with low-dimensional observations) showed superior performance compared to Diffusion Policy (84% with CNN backbone) and other methods (Table V), indicating its strong capability in representing such distributions, stemming from the generative nature of the underlying diffusion model.
- **Ablation Studies:** Ablation studies (Table VI) confirmed the effectiveness of VILP's conditioning mechanism and its multi-view observation handling, showing performance drops when using conditional concatenation or omitting multi-view fusion. The horizon ablation (Fig. 7) provided guidance on selecting video and action horizons, indicating that both excessively small and large video horizons can negatively impact performance, and larger action horizons tend to enhance it.

#### 4.3. Real-World Task Performance

- On the Real-Arrange-Blocks task, requiring high-level planning, multi-modal action capturing, and precise closed-loop control, VILP-16 achieved a "Two Blocks Arranged" success rate of 7/15 and "One Block Arranged" of 6/15, with an inference time of 0.238 seconds (Table VII). In contrast, UniPi-4 and UniPi-16 failed to arrange any blocks, highlighting VILP's ability to handle complex real-world tasks and enable real-time closed-loop control. UniPi's slower inference led to disjointed action sequences, hindering performance on tasks requiring fine control.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The VILP framework introduces several significant contributions and has potential impacts on the field of robotic learning:

1. **Enabling Real-time Receding Horizon Planning:** VILP is presented as the first method capable of real-time video generation for robotic policies, achieving high inference frequencies (e.g., 14 Hz). This capability addresses a critical limitation of previous video planning approaches and facilitates responsive, closed-loop control in dynamic environments. This advancement has the potential to make video planning a more viable and practical approach for real-world robot deployment.
2. **Reduced Reliance on Extensive Action-Labeled Data:** The work demonstrates that VILP can achieve robust performance with less dependence on extensive, high-quality task-specific robot action data. By leveraging large amounts of video data for planning and then bridging this with smaller, potentially off-target, action-labeled datasets, VILP offers a pathway to more data-efficient imitation learning. This could alleviate a significant bottleneck in robot learning, where collecting diverse action-labeled demonstrations is often costly and time-consuming.
3. **Robust Multi-modal Action Representation:** VILP exhibits strong capabilities in representing multi-modal action distributions, as evidenced by its performance on tasks like Sim Push-T. This feature is crucial for complex robotic tasks where multiple successful strategies or action sequences exist, allowing the robot to adapt to various scenarios and recover from errors.
4. **Effective Integration of Generative Models:** The paper provides a practical blueprint for effectively integrating advanced video generation models, particularly latent diffusion models, into robotic policies. This detailed methodology, including the use of latent space compression, cross-attention conditioning, and multi-view generation, offers insights for developing more general-purpose robot agents.
5. **Future Research Directions:** VILP lays the groundwork for several promising future research avenues:
 - **Unified Robot Video Generation Models:** The concept of view-invariant or task-invariant image representations could lead to unified video generation models capable of handling diverse camera views and tasks, transcending single-task and single-dataset limitations towards more generalizable and adaptable policies.
 - **Decoupling Video Generation and Action Mapping:** The finding that video generation models can provide rich task information with less reliance on action-labeled data suggests exploring more generalized methods for video-to-action translation. This could enable large-scale video generation models (e.g., models trained on web-scale video data) to perform reasoning and planning, with a simpler, universal model handling the final action execution.
 - **Cross-Domain Multi-modalities:** Investigating VILP's ability to capture multi-modal action distributions from cross-domain videos, particularly in longer-horizon tasks, could further extend its applicability and robustness.

Overall, VILP represents a step in making video-based planning a more practical and performant approach for imitation learning in robotics, with implications for data efficiency, real-time control, and the integration of advanced generative AI capabilities into autonomous systems.
