# TurnOPD: Making On-Policy Distillation Turn-Aware for Efficient Long-Horizon Agent Training

> [!summary] Summary
> TurnOPD introduces a turn-aware strategy for on-policy distillation in long-horizon language agent training, dynamically adjusting rollout depth and loss allocation. This method effectively reduces training time by up to 2.29x while simultaneously improving task performance across various benchmarks.

## Problem

- Vanilla on-policy distillation (OPD) for long-horizon agent tasks suffers from inefficient rollout data collection, often gathering low-value supervision from deep turns.
- Standard trajectory-level KL objectives lead to imbalanced loss allocation, concentrating optimization on shallow turns and under-training critical deeper decisions.
- The "contamination-compression mechanism" describes how self-generated context in deep turns can degrade the effective KL divergence signal, making it less reliable for distinguishing good policies.

## Method

- **TurnOPD**'s ==Adaptive Rollout-Depth Budgeting== dynamically adjusts the length of student rollouts by estimating an "optimal" horizon balancing efficiency and coverage, using a centroid of effective distillation signal and a success-conditioned completion depth quantile.
- ==Progressive Turn-Normalized Loss Budgeting== uses a linear blending scheme to transition from token-count-based loss weighting to uniform turn-wise weighting as training progresses, ensuring balanced supervision across all decision turns.
- These two controllers work synergistically to optimize both data collection and gradient allocation during ==on-policy distillation==.

## Results

- TurnOPD consistently advanced the accuracy-time frontier across ALFWorld, WebShop, and Multi-Hop Search benchmarks, achieving higher accuracy for equivalent training budgets.
- It achieved up to a **2.29x** speedup in wall-clock training time (e.g., **4.42 hours** to **1.93 hours** for ALFWorld-1.7B) while simultaneously improving average task accuracy (e.g., **83.0** to **86.3** Avg@4 for ALFWorld-1.7B).
- Ablation studies confirmed the synergistic effect of its two components: adaptive rollout-depth budgeting significantly improved efficiency, while progressive turn-normalized loss budgeting improved accuracy by rebalancing optimization across turns.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The quality and informational value of the reverse-KL supervision signal vary significantly with turn depth, with tail turns often providing weak or noisy signals that degrade effective supervision.
> - Standard loss aggregation disproportionately allocates optimization budget to early interaction turns, leaving critical deeper decisions under-trained despite their importance for task completion.
> - A "contamination-compression mechanism" explains that increasing self-generated context in deeper turns can force or surface-determine parts of the next-token distribution, compressing and potentially corrupting the observable KL divergence.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is situated within the field of language model agents, specifically focusing on the training efficiency and effectiveness of such agents for complex, long-horizon tasks. Language models are increasingly employed as agents capable of planning, utilizing tools, and interacting with environments. On-policy distillation (OPD) has emerged as a framework for training these agentic models. OPD involves a student model sampling its own trajectories and receiving supervision from a stronger teacher model through a reverse-KL divergence objective at the states visited by the student. This approach provides dense, on-policy feedback, circumventing the challenges associated with sparse reward signals often encountered in reinforcement learning.

However, the application of standard OPD to long-horizon agent tasks presents specific challenges not typically found in simpler sequence-generation problems. Agent tasks often involve multiple interaction turns, tool calls, dynamic environmental shifts, and state changes, where early decisions critically influence subsequent states. Existing OPD methodologies, such as MiniLLM and GKD, have primarily focused on general large language model (LLM) distillation, treating each example as a single response sequence. More recent work has begun to acknowledge the temporal nature of agent interaction; for instance, TCOD introduced a curriculum over trajectory length, and SOD reweighted distillation at the step level for tool-assisted reasoning.

This paper extends the discussion beyond flat sequence objectives by providing a systematic, turn-level analysis of how supervision signals and optimization budgets are distributed and allocated across decision turns within an interaction trajectory. It aims to complement previous research by addressing specific inefficiencies in rollout data collection and gradient allocation that arise from the multi-turn, stateful nature of long-horizon agent tasks. By doing so, it contributes to the development of more tailored and efficient training paradigms for language agents in increasingly realistic and complex environments, such as those involving embodied planning, web navigation, and multi-hop search.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the efficiency and performance of on-policy distillation (OPD) for training long-horizon language agents. The authors identify two key inefficiencies inherent in vanilla agent OPD when applied to multi-turn, interactive tasks:

1. **External Mismatch: Inefficient Rollout Collection**: Vanilla OPD typically uses a fixed, maximum-horizon rollout length for data collection. The authors argue that this approach often leads to wasted computational resources. Their diagnosis indicates that the quality and informational value of the reverse-KL supervision signal vary significantly with turn depth. Specifically, tail turns of a long rollout often provide weak and noisy KL supervision, or the effective "correction signal" diminishes as the student's context becomes more constrained or stereotyped. Collecting data from these low-value tail turns expends compute without yielding proportional gains in supervision quality, thus decreasing overall training efficiency.

2. **Internal Mismatch: Imbalanced Loss Allocation**: Standard trajectory-level KL objectives, which typically average token-level KL losses uniformly across an entire trajectory, tend to concentrate most of the optimization budget on shallow tokens (i.e., tokens in early decision turns). This occurs because early turns often have higher initial KL divergence and contribute more tokens to the overall loss. Consequently, as training progresses and initial behaviors align, deeper and potentially more critical decision turns receive a disproportionately small share of the total loss, leading to them being under-trained or poorly refined. While naive turn-level normalization could address this, it risks over-weighting deep turns that may have sparse data or unreliable signals.

To provide a theoretical foundation for these observed phenomena, the authors formalize a "contamination-compression mechanism." This mechanism suggests that as a student rollout progresses, increasing self-generated context can lead to a larger portion of the next-token distribution being "forced" or surface-determined (e.g., by repeated strings, formatting constraints). This "forced mass" (λ(c)) compresses the observable KL divergence, potentially masking genuine policy disagreements in the "free component." This effect can lead to the observed degradation and even inversion of the KL signal's ability to distinguish successful from failed trajectories at deeper turns. Failed trajectories, in particular, may quickly fall into stereotyped patterns, increasing the forced mass and artificially reducing measured KL divergence, even when significant policy gaps remain.

Motivated by these identified inefficiencies and the contamination-compression mechanism, the research aims to develop a turn-level budgeting strategy to optimize both the collection of interaction data and the allocation of the supervision signal. The overarching objective is to advance the accuracy–time frontier for long-horizon agent training, enabling student models to achieve higher task performance with reduced computational resources and training time.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

TurnOPD addresses the identified inefficiencies through a two-pronged turn-level budgeting strategy: adaptive rollout-depth budgeting and progressive turn-normalized loss budgeting. These two controllers work synergistically to optimize resource allocation during on-policy distillation.

#### 3.1. Adaptive Rollout-Depth Budgeting

This controller is designed to mitigate the "external mismatch" by dynamically adjusting the length of student rollouts, aiming to collect supervision from turns that provide the most valuable signal while avoiding unproductive deep turns. The core idea is to estimate an "optimal" rollout horizon ($H^\star$), which balances efficiency and coverage. The controller determines the rollout depth ($H_{ctrl}$) at each training step $k$ using the following formula:
$H_{ctrl} = \max(H_{eff}, H_{cov})$

1. **Efficiency-Side Mass ($H_{eff}$)**: This component estimates the centroid of the effective distillation signal. For each turn $t$, a distillation mass ($m_t$) is calculated, which is the product of the positive part of the mean per-token reverse-KL ($[K_t]_+$) and the on-policy survivor probability ($n_t/n_0$). The survivor probability accounts for how many trajectories actually reach turn $t$. These masses are then normalized to form a distribution ($q_t$), and $H_{eff}$ is computed as the rounded mean of turn indices weighted by $q_t$. This ensures that the estimated optimal depth adapts to where the "meaningful" supervision is concentrated at any given training stage.

2. **Coverage-Side Lower Bound ($H_{cov}$)**: To prevent overly aggressive truncation that might miss critical late-stage decisions, $H_{cov}$ sets a minimum rollout depth. It is defined as the $p$-quantile (typically $p=0.80$) of the success-conditioned completion depth. This means the rollout must be deep enough to encompass at least 80% of successful trajectories observed so far. This component ensures that the student agent is exposed to a sufficient range of task-relevant interactions, including those leading to successful task completion.

**Dynamic Update**: The controller relies on periodic full-length *probe rollouts* to gather uncensored turn statistics for $K_t$, $n_t$, and $L_{succ}$. These probes occur at a defined interval and during a warm-up phase. This prevents bias that would arise from estimating statistics using only capped trajectories. The selected rollout cap for routine training steps ($\hat{H}_{k+1}$) is an exponentially moving average (EMA) of $H_{ctrl,k}$, clipped between minimum and maximum allowed depths ($H_{min}$, $H_{max}$). Truncated rollouts still contribute to OPD updates but are excluded from depth estimation to maintain accuracy.

#### 3.2. Progressive Turn-Normalized Loss Budgeting

This controller addresses the "internal mismatch" by dynamically adjusting how the collected KL loss is weighted across turns, countering the tendency for shallow turns to dominate the optimization budget. It uses a linear blending scheme:
$q_{blend,t} = (1 - \alpha) q_{traj,t} + \alpha q_{turn,t}$

1. **Trajectory-Level Normalization ($q_{traj,t}$)**: This is the standard approach, where the weight for turn $t$ is proportional to the number of supervised tokens ($n_t$) within that turn relative to the total number of tokens in the trajectory ($\sum n_j$). This method is suitable for early training phases when basic interactions and initial errors are more prevalent.

2. **Uniform Turn-Wise Normalization ($q_{turn,t}$)**: This approach assigns an equal weight ($1/T$) to each observed turn $t$, where $T$ is the total number of turns in the collected trajectory. This method aims to balance supervision across all decision points, including deeper turns that might have fewer tokens but contain critical decisions.

**Blend Coefficient ($\alpha$)**: The blending coefficient $\alpha$ gradually increases from 0 to 1 over the course of training, typically tied to normalized training progress. In early training ($\alpha=0$), the loss allocation prioritizes trajectory-level (token-count based) weighting, ensuring stability. As training progresses and $\alpha$ increases, the loss aggregation smoothly transitions towards a more turn-balanced approach, reallocating supervision to empower the refinement of deeper, more challenging decisions.

#### 3.3. Unification and Algorithm

TurnOPD integrates both controllers. Algorithm 1 (in the appendix) outlines the full training process: at each step, an effective rollout horizon is determined, trajectories are collected up to this horizon, and then the KL loss is computed using the adaptively blended turn-level normalization. This ensures that resources are efficiently allocated both during data collection and gradient computation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of TurnOPD was evaluated across three multi-turn agent benchmarks: ALFWorld (embodied planning), WebShop (web navigation), and Multi-Hop Search (diverse QA tasks). Experiments utilized student-teacher pairs involving Qwen3 and Qwen3.5 models of various sizes, with teachers trained using GRPO. The evaluation compared TurnOPD against vanilla OPD and TCOD-F2B, focusing on accuracy-time tradeoffs.

#### 4.1. Overall Performance

- **Accuracy-Time Frontier Advancement**: TurnOPD consistently demonstrated superior performance on the accuracy-time frontier across all tasks and model combinations (Table 2, Figure 5). It achieved higher validation accuracy for an equivalent wall-clock training budget compared to baselines.
- **Least-Time Accuracy**: Under the Least-Time protocol (accuracy at the minimal wall-clock time for any method to reach 100 steps), TurnOPD achieved the highest overall Avg@4 accuracy among student-training methods across all benchmarks. For example, on ALFWorld-1.7B, TurnOPD achieved 85.60 Avg@4 compared to 73.52 for vanilla OPD and 80.06 for TCOD-F2B.
- **Same-Step Accuracy**: In the Same-Step protocol (accuracy after exactly 100 training steps), TurnOPD generally matched or surpassed the best baselines. On ALFWorld-1.7B, it reached 86.29 Avg@4, significantly higher than vanilla OPD (83.00) and TCOD-F2B (80.06).
- **Training Efficiency and Speedup**: TurnOPD substantially reduced wall-clock training time. For ALFWorld-1.7B, it cut the 100-step training time from 4.42 hours (vanilla OPD) to 1.93 hours, representing a 2.29x speedup, while simultaneously improving accuracy. Similar speedups were observed on Multi-Hop Search (1.51x) and WebShop (1.26x).
- **Teacher Performance**: In some cases, such as ALFWorld-4B, TurnOPD's student performance (92.21 Avg@4) even slightly exceeded the GRPO-trained teacher's reference accuracy (90.75), indicating effective distillation and generalization.
- **Category-Level Performance**: Detailed breakdowns (Tables 3 and 4) showed TurnOPD as the strongest student-training method across nearly all ALFWorld categories and demonstrated improvements on PopQA and 2Wiki sub-benchmarks in Multi-Hop Search.

#### 4.2. Diagnostic–Controller Alignment

- **Adaptive Controller Behavior**: Analysis of the adaptive rollout-depth controller's components (Figure 6) demonstrated alignment with its design principles.
 - In ALFWorld, the coverage constraint ($H_{cov}$) quickly became dominant, promoting longer rollouts.
 - For WebShop, the controller maintained a moderate coverage lower bound.
 - In Multi-Hop Search, the balance between the efficiency-centric ($H_{eff}$) and coverage-based ($H_{cov}$) arms shifted dynamically, reflecting the task's characteristics.
- This alignment indicated that the controllers effectively responded to and tracked the underlying diagnostic signals (KL strength and success coverage), supporting the adaptive design.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies and Analysis (ALFWorld-1.7B)

- **Intervention Component Decomposition (Table 5, Figure 7)**:
 - **Adaptive Rollout-Depth Alone**: Applying only adaptive depth significantly reduced wall-clock time (from 4.42h to 1.96h) but resulted in a slight decrease in accuracy (from 83.0 to 82.8 Avg@4). This indicates that while it is an efficiency lever, it does not fully address the optimization challenges.
 - **Linear Blend Normalization Alone**: Using only the linear blend normalization (with fixed full-length rollouts) improved accuracy (to 85.1 Avg@4) but still incurred a higher wall time (2.59h). This demonstrates its role as an optimization lever.
 - **Full TurnOPD (Both Combined)**: Combining both controllers yielded the best performance, achieving the highest accuracy (86.3 Avg@4) with the lowest wall time (1.93h), confirming the synergistic effect of the two components.
- **Effect of KL Normalization (Table 6)**:
 - **Trajectory-level KL**: Allocated a minimal budget to deep turns (3.2% early, 1.2% late), leading to lower accuracy.
 - **Hard Turn-level KL**: Abruptly shifted a large budget to deep turns (~30%), improving accuracy (85.0 Avg@4) but potentially over-weighting unreliable estimates early on.
 - **Linear Blend (TurnOPD)**: Provided a smooth transition, increasing the deep-turn budget from 12.8% to 27.7% over training, balancing stability and targeted allocation, resulting in the highest accuracy (85.1 Avg@4).
- **Adaptive-Depth Coverage Sensitivity (Figure 8)**:
 - **Quantile Parameter ($p$)**: Higher $p$ values (e.g., 0.8) for $H_{cov}$ led to deeper rollouts and slightly higher final accuracy but increased training time. Lower $p$ values (e.g., 0.4) were more efficient but might compromise peak accuracy.
 - **CDF Source (Success-Conditioned vs. Full-Population)**: Using a success-conditioned CDF for $H_{cov}$ was more efficient. A full-population CDF, which includes many failed trajectories that might stall at maximum depth, tended to overestimate the required rollout depth, leading to higher wall times for equivalent accuracy. This suggests that while success signals are beneficial for efficiency, the method remains effective in general settings, provided $p$ is tuned appropriately.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research on TurnOPD contributes to the field of language model agents by providing a systematic analysis and practical solution to key inefficiencies in on-policy distillation for long-horizon tasks.

**Key Contributions:**
- **Diagnostic Understanding**: The work offers a detailed, turn-level analysis of how supervision and optimization signals are distributed within agent interaction trajectories during vanilla OPD, highlighting the fundamental "external" and "internal" mismatches.
- **Formalization of Mechanism**: The introduction of the "contamination-compression mechanism" provides a theoretical explanation for the observed degradation and unreliability of the raw KL signal at deeper turns, offering insights into why standard OPD struggles in these contexts.
- **Novel Methodology**: TurnOPD introduces a two-component budgeting strategy: adaptive rollout-depth budgeting and progressive turn-normalized loss budgeting. This represents a turn-aware approach to managing computational resources for both data collection and gradient allocation.
- **Empirical Advancement**: The experimental results consistently demonstrate that TurnOPD advances the accuracy–time frontier across diverse long-horizon tasks and model scales. It achieves comparable or superior performance with substantially faster training times (up to 2.29x speedup).

**Potential Impact:**
- **Enhanced Training Efficiency**: By reducing the wall-clock time and computational resources required for training, TurnOPD can make the development and deployment of sophisticated language agents more accessible and cost-effective. This is particularly relevant as agent tasks become more complex and demand longer interaction sequences.
- **Improved Agent Performance**: The method's ability to reallocate supervision effectively towards deeper, critical decision turns can lead to the development of more capable and robust agents that perform better on complex, multi-step tasks. This addresses a core limitation of previous OPD approaches.
- **Broader Applicability**: The principles underlying TurnOPD—that the unit of supervision in long-horizon agents should be a turn-conditioned decision rather than a flat token position—offer a broader lesson for agent training. This perspective could inform the design of future training algorithms across various interactive AI systems.
- **Foundation for Future Research**: TurnOPD opens new avenues for research into turn-aware, budget-adaptive training strategies. Future work could explore more advanced adaptive mechanisms, different weighting schemes, or integration with other curriculum learning techniques for even greater efficiency and performance in complex, open-ended environments.
