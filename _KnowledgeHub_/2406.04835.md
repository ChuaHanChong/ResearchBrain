# SLR: Learning Quadruped Locomotion without Privileged Information

> [!summary] Summary
> Tsinghua University researchers developed Self-learning Latent Representation (SLR), an end-to-end reinforcement learning framework, enabling quadruped robots to learn robust locomotion policies using only proprioceptive data. This approach avoids reliance on difficult-to-obtain privileged information, achieving superior performance in simulation and demonstrating enhanced real-world adaptability on a Unitree Go2 robot across various challenging terrains.

## Problem

- Quadruped robot locomotion policies learned via reinforcement learning heavily depend on 'privileged information' (e.g., precise friction, terrain height maps) during training, which is largely unavailable in real-world deployment.
- This reliance creates a significant 'Sim2Real' gap, hindering the transferability and robustness of policies to dynamic, unstructured real-world environments.
- Meticulous selection and precise estimation of privileged parameters increase complexity and limit the autonomy of the robot's learning process.

## Method

- SLR constructs a one-stage, end-to-end reinforcement learning system to learn control policies using only proprioceptive sensor data as input.
- An encoder network processes historical proprioceptive observations to generate a self-learned latent representation of environmental states, without any human-defined privileged information.
- The latent representation is guided by backpropagation from a Critic network and a Transition model, and its distinctiveness is enforced using a triplet loss function alongside Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) for the Actor-Critic architecture.

## Results

- SLR consistently achieved higher mean rewards and reached higher terrain levels in simulation compared to state-of-the-art methods, demonstrating enhanced robustness and ability to handle more challenging environments.
- The method exhibited superior velocity tracking capabilities with lower linear and angular velocity tracking errors across most velocity ranges and environments in simulation.
- In real-world deployment on a Unitree Go2 robot, SLR achieved significantly higher success rates (e.g., 100% on bush and curb, 70% on rocky terrain) and better performance metrics on challenging indoor and outdoor terrains compared to leading RL methods and the commercial MPC controller.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Self-learned latent representations, derived purely from proprioceptive data, are more effective at differentiating diverse terrain conditions than methods relying on explicit or implicitly encoded privileged information.
> - Surprisingly, incorporating additional explicit or implicit privileged information can reduce the performance of policies already utilizing self-learned latent representations.
> - The latent space learned by SLR effectively forms distinct clusters for different terrain types and captures fine-grained information about terrain transitions, demonstrating a deep environmental understanding.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot locomotion, especially for complex quadrupedal systems, presents significant challenges. Historically, model-based control methods have been employed, leveraging detailed knowledge of robot mechanics and dynamics. While successful in some controlled environments, these methods often struggle with real-time computational efficiency and require extensive expert knowledge to design accurate models that can cope with unpredictable real-world terrains.

In recent years, Reinforcement Learning (RL) has emerged as a powerful alternative, enabling robots to learn complex locomotion policies through interaction with simulated environments and customized reward functions. This paradigm shift has led to impressive results, with robots demonstrating adaptive and dynamic gaits. However, a prevalent approach in mainstream RL for quadruped robots is "privileged learning." This technique involves providing the learning agent with "privileged information" during training—data that is readily available in simulation but difficult or impossible to obtain accurately in real-world deployment. Examples include precise friction coefficients, restitution coefficients, or high-fidelity terrain scan-dots. This privileged information helps the robot "understand" its environment and itself during the learning process.

This paper, "SLR: Learning Quadruped Locomotion without Privileged Information," directly challenges this reliance on privileged information. The authors argue that such reliance imposes significant constraints:
1. **Meticulous Selection and Estimation:** Privileged parameters require careful selection and precise estimation, adding complexity to the development process.
2. **Real-World Acquisability:** These parameters are often unobtainable or highly inaccurate in real-world scenarios, creating a substantial "Sim2Real" (simulation-to-reality) gap that hinders deployment.
3. **Effectiveness for Neural Networks:** The authors question whether these explicit physical parameters genuinely enhance the interpretability or effectiveness of neural networks, as they are simply inputs rather than components of dynamic equations. They draw an analogy to human cognition, which navigates terrains without explicit knowledge of underlying physical parameters.

The work builds upon and contrasts with existing privileged learning methods, which can be broadly categorized into "explicit estimation" (directly fitting specific physical parameters) and "implicit estimation" (encoding high-dimensional privileged information into a latent representation). While implicit estimation is a step towards abstraction, it still relies on *privileged* high-dimensional information as its source. SLR proposes to bypass both explicit and implicit privileged information by enabling the robot to *self-learn* a latent representation of environmental states purely from its proprioceptive sensory data. This positions SLR as a significant step towards more autonomous, less human-engineered, and ultimately more generalizable robot learning, pushing the boundaries of what is achievable with limited real-world sensory input.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a high-performance control policy for quadruped robots that operates effectively *without* the need for privileged information. The authors aim to achieve this by enabling the robot to autonomously learn a latent representation of its environmental states.

The motivation for this ambitious goal stems from several critical limitations inherent in the current state-of-the-art reinforcement learning approaches for quadruped locomotion:
- **Reducing Dependency on External Knowledge:** Current methods often necessitate human-defined, precisely estimated physical parameters (e.g., friction, restitution, terrain height maps) as privileged information during training. This dependency complicates the learning process and limits the autonomy of the robot.
- **Bridging the Sim2Real Gap:** The precise privileged information available in simulation is rarely accessible or reliably measurable in real-world deployments. This discrepancy leads to a significant performance degradation when transferring policies trained with privileged information from simulation to reality. By removing this dependency, the authors seek to make policies more robust and directly applicable in diverse real-world environments.
- **Enhancing Generalizability and Adaptability:** The authors hypothesize that a self-learned latent representation, derived directly from the robot's proprioceptive sensor data, would be inherently more suitable and adaptive than representations based on manually selected or estimated physical parameters. Such a representation could enable the robot to better infer and adapt to varying terrain conditions without explicit pre-programmed knowledge.
- **Fair and Rigorous Evaluation:** A key motivation was to conduct a truly fair comparison against existing state-of-the-art algorithms. The authors explicitly state their intention to implement SLR directly within the open-source code repositories of other SOTA methods, using their original configuration parameters. This addresses common challenges in robotics research where comparisons are often biased due to different environmental setups, reward functions, or hyperparameter tuning.

In essence, the research is driven by the desire to push robot locomotion towards greater autonomy, robustness, and real-world applicability by eliminating a critical human-engineered dependency in the learning process.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, Self-learning Latent Representation (SLR), constructs a one-stage, end-to-end reinforcement learning system designed to learn control policies using only proprioceptive sensor data as input. The core idea is to guide the self-learning of a latent representation through a standard Markov Decision Process (MDP) without any human-defined privileged information.

The SLR framework comprises several interconnected neural networks, primarily Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs), as illustrated in Figure 2:

1. **Observation Space:** The robot's observations ($o_t$) consist of 45 dimensions, including base angular velocity, joystick commands (desired linear and angular velocities), gravity vector, joint positions and velocities, and the previous action taken.
2. **Action Space:** The action space is a 12-dimensional vector ($a_t$) corresponding to the target joint positions for the quadruped's four legs (three motors per leg). These outputs are converted to actual motor torques via a PD controller.
3. **Encoder ($\phi$):** This network is crucial for the self-learning aspect. It takes the observation history ($o_H_t$), consisting of proprioceptive information from the previous 10 time steps, and processes it to output a 20-dimensional latent representation ($z_t$). The encoder's weights are updated via backpropagation from the Critic network and the Transition model, enabling it to learn meaningful environmental features.
4. **Actor-Critic Architecture:**
 - **Actor ($\pi$):** The policy network takes the current proprioceptive observation ($o_t$) and the latent representation ($z_t$) as input. It then outputs the robot's actions ($a_t$). A key detail here is the use of a "stop gradient" operator (`sg[z_t]`) on the latent representation input to the Actor. This means that while the Actor uses $z_t$, its policy updates do not directly influence the encoder's weights.
 - **Critic ($V$):** The value network shares the same inputs as the Actor ($o_t, z_t$) and outputs the state value ($v_t$). Critically, the Critic's backpropagation *does* propagate gradients to the encoder, thereby guiding the encoder to learn latent representations that are beneficial for maximizing cumulative rewards.
 - **Training Algorithm:** Both Actor and Critic are trained jointly using the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm.
5. **Transition Model ($\mu$):** This model is designed to simulate the dynamics of environmental state transitions. It takes the current latent representation ($z_t$) and the action ($a_t$) as input, and predicts the estimated next time step's latent representation ($\tilde{z}_{t+1}$).
6. **Loss Function for Latent Representation ($L_{trip}$):** To ensure the encoder learns effective and distinctive latent representations, a triplet loss function is employed, inspired by. This loss function aims to:
 - Pull the estimated next latent state ($\tilde{z}_{t+1}$) closer to the actual next latent state ($z_{t+1}$).
 - Push $z_{t+1}$ away from other randomly sampled latent states ($z_{t+n}$) at different time steps.
 - A margin `m` (set to 1.0) is enforced to ensure distinctiveness.
 This loss function, multiplied by a triplet coefficient (set to 1.0), is added to the standard PPO loss ($L_{ppo}$) during the overall network update process (Algorithm 1 in Appendix).

**Reward Functions:** The training uses a set of standard reward terms adapted from previous works (detailed in Table A1), which incentivize velocity tracking, minimize power consumption, maintain base height, and penalize undesired movements like high joint acceleration or torques.

**Domain Randomizations:** To enhance the robustness and Sim2Real transferability of the learned policy, domain randomizations are extensively applied during training (details in Table A2). These randomizations involve varying physical parameters of the robot (e.g., body mass, link mass, CoM, payload), ground properties (friction, restitution), motor characteristics, and external disturbances.

**Fair Comparison Strategy:** A significant aspect of the methodology is the commitment to fair quantitative comparison. The authors explicitly state that SLR was implemented directly within the open-source code repositories of several state-of-the-art methods. Only the algorithm framework was modified, ensuring that other variables like reward functions, hyperparameters, and environment settings remained consistent with the original implementations. This approach mitigates common issues of experimental bias and provides a more credible evaluation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted both in simulation and real-world deployment provide compelling evidence for the efficacy and superiority of the SLR algorithm.

#### 4.1. Ablation Study for Latent Representations (Section 4.1)

The ablation study, comparing SLR with various forms of privileged information, yielded crucial insights:
- **SLR Outperformance:** SLR, using only self-learned latent representations, significantly outperformed methods that relied on explicit or implicit privileged information (Implicit, Explicit, SLR w/ explicit, SLR w/ implicit, SLR w/o latent). This suggests that human-defined privileged information, even when implicitly encoded, struggles to effectively represent diverse terrains compared to a self-learned representation.
- **Detrimental Effect of Additional Privileged Information:** Surprisingly, incorporating additional explicit or implicit privileged latent information *reduced* SLR's performance. This indicates that the self-learned representation by SLR might be more optimal or that the additional inputs interfere with the Critic's guidance for updating the self-learning latent, making the policy input redundant or even disruptive.

#### 4.2. Latent Representation Analysis (Section 4.2)

To understand what SLR's encoder was learning, a t-SNE visualization of the latent space was performed across four different terrains (upward slope, descending stairs, flat ground, ascending stairs).
- **Distinct Terrain Differentiation:** The self-learned latent representations from SLR formed clear, distinct, and spatially separated ring-shaped clusters for each terrain type. This demonstrates SLR's ability to effectively identify and differentiate various terrain conditions. Interestingly, regions for "up slope" and "ascending stairs" showed similarity, reflecting a plausible perception of shared locomotion dynamics.
- **Effective Terrain Transition Representation:** A notable finding was the presence of "tails" extending from one terrain's cluster towards another during terrain transitions. These tails, with colors similar to the destination terrain, precisely indicated when the robot was at the boundary between terrains. This granular representation of transitions highlights the latent space's detailed understanding of environmental changes. In contrast, the Implicit method's latent representations appeared scattered and overlapping, indicating a less coherent and less discriminative understanding of terrains.

#### 4.3. Simulation Comparisons (Section 5.2)

SLR was directly compared against five state-of-the-art algorithms (MoB, RLvRL, ROA, HIM, Baseline) by implementing SLR in their original open-source code repositories.
- **Superior Mean Rewards:** SLR consistently achieved higher mean rewards during training compared to Baseline, RLvRL, and ROA across various repositories. It showed rewards comparable to HIM and slightly higher than MoB (Figure 6).
- **Higher Terrain Level Achievement:** Crucially, when considering the terrain curriculum, SLR achieved a *higher terrain level* than HIM, even when their mean rewards appeared similar (Figure 8). This is a strong indicator of SLR's superior robustness and ability to handle more challenging environments.
- **Enhanced Velocity Tracking:** SLR demonstrated superior velocity tracking capabilities, exhibiting lower Linear Velocity Tracking Error (LVTE) and Angular Velocity Tracking Error (AVTE) in most velocity ranges and environments (Table 1). This is attributed to SLR's implicit inference of velocity from proprioceptive data and the Critic's backpropagation optimizing for the significant velocity tracking term in the reward function.
- **Generalizability Across Task Complexities:** SLR's effectiveness was observed even in repositories designed for complex tasks, such as MoB (multiple commands) and ROA (manipulator-equipped robots). This suggests that self-learning latent representations offer more general benefits compared to manually selected privileged information.

#### 4.4. Real-World Deployment (Section 5.3)

The trained policies were successfully deployed on a Unitree Go2 robot in real-world indoor and outdoor environments, and compared against HIM, MoB, Baseline, and the Unitree built-in MPC control method.
- **Outperforming SOTA and MPC:** SLR demonstrated significantly superior efficacy in real-world scenarios (Table 2). It consistently achieved higher success rates and better performance metrics across various challenging terrains:
 - **Indoor Stairs:** Climbed more stairs and higher maximum step heights than all comparative methods, including the commercial MPC controller.
 - **Outdoor Terrains:** Achieved 100% success rates on bush, cobbled road, and curb terrains, where other methods often failed or had very low success rates (e.g., Curb: SLR 100% vs. MoB 20%, Baseline 0%, MPC 40%). It also performed strongly on earthen slopes and stairs.
 - **Extreme Terrain (Rock):** While challenging for all, SLR achieved a 70% success rate on rocky terrain, significantly outperforming HIM (30%) and others (0%).

These findings collectively underscore SLR's capability to learn robust and adaptive locomotion policies without privileged information, showcasing strong performance in both simulation and challenging real-world environments.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "SLR: Learning Quadruped Locomotion without Privileged Information" paper represents a significant advancement in the field of robot learning, particularly for legged locomotion. Its core contribution lies in demonstrating that high-performance, robust control policies can be learned without relying on the difficult-to-obtain "privileged information" commonly used in state-of-the-art methods.

The significance and potential impact of this work are multi-faceted:

- **Paradigm Shift in Robot Learning:** This research challenges the established paradigm of privileged learning by showing that robots can autonomously infer crucial environmental context from limited proprioceptive data. This shifts the focus from engineering explicit environment representations to enabling robots to learn these representations themselves, moving closer to how biological systems adapt to varied environments.
- **Enhanced Real-World Applicability and Sim2Real Transfer:** By eliminating the dependency on privileged information, SLR directly addresses one of the most significant hurdles in robot learning: the Sim2Real gap. Policies trained with SLR are inherently more suitable for direct deployment in dynamic, unknown, and unstructured real-world environments, where precise environmental parameters are unavailable. This significantly broadens the practical applicability of reinforcement learning in robotics.
- **Increased Robustness and Generalization:** The self-learned latent representations are shown to effectively differentiate terrains and capture transitions, leading to policies that are more robust and adaptable to a wider variety of challenging terrains. The superior performance in real-world tests, particularly on complex and varied outdoor terrains, validates this enhanced robustness.
- **Reduced Development Complexity and Human Effort:** The ability to learn without meticulously selecting and estimating privileged parameters simplifies the robot control development process. It reduces the need for extensive human engineering knowledge specific to particular environments, making the learning process more generalizable across different robotics applications.
- **Paving the Way for Truly Autonomous Agents:** SLR lays a crucial foundation for developing more autonomous and intelligent robotic agents. It demonstrates the potential for robots to build internal models of their environment purely from their interactions, paving the way for sophisticated cognitive abilities in future robotic systems.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The authors explicitly point to future directions, such as integrating privileged information (if available) and external perceptions (like vision sensors) into the self-learning framework. This suggests that SLR is not an endpoint but a robust foundation upon which more complex and capable "non-blind" policies can be built, further enhancing trajectory planning and navigation in even more complex scenarios.
- **Practical Implications for Quadruped Robots:** The impressive real-world deployment results on the Unitree Go2 robot showcase immediate practical utility. Robots equipped with SLR-trained policies could be deployed in diverse fields such as disaster response, inspection in hazardous environments, logistics, or even entertainment, where robust and adaptive locomotion in unpredictable terrains is critical.

In conclusion, SLR represents a substantial leap forward by demonstrating that blind policies, based purely on self-learned latent representations from proprioceptive data, can achieve and even surpass the performance of state-of-the-art privileged learning methods. Its impact will likely resonate across robot learning research, fostering the development of more autonomous, adaptable, and deployable robotic systems.
