# Learning Vision-Based Bipedal Locomotion for Challenging Terrain

> [!summary] Summary
> A fully learned vision-locomotion system for the bipedal robot Cassie. An RL locomotion policy pairs a pretrained blind LSTM controller with a vision-based modulator conditioned on a local-frame heightmap, while an LSTM plus U-Net predictor reconstructs that heightmap from egocentric depth images and robot states. With heavy domain randomization it transfers zero-shot to hardware, crossing stairs, random blocks, and a 0.5m step-up at 1m/s without odometry.

## Problem

- Blind proprioception-only bipedal RL controllers cannot anticipate local terrain, producing aggressive high-swing gaits and failing where footholds must be chosen in advance.
- Model-based perceptive locomotion stacks need whole-body control, footstep planning, calibrated multi-sensor mapping, and global pose estimation that drifts under frequent legged contacts.
- Vision-based learning successes are largely quadrupedal; bipeds are far less forgiving because contact placement governs stability and stray contacts easily tip the robot.

## Method

- Two-part policy: a pretrained blind ==LSTM== controller supplies baseline actions, and a vision-based ==modulator== consumes those actions plus a 1.5m by 1m local-frame ==heightmap== to emit modulating and clock actions.
- ==PPO== training with a ==mirror loss== over proprioceptive and visual inputs, a ==privileged critic== seeing global foot positions and per-foot heightmaps, plus foot-acceleration and foot-collision reward terms.
- Two-stage heightmap predictor: an ==LSTM== maps depth-image and state history to a raw heightmap, then a ==U-Net== refines it under ==L1 loss==, trained on 30,000 randomized simulated episodes.

## Results

- Heightmap reconstruction error is lowest for the LSTM at **2.806cm** MAE, versus **4.221cm** for a Transformer, **4.932cm** for an MLP, and **4.448cm** without robot states.
- Ablations show removing the learned clock or the privileged critic sharply cuts success on hard terrain, and dropping the collision penalty greatly raises foot-collision episodes despite similar success rate.
- Hardware Cassie traverses stairs, random blocks, and a **0.5m** step-up near **60%** of leg length at up to **1m/s**, with under **20ms** end-to-end vision-to-control delay.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Predicting terrain in the robot's own local frame sidesteps global odometry drift, a decoupling that generalizes to any contact-rich system with unreliable pose estimation.
> - Shaping the terrain-generation distribution regulated step length and height better than adding heuristic reward terms, so curriculum design can substitute for reward engineering.
> - Soft penalties can be gamed: without a collision cost the policy exploited foot contact as tactile feedback, which reads as clever in simulation but blocks hardware transfer.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The utility of robotic systems for practical applications is significantly influenced by their capacity to navigate diverse environments. Bipedal robots, in particular, possess the potential for human-like locomotion capabilities, but their current performance lags behind. Achieving robust bipedal locomotion, especially over varied and challenging terrains, necessitates the integration of perception, state assessment, and dynamic gait adaptation. This integration, particularly involving visual perception, remains an area of active research.

Traditional control approaches for vision-based legged locomotion typically employ a hierarchical decomposition, involving modules for whole-body control, footstep planning, odometry estimation, and terrain mapping. Each of these modules often relies on specific modeling assumptions to process high-dimensional sensory data. While these methods have demonstrated capabilities, they can be complex to develop and sensitive to inaccuracies in modeling or estimation.

In contrast, recent learning-based approaches aim to establish direct mappings from high-dimensional sensory inputs to actuator commands, requiring fewer explicit modeling assumptions. These methods have shown empirical success in "blind" bipedal locomotion (relying solely on proprioception) and vision-based quadrupedal locomotion in real-world environments. A critical challenge for these learning-based systems is achieving reliable sim-to-real transfer, particularly when vision constitutes a primary input modality, as visual disparities between simulation and reality can hinder performance.

Within this context, the presented work focuses on addressing the gap in vision-based bipedal locomotion for challenging terrains using a fully-learned, sim-to-real approach. Previous work on quadrupedal robots has successfully utilized learning for vision-based locomotion, often employing elevation maps or direct camera inputs with techniques like teacher-student training and extensive domain randomization. However, applying these methods to bipedal robots presents additional challenges due to their inherent instability and the critical role of precise contact locations. Existing terrain mapping techniques often rely on global pose estimation, which is susceptible to drift during aggressive legged motion. This paper distinguishes itself by learning to combine proprioceptive and visual information entirely within the robot's local frame, thus bypassing the need for explicit global odometry estimation. This aspect is noted as a novel contribution to vision-based bipedal locomotion.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and demonstrate a fully-learned system that enables bipedal robots to react to local terrain changes while maintaining commanded travel speed and direction. This system aims to facilitate navigation over challenging terrains, such as high blocks, stairs, and significant step-ups, which are difficult for blind controllers.

The motivation for pursuing this objective stems from several key challenges and limitations in the field:
- **Limitations of Proprioceptive-only Control:** While reinforcement learning has enabled robust bipedal gaits using only proprioceptive sensing for moderate terrains, these "blind" controllers are insufficient for environments requiring anticipation and adaptation to local terrain features. Such scenarios necessitate visual perception.
- **Complexity of Hierarchical Control:** Traditional model-based and hierarchical control strategies for vision-based locomotion involve multiple complex components (e.g., whole-body control, footstep planning, odometry, terrain mapping), each with specific modeling assumptions. This complexity can hinder integration and robustness.
- **Challenges of Sim-to-Real Transfer for Vision-based Bipeds:** Learning-based methods, particularly those incorporating visual inputs, face significant difficulties in transferring trained policies from simulation to real hardware. This gap is amplified for bipedal robots due to their stability requirements, where precise foot placement and contact forces are crucial. Prior successes in vision-based quadrupedal locomotion do not directly translate to bipeds, which are inherently less stable and more susceptible to errors in contact.
- **Avoidance of Global Odometry Estimation:** Many terrain mapping and perception systems for legged robots rely on accurate pose estimation of the floating base in a global frame. This can be problematic for legged platforms due to potential drift from frequent ground contacts and aggressive maneuvers. The authors aim to circumvent this issue by learning to process sensory information entirely within the robot's local frame, removing the dependency on potentially noisy global estimates for reactive control.
- **Achieving Adaptive and Agile Gaits:** The objective is to enable bipedal robots to dynamically adapt their gaits in response to perceived terrain, moving beyond fixed or pre-planned movements to achieve more agile and natural locomotion over complex surfaces.

In essence, the paper aims to present the first successful demonstration of sim-to-real learning for vision-based bipedal locomotion on challenging terrains, achieved through a novel architecture that integrates a terrain-aware control policy with an egocentric heightmap predictor, without relying on global odometry.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed system integrates vision and locomotion through a fully-learned, two-component architecture trained in simulation for sim-to-real transfer.

**A. System Overview**
The overall system comprises two main neural network components:
1. **Locomotion Policy:** This policy generates PD setpoints for the robot's actuators based on proprioceptive information, a local terrain heightmap, and user commands (e.g., desired speed and direction).
2. **Heightmap Predictor:** This predictor processes a history of egocentric depth images and robot states to output the local terrain heightmap required by the locomotion policy.

The training pipeline operates in two stages:
1. **Policy Training:** The locomotion policy is trained first using Reinforcement Learning (RL) in a simulated environment. This stage incorporates extensive randomization of terrain, user commands, and physical parameters to facilitate sim-to-real transfer.
2. **Predictor Training:** Once the locomotion policy is trained, it is used to collect data in simulation. This data, consisting of simulated depth images paired with ground-truth heightmaps, is then used for supervised learning of the heightmap predictor. Domain randomization and noise are also applied during predictor training to enhance sim-to-real transfer.

**B. Learning a Terrain-Aware Locomotion Policy**

The control objective for the locomotion policy is to follow speed and heading commands while maintaining balance over challenging terrains.

- **Control Policy Design:**
 - **Observation Space:** The policy's input includes:
 - **Proprioceptive information:** Orientation (quaternion) and angular velocity of the floating base, positions and velocities of all actuated and unactuated joints.
 - **Terrain Heightmap:** A 1.5m x 1m area in front of the robot, represented at a 5cm resolution, encoding ground height relative to the robot's floating base. This relative encoding avoids global mapping and odometry.
 - **User Commands:** Desired linear velocities (X, Y) and rotational velocity (yaw).
 - **Periodic Clock:** Two functions (sin and cos) for each leg, derived from a phase variable (ϕ) and period shifts (γ), to encode gait timing and sequence.
 - **Action Space:** The policy, operating at 50Hz, outputs PD setpoints for all motors. It also outputs three additional values: a clock increment (Δϕ) and residuals of period shifts (Δγ_left, Δγ_right) for flexible gait adaptation.
 - **Policy Architecture:** A neural network represents the policy, composed of two main parts:
 - **Blind Policy:** Based on prior work, this LSTM network uses all observations *except* the heightmap. It is pre-trained on flat ground with various gait parameters to provide a baseline locomotion control robust to moderate disturbances.
 - **Vision-based Modulator:** This component takes all available observations, including the heightmap and the action produced by the blind policy, as input. It outputs a "modulating action" to adjust the baseline control and "clock actions" to modify gait parameters based on local terrain details.

- **Policy Training:**
 - **Algorithm:** The policy is trained using the PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization) actor-critic RL algorithm in a MuJoCo simulation environment.
 - **PPO Modifications:** Two modifications were introduced to accelerate and stabilize learning:
 - **Mirror Loss:** Added to the PPO loss function, encouraging symmetric actions for symmetrically mirrored proprioceptive and visual inputs.
 - **Privileged Inputs for Critic:** The critic received additional "privileged" information (robot height, global foot positions, detailed foot heightmaps, rangefinder sensor readings) not available to the policy. This provides the critic with a more accurate understanding of the 3D environment, improving its reward prediction and addressing potential unfavorable collision events.
 - **Training Episode Generation:**
 - **Terrain Randomization:** Each episode uses a randomly generated 20m x 20m map from one of five terrain types: flat, hills, blocks, ridges, and stairs. The majority of episodes (35% each) are on blocks and stairs, with ridges at 20%, to focus learning on challenging terrains. Terrain parameters (height, length, width, number of steps) are randomized within specified ranges (Table I).
 - **Robot Spawning & Commands:** The robot spawns randomly near the center. Commands (step-in-place, walk, turn) are randomly sampled. Backward and sideways movements are excluded on difficult terrains due to the single forward-facing camera.
 - **Episode Termination:** Episodes end after 400 timesteps (8 seconds) or upon undesirable robot behaviors (e.g., excessive roll/pitch, high linear velocity, base height below 40cm, body collision with terrain).
 - **Reward Function:** The reward function (R = R_0 + R_accel + R_collision) equally weights three components:
 - **R_0 (Base Locomotion):** Adopted from prior blind locomotion work, regulating contact sequence and timing.
 - **R_accel (Foot Acceleration):** Penalizes high foot accelerations to prevent aggressive swing motions that can hinder sim-to-real transfer (0.05 * exp(-0.02 * (||¨x_l|| + ||¨x_r||))).
 - **R_collision (Foot Collision):** A negative penalty (-5) for foot stumbles, acting as a soft constraint to encourage collision-free swing trajectories and aid sim-to-real transfer.
 - **Domain Randomization (Table II):** Extensive randomization is applied during training to bridge the sim-to-real gap and diversify data:
 - **Model Parameters:** Joint damping, mass, center of mass location, passive spring stiffness, torque efficiency (crucial for extreme terrains), and torque delay are randomized per episode.
 - **Heightmap Randomization:** The entire heightmap is shifted in XY and Z directions per episode and per policy step to simulate noise from the estimator. A randomized delay (up to 100ms) is also applied to the heightmap input.

**C. Heightmap Prediction from Egocentric Vision**

The heightmap predictor estimates a local heightmap from a history of egocentric depth images and robot states, addressing challenges like aggressive camera motions, occlusions, and noisy images.

- **Network Architecture and Losses:**
 - **Stage 1 (Raw Heightmap Prediction):** An LSTM network takes the history of robot states and depth images as input. It is trained by minimizing the mean-squared error (MSE) against the ground truth heightmap (relative to the robot base).
 - **Stage 2 (Heightmap Refinement):** A U-Net architecture processes the raw heightmap from Stage 1 to produce a "cleaned" version. This stage uses L1 loss to refine the heightmap, aiming for sharper corners, edges, and flatter surfaces.
- **Simulation-Based Data Generation:**
 - **Data Collection:** After the locomotion policy is trained, it runs in stochastic mode in the simulator to collect 30,000 episodes of training data. Each trajectory includes robot state (s_t), depth image (I_t), and ground truth heightmap (m_t).
 - **Camera Setup:** A simulated egocentric camera (pose at floating-base top, tilted 60 degrees, 2.5m x 2.5m view) generates depth images (848x480, resized to 128x128). Robot states include foot positions in the local frame.
- **Depth Image Randomization:** To simulate real-world camera noise and bridge the sim-to-real gap, several randomizations are applied:
 - **Camera Parameters:** Camera pose (XYZ, pitch) and Field of View (FOV) are randomized per episode.
 - **Post-processing Noise:** After rendering, Gaussian noise, image rotation, edge noise, random small objects, and spot noise are added to the depth images with a 0.3 probability for each type.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation involved both simulated ablation studies and real-world hardware demonstrations.

**A. Simulation Results**

1. **Policy Performance (Ablation Study):**
 - **Success Rate:** On "easy" terrain modes (Table I), all policies showed comparable success rates. However, on "hard" terrain modes, policies trained "w/o Learned Clock" and "w/o Privilege Critic" exhibited significantly lower success rates compared to "Ours" (the full proposed policy). This suggests that control over gait contact timing and sequence, along with accurate value estimation for the critic, are important for performance on challenging terrains.
 - **Episodes with Foot Collision:** Policies other than "Ours" generally experienced a higher number of foot collision events. The "Blind policy" showed the most aggressive swing leg motions and the worst overall performance.
 - **Termination due to Foot Collision:** Foot collisions were a primary cause of failure. The policy "w/o Foot Collision Reward," despite having a similar success rate to "Ours," allowed the robot to learn to cope with collisions, sometimes using them as proprioceptive feedback to slide a foot up vertical surfaces, which could be less robust in real-world scenarios.

2. **Local Terrain Reconstruction:**
 - **Reconstruction Loss (MAE):** An ablation study of different heightmap predictor architectures (Table III) demonstrated that the LSTM model achieved the lowest reconstruction loss (2.806 cm MAE). The Transformer-based model (4.221 cm) and MLP model (4.932 cm) had higher losses.
 - **Importance of Robot States:** An LSTM model trained "w/o robot states" (4.448 cm) performed worse than the full LSTM, indicating that the integration of proprioceptive robot states with egocentric vision is important for accurate local terrain estimation. LSTM's implicit history representation was noted as a potential benefit.

3. **Closed-loop Evaluation:**
 - When different heightmap predictor architectures were coupled with the "Ours" locomotion policy for closed-loop evaluation (Figure 7-B), all predictors resulted in similar success rates across terrain modes. This finding suggests that the learned policy exhibits robustness to certain levels of reconstruction error from the heightmap predictor.
 - However, models other than LSTM for prediction resulted in a higher number of episodes with foot collisions and a higher percentage of terminations due to foot collisions, despite similar success rates. This implies that while the policy can tolerate some reconstruction error to avoid falling, better heightmap quality (from LSTM) contributes to smoother, less collision-prone locomotion.

**B. Sim-to-Real Transfer**

1. **Experimental Setup:**
 - The system was deployed on a Cassie bipedal robot equipped with an Intel Realsense D455 depth camera and an NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano module for vision inference.
 - The camera mount was calibrated by matching real and simulated depth images. Depth images were pre-processed with hole-filling and distance clipping before being sent to the predictor.
 - The main control policy ran on Cassie's onboard computer, while the heightmap predictor ran asynchronously on the Jetson. The end-to-end delay, including communication, camera stream, and inference, was measured at up to 20ms, with heightmap prediction inference alone capable of 200Hz.
 - Structured wooden blocks were used in a lab environment to create challenging terrains mimicking those in simulation.

2. **Evaluation:**
 - The deployed system successfully enabled the Cassie robot to traverse various challenging terrains, including single high blocks, stairs (upward and downward), and random block configurations with diverse dimensions.
 - The robot demonstrated the ability to navigate terrains where the egocentric camera could not directly observe the ground beneath it, suggesting that the policy maintained an internal representation or "odometry" for reasoning about the swing trajectory and upcoming terrain.
 - The system also allowed Cassie to ascend a 0.5m high step-up, a scenario where the leading stance leg's knee motor experiences torque saturation while lifting the robot, demonstrating the policy's capacity to handle extreme terrain and physical limits.
 - Further testing on a treadmill with continuously fed random blocks demonstrated the robot's ability to adapt to dynamic, unpredicted obstacles while maintaining commanded speed and heading.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a notable advancement in the field of bipedal robot locomotion, particularly concerning autonomous navigation over challenging and unstructured terrains.

The primary significance lies in the **first reported successful sim-to-real transfer of a fully-learned, vision-based bipedal locomotion system for challenging terrains**. This accomplishment addresses a long-standing challenge in robotics, where the instability of bipedal platforms combined with the complexities of real-world visual perception has historically impeded the deployment of learned policies.

Key contributions that underscore its significance include:
- **Decoupled Architecture:** The system's two-stage architecture—a terrain-aware locomotion policy and a separate heightmap predictor—allows for modular development and training. The policy learns to react to local terrain features represented by a heightmap, while the predictor translates raw egocentric depth images into this structured representation.
- **Avoidance of Global Odometry:** By relying solely on an egocentric heightmap relative to the robot's floating base, the system eliminates the need for explicit global pose estimation and terrain mapping. This simplifies the perception pipeline, reduces reliance on potentially drifting global estimates, and contributes to the system's robustness in dynamic environments where such estimates can be inaccurate.
- **Robust Sim-to-Real Pipeline:** The extensive use of domain randomization for both physical robot parameters and visual inputs (heightmap and depth image noise) is crucial for bridging the simulation-to-reality gap without requiring real-world fine-tuning. This approach reduces the labor and risk associated with hardware-in-the-loop training.
- **Adaptive Gaits and Collision Avoidance:** The policy's ability to adapt gait parameters and incorporate foot acceleration and collision penalties in its reward function enables it to generate more agile and robust movements, facilitating navigation over obstacles and reducing unfavorable contact events that could lead to falls.
- **Hardware Demonstration:** The successful deployment on a Cassie bipedal robot over various challenging terrains, including a 0.5m step-up and dynamic obstacles on a treadmill, provides empirical evidence of the system's practical viability and robustness. The observation of the policy implicitly managing internal odometry for unobservable terrain is also noteworthy.

The potential impact of this work is substantial for several reasons:
- **Advancement in Bipedal Robotics:** It moves bipedal robots closer to human-level locomotion capabilities in complex environments, expanding their potential applications in unstructured settings such as disaster response, urban exploration, and domestic assistance.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The methodology, particularly the sim-to-real pipeline and the local frame approach, provides a foundation for future research in learning vision-based dynamic locomotion for various legged robots. It can inspire further investigations into more complex visual perception tasks, multi-modal sensing, and higher-level navigation.
- **Reduced Development Costs:** The reliance on simulation for comprehensive training and the successful transfer without real-world fine-tuning can significantly reduce the time, cost, and safety concerns associated with developing and testing advanced robotic locomotion systems.

In summary, this research contributes a novel and empirically validated solution for vision-based bipedal locomotion, addressing critical integration and transfer challenges and demonstrating capabilities that were previously elusive for this class of robots.
