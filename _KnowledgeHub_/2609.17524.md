# Modality-Autoregressive World-Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> ModAR is a world-action model that denoises future observation modalities one at a time, point tracks, then DINO features, then depth, then RGB, with each conditioned on earlier predictions, before generating robot actions last as an inverse-dynamics step. Trained from scratch at 30.1M parameters, it beats joint, disjoint, and independent-noise WAM formulations on RoboTwin and slightly outperforms the 6B video-pretrained Flex-π using about 20x less compute.

## Problem

- Most world-action models predict future RGB through frozen video VAE latents, which reconstruct appearance rather than the geometric, semantic, and motion structure that manipulation actually depends on.
- Alternative future modalities like depth, point tracks, and DINO features each carry useful inductive biases, but how to combine several of them within one WAM remained open.
- Existing WAMs mostly initialize from large pretrained video generators, which confounds comparison and makes it hard to isolate how formulation, target modalities, and actionless-data scale affect performance.

## Method

- **Modality-autoregressive factorization**: a block-causal shared diffusion transformer generates tracks, DINO, depth, then RGB, each block conditioned on earlier completed blocks, with actions denoised last acting as an inverse dynamics model.
- ==Shared world-action backbone==: cross-modal DiT blocks fuse all causally available tokens, followed by small modality-specific expert stacks and heads, with robot configuration, task embedding, and per-modality timesteps injected through adaLN.
- ==Training recipe==: Latent-Forcing-style context noise corrupts earlier blocks during training to curb cascading error, all streams use x-prediction flow matching, and actionless demos supervise only the future-observation losses.

## Results

- On six RoboTwin tasks ModAR averages **66%/75%/76%** at 50/250/1,250 demos versus Unified's **63%/67%/64%**, and at 250 demos edges the 6B video-pretrained Flex-π (**72%**).
- At 250 demos, removing context noise drops success from **75%** to **63%** and reversing modality order to **65%**; dropping tracks costs most (**61%**), dropping RGB costs nothing.
- On three real bimanual tasks ModAR reaches **83.3%** versus **66.7%** Unified and **52.2%** Action-only; actionless human videos raise it from **70.0%** robot-only to **83.3%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Structured future targets like point tracks, DINO features, and depth help action prediction more than RGB, whose appearance detail added cost but no consistent success gain.
> - Order a multi-modality generation chain from compact, structured targets to detailed ones, and add training-time noise to earlier outputs, or cascading errors erase the gain.
> - The WAM formulation, not just data volume, decides whether actionless videos help: sequential conditioning converts them into gains, while disjoint auxiliary prediction degraded as actionless data grew.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

This work addresses the emerging area of **world-action models (WAMs)** for robotics. WAMs jointly model how the environment will evolve and what actions a robot should take. They extend conventional imitation-learning policies by predicting future observations alongside robot actions. This creates opportunities to train models using both action-labeled robot demonstrations and larger collections of actionless human or internet videos.

Most recent WAMs represent future observations primarily as **RGB video**, often using a pretrained video-generation model or visual autoencoder. RGB provides detailed appearance information, but it does not explicitly prioritize the geometric, semantic, and motion-related structures that are especially relevant to manipulation. Alternative representations have therefore been investigated. These include:

- **Depth**, which directly represents scene geometry.
- **Point tracks**, which describe how image points move through time and provide motion and correspondence information.
- **DINO features**, which encode semantic and structural information using pretrained visual representations.
- **RGB**, which preserves detailed appearance but can be difficult and computationally expensive to predict.

Existing WAMs also differ in how future observations and actions are generated. Some models denoise them simultaneously, some predict futures only as an auxiliary training objective, and others generate visual futures first and infer actions afterward. The paper identifies a gap in this literature: although multiple future modalities may provide complementary information, it was not established how they should be combined or whether their generation order matters.

Modality-Autoregressive World-Action Models, or **ModAR**, investigates this question through a controlled model family trained from scratch. This design allows the authors to compare target modalities, generation strategies, and actionless-data scaling without the confounding effects of different pretrained video models.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective is to determine how a WAM should combine multiple representations of the predicted future. The authors focus on three related questions.

First, they ask whether predicting structured modalities such as point tracks, DINO features, and depth improves robot action prediction compared with predicting only RGB or directly predicting actions. Each modality has a distinct inductive bias. Point tracks emphasize motion and object correspondence, DINO features emphasize semantic identity and scene structure, and depth emphasizes spatial geometry. These properties may be more useful for manipulation than pixel-level appearance.

Second, the paper investigates the **generation relationship between modalities and actions**. In a standard joint-generation WAM, all future streams and actions are denoised concurrently. ModAR instead generates one future modality completely before generating the next, with robot actions generated last. The intended interpretation is that earlier, more structured predictions serve as intermediate “scratchpads” for later predictions. For example, predicted tracks could provide motion structure, DINO features could provide semantic context, and depth could provide geometric information before the model generates actions.

Third, the authors study whether actionless demonstrations can improve robot policies. Future-observation prediction can use human videos that lack robot action labels, while action prediction still requires robot demonstrations. The paper tests whether multimodal future prediction allows a model to benefit from increasing quantities of actionless data and whether human videos collected across embodiments and domains can transfer to bimanual robot manipulation.

A further objective is to compare the resulting compact, from-scratch model against **Flex-π**, a much larger WAM initialized from a pretrained video model. This comparison examines whether pretraining and scale are necessary for effective WAM performance in the studied, relatively controlled tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

At time \(t\), the model receives a multimodal current observation, robot configuration, and discrete task label. The robot configuration includes joint positions and gripper opening. The model predicts future observations over a horizon of \(H=16\) control steps and generates an action chunk covering those steps.

The future-observation modality set is:

1. RGB images.
2. Depth maps.
3. DINO visual features.
4. Point tracks.

The experiments use sparse future visual targets at two time points, \(t+8\) and \(t+16\), together with dense actions at every step in the horizon.

ModAR factorizes the joint distribution into a sequence:

- Predict the first modality from the current observation.
- Predict the second modality conditioned on the current observation and the first predicted modality.
- Continue until all selected future modalities have been generated.
- Predict robot actions conditioned on the current observation and all generated futures.

The principal ordering is **point tracks → DINO features → depth → RGB → actions**. The ordering proceeds from comparatively structured representations toward more detailed representations.

#### 3.2. Modality Encoding

RGB and depth are divided into spatial patches. DINO targets are extracted from the spatial patch tokens of a frozen DINOv2 ViT-S/14 encoder. Point tracks are produced by placing a grid of queries at patch centers and tracking them using CoTracker3. Each point-time token represents displacement from the initial location and visibility.

All modalities are projected into a shared token dimension. Learned modality embeddings distinguish the streams. The model applies rotary positional embeddings over time and spatial dimensions for visual tokens, and over time for action tokens.

#### 3.3. Network Architecture

The model uses a diffusion transformer, or DiT, with a shared backbone. Shared transformer blocks attend across all causally available modality tokens. These are followed by modality-specific expert blocks and output heads. The shared blocks allow information exchange across modalities, while the expert blocks specialize processing for the characteristics of individual streams.

Robot configuration, task embeddings, and modality-specific flow timesteps condition the transformer through adaptive layer normalization. The resulting model contains approximately **30.1 million parameters**.

During autoregressive generation, the model applies a block-causal attention mask. When predicting a modality, it can attend to the current observation and previously completed modality blocks, but not to later target modalities. At inference, each modality is denoised independently from Gaussian noise, re-embedded, and supplied as context for the next stage.

#### 3.4. Training Objective

The authors train using an \(x\)-prediction objective combined with flow matching. For each stream, Gaussian noise and a flow timestep are sampled, producing an interpolation between noise and the clean target. The model predicts the clean target directly. The action stream uses the same type of objective.

Only action-labeled examples receive an action loss. Actionless examples contribute losses for whichever future modalities are available. The training batches contain equal-sized samples from action-labeled and actionless data pools.

Because autoregressive prediction can amplify early errors, the authors introduce **context noise**. During training, previously generated modality contexts are partially corrupted with noise. This exposes later prediction stages to imperfect inputs resembling those encountered during inference. The context noise is removed at test time.

#### 3.5. Experimental Design

In simulation, the authors evaluate six RoboTwin tasks: dumping a bin, picking bottles, placing bread, putting bottles away, stacking bowls, and turning a switch. Each model retains 50 action-labeled demonstrations per task while the total dataset size increases to 250 and 1,250 demonstrations through the addition of actionless data. Performance is measured using 50 held-out initial conditions per task.

The controlled baselines are:

- **Action-only**, which predicts actions directly.
- **Disjoint**, which predicts future observations and actions independently.
- **Unified**, which jointly denoises all streams.
- **Independent-noise**, which jointly generates streams but uses independently sampled training noise levels.
- **ModAR**, which generates modalities sequentially.

The authors also evaluate different subsets of predicted modalities and compare ModAR with Flex-π.

For real-world evaluation, the model is tested on bimanual tasks involving stacking cups, folding a crumpled towel, and placing an object in a drawer and closing it. Each task includes 100 robot demonstrations, 200 in-domain human demonstrations, and 1,000 EgoDex human demonstrations. Results are based on 30 rollouts per task.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Modality-Autoregressive Generation

In RoboTwin, ModAR achieves the highest average success rate among the evaluated formulations at all data scales. Its average success rates are:

- **66%** with 50 total demonstrations per task.
- **75%** with 250 demonstrations.
- **76%** with 1,250 demonstrations.

Unified obtains 63%, 67%, and 64%, respectively. Disjoint obtains 55%, 55%, and 48%, while Independent-noise performs substantially worse, reaching 37%, 34%, and 33%. The action-only baseline achieves 46% at the 50-demonstration scale and cannot exploit actionless data.

ModAR also benefits most from additional actionless demonstrations. Increasing the dataset from 50 to 1,250 total demonstrations improves ModAR by 10 percentage points, whereas Unified improves only marginally and Disjoint declines. This suggests that the particular coupling between future prediction and action generation determines whether actionless supervision transfers positively or causes negative interference.

The separate inverse-dynamics experiment supports the claim that ModAR generates more useful future representations, rather than benefiting only from having a cleaner action-prediction stage. When both ModAR and Unified use the same separately trained inverse-dynamics model, ModAR still performs better.

Matching the total number of denoising steps does not explain the result. Giving Unified, Disjoint, and Independent-noise the same 40-step sampling budget as ModAR does not close the performance gap.

#### 4.2. Contribution of Individual Modalities

The modality experiments show that point tracks, DINO features, and depth generally provide complementary benefits. At 250 demonstrations per task, the average success rates are approximately:

- RGB only: 54%.
- Depth only: 63%.
- Tracks only: 53%.
- DINO only: 65%.
- Tracks plus DINO: 72%.
- Tracks plus DINO plus depth: 75%.
- Tracks plus DINO plus depth plus RGB: 75%.

The ablation results further indicate that removing tracks reduces performance from 75% to 61%, removing DINO reduces it to 65%, and removing depth reduces it to 70%. Removing RGB leaves the average success rate unchanged. Thus, RGB does not consistently add useful information once the structured modalities are available, despite its higher representational detail.

The authors suggest that future RGB prediction may introduce high-variance appearance information without providing enough additional manipulation-relevant structure. The best modality subset nevertheless varies by task, indicating that no single combination is universally optimal.

#### 4.3. Ablation of Ordering and Context Noise

Removing context noise reduces performance from 75% to 63%, demonstrating that training with imperfect intermediate predictions is important for stable autoregressive generation.

Reversing the modality order to RGB → depth → DINO → tracks reduces performance to 65%. This supports the hypothesis that compact and structured modalities are useful as early intermediate representations. However, the paper does not conduct a complete search over all possible modality orderings.

#### 4.4. Comparison with Flex-π

At the 250-demonstration scale, ModAR achieves a 75% average success rate, compared with 72% for Flex-π. ModAR has approximately 30.1 million parameters, whereas Flex-π has 6 billion. The authors estimate that ModAR uses approximately 20 times fewer training FLOPs and does not rely on video-model pretraining. This comparison is not fully controlled because the systems differ in architecture, target modalities, initialization, and training procedures, but it indicates that a smaller model can be competitive on the evaluated in-distribution tasks.

#### 4.5. Real-World Manipulation

On the three bimanual tasks, ModAR achieves an overall success rate of **83.3%**, compared with **66.7%** for Unified and **52.2%** for Action-only. ModAR is reported to have the highest success rate on each task.

Human videos improve performance even without action labels. Using only robot demonstrations produces a 70.0% average success rate. Adding 200 in-domain human demonstrations raises this to 81.1%, and adding 1,000 out-of-domain EgoDex demonstrations raises it further to 83.3%. These results indicate that multimodal future prediction can extract transferable supervision from human manipulation videos.

End-to-end inference for the full four-modality model takes 147.9 milliseconds, corresponding to approximately 6.76 Hz on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5090. Sequential generation therefore has a measurable latency cost.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The paper’s primary significance is methodological. It shows that the representation and ordering of predicted futures are important design choices in WAMs. Rather than treating future observation generation as a single RGB-prediction problem, ModAR frames it as a sequence of structured prediction problems whose outputs can condition action generation.

The findings support the use of motion, semantic, and geometric representations as complementary supervision for manipulation. They also suggest that actionless human videos can improve robot policies when future observations are trained in a way that transfers across embodiments. This could reduce the dependence on large collections of robot demonstrations, which are expensive and time-consuming to collect.

The results also have computational implications. The compact from-scratch model performs competitively with a much larger pretrained WAM in the reported setting. However, sequential denoising increases inference cost, and the experiments are limited to six simulated tasks and three real-world tasks. The models use discrete task labels rather than natural-language instructions, and broad generalization across objects, scenes, embodiments, and task families is not established.

Overall, ModAR provides evidence that modality-autoregressive generation is a useful alternative to simultaneous multimodal WAM formulations. Its broader impact will depend on whether the observed benefits persist at larger scales, on more diverse tasks, with heterogeneous human and internet video, and under settings requiring language-conditioned generalization and real-time control.
