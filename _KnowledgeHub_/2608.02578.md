# CoWAM: Coordination Contracts for Selective Policy Intervention with WAMs

> [!summary] Summary
> CoWAM introduces coordination contracts for selective policy intervention in bimanual robotic manipulation, leveraging World Action Models (WAMs) to ensure synchronization, role compatibility, and collision avoidance. This approach achieved a 9.6 percentage-point improvement in closed-loop task success, reaching 62.9%, and demonstrated 93.3% coordination-valid selections across various tasks.

## Problem

- World Action Models (WAMs) can predict future outcomes for various actions, but a principled method to decide when to intervene and override a nominal policy action based on these predictions is lacking, often leading to unhelpful interventions.
- Bimanual manipulation tasks require complex coordination, including synchronization, role compatibility, and collision avoidance, which general visual plausibility scores from WAMs do not sufficiently capture.
- Existing intervention methods struggle to balance improved performance with conservative, interpretable decisions, frequently resulting in detrimental overrides or failing to identify genuine opportunities for improvement.

## Method

- **CoWAM** implements a ==selective intervention layer== that processes WAM-generated action-future candidates, maintaining independence from the underlying WAM's generation process.
- It defines explicit ==coordination contracts== for synchronization, role compatibility, and collision convergence as deterministic predicates, evaluated on the predicted world trajectories.
- An ==event-conditioned verifier== provides learned evidence for future-dependent failures, which, when combined with conservative, ==calibrated intervention gates==, enables informed decisions to preserve nominal actions, override with qualified alternatives, or abstain.

## Results

- CoWAM improved natural closed-loop task success to **62.9%** (151/240 episodes), a **9.6 percentage-point** gain over the Selective Control baseline (**53.3%**).
- It achieved a **93.3%** coordination-valid selection rate (140/150 opportunities), marking a **16.7 percentage-point** improvement over the Contract-only variant, while maintaining low rates of false (**3.3%**) and harmful (**0.4%**) interventions in closed-loop.
- The method significantly reduced specific failure categories compared to the nominal policy, including inter-arm collisions by **66.7%**, role conflicts by **75.0%**, and asynchronous releases by **71.4%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Formalizing coordination requirements as explicit "coordination contracts" offers a structured and interpretable framework for evaluating WAM predictions in bimanual tasks, moving beyond generic plausibility scores.
> - Combining deterministic contract checks with learned, event-conditioned verification and conservative intervention gates is crucial for making reliable, low-risk override decisions, effectively minimizing false and harmful interventions.
> - Decoupling the intervention logic from the WAM's action generation process creates a reusable interface that can effectively leverage diverse WAM sources and robustly transfer across different model architectures.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research contributes to the field of robotic manipulation, specifically focusing on bimanual tasks and the utilization of World Action Models (WAMs). WAMs are models that augment robot policies by predicting future states conditioned on proposed actions. Recent advancements in WAMs, such as Unified World Models, UVA, Fast-WAM, X-WAM, and DreamZero, have enabled robots to generate action-conditioned predicted observations or latent states, exposing potential failures before execution. This capability has led to applications in policy steering, verification, and adaptive execution in robot control.

However, the existing research landscape identified a gap: while WAMs can generate plausible future scenarios for various candidate actions, determining *when* an alternative action should replace a nominal (top-ranked) policy action based solely on visual plausibility remains a challenge. Simple reranking of WAM proposals often leads to excessive or harmful interventions. Bimanual manipulation, characterized by coupled action streams, shared objects, timing, workspace, and arm roles, further exacerbates this challenge by introducing complex coordination requirements such as synchronization, role compatibility, and collision avoidance.

This work addresses this specific gap by introducing a selective intervention layer. Instead of focusing on generating better actions or predictions, CoWAM integrates coordination-specific criteria to make informed decisions about whether to preserve the nominal action, override it with a qualified alternative, or abstain. It connects the predictive power of WAMs with conservative policy intervention, moving beyond general plausibility to structured, auditable coordination contracts. This approach is distinct from methods that integrate constraints directly into action generation or that rely on less structured future-agreement scores for intervention decisions. The paper positions CoWAM as a reusable interface that operates independently of the underlying WAM's generation objective, allowing it to leverage diverse WAM sources.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a selective intervention mechanism that effectively leverages action-conditioned predicted futures from World Action Models (WAMs) to improve the coordination and success rate of bimanual robotic manipulation tasks, while simultaneously minimizing harmful and unnecessary interventions.

The motivation for CoWAM stems from several observed limitations in the existing approaches:

1. **Insufficient Justification for Intervention:** WAMs can predict a range of future outcomes for different actions, but merely observing a "plausible" future for an alternative action does not inherently justify overriding the nominal, first-ranked action proposed by a policy. A visually coherent rollout might still contain critical coordination failures such as delayed grasps, incompatible arm assignments, or impending collisions. Conversely, an unsupported override could convert uncertainty into task failure.
2. **Complexity of Bimanual Coordination:** Bimanual tasks intrinsically demand high levels of coordination across multiple dimensions. These include:
 - **Synchronization:** Ensuring that contacts and releases between arms occur with correct timing.
 - **Role Compatibility:** Maintaining consistent object and support roles for each arm throughout the task.
 - **Spatial Compatibility and Collision Convergence:** Preventing inter-arm or arm-object collisions and ensuring safe motion in shared workspaces.
 - **Phase Consistency:** Coordinating actions across different stages of a multi-stage task.
 These coordination requirements are critical for successful task execution and are not consistently captured by general plausibility scores from WAMs.
3. **Need for Conservative and Interpretable Intervention:** Aggressive reranking based purely on predicted future scores can lead to frequent, potentially detrimental interventions. A robust intervention system should be conservative, only intervening when a clear, low-risk improvement is identified, and should provide interpretable reasons for its decisions. This necessitates separating the quality of action proposals from the quality of the intervention decision itself.
4. **Desire for a Reusable Interface:** To promote broader applicability and leverage advancements in various WAM architectures, there is a need for an intervention layer that is decoupled from the specific WAM's generation process. Such an interface would consume WAM outputs (action candidates and predicted futures) and apply a consistent, calibrated decision-making process.

To address these motivations, CoWAM was designed to:
- Express synchronization, role compatibility, and collision convergence as "coordination contracts."
- Combine typed admissibility checks with event-conditioned verification.
- Employ calibrated intervention gates to ensure interventions are low-risk and demonstrably beneficial.
- Operate on identical candidate pools in an "outcome-blind same-pool evaluation" protocol to isolate selector quality from proposal quality.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

CoWAM is presented as a selective intervention layer that processes action-future candidates from a World Action Model (WAM) to make informed decisions about policy execution in bimanual robotic tasks. The methodology is structured around a candidate interface, coordination contracts, event-conditioned contract evidence, and a selective policy intervention mechanism.

**World-Action Candidate Interface:**
At each replanning step `t`, a frozen WAM (referred to as the "proposer") generates an ordered pool of `K` candidate actions and their corresponding predicted world trajectories: `P_t = {(a_i, ẑ_i)}` for `i = 0` to `K-1`. Here, `a_i` is a synchronized left-right action chunk, `ẑ_i` is its predicted world trajectory (e.g., multi-view RGB-D observations and proprioception), and `i = 0` represents the nominal (first-ranked) candidate. CoWAM does not resample this pool or modify the proposer; the candidate identities and order remain fixed. This separation ensures that the selector's performance is not conflated with the proposer's generation capabilities.

The controller outputs one of three decisions:
1. **Preserve:** Execute the nominal action (`a_0`).
2. **Override:** Execute a verified alternative action (`a_i`, where `i > 0`).
3. **Abstain:** Invoke a task-defined fallback if no candidate is admissible.

**Coordination Contracts:**
The core of CoWAM's approach is the concept of a coordination contract, which formally specifies the event obligations and required evidence for intervention. Three typed families of coordination contracts are defined:
1. **Synchronization:** Addresses temporal compatibility of contacts and releases.
2. **Role Compatibility:** Ensures arms maintain consistent object and support roles.
3. **Collision Convergence:** Prevents inter-arm motion from leading to unsafe contacts.

For each candidate `i` and active event `e` (indicated by `m_{t,e} ∈ {0, 1}`), a deterministic predicate `c_{i,e} ∈ {0, 1}` is evaluated. The overall contract-admissibility indicator `g_i` for a candidate `i` is a conjunctive product across all active events: `g_i = Y_{e∈E} (1 - m_{t,e} + m_{t,e}c_{i,e})`. This means that if an event type is inactive, it imposes no constraint; otherwise, every active predicate must pass. These predicates utilize information from the paired action and predicted future, such as inter-arm distance, object assignments, and contact progress.

Contracts embed calibrated decision thresholds and a predefined fallback for contract failure. They are designed to be typed (not collapsed into a single score), allowing for diagnosis of specific coordination violations. Three invariants govern contracts: activation is determined before candidate outcomes are known, all active obligations are evaluated equally across candidates, and failure of one obligation cannot be offset by high utility in another.

**Event-Conditioned Contract Evidence:**
Beyond deterministic predicates, an "event-conditioned verifier" provides learned evidence for future-dependent failures. This verifier, denoted `f_θ`, maps the current observation `o_t`, candidate action `a_i`, predicted future `ẑ_i`, and active event `e` to a tuple of estimates:
`f_θ(o_t, a_i, ẑ_i, e) = (ˆp_{i,e}, ˆr_{i,e}, ˆu_i, ˆo_i, ˆq_i)`
where:
- `ˆp_{i,e}`: event satisfaction probability
- `ˆr_{i,e}`: coordination risk
- `ˆu_i`: task utility
- `ˆo_i`: opportunity value
- `ˆq_i`: confidence

The verifier is trained and calibrated on task-seed-disjoint groups, with independently seeded models forming an ensemble. Conservative bounds for risk (`R^+_i`) and utility (`U^-_i`) are constructed using ensemble means and predictive dispersion, scaled by frozen calibration multipliers (`κ_r`, `κ_u`). A combined score `S_i = U^-_i + λ_oˆo_i - λ_rR^+_i` is computed, where `λ_o` and `λ_r` are non-negative coefficients. Conditioning on `e` allows for event-specific interpretation of predicted motion.

**Selective Policy Intervention:**
For an alternative `i > 0`, intervention is only considered if all conjunctive intervention conditions are met:
`G_i = g_i ⋅ 1[R^+_i ≤ τ_r] ⋅ 1[U^-_i ≥ U^-_0 - ϵ_u] ⋅ 1[ˆo_i ≥ τ_o] ⋅ 1[Q_i ≥ τ_q] ⋅ 1[S_i - S_0 ≥ τ_m]`
This gate ensures:
- `g_i = 1`: Contract validity (all active deterministic predicates pass).
- `R^+_i ≤ τ_r`: Bounded coordination risk.
- `U^-_i ≥ U^-_0 - ϵ_u`: Utility retention relative to the nominal action (`U^-_0`).
- `ˆo_i ≥ τ_o`: An active opportunity (the alternative is predicted to be useful).
- `Q_i ≥ τ_q`: Sufficient event confidence.
- `S_i - S_0 ≥ τ_m`: A selective margin (the alternative is decisively better than the nominal).

If multiple alternatives satisfy `G_i = 1`, the one with the highest `S_i` is chosen, breaking ties by the original proposer order. If no alternative qualifies, CoWAM preserves the nominal action (`a_0`) if `g_0 = 1`; otherwise, it invokes the task-defined abstention fallback.

**Evaluation Protocol:**
A distinct evaluation protocol, "outcome-blind same-pool evaluation," is used. Every selector receives the same ordered candidate pool and commits its decision (selected index, mode, contract outcomes, scores, etc.) *before* any outcome labels are available from the simulator. A shared simulator pass then labels all candidates for task success, coordination validity, progress, and failure mode. This protocol allows for clear differentiation between selector quality (how well it chooses) and proposal headroom (the quality of the available candidates). An override is considered beneficial if it repairs a nominal failure without introducing new issues, harmful if it causes a new required outcome to be lost, and false if it changes the action without task or coordination benefit.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of CoWAM was conducted across eight simulated bimanual tasks in RoboTwin 2.0, involving three coordination event families (synchronization, role compatibility, collision convergence). The results cover coordination-valid intervention, natural closed-loop performance, and robustness analyses.

#### 4.1. Coordination-Valid Intervention (Table 1, Figure 3a, 3b)

- **Valid Selection:** CoWAM demonstrated a coordination-valid selection rate of 93.3% (140 out of 150 opportunities), representing a 16.7 percentage-point improvement over the Contract-only variant (76.7%, 115/150). This gain was statistically significant (p = 1.6×10⁻⁶).
- **Intervention Errors:** CoWAM exhibited low error rates, making 5 false interventions and 1 harmful intervention out of 180 matched negatives. In comparison, Future-Consensus, an aggressive reranking baseline, made 112 false and 16 harmful interventions. The RGB-D selector, the strongest non-CoWAM baseline, achieved 84.0% valid selection but with 10 false and 2 harmful interventions.
- **Event Family Consistency:** The improvement in valid selection was consistent across all three event families: CoWAM converted 47 of 50 synchronization, 46 of 50 role-compatibility, and 47 of 50 collision-convergence opportunities, compared to 39, 37, and 39, respectively, for Contract-only. This indicates that the event-conditioned evidence and calibrated gates enable more accurate coordination decisions rather than merely increasing intervention frequency.

#### 4.2. Natural Closed-Loop Performance (Table 2, Figure 3c)

- **Task Success:** CoWAM improved natural closed-loop success to 62.9% (151 out of 240 episodes), compared to 53.3% (128/240) for the Selective Control baseline and 40.0% (96/240) for Policy Top-1. This represents a 9.6 percentage-point gain over the strongest selective baseline and was statistically significant (p = 4.3×10⁻⁴). CoWAM closed 55 of the 80-success gap between the nominal policy and the oracle upper bound (73.3%, 176/240).
- **Coordination Validity in Closed-Loop:** CoWAM achieved 95.4% coordination validity in closed-loop episodes, while limiting false and harmful interventions to 3.3% and 0.4%, respectively.
- **Per-Task Consistency:** Positive gains in success were observed across all eight evaluated tasks, demonstrating broad applicability rather than concentration in specific task types.
- **Failure Reduction:** CoWAM reduced specific failure categories significantly compared to Policy Top-1, including inter-arm collision (from 18 to 6 episodes, 66.7% reduction), role conflict (from 16 to 4, 75.0% reduction), and asynchronous release (from 14 to 4, 71.4% reduction).

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies (Table 3, Figure 4, Figure A4, A5)

- **Contract-only vs. Full CoWAM:** Removing learned verification and the full gate stack (Contract-only) resulted in a 16.7 percentage-point reduction in valid selection, highlighting the value of event-conditioned evidence and calibrated gates.
- **Event Conditioning:** Removing event conditioning reduced validity by 18.7 percentage points, and the Scalar verifier (without event conditioning) reached 69.3% validity, confirming the contribution of obligation-specific evidence.
- **Conservative Gates:** Removing uncertainty bounds increased false interventions from 2.8% to 14.4% and harmful interventions from 0.6% to 3.3%, even with a slight rise in positive selection. Without baseline preservation, these rates rose to 28.9% and 7.8%. These results emphasize that both identifying valid alternatives and conservatively controlling interventions are crucial.
- **Modality Contribution:** The no-depth variant achieved 85.3% validity, indicating a useful contribution from 3D correspondence within the multimodal evidence.
- **Learned Evidence Quality:** Event-conditioned CoWAM achieved 0.92 AUPRC, 0.03 expected calibration error (ECE), and 0.89 pair accuracy for coordination-risk ranking, outperforming the Scalar verifier (0.80 AUPRC, 0.06 ECE, 0.79 pair accuracy) and demonstrating that event structure improves both discrimination and calibration. Shuffling predicted futures reduced AUPRC to 0.63, indicating that the verifier effectively uses candidate-specific temporal evidence.

#### 4.4. Robustness Analysis (Table 4, Figure A3)

- **Candidate Count Scaling:** As the number of candidates (K) increased from 4 to 32, CoWAM consistently rescued 92.9–95.0% of available opportunities, while false interventions remained stable (4-5 states). In contrast, Future-Consensus accumulated significantly more false interventions (34-43 states). This indicates that CoWAM leverages richer proposal pools effectively without increasing intervention error.
- **Cross-Proposer Transfer:** CoWAM demonstrated robust transferability, achieving 10.0–12.5 percentage-point gains in success over the strongest corresponding baselines when using candidate pools from different WAM sources (X-WAM, LeWorldModel, and mixed pools). False and harmful selections remained low (5-7 false, 1 harmful out of 120 pools) across these diverse proposers, suggesting the interface-level reuse of contracts, verifier outputs, and conservative gates.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presented in CoWAM establishes coordination contracts as a structured and effective interface for policy intervention with predicted world-action evidence in bimanual robotic manipulation. This work offers several significant contributions and potential impacts:

1. **Structured Intervention Paradigm:** CoWAM shifts the paradigm from general "plausibility" or "future agreement" of WAM predictions to a structured, auditable system of "coordination contracts." By formalizing synchronization, role compatibility, and collision convergence as explicit obligations, it provides a clear and interpretable framework for decision-making. This structured approach can reduce the ambiguity inherent in purely learned end-to-end intervention systems.

2. **Conservative and Reliable Intervention:** The methodology prioritizes conservative intervention, ensuring that an override occurs only when an alternative satisfies every active obligation, is low-risk, preserves task utility, and offers a clear improvement. The low rates of false and harmful interventions (below 1% in core evaluations and low in closed-loop) indicate a high degree of reliability, which is crucial for deploying robotic systems in real-world scenarios where unintended failures are costly. This contrasts with more aggressive reranking methods that tend to increase intervention errors significantly.

3. **Improved Task Performance and Coordination:** CoWAM demonstrably enhances coordination validity and natural closed-loop task success across a range of bimanual tasks. The observed reductions in inter-arm collision, role conflict, and asynchronous release failures illustrate its direct impact on improving the robot's ability to execute complex bimanual manipulations. This can lead to more robust and capable robotic systems in various applications requiring fine-grained interaction.

4. **Separation of Concerns:** The "outcome-blind same-pool evaluation" protocol is a methodological contribution that effectively separates the quality of action proposals (WAM generation) from the quality of the intervention decision (CoWAM's selection logic). This allows for a more rigorous assessment of intervention strategies and provides insights into the "proposal headroom" available from WAMs versus how much of that headroom is actually leveraged by the intervention layer. This separation can guide future research in both WAM development and intervention design.

5. **Generalizability and Reusability:** The robustness analyses, including performance across varying candidate counts and different WAM sources (X-WAM, LeWorldModel), indicate that CoWAM is not tied to a specific WAM architecture. Its interface-level design, where the same contract fields, verifier outputs, and conservative gates operate on diverse candidate pools, supports reusability. This suggests that CoWAM could serve as a general-purpose intervention layer that can be integrated with future advancements in WAMs and applied to new bimanual tasks by simply instantiating new contracts and calibrating the verifier.

6. **Interpretability and Debuggability:** The conjunctive nature of the intervention gate means that each accepted override has a complete, inspectable reason record. This enhances the interpretability of the robot's decisions, which is valuable for debugging, system auditing, and building trust in autonomous agents.

In summary, CoWAM provides a principled, conservative, and effective mechanism for leveraging predicted futures to enhance coordinated bimanual robotic control. Its emphasis on structured contracts, learned evidence, and calibrated decision-making represents a step towards more robust, reliable, and interpretable robot intelligence in complex manipulation tasks.
