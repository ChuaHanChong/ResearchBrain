# Extended Reality as a Mediation Layer for Situated Human Control in Human-Robot Teaming

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers define Extended Reality (XR) as a mediation layer to enable situated human control in human-robot teaming. Their work provides a conceptual framework, identifying four mediation functions and six design dimensions for creating XR interfaces that support active human influence over robotic actions within real-world contexts.

## Problem

- Robot actions, while technically sound, often lack contextual appropriateness (e.g., social norms, human access) in dynamic human-robot shared environments.
- High-level, multimodal human input for robot control frequently introduces ambiguity regarding intent and scope, leading to misinterpretation by autonomous systems.
- Existing Extended Reality (XR) applications in Human-Robot Interaction (HRI) primarily provide awareness of robot intent, lacking mechanisms for active human control, intervention, and recovery.

## Method

- Propose a conceptual framework defining Extended Reality (XR) as a **"mediation layer for situated human control,"** enabling humans to actively comprehend, influence, authorize, and halt robot actions in context.
- Analyze three distinct human-robot interaction scenarios (==bedside nursing==, ==multi-arm supervisory control==, ==collaborative assembly==) to identify recurring coordination challenges.
- Identify four key ==mediation functions== for XR (e.g., bridging human intent and robot autonomy, robot plans and human judgment) that address identified HRI problems.

## Results

- A conceptual framework was established, reframing Extended Reality (XR) as a **"mediation layer for situated human control"** in human-robot teaming.
- **Four** specific XR mediation functions were identified, detailing how XR can bridge the gap between human and robot activities (e.g., mediating human intent/robot autonomy, robot plans/human judgment).
- **Six** design dimensions were derived from scenario analysis and mediation functions, providing a structured guide for developing XR interfaces that support effective human control and coordination in HRI.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Extended Reality (XR) can serve as a bidirectional "mediation layer" that actively couples human state, robot autonomy, and the shared physical environment, moving beyond passive visualization.
> - Situated human control is crucial for human-robot teaming, allowing humans to assess contextual appropriateness and intervene effectively rather than just observe robot actions.
> - Resolving ambiguity in high-level human input and enabling anticipatory recovery are essential for robust and fluid human-robot collaboration in complex, dynamic settings.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper addresses the evolving landscape of Human-Robot Interaction (HRI), particularly in contexts where humans and robots collaborate in shared physical environments. The current trend in HRI research is increasingly focused on developing effective human-robot teams, where robots operate with varying degrees of autonomy (autonomous, semi-autonomous, or teleoperated). A significant challenge in these dynamic environments is achieving robust coordination, which necessitates "reciprocal anticipation"—humans understanding robot behavior, and robots accounting for human actions, attention, and task state.

Prior research has established Extended Reality (XR) as a valuable tool for HRI, primarily by visualizing robot state, intent, planned motion, reachability, and task progress directly within the workspace. This body of work demonstrates XR's utility in enhancing the spatial legibility of robot behavior (e.g.,). However, the authors identify a gap: while existing XR applications provide awareness of robot intent, many emerging HRI scenarios demand more than mere observation. Human collaborators increasingly need the ability to actively *control* robot actions – to assess their appropriateness, constrain or correct plans, approve execution, and interrupt actions in response to changing situations.

Furthermore, the paper highlights XR's bidirectional potential, moving beyond merely displaying robot-generated information. XR devices can also gather information about the human collaborator, such as head pose, gaze, hand motion, and gestures. This human input, though often uncertain, provides additional evidence that robots can integrate with task context and explicit commands for better interpretation (e.g.,).

This paper positions itself by arguing that XR's role should be elevated to that of a "mediation layer for situated human control." This perspective expands the traditional understanding of robot control beyond direct teleoperation to encompass broader forms of human influence, including supervisory control, constraint specification, plan approval, role handover, and recovery mechanisms. By focusing on *situated human control*, the authors aim to bridge the gap between current XR-HRI work primarily focused on awareness and the growing need for actionable human intervention in complex, dynamic collaborative environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this paper is to propose and define Extended Reality (XR) as a "mediation layer for situated human control" in human-robot teaming, and to derive a conceptual framework for its design and evaluation. The authors define "situated human control" as the human collaborator's capacity to comprehend, influence, authorize, and halt robot actions within the specific physical, social, and temporal context of their unfolding. This concept extends beyond direct manipulation to include supervisory oversight, specifying constraints, approving actions, managing role handovers, and recovering from unforeseen issues.

This theoretical framing is driven by two recurring challenges in contemporary HRI:

1. **Contextual Appropriateness of Robot Plans:** Robot plans are frequently generated based on technical feasibility (e.g., collision avoidance, efficiency). However, a technically sound plan may be inappropriate or problematic within its actual operational context. For instance, a robot trajectory might avoid collisions but obstruct a human's access to a workspace, pass uncomfortably close to a vulnerable patient, violate social norms, or rely on uncertain sensor data. Human collaborators require interfaces that enable them to judge these contextual nuances and intervene accordingly. The motivation is to move beyond mere technical correctness to contextually appropriate and acceptable robot behavior.

2. **Ambiguity of High-Level, Multimodal Human Input:** As HRI advances, human input becomes increasingly high-level and multimodal, incorporating speech, gaze, and gesture for more efficient task specification (e.g., "move this aside"). While efficient, this input often introduces ambiguity regarding referents, human intent, and the precise scope of control. In multi-person environments, such as care teams or industrial settings with supervisors and trainees, this ambiguity is compounded by dynamic role changes and control handovers. The motivation is to reduce ambiguity and ensure that human intent is accurately translated into robot action, especially when commands are given naturally and at a high level.

To address these motivations, the paper makes three specific contributions:
- **Definition of XR Mediation:** To define XR mediation as a bidirectional, situated coupling that connects the state of the human or team, the robot's autonomy, and the shared physical environment.
- **Scenario-Grounded Problem Identification:** To utilize three diverse HRI scenarios (robot-assisted bedside nursing, multi-arm supervisory control, and collaborative assembly under divided attention) to identify common coordination problems, including spatial consequences, ambiguous input, changing plan validity, divided control, and recovery needs.
- **Derivation of Design Dimensions and Research Agenda:** To extract six scenario-grounded design dimensions for XR interfaces that support situated human control, alongside outlining associated research questions and evaluation considerations.

These objectives collectively aim to develop a more nuanced understanding of XR's role in HRI, pushing its application beyond visualization to facilitate active, context-aware human influence over robotic systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The paper employs a conceptual and theoretical methodology, leveraging scenario-based analysis to build a framework rather than conducting empirical studies or presenting novel technical implementations. The core approach involves:

1. **Conceptual Framing:** The authors begin by establishing their central argument: that XR should be understood as a "mediation layer for situated human control." This involves defining "situated human control" and elaborating on the bidirectional nature of XR in this context – transmitting robot information to humans and capturing human input for robots.

2. **Motivating Scenarios:** Three distinct, yet representative, Human-Robot Interaction (HRI) scenarios are introduced to ground the theoretical argument in practical challenges:
 - **Robot-Assisted Bedside Nursing:** This scenario highlights the need for robot actions to be judged not only by technical feasibility but also by social and ethical criteria, such as patient comfort, nurse access, and caregiver authority in a multi-person care environment.
 - **Multi-Arm Supervisory Control:** This scenario emphasizes the challenges of ambiguous multimodal input (speech, gaze, gesture) when controlling multiple robots remotely, and the need for legible transitions between high-level instruction and precise control.
 - **Collaborative Assembly Under Divided Attention:** This scenario focuses on situations where humans have divided attention and require low-effort intervention mechanisms to anticipate and recover from misalignments before they become failures.
 These scenarios serve to illustrate recurring coordination breakdowns that current XR-HRI approaches might not fully address and to demonstrate the necessity for situated human control.

3. **Identification of Mediation Functions:** Drawing upon the common problems identified across these scenarios, the paper systematically distinguishes four "mediation functions" of XR. These functions describe the specific ways XR can bridge the gap between human and robot activity:
 - **Human Intent and Robot Autonomy:** How XR helps robots interpret ambiguous human commands and externalizes the robot's interpretation for human confirmation.
 - **Robot Plans and Human Judgment:** How XR contextualizes robot plans, allowing humans to evaluate them against criteria beyond technical feasibility (e.g., social appropriateness, comfort, access).
 - **Levels of Shared Control:** How XR makes the current mode of human-robot control legible and supports seamless transitions between different levels of involvement (e.g., supervisory, direct manipulation).
 - **Team Roles, Handover, and Recovery:** How XR manages control rights in multi-person teams, facilitates explicit handover, and supports the revision of robot actions when circumstances change.
 For each function, the authors detail the information flow, XR-specific advantages, example evaluation approaches, and relationships to subsequent design dimensions.

4. **Derivation of Design Dimensions:** Based on the four mediation functions and the recurring problems identified in the motivating scenarios, the paper synthesizes six "design dimensions." These dimensions represent critical areas for the design and evaluation of XR interfaces aimed at supporting situated human control:
 - Joint Action Possibilities
 - Socio-Physical Constraints
 - Uncertainty, Plan Validity, and Situational Awareness
 - Multimodal Control and Correction
 - Roles, Handover, and Accountability
 - Anticipatory Recovery
 Each dimension is described in detail, highlighting specific considerations and open research questions.

5. **Outline of Research Agenda:** Implicit throughout the discussion of design dimensions, and explicit in the concluding sections, the paper outlines a research agenda. This agenda suggests areas for future work, including the development of compact representations for joint action, methods for visualizing qualitative appropriateness, techniques for actionable uncertainty cues, novel multimodal interaction vocabularies, dynamic authority models, and systems that support anticipatory recovery. Evaluation considerations, emphasizing scenario-based and longitudinal studies, are also presented.

In summary, the methodology is a structured, conceptual analysis that progresses from identifying a broad research gap in HRI to proposing a theoretical framework, grounding it with illustrative scenarios, dissecting its constituent functions, and finally translating these into actionable design principles and a future research roadmap for XR in human-robot teaming.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper presents a conceptual framework rather than empirical results, primarily identifying and structuring key components for understanding and designing XR in human-robot teaming. The main findings and results can be summarized as follows:

1. **Redefinition of XR's Role in HRI:** The paper establishes XR not merely as a visualization tool for robot intent, but as a "mediation layer for situated human control." This is a central conceptual shift, arguing that XR's primary utility lies in enabling humans to understand, shape, authorize, and interrupt robot actions within their concrete physical, social, and temporal contexts.

2. **Identification of Core HRI Challenges through Scenarios:** Through the analysis of three distinct scenarios—robot-assisted bedside nursing, multi-arm supervisory control, and collaborative assembly under divided attention—the authors identify recurring challenges in human-robot teaming. These include:
 - Robot actions that are technically feasible but contextually inappropriate or unsafe.
 - The inherent ambiguity of high-level, multimodal human instructions.
 - The need for dynamic adjustments to robot plans as situations change.
 - The complexities of control and responsibility in multi-human, multi-robot environments.

3. **Four Identified XR Mediation Functions:** The paper distills the complex interplay between humans, robots, and the environment into four key functions that XR can fulfill to enable situated human control:
 - **Mediating Between Human Intent and Robot Autonomy:** XR provides spatial context for interpreting ambiguous multimodal human input (e.g., gaze, gesture) and externalizes the robot's inferred interpretations (e.g., selected referent, planned trajectory) for confirmation or correction *before* execution.
 - **Mediating Between Robot Plans and Human Judgment:** XR grounds robot plans and their anticipated consequences (e.g., blocked access, proximity to sensitive areas, timing conflicts) within the shared environment, allowing humans to assess their appropriateness against a broader set of criteria (social, ergonomic, ethical) beyond just technical feasibility.
 - **Mediating Between Levels of Shared Control:** XR makes the current control mode (e.g., autonomous, supervised, direct manipulation) and the transitions between them explicit and legible in the workspace, reducing mode confusion and enabling deliberate shifts in human involvement.
 - **Mediating Team Roles, Handover, and Recovery:** XR visualizes active operators, control scope, pending approvals, and override mechanisms in multi-person teams, facilitating explicit handover of control and supporting proactive recovery from misalignments or plan invalidation during execution.

4. **Six Derived Design Dimensions for XR Interfaces:** Building on the mediation functions, the paper derives six specific design dimensions that serve as a framework for developing and evaluating XR systems for situated human control:
 - **Joint Action Possibilities:** Interfaces should represent what the *team* can achieve collaboratively, rather than just individual robot capabilities.
 - **Socio-Physical Constraints:** XR should visualize non-collision constraints such as personal spaces, access zones, and comfort levels.
 - **Uncertainty, Plan Validity, and Situational Awareness:** XR needs to communicate the reliability of robot assumptions, identify where human attention or correction is required due to uncertainty, and support comprehensive situational awareness.
 - **Multimodal Control and Correction:** XR requires interaction vocabularies that match diverse human input modalities (speech, gaze, gesture, direct manipulation) to specific control acts (specifying intent, resolving references, adjusting paths).
 - **Roles, Handover, and Accountability:** Interfaces must dynamically represent control authority, scope, and facilitate explicit handover to ensure clarity and accountability in multi-human teams.
 - **Anticipatory Recovery:** XR should enable proactive intervention by visualizing emerging conflicts, timing mismatches, and potential interference points, allowing for low-latency revision of robot actions before failures occur.

These findings collectively provide a structured conceptual framework for researchers and designers to approach XR-enhanced human-robot teaming, shifting the focus towards robust and contextually aware human intervention and control.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The significance and potential impact of this work are substantial, primarily by offering a new conceptual lens and a structured framework for the design and evaluation of Extended Reality (XR) in Human-Robot Interaction (HRI).

1. **Shifting the HRI Paradigm:** The paper's most significant contribution is its re-conceptualization of XR's role in HRI, moving from merely providing "awareness of robot intent" to enabling "situated human control." This paradigm shift emphasizes human agency, empowering collaborators to actively shape, authorize, interrupt, and recover robot actions in context. This can lead to more intuitive, trustworthy, and effective human-robot teams, as humans are granted more meaningful influence over autonomous systems.

2. **Comprehensive Design and Evaluation Framework:** The derived four mediation functions and six design dimensions provide a robust and actionable framework for researchers and developers. This framework can guide the systematic design of XR interfaces that address critical aspects of human-robot coordination, such as ambiguity resolution, contextual judgment, dynamic control transitions, and multi-user management. For evaluators, the framework suggests specific metrics beyond task completion and usability, including plan comprehension, detection of inappropriate actions, intervention latency, trust calibration, and authority clarity, thereby promoting more rigorous and relevant assessments of XR-HRI systems.

3. **Addressing Real-World HRI Challenges:** By grounding its arguments in practical scenarios like robot-assisted bedside nursing, multi-arm supervisory control, and collaborative assembly, the paper directly addresses complex challenges prevalent in critical domains. In healthcare, it highlights the need for robot actions to be socially appropriate and respectful of patient dignity, while in industry, it focuses on efficiency, safety, and managing cognitive load. The framework offers pathways to solutions for these intricate problems, potentially leading to safer, more comfortable, and more productive collaborative environments.

4. **Enhancing Human Agency and Accountability:** The emphasis on making robot autonomy inspectable, adjustable, and accountable directly enhances human agency. By clarifying who has control, what is being approved, and what the potential consequences are, the framework can foster a sense of shared responsibility and trust, which is crucial for the successful integration of autonomous robots into human workflows. This can help prevent scenarios where humans feel out of control or are unable to intervene effectively.

5. **Guiding Future XR Research and Development:** The paper outlines a clear research agenda, pointing to specific areas requiring further investigation, such as developing compact visualizations for joint action possibilities, encoding socio-physical constraints, creating actionable uncertainty cues, and designing novel multimodal interaction vocabularies. This serves as a roadmap for future work in XR-HRI, encouraging innovation in interaction techniques, representation methods, and robust system architectures that leverage XR's unique capabilities (spatial grounding, embodied interaction, mobility).

6. **Fostering Proactive Conflict Resolution:** The concept of "anticipatory recovery" is particularly impactful, shifting the focus from reactive error correction to proactive mitigation of misalignments. By enabling users to identify and correct potential issues before they escalate into failures, the framework contributes to more resilient and fluid human-robot teamwork, minimizing disruptions and maximizing efficiency.

In essence, this paper provides a foundational theoretical contribution that can significantly influence the trajectory of HRI research and practice. By clearly articulating the necessity and mechanisms for situated human control through XR, it aims to facilitate the development of more intelligent, human-centric, and ultimately, more effective human-robot collaborative systems.
