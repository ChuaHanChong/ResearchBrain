# Legged Robot State Estimation using Invariant Kalman Filtering and Learned Contact Events

> [!summary] Summary
> A learning-based contact estimator for legged robots that removes the need for dedicated force/contact sensors. A 1D-CNN takes a 150-step window of joint encoder, IMU, and kinematics data and classifies the 16 possible four-leg contact states. Estimated contacts feed a contact-aided invariant EKF, giving ~97.8% per-leg accuracy and odometry comparable to visual SLAM across eight terrains.

## Problem

- Proprioceptive state estimators need reliable contact data, but many legged robots lack dedicated contact or force sensors and adding them forces hardware redesign.
- Model-based ground-reaction-force thresholding and generalized-momentum detectors give noisy estimates on lightweight robots and still need empirically tuned thresholds or cut-off frequencies.
- Prior learned or probabilistic detectors assume end-effector wrench sensors, gait periodicity, or simple regression, so they saturate with data and fail to generalize across gaits and terrains.

## Method

- A ==1D convolutional contact network==: two blocks of paired ==Conv1D== layers with dropout plus max pooling, then three fully connected layers, classifying a 150x54 window into 16 binary contact states via ==cross-entropy loss==.
- Multi-modal proprioceptive input stacking joint angles, joint velocities, IMU linear acceleration and angular rate, plus ==forward-kinematic== foot positions and velocities in the hip frame, windowed over time.
- A ==self-supervised labeling== algorithm that low-pass filters foot height and connects local minima between peaks, removing touchdown bounce; labels feed a ==contact-aided invariant EKF== on the SE_{L+2}(3) state.

## Results

- The network reaches **93.88%** accuracy on all **16** joint contact states and **97.82%** per leg, with **1.82%** false-positive and **2.88%** false-negative rates across eight terrains.
- It beats GRF thresholding (**71.30%**) and gait-cycle detection (**85.11%**) on the concrete loop at **98.18%** accuracy, cutting the false-positive rate from **37.07%** and **22.95%** to **1.45%**.
- Ablations show **2** blocks beat **1** block, **4** blocks, and Conv-Pool-Conv-Pool; inference runs at **830 Hz** on a Jetson AGX Xavier after TensorRT, above the **500 Hz** encoder rate.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Physical sensors can often be replaced by learned inference over sensors a robot already carries, when enough multi-modal proprioceptive data is windowed over time.
> - For state estimation, false positives matter far more than false negatives: a wrongly asserted contact breaks the no-slip constraint and injects drift into the filter.
> - Self-supervised labels derived from kinematic signal geometry trade some accuracy for scalability yet preserve the low false-positive rate that downstream filters depend on.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Accurate state estimation is a fundamental requirement for the autonomous operation of legged robots, enabling tasks such as navigation, mapping, and stable control in diverse and challenging environments. The broader research landscape for legged robot state estimation primarily divides into vision-based and proprioceptive approaches.

Vision-based methods, such as visual SLAM, rely on cameras to estimate a robot's pose and build maps. While effective in well-lit and feature-rich environments, these systems can degrade or fail entirely in perceptually challenging conditions, including darkness, fog, snowstorms, or areas with poor illumination and texture.

Proprioceptive state estimators, in contrast, utilize onboard sensors that measure the robot's internal state, such as Inertial Measurement Units (IMUs) for acceleration and angular velocity, and joint encoders for leg kinematics. These systems are robust to visual degradation and can operate at high update rates (e.g., 100-2000 Hz), providing essential real-time odometry for control and high-level planning. A critical component of many proprioceptive state estimators, particularly for legged robots, is accurate contact event detection, which informs leg odometry by identifying when a foot is in firm contact with the ground, thereby enabling the application of no-slip constraints.

Existing methods for contact detection in legged robots exhibit various limitations:
- **Model-based approaches** often estimate Ground Reaction Forces (GRF) from robot dynamics. While these methods can detect touchdown events, the estimated GRF can be noisy, especially for lightweight robots, and often requires empirical thresholds for contact detection. Generalized Momentum (GM) methods attempt to mitigate noise but still necessitate empirical cut-off frequencies.
- **Probabilistic methods**, such as Hidden Markov Models (HMM), aim to estimate contact states by fusing dynamics and kinematics. These often focus on early contact detection for stability control rather than precise contact intervals for long-term state estimation across varied terrains. Some approaches combine GM-based methods with probabilistic representations, but may assume periodic gait phases, leading to performance degradation on uneven terrains.
- **Data-driven approaches** leverage machine learning. Earlier methods include logistic regression for learning GRF thresholds, but these tend to saturate in performance with larger datasets and may require specific training for different gaits, loading conditions, or terrains. More complex methods like fuzzy C-means clustering or Gaussian Mixture Models for gait phase estimation have been proposed, but often assume the availability of specialized sensors (e.g., contact wrench sensors or additional IMUs at end-effectors) or have primarily been tested in simulation.

The current work addresses the gap in proprioceptive state estimation by developing a method that:
1. Bypasses the need for dedicated physical contact sensors, which are often absent on many legged robots and challenging to integrate without hardware redesign.
2. Utilizes readily available multi-modal proprioceptive data (joint encoders, IMU, kinematics).
3. Employs a deep learning approach designed to generalize across different terrains and gaits, overcoming the limitations of simpler data-driven models.
4. Integrates this learned contact information into a robust, contact-aided invariant Extended Kalman Filter (InEKF) to provide accurate odometry, comparable to or exceeding state-of-the-art visual SLAM systems in certain conditions.

By focusing on learning contact events from standard proprioceptive sensors and integrating this with an established invariant filtering framework, this research contributes to enhancing the robustness and autonomy of legged robots in environments where traditional vision-based or sensor-dependent methods are insufficient.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust and reliable state estimation system for legged robots that can operate effectively in environments where vision-based methods may fail. This objective is motivated by the inherent limitations of visual state estimators, which are susceptible to failure in conditions such as low light, fog, or snow, all of which are common in extreme or hazardous environments where legged robots could be deployed for scientific exploration or rescue missions.

A core challenge in proprioceptive state estimation for legged robots lies in accurately determining ground contact events. Leg odometry, a vital component of proprioceptive state estimators, relies on reliable kinematic and contact information. However, many legged robots are not equipped with dedicated contact sensors, and retrofitting such sensors often requires significant hardware redesign, which can be impractical or costly. Existing model-based and simpler data-driven contact detection methods often suffer from issues like noise, reliance on empirical thresholds, or limited generalizability across varied terrains and gaits.

To address these challenges, the authors outline several specific objectives:

1. **Develop a learning-based contact estimator:** The main motivation is to create a system that can accurately detect contact events without requiring dedicated physical contact sensors. This estimator is designed to leverage multi-modal proprioceptive sensory data (joint encoders, IMU, kinematics) that are commonly available on legged robots. The deep learning approach is intended to overcome the limitations of traditional model-based or simpler data-driven methods, particularly in generalizing to different terrains and robot gaits.
2. **Create and open-source comprehensive contact datasets:** To facilitate the development and evaluation of learning-based contact estimators, a significant objective was to collect and release high-quality, diverse contact datasets. These datasets, recorded using an MIT Mini Cheetah robot across eight different terrains and various gaits, include proprioceptive sensor measurements and self-supervised ground truth contact labels. This contribution is aimed at supporting broader research in the field.
3. **Integrate the contact estimator with a robust state estimation framework:** The goal is to demonstrate the practical utility of the learned contact events by fusing them with IMU and joint encoder data within a contact-aided invariant extended Kalman filter (InEKF). This integration aims to produce accurate and robust odometry trajectories. A specific contribution is the development of a quadruped-specific version of the InEKF compatible with the Lightweight Communications and Marshalling (LCM) interface.
4. **Provide experimental validation across diverse conditions:** A critical objective is to rigorously evaluate the proposed system's performance on a physical robot (MIT Mini Cheetah) across a variety of terrains. The validation seeks to demonstrate that the resulting proprioceptive odometry is comparable to state-of-the-art visual SLAM systems, thus establishing its capability as a reliable alternative or complementary system, especially in visually degraded environments. Furthermore, a secondary objective is to demonstrate the generalizability of the framework to different robot platforms, specifically by conducting experiments on a Cassie-series bipedal robot.

In essence, the research is motivated by the need for robust, sensor-agnostic, and terrain-generalizable state estimation for legged robots, achieved by developing a deep learning approach to infer critical contact information from readily available proprioceptive sensors.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology developed in this work integrates a deep learning-based contact estimator with a contact-aided Invariant Extended Kalman Filter (InEKF) for robust legged robot state estimation.

#### 3.1. State Estimation Framework: Invariant Extended Kalman Filter (InEKF)

The core state estimator is a contact-aided Invariant Extended Kalman Filter, which operates on the Lie group SE$_{L+2}$(3). This framework is designed to handle the nonlinear nature of robot state estimation while maintaining consistency.

- **State Representation:** The robot's state X$_{t}$ at time t is defined as (R$_{t}$, v$_{t}$, p$_{t}$, d$_{lt}$), where:
 - R$_{t}$ ∈ SO(3) is the orientation of the body (IMU) frame in the world frame.
 - v$_{t}$ ∈ ℝ³ is the linear velocity of the body in the world frame.
 - p$_{t}$ ∈ ℝ³ is the position of the body in the world frame.
 - d$_{lt}$ ∈ ℝ³ represents the position of a contact foot in the world frame. When a leg `l` detects contact, its foot position d$_{lt}$ is augmented into the state, imposing a no-slip (zero-velocity) condition. When contact breaks, d$_{lt}$ is marginalized from the state, allowing foot movement. L denotes the number of active contacts, with L ≤ 4 for a quadruped.
- **Process Model:** The InEKF propagates the state using IMU measurements (angular velocity ˜ω$_{t}$ and linear acceleration ˜a$_{t}$), which are modeled with white Gaussian noise. To account for small foot slippage during contact, the contact velocity in the body frame is also modeled with white Gaussian noise. The differential equations for R$_{t}$, v$_{t}$, p$_{t}$, and d$_{t}$ (for a contact foot) are derived based on these assumptions and forward kinematics.
- **Measurement Model:** Joint encoder measurements ˜α$_{t}$ are used in a right-invariant model. The key assumption during contact is that the contact point remains fixed in the world frame. This leads to a measurement model where the foot position relative to the body, calculated via forward kinematics h$_{p}$(˜α$_{t}$), can be related to the body pose and the world-frame contact point d$_{t}$.

#### 3.2. Contact Estimation: Deep Learning Approach

The central novelty is the deep learning network for predicting contact events, eliminating the need for physical contact sensors.

- **Problem Formulation:** Contact state for each of the four legs (RF, LF, RH, LH) is defined as c$_{l}$ ∈ {0, 1} (0 for no contact, 1 for firm contact). The combined contact state C is a 4-element binary vector. This leads to 2⁴ = 16 possible contact states. The problem is formulated as a 16-class classification task, mapping C to an integer S ∈ {0..., 15} via binary-to-decimal conversion.
- **Input Data Preprocessing:** The network takes multi-modal proprioceptive sensor measurements as input. For each time step `n`, a 54 × 1 vector z$_{n}$ is formed by concatenating:
 - Joint angles q$_{n}$ (12 values).
 - Joint velocities ˙q$_{n}$ (12 values).
 - IMU linear accelerations a$_{n}$ (3 values).
 - IMU angular velocities ω$_{n}$ (3 values).
 - Foot positions p$_{f n}$ (12 values from forward kinematics in hip frame).
 - Foot velocities v$_{f n}$ (12 values from forward kinematics in hip frame).
 To capture temporal dynamics, a window of `w` (set to 150) previous measurements is used, forming a 2D array D$_{n}$ (w × 54) as input to the network. Input data is normalized along the time domain.
- **Network Architecture:** The proposed network (Figure 2, Appendix Table 5) consists of:
 - **Two blocks of convolutions:** Each block comprises two one-dimensional convolution layers (with ReLU activation and dropout for the second layer) followed by a one-dimensional max-pooling layer. This structure is designed to extract deep features from the time-series input.
 - **Three fully connected layers:** These layers process the features from the convolutional blocks, with dropout applied to the first two, to produce the final 16-class output.
 - **Loss Function:** Cross-entropy loss is used for the classification task.
- **Contact Data Set Generation:**
 - **Robot and Data Collection:** An MIT Mini Cheetah robot was used to collect approximately 1,000,000 data points across eight diverse terrains (asphalt, concrete, forest, grass, pebbles, rock road, sidewalk) and "in-air" sequences. Sensor data includes IMU (1000 Hz) and other proprioceptive measurements (500 Hz, upsampled to 1000 Hz).
 - **Ground Truth Labeling (Self-supervised, Offline):** Ground truth contact labels were generated using an algorithm (Algorithm 1 in Appendix) that processes the robot's foot height in the hip frame. This algorithm applies a low-pass filter to the foot height signal and identifies contact intervals by connecting local minima between peaks. It specifically handles and removes "bouncing" events upon touchdown, where the foot might briefly lose and regain contact, by setting a conservative contact start if only one local minimum is found between two peaks.
 - **Ground Truth Trajectories:** For evaluating the InEKF, ground truth trajectories were obtained from a motion capture system (for grass terrains) or from a state-of-the-art visual SLAM system (ORB SLAM2) (for other terrains) as a proxy.
- **Experimental Procedure:**
 - **Training:** The collected data was split into 70% training, 15% validation, and 15% testing sets. Training involved a batch size of 30, a learning rate of 10⁻⁴, and 30 epochs (with convergence typically around 7 epochs). Training was performed on an NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU.
 - **Evaluation:** The contact estimator's performance was evaluated using accuracy (for 16 states and individual legs), False Positive Rate (FPR), and False Negative Rate (FNR). The InEKF's performance with learned contacts was compared qualitatively against baselines using GRF thresholding and gait cycle information, and against ground truth contacts.
 - **Generalizability Test:** The framework was also applied to a Cassie-series bipedal robot, training a similar network using ground truth labels from spring deflections (physical sensor) and kinematics-generated labels to assess applicability across different platforms and labeling methods.
 - **Ablation Study:** Different network architectures were tested to understand the impact of convolutional block count and structure on performance.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several key findings regarding the performance of the learning-based contact estimator, its integration with the InEKF, and its generalizability.

#### 4.1. Contact Estimation Performance (Mini Cheetah)

- **High Accuracy:** The trained deep learning network achieved an average accuracy of 93.88% in classifying the 16 combined contact states across all four legs. When evaluated for individual leg contact, the average accuracy reached 97.82%.
- **Low Error Rates:** The method demonstrated an average False Positive Rate (FPR) of 1.82% and a False Negative Rate (FNR) of 2.88% on the test sets. The ability to distinguish "in-air" gaits (where the robot walks without ground contact) from actual ground contact was confirmed with 100% accuracy in specific air trotting and pronking datasets.
- **Terrain Generalization:** The network maintained high accuracy (above 96% for each leg individually) across all eight diverse terrains, including concrete, forest, grass, and various pebble and road types (Table 6 in Appendix). This indicates robustness to varied ground conditions.

#### 4.2. Comparison of Contact Detection Methods

- **Superiority to Baselines:** The proposed deep learning method consistently outperformed traditional contact detection methods:
 - **GRF Thresholding:** Showed the lowest accuracy (e.g., 71.30% on concrete) and high FPR (e.g., 37.07% on concrete), making it unreliable for state estimation.
 - **Gait Cycle:** Performed better than GRF thresholding (e.g., 85.11% on concrete) but still significantly lower than the proposed method, and exhibited higher FPR (e.g., 22.95% on concrete). The gait cycle method also failed to remove false positives when the gait cycle was violated, a common issue on uneven terrains.
- **Crucial FPR Reduction:** The proposed method's significantly lower FPR is highlighted as critical for state estimation tasks, where false positives (incorrectly detecting contact) can lead to severe errors in odometry by enforcing invalid no-slip constraints.

#### 4.3. Contact-Aided Invariant Extended Kalman Filter (InEKF) Performance

- **Accurate and Smoother Trajectories:** When integrated with the proposed contact estimator, the InEKF produced odometry trajectories that qualitatively exhibited smaller drifts and were smoother compared to trajectories generated using baseline contact detection methods (GRF thresholding or gait cycle). Specifically, baselines resulted in noticeable height (Y or Z axis) drifts and zig-zagging patterns (Figure 4, 8, 9).
- **Complementary Role:** The proprioceptive state estimator, utilizing the learned contacts, demonstrated its robustness by providing reliable odometry in scenarios where a state-of-the-art visual SLAM system (ORB SLAM2) failed (Figure 10). This confirms its potential as a complementary or primary system in perceptually degraded environments.
- **Odometry Across Unseen Terrains:** The InEKF, using the proposed contact estimator, successfully estimated robot pose across five test terrains (forest, small/middle pebbles, sidewalk/asphalt road) that were not included in the training process, further demonstrating generalization capabilities (Figures 10, 11, 12 in Appendix).

#### 4.4. Cassie-Series Robot Experiment (Generalizability)

- **Framework Adaptability:** The framework successfully adapted to a Cassie-series bipedal robot, indicating its generalizability beyond quadrupedal platforms.
- **Effectiveness of Self-Supervised Labels:** When trained with ground truth contacts derived from spring deflections (a physical sensor), the network achieved over 98% accuracy. Crucially, when trained solely with kinematics-generated labels (the self-supervised method described in the paper), it still achieved over 92% accuracy while maintaining a low FPR. This validates the practical utility of the self-supervised labeling process for generating training data in the absence of dedicated physical sensors.

#### 4.5. Ablation Study and Runtime

- **Optimal Architecture:** The ablation study showed that the proposed 2-block convolutional network architecture yielded the best balance of accuracy and error rates among tested configurations (Table 4).
- **Real-time Performance:** The contact estimator achieved an inference speed of approximately 830 Hz on an NVIDIA Jetson AGX Xavier (on-robot hardware) after TensorRT optimization. This speed is sufficient for real-time operation, as the Mini Cheetah's encoder measurements are sampled at 500 Hz. The InEKF runs at 2000 Hz on an Intel i7-8750H CPU.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work presents a significant advancement in legged robot state estimation, offering solutions to critical challenges faced in real-world autonomous deployments. The significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Enabling Robust Autonomy in Challenging Environments:** The primary impact is enabling legged robots to achieve robust and accurate state estimation in perceptually degraded environments (e.g., darkness, fog, unstructured terrains) where vision-based systems typically struggle or fail. By relying solely on proprioceptive data and learned contact events, robots can maintain situational awareness, which is crucial for tasks like scientific exploration, search and rescue, or industrial inspection in hazardous scenes.
- **Reduced Hardware Dependency and Cost:** The developed deep learning contact estimator eliminates the need for dedicated physical contact sensors, which are often expensive, add complexity, and may require significant hardware redesign for integration. This approach lowers the entry barrier for developing and deploying capable legged robots, making them more accessible and versatile.
- **Enhanced Generalizability and Adaptability:** The deep learning approach, trained on diverse datasets and validated across multiple terrains and robot platforms (quadruped Mini Cheetah and bipedal Cassie), demonstrates strong generalization capabilities. This suggests the framework can be broadly applied to various legged robots without extensive retraining for every new terrain or gait, thereby improving adaptability and reducing deployment effort.
- **Improved Odometry Accuracy and Smoothness:** The integration of the learned contact events into a contact-aided InEKF yields more accurate and smoother odometry trajectories compared to traditional contact detection methods. This improvement is vital for maintaining stable control, executing precise planned policies, and generating reliable inputs for localization and mapping tasks. The low false positive rate of contact detection is particularly impactful, as it prevents the imposition of erroneous no-slip constraints that can destabilize state estimation.
- **Open-Source Contributions to Research:** The release of open-source contact datasets and software for the contact estimator and InEKF is a valuable contribution to the robotics community. These resources can accelerate further research and development in legged robot state estimation, fostering innovation and enabling comparative studies.
- **Foundation for Future Advancements:** The work lays a foundation for future research directions, such as modeling the "quality" of contact and estimating its covariance online. Integrating such uncertainty estimates directly into the state estimator could further enhance accuracy and robustness. Additionally, exploring multi-task learning for foot-specific contact prediction could lead to more nuanced and flexible contact estimation.
- **Bridging the Gap between Learning and Filtering:** This research successfully bridges advancements in deep learning with established principles of invariant filtering, demonstrating how data-driven insights can enhance the performance of model-based state estimators. This synergy is likely to be influential in other areas of robot perception and control.

In summary, this work provides a practical, robust, and generalizable solution for a fundamental challenge in legged robot autonomy, with the potential to significantly expand the operational capabilities of these robots in a wide range of real-world applications.
