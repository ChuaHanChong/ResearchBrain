# Beyond GRPO and On-Policy Distillation: An Empirical Sparse-to-Dense Reward Principle for Language-Model Post-Training

> [!summary] Summary
> An empirical "reward-density principle" is proposed for LLM post-training, advocating a "teacher-first" data allocation strategy to efficiently utilize scarce labeled data. This approach trains a stronger teacher with sparse rewards, then distills its behavior to a smaller student via a two-stage dense bridge, enabling the student to achieve higher task performance than direct sparse reinforcement learning.

## Problem

- The scarcity of labeled, verifiable training data poses a significant bottleneck for effective post-training of large language models (LLMs).
- Directly applying sparse, sequence-level rewards to smaller, less capable student models often leads to inefficient data utilization and poor learning due to exploration challenges and collapsed gradient signals.
- Traditional knowledge distillation methods may suffer from coverage mismatch issues when transferring complex behaviors from a strong teacher to a "cold" student model.

## Method

- A ==reward-density principle== is introduced, suggesting ==sparse rewards== are best for "discovery" on strong teacher models, while ==dense rewards== are ideal for "compression" into student models.
- A ==multi-stage post-training pipeline== is proposed: (1) teacher-side ==sparse reinforcement learning== on labeled data, (2) a ==two-stage dense bridge== (==Forward-KL warmup== followed by ==On-Policy Distillation==) to transfer teacher behavior to the student, and (3) optional post-bridge student sparse RL.
- This ==teacher-first allocation strategy== prioritizes using scarce labeled data on the most capable model to learn reward-shaped behavior, then efficiently transferring this knowledge downstream.

## Results

- The teacher-first allocation strategy (RL-shaped 8B teacher distilled to a 1.7B student) achieved **79.3% MATH accuracy**, outperforming direct student-side GRPO (**75.9% MATH**) on the same student model.
- Reward-shaping the teacher is crucial; distillation from raw or SFT-trained teachers consistently underperformed or marginally improved over direct student GRPO, indicating that teacher scale alone is insufficient.
- The two-stage FKL-to-OPD dense bridge consistently surpassed single-stage distillation methods (**79.3% vs. 77.6%** for OPD-only on MATH accuracy), and subsequent sparse student RL on new data further improved performance from **75.4% to 78.5%** MATH accuracy.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Sparse, sequence-level rewards are most productive when applied to stronger models capable of effective exploration and deriving informative gradients, making them suitable for teacher-side "discovery."
> - Dense, token-level rewards derived from a reward-shaped teacher are highly efficient for "compression," enabling smaller student models to acquire complex behaviors without facing long-horizon credit assignment problems.
> - The two-stage dense bridge (FKL then OPD) is critical for effective knowledge transfer, with FKL mitigating initial coverage mismatch and OPD providing robust on-policy supervision for the student.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in this paper is situated within the field of large language model (LLM) post-training, specifically addressing the challenge of efficiently improving LLM performance on verifiable tasks. A central constraint in this area is the scarcity of labeled, verifiable training data. Traditional post-training approaches often involve supervised fine-tuning (SFT) or reinforcement learning (RL) methods like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) or Generalized Reinforcement Policy Optimization (GRPO) applied directly to the deployment model. These methods typically use sparse, sequence-level rewards obtained at the end of a generated response.

Another prominent approach is knowledge distillation, where a larger, more capable "teacher" model transfers its knowledge to a smaller "student" model through dense, token-level supervision. This includes methods like teacher-sample SFT, where the student imitates teacher-generated trajectories, and on-policy distillation (OPD), where the student learns from teacher signals on its own generated trajectories. Recent advancements have explored combining these paradigms, such as DeepSeek-R1 demonstrating that RL-improved teachers can guide smaller models, and self-distillation techniques converting sparse rewards into dense supervision.

This paper positions itself by reframing the problem not as a choice between competing post-training algorithms, but as an *allocation problem* concerning scarce labeled data. It investigates which model (teacher or student) should receive this data, at what signal density (sparse or dense), and in what order, aiming to optimize the overall training pipeline rather than a single component in isolation. This perspective differentiates it from prior work that primarily focuses on improving individual algorithms or distillation techniques without explicitly considering the optimal allocation strategy for limited verifiable data.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the observation that labeled, verifiable training data constitutes a bottleneck in the post-training of large language models. While pretraining text and teacher rollouts can be scaled with computational resources, obtaining examples with checkable answers and reliable graders for verifiable tasks (such as mathematical reasoning) is a labor-intensive process. This scarcity necessitates an efficient strategy for utilizing each available labeled example.

The paper's key objective is to propose and empirically validate a "reward-density principle" and an associated four-stage workflow for allocating this scarce labeled data. The principle posits that:
- **Sparse sequence-level reward** is most effectively utilized by models with a greater capacity for exploration and discovery, typically larger "teacher" models. This process helps shape the teacher's behavior towards high-reward trajectories.
- **Dense token-level teacher supervision** is better suited for compressing the discovered behavior into smaller "deployment" models, serving as an efficient form of knowledge transfer.

Based on this principle, the research proposes a specific allocation rule:
1. Allocate scarce labeled data "upstream" to the strongest available teacher model for reinforcement learning (RL-based discovery).
2. Transfer this reward-shaped behavior "downstream" to the deployment student model via a dense, token-level bridge.
3. Optionally, use any remaining labeled data for further sparse-reward RL on the student *after* it has been prepared by the dense bridge.

The paper articulates three specific contributions that serve as its objectives:
1. **Teacher-first allocation:** To demonstrate that, for a fixed deployment-student size and labeled data pool, allocating data to teacher RL followed by dense transfer yields a stronger student compared to direct student RL. This also involves showing that teacher quality, specifically being reward-shaped, is critical, as transfer from a raw (non-RL-trained) teacher underperforms direct student GRPO.
2. **A two-stage dense bridge:** To establish that a combined forward-KL (FKL) warmup on teacher rollouts followed by on-policy distillation (OPD) on student rollouts is more effective than single-stage distillation methods (e.g., teacher-sample SFT or OPD-only). The FKL warmup is motivated by the need to reduce the student's coverage mismatch with the teacher, thereby better conditioning the subsequent OPD stage.
3. **Post-bridge student RL:** To investigate whether sparse-reward RL on the student, applied after the dense bridge, can further enhance performance and if this improvement is attributable to new labeled data rather than simply additional updates on already-seen data.

Overall, the motivation is to develop a more effective and principled approach to LLM post-training that maximizes the utility of limited verifiable data by strategically distributing learning signals based on their density and the capabilities of the models involved.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research proposes and evaluates a four-stage workflow designed to implement the reward-density principle for LLM post-training. This workflow utilizes a fixed pool of labeled training data (D), a deployment student model ($\pi_\theta$), and a larger teacher model ($\pi_T$) sharing the student's tokenizer.

#### 3.1. The Four-Stage Workflow

1. **Stage 1: Teacher-side sparse-reward RL.** The teacher model ($\pi_T$) undergoes sparse-reward reinforcement learning, specifically using GRPO, on the labeled dataset D. The objective is to shape the teacher's policy ($\pi_T$) to concentrate on high-reward trajectories, approximating a reward-shaped target distribution ($\pi^*_R$). This stage addresses the "optimality" condition (C1) for effective dense transfer.
2. **Stage 2a: Forward-KL warmup on teacher rollouts.** The student model ($\pi_\theta$) is trained to minimize the forward KL divergence from the teacher on states sampled from the teacher's distribution. Specifically, $L_F(\theta) = E_{s \sim d_{\pi_T}} [KL(\pi_T(\cdot | s) \parallel \pi_\theta(\cdot | s))]$. This process involves sampling rollouts from the RL-trained teacher ($\pi_T$) and training the student to match the teacher's next-token distributions on these rollouts. This is a mode-covering, off-policy training step that moves the student onto the teacher's support, addressing the "minimal deviation" condition (C2) for subsequent OPD by reducing coverage mismatch.
3. **Stage 2b: On-policy distillation (OPD) under student rollouts.** Following the FKL warmup, the student model ($\pi_\theta$) is further trained by minimizing the reverse KL divergence to the teacher on states sampled from the student's own distribution. Specifically, $L_R(\theta) = E_{s \sim d_{\pi_\theta}} [KL(\pi_\theta(\cdot | s) \parallel \pi_T(\cdot | s))]$. In this stage, the teacher's log-probabilities are queried on-policy at the student's sampled prefixes, with the teacher model frozen. This step corrects the student's policy on its own state distribution, leveraging the local implicit-reward interpretation of OPD.
4. **Stage 3 (optional): Post-bridge student-side sparse-reward RL.** If a portion of the labeled data was held out from Stages 1 and 2, this data can be used to perform additional GRPO-based sparse-reward RL directly on the student model after it has completed Stages 2a and 2b. This stage investigates the student's trainability after the dense bridge.

#### 3.2. Theoretical Justification

The workflow is justified through a trust-region interpretation of on-policy distillation. The paper argues that:
- A sequence-level verifier reward R(x,y) implicitly defines a KL-regularized target policy $\pi^*_R$.
- OPD (minimizing reverse-KL) can be viewed as a local trust-region update that maximizes an "implicit reward" $E_{\pi} [\tilde{R}^k_T]$, where $\tilde{R}^k_T(x,y) = \sum_t \beta \log (\pi_T(y_t|s_t) / \pi_k(y_t|s_t))$. This dense, per-token implicit reward is derived from the teacher-student likelihood ratio.
- For this implicit reward to be effective, two conditions are crucial:
 - **(C1) Optimality:** The teacher ($\pi_T$) must itself be near-optimal with respect to the original sparse verifier reward R. Stage 1 (Teacher-side sparse-reward RL) ensures this.
 - **(C2) Minimal Deviation:** The teacher must be "close" to the student in terms of state-action coverage, meaning KL($\pi_T \parallel \pi_\theta$) should be small at the current student policy. Without this, the implicit reward signal becomes high-variance and unstable. Stage 2a (Forward-KL warmup) is designed to move the student's policy closer to the teacher's support, thereby enforcing C2.

#### 3.3. Experimental Setup

- **Tasks:** The evaluation focuses on verifiable mathematical reasoning tasks, utilizing benchmarks such as MATH-500, AIME 2024, and AIME 2025. Performance is measured by average correctness over 16 independent samples (avg@16).
- **Models:**
 - **Qwen3 Family:** Qwen3-1.7B is used as the deployment student. Teacher models include Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-14B, evaluated in "raw" (pre-RL), SFT-trained, and RL-trained variants.
 - **Llama Family (Replication):** Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct serves as the student, with Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct as the teacher for a cross-family replication of key findings.
- **Experimental Design:**
 - **Direct GRPO Baselines:** Establishing performance ceilings for direct RL on different model scales.
 - **Component Ablation:** Systematically removing each stage (Stage 1, Stage 2a, Stage 2b) from the full workflow to assess its contribution. This includes comparing raw teachers, OPD-only, and teacher-sample SFT against the full two-stage bridge.
 - **Stage 3 Evaluation:** Comparing the effectiveness of sparse-reward RL on the bridged student using held-out labeled data versus reusing already-seen data ("replay control").
 - **Cross-Family Replication:** Replicating the core "teacher quality" contrast (raw vs. RL-trained teacher) on Llama models to assess generalizability.
- **Training Details:** The training stack is based on verl/HybridFlow. Specific hyperparameters for GRPO and distillation stages are provided in the appendices, ensuring reproducibility of the contrasts.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation across Qwen3 and Llama model families on verifiable math benchmarks yielded several key findings that support the proposed reward-density principle and workflow.

#### 4.1. Direct GRPO Baseline (Table 1)

- Larger Qwen3 models consistently achieved higher accuracies under direct GRPO: Qwen3-8B reached 88.4% on MATH (vs. 75.9% for 1.7B), and Qwen3-14B reached 89.5%. This established that the smaller 1.7B student had significant headroom for improvement if the inefficiency of direct sparse-reward RL could be overcome.

#### 4.2. Component Ablation (Table 2)

The central ablation study on the Qwen3-1.7B student confirmed the three predictions derived from the reward-density principle:

- **Prediction P1 (Stage 1 enforces C1 - Teacher Optimality):**
 - Allocating labeled data to RL-train an 8B teacher, then distilling its knowledge through the two-stage bridge, resulted in a 1.7B student achieving 79.3% on MATH, outperforming direct GRPO on the same student (75.9%). On AIME 2024, the improvement was from 19.8% to 25.2%.
 - However, replacing the RL-trained teacher with a *raw* (non-RL-trained) 8B teacher, even a larger model, and applying the same bridge, yielded only 71.5% on MATH. This was 7.8 points lower than the full workflow and also below the direct GRPO baseline (75.9%). This indicates that teacher scale alone is insufficient; the teacher must be reward-shaped (i.e., Stage 1 is crucial for C1). Similar patterns were observed with the 14B teacher.

- **Prediction P2 (Stage 2a enforces C2 and Stage 2b uses local IRL identity):**
 - Removing the **Forward-KL warmup (Stage 2a)** (i.e., using OPD only) degraded MATH performance. For the 8B teacher, it fell from 79.3% to 77.6% (-1.7 points). This supports the role of FKL in reducing coverage mismatch and better conditioning the OPD update.
 - Removing **On-Policy Distillation (Stage 2b)** (i.e., using only teacher-sample SFT) further degraded performance. For the 8B teacher, it resulted in 76.0% on MATH, 3.3 points lower than the full bridge. This indicates the importance of on-policy feedback for correcting the student on its own state distribution.

- **Prediction P3 (Stage 3 adds value after the bridge - Post-bridge Trainability):**
 - After the two-stage bridge was applied using half of the labeled data (1H), subsequent sparse-reward GRPO (Stage 3) on the student using the *held-out second half* of the data (2H) further improved MATH scores. For the 8B teacher, performance increased from 75.4% (bridge only on 1H) to 78.5% (+3.1 points).
 - A "replay control," which ran the same number of Stage 3 updates but reused the already-seen 1H bridge data, showed minimal improvement (e.g., to 75.7% for the 8B teacher), confirming that the gains from Stage 3 were due to fresh labeled examples and that the bridge enhanced the student's trainability for sparse-reward RL.

#### 4.3. Cross-Family Replication on Llama (Table 3)

- The core finding regarding teacher quality was replicated in the Llama family. For a Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct student with a Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct teacher:
 - Direct GRPO achieved 59.8% on MATH.
 - Transfer from a *raw* 70B teacher resulted in 55.4% on MATH, performing worse than direct student GRPO.
 - Transfer from an *RL-trained* 70B teacher yielded 62.1% on MATH, an improvement of 2.3 points over direct GRPO.
- This confirmed that the principle of needing a reward-shaped teacher for effective transfer generalizes beyond the Qwen3 family, and teacher scale alone is not the determining factor for performance gains.

#### 4.4. Held-Out-Half Allocation (Appendix C)

- A supplementary experiment explored whether the held-out half of labeled data (2H) should be allocated to Stage 1 (teacher RL) or Stage 3 (student RL). The teacher-side allocation (using full DAPO for teacher RL and bridge, no Stage 3) slightly outperformed the student-side allocation (1H for bridge, 2H for Stage 3) by 0.8 MATH points. This indicates a marginal advantage for upstream allocation to the teacher, though the difference was small compared to other ablation gaps.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research offers a principled and empirically supported framework for optimizing the use of scarce labeled data in large language model post-training. Its significance and potential impact span both theoretical understanding and practical deployment strategies:

- **Rethinking Data Allocation:** The paper challenges the common practice of directly applying reinforcement learning to the deployment model. Instead, it proposes that the most effective use of limited verifiable data is to allocate it strategically across a teacher-student hierarchy. This shift from "which algorithm is best" to "how to best allocate resources" represents a conceptual advance in LLM fine-tuning.
- **Validation of the Reward-Density Principle:** The empirical results provide strong evidence for the reward-density principle, demonstrating that sparse rewards are more effective for "discovery" in larger, more explorative teacher models, while dense supervision is superior for "compression" and efficient transfer to smaller student models. This offers a theoretical underpinning for combining RL and distillation in a staged manner.
- **Improved Student Performance:** The proposed four-stage workflow leads to tangible performance improvements for deployment-sized models (e.g., a 3.4 percentage point increase on MATH for Qwen3-1.7B over direct GRPO). This suggests a practical pathway for enhancing the capabilities of smaller LLMs that are more amenable to deployment.
- **Importance of Teacher Quality:** The findings underscore that simply using a larger teacher is insufficient for effective distillation. The teacher must itself be shaped by sparse reward (i.e., be "reward-aware") for its dense supervision to be informative. This has implications for how teacher models are prepared for distillation tasks.
- **Enhanced Student Trainability:** The research indicates that the dense bridge not only provides a good initial policy but also prepares the student model to be more receptive to subsequent sparse-reward learning. Post-bridge student RL, when supplied with fresh labeled data, continues to add value, suggesting a synergistic relationship between dense transfer and targeted sparse-reward refinement.
- **Operational Guidance for Model Families:** For organizations developing and maintaining families of LLMs at different scales, the paper provides clear operational guidance. It advocates for a pipeline where labeled data is preferentially used on larger models for RL-driven discovery, with the resulting behavior being efficiently transferred to smaller deployment models through the FKL-to-OPD bridge, reserving student-side RL for optional further refinement.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The work opens avenues for further investigation into the dynamics of sparse and dense reward signals, the scalability of the teacher-first advantage to even larger models, and its applicability to diverse tasks beyond verifiable math (e.g., code generation, instruction following, creative writing), which may require different verifier-density considerations. The nuanced results on data allocation for the "held-out half" also invite further optimization of resource distribution.

In summary, this paper offers a significant contribution by providing a structured, theoretically grounded, and empirically validated approach to optimize the use of scarce labeled data in LLM post-training, potentially leading to more efficient and capable deployment models.
