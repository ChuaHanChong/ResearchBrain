# RoboICL: Embodied In-Context Learning with GPT-6 Astra

> [!summary] Summary
> RoboICL is an in-context robot-control framework that drives a frozen general-purpose VLM (GPT-6 Astra) to emit continuous action chunks without robot-specific training. Demonstrations and the robot's own rollout share one observation-action-receipt-observation grammar, kept in bounded anchored memory. On 30 RoboDojo tasks it scores 50.64 Overall versus 33.68 for the strongest baseline, and real-robot progress rises from 14.45 to 78.89 with three demonstrations.

## Problem

- Frozen general-purpose VLMs like GPT-6 Astra can output robot actions zero-shot but remain substantially weaker on high-precision and long-horizon manipulation tasks.
- Most VLA and world-action-model systems gain their capabilities only through substantial robot-specific training or post-training, so no broadly deployable embodied foundation model exists as in language modeling.
- Prior frozen-model controllers leave open how to organize actions, their realized consequences, and accumulated long-horizon experience; recent-only history windows progressively drop earlier task stages.

## Method

- ==Shared interaction grammar==: demonstration context and live interaction memory both serialize as observation, action chunk, execution receipt, result observation, so the model sees one before-action-after pattern.
- ==Bounded anchored memory== keeps fixed temporal anchors plus the latest interaction, with explicit ==TRAJECTORY_GAP== records; demonstrations contribute uniformly sampled non-overlapping action blocks under a 50-image budget.
- ==Minimal harness== with a single act tool emitting per-step Cartesian increments and gripper targets, step-limit-based ==adaptive horizons== (5-25 steps), ==cache-stable prompt prefixes==, and optional ==Jev-gated action-suffix reuse==.

## Results

- On 30 RoboDojo tasks, RoboICL beats official zero-shot GPT-6 Astra by **20-27 points** in every category, scoring **50.64** Overall versus **33.68** for the best leaderboard baseline.
- Anchored memory beats first-plus-recent memory **80.25 vs 73.50**; balanced 12/12 demonstration/memory allocation beats 22/2 (**60.75**); Deposit Coin reaches **78.00** where every baseline scores at most **18.67**.
- Real Franka tasks rise from **14.45** zero-shot to **78.89** with three demonstrations; KV-cache hit rate is **92.4%**; Jev gating cuts GPT-6 Astra calls by **33-48%** with task-dependent success.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - How context is organized, not model weights, can close much of a frozen VLM's control gap; formatting demonstrations and self-experience identically lets the model learn from both.
> - Temporally distributed memory beats recency windows for long-horizon control, and denser demonstrations are not free: they compete with interaction memory for a fixed context budget.
> - Demonstrations ground procedure but risk over-literal imitation: a larger unseen towel dropped folding to 40, so in-context imitation may copy motion scale rather than adapt it.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

RoboICL addresses a central problem in general-purpose robot control: how to adapt a fixed vision-language model to new tasks using information supplied at inference time rather than robot-specific parameter training. The work lies at the intersection of in-context learning, vision-language-action models, multimodal agents, and closed-loop manipulation.

The broader field has developed along two main paths. The first involves robot-specific policies, including vision-language-action (VLA) and world-action-model systems trained or post-trained on robot trajectories, videos, or physical interaction data. These methods can acquire specialized control capabilities, but they require substantial data and computational resources and may be tied to particular embodiments or action interfaces. The second path uses frozen language or vision-language models to generate skills, programs, spatial plans, or numerical actions. This approach reduces training requirements but often struggles with continuous control, contact-rich interaction, precision, and long-horizon execution.

GPT-6 Astra is presented as an example of a general-purpose vision-language model capable of directly producing robot actions. According to the paper, its performance is relatively strong on open-ended, language-conditioned, and image-conditioned manipulation, but weaker on tasks requiring fine alignment, repeated correction, memory of earlier states, or extended sequences of dependent actions.

RoboICL builds on earlier work in few-shot imitation learning, multimodal in-context learning, feedback-conditioned agents, ReAct-style interaction, and recent systems that combine demonstrations with online execution history. Its distinguishing focus is not a new learned policy, but the organization of inference-time context. The method treats recorded demonstrations and the robot’s own experience as related but distinct information sources and presents both through a common observation–action–receipt–observation format.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

#### 2.1. Main objectives

The paper has four closely related objectives:

1. It seeks to improve the precision and long-horizon performance of a frozen general-purpose vision-language model without updating model parameters.
2. It aims to combine demonstrations with execution feedback in a structured and auditable way.
3. It investigates how long trajectories can be represented within a bounded multimodal context window.
4. It evaluates whether the approach transfers from simulated bimanual manipulation to a different real-robot embodiment.

The authors argue that ordinary few-shot prompting is insufficient for robotics because the model’s actions change the state of the environment. A grasp may fail, an object may slip, a controller may execute only part of a proposed action sequence, or the robot may encounter a state not represented in the demonstrations. Consequently, the model must reason not only from examples of successful behavior but also from the consequences of its own previous actions.

#### 2.2. Motivation for separating demonstrations and interaction memory

RoboICL distinguishes between “demonstration context” and “interaction memory.” Demonstrations are fixed recorded examples supplied before and during an episode. Interaction memory is updated during execution and records the model’s own proposed actions, which parts were executed, controller feedback, and subsequent observations.

This distinction is intended to preserve two forms of information. Demonstrations provide procedural guidance, such as action order, manipulation strategy, and embodiment-specific movement patterns. Interaction memory provides current-episode evidence, including errors, progress, failed grasps, interrupted actions, and successful corrections. The shared representation is designed to make both types of information usable by the same frozen model.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Context representation

At each decision round, GPT-6 Astra receives the task instruction, any selected demonstrations, the retained interaction memory, the current visual observation, and robot state. The serialized request labels demonstration material as “TRAIN” and live experience as “LIVE”; “TRAIN” refers to examples included in the prompt and does not imply parameter training.

Each retained interaction has the form:

- an observation and proprioceptive state before action,
- an action chunk,
- an execution receipt,
- an observation and proprioceptive state after action.

For demonstrations, the receipt is synthetic and describes the recorded action interval, action counts, target information, and resulting state. For live interaction, the receipt records proposed, dispatched, and executed actions, controller interruptions, target tracking, and any unexecuted action suffix.

The system uses a single `act` function tool. In the RoboDojo bimanual setting, each action row contains 14 values: three translation increments, three rotation-vector increments, and one gripper target for each arm. Actions are expressed as world-frame Cartesian increments and executed at 25 Hz. Deterministic software validates dimensions and bounds, solves inverse kinematics, and executes the admissible action prefix.

#### 3.2. Demonstration sampling

Rather than include complete demonstrations, RoboICL samples non-overlapping action blocks distributed over the trajectory. The selection procedure attempts to cover early, intermediate, and late stages while preserving valid action windows and trajectory endpoints. Each block includes its starting observation, recorded actions, synthetic receipt, and result observation.

The main one-shot configuration uses one demonstration with 12 retained blocks. The paper also studies three-shot conditions and a smaller five-block-per-demonstration configuration. When portions of a trajectory are omitted, the prompt includes an explicit `<TRAJECTORY_GAP>` marker so that the model does not incorrectly interpret separated observations as consecutive states.

#### 3.3. Bounded anchored interaction memory

The key memory mechanism is “bounded anchored memory.” If an episode has a maximum of \(T\) environment steps and \(B\) memory slots, the first \(B-1\) slots retain interactions at approximately fixed temporal locations across the episode. The final slot retains the latest completed interaction.

This design provides both long-range and local information. Earlier anchors preserve the initial scene and intermediate task stages, while the latest interaction supports immediate correction. Only positive-length executed transitions are committed as anchors. A rejected action that executes nothing remains in the immediate request tail, allowing the model to replan from the same state.

The authors compare this strategy with a first-plus-recent selector that retains the first interaction and the latest 11 interactions. On four development tasks, anchored memory achieves a mean score of 80.25 compared with 73.50 for the first-plus-recent approach.

#### 3.4. Adaptive action horizons and efficiency

RoboICL predicts action chunks rather than single actions. The horizon is selected from the task’s public step limit: shorter horizons of 5 or 10 steps are used for shorter tasks, while longer tasks use horizons of 15, 20, or 25 steps. Shorter chunks permit more frequent visual correction, whereas longer chunks reduce the number of model calls.

The system also arranges stable context prefixes to support provider-side key-value cache reuse. Fixed task instructions, demonstrations, camera calibration, completed anchors, and closed trajectory gaps remain unchanged across requests, while the latest interaction and pending corrections form a mutable suffix.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. RoboDojo simulation results

Across 30 RoboDojo tasks, RoboICL uses zero-shot control in the Open category because demonstrations are unavailable and one demonstration elsewhere. Its category scores are:

- Open: 54.75
- Memory: 70.00
- Precision: 38.53
- Long-Horizon: 44.11
- Overall: 50.64

The paper reports absolute improvements of approximately 20–27 progress-score points over official zero-shot GPT-6 Astra in every category. RoboICL leads the listed methods on Open and Memory, is comparable to Liber-0 Preview on Precision, and ranks below the strongest baselines on Long-Horizon. Its overall score of 50.64 is 16.96 points above the strongest comparison baseline, which scores 33.68.

The results suggest that the method is particularly useful when task completion depends on remembering earlier states or applying corrections over multiple stages. For example, RoboICL scores 100 on Cover Blocks compared with 49.30 for official zero-shot GPT-6 Astra, and 80 on Swap T compared with 18.00. On Deposit Coin, a precision task involving insertion of a coin into a narrow slot, RoboICL reaches a mean score of 78.00, including 37 full-score episodes out of 50, whereas comparison methods score no more than 18.67.

#### 4.2. Ten-task comparison and demonstration effects

On a separate ten-task panel, one-shot RoboICL obtains an overall mean progress score of 60.60. This compares with 45.60 for zero-shot RoboICL and 38.50 for the GPT-as-Policy Direct baseline. The score is within two points of the reported 62.60 score for a hybrid VLA–VLM method.

Demonstrations produce substantial but uneven improvements. On Build Tower, the score rises from 16 in the zero-shot condition to 100 with one demonstration. Imitate Sorting Sequence remains at 90 in both conditions, while Put Bottles into Dustbin changes only from 70 to 73. Performance on Classify Objects declines from 100 to 71, showing that additional context does not guarantee improvement and may sometimes distract or consume capacity.

#### 4.3. Real-robot transfer

The authors evaluate Peg in Hole, Folding Towel, and Building Bridge on a Franka Research 3 robot using wrist-mounted and external RGB cameras. A human collects demonstrations through GELLO. Each condition contains five trials with randomized object positions and orientations.

The mean progress score across the three tasks increases from 14.45 with no demonstrations to 63.33 with one demonstration and 78.89 with three demonstrations. All three tasks improve across the shot conditions. However, performance remains incomplete on difficult cases. For example, only two of five three-shot Peg in Hole trials complete insertion. On Folding Towel, performance on a seen towel is 100, while an unseen towel scores 90 and a larger unseen towel scores 40, indicating limited adaptation to object-scale changes.

#### 4.4. Inference cost and Jev-gated reuse

Across 1,810 completed requests, the provider reports a token-weighted KV-cache hit rate of 92.4 percent. Demonstrations sometimes reduce the number of calls by shortening successful episodes, although they can also increase per-request context and total token use when they do not improve execution.

An optional Jev-based gate approves reuse of part of a previously predicted action sequence when structured feedback indicates that replanning may not be necessary. On two development tasks, GPT-6 Astra calls decrease by 33 percent for Align Blocks and 48 percent for General Pickup. Token use falls from 9.53 to 5.08 million on Align Blocks and from 4.95 to 1.39 million on General Pickup. The effect on success is task-dependent: General Pickup improves from four of five to five of five successful layouts, while Align Blocks declines from three of five to two of five.

### 5. Significance, Potential Impact, and Limitations

RoboICL contributes a practical framework for using a frozen multimodal model in embodied control. Its central significance is that adaptation occurs through context organization, structured execution feedback, and memory retention rather than through robot-specific parameter updates. This could reduce the need for collecting and labeling large robot datasets when a small number of demonstrations and online feedback are available.

The shared interaction grammar also improves auditability. Each action can be related to the observation before execution, the controller’s receipt, and the resulting observation. Explicitly distinguishing proposed from executed actions is useful when safety checks or continuity failures interrupt a sequence.

The results indicate potential for improving precision manipulation, task-stage retention, and recovery from execution errors. Nevertheless, the evidence has several limitations. The main benchmark uses one model, one simulation environment, and mostly seed-zero evaluations. The real-robot study contains only three tasks and five trials per condition. Context allocation and Jev settings were selected using development tasks, so their generality requires held-out evaluation. Performance also depends on expensive multimodal inference, and added demonstrations can reduce performance on some tasks. The larger unseen towel result shows that demonstrations may cause the model to reproduce motions too closely rather than adapt them to changed geometry. Finally, the paper does not establish whether the framework transfers to other foundation models, robot platforms, camera configurations, or control interfaces.

Overall, the work positions context design as an important control variable for general-purpose robot policies. Its findings support further investigation of structured memory, execution receipts, and adaptive action reuse as complements to—and in some cases alternatives to—robot-specific policy training.
