# Flow Motion Policy: Manipulator Motion Planning with Flow Matching Models

> [!summary] Summary
> The Flow Motion Policy (FMP) introduces an end-to-end neural motion planner for robotic manipulators that leverages flow matching to model multi-modal path distributions, enabling efficient inference-time best-of-N sampling. This approach yields competitive success rates while substantially reducing planning times compared to traditional and prior neural methods, performing robustly in both simulated and real-world environments.

## Problem

- Traditional motion planners suffer from high computational costs associated with collision checking, which can consume up to 90% of planning time.
- Existing end-to-end neural motion planners often produce only a single, deterministic path, limiting their ability to handle multi-modal solutions and leverage best-of-N sampling.
- Previous attempts at multi-modal neural planners (e.g., using Gaussian Mixture Models) have limited capacity to represent the complex distributions of feasible paths.

## Method

- The **Flow Motion Policy (FMP)** utilizes a ==flow matching framework== to learn a conditional distribution over feasible path increments, capturing the multi-modality of planning data.
- A neural network is trained to regress a ==conditional vector field==, which is then integrated during inference to autoregressively generate diverse candidate paths from Gaussian noise.
- An efficient ==inference-time best-of-N sampling== mechanism generates multiple paths in parallel, followed by batched collision checking to select the first collision-free solution.

## Results

- **FMP-100** (N=100) achieved competitive success rates (e.g., **84%** on TableTop) with significantly lower planning times (**0.58s**) compared to traditional planners like Bi-RRT (**88%** in **2.85s**).
- **FMP** consistently outperformed an adapted Neural MP (GMM-based) baseline across all evaluation tasks due to its superior generative modeling capacity and smaller network size (**1.4M** vs. **20M** parameters).
- Real-world deployment on a UR5e manipulator demonstrated **FMP-100** achieving an **86.7%** success rate across tasks, a substantial improvement over FMP-1 (**33.4%**) for complex scenarios.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Flow matching effectively models the complex, multi-modal distributions of robot trajectories, providing a more expressive and efficient alternative to GMMs and diffusion models for this task.
> - Leveraging stochastic generative modeling with best-of-N sampling during inference significantly improves planning success rates without increasing the planning time per candidate path generation.
> - The FMP architecture achieves a favorable balance of network size, planning speed, and success rate, allowing for fast inference even with multiple samples due to its non-iterative sampling process.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Motion planning is a fundamental aspect of robotic manipulation systems, aiming to compute collision-free paths for robots from a start to a goal configuration. Traditionally, this field has been dominated by methods such as sampling-based planners (SBPs) and trajectory optimization. SBPs, like RRT and its variants, build search trees stochastically and are probabilistically complete, but their efficiency is often hindered by the high computational cost of collision checking, which can account for up to 90% of planning time. Trajectory optimization methods refine an initial trajectory iteratively to satisfy constraints, but they are susceptible to local minima and also depend on continuous collision checking. Both traditional categories generally require a "privileged" collision checker during the planning process, limiting their potential for parallelization and inference-time optimization.

Recent advancements have seen deep learning applied to motion planning to mitigate these limitations. Neural networks have been employed as informed samplers to guide SBPs or as implicit collision checkers to accelerate queries. However, these applications often retain the need for collision checking during planning, thereby restricting their efficiency, particularly in scenarios requiring multiple path evaluations (best-of-N sampling).

A distinct line of research involves open-loop, end-to-end neural motion planners, which learn a direct mapping from planning problem information (e.g., sensor observations, start/goal configurations) to a feasible path, bypassing explicit collision checking during the planning phase. While these methods eliminate the collision checking bottleneck during policy inference, many existing approaches produce only a single, deterministic path. This deterministic output limits their ability to leverage best-of-N sampling, a strategy that can enhance planning success by evaluating multiple candidate paths. Approaches like Neural MP have attempted to address this by using Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs) for multi-modal output, but GMMs may have limited capacity to represent the complex, multi-modal distributions inherent in motion planning problems.

This work, "Flow Motion Policy," positions itself within this landscape by advancing end-to-end neural motion planning. It leverages flow matching models, a class of stochastic generative models, to capture the inherent multi-modality of planning datasets more effectively than GMMs. By doing so, it enables efficient inference-time best-of-N sampling in an open-loop, end-to-end framework, aiming to improve both planning success and efficiency without relying on a privileged collision checker during path generation. This approach builds upon the strengths of end-to-end learning while addressing the limitations of single-path output and the representational capacity of simpler multi-modal models.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an open-loop, end-to-end neural motion planner for robotic manipulators that can leverage stochastic generative modeling to capture the multi-modality of motion planning solutions and enable efficient inference-time optimization.

The motivation for this objective stems from several limitations observed in current motion planning paradigms:

1. **Computational Bottleneck of Collision Checking:** Traditional sampling-based and trajectory optimization planners heavily rely on collision checking throughout the planning process. This operation is computationally intensive, often consuming a significant portion of the planning time (up to 90%). This dependence hinders real-time performance, particularly in complex environments, and makes parallel planning or best-of-N sampling strategies challenging to implement efficiently, as each candidate path requires iterative collision queries during its generation.
2. **Deterministic Output of Existing End-to-End Neural Planners:** While open-loop, end-to-end neural motion planners eliminate the need for an explicit collision checker during path generation, many current methods are deterministic. They produce a single path for a given planning problem, irrespective of the inherent multi-modality (i.e., multiple valid paths) that often exists in complex environments. This determinism means they cannot exploit inference-time optimization techniques, such as best-of-N sampling, where multiple candidate paths are generated and the optimal or first collision-free one is selected. This limitation can lead to lower success rates, especially in cluttered or constrained workspaces where finding a single feasible path is difficult.
3. **Limited Capacity of Previous Multi-modal Models:** Attempts to introduce multi-modality into end-to-end planners, such as using Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs), have shown promise. However, GMMs might possess limited capacity to accurately represent the diverse and complex distributions of feasible paths in high-dimensional configuration spaces. A more expressive generative modeling framework could better capture this multi-modality, leading to a richer set of candidate paths for selection.
4. **Desire for Efficient Inference-Time Optimization:** The ability to generate multiple candidate paths and select the best one (best-of-N sampling) during inference time, without requiring iterative collision checking *during* path generation, is appealing. This would combine the efficiency of open-loop neural inference with the robustness-enhancing capability of considering multiple options, leading to higher success rates without proportional increases in planning time.

To address these motivations, Flow Motion Policy aims to:
- Leverage the stochastic generative formulation of flow matching models to learn a conditional distribution over feasible path increments, thereby capturing the multi-modality of planning data.
- Implement an efficient inference-time best-of-N sampling mechanism where multiple paths are generated in parallel and subsequently checked for collisions, allowing the selection of the first collision-free solution.
- Demonstrate that this approach can yield a better balance between planning success rate and computational efficiency compared to both traditional and existing neural motion planners.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Flow Motion Policy (FMP) is designed as an open-loop, end-to-end neural motion planner that integrates a flow matching framework to model the multi-modality of motion planning datasets and facilitate inference-time best-of-N sampling.

**Problem Definition:**
The planning problem involves a robotic manipulator with `n` degrees of freedom (DOF), represented by a configuration `q ∈ R^n`. Given a start configuration `q_start`, a goal configuration `q_goal`, and an environment observation `P` (represented as a point cloud), the objective is to generate a collision-free path `q = {q_start, q_1..., q_goal}`. FMP models this as learning a conditional distribution `p_θ(δq_t:t+H-1 | q_t, q_goal, P)`, which predicts joint configuration increments `δq` over a planning horizon `H`, conditioned on the current configuration `q_t`, goal `q_goal`, and environment `P`. The path is generated autoregressively by predicting these increments.

**Flow Matching as Motion Policy:**
FMP utilizes a flow-based policy, learned under the continuous normalizing flow matching framework, to model the distribution of planning data `p_0(x_0)`, where `x_0` represents the target increment action sequence `δq_t:t+H-1`.
1. **Probability Path Construction:** An optimal transport probability path `x_τ = (1 - τ)x_0 + τϵ` is defined, which linearly interpolates between the clean data `x_0` (at `τ = 0`) and Gaussian noise `ϵ ∼ N(0, I)` (at `τ = 1`).
2. **Conditional Vector Field:** The corresponding conditional generating vector field `u_τ(x_τ | x_0):= d/dτ x_τ = ϵ - x_0` can be computed directly from samples `x_0` and `ϵ`.
3. **Model Learning:** A neural network `v_θ(x_τ, τ, O)` (the vector field estimator) is trained to regress towards this conditional vector field, conditioned on the planning observation `O`. The loss function `L` is an expected squared difference between the model's prediction and the true vector field, averaged over `τ` from a uniform distribution `U`, data `x_0`, and interpolated states `x_τ`.
4. **Inference:** During inference, samples `ˆx_0` from `p_0` are obtained by numerically integrating the learned vector field `v_θ(ˆx_τ, τ, O)` from `τ = 1` down to `τ = 0`, starting from Gaussian noise `ϵ`. FMP is evaluated autoregressively: each predicted joint action chunk is fed back as input for subsequent predictions until the full path is rolled out.

**Best-of-N Sampling:**
To enhance planning efficiency, FMP incorporates a lightweight inference-time optimization. Leveraging the stochastic nature of flow matching, `N` candidate paths (`Q* = {q_i}^N_i=1`) are sampled from the learned distribution. Each trajectory `q_i` is then evaluated using a collision checker. The cost function `C(q_i)` counts the number of configurations in the trajectory that are in collision, defined by `I{d(q^i_t, P) < δ_safe}`, where `d` is the minimum signed distance to the environment `P`, and `δ_safe` is a safety threshold. The method selects the first collision-free path (`q* = arg min_{q_i} C(q_i)`) for execution. In simulation, the `cuRobo` library is used for batched collision checking of all candidate paths, enabling parallel evaluation.

**Network Architecture:**
The FMP architecture comprises several key components (illustrated in Figure 2):
1. **Embedding Robot and Workspace Planning Information:**
 - Current (`q_t ∈ R^6`) and goal (`q_goal ∈ R^6`) configurations are embedded into `d`-dimensional vectors (`h_t, h_goal ∈ R^d`) using a shared multi-layer perceptron (MLP).
 - Robot (`P_r ∈ R^{N_r × 3}`) and scene (`P_w ∈ R^{N_w × 3}`) point clouds are down-sampled and embedded using set abstraction layers from PointNet++, resulting in `h_r ∈ R^{K_r × d}` and `h_w ∈ R^{K_w × d}` embeddings.
2. **Transformer Encoder:** These embeddings (`h_t, h_goal, h_r, h_w`) are augmented with learnable token embeddings (`e_t, e_goal, e_r, e_w ∈ R^d`) to encode their semantic roles. These augmented representations are then fed as tokens into a transformer encoder, which processes them to integrate planning information.
3. **Flow Head:** The output of the transformer encoder conditions an MLP that serves as the flow head. This MLP operates on learnable action tokens `ˆx_0 ∈ R^H` and predicts the continuous-time vector field `v_t`, which is then used for flow-based sampling to generate actions.

**Training Data Collection:**
To train FMP, a large dataset of motion planning environments is generated using an LLM-based workspace generator. Optimal paths for these environments are then collected using `cuRobo`, a fast, optimization-based motion planner, which acts as an oracle to provide target paths for supervised learning.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of Flow Motion Policy (FMP) involved comparisons against several benchmark planners and ablation studies on its components, across various held-out planning tasks (TableTop, Box, Bins, Shelf Task I, II, III) and real-world deployment.

**Comparison with Benchmark Planners (Table I):**
- **Success Rate and Planning Time:** FMP with inference-time optimization (FMP-100, N=100) demonstrated competitive success rates compared to traditional sampling-based planners (Bi-RRT, BIT*) while requiring significantly lower planning times. For instance, in the TableTop task, Bi-RRT achieved 88% success in 2.85s, while FMP-100 achieved 84% in 0.58s.
- **Neural Informed Samplers:** FMP-1 (without inference-time optimization) performed comparably to neural informed samplers (MPNets, SIMPNet, GAIDE) but with substantially lower planning times. These neural samplers still require collision checking during tree expansion, which is circumvented by FMP's direct path prediction.
- **End-to-End Neural Planners:** FMP-1 exhibited comparable performance to PerFACT, another end-to-end neural planner, but with reduced planning time due to its smaller network size (1.4M parameters vs. PerFACT's 4.15M). FMP-100 further improved success rates over PerFACT with a slight increase in planning time, attributable to its stochastic path generation and best-of-N sampling capability.

**Best-of-N Sampling (Figure 3):**
- FMP consistently outperformed an adapted Neural MP baseline (which uses a Gaussian Mixture Model, GMM, for multi-modal output) across all evaluation tasks, both with (N=100) and without (N=1) inference-time optimization.
- This performance gain was attributed to two factors: the flow matching formulation's capacity for expressive generative modeling, enabling it to capture the multi-modal path distributions more effectively than GMMs, and FMP's substantially smaller network size (1.4M vs. Neural MP's 20M parameters), leading to faster inference.

**Ablation Study: Policy Head Architecture (Table II, Figure 4):**
- **Flow Motion Policy Heads:** Among the tested flow-based policy head architectures (MLP, U-Net, Transformer, DiT-Block), the MLP-based flow head achieved the fastest planning time. While more complex heads sometimes yielded slightly higher success rates, these came with increased architectural complexity and larger parameter counts.
- **Flow vs. Diffusion:** When comparing FMP (flow-based) against Diffusion Motion Policy (DMP) using the same backbone architectures, FMP consistently demonstrated faster planning times. This difference was attributed to the iterative denoising process in diffusion models, which requires more steps during inference (100 denoising steps in this comparison).
- **Diffusion Timestep Ablation (Figure 5, 6):** Decreasing the number of denoising steps (k) in Diffusion Motion Policy significantly reduced planning time but also led to a reduction in the average planning success rate. This highlights a trade-off between speed and solution quality in diffusion-based approaches.

**Ablation Study: Inference-time Optimization (Figures 6, 7):**
- Increasing the number of sampled trajectories (N) for best-of-N sampling generally improved the planning success rate across all policy head architectures for both Flow Motion Policy and Diffusion Motion Policy.
- However, this improvement was accompanied by longer planning times, as more trajectories needed to be generated and evaluated. More complex policy heads (U-Net, Transformer, DiT) incurred a higher computational overhead per trajectory, leading to a more rapid increase in planning time with increasing N.

**Ablation Study: Inference Flow Steps (Figures 8, 9):**
- Increasing the number of Euler solver steps during FMP inference (ranging from 5 to 90 steps) did not lead to a significant improvement in success rate, either with or without best-of-N sampling.
- Conversely, increasing the number of solver steps directly increased the average planning time. This suggests that the policy heads were capable of generating sufficiently accurate motion samples with a relatively small number of Euler steps, and further steps primarily added computational cost without notable performance gains.

**Real-world Deployment (Table IV, Figures 10, 11):**
- FMP was deployed on a UR5e robotic manipulator using an Intel RealSense D435i RGB-D camera for environment sensing.
- FMP-100 (with best-of-N sampling) achieved a substantially higher success rate (86.7%) compared to FMP-1 (without optimization, 33.4%) across real-world tasks (Bins, Articulated, Shelves).
- In "Bins" and "Articulated" environments, FMP-100 achieved 100% success. Performance dropped in "Shelves" environments for both variants (FMP-100: 6/10, FMP-1: 2/10), attributed to limited representation of similar scenarios in the training dataset and consequently poor generalization, even with inference-time optimization.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Flow Motion Policy (FMP) introduces a framework that integrates stochastic generative modeling via flow matching with an end-to-end neural motion planner for robotic manipulators. The significance and potential impact of this work can be summarized as follows:

1. **Enhanced Planning Efficiency and Success Rate:** By employing flow matching, FMP effectively captures the multi-modality of feasible paths in motion planning problems. This capability, combined with efficient inference-time best-of-N sampling and parallel collision checking, allows the system to achieve planning success rates comparable to or higher than traditional sampling-based planners, while significantly reducing planning time. This efficiency is critical for real-time robotic applications in dynamic or unstructured environments.
2. **Addressing Limitations of End-to-End Neural Planners:** FMP directly addresses the limitation of many existing end-to-end neural planners that produce only a single, deterministic path. By enabling the generation and evaluation of multiple candidate paths, it increases the robustness and reliability of the planner, especially in complex and cluttered environments where a single prediction might not suffice.
3. **Demonstrating the Efficacy of Flow Matching for Robotics:** The research demonstrates that flow matching, a generative modeling technique, is effective for learning complex, multi-modal distributions of robot trajectories. This positions flow matching as a viable and potentially more efficient alternative to other generative models, such as Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs) or diffusion models, for certain robotics applications, particularly given its faster inference characteristics in this context.
4. **Reduced Computational Overhead:** The FMP architecture, particularly with an MLP-based flow head, is lightweight compared to some other neural planners and diffusion-based models. This reduced parameter count contributes to faster inference times, making it suitable for deployment on hardware with computational constraints.
5. **Practical Application and Real-World Relevance:** The successful deployment of FMP on a UR5e robotic manipulator in real-world environments demonstrates its practical applicability. The significant improvement in success rate observed with inference-time optimization in real-world tasks highlights its potential for improving robotic autonomy in industrial, service, and exploration robotics.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Advanced Robotic Autonomy:** The ability to plan motions efficiently and robustly in complex environments could accelerate the development of more autonomous robotic systems capable of operating in unstructured human-centric spaces.
- **Benchmark for Future Research:** FMP provides a new benchmark and a viable approach for future research in end-to-end neural motion planning, particularly regarding the exploration of generative models for multi-modal policy learning and inference-time optimization strategies.
- **Industrial and Service Robotics:** Faster and more reliable motion planning can lead to increased throughput and safety in manufacturing, logistics, and service robotics applications, where robots frequently interact with complex workspaces and need to adapt to changing conditions.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The paper acknowledges several limitations, including the current formulation's assumption of static environments, suggesting future integration with reactive controllers for dynamic obstacles. Additionally, the generated paths may lack optimal smoothness or local optimality, indicating a need for post-processing before deployment on physical robots, or for incorporating smoothness objectives directly into the learning process. Addressing these limitations could further enhance the applicability and performance of the Flow Motion Policy.
