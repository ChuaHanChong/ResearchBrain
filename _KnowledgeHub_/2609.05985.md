# A Brain-inspired Hierarchical Framework for Zero-Shot Robot Task Reasoning and Execution

> [!summary] Summary
> ActionReasoning 2.0 is a zero-shot hierarchical robot framework with three brain-inspired modules: visual perception inferring explicit object states, a grounding stage composing reusable atomic actions into candidate sequences, and a cost-based selector ranking them by estimated execution time. Closed-loop verification from refreshed RGB-D observations triggers replanning. It beats ReKep, Dream2Flow, and pi-0.5 on every evaluated condition across three task families.

## Problem

- End-to-end vision-language-action policies map pixels and language straight to actions, leaving no explicit structure for long-horizon decomposition, physical feasibility checking, or failure recovery.
- Scaling training data across diverse robot datasets improves generalization but supplies no mechanism to evaluate whether a generated action is physically feasible before execution.
- Language-model planners score semantic plausibility only, so plans can drift from the observed physical state and intermediate execution outcomes go unverified.

## Method

- **Visual-cortex module** uses ==SAM 3== concept-conditioned masks plus registered depth to build object-state descriptors encoding pose, extent, and discrete states like flat, side-oriented, vertical, stacked.
- **Cerebellum module** splits reasoning into grounding (goal, tolerance, verification condition) then composition of six shared ==atomic actions== (MOVE, DESCEND, ROTATE, CLOSE, LIFT, RELEASE) into candidate sequences.
- **Prefrontal module** scores each action by translation, yaw, and gripper terms weighted by measured robot speeds, sums to sequence cost, and picks the argmin; executed steps are verified against refreshed observations.

## Results

- Achieves **10/10** on clean board and pick-and-place and **4/5** on both pyramid layouts, with mean task progress of **99.03%**, **100.00%**, and **96.67%** respectively.
- On pyramid stacking with irregular layouts, all three baselines score **0/5** while the proposed method reaches **80%**; the best baseline pi-0.5 hits only **26.67%** task progress.
- Ablations show removing cost-based selection drops flat pyramid to **40%** and irregular to **0%**, while removing state inference matters only for irregular layouts (**80%** to **0%**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A compact, parameterized atomic-action vocabulary transfers across task families; task differences live in grounding, parameterization, and verification conditions rather than new primitives.
> - Explicit discrete object-state inference only pays off when configurations deviate from nominal, so its benefit is invisible on clean setups and decisive under irregular ones.
> - Generating multiple candidate plans and ranking them by a physically calibrated cost outperforms trusting a single language-reasoned plan, but raises completion time substantially.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics is progressing towards autonomous systems capable of executing complex, long-horizon manipulation tasks based on open-ended language instructions. Recent advancements in Large Language Models (LLMs) and Vision-Language Models (VLMs) have led to the development of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, which facilitate language-conditioned embodied decision-making and action generation. However, a significant challenge in this area is ensuring reliable long-horizon execution in the physical world, where robot actions must adhere to physical constraints such as geometry, object states, and interaction conditions.

Current end-to-end VLA policies, such as RT-1, RT-2, OpenVLA, and π$_{0.5}$, often map visual and language inputs directly to robot actions. While these models have demonstrated improved cross-task generalization and flexible policy representations, they typically lack explicit mechanisms for long-horizon task decomposition, physical verification, and recovery from errors. Their reliance on extensive and diverse training datasets, exemplified by BridgeData V2, DROID, and Open X-Embodiment, improves generalization but does not inherently provide explicit means for evaluating the physical feasibility of generated actions or verifying intermediate execution states.

Structured visuomotor grounding approaches, like VIMA, CLIPort, PerAct, and OmniManip, attempt to address this by incorporating structured intermediate representations to connect perception with action. Similarly, physics-aware planning and LLM reasoning methods, including task-and-motion planning, SayCan, Code as Policies, VoxPoser, ReKep, and Dream2Flow, integrate high-level reasoning with robot execution, often by considering motion feasibility, relational constraints, or affordance values.

This paper builds upon previous work, including ActionReasoning, which focused on physics-aware 3D action reasoning for brick stacking. The present work, ActionReasoning 2.0, aims to bridge the gap between the flexibility and generalization capabilities of foundation models and the need for robust, adaptive physical execution. It does so by introducing a more structured organization of reasoning and execution, functionally inspired by human brain organization. Unlike many end-to-end VLA policies, ActionReasoning 2.0 maintains explicit intermediate action sequences and performs cost-based evaluation and verification before and during execution. This approach distinguishes it by prioritizing explicit object-state reasoning, compositional atomic actions, cost-based plan selection, and closed-loop execution verification to achieve reliable long-horizon robot tasks in zero-shot settings.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a hierarchical reasoning framework, termed ActionReasoning 2.0, for zero-shot robot task execution that effectively connects semantic intent from open-ended language instructions with physical world understanding and interaction.

The motivation behind ActionReasoning 2.0 stems from several critical limitations observed in the current landscape of robot learning and embodied AI:

- **Ensuring Physical Consistency and Feasibility:** Existing end-to-end VLA policies often fail to explicitly account for physical constraints such as geometry, object states, and interaction conditions, which are crucial for reliable long-horizon execution in the physical world. There is a need for robot actions to be consistent with these real-world constraints.
- **Addressing Long-Horizon Task Decomposition:** Complex manipulation tasks often require sequences of many individual robot actions. Current VLA models frequently map inputs directly to actions without sufficient explicit structure for long-horizon decomposition, making robust execution challenging and recovery from errors difficult.
- **Enabling Intermediate State Verification and Recovery:** For reliable execution, especially in dynamic or uncertain environments, robots need mechanisms to verify intermediate physical outcomes and adapt their plans if the observed state deviates from the expected. Most VLA policies do not provide explicit means for such verification.
- **Bridging Generalization and Robustness:** While foundation models (LLMs, VLMs) offer significant flexibility and generalization capabilities, there remains a practical gap in translating this into robust and adaptive physical execution across diverse robot tasks. The goal is to retain the generalization of foundation models while achieving reliable physical interaction.
- **Structured Reasoning for Complex Tasks:** Commands like "build a pyramid" or "clean the whiteboard" involve complex sequences of reusable elementary operations (atomic actions). The research is motivated by the need for a structured organization of reasoning and execution that can coordinate complementary functional roles to compose these atomic actions into coherent, task-conditioned sequences.

To address these motivations, the authors established the following key contributions:

- **Introduction of a Hierarchical Framework:** To integrate object-state perception, language grounding, atomic-action composition, cost-based plan selection, and execution verification within a unified, brain-inspired hierarchical architecture for zero-shot robot task execution.
- **Development of a Unified Action-Sequence Generation:** To formulate action-sequence generation using a shared atomic action library, enabling the same reasoning pipeline to support diverse tasks such as pick-and-place, stacking, and surface cleaning through cost-based plan ranking and task-specific execution verification.
- **Empirical Validation:** To benchmark ActionReasoning 2.0 against current state-of-the-art methods across various task families, aiming to demonstrate superior success rates and validate the effectiveness of the proposed components through ablation studies.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

ActionReasoning 2.0 is designed as a hierarchical framework that processes a natural language command, RGB-D observations of the workspace, and the robot's current state to generate structured scene and task representations, culminating in physically verified robot execution. The framework’s organization draws a functional analogy to the division of roles in the human brain.

The core modules and their functions are:

1. **Visual Perception and State Inference (Visual Cortex Analogy):**
 - At each execution step, the robot acquires an RGB-D observation ($O_t$) and its current robot state ($q_t$).
 - This module extracts task-relevant objects and regions using SAM 3 for concept-conditioned segmentation masks.
 - Registered depth information projects these masks into the robot's coordinate frame, recovering object positions, orientations, and geometric extents.
 - The scene state is represented as $S_t = \Phi(O_t, q_t) = (D_t, R_t, q_t)$, where $D_t$ is a set of object-state descriptors, $R_t$ denotes spatial relations, and $q_t$ is the current robot state.
 - Each object-state descriptor $d_{t,j}$ includes entity identity ($\omega_j$), segmentation mask ($M_{t,j}$), pose ($T_{t,j}$), geometric extent ($b_{t,j}$), and an inferred task-relevant state ($z_{t,j}$). For instance, in brick construction, $z_{t,j}$ could encode states like 'flat', 'side-oriented', 'vertical', or 'stacked'.
 - This module provides a common interface across tasks, with the specific state information adapted to the task's physical interaction requirements (e.g., object poses for pick-and-place, support relations for stacking, cleaning tool and residual region for cleaning). Object identities are maintained across steps, while other descriptors are dynamically updated.

2. **Language Grounding and Action-Sequence Generation (Cerebellum Analogy):**
 - This module translates the language command and perceived object states into a set of candidate action sequences.
 - It avoids generating a single, monolithic long-horizon plan by separating task grounding from action composition, following a staged intermediate reasoning principle.
 - **Stage 1: Task Grounding:** The module receives the language command ($L$), current scene state ($S_t$), and the verified execution record ($M_t$). It generates a grounded task $G_t = \Gamma(L, S_t, M_t)$, which is a set of grounded goals. Each goal $g_{t,k}$ defines the object/region ($\omega_k$), desired physical state/spatial relation ($\tau_k$), admissible tolerance ($\epsilon_k$), and a verification condition ($v_k$). This stage specifies the desired physical outcome without prescribing a fixed action sequence.
 - **Stage 2: Action Composition:** This stage uses a shared atomic action library ($A_{atom}$) to compose candidate action sequences. The library consists of core atomic actions: MOVE, DESCEND, ROTATE, CLOSE, LIFT, and RELEASE. These are elementary robot operations with explicit geometric or interaction parameters, operating above the low-level controller. Task-dependent operations are achieved through parameterization, selection, and ordering of these shared actions. For example, 'MOVEABOVE' and 'MOVETOTARGET' are parameterized instances of 'MOVE'. Multiple candidate sequences can be generated for a given grounded task, accounting for different object assignments, orderings, or geometric parameters. The generated set of candidate sequences $C_t = \Psi(S_t, G_t, M_t; A_{atom})$ is then passed to the next module.

3. **Cost-Based Plan Selection (Prefrontal Cortex Analogy):**
 - This module selects the optimal task plan from the candidate action sequences provided by the cerebellum, based on an execution cost that prioritizes shorter motions and fewer additional operations.
 - **Per-action Cost Evaluation:** For each atomic action $a^{(i)}_{t,h}$ in a candidate sequence, an execution-time cost $c^{(i)}_{t,h}$ is calculated. This cost is a linear combination of translational displacement ($d^{(i)}_{t,h}$), absolute yaw rotation ($\theta^{(i)}_{t,h}$), and the number of gripper operations ($g^{(i)}_{t,h}$), weighted by coefficients ($\alpha_d, \alpha_\theta, \alpha_g$) calibrated from real-robot executions.
 - **Sequence-Cost Aggregation:** The total cost of a complete candidate action sequence $J^{(i)}_t$ is the sum of the estimated execution times of its atomic actions.
 - **Ranking and Selection:** The candidate sequences are ranked according to their total cost, and the task plan with the minimum estimated execution time, $a^*_t = \arg\min_{a^{(i)}_t \in C_t} J^{(i)}_t$, is selected for execution.

4. **Real-Robot Execution and Verification:**
 - The selected action sequence is executed on the robot. After each atomic action $a^*_{t,h}$ is executed, a new RGB-D observation $O_{t,h+1}$ is acquired, and the scene state $S_{t,h+1}$ is refreshed by the perception module.
 - Each atomic action is associated with a set of task-dependent verification conditions (Table I). The module evaluates whether these conditions are met based on the refreshed scene state.
 - If all conditions are satisfied ($\chi_{t,h}=1$), execution proceeds to the next atomic action in the sequence.
 - If verification fails, the remaining part of the current sequence is discarded, and a new action sequence is generated from the refreshed scene state, leveraging previously verified task progress.
 - The task terminates only when all terminal verification conditions defined in the grounded task $G_t$ are physically satisfied. This closed-loop approach allows the framework to adapt to unforeseen changes or errors during execution.

The framework utilizes a Qwen3-7B model as a backbone for visual state reasoning, task grounding, and action composition, maintaining zero-shot capability without task-specific weight updates or manually specified action sequences.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of ActionReasoning 2.0 was evaluated across three distinct task families: repeated Pick-and-Place, support-sensitive Pyramid Stacking (under both flat and irregular initial layouts), and continuous-contact Clean Board. The framework was benchmarked against three baseline methods: ReKep, Dream2Flow, and π$_{0.5}$, with π$_{0.5}$ fine-tuned with 50 episodes per task for fair comparison. All experiments were conducted in a zero-shot setting for ActionReasoning 2.0, meaning no task-specific weight updates or manually specified action sequences were used.

**Key Quantitative Findings (Table III):**

- **Overall Superior Performance:** ActionReasoning 2.0 achieved the highest success rate (SR) and mean task progress (TP) across all evaluated conditions.
 - **Clean Board:** ActionReasoning 2.0 achieved 100% SR (10/10 trials) and 99.03% TP. In contrast, the baselines achieved SRs of 0% (ReKep), 10% (Dream2Flow), and 30% (π$_{0.5}$), with corresponding TPs ranging from 15.22% to 52.93%.
 - **Pick-and-Place:** ActionReasoning 2.0 achieved 100% SR (10/10 trials) and 100.00% TP. Baselines showed SRs of 10% (ReKep), 0% (Dream2Flow), and 50% (π$_{0.5}$), with TPs between 53.33% and 70.00%.
 - **Pyramid Stacking (Flat Layout):** ActionReasoning 2.0 achieved 80% SR (4/5 trials) and 96.67% TP. Baselines ranged from 0% SR (ReKep, Dream2Flow) to 40% SR (π$_{0.5}$), with TPs from 0.00% to 73.33%.
 - **Pyramid Stacking (Irregular Layout):** This condition presented the most significant challenge for baselines, with none achieving any successful trials (0% SR). ActionReasoning 2.0, however, achieved 80% SR (4/5 trials) and 96.67% TP, demonstrating its robustness to non-nominal initial configurations.

- **Completion Time:** While ActionReasoning 2.0 consistently achieved higher success rates, its completion times were generally longer than the successful trials of baselines, when applicable. This suggests that the framework prioritizes robustness and successful completion over raw execution speed, which is a secondary metric for this evaluation.

**Qualitative Results (Figs. 3-5):**

- **Adaptation to Disturbances (Pick-and-Place, Fig. 3):** ActionReasoning 2.0 demonstrated its ability to recover from external human disturbances. When an object's position and orientation were altered during execution, the framework updated its object state perception and continued execution from the new scene state, highlighting the effectiveness of its closed-loop perception and verification.
- **Adaptive Task Completion (Clean Board, Fig. 4):** For continuous-contact tasks like cleaning, task completion is often determined by the observed outcome rather than just completing a motion. ActionReasoning 2.0 updated the residual region after wiping and initiated new action sequences as needed to ensure the board was sufficiently clean, demonstrating the value of execution verification for such tasks.
- **Error Recovery (Pyramid Stacking, Fig. 5):** A notable finding was the framework's ability to recover from execution errors. In a scenario where a failed grasp left a brick in an irregular orientation during pyramid stacking, ActionReasoning 2.0 re-inferred the brick's state, composed a reorientation sequence (using ROTATE and MOVE atomic actions), and then resumed the nominal placement sequence to complete the structure. This exemplifies the interaction between object-state inference, state-conditioned action-sequence generation, and intermediate verification for long-horizon construction tasks.

**Ablation Studies (Table IV):**

The ablation studies confirmed the individual contributions of ActionReasoning 2.0's hierarchical components:

- **Single Agent (Merging Specialized Roles):** Reducing the specialized reasoning roles significantly degraded performance across all tasks, particularly Pyramid Stacking. This indicates the importance of separating task grounding, action generation, and plan selection for complex, long-horizon tasks.
- **Single LLM (Direct Model Call):** A direct LLM call performed reasonably well for Pick-and-Place but failed to complete Clean Board and Pyramid Stacking. This supports the staged design of ActionReasoning 2.0, where an explicit grounded-task representation separates language understanding from action-sequence composition, proving more reliable for state-dependent and long-horizon decisions.
- **No State Inference:** Removing visual state inference had minimal impact on the flat Pyramid layout but caused substantial degradation under the irregular layout. This confirms the critical role of the visual-cortex module in inferring object states (e.g., flat, side-oriented) to adapt subsequent action sequences to non-nominal configurations.
- **No Cost-Based Selection:** Disabling the prefrontal-cortex selection module degraded performance in both Pyramid conditions, more pronouncedly in the irregular layout. This highlights the importance of cost-based evaluation in systematically selecting shorter and simpler action sequences when multiple alternatives can achieve the same grounded task.

In summary, the results demonstrate that ActionReasoning 2.0's brain-inspired hierarchical architecture, with its explicit object-state reasoning, compositional atomic actions, cost-based plan selection, and closed-loop execution verification, provides a robust and adaptable framework for zero-shot robot task execution, outperforming baselines in diverse and challenging manipulation scenarios.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

ActionReasoning 2.0 presents a significant contribution to the field of robotic manipulation and embodied AI by introducing a hierarchical, brain-inspired framework that effectively addresses the complexities of zero-shot, long-horizon robot task execution. Its functional organization, drawing an analogy to the human visual cortex, cerebellum, and prefrontal cortex, provides a structured approach to integrating language-guided task understanding with physically grounded robot actions.

The primary significance of this work lies in its ability to bridge a critical gap between the high-level generalization capabilities of large language models and the demand for robust, adaptive physical execution in real-world robotic systems. By explicitly structuring reasoning into distinct modules for visual perception, language grounding, action composition, cost-based plan selection, and closed-loop execution verification, ActionReasoning 2.0 offers several advantages over end-to-end VLA policies:

- **Enhanced Robustness and Adaptability:** The framework's capacity for explicit object-state inference and intermediate physical verification allows robots to adapt to unforeseen changes, external disturbances, and execution errors during long-horizon tasks. This is particularly evident in its ability to recover from failed grasps or adapt to irregular object layouts, a capability often lacking in less structured approaches.
- **Improved Reliability for Complex Tasks:** Decomposing complex tasks into sequences of reusable atomic actions, coupled with task-specific grounding and verification conditions, provides a more reliable method for achieving long-horizon objectives. This modularity allows for systematic plan generation, evaluation, and correction.
- **Transparency and Interpretability:** By maintaining explicit intermediate representations of object states and action sequences, the framework offers greater transparency into the robot's decision-making process compared to opaque end-to-end policies. This can facilitate debugging, analysis, and trust in autonomous systems.
- **Generalizability Across Task Families:** The unified action-sequence generation formulation based on a shared atomic action library, combined with task-specific grounding and verification, demonstrates a strong potential for generalization across diverse manipulation tasks (e.g., pick-and-place, stacking, surface cleaning) without requiring task-specific retraining. This modularity suggests that the framework can be extended to an even broader range of robotic tasks and embodiments.

The potential impact of ActionReasoning 2.0 is substantial for the future development of autonomous robots:

- **Foundation for Open-Ended Robot Learning:** By enabling robots to reliably follow open-ended language instructions in zero-shot scenarios, the framework moves towards more flexible and intuitive human-robot interaction.
- **Advancement in Generalist Robotics:** The principles demonstrated here—explicit physical reasoning, structured planning, and real-time verification—can serve as a foundation for building more generalist robotic systems capable of operating effectively in complex, unstructured, and dynamic environments.
- **Inspiration for Brain-Inspired AI:** The functional analogy to human brain organization, while not neurobiological, provides a novel conceptual framework for organizing computational modules in embodied AI, potentially inspiring new directions in artificial intelligence architectures.
- **Reduced Development Effort:** The zero-shot capability and the ability to compose actions from a shared library could reduce the need for extensive, task-specific data collection and model retraining, thereby accelerating the deployment of robots in new applications.

Future work aims to further enhance reasoning efficiency, expand the atomic action library to cover a wider array of manipulation primitives, and rigorously evaluate the framework across an even broader range of manipulation scenarios and robotic platforms. This research marks a step towards creating more intelligent, robust, and adaptable robotic agents.
