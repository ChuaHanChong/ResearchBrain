# Mathematical methods of reinforcement learning

> [!summary] Summary
> This survey systematically organizes and explains the mathematical foundations of modern reinforcement learning, providing a unified framework that synthesizes concepts from probability, optimization, and operator theory. It details the theoretical underpinnings, convergence guarantees, and analytical techniques for a wide range of RL algorithms and their applications.

## Problem

- Reinforcement learning (RL) addresses sequential decision-making under uncertainty, often with initially unknown environment dynamics.
- A unified mathematical perspective was needed to connect the diverse theoretical tools (probability, optimization, operator theory) that underpin modern RL algorithms.
- Performance evaluation in RL distinguishes itself from classical MDP theory by relying on regret-based criteria, necessitating techniques like optimistic exploration.

## Method

- The paper systematically organizes existing mathematical structures underlying RL, starting from ==dynamic programming== and progressively covering generative models, online interaction, and continuous settings.
- It unifies diverse algorithmic templates (e.g., ==dynamic programming==, ==temporal-difference== learning, ==policy gradients==) under common operator and variational lenses.
- The survey provides a comprehensive exposition of fundamental concepts like ==Markov Decision Processes (MDPs)==, ==Bellman operators==, ==stochastic approximation==, and ==convex duality==, detailing their role in algorithm design and analysis.

## Results

- The paper reaffirms that Value Iteration and Policy Iteration **linearly converge** to optimal solutions due to the contraction property of Bellman operators.
- It compiles crucial **sample complexity** bounds (e.g., $\tilde{O}(SA/((1-\gamma)^3 \epsilon^2))$) for achieving optimal policies in generative model settings and near-optimal **regret** bounds (e.g., $\tilde{O}(\sqrt{H^2 SAT})$) for online algorithms like UCRL, UCBVI, and PSRL.
- The survey details how function approximation, kernel methods, and policy gradient techniques extend RL to continuous state-action spaces and complex tasks like those in Natural Language Processing.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Optimal value functions and policies in MDPs are unique fixed points of Bellman operators, which act as contraction mappings, guaranteeing iterative algorithm convergence.
> - Many RL problems can be framed using convex optimization, particularly through linear programming formulations over occupancy measures, revealing 'hidden convexity' in policy optimization.
> - The exploration-exploitation trade-off in online RL is effectively managed by 'Optimism in the Face of Uncertainty' (OFU) or randomized algorithms, with performance quantified through regret analysis.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement learning (RL) is presented as a framework for sequential decision-making under uncertainty, synthesizing concepts from optimal stochastic control, Markov decision processes (MDPs), and statistical decision theory. This field distinguishes itself from classical MDP theory by addressing scenarios where environment dynamics are initially unknown, requiring agents to learn through interaction. This statistical learning aspect shifts performance evaluation from likelihood-based risk functions to regret-based criteria, necessitating techniques such as optimistic exploration.

The paper delineates two primary methodological paradigms within RL: model-based and model-free approaches. Model-based methods explicitly estimate environmental dynamics, while model-free methods learn value functions or policies directly from interaction. Optimization theory plays a central role, with many algorithms interpretable as gradient-based, mirror-descent, or proximal update schemes, often incorporating regularization for enhanced stability and favorable geometric properties.

The mathematical backbone of RL relies on foundational tools, including the Bellman principle for recursive fixed-point relations, the performance difference lemma for policy improvement analysis, the log-derivative trick for policy gradient estimation, and concentration inequalities for dependent data to provide finite-sample guarantees. The paper also discusses various time-horizon formulations—finite-horizon episodic, infinite-horizon discounted, and average reward settings—each presenting distinct analytical challenges and relying on concepts like ergodic properties and Markov chain theory. Finally, the distinction between tabular and function-approximation regimes is highlighted, with the latter leveraging approximation theory for scalability in large or continuous state-action spaces. This survey aims to unify these diverse mathematical foundations, providing a coherent framework for understanding modern RL algorithms.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to organize and present the mathematical structures that underpin the design and analysis of modern algorithms in reinforcement learning. The authors observe that RL is increasingly grounded in tools from probability, optimization, and operator theory, and that a unified perspective on these mathematical foundations is needed.

The key motivations driving this survey are:
- **Providing a Unified Mathematical Entry Point:** The paper is explicitly intended to serve as a coherent mathematical entry point for researchers in probability, optimization, and statistics who are interested in reinforcement learning. This suggests a goal of bridging disciplinary gaps and making the core mathematical principles of RL accessible to a broader scientific audience.
- **Organizing Foundational Structures:** The authors aim to systematize the core mathematical concepts that form the basis of RL algorithms. This includes fundamental elements like Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) and Bellman operators, extending to advanced topics such as stochastic approximation, convex duality, and function approximation.
- **Emphasizing Key Analytical Tools:** A significant motivation is to highlight the specific mathematical tools and concepts that provide convergence guarantees and rates. This includes contraction mappings, monotonicity, fixed-point theory, martingale methods, and concentration inequalities.
- **Unifying Algorithmic Templates:** The survey seeks to demonstrate how diverse algorithmic approaches, from dynamic programming to temporal-difference schemes and policy gradient methods, can be understood and analyzed through common operator and variational lenses.
- **Highlighting Regret-Based Objectives:** The paper aims to illustrate how the regret-based performance criterion, central to online learning, influences the design of exploration strategies in RL.
- **Connecting Theory to Practice:** By covering a wide range of settings—from full-knowledge and generative models to online interaction and continuous state spaces—and concluding with applications in natural language processing, the authors intend to show the practical relevance of these mathematical underpinnings.

In essence, the work is motivated by the need for a consolidated and structured mathematical exposition of reinforcement learning, intended to clarify the theoretical landscape and facilitate further research across related quantitative fields.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

This paper is a comprehensive survey that systematically organizes and explains the mathematical foundations of reinforcement learning. Its methodology involves structuring existing knowledge from various subfields of mathematics and computer science into a coherent narrative, highlighting interconnections and fundamental principles.

The approach begins with the most basic building blocks of sequential decision-making:
- **Dynamic Programming (DP):** The survey starts by introducing Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) and the Bellman equations in the full-knowledge setting. It meticulously details how value functions and policies are characterized as fixed points of Bellman operators, emphasizing the role of contraction mappings and Banach's fixed-point theorem for guaranteeing existence, uniqueness, and convergence of iterative methods like Value Iteration (VI) and Policy Iteration (PI). Linear Programming (LP) formulations for both discounted and average-reward MDPs are also presented as alternative solution methods, along with discussions on accelerated techniques and the concept of "hidden convexity" in RL problems.

The paper then transitions to scenarios where the environment model is unknown:
- **Generative Model Setting:** This section introduces the concept of a generative model, an oracle that provides samples of next states and rewards for any given state-action pair. It contrasts model-based approaches, which explicitly estimate transition probabilities before optimizing, with model-free approaches, which directly update value functions or policies. The discussion includes analysis of sample complexity, presenting minimax lower bounds and the performance of algorithms like Q-learning, Polyak-Ruppert averaging, and variance-reduced Q-learning. Stochastic Mirror Descent is introduced as an optimization framework for solving LP formulations in this setting.

A dedicated section focuses on evaluating policies:
- **Policy Evaluation:** The problem of estimating the value function for a given policy is addressed through Temporal Difference (TD) learning (TD(0), TD(k), TD(λ)). The mathematical analysis is grounded in the Linear Stochastic Approximation (LSA) framework, differentiating between i.i.d. and Markovian noise. The challenges of linear function approximation, particularly divergence in off-policy settings, are discussed alongside solutions like Generalized Temporal Difference (GTD2) and Temporal-Difference with Gradient Correction (TDC), analyzed using two-time-scale stochastic approximation.

The survey expands to more realistic online interaction settings:
- **Forward Model Setting:** This chapter covers online reinforcement learning where an agent interacts sequentially with the environment without a generative model. It starts with an overview of Stochastic Multi-Armed Bandits (MAB), covering concepts like regret, the exploration-exploitation trade-off, and algorithms such as UCB and Thompson Sampling. For general MDPs, it provides lower bounds on regret and details model-based optimistic algorithms like UCRL and UCBVI (with Hoeffding and Bernstein bonuses). Model-free counterparts are also discussed, including Q-learning with exploration strategies. The approach further includes randomized algorithms like Posterior Sampling for Reinforcement Learning (PSRL) and Randomized Least-Squares Value Iteration (RLSVI), which leverage stochastic perturbations for exploration.

Finally, the paper addresses complex and modern extensions:
- **Continuous Setting:** This section tackles environments with infinite state or action spaces. Methods like sampling-based Fitted Value Iteration (FVI) are presented for continuous state spaces with a generative model. For online interaction in metric spaces, Kernel-UCBVI is introduced, using kernel smoothing and Lipschitz continuity assumptions. Q-learning with adaptive discretization and Least-Squares Value Iteration with UCB (LSVI-UCB) for linear MDPs are also covered, focusing on how complexity scales with covering numbers and feature dimensions.
- **Policy Gradient-Based Reinforcement Learning:** This section focuses on direct policy optimization using differentiable, parameterized policies. It covers the policy gradient theorem, REINFORCE, and actor-critic methods, including concepts like TD residuals, advantage estimation (e.g., GAE), and two-time-scale analysis. Trust-region methods (TRPO, PPO) and Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) are explained as strategies for stable policy updates, often incorporating entropy regularization.
- **Reinforcement Learning Applications in Natural Language Processing (NLP):** The survey concludes by contextualizing RL in the domain of large language models (LLMs). It describes sequence-level optimization, Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), and agentic RL for multi-step reasoning and tool use. Multi-agent RL formulations are also discussed in the context of LLM systems.

Throughout these sections, the authors unify algorithmic templates under common operator and variational lenses, presenting both finite-sample bounds and asymptotic results. The methodological approach is characterized by a hierarchical organization, starting from fundamental mathematical principles and progressively building towards complex algorithms and their applications, with a consistent focus on theoretical guarantees and analytical techniques.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

As a comprehensive survey, the paper does not present novel empirical or theoretical research findings but rather systematically compiles, organizes, and explains established mathematical findings and results that underpin modern reinforcement learning. The core contribution lies in this structured unification and detailed exposition.

The main findings and results synthesized in the paper include:

- **Fixed-Point Theory and Contraction Mappings in Dynamic Programming:** The paper details how optimal value functions and policies in MDPs are unique fixed points of Bellman operators. It reiterates the fundamental result that these operators are $\gamma$-contractions in the $L_\infty$-norm, ensuring the existence and uniqueness of solutions to Bellman equations. This property is shown to guarantee the linear convergence of Value Iteration and Policy Iteration, providing explicit convergence rates.
- **Convex Optimization Formulations:** The survey presents linear programming (LP) formulations for solving both discounted and average-reward MDPs. It highlights the concept of "hidden convexity" in RL problems, demonstrating how the non-convex optimization over policies can be recast into convex problems over occupancy measures, thereby enabling the application of convex optimization techniques and providing insights into the problem structure.
- **Sample Complexity Bounds for Generative Models:** The paper summarizes crucial results regarding the sample complexity required to achieve $\epsilon$-optimal policies in the generative model setting. It presents minimax optimal bounds for model-based approaches (e.g., $\tilde{O}(SA/((1-\gamma)^3 \epsilon^2))$ for discounted MDPs), emphasizing how these bounds are achieved by estimating environment dynamics and applying dynamic programming. For model-free Q-learning, it outlines the challenges of suboptimal scaling but also how techniques like Polyak-Ruppert averaging and variance reduction can achieve minimax optimality.
- **Convergence of Stochastic Approximation Algorithms:** For policy evaluation, the paper details the convergence properties of Temporal Difference (TD) learning algorithms within the Linear Stochastic Approximation (LSA) framework. It establishes that TD(0) converges almost surely to the true value function under appropriate conditions, particularly highlighting the role of martingale-difference sequences in the convergence analysis. Challenges and solutions for linear function approximation, including issues of divergence in off-policy settings and the development of stable algorithms like GTD2 and TDC, are discussed.
- **Regret Analysis for Online Reinforcement Learning:** A significant portion of the survey focuses on regret bounds for algorithms operating in the online setting (without a generative model), particularly for episodic MDPs and multi-armed bandits. It reviews information-theoretic lower bounds on regret (e.g., $\Omega(\sqrt{H^2 SAT})$ for episodic MDPs) and demonstrates how various algorithms achieve near-optimal performance.
 - **Optimistic Algorithms:** Methods like UCRL and UCBVI are presented as examples of the "Optimism in the Face of Uncertainty" (OFU) principle. UCBVI with Bernstein-type bonuses is shown to achieve regret bounds matching the lower bounds (e.g., $\tilde{O}(\sqrt{H^2 SAT})$), by carefully balancing exploration and exploitation.
 - **Randomized Algorithms:** The paper highlights randomized approaches like Posterior Sampling for Reinforcement Learning (PSRL) and Randomized Least-Squares Value Iteration (RLSVI). These methods achieve competitive regret bounds (e.g., $\tilde{O}(\sqrt{H^2 SAT})$ for SSR, a single-seed variant of RLSVI) by introducing noise into value function estimates or sampled models, providing an alternative to explicit optimistic bonuses.
- **Approaches for Continuous State Spaces:** For large or continuous state-action spaces, the paper outlines how traditional tabular methods are extended using function approximation. Results for Fitted Value Iteration (FVI) are presented, showing how its performance depends on the approximation power of the function class, concentrability coefficients, and covering numbers of the state space. Kernel-based methods (e.g., Kernel-UCBVI) leverage Lipschitz continuity and kernel smoothing, yielding regret bounds dependent on covering dimensions. LSVI-UCB for linear MDPs is shown to achieve regret bounds that scale polynomially with the feature dimension ($d$) rather than the state space size ($S$).
- **Policy Gradient Methods and Stability:** The fundamental policy gradient theorem is presented, alongside its application in REINFORCE and actor-critic algorithms. The paper discusses techniques for stabilizing policy updates, such as generalized advantage estimation (GAE), natural gradients, and trust-region methods like TRPO and PPO. It also touches upon Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) as an approach that bypasses explicit reward modeling and policy rollouts.
- **Mathematical Foundations of RL in NLP:** The survey demonstrates how the mathematical frameworks of RL are directly applied to training and aligning large language models (LLMs). It details the use of policy gradient methods for sequence-level optimization, the multi-stage pipeline of Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), agentic RL for multi-step reasoning, and multi-agent RL for coordinating LLM agents.

In summary, the paper's main "findings" are a comprehensive and coherent synthesis of established theoretical results across the diverse landscape of reinforcement learning, structured to highlight common mathematical principles and the performance guarantees associated with various algorithmic paradigms.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The significance and potential impact of this survey paper are substantial, particularly given the rapid growth and interdisciplinary nature of reinforcement learning.

- **Unified Theoretical Framework:** The primary impact is the provision of a unified mathematical framework for understanding a broad spectrum of RL algorithms. By organizing concepts from probability, optimization, and operator theory, the paper consolidates disparate theoretical strands. This unification is crucial for developing a holistic understanding of the field, moving beyond isolated algorithmic descriptions to a principled appreciation of their underlying mathematical structures.
- **Cross-Disciplinary Accessibility:** The explicit aim to serve as an entry point for researchers from probability, optimization, and statistics is highly impactful. It facilitates knowledge transfer and collaboration across these disciplines, potentially attracting new theoretical insights and methodological advancements into RL. By making complex RL mathematics accessible, it can accelerate research by a wider group of quantitative scientists.
- **Enhanced Algorithmic Design and Analysis:** For RL researchers and practitioners, the detailed exposition of convergence guarantees, sample complexity bounds, and regret analyses provides a strong theoretical foundation. This understanding can guide the design of new algorithms, inform hyperparameter tuning, and aid in predicting algorithmic performance under different environmental conditions. It clarifies why certain algorithms are stable, efficient, or prone to divergence, thus enabling more robust and theoretically sound development.
- **Guidance for Future Research:** By outlining various mathematical challenges—such as closing gaps in sample complexity bounds, ensuring stability in off-policy learning, or extending theoretical guarantees to continuous and high-dimensional spaces—the survey implicitly points towards fertile grounds for future theoretical research. The detailed discussion of hard instances for lower bounds is particularly valuable for identifying fundamental limits and inspiring algorithms that can overcome them.
- **Contextualizing Practical Applications:** The inclusion of a dedicated section on RL applications in Natural Language Processing, especially in the context of Large Language Models (LLMs), bridges the gap between abstract mathematical theory and cutting-edge practical deployments. This demonstrates the real-world relevance of the mathematical foundations, offering practitioners a deeper understanding of the mechanisms behind advanced techniques like RLHF, agentic RL, and multi-agent systems in LLMs.
- **Pedagogical Value:** The comprehensive and structured nature of the report, coupled with its neutral and factual tone, positions it as a valuable pedagogical resource. It can serve as a textbook or reference for advanced students and researchers, offering a rigorous yet comprehensible overview of the mathematical landscape of RL.

In conclusion, this paper serves as a significant contribution by synthesizing the fragmented mathematical literature of reinforcement learning into a cohesive and accessible framework. Its impact is likely to be felt across academic disciplines and industrial applications, fostering a deeper theoretical understanding and accelerating the development of more sophisticated and robust RL systems.
