# Human Preference Modeling Using Visual Motion Prediction Improves Robot Skill Learning from Egocentric Human Video

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Carnegie Mellon University developed a Motion Prediction Reward (MPR) system that extracts short-term human preferences from egocentric videos by predicting task-relevant object motion. Integrating this with a residual reinforcement learning framework allows robots to learn skills from minimal on-robot demonstrations, achieving up to 76.7% success on 'Open Microwave' and 73.3% on 'Fold Cloth' tasks with real hardware.

## Problem

- Collecting extensive on-robot teleoperation demonstrations is costly and time-consuming, often leading to limited task space coverage and a 'skill-gap'.
- Leveraging abundant egocentric human video for robot learning is challenging due to the embodiment mismatch between humans and robots, environmental differences, and noise in human actions.
- Prior reward learning methods based on temporal distance to a goal state are susceptible to noise, struggle to generalize across the human-robot gap, and may not accurately identify failed attempts.

## Method

- A Motion Prediction Reward (MPR) is introduced, which learns to predict the visual motion of task-relevant objects from preprocessed egocentric human videos using a modified Diffusion Transformer (DiT) architecture with a frozen DinoV2 visual encoder.
- The reward signal for robot actions is computed as the sum of positive cosine similarities between predicted and observed object motion vectors, indicating alignment with desired human outcomes.
- This learned reward is then used within a sample-efficient residual reinforcement learning framework, built upon a base diffusion policy trained with a small number of on-robot demonstrations, to refine robot skills.

## Results

- MPR consistently outperformed a temporal-distance-based baseline (VIP) in both simulation and real-world robot experiments, achieving success rates of **76.7%** for 'Open Microwave' and **73.3%** for 'Fold Cloth' on a Franka robot with minimal on-robot training.
- The method demonstrated superior sample efficiency and policy stability on hardware, with MPR runs consistently improving over the base policy, while VIP often showed 'unlearning' behavior.
- MPR accurately discriminated between successful and failed task episodes by assigning zero reward to failed attempts, whereas VIP assigned some positive reward to such failures, potentially leading the robot into local optima.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Modeling short-term human preferences through visual motion prediction offers a more robust and localized reward signal, less susceptible to noise and suboptimality in human demonstrations compared to long-term value estimation.
> - Focusing the reward on task-relevant object motion minimizes the embodiment gap between human and robot, enabling more effective knowledge transfer by emphasizing observable outcomes rather than human-specific actions.
> - The integration of a dense, outcome-oriented reward function with a stable residual reinforcement learning framework dramatically reduces the required number of on-robot interactions for skill acquisition and improvement.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a central challenge in robot learning: reducing the reliance on extensive and costly on-robot demonstrations to teach robots new skills. Traditional robot teleoperation demonstrations are expensive to collect and often cover only a limited portion of the task space, leading to a "skill-gap" where learned policies underperform.

A promising avenue for addressing this challenge involves leveraging the vast availability of human video demonstrations, particularly egocentric videos of humans performing household tasks. However, transferring information from human video to robots presents several difficulties, primarily due to the embodiment mismatch between human and robot actors and differences in environments.

Prior research has explored various strategies to bridge this gap:
- **Vision Encoders:** Learning general-purpose visual encoders from large egocentric video datasets (e.g., Ego4D, Epic Kitchens) using objectives like contrastive learning or masked autoencoding. These encoders primarily aim to reduce the visual learning burden or measure state similarity.
- **Vision-Language-Action (VLA) Models:** Adapting VLAs to use video generation models trained on human videos as backbones, showing improvements but often being computationally expensive.
- **World Models:** Training world models with human video data to forecast future actions.
- **Direct Translation of Human Videos:**
 - **Trajectory Mimicry:** Directly mimicking or forecasting object trajectories and then solving for robot actions to match them. These methods can learn from single demonstrations but often lack robustness, especially in contact-rich tasks, and may require additional robot data.
 - **Imitation Learning with Retargeting:** Tracking human hands and retargeting these actions to a robot's action space. These approaches can generalize but depend heavily on the retargeting quality and may require in-domain videos or significant robot data.
 - **Reinforcement Learning for Trajectory Tracking:** Using RL to improve trajectory tracking, which often requires complex simulation environments and sim-to-real transfer.

Within the domain of learning feedback signals (rewards or costs) from human video, a common strategy is to estimate the long-term value of a visual state as its temporal distance to a goal state (typically the last frame of a successful demonstration). This approach assigns lower value to states temporally further from the goal. While convenient for video data and successful in some contexts, this temporal-distance strategy has limitations:
- **Susceptibility to Noise:** Noise or non-time-optimal actions (e.g., hesitation, multitasking) in demonstrations can negatively bias the value of all preceding states.
- **Generalization Issues:** Difficulty in generalizing learned value functions across the significant distribution shift between human and robot videos.
- **Recognizing Failures:** Challenges in accurately recognizing failed attempts.

The presented work positions itself by proposing an alternative to these temporal-distance-based methods. Instead of estimating long-term value, it focuses on modeling short-term human preferences through visual motion prediction. The paper integrates this novel reward learning approach with a sample-efficient residual reinforcement learning framework, aiming to provide a robust and practical solution for online robot skill learning on real hardware with minimal on-robot data. It draws insights from existing work in off-policy and residual reinforcement learning to ensure stability and efficiency.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to dramatically reduce the number of on-robot demonstrations required for robots to learn basic skills. This reduction is sought by effectively transferring information from readily available egocentric human video demonstrations to robots.

The motivation for this objective stems from several identified limitations in current approaches:

1. **High Cost of On-Robot Demonstrations:** Collecting teleoperation demonstrations on a real robot is expensive and time-consuming. These datasets are often small and may not cover the full range of the task space, leading to a "skill-gap" where learned policies do not match expert performance.
2. **Challenges with Human Video Data:** While human videos offer a vast source of supervision, leveraging them for robot learning is difficult due to:
 - **Embodiment Gap:** Mismatches between human and robot body kinematics and action spaces.
 - **Environment Gap:** Differences in environments between human videos and robot operational settings.
 - **Noise and Suboptimality:** Human videos often contain successful demonstrations mixed with multitasking, hesitations, or non-time-optimal actions, which can mislead reward functions.
 - **Distribution Shift:** Learned functions from human videos must be robustly transferred to robot videos, which often have different visual characteristics.
3. **Limitations of Temporal Distance-Based Reward Learning:** Prior work that estimates state value based on temporal distance to the goal (e.g., last frame of a video) is particularly vulnerable to the noise and suboptimality in human demonstrations. A single hesitation can negatively bias the value of all preceding states. Furthermore, these methods struggle to generalize learned value functions across the human-to-robot embodiment gap and may not accurately identify failed attempts.

To overcome these challenges, the authors propose a novel approach based on modeling *short-term preferences* of human demonstrators at each step, rather than estimating long-term value. Their core hypothesis is that representing human preferences via visual motion features is a more effective way to learn reward functions from human video. This approach aims to:

- Provide a reward signal that is less susceptible to biases from hesitation or distractions within demonstrations.
- Mitigate the distribution shift between human and robot videos by focusing on transferable features (object motion).
- Incentivize the robot to match the *outcomes* of human actions (how task-relevant objects move) rather than directly imitating human movements, which may not be feasible or optimal for a robot.
- Enable a robot to rapidly improve its performance on a task with minimal on-robot interaction time (e.g., one hour).

Ultimately, the research seeks to create a robust and sample-efficient pipeline for robot skill learning that effectively leverages abundant human video data for real-world deployment.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology presented in this paper involves a multi-stage pipeline designed to transfer knowledge from egocentric human videos to robots for skill learning, leveraging a novel reward function and a residual reinforcement learning framework.

**Problem Formulation:**
The task involves learning a policy π for a specific task T, given a large set of human video demonstrations (V) and a small set of on-robot demonstrations (D). The goal is to derive a reward signal R(s, a) that, when maximized by adapting π, increases the robot's performance on task T. A key distinction is made between observations (o_t), which are single camera views available in both human and robot data, and states (s_t), which include visual and proprioceptive robot information, available only in robot data.

**A. Reward Learning as Modeling Expert Preferences**
The first stage focuses on training a motion prediction model, F_θ, from human video data.
1. **Human Video Preprocessing:**
 - Videos are selected from datasets like Ego4D and Epic Kitchens based on language tags for a given task T.
 - Task-relevant objects are identified, segmented, and tracked across frames using a combination of OWLViT2 (object detection), SAM2 (segmentation), and Cutie (mask tracking).
 - Videos are trimmed to ensure task-relevant objects are consistently visible.
 - A grid of points is tracked throughout the video using CoTracker3. These points are then categorized as "object points" (within masks) or "background points" (outside masks).
 - To compensate for egocentric camera motion, the mean motion of background points is subtracted from both object and background point deltas.
 - For training F_θ, all object points and a sampled subset of background points are used to focus on object motion while accounting for residual background movement.
2. **Motion Prediction Model (F_θ) Training:**
 - F_θ is designed to predict the locations of a set of points at time t+1 (P_t+1) given an observation at time t (o_t), the points' locations at time t (P_t), and a normalized task progress indicator (t/T).
 - The model is a modified Diffusion Transformer (DiT) architecture, adapted for regression rather than diffusion. It incorporates a cross-attention layer that uses visual tokens from a frozen DinoV2 pretrained vision transformer, providing robust visual features and spatial information to improve point motion prediction.
 - F_θ is trained using a simple regression loss: L(θ) = ||F_θ(o_t, P_t, t/T) - P_t+1||^2.
3. **Reward Signal Computation:**
 - For a robot's behavior episode, observations are extracted, and task-relevant object masks and points (P_track) are tracked.
 - F_θ is then used to predict point locations for each step (P_pred).
 - The reward signal (r_t) for a given frame t is calculated as the sum of positive cosine similarities between the predicted point motion vectors (∆p_pred_t) and the observed point motion vectors (∆p_track_t) for task-relevant points. This measures the alignment between desired (predicted) and actual (observed) object motion.
 - The reward range is shifted from to [-1, 0] by subtracting one to discourage the robot from merely manipulating objects indefinitely for positive reward without completing the task.

**Advantages of this Reward Approach:**
- **Localized Bias:** Unlike temporal distance-based methods, only the reward at time t is potentially biased by hesitation or distraction at that moment, not all preceding states.
- **Reduced Distribution Shift:** By focusing on point deltas of task-relevant objects, the approach minimizes the embodiment gap between human and robot, concentrating on features consistent across domains.
- **Outcome-Oriented:** It rewards the robot for matching the *outcomes* of human actions (object movements) rather than attempting to mimic the actions themselves, which may be inappropriate for a robot.
- **DAgger Analogy:** Optimizing this reward aligns with the DAgger principle of learning to match expert preferences in the learner's (robot's) own state distribution.

**B. Residual Reinforcement Learning**
The second stage utilizes the learned reward signal to refine a robot policy.
1. **Base Policy (π_base):** A diffusion policy is initially trained using the small set of on-robot demonstrations (D). This policy serves as a "kickstart" and is kept frozen during subsequent reinforcement learning.
2. **Residual RL Framework:** A modified Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) algorithm is employed. Instead of learning a full policy, it learns a residual policy (π_RL) that applies corrective actions (∆a) on top of the base policy: π(s) = π_base(s) + απ_RL(s), where α is a scalar limiting the magnitude of residual actions.
3. **Sample Efficiency and Stability Modifications:** Drawing from prior works (RLPD, SERL, ResiP, ResFit), the SAC implementation incorporates:
 - **Dual Critic Networks:** Using the minimum Q-value across multiple critic networks to mitigate overestimation in low-data regions.
 - **Layer Normalization:** Applying LayerNorm in critic networks to enhance learning stability.
 - **Mixed Replay Buffer:** Sampling transitions evenly from both the offline demonstration buffer (D) and an online buffer (B) containing data collected during robot interaction.
 - **Critic Pretraining:** Warm-starting the critic network by pretraining it on the offline demonstration data (D) labeled with rewards from the motion prediction model.
 - **Residual Action for Offline Data:** For offline data, the ground truth actions (a_t) are converted to residual actions (∆a_t) by subtracting the base policy's output (π_base(s_t)).
 - **Regularization:** Freezing the base policy and using the offline dataset (D) as a regularizer, along with limiting residual action magnitude, helps prevent the actor from deviating too far from the demonstration distribution.
4. **Hardware Considerations:** Reward computation and policy/critic updates are performed at the end of each episode to optimize for efficiency on real robot hardware. Observations for reward estimation are typically taken from a fixed "world camera." Robot control uses relative position deltas, with additional damping and clipping for rotation commands to enhance stability. A frozen pretrained DinoV2 encoder processes image components of the state.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates the proposed Motion Prediction Reward (MPR) approach against several baselines in both simulated and real-world robot settings.

**A. Simulation Results (Franka Kitchen Benchmark)**
- **Tasks:** "Open Microwave" and "Open Cabinet."
- **Baselines:** Sparse reward (1 for success, 0 otherwise), hand-designed dense reward (using privileged simulation state information), and Value Implicit Pretraining (VIP), a representative temporal-distance-based reward learning method. A behavior cloning policy (π_base) trained on 20 demonstrations initialized all methods.
- **Evaluation:** Models trained for 300,000 simulated steps, evaluated every 50,000 steps over 20 episodes, with results averaged across 8 random seeds.
- **Key Findings:**
 - **"Open Cabinet" Task:** MPR, VIP, and the handcrafted dense reward function all exhibited similar performance, significantly outperforming sparse reward and the initial behavior cloning policy.
 - **"Open Microwave" Task:** MPR clearly outperformed VIP and closely tracked the performance of the handcrafted dense reward function.
 - **Reward Signal Analysis:** A comparison of reward signals across successful "Open Microwave" demonstrations revealed that MPR's signal closely matched the handcrafted reward, showing minimal bias or false positives. In contrast, VIP exhibited biases in its reward signal for certain states, which could lead a learning agent into local minima and hinder learning efficiency.

**B. Hardware Experiments (Franka Research 3 Robot)**
- **Tasks:** "Open Microwave," "Fold Cloth," and "Wipe Counter."
- **Setup:** A Franka Research 3 robot with UMI fingers and two RealSense D435 cameras (world camera for reward, wrist camera for observation). Each task involved training a base diffusion policy with 10 teleoperated demonstrations, followed by 100 episodes (approx. 1-1.5 hours) of real-world interaction for residual RL refinement. Three independent runs were performed for each method and task.
- **Key Findings:**
 - **MPR Outperforms VIP:** MPR consistently outperformed VIP across all three hardware tasks (Table I). For "Open Microwave," MPR achieved a 76.7% success rate compared to VIP's 23.3% and the base policy's 45.0%. Similar trends were observed for "Fold Cloth" (MPR: 73.3% vs. VIP: 8.3% vs. Base: 45.0%) and "Wipe Counter" (MPR: 56.7% vs. VIP: 21.7% vs. Base: 40.0%).
 - **Sample Efficiency:** The significant performance difference on hardware, where training involved only ~15,000 steps (compared to 300,000 in simulation), indicated that MPR reward signals lead to more sample-efficient learning than VIP.
 - **Stability and "Unlearning":** VIP runs often showed issues with stability and demonstrated "unlearning" behavior (Figure 4), where performance would increase but then decrease, sometimes falling below the base policy's success rate. MPR runs, however, consistently improved over the base policy.
 - **Discrimination of Success/Failure:** Analysis of estimated reward and value functions for successful and failed episodes (Figure 5) revealed a critical difference:
 - Both MPR and VIP could recognize successful episodes.
 - In a failed episode (e.g., robot pulling back without grasping the microwave handle), MPR assigned no reward, and its learned value function correctly identified the failure, assigning low value to subsequent states.
 - VIP, however, still assigned some positive reward to such failed episodes, and its learned value function failed to recognize the task failure or the point of failure. This false positive reward for "going through the motions" could mislead the actor, leading to local maxima and the observed "forgetting" behavior during training.
 - **Base Policy Sensitivity:** In the "Wipe Counter" task, where the base policy trained on only 10 demos was inconsistent due to sensitivity to reset conditions, both reward models struggled with stability, highlighting the importance of a robust base policy for residual RL.

**C. Ablation Study (Appendix)**
- Removing object masking from MPR's training and inference pipeline (i.e., predicting motion for all points without compensating for camera motion) resulted in noisier reward signals and less sample-efficient learning in simulation, confirming the benefit of focusing on task-relevant objects and camera motion compensation.

**D. Comparison to RoboReward (Appendix)**
- When compared in simulation, RoboReward (an 8-billion-parameter vision-language reward foundation model providing sparse, episodic rewards) underperformed all methods that provided dense, per-step rewards, including MPR and VIP.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a notable advancement in robot skill learning by introducing a novel approach to derive reward signals from egocentric human video. The significance and potential impact of this work are multifaceted:

1. **Reduced Reliance on On-Robot Demonstrations:** The core contribution lies in significantly lowering the barrier for robot skill acquisition by demonstrating that robots can learn and rapidly improve complex skills on real hardware with a minimal number of costly on-robot demonstrations (as few as 10 demonstrations coupled with approximately one hour of real-world training). This addresses a critical bottleneck in the widespread deployment of robots for various tasks.

2. **Robust and Sample-Efficient Reward Learning:** The Motion Prediction Reward (MPR) approach offers a more robust and sample-efficient alternative to existing methods, particularly those relying on temporal distance for value estimation. By focusing on predicting the visual motion of task-relevant objects, MPR overcomes key limitations:
 - **Tolerance to Noise and Suboptimality:** It is less susceptible to biases introduced by hesitations, distractions, or non-time-optimal actions in human demonstrations, as it models short-term preferences rather than long-term value.
 - **Bridging the Embodiment Gap:** By concentrating on object motion features, which are more consistent across human and robot embodiments, MPR facilitates more effective transfer of knowledge from human videos.
 - **Improved Discrimination of Success/Failure:** MPR's ability to accurately differentiate between genuinely successful task executions and superficial motions (as demonstrated in the failed microwave task scenario) leads to more accurate reward signals, preventing the robot from converging to undesirable local optima. This robustness is crucial for stable reinforcement learning in real-world environments.

3. **Enhanced Robot Policy Stability and Performance:** The integration of MPR with a carefully designed residual reinforcement learning framework, incorporating insights from state-of-the-art RL techniques (e.g., dual critics, LayerNorm, mixed replay buffers), enables stable and consistent policy improvement. The observed increase in success rates (e.g., 31.7% for "Open Microwave," 28.3% for "Fold Cloth") over initial behavior-cloned policies, while VIP struggled with "unlearning," underscores the practical efficacy of the proposed method.

4. **Foundation for Dense Reward Models:** The work highlights the potential for "dense reward foundation models" in robotics. By demonstrating that detailed, per-step feedback derived from abundant, unstructured human video can drive robot learning, it opens avenues for developing more general-purpose and scalable reward models for a wide range of tasks. This could democratize access to robot skill learning by reducing the need for expert human engineering in reward function design.

5. **Future Research Directions:** The authors identify several promising avenues for future work, including the development of language-conditioned multi-task variants of MPR, exploring integration with world models for policy optimization, and further refinement of motion prediction models using advanced techniques like diffusion or flow-matching architectures. These directions suggest that the current work is a foundational step towards even more capable and versatile robot learning systems.

In summary, this research contributes a practical and effective method for transferring human expertise from video to robots, making significant strides toward more autonomous and adaptive robotic systems capable of learning complex skills with reduced human intervention and data requirements.
