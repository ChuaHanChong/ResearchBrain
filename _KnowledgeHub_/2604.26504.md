# HiPAN: Hierarchical Posture-Adaptive Navigation for Quadruped Robots in Unstructured 3D Environments

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at KAIST developed HiPAN, a hierarchical framework enabling quadruped robots to autonomously navigate unstructured 3D environments. It integrates strategic goal-seeking with adaptive body posture adjustments using onboard depth images, achieving high success rates (e.g., 94.7% in complex simulations) and demonstrating robust real-world performance.

## Problem

- Quadruped robots struggle with long-horizon goal-directed motion, often getting trapped in local minima in complex, unstructured 3D environments.
- Integrating adaptive body posture (crouching, tilting) into autonomous navigation for confined spaces typically relies on reactive or manual commands, lacking strategic integration.
- Traditional navigation systems for 3D environments are computationally intensive due to explicit mapping and planning, limiting real-time performance on resource-constrained robotic platforms.

## Method

- **HiPAN** employs a two-level ==hierarchical reinforcement learning== framework, comprising a high-level policy for strategic navigation and a low-level policy for posture-adaptive locomotion.
- A ==teacher-student training== paradigm, coupled with extensive domain randomization, enables robust sim-to-real transfer and allows the system to operate directly on onboard depth images.
- ==Path-Guided Curriculum Learning== (**PGCL**) and an intrinsic reward mechanism are utilized to encourage non-myopic exploration and guide the agent toward efficient long-horizon navigation.

## Results

- HiPAN achieved superior performance in simulation, demonstrating **94.7%** success rate and 83.6 SPL in the most challenging 'Complex-2' environment, outperforming baselines and ablation variants.
- Ablation studies confirmed the critical roles of both posture adaptation and Path-Guided Curriculum Learning, as their removal led to significant performance degradation in terms of success rate and path efficiency.
- Real-world experiments on a Unitree Go1 robot validated HiPAN's robustness, showcasing successful goal-directed, posture-adaptive navigation in diverse scenarios, including cluttered indoor, dead-end, and outdoor environments with varying lighting, using only onboard depth perception.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Hierarchical policy decomposition effectively manages the complexity of learning strategic navigation and precise locomotion, facilitating robust real-world deployment.
> - Path-Guided Curriculum Learning is crucial for overcoming myopic behavior and sparse rewards, enabling agents to learn efficient, long-horizon navigation strategies in challenging 3D environments.
> - Explicitly commanding body posture at the high-level allows for strategic adaptation to spatial constraints, significantly improving the robot's ability to traverse confined or height-limited spaces.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Quadruped robots are increasingly being deployed in complex, unstructured three-dimensional (3D) environments such such as disaster zones, inspection sites, and rough terrains. Navigating these environments requires capabilities beyond simple planar movement, including goal-directed strategic motion, effective exploration to avoid or escape local minima, and adaptive body posture adjustment to traverse confined or height-constrained spaces.

Current approaches to quadruped robot navigation typically fall into a few categories, each with limitations that this work aims to address:

- **Conventional Mapping-Planning Pipelines:** These systems integrate 3D mapping with path planning to generate locomotion commands. However, they are often computationally and memory intensive, which can be a significant drawback for resource-constrained robotic platforms. This overhead can restrict real-time performance and introduce errors due to accumulated perception inaccuracies.
- **Local Perception-Based Navigation:** Algorithms such as Wall-Following and BUG offer computational and memory efficiency by relying on immediate local perception and relative goal position. Their heuristic nature, however, often leads to inefficient paths and failures in cluttered or highly unstructured environments.
- **Posture-Adaptive Locomotion:** Recent advances in reinforcement learning (RL) have enabled quadruped robots to adapt their body posture (e.g., crouching, tilting) to handle spatial constraints, allowing them to traverse narrow passages or pass under obstacles. Nevertheless, most of these methods are primarily focused on reactive obstacle avoidance or depend on manual commands for high-level navigation, thereby limiting the robot's autonomy in unknown or dynamic environments.
- **Reinforcement Learning for Goal-Directed Navigation:** RL has shown promise for generating goal-directed navigation strategies from local perception, reducing the need for hand-crafted rules. A common issue with these approaches, particularly when optimized solely with distance-to-goal rewards, is myopic behavior. Agents may prioritize immediate progress, leading to entrapment in local minima such as dead-end corridors or enclosed rooms, especially in complex unstructured settings. Furthermore, existing RL methods with structured learning schemes (like subgoal-based approaches) largely focus on planar navigation, lacking the 3D posture adaptation crucial for navigating complex environments with varying vertical clearances.

This research contributes to the field by proposing a hierarchical framework that aims to overcome these limitations. It integrates strategic, long-horizon goal-directed navigation with explicit posture adaptation, operating directly from onboard depth images without explicit 3D mapping. By combining hierarchical control with structured curriculum learning, this work extends the capabilities of RL-based navigation to complex 3D environments, addressing both efficient long-range traversal and adaptability to spatial constraints.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable quadruped robots to perform robust, autonomous, and long-horizon navigation in unstructured 3D environments. This overarching goal is motivated by several key challenges inherent to these environments and the limitations of existing robotic navigation paradigms.

The detailed objectives and motivations are as follows:

- **Enabling Goal-Directed Motion and Escape from Local Minima:** Unstructured 3D environments frequently present dead-ends, cluttered layouts, and semi-enclosed spaces. Robots need to move strategically towards a goal while also possessing the ability to explore and backtrack to escape local minima. Current RL-based navigation policies, when trained solely on goal proximity, often exhibit myopic behavior, leading them to get trapped in such situations. This research is motivated to develop strategies that encourage effective, non-greedy exploration for long-horizon tasks.
- **Integrating Adaptive Body Posture for Confined Spaces:** Traversing environments with limited horizontal or vertical clearance (e.g., low-hanging obstacles, narrow passages) necessitates adaptive body posture adjustments like crouching or tilting. While some progress has been made in posture-adaptive locomotion, these capabilities are often reactive or manually commanded, rather than integrated into an autonomous, goal-directed navigation strategy. A key objective is to allow the robot to autonomously decide when and how to adapt its posture based on perceived environmental constraints.
- **Reducing Computational Overhead and Reliance on Explicit Mapping:** Traditional autonomous navigation systems rely on computationally intensive processes of explicit 3D mapping and global path planning. These processes can incur high computational and memory overhead, limiting their deployment on resource-constrained robotic platforms and making real-time performance challenging. This work aims to develop a system that operates directly on raw onboard sensory data (depth images) during deployment, thereby removing the need for explicit map reconstruction and reducing computational requirements.
- **Improving Robustness and Generalization:** The ability of robots to operate in diverse, unseen environments is crucial for practical applications. Existing heuristic or reactive methods often fail in highly complex or dynamic settings. The objective is to develop a learning-based framework that can generalize to various unstructured layouts, dynamic obstacles, and environmental conditions (e.g., lighting variations).
- **Leveraging Hierarchical Control:** Complex robotic tasks benefit from decomposition into modular sub-tasks. For navigation, separating high-level strategic planning from low-level motor control can enhance training efficiency, improve the interpretability of each component, and allow for robust pre-training and reuse of low-level controllers across different tasks. The motivation is to design a hierarchical framework where a high-level policy makes strategic navigation decisions, which are then executed by a robust, posture-adaptive low-level locomotion controller.
- **Addressing Learning Challenges with Curriculum Design:** Training reinforcement learning agents for long-horizon navigation in complex environments can be challenging due to sparse rewards and the need for complex exploratory behaviors. The motivation is to introduce structured learning schemes, such as curriculum learning, to progressively increase task difficulty and guide the agent towards acquiring both reactive obstacle avoidance skills and strategic exploration behaviors, thereby mitigating the problem of myopic policies.

In summary, the research is motivated by the need for quadruped robots to achieve truly autonomous and versatile navigation in demanding 3D environments, blending strategic pathfinding with agile physical adaptation, while remaining computationally efficient for onboard deployment.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework, Hierarchical Posture-Adaptive Navigation (HiPAN), employs a two-level hierarchy of reinforcement learning policies. These policies are optimized in a stage-wise manner using a teacher-student paradigm to enable robust sim-to-real transfer and real-time operation on onboard sensors.

#### 3.1. Task and Environment Formulation

The navigation task involves guiding a quadruped robot to a target goal position `g ∈ R^3` within unstructured 3D environments. These environments feature challenges such as uneven terrain, overhanging structures requiring posture adjustments, and local trap regions (e.g., dead-end corridors) that demand non-greedy navigation strategies. Environments are procedurally generated using the Wave Function Collapse algorithm, which assembles terrain tiles (flat, rough, obstructed surfaces) stochastically and incorporates randomly placed obstacles like walls, tables, and floating boxes to create diverse layouts and trap regions.

#### 3.2. Low-Level Locomotion Policy with Posture Adaptation (π_L)

The low-level policy is responsible for executing high-level navigation commands by translating them into coordinated joint actions, while maintaining robustness to physical variations and external perturbations.

- **Navigation Command (c):** The high-level policy outputs a five-dimensional vector `c = [v_c_x, v_c_y, ω_c_z, h_c, θ_c_x]`. The first three elements specify desired forward, lateral, and yaw velocities in the body frame, controlling planar motion. The last two elements define the target body height and roll angle in the world frame, enabling posture adjustments for confined spaces.
- **Policy Learning:** A two-stage teacher-student framework is used for robust sim-to-real transfer.
 - **Teacher Policy (π_T_L):** Trained via Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) in a fully observable Markov Decision Process (MDP). The teacher policy maximizes expected return across a broad range of command spaces, which is gradually expanded using a grid-adaptive curriculum by introducing higher planar velocity commands only after postural commands are reliably tracked.
 - **Student Policy (π_S_L):** Trained under partial observability using only onboard sensory inputs. It is distilled from the optimized teacher policy using the Dataset Aggregation (DAgger) algorithm. The student learns to imitate the teacher's actions and estimate privileged information (e.g., domain latent vector, motion states) that is only available in simulation.
- **Policy Architecture:** Both teacher and student policies utilize identical backbone networks that output joint displacements as actions, which are then fed to proportional-derivative (PD) controllers.
 - **Teacher Inputs:** Proprioceptive observations (joint positions/velocities, foot contact, body orientation, angular velocity, previous action), the navigation command `c`, privileged motion states (body linear velocity, height, roll angle), and privileged domain parameters (local terrain heightmaps, randomized variables like friction). Domain parameters are embedded into a latent vector by an encoder.
 - **Student Inputs:** A history of proprioceptive observations and the current navigation command `c`. To compensate for the lack of privileged information, the student policy incorporates a domain estimator and a motion estimator, which predict domain features and motion states from the proprioceptive history.

#### 3.3. High-Level Navigation Policy (π_H)

The high-level policy acts as the strategic navigation layer, generating commands `c` to guide the robot toward goals, making decisions for posture adaptation, obstacle avoidance, and non-greedy exploration.

- **Policy Learning:** Also uses a teacher-student paradigm.
 - **Teacher Policy (π_T_H):** Optimized via PPO in a fully observable MDP. The low-level student policy `π_S_L` is embedded within the high-level transition dynamics to account for execution effects.
 - **Student Policy (π_S_H):** Distilled from the teacher using DAgger. It collects depth image observations and is trained to imitate the teacher's command `c` and spatial-perceptual latent features. This enables the student to rely solely on onboard depth perception during deployment.
- **Path-Guided Curriculum Learning (PGCL):** This strategy addresses myopic behavior and promotes effective long-horizon exploration.
 - The navigation task is broken down into a sequence of progressively harder sub-goal-reaching tasks.
 - Initially, the agent receives multiple intermediate sub-goals along a privileged global path (computed using map information). The distance between sub-goals (`d`) increases incrementally as the agent masters each curriculum level.
 - This curriculum starts with reactive obstacle avoidance and gradually extends the navigation horizon to direct start-to-goal traversal.
 - An Intrinsic Reward (IR) based on state visitation counts is also incorporated to encourage general state exploration.
- **Policy Architecture:** Teacher and student policies share a common backbone architecture.
 - **Teacher Inputs:** Privileged 3D occupancy and 2.5D elevation local maps around the robot (dual-map design for efficiency), high-level proprioceptive observations, privileged motion states, and the relative sub-goal position. A spatial-perception encoder maps the map information into a latent embedding.
 - **Student Inputs:** Onboard depth images from a front-facing camera, high-level proprioceptive observations, estimated motion states from the low-level motion estimator, and relative goal information. A spatial-perception estimator, using recurrent memory, encodes the depth image into a latent vector.

#### 3.4. Implementation Details

The framework is implemented using the Unitree Go1 robot and the Isaac Gym simulator for parallel data collection. PPO is used for teacher policy optimization, and DAgger for student distillation. The high-level policy operates at 10 Hz, and the low-level at 50 Hz. To bridge the sim-to-real gap, domain randomization is applied to robot parameters (payload, CoM offset, friction, etc.), random body-force impulses are injected, and simulated depth images are augmented with various noise and blurring techniques, alongside camera pose randomization. A* search over a Generalized Voronoi Diagram is used to generate intermediate sub-goals for PGCL. Reward functions for both levels include task-specific and regularization terms.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of HiPAN involved extensive experiments in both simulation and real-world environments, utilizing benchmark environments and standard metrics such as Success Rate (SR) and Success weighted by Path Length (SPL).

#### 4.1. Simulation Results

Quantitative results (Table IV) demonstrated HiPAN's performance across four procedurally generated unstructured 3D environments (Corridor, Room, Complex-1, Complex-2), which varied in complexity.

- **Overall Performance:** HiPAN (Ours) consistently achieved the highest average success rates and path efficiencies across all environments. For example, in the most complex environment, Complex-2, HiPAN achieved 94.7% SR and 83.6 SPL.
- **Role of Posture Adaptation:** A HiPAN variant without posture adjustment (`HiPAN w/o Pos. Adjustment`) showed higher SR and SPL than classical navigation methods (Bug Algorithm, Wall-Following). However, it exhibited lower SPL than the full HiPAN in environments requiring posture changes (e.g., Room: 74.1 SPL vs. 88.8 SPL for HiPAN), indicating that the inability to adapt posture led to less efficient detours around overhanging obstacles. Classical methods largely failed in complex environments due to dense obstacles and irregular layouts.
- **Contributions of Path-Guided Curriculum Learning (PGCL) and Intrinsic Reward (IR):** Ablation studies confirmed the importance of both components. Removing PGCL (`HiPAN w/o PGCL`) resulted in a more significant performance degradation (e.g., Complex-2: 74.5% SR, 66.5 SPL) than removing IR (`HiPAN w/o IR`) (e.g., Complex-2: 90.7% SR, 82.1 SPL). This suggests that structured guidance from PGCL is particularly critical for long-horizon navigation. The combination of IR (for general exploration) and PGCL (for task-relevant states) was found to be complementary, leading to the superior performance of the full HiPAN framework.
- **Comparison with End-to-End RL:** The `Flat-RL` baseline, an end-to-end policy, performed significantly worse across all environments (e.g., Complex-2: 43.7% SR, 40.1 SPL). This was attributed to a higher learning burden, which led to myopic navigation strategies, frequent collisions, and a tendency to get stuck or take inefficient detours, especially when a direct path was unavailable.
- **Alternative Low-Level Posture Adaptation:** When HiPAN was integrated with alternative implicit posture-adaptive locomotion policies (`HiPAN w/ RPL` and `HiPAN w/ MoE-Loco`), performance was slightly degraded compared to the explicit posture commands in HiPAN (Ours). This was due to limitations in joint exploration of velocity and posture at the navigation level, distributional shifts, or reliance on physical contact for adaptation, leading to increased collisions (Figure 7). PGCL remained essential for long-horizon navigation in these variants as well.

#### 4.2. Real-World Experiments

Real-world validation was conducted on a Unitree Go1 robot equipped with a RealSense D435i camera and an Intel NUC for onboard processing. Sensor noise was mitigated by clipping, normalizing, and hole-filling depth images.

- **Diverse Scenarios:** Four real-world scenarios were presented, including cluttered indoor environments, dead-end corridors requiring backtracking, areas with abrupt illumination changes, and outdoor settings with direct sunlight, uneven terrain, and novel obstacles (bicycles, traffic cones).
- **Successful Deployment:** HiPAN successfully guided the robot to goal positions in all tested scenarios, demonstrating posture-adaptive navigation, effective recovery from local minima, and robustness to varying lighting conditions and unseen obstacles. The robot operated solely on onboard depth perception.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework that addresses several critical challenges in autonomous navigation for quadruped robots, with significant implications for their deployment in complex real-world environments.

- **Enhanced Autonomous Navigation Capabilities:** HiPAN enables quadruped robots to perform long-horizon navigation in unstructured 3D environments, incorporating both strategic goal-seeking and adaptive body posture adjustments. This capability goes beyond previous reactive or manually-commanded systems, increasing robot autonomy in challenging terrains.
- **Improved Efficiency and Robustness:** The hierarchical design combined with Path-Guided Curriculum Learning (PGCL) and intrinsic rewards allows for more efficient and robust navigation. The system successfully avoids common pitfalls like local minima and myopic behaviors seen in simpler RL approaches, while adapting to spatial constraints without relying on detailed pre-existing maps.
- **Practical Applicability and Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The demonstrated success in real-world environments using only onboard depth perception validates the practical applicability of the framework. The teacher-student training paradigm, coupled with extensive domain randomization and depth image augmentation, effectively bridges the simulation-to-reality gap, making the approach deployable on physical hardware.
- **Computational Efficiency:** By operating directly on onboard depth images and avoiding explicit 3D map reconstruction, HiPAN offers a computationally efficient solution suitable for resource-constrained robotic platforms, enabling real-time performance.
- **Methodological Contributions:**
 - The hierarchical policy structure effectively decomposes the complex navigation problem, making it more manageable and interpretable.
 - The Path-Guided Curriculum Learning method provides a structured and effective way to train RL agents for long-horizon tasks, overcoming the challenges of sparse rewards and local minima.
 - The explicit inclusion of body posture commands in the high-level policy allows for strategic, rather than purely reactive, adaptation to environmental constraints.

**Potential Impact:**
The advancements presented in HiPAN could significantly impact the use of quadruped robots in various domains:

- **Disaster Response and Search & Rescue:** Robots could autonomously navigate complex, damaged environments to locate survivors or assess damage, adapting their posture to squeeze through rubble or under debris.
- **Industrial Inspection:** Robots could perform autonomous inspections in cluttered factory floors, construction sites, or hazardous facilities, adjusting their height or roll to maneuver around machinery and under pipes.
- **Exploration of Unknown Environments:** For scientific exploration or military applications, robots could venture into unknown or hostile territories, autonomously navigating diverse terrains and confined spaces.

The research also identifies areas for future work, specifically addressing the limitations of depth sensors with transparent or specular surfaces. Incorporating semantic awareness to identify such obstacles and interpret environmental cues (e.g., exit signs) represents a logical next step to further enhance navigation effectiveness and robustness in real-world scenarios.
