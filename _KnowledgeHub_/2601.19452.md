# APC-RL: Exceeding Data-Driven Behavior Priors with Adaptive Policy Composition

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Örebro University developed Adaptive Policy Composition (APC), a hierarchical reinforcement learning method that robustly utilizes multiple demonstration datasets, even when misaligned or suboptimal. The approach enabled agents to achieve optimal performance in challenging environments, for example, reaching 100% success on misaligned PointMaze tasks and surpassing suboptimal human demonstrations in CarRacing within 30k steps.

## Problem

- Existing demonstration-guided reinforcement learning methods often implicitly assume demonstrations are optimal, comprehensive, and perfectly aligned with the target task.
- This assumption is frequently violated in real-world settings, where demonstrations can be sparse, suboptimal, or misaligned, leading to performance degradation, sample inefficiency, or learning failures.
- Strict adherence to imperfect data can hinder exploration, introduce negative biases, and prevent agents from discovering truly optimal policies.

## Method

- APC proposes a hierarchical framework with a higher-level 'learning-free ==arbitrator==' that selects among multiple lower-level actors based on their estimated value functions.
- The lower-level actors include both 'prior-free' policies learning from scratch and 'prior-based' policies, each pre-trained with a Normalizing Flow on a distinct demonstration dataset.
- A reward-sharing mechanism, leveraging the invertibility of Normalizing Flows, ensures all actors are updated with every executed action, preventing sample inefficiency and allowing continuous learning across all components.

## Results

- APC achieved **100%** success in approximately **0.5M** steps on misaligned PointMaze tasks, significantly outperforming baselines like PARROT and Imitation Learning (IL) which showed minimal success (0-**7%**).
- In the CarRacing environment, APC reached optimal returns (around 900) in about 30k steps using suboptimal human demonstrations, surpassing from-scratch SAC and other demonstration-guided baselines.
- Ablation studies confirmed that both the learning-free arbitrator and the reward-sharing mechanism are critical for APC's robust exploration, accelerated learning, and mitigation of primacy bias.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Composing multiple policies, including a prior-free actor and several prior-based actors, allows the agent to robustly leverage imperfect demonstration data while retaining the flexibility to diverge from unhelpful priors.
> - A 'learning-free' arbitrator, which dynamically switches between actors based on their estimated values, effectively mitigates primacy bias and avoids the instability typically associated with training a high-level selector.
> - The reward-sharing trick ensures all lower-level actors continuously receive learning signals, promoting balanced exploration and enabling faster convergence even when one actor initially outperforms others.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) frequently utilizes demonstration data to expedite learning, particularly in tasks that are otherwise challenging for agents to explore efficiently. Existing methods for incorporating demonstrations, such as policy regularization (imitation learning) or generative modeling of actions, often implicitly assume that demonstrations are optimal, comprehensive, and perfectly aligned with the target task. This assumption is frequently violated in real-world scenarios, where demonstrations can be sparse, suboptimal, or misaligned with the specific reward function or environmental conditions of the online RL task. Strict adherence to such imperfect data can lead to performance degradation, sample inefficiency, or a complete failure to learn optimal policies.

This paper addresses a critical gap in the field by proposing a method that does not rely on these stringent assumptions about demonstration quality. It integrates into the landscape of hierarchical RL and learning from demonstrations, specifically focusing on overcoming the brittleness of prior methods when faced with suboptimal or misaligned behavioral priors. Previous work, such as PARROT, uses Normalizing Flows (NFs) to create data-driven priors, but the paper argues that escaping a misaligned NF prior in practice is difficult. The proposed approach aims to provide a more flexible and robust mechanism for leveraging demonstration data by adaptively combining multiple priors, including a non-demonstration-based fallback, and dynamically deciding their applicability based on online feedback. It aims to bridge the gap between data-driven initialization or guidance and pure reward-driven adaptation, allowing RL agents to benefit from imperfect prior knowledge without being constrained by its limitations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the practical limitations of current demonstration-guided reinforcement learning (RL) techniques. While incorporating demonstration data can significantly accelerate RL, existing approaches often implicitly assume that these demonstrations are optimal and perfectly aligned with the target task. This assumption is frequently unrealistic in practical settings, where demonstrations may be sparse, suboptimal, or originate from tasks that are related but not identical to the target task (misaligned). When such imperfect demonstrations are rigidly integrated into the learning process, they can hinder exploration, introduce negative biases, and ultimately degrade the agent's performance.

The key objectives of the proposed Adaptive Policy Composition (APC) method are:

1. **Develop a flexible framework for integrating demonstration data:** Design a method that does not rely on the assumption of optimal and complete demonstrations, allowing the system to adaptively decide when and how long to rely on the provided data based on online feedback.
2. **Ensure robustness under demonstration misalignment:** Create a system capable of accelerating learning when demonstrations are aligned, while simultaneously remaining robust and avoiding performance degradation even when demonstrations are severely misaligned with the target task. This involves the ability to identify and sidestep unhelpful priors.
3. **Leverage suboptimal demonstrations for efficient exploration:** Enable the agent to use suboptimal demonstrations as a bootstrap for exploration, guiding initial learning without imposing a strict performance ceiling. The system should be able to surpass the performance level of the provided suboptimal demonstrations.
4. **Allow for divergence from demonstration priors:** Provide a mechanism for the agent to learn entirely new behaviors or diverge from all given demonstration priors if they prove insufficient or counterproductive for optimizing the online RL task. This is achieved through the inclusion of a "prior-free" learning component.
5. **Improve sample efficiency and mitigate primacy bias:** Introduce architectural and algorithmic mechanisms to ensure efficient training across multiple learning components and prevent early suboptimal learning decisions from permanently biasing the policy ("primacy bias").

In summary, the research is motivated by the need for more adaptable and resilient RL systems that can effectively utilize imperfect real-world demonstration data, transforming a potential liability into a consistent advantage for learning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Adaptive Policy Composition (APC) method is a hierarchical reinforcement learning approach designed to leverage multiple demonstration datasets without strict adherence, offering flexibility to adapt to online feedback.

#### 3.1. Compositional Policy Model

APC comprises a higher-level selector and multiple lower-level actors.
- **Lower-Level Actors:** The system includes $n+1$ actors.
 - **Prior-Free Actor:** One actor, denoted $\pi^{(0)}_a$, learns from scratch. It uses an identity Normalizing Flow (NF) $T^{(0)}(z; s) = z$, meaning its latent policy $\pi^{(0)}_z$ learns directly in the action space $A$.
 - **Prior-Based Actors:** $n$ actors, denoted $\pi^{(l)}_a$ for $1 \le l \le n$, are each associated with a distinct demonstration dataset $D^{(l)}$. Each of these actors pre-trains its own behavior prior using a Normalizing Flow (NF) model, $T^{(l)}(z; s) = a$. These NFs map latent actions $z$ from a base distribution to the complex, possibly multi-modal, per-state action distribution observed in their respective demonstration datasets. Each prior-based actor then learns a latent policy $\pi^{(l)}_z$ in the NF's latent space $Z$. The NF parameters are fixed after pre-training and are not updated during online learning.
- **Higher-Level Selector:** A categorical distribution $\pi_\beta = \text{Cat}(p_0(s), p_1(s), \ldots, p_n(s))$ decides which of the $n+1$ lower-level actors to execute in a given state $s$. The overall policy $\pi(a|s)$ is a mixture over these lower-level actors, weighted by the selector's probabilities.

#### 3.2. Lower-Level Actor Learning

Each of the $n+1$ latent policies $\pi^{(i)}_z$ is independently optimized using a Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) algorithm. When a specific lower-level actor is chosen by the selector at time $t$, it samples a latent action $z_t$ from its policy, which is then transformed into an environment action $a_t = T^{(\beta_t)}(z_t; s_t)$ using its corresponding NF (or identity flow for the prior-free actor). The resulting transition $(s_t, z_t, r_t, s_{t+1})$ is used to update the selected actor. Separate replay buffers $B^{(0)}, B^{(1)}, \ldots, B^{(n)}$ are maintained for each latent policy.

#### 3.3. Reward Sharing Trick for Balanced Actor Updates

To address sample inefficiency and prevent primacy bias (where an initially well-performing actor dominates selection and prevents others from catching up), APC employs a reward sharing mechanism.
- It leverages the invertibility of NFs: any executed action $a_t$ can be mapped into the latent space of *every* prior-based actor via $z_t^{(i)} = \tilde{T}^{(i)}(a_t; s_t)$.
- At each time step, after an action $a_t$ is executed by the selected actor $\beta_t$, a transition $(s_t, z_t, r_t, s_{t+1})$ is generated for *all* $n+1$ actors (using their respective latent representations $z_t^{(i)}$).
- All replay buffers $B^{(i)}$ are populated with this shared experience, allowing all actors to update continuously, regardless of which one produced the executed action.

#### 3.4. High-Level Selector Design

The high-level selector $\pi_\beta$ is implemented as a "learning-free arbitrator."
- Instead of training a separate neural network policy for the selector, the probabilities $p_l(s)$ of selecting actor $l$ are derived directly from the value estimates $V^{(l)}(s)$ of the lower-level actors using a Boltzmann distribution:
 $p_l(s) = \frac{1}{Z} \exp\left(\frac{1}{\eta} V^{(l)}(s)\right)$, where $Z$ is a normalization constant and $\eta$ is a temperature parameter.
- Since SAC does not directly provide a value function, $V^{(l)}(s)$ is approximated using Monte Carlo estimates of each lower-level agent's Q-function (sampling a single latent action $z^{(l)}$ and evaluating $Q^{(l)}(s, z^{(l)})$).
- This design eliminates the computational overhead of training a separate high-level policy and helps mitigate instabilities and primacy bias common in hierarchical RL.

#### 3.5. Normalizing Flow Pre-training Details

Each NF prior is pre-trained using maximum-likelihood estimation on its respective demonstration dataset. To improve stability, especially for low-variance action distributions, two regularization terms are added:
- **Inverse-consistency penalty ($L_{ic}$):** Encourages nearby actions in real space to map to similar latent codes.
- **Forward-smoothness penalty ($L_{fs}$):** Promotes local smoothness in the mapping from latent to real actions.
The NF parameters are kept fixed during the online RL phase.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates APC across various continuous-control benchmarks, including Maze Navigation, Franka Kitchen, and Car Racing, comparing its performance against SAC (from-scratch), Imitation Learning (IL), QFilter, and PARROT. The experiments focus on APC's robustness under demonstration misalignment, efficiency with aligned demonstrations, and ability to exceed suboptimal demonstrations.

#### 4.1. Robustness Under Demonstration Misalignment

- **PointMaze Navigation:** When provided with demonstrations for one task and evaluated on three misaligned tasks, PARROT and IL showed minimal to no success (0% and 7% average success rates, respectively, after 1.5M steps). In contrast, APC reliably converged to 100% success in approximately 0.5M steps. APC even surpassed the performance of from-scratch SAC, suggesting an ability to exploit misaligned priors for exploration without being constrained by their inaccuracies. QFilter avoided complete failure but learned slower than APC.
- **Franka Kitchen:** In the higher-dimensional manipulation environment, APC consistently solved target tasks even when provided with misaligned demonstrations. PARROT and IL experienced severe sample efficiency losses or outright failure in these scenarios, highlighting their brittleness. This demonstrated APC's capacity to bypass misaligned priors in complex environments.

#### 4.2. Efficiency with Aligned Demonstrations

- When demonstrations were perfectly aligned with the target task, PARROT generally achieved the fastest convergence. This is attributed to its behavior cloning pre-training, which allows it to solve the task nearly optimally by sampling in the latent space.
- APC, despite having access to the same near-optimal prior, required slightly longer to converge due to the additional need for its arbiter to learn accurate Q-function estimates to identify the beneficial prior.
- Compared to the IL baseline, APC converged substantially faster on PointMaze, and was comparable or slightly slower on Franka Kitchen. All demonstration-guided methods (APC, PARROT, IL, QFilter) significantly outperformed from-scratch SAC, confirming their ability to leverage aligned demonstrations to accelerate learning without substantial sample inefficiency costs for APC's added adaptability.

#### 4.3. Exceeding Suboptimal Demonstrations

- In the CarRacing environment, using approximately 30k transitions from a suboptimal human driver, APC demonstrated its ability to exceed the performance ceiling imposed by such data.
- SAC, trained from scratch, reached optimal performance (around 900 return) in about 250k steps. PARROT, however, only marginally improved upon the human driver's mean score, remaining constrained by the suboptimal prior. IL eventually surpassed human performance but was significantly slowed. QFilter exceeded human performance faster than IL, reaching optimal returns around 100k steps.
- APC achieved optimal returns in approximately 30k steps, outperforming SAC and all demonstration-guided baselines, indicating that it could effectively use suboptimal priors to warm-start learning and guide exploration without suffering from their limitations.

#### 4.4. Importance of Arbitrator and Reward Sharing (Ablation Studies)

- An ablation study in CarRacing investigated the contributions of the learning-free arbitrator selector and the reward-sharing trick.
- **Full APC (arbitrator + reward sharing)** consistently achieved optimal returns.
- Replacing the arbitrator with a **learned hierarchical selector** (with or without reward sharing) led to strong primacy bias. The learned selector overcommitted to the initially better-performing prior-based actor, hindering the prior-free actor's ability to improve and ultimately leading to subpar returns.
- Using the **arbitrator without reward sharing** also showed slower learning and an amplified dominance of initially stronger actors, as only the selected actor benefited from collected transitions.
- These results confirm that both the learning-free arbitrator (for mitigating primacy bias and making fair comparisons between actors' values) and the reward-sharing mechanism (for ensuring data-efficient and equitable learning progress across all actors) are critical components for APC's robust exploration and accelerated learning.

#### 4.5. Additional Findings (Appendices)

- **Effect of number of priors:** In PointMaze, increasing the number of misaligned priors could initially weaken the beneficial exploration bias from an aligned prior. However, APC generally performed better or on par with SAC, demonstrating its ability to adapt and eventually disregard unhelpful priors once their Q-values reflected low utility.
- **Arbitrator temperature sensitivity:** The arbitrator was largely insensitive to its temperature coefficient $\eta$. However, very high temperatures could degrade robustness in misaligned prior scenarios, as value differences between actors became less influential, leading to less effective filtering of misaligned behaviors.

In summary, the experiments establish APC as a robust, efficient, and adaptable method for integrating demonstrations into online RL, capable of handling a spectrum of demonstration qualities from perfectly aligned to severely misaligned and suboptimal.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Adaptive Policy Composition (APC) method represents a significant contribution to the field of reinforcement learning by addressing a fundamental limitation of demonstration-guided learning: the reliance on optimal and perfectly aligned demonstration data. This work enhances the practicality and applicability of RL in real-world scenarios where obtaining such ideal demonstrations is often infeasible.

The key significance and potential impact of APC are:

1. **Increased Robustness and Adaptability:** APC's ability to remain robust under severe demonstration misalignment and even leverage suboptimal demonstrations without performance degradation marks a step toward more resilient RL systems. This means that imperfect human demonstrations, or data collected from related but not identical tasks, can be utilized effectively, reducing the barriers to deploying RL in complex domains like robotics.
2. **Efficient Exploration with Imperfect Priors:** By adaptively composing multiple data-driven priors and a prior-free actor, APC offers a novel strategy for exploration. It can warm-start learning with available data, even if suboptimal, and dynamically switch to unconstrained exploration when prior knowledge is unhelpful, thereby accelerating learning compared to purely from-scratch methods.
3. **Mitigation of Primacy Bias in Hierarchical RL:** The introduction of the learning-free arbitrator and the reward-sharing trick provides a mechanism to overcome common challenges in hierarchical reinforcement learning, specifically the primacy bias and issues related to sample efficiency across sub-policies. These contributions could inform the design of future hierarchical and compositional RL architectures.
4. **Bridging the Gap between Data-Driven and Reward-Driven Learning:** APC effectively integrates behavior priors derived from demonstrations with online reward-driven adaptation. This allows the system to benefit from observational data for initialization and guidance while retaining the flexibility to discover optimal policies that may diverge from the observed demonstrations.
5. **Broad Applicability:** The method's demonstrated performance across diverse continuous-control benchmarks (maze navigation, robot manipulation, car racing) suggests broad applicability. This could be particularly impactful in fields like autonomous systems, robotics, and complex control problems where human expertise or previous task data can provide valuable, albeit imperfect, starting points.
6. **Guidance for Future Research:** The identified limitations, such as computational overhead, also highlight important avenues for future work, including optimizing the computational efficiency of multi-actor systems and exploring more complex policy blending strategies.

In essence, APC provides a more general and flexible framework for integrating human knowledge or prior data into RL, making the deployment of RL agents more practical and effective in environments characterized by imperfect information and dynamic task requirements.
