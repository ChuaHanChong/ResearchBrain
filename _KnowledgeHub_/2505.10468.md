# AI Agents vs. Agentic AI: A Conceptual Taxonomy, Applications and Challenges

> [!summary] Summary
> This work from Cornell University and the University of the Peloponnese establishes a conceptual taxonomy to differentiate between AI Agents and Agentic AI within the post-ChatGPT era. It clarifies their distinct architectures, capabilities, and application domains, while also mapping out associated opportunities and challenges for the developing field.

## Problem

- The rapid evolution of AI, particularly after the rise of large-scale generative models, led to ambiguous and interchangeable use of terms like "AI Agents" and "Agentic AI."
- This conceptual ambiguity caused challenges in system design, inconsistent evaluation, and inefficient deployment of autonomous AI systems.
- A lack of structured understanding hindered researchers and practitioners in aligning AI capabilities with specific task complexities.

## Method

- The researchers developed a structured, multi-stage methodology involving a comprehensive literature review across academic databases and AI-powered tools.
- They created a ==formal conceptual taxonomy== that systematically categorizes ==Generative AI==, ==AI Agents==, and ==Agentic AI== based on dimensions like autonomy, coordination, interaction, and reasoning scope.
- The approach included ==comparative analysis of architectural evolution==, mapping of application domains, and a detailed examination of inherent limitations and potential solutions.

## Results

- A formal taxonomy was established, clearly differentiating Generative AI, AI Agents, and Agentic AI, and detailing their architectural and operational characteristics.
- Application domains for both AI Agents (e.g., customer support, email filtering) and Agentic AI (e.g., multi-agent research assistants, intelligent robotics coordination) were mapped, illustrating their distinct practical utilities.
- The paper outlined a roadmap for future development, including technical solutions like Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) and causal modeling, and governance-aware architectures to address identified challenges in explainability, safety, and ethics.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Generative AI serves as a reactive, input-driven precursor, while AI Agents are autonomous, tool-augmented entities designed for specific goal-directed tasks within bounded environments.
> - Agentic AI represents a paradigm shift towards collaborative, multi-agent ecosystems capable of complex, high-level objectives through goal decomposition, inter-agent communication, and persistent memory.
> - Key challenges for AI Agents include inherited LLM limitations (e.g., hallucinations, prompt brittleness) and limited long-horizon planning, while Agentic AI faces amplified issues like communication bottlenecks, emergent behavior, scalability, and ethical governance concerns.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper provides a timely and crucial contribution to the rapidly evolving field of artificial intelligence, particularly in the post-ChatGPT era. Prior to 2022, the development of intelligent agents was rooted in foundational paradigms such as multi-agent systems (MAS) and expert systems. These early "classical agent-like systems" were typically rule-based, task-specific, and offered limited autonomy and adaptability. Examples like MYCIN, DENDRAL, and ELIZA demonstrated early forms of AI capability but lacked self-learning, generative reasoning, and adaptability to unstructured environments. Multi-agent systems from this era focused on distributed problem-solving and collaboration among entities with predefined rules and limited communication.

The introduction of large-scale generative models, epitomized by ChatGPT in November 2022, marked a "pivotal inflection point." This event catalyzed a surge of interest in autonomous, task-oriented AI systems, leading to the emergence of what the authors categorize as "Generative Agents," "AI Agents," and "Agentic AI." While Generative AI (e.g., ChatGPT, Midjourney) excels at producing novel content from prompts, it remains fundamentally reactive and lacks autonomous goal pursuit or self-initiated reasoning.

This paper identifies a critical gap in the existing literature: the conflation of "AI Agents" and "Agentic AI" under a broad, often ambiguous, umbrella of intelligent agents. This ambiguity leads to misaligned system design, inappropriate benchmarking, and inefficient development. The authors highlight the significant rise in global search trends for both terms since late 2022, underscoring the urgency for conceptual clarity.

By proposing the "first structured taxonomy" that formally distinguishes these paradigms across dimensions of autonomy, coordination, interaction, and reasoning scope, the paper aims to provide a foundational understanding for researchers and practitioners. It positions AI Agents as modular, single-entity systems leveraging Large Language Models (LLMs) and Large Image Models (LIMs) for tool-assisted task automation, while Agentic AI is presented as a paradigm shift towards orchestrated, multi-agent ecosystems capable of complex, collaborative, and autonomous goal achievement. This work is essential for bringing conceptual rigor to a fast-moving field, enabling more precise alignment of AI capabilities with specific task complexities, and guiding the development of the next generation of intelligent systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the rapid and transformative advancements in artificial intelligence following the widespread adoption of large-scale generative models, particularly post-2022. This accelerated progress has led to a proliferation of terms like "AI Agents" and "Agentic AI," often used interchangeably or without clear conceptual boundaries. The authors observe that this lack of precise definition and structured understanding creates significant challenges in the research and development landscape, including ambiguous system design, inconsistent evaluation metrics, and inefficient deployment strategies.

Against this backdrop, the paper sets forth several key objectives:

1. **Distinguish and Clarify:** To critically differentiate between "AI Agents" and "Agentic AI," highlighting their divergent design philosophies, capabilities, and levels of autonomy.
2. **Develop a Conceptual Taxonomy:** To provide a structured, formal taxonomy that systematically categorizes these paradigms, offering a shared vocabulary for the AI community.
3. **Map Applications:** To map the typical application domains for both AI Agents and Agentic AI, illustrating their real-world utility and operational differences across various sectors.
4. **Analyze Opportunities and Challenges:** To comprehensively analyze the specific opportunities and unique challenges inherent to each paradigm, including technical limitations and ethical considerations.
5. **Propose Solutions and a Roadmap:** To outline potential solutions for overcoming current limitations and propose a forward-looking roadmap for developing robust, scalable, and explainable AI-driven systems.
6. **Inform Next-Generation Design:** Ultimately, the study aims to establish a foundational understanding that will guide the design, deployment, and evaluation of future intelligent agent systems across academic and industrial domains, ensuring more precise alignment of system capabilities with task complexity.

By addressing these objectives, the authors seek to bridge the critical gap in existing literature, moving beyond broad discussions of intelligent agents to provide a nuanced framework that enables more effective and responsible development of autonomous AI.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors adopted a "structured, multi-stage methodology" to achieve their objectives, focusing on capturing the evolution, architecture, application, and limitations of AI Agents and Agentic AI. This approach is described as sequential and layered, moving from foundational concepts to advanced systems.

**1. Search Strategy:**
The literature review employed a hybrid search methodology, combining traditional academic databases with modern AI-powered tools.
- **Academic Databases:** Google Scholar, IEEE Xplore, ACM Digital Library, Scopus, Web of Science, ScienceDirect, and arXiv were extensively queried.
- **AI-Powered Interfaces:** Notably, the authors utilized ChatGPT, Perplexity.ai, DeepSeek, Hugging Face Search, and Grok, demonstrating a pragmatic embrace of the very technologies under review for their literature discovery.
- **Search Queries:** Boolean combinations of terms such as "AI Agents," "Agentic AI," "LLM Agents," "Tool-augmented LLMs," and "Multi-Agent AI Systems" were used. Targeted queries like "Agentic AI + Coordination + Planning" and "AI Agents + Tool Usage + Reasoning" were also employed to ensure comprehensive coverage.
- **Inclusion Criteria:** Literature was selected based on its significance in terms of novelty, empirical evaluation, architectural contribution, and citation impact, ensuring a focus on high-quality and influential works.

**2. Analytical Framework and Stages:**
The review followed a structured progression, organized into distinct conceptual layers:
- **Foundational Understanding of AI Agents:** This initial stage established core definitions, design principles, and architectural modules (perception, reasoning, action selection) of traditional AI Agents.
- **Role of Foundational Models:** The critical role of LLMs and LIMs as the "core reasoning and perceptual engines" for modern AI Agents was examined, including their capabilities and inherent limitations.
- **Emergence of Agentic AI:** The paper then discussed the conceptual leap from single, tool-augmented agents to collaborative, distributed ecosystems of interacting agents, driven by the need for advanced goal decomposition and dynamic adaptation.
- **Architectural Evolution:** A comparative analysis detailed the architectural enhancements differentiating AI Agents from Agentic AI, including persistent memory, meta-agent coordination, multi-agent planning loops (e.g., ReAct, Chain-of-Thought), and semantic communication protocols.
- **Application Domains:** Real-world deployments were categorized and analyzed across four representative areas for both AI Agents (e.g., customer support, email filtering) and Agentic AI (e.g., multi-agent research assistants, intelligent robotics).
- **Challenges and Limitations:** The study systematically identified and examined the inherent limitations for both paradigms, such as hallucination, prompt brittleness, emergent behavior, scalability issues, and ethical risks.
- **Potential Solutions and Future Roadmap:** Finally, the paper outlined emerging solutions (e.g., Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG), causal modeling, multi-agent memory) and a forward-looking roadmap for future development.

**3. Conceptual Tools:**
The authors utilized several conceptual tools to structure their analysis:
- **Taxonomy and Comparative Analysis:** Extensive use of tables (Tables 1-9) provided multi-dimensional comparisons across Generative AI, AI Agents, Agentic AI, and an "inferred Generative Agent" category, examining aspects like initiation, adaptation, coordination, architecture, and operational mechanisms.
- **Illustrative Analogies:** The analogy of smart home systems (Fig. 7) was used effectively to delineate the functional differences between single AI Agents and coordinated Agentic AI systems.
- **Visualizations:** Figures (e.g., Fig. 1 for Google Trends, Fig. 2 for mind map, Fig. 3 for methodology pipeline, Fig. 8 for architectural evolution, Fig. 9-11 for applications, Fig. 12-14 for challenges/solutions/roadmap) were employed to visually summarize complex information and enhance understanding.

**4. Transparency in AI Assistance:**
The authors explicitly stated their use of AI writing assistants (ChatGPT, Perplexity) for grammatical accuracy and sentence structure refinement, and ChatGPT-4o for generating visualizations. This level of transparency is commendable and reflects an honest engagement with the evolving landscape of AI-assisted academic writing.

Overall, the methodology is robust and systematic, combining a comprehensive literature review with a structured analytical framework and transparent use of AI tools to build a coherent and nuanced taxonomy of AI Agents and Agentic AI.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper delivers a comprehensive conceptual taxonomy, delineating critical distinctions, applications, and challenges across Generative AI, AI Agents, and Agentic AI.

**1. Generative AI as a Precursor:**
The study positions Generative AI (e.g., LLMs, LIMs) as the foundational precursor. These models are primarily reactive, input-driven, stateless, and optimized for content generation (text, image, code) based on prompts. They lack inherent autonomy, persistent memory, self-initiated reasoning, and direct interaction with external tools without explicit wrappers or scaffolding.

**2. Foundational Understanding of AI Agents:**
AI Agents are defined as autonomous software entities designed for goal-directed task execution within bounded digital environments. Key characteristics include:
- **Autonomy:** Function with minimal human intervention post-deployment, but within specific, defined tasks.
- **Task-Specificity:** Purpose-built for narrow, well-defined operations (e.g., email filtering, scheduling).
- **Reactivity and Adaptation:** Respond to dynamic inputs and integrate basic learning capabilities through feedback loops or context buffers.
- **Role of Foundational Models:** LLMs and LIMs serve as core reasoning and perception engines, enabling natural language understanding, planning, and limited decision-making.
- **Tool-Augmented Functionality:** A crucial advancement where agents utilize external tools, APIs, and computation platforms (function calling) to access real-time information, execute code, and perform actions beyond their internal knowledge base (e.g., ChatGPT invoking web search).

**3. The Emergence of Agentic AI:**
Agentic AI represents a significant conceptual leap, moving from isolated AI Agents to collaborative, multi-agent ecosystems. These systems are characterized by:
- **Multi-Agent Collaboration:** Composed of multiple specialized agents working synergistically towards complex, high-level objectives.
- **Goal Decomposition:** User objectives are automatically parsed and divided into smaller, manageable subtasks distributed across the agent network.
- **Multi-step Reasoning and Planning:** Dynamic sequencing of subtasks, allowing real-time adaptation to environmental changes or partial failures.
- **Inter-Agent Communication:** Mediated through distributed channels (e.g., message queues, shared memory) to facilitate coordination without continuous central oversight.
- **Persistent Memory Architectures:** Incorporate episodic, semantic, and vector-based memory to preserve and share knowledge across task cycles and agents, enabling sustained context and iterative refinement.
- **Orchestration Layers / Meta-Agents:** Supervisory agents (orchestrators) coordinate subordinate agents, manage dependencies, assign roles, and resolve conflicts, ensuring coherent system-level behavior (e.g., MetaGPT, ChatDev).
- **Role Specialization:** Agents are assigned distinct functions, enhancing interpretability and scalability.

**4. Application Domains:**
The paper illustrates the operational divergence through practical applications:
- **AI Agents Applications:** Customer Support Automation, Internal Enterprise Search, Email Filtering and Prioritization, Personalized Content Recommendation, and Autonomous Scheduling Assistants. These typically involve structured queries, response generation, and automated workflows for discrete tasks.
- **Agentic AI Applications:** Multi-Agent Research Assistants (automated grant writing, literature reviews), Intelligent Robotics Coordination (multi-robot harvesting, drone inspection), Collaborative Medical Decision Support (ICU management, diagnostics), and Multi-Agent Game AI/Adaptive Workflow Automation (cybersecurity incident response). These demand sustained context, distributed labor, multi-modal coordination, and strategic adaptation.

**5. Challenges and Limitations:**
- **AI Agents:**
 - **Lack of Causal Understanding:** Inability to distinguish correlation from causation, limiting counterfactual reasoning and robustness under distributional shifts.
 - **Inherited LLM Limitations:** Hallucinations, prompt sensitivity/brittleness, shallow reasoning, high computational cost/latency, static knowledge cutoffs, and bias propagation.
 - **Incomplete Agentic Properties:** Partial autonomy, limited proactivity, and underdeveloped social ability compared to canonical definitions.
 - **Limited Long-Horizon Planning and Recovery:** Struggles with extended temporal consistency, contingency planning, and systematic error recovery.
- **Agentic AI:** These challenges are often amplified due to increased complexity.
 - **Amplified Causality Challenges:** Inter-agent distributional shifts and error cascades due to interdependent actions without robust causal modeling.
 - **Communication and Coordination Bottlenecks:** Lack of unified semantic understanding, ambiguous natural language protocols, and resource contention.
 - **Emergent Behavior and Predictability:** Unintended outcomes, system instability (infinite loops, deadlocks), and opacity in decision logic.
 - **Scalability and Debugging Complexity:** Black-box reasoning, non-compositionality, and difficulty in tracing faults across multiple asynchronous agents.
 - **Trust, Explainability, and Verification:** Challenges in interpreting multi-agent behavior, assigning accountability, and formal verification.
 - **Security and Adversarial Risks:** Expanded attack surface, single points of compromise, and susceptibility to inter-agent manipulation.
 - **Ethical and Governance Challenges:** Accountability gaps, bias amplification, value misalignment, and lack of standardized ethical frameworks.
 - **Immature Foundations and Research Gaps:** Absence of standard architectures, robust causal foundations, and comprehensive benchmarks.

**6. Potential Solutions and Future Roadmap:**
The paper proposes a multi-faceted approach to address these challenges:
- **Technical Solutions:** Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG), advanced Tool-Augmented Reasoning, Agentic Loop mechanisms (ReAct), sophisticated Memory Architectures (episodic, semantic, vector), Multi-Agent Orchestration with Role Specialization, Reflexive and Self-Critique Mechanisms, Programmatic Prompt Engineering, Causal Modeling and Simulation-Based Planning, and robust Monitoring, Auditing, and Explainability Pipelines.
- **Governance Solutions:** Governance-Aware Architectures incorporating accountability, role isolation, compliance protocols, and ethical alignment checks.
- **Future Trajectories:** AI Agents will evolve towards proactive intelligence, robust tool integration, causal reasoning, continuous learning, and trust-centric operations. Agentic AI will focus on multi-agent scaling, unified orchestration, persistent memory, simulation planning, ethical governance, and domain-specific systems. The "Absolute Zero: Reinforced Self-play Reasoning with Zero Data (AZR)" framework is highlighted as a transformative direction for self-evolving, data-independent learning.

These findings collectively underscore that while AI Agents excel in constrained, tool-integrated scenarios, Agentic AI represents a more advanced paradigm focused on distributed cognition, complex problem-solving, and adaptive decision-making within collaborative ecosystems.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research paper carries significant weight and offers a profound potential impact across both academic and industrial landscapes in artificial intelligence.

**1. Conceptual Clarity and Standardization:** The most immediate and crucial impact is the provision of a clear, structured taxonomy differentiating AI Agents from Agentic AI. In a field characterized by rapid innovation and often ambiguous terminology, this paper offers a much-needed shared vocabulary and conceptual framework. This clarity will reduce confusion, facilitate more precise communication among researchers and developers, and serve as a foundational reference point for future studies and discussions.

**2. Improved System Design and Development:** By formalizing the distinctions between these paradigms, the paper directly guides practitioners in choosing the appropriate architectural approach for their applications. It helps prevent "under-engineering" complex scenarios that require multi-agent coordination or "over-engineering" simple tasks solvable by a single AI Agent. This precise alignment of design principles with problem complexity will lead to more efficient, effective, and robust AI system development, minimizing resource waste and maximizing functional utility.

**3. Enhanced Evaluation and Benchmarking:** A clear taxonomy enables the development of more appropriate performance metrics, safety protocols, and resource allocation strategies tailored to each paradigm. Evaluating a single AI Agent differs significantly from assessing a complex Agentic AI ecosystem, and this paper lays the groundwork for creating specialized benchmarks that truly reflect the capabilities and limitations of each system type.

**4. Roadmap for Future Research and Innovation:** The detailed analysis of challenges (e.g., lack of causal understanding, emergent behavior, ethical concerns) coupled with proposed solutions (e.g., RAG, causal modeling, orchestration) provides a comprehensive roadmap for future research. It highlights critical areas needing attention, such as developing robust memory architectures, improving inter-agent communication protocols, and building governance-aware designs. The introduction of the "Absolute Zero: Reinforced Self-play Reasoning with Zero Data (AZR)" framework points towards a paradigm shift in autonomous learning, inspiring new directions for self-evolving and self-improving AI systems.

**5. Accelerating Real-World Adoption in High-Stakes Domains:** The paper’s exploration of diverse applications, from agricultural robotics to medical decision support and cybersecurity incident response, showcases the immense potential of Agentic AI. By identifying and addressing the limitations, particularly regarding reliability, explainability, and safety, the research contributes to building more trustworthy AI systems. This is vital for enabling the safe and responsible deployment of autonomous AI in mission-critical environments where errors can have severe consequences.

**6. Ethical and Governance Foundations:** The explicit discussion of ethical and governance challenges, such as accountability gaps, bias propagation, and value misalignment in multi-agent systems, is highly significant. By proposing governance-aware architectures and emphasizing the need for role isolation, traceable decision-logging, and participatory oversight, the paper contributes to the crucial discourse on responsible AI development. This foresight is essential for proactively addressing societal concerns as AI systems become increasingly autonomous and integrated into human lives.

In essence, Sapkota, Roumeliotis, and Karkee's work serves as a foundational reference, not merely describing the current state of AI agents but actively shaping its future trajectory by providing clarity, identifying critical challenges, and charting a path towards more intelligent, scalable, and ethically robust autonomous AI systems.
