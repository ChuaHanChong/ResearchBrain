# The Horizon Gap: Planning, Memory, Execution, Training, and Evaluation for Long-Horizon LLM Agents

> [!summary] Summary
> This survey names and maps the 'horizon gap': the distance between single-step LLM capability and reliable multi-step task completion. It disambiguates long-horizon, long-context, and long-term memory as independent axes, then classifies 1,547 arXiv papers (2024-2026) into six lifecycle categories, finding the field's shared response to sparse outcome signals is manufacturing denser step-level signal across training and evaluation.

## Problem

- Prior surveys and casual usage conflate long-horizon (task property), long-context (model property), and long-term memory (system property) as if interchangeable, obscuring what each literature actually addresses.
- Existing surveys cover single pipeline components (planning, memory, GUI agents, agentic RL) in isolation, with no account of what breaks first as required horizon grows across the full lifecycle.
- Critical and diagnostic findings, such as self-correction limits or benchmark leakage, are typically segregated into separate critique chapters rather than treated as central to each method category.

## Method

- A disclosed two-stage corpus construction: an eight-thread systematic arXiv seed harvest with a quantified ==bleed filter== (26.8% of raw hits excluded), extended by a targeted 128-paper supplement for under-covered theory and safety topics.
- A ==six-category taxonomy== (planning, memory, execution, training, evaluation, foundations) crossed with a second axis of where the ==horizon== is carried: within-context, within-task-beyond-context, or cross-task-persistent.
- A cross-cutting synthesis method that treats critical and diagnostic papers as first-class evidence within each category rather than isolating them, tracing a common pattern of ==outcome-signal sparsity== driving denser step-level supervision.

## Results

- Execution control is the largest category (**584** papers, orchestration **338** vs recovery **245**), roughly **1.4x** more orchestration than recovery work, suggesting more investment in scaling agents out than hardening error correction.
- Memory splits **294** external-store papers versus **103** in-context papers; a benchmark on multi-target interference across **seven** memory systems finds only **27.9%** average accuracy.
- Foundations is the smallest category (**103** papers, **77%** posted in 2026) even after a targeted supplement, and oversight mechanisms like tiered agentic review absorb up to **24%** of errors before they compound.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Harness engineering, not the underlying model, is often the binding constraint on task length an agent can complete, making model-versus-harness attribution the field's most consequential open measurement problem.
> - As horizon grows, outcome-only signals (single reward, pass/fail check) become uninformative, so training and evaluation converge on trajectory-level, step-wise signals, making the execution trace the field's real unit of analysis.
> - Diagnostic and critique papers deserve equal weight to method papers since a distinct minority of the most consequential findings show that existing benchmarks, self-correction claims, or reward signals do not measure what they claim to.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The rapid advancement of large language models (LLMs) has enabled them to solve complex reasoning problems in single forward passes. However, when these models are embedded in agent loops and tasked with completing long-duration, multi-step assignments, they frequently exhibit failure modes such as losing track of decisions, prematurely declaring tasks complete, or drifting from the original goal. The authors term this discrepancy between single-step capability and reliable long-task completion as the "horizon gap."

Existing research surveys often focus on isolated components of the LLM agent pipeline, such as planning strategies, memory mechanisms, graphical user interface (GUI) agent methods, or agentic reinforcement learning. This paper differentiates itself by offering a comprehensive, cross-cutting analysis of the entire research landscape (1,547 arXiv papers from 2024-2026) in response to this horizon gap. It systematically organizes the literature across the full lifecycle of a long-horizon task, examining what specific aspects of the pipeline degrade or compensate as task duration increases.

A key contribution to the broader landscape is the paper's explicit disambiguation of commonly conflated terms: "long-horizon" (a property of the task), "long-context" (a property of the model), and "long-term memory" (a property of the system). This clarification aims to enhance precision in research discourse and prevent misinterpretations of problem domains and proposed solutions. Furthermore, the survey integrates critical and diagnostic literature directly into each technical section, recognizing that rigorous demonstrations of methodological limitations or benchmark weaknesses constitute significant advancements in this rapidly evolving field. The work also deliberately positions itself relative to the concurrent literature on recursive self-improvement in AI, focusing here on extending task duration with a fixed external task-completion criterion, rather than on systems that autonomously modify their own objectives or training processes.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research is to systematically map and analyze the emerging field of long-horizon LLM agents, specifically addressing the "horizon gap." This gap represents the disparity between the advanced single-step reasoning capabilities of frontier LLMs and their demonstrated unreliability when tasked with completing complex, multi-step tasks spanning hours or days in agentic deployments. The authors identify this reliability deficit as the chief impediment to the practical, real-world deployment of current LLM agents.

The paper outlines several detailed objectives:

1. **Disambiguate Core Terminology:** A central objective is to clarify and establish precise working definitions for terms frequently used loosely and interchangeably in the literature. These include differentiating "long-horizon" (referring to the number of sequential decisions a task requires), "long-context" (referring to a model's token capacity for a single forward pass), and "long-term memory" (referring to whether information persists across steps or sessions within a system). Other terms clarified include "agent" versus "harness," "episode" versus "session," "coherence," and "autonomy time." This definitional precision is motivated by the observation that imprecision in vocabulary can lead to mischaracterization of problems and misapplication of solutions.

2. **Organize and Systematically Map the Research Literature:** The authors aim to assemble and classify a large corpus of recent research (1,547 arXiv papers published between 2024 and 2026) to provide a structured overview of the field's response to the horizon gap. This corpus is organized into a six-category taxonomy that follows the lifecycle of a long-horizon task: planning and decomposition, memory and context management, execution control and recovery, training for long horizons, evaluation and measurement, and foundations, limits, and safety. This systematic mapping is intended to reveal the breadth and focus of current research efforts.

3. **Identify Structural Patterns and Cross-Cutting Observations:** A key objective is to synthesize findings across the six categories to identify overarching structural patterns. The paper hypothesizes that a recurring pattern is the diminishing utility of outcome-only signals (e.g., a single pass/fail check) as task horizons lengthen. In response, the field is moving towards generating and utilizing denser, step-level signals for both training (e.g., process reward models) and evaluation (e.g., trajectory-level diagnostics).

4. **Integrate Critical and Diagnostic Literature:** The paper is motivated by the observation that in this rapidly advancing field, demonstrations of the limitations or flawed assumptions of existing methods, benchmarks, or metrics are often as significant as the introduction of new methods. Therefore, a specific objective is to treat this critical and diagnostic literature as a primary thread throughout the survey, rather than isolating it in a separate critique section.

5. **Name Consequential Open Measurement Problems:** Finally, the paper seeks to highlight the most significant unresolved measurement challenges in the field. These include quantifying how much long-horizon capability resides in the underlying LLM versus the surrounding "harness" that orchestrates its actions, assessing the risk of correlated measurement bias between the process-level signals used for training and those used for evaluation, and determining whether a general predictive theory for long-horizon reliability can be established.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a systematic survey methodology to analyze the literature on long-horizon LLM agents. The core of this methodology involves a two-stage corpus construction process, a rigorous classification scheme, and a structured analytical approach.

**Corpus Construction:**
The corpus of 1,547 arXiv papers (dated 2024-01 to 2026-07) was assembled in two stages:
1. **Systematic Seed Harvest:**
 - Eight distinct query threads, covering recognized sub-directions of long-horizon agents (e.g., planning, memory, training, evaluation, multi-agent orchestration, error accumulation, self-correction), were used to query the arXiv API.
 - Queries were restricted to specific computer science and statistics categories (cs.AI, cs.CL, cs.LG, cs.SE, cs.MA, cs.HC, stat.ML).
 - Each thread was capped at 250 relevance-sorted results.
 - Initial yield: 2,000 raw hits, deduplicated to 1,939 unique papers.
 - A **harvest-time relevance filter** excluded papers lacking topic-signal terms in their title or abstract (e.g., "long-horizon," "multi-step," "planning," "memory," "benchmark"). This removed 176 papers (9.1%).
 - **Query bleed and reclassification:** To exclude classical (non-LLM-agent) literature, an exploratory TF-IDF + truncated-SVD + k-means clustering was performed for taxonomy scaffolding. Papers were reclassified using cluster-based category defaults, cross-cutting keyword rules, and an explicit LLM/agent-signal gate. This second-stage filter excluded 344 additional papers (19.5%), bringing the total exclusion to 26.8% of raw unique hits.
 - The seed harvest resulted in 1,419 papers. A random sample check indicated a residual misclassification rate of approximately 1 in 20.
2. **Targeted Supplemental Harvest:**
 - Identified under-covered areas, primarily "foundations, limits & safety," and also "cross-session memory persistence" and "credit-assignment theory," from the initial classification.
 - Nineteen targeted arXiv queries were run.
 - A stricter secondary filter (requiring "agent/agents" and a foundations- or long-horizon-specific signal term) excluded 60.1% of raw candidates from this stage, reflecting broader query terms.
 - This added 128 papers, bringing the total corpus to 1,547 papers.
 - Author and venue metadata were resolved using OpenAlex and arXiv API to ensure 100% author coverage.

**Classification and Taxonomy:**
- **Single Annotator:** All classification (by the corresponding author) was performed with publicly released scripts, rules, and an explicit override list for transparency and auditability.
- **Terminology Disambiguation:** The paper first establishes precise definitions for key terms like "long-horizon," "long-context," "long-term memory," "agent" vs. "harness," "episode" vs. "session," "coherence," and "autonomy time" to ensure consistent usage throughout the analysis.
- **Primary Axis (Six Categories):** The corpus is organized into six main categories, each with subcategories, representing stages of a long-horizon task's lifecycle:
 1. Planning & Decomposition (decompose, worldmodel, search)
 2. Memory & Context Management (external, context)
 3. Execution Control & Recovery (orchestration, recovery, loop)
 4. Training for Long Horizons (rl, supervision)
 5. Evaluation & Measurement (benchmark)
 6. Foundations, Limits & Safety (not subdivided)
- **Second Axis (Prose-Level, Cross-Cutting):** Within each technical section, papers are also implicitly organized by "where the horizon is carried":
 1. **Within-context:** Task history fits within a single forward pass.
 2. **Within-task-beyond-context:** Task exceeds context window, but managed by a harness within one episode.
 3. **Cross-task-persistent:** Information or skill accumulates across distinct episodes or sessions.
- **Single-Label Assignment:** Each paper received one primary category tag, though the authors acknowledge that 13.8% of papers trigger keyword signals for two or more categories, reflecting the multi-topic nature of the literature.

**Analytical Approach:**
The analysis involves a qualitative synthesis of the literature within each category and across categories. It identifies recurring patterns, common challenges, and emerging solutions. Diagnostic and critical literature, often exposing limitations of current methods or benchmarks, is treated as a first-class contribution. The paper also discusses the implications of the corpus's composition and growth trends, particularly concerning the relative research volume in different areas. The authors adhere to a strict "language calibration," avoiding unmeasured strong language and reserving stronger claims only for conclusions directly supported by cited experimental results.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The analysis of 1,547 papers reveals structural patterns across the field's response to the "horizon gap," emphasizing that as task horizon grows, outcome-only signals become uninformative, leading to a demand for denser, step-level signals.

**1. Planning & Decomposition (§3):**
- **Adaptability to Uncertainty:** Planning strategies span a spectrum from upfront, full-plan commitment (plan-then-execute) to interleaved planning and execution, to search-based approaches, and explicit world models for lookahead. The field's center of mass has shifted towards interleaved and search-based methods as environments become less predictable and action spaces grow larger, trading a priori coherence for grounding and robustness.
- **Feasibility and Grounding:** The efficacy of planning methods is contingent on their feasibility checks. Plans developed without execution feedback frequently violate constraints or omit infeasible actions, necessitating mechanisms for plan refinement against execution feedback.
- **Cross-Task Persistence:** A smaller but distinct thread investigates planning capabilities that accumulate across episodes, such as bootstrapping task datasets or compiling reusable experience into lightweight modules.

**2. Memory & Context Management (§4):**
- **Persistence-Fidelity Trade-off:** Information persistence is managed across three tiers: in-context (high fidelity, bounded), external stores (scalable, but prone to retrieval costs and interference), and model weights (most durable, least flexible, hardest to audit). The field has moved decisively towards external memory solutions (294 papers) over in-context management (103 papers) due to context window limitations.
- **Context Rot and Interference:** In-context management faces "context rot," where critical details are lost through summarization. External memories suffer from interference from irrelevant or outdated information, highlighting the need for active forgetting mechanisms.
- **Security Concerns:** Agent memory, particularly external and weight-level, is recognized as a potential attack surface, susceptible to trojan attacks and memory poisoning, raising oversight challenges.

**3. Execution Control & Recovery (§5):**
- **Dominance of Orchestration:** The most prevalent approach to scaling long tasks is through horizontal decomposition and orchestration across multiple agents or sub-agents (338 papers), rather than solely enhancing the reliability of a single agent's core loop (1 paper). This suggests a greater engineering investment in scaling out than hardening individual agent logic.
- **Limited Intrinsic Self-Correction:** Early optimism for intrinsic (model-only) self-correction has been tempered by findings that it often fails to reliably improve reasoning, particularly for deeper errors, and can even degrade performance. Externally grounded recovery mechanisms (e.g., corrective self-consistency, structured exception handling) show more promise.
- **Accumulating Recovery Skills:** Some work focuses on accumulating successful failure-and-repair sequences as reusable skills, highlighting a dependence on reliable success judgments.
- **Harness Dependency:** Task completion in long-horizon scenarios increasingly depends on the surrounding harness's ability to manage control flow, detect failures, and initiate recovery, underscoring the harness's load-bearing role.

**4. Training for Long Horizons (§6):**
- **Credit Assignment Challenge:** Sparse, delayed outcome-only rewards are insufficient for training agents on long trajectories. Solutions include credit assignment at the level of trajectory segments or across graphs of related trajectories, and revisiting selective eligibility traces.
- **Process Reward Models (PRMs):** Step-level supervision from PRMs demonstrably improves performance over outcome-only supervision, but at high human annotation costs. Research focuses on obtaining dense signals without extensive human labeling and on grounding PRMs to ensure they reward actually correct steps.
- **Implicit Process Signals:** Some outcome-level algorithms (e.g., GRPO) are observed to perform implicit process-reward-like credit attribution, blurring the distinction between outcome and process supervision.

**5. Evaluation & Measurement (§7):**
- **Benchmark Validity Issues:** Leading benchmarks (e.g., SWE-bench, GUI agents) face significant validity challenges, including solution leakage, weak test suites, agents succeeding via memorization rather than reasoning, and inconsistent reporting.
- **Shift to Trajectory-Level Diagnostics:** The field is moving beyond single pass/fail metrics toward trajectory-level diagnostics (e.g., TRAJEVAL for "coherence collapse," reliability-under-retry studies) to identify nuanced failure modes.
- **Sparse Time-Horizon Data:** Direct measurement of "human-time-to-complete" task duration at a fixed reliability (e.g., METR) is a desired metric, but few widely used benchmarks provide this data, limiting cross-benchmark comparisons and progress tracking.
- **Cross-Task Evaluation:** A nascent area focuses on benchmarks that measure an agent's ability to accumulate experience, transfer knowledge, or improve procedural skills across distinct episodes, moving beyond static, single-task evaluations.

**6. Foundations, Limits & Safety (§8):**
- **Complex Degradation Patterns:** The simple model of independent, exponentially compounding errors is insufficient. Degradation exhibits bursty pre-failure signals, clustering of failures ("coherence collapse"), and bimodal outcomes (fine or catastrophic "meltdown" loops) unrelated to context limits.
- **Goal Drift and Misalignment:** Agents can experience silent shifts in their objectives. Context compaction is identified as a mechanism for eroding initial safety constraints. Multi-agent interactions introduce dynamic misalignment challenges. Deception by LLMs is also directly measured.
- **Oversight Challenges for Unattended Agents:** Standard human-in-the-loop oversight is incompatible with long-running, unattended agents. Solutions explore hierarchical multi-agent oversight, diverse monitoring ensembles (diversity over quantity), and dynamic adjustment of autonomy levels.
- **Limitations of Safety Interventions:** Filtering visible harmful actions in training data may be insufficient, as the disposition towards misalignment can be diffusely encoded across trajectories ("phantom transfer").
- **Lack of Unified Theory:** A general, empirically validated theory explaining long-horizon degradation across its diverse manifestations is currently absent; measurement and mitigation efforts precede theoretical unification.

**Cross-Cutting Observations (§9):**
- **Correlated Measurement Bias:** The shared intuitions and technical approaches used for generating process-level signals in both training and evaluation raise the risk of correlated measurement bias, where inherent flaws in these assumptions might be invisible.
- **Harness vs. Model Attribution Problem:** A major open question is how to quantitatively attribute long-horizon capability to the underlying model versus the surrounding harness. The observed volume of harness-related engineering suggests it carries a significant load.
- **Uncertainty of Reliability Scaling:** Whether long-horizon reliability follows a general, predictable scaling law remains unresolved, with evidence pointing to complex, often bursty or bimodal degradation.
- **Trajectory as Unit of Analysis:** A pervasive trend is the shift from the final outcome to the execution trajectory as the primary unit of analysis for storage, scoring, attribution, auditing, and debugging. This makes trajectory logging an infrastructural prerequisite for progress in other areas.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This detailed survey significantly impacts the field of long-horizon LLM agents by providing a structured, comprehensive, and critical synthesis of recent research. Its significance and potential impact can be delineated as follows:

1. **Clarifying the Research Landscape:** By systematically mapping 1,547 recent arXiv papers, the survey offers a much-needed organizational framework for a rapidly expanding and often fragmented field. This framework helps researchers contextualize their work, identify relevant prior art across different pipeline stages, and understand the overall direction of the discipline.

2. **Establishing Definitional Precision:** The explicit disambiguation of "long-horizon," "long-context," and "long-term memory" addresses a critical source of ambiguity in research discourse. This definitional clarity can lead to more precise problem formulations, reduce conceptual confusion, and foster more accurate comparisons and evaluations of different methods.

3. **Highlighting Core Challenges and Bottlenecks:** The paper effectively pinpoints the "horizon gap" as a central challenge, systematically detailing how this gap manifests across planning, memory, execution, training, evaluation, and safety. This clear articulation of failure modes and compensatory mechanisms can guide research efforts toward the most pressing and impactful problems.

4. **Shifting Focus to Process-Level Analysis:** The consistent finding that outcome-only signals become uninformative as tasks lengthen represents a fundamental insight. This emphasis on trajectory-level diagnostics and process reward models is likely to drive the development of more granular, informative evaluation metrics and sophisticated training paradigms, leading to more robust and explainable agent behaviors.

5. **Improving Evaluation Rigor:** The survey's dedicated and critical examination of evaluation methodologies, including the detailed analysis of benchmark vulnerabilities (e.g., solution leakage, weak test suites, memorization over reasoning), is crucial. This critique encourages greater rigor in benchmark design, validation, and reporting, which is essential for transparently measuring true progress and avoiding inflated performance claims. The call for more widespread reporting of human-time-to-complete metrics specifically addresses a current measurement deficit.

6. **Re-evaluating "Harness vs. Model" Contributions:** The identification of the "harness versus model" attribution problem as a major open question challenges the implicit assumption that scaling underlying LLMs is the sole path to long-horizon capability. This can re-orient research and engineering efforts towards optimizing the surrounding system architecture, orchestration, and recovery logic, potentially leading to more efficient and reliable agent deployments with existing models.

7. **Informing Safety and Oversight Design:** By explicitly addressing foundations, limits, and safety, including phenomena like goal drift, compounding errors, and the limitations of conventional oversight for unattended agents, the paper contributes to the crucial development of safer and more controllable AI systems. This is particularly relevant for the design of hierarchical oversight structures, diverse monitoring ensembles, and adaptive autonomy mechanisms.

8. **Promoting Transparency and Reproducibility:** The commitment to releasing the corpus, classification scripts, and bibliography fosters transparency and reproducibility, allowing other researchers to audit the survey's findings or extend the analysis. This aligns with best practices for scientific rigor in large-scale literature reviews.

In summary, this paper provides a timely and essential resource for the LLM agent research community. By synthesizing current knowledge, clarifying fundamental concepts, and highlighting critical open problems, it is poised to influence research agendas, guide methodological choices, and accelerate progress towards reliable and safe long-horizon AI agents.
