# Chain-of-Context Learning: Dynamic Constraint Understanding for Multi-Task VRPs

> [!summary] Summary
> Chain-of-Context Learning (CCL) is presented as an end-to-end framework that dynamically understands and adapts to evolving constraints and node states in multi-task Vehicle Routing Problems. It achieves superior performance on diverse VRP variants, including strong generalization to unseen constraints, by dynamically reformulating context and efficiently re-embedding node features.

## Problem

- Existing neural solvers for multi-task VRPs struggle to adapt to diverse and evolving constraints, often requiring extensive re-training for new task variations.
- Prior reinforcement learning-based VRP solvers use static node embeddings and uniform attention to constraints, failing to account for the dynamic evolution of constraint importance and node priorities during sequential decision-making.
- Naive dynamic refinement of node features for multiple parallel solution trajectories in multi-task VRPs incurs prohibitive computational costs.

## Method

- Developed **Chain-of-Context Learning** (CCL), an end-to-end ==encoder-decoder== framework that integrates dynamic constraint understanding into step-wise context formulation.
- Introduced Relevance-Guided Context Reformulation (RGCR), a module that dynamically emphasizes salient constraints at each decoding step by formulating constraint-specific embeddings and computing their correlation to the current node.
- Implemented Trajectory-Shared Node Re-embedding (TSNR) for efficient, dynamic refinement of node features, using a multi-head attention mechanism with a distance-based bias to integrate contextual semantics from all nodes and multi-trajectory contexts.

## Results

- CCL achieved superior performance, with statistically significant reductions in performance gap, across all **16** in-distribution multi-task VRPs (N=**50** and N=**100**) compared to state-of-the-art neural solvers.
- Demonstrated strong generalization on **32** out-of-distribution (unseen constraint combinations) tasks, consistently outperforming other methods on most variants for both N=**50** and N=**100** problem sizes.
- Ablation studies confirmed that both RGCR (especially for time-sensitive constraints) and TSNR (with node-level attention, embedding updates, and distance bias) are crucial and complementary to CCL's performance, providing a favorable performance-cost trade-off and zero-shot generalization on large-scale real-world instances.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The relevance and criticality of individual constraints change dynamically as a vehicle's route progresses, requiring adaptive prioritization rather than uniform attention.
> - Node importance and features evolve based on previous routing decisions within a trajectory, necessitating progressive updates to node representations to maintain alignment with the current decoding context.
> - Efficient dynamic refinement of node features across multiple parallel trajectories can be achieved by sharing contextual information, preventing excessive computational overhead compared to individual re-embedding.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The Vehicle Routing Problem (VRP) is a combinatorial optimization problem central to logistics and supply chain management. Its efficient solution can lead to reductions in transportation costs and improvements in service quality. Historically, VRPs have been addressed using heuristic-based solvers, such as LKH and HGS. While these methods can be effective in specific contexts, they often demand significant computational resources and extensive manual rule engineering to adapt to different problem variations.

More recently, neural networks have emerged as an alternative approach for solving VRPs. These neural solvers learn generalizable policies offline, allowing for rapid inference at test time. This paradigm shift has enabled progress in handling various VRP instances. However, real-world VRPs frequently involve multiple, diverse constraints beyond basic vehicle capacity, giving rise to "multi-task VRPs." In this scenario, existing neural solvers designed for single tasks become less effective, typically requiring substantial re-training or fine-tuning for each new task variant.

To address multi-task VRPs, a segment of the research community has developed neural models leveraging a unified reinforcement learning (RL) framework. These models typically encode constraint information and node attributes into static embeddings, with the decoding process following a Markov Decision Process (MDP). During decoding, a global context (e.g., current time or remaining capacity) is combined with these static node embeddings to determine the next node. A notable limitation of this approach, identified by the authors, is the failure to account for the dynamic evolution of constraint requirements and node priorities throughout the sequential decision-making process. Static node embeddings, which remain fixed during decoding, cannot accurately reflect the changing importance of nodes or constraints as routes are constructed. This misalignment between the evolving context and static node representations can lead to suboptimal decisions.

This paper positions itself within this landscape by proposing a framework that specifically addresses the dynamic nature of constraints and node states in multi-task VRPs. It aims to overcome the limitations of prior RL-based multi-task VRP solvers by explicitly integrating constraint dynamics into step-wise context formulation and adaptively refining node representations, thereby providing a more accurate representation of the problem state during sequential decision-making.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a novel framework, Chain-of-Context Learning (CCL), that can effectively solve multi-task Vehicle Routing Problems by dynamically understanding and adapting to evolving constraints and node states throughout the routing process. The motivation stems from observed limitations in existing neural multi-task VRP solvers, which the authors articulate as three specific challenges:

1. **Dynamic Constraint Importance:** Existing methods often apply uniform attention across all constraints at each decoding step. However, the relevance or criticality of individual constraints can change as a vehicle's route progresses. For instance, an "open route" constraint becomes more significant as a sub-route approaches completion. A solver that fails to adaptively prioritize constraints based on the current context may allocate computational effort inefficiently or make suboptimal decisions. The motivation here is to enable the model to identify and focus on the most salient constraints at any given step.

2. **Computational Efficiency of Node Refinement:** In single-task VRPs, dynamic decoding mechanisms (e.g., removing visited nodes) have been used to reflect evolving routing decisions. Extending such dynamic refinement to multi-task settings poses a significant computational burden, especially when dealing with multiple solution trajectories in parallel. If each trajectory requires individual re-embedding of nodes based on its unique context, the computational cost can become prohibitive. The motivation is to design an efficient mechanism for refining node features that can leverage shared information across multiple parallel trajectories without incurring excessive computational overhead.

3. **Capturing Sequential Dependencies and State Alignment:** Current multi-task VRP solvers typically refine node representations at a given step using only their initial (step-0) embeddings and the current global context. This approach overlooks the fact that node importance and features can evolve based on previous routing decisions within the current trajectory. A static initial embedding, even with an updated global context, may not accurately capture the nuanced state of a node in a specific decoding step, thus limiting the model's ability to maintain a precise representation of the Markov property essential for coherent sequential decision-making. The motivation is to ensure that node representations are progressively updated to reflect their evolving status and dependencies on prior steps, maintaining alignment with the current decoding context.

By addressing these challenges, the authors aim to improve the accuracy of state estimation, thereby enabling more precise and effective policy learning for multi-task VRPs.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology is Chain-of-Context Learning (CCL), an end-to-end framework built upon an encoder-decoder paradigm. It integrates two key modules: Relevance-Guided Context Reformulation (RGCR) and Trajectory-Shared Node Re-embedding (TSNR), primarily within the decoding stage.

#### 3.1. Encoder

The encoder is responsible for initially embedding the VRP instance. It processes both static constraint flags and dynamic node attributes. The input includes a one-hot vector representing the presence of four core constraints (Backhaul, Open Routes, Duration Limits, Time Windows) and features for the depot and all customer nodes. Depot features comprise coordinates and flags for open routes and duration limits. Customer features include coordinates, linehaul/backhaul demands, earliest/latest service times, and service durations. These inputs are projected through linear layers and fed into a 6-layer Transformer encoder. The encoder utilizes a dual-attention mechanism, processing original node embeddings and unified constraint-node embeddings through sparse and global branches, respectively, as described in prior work (Li et al., 2025). The output consists of normalized node embeddings, `H`, which serve as the initial node representations for the decoding process.

#### 3.2. Relevance-Guided Context Reformulation (RGCR)

The RGCR module aims to generate step-wise contextual information by dynamically emphasizing salient constraints. For each trajectory `i` at decoding step `j`, RGCR operates in three phases:
1. **Constraint Embedding Formulation:** Constraint-specific attributes are extracted for the current node `τ_i,j`. These attributes include remaining vehicle capacity (for Backhaul), remaining route distance (for Duration Limits), total distance traveled (for Open Routes), and time window-related values (for Time Windows). Each set of attributes is then projected into an embedding space using separate linear layers, resulting in constraint embeddings `C_k_i,j` for each constraint type `k`.
2. **Correlation Computing:** The relevance of each constraint embedding `C_k_i,j` to the current node embedding `H_τ_i,j` is calculated using a dot product, yielding correlation scores `s_k_i,j`.
3. **Adaptive Constraint Aggregation:** A unified constraint embedding `S_i,j` is formed by combining an "original" part and an "enhanced" part. The original part is a linear projection of the concatenation of all individual constraint embeddings (`C_k_i,j`). The enhanced part is a weighted sum of the individual constraint embeddings, where the weights are the previously computed correlation scores `s_k_i,j`. The final context embedding `C̃_i,j` for a specific trajectory and step is then aggregated from this unified constraint embedding `S_i,j` and the current node embedding `H_τ_i,j` through concatenation and a linear layer.

#### 3.3. Trajectory-Shared Node Re-embedding (TSNR)

TSNR is designed to efficiently refine node features by integrating contextual semantics from all nodes and multi-trajectory contexts. This is achieved via a multi-head attention mechanism:
1. **Query, Key, and Value Generation:** The node embeddings from the previous step, `H_j-1`, are normalized and linearly projected to form the query `q_j`. The keys and values, `k_j` and `v_j`, are derived by concatenating `H_j-1` with the aggregated context embeddings `C̃_j` (from all trajectories) and then normalizing and linearly projecting them.
2. **Distance-Based Bias:** A distance-based bias `B_j` is incorporated into the attention calculation to prevent overfitting, particularly to Time Window constraints. This bias consists of two parts: node-to-node Euclidean distances and current node (per-trajectory) to all candidate node Euclidean distances.
3. **Attention Calculation and Node Update:** Attention weights `A_j` are computed using the queries, keys, and the distance bias. These attention weights are then applied to the values to produce re-embedded node representations `H̃_j`. A residual connection and a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) are applied to further refine `H̃_j`, resulting in the updated node embeddings `H_j`.
Crucially, these updated node embeddings `H_j` are preserved and used as queries for the next decoding step, establishing a "Chain-of-Context." The frequency of these node updates is controlled probabilistically during both training (`P_tr`) and testing (`P_ts`).

#### 3.4. Step-wise Decision and Training Objective

With the context embedding `C̃_j` and the refined node embeddings `H_j` at hand, a classic decoder (a multi-head attention mechanism) is used to calculate the probability distribution for selecting the next node. A mask `M_j` is applied to prevent revisiting previously selected nodes. The node with the highest probability that satisfies all constraints is chosen; otherwise, the depot is selected.
The model generates multiple solution trajectories in parallel. The training objective is based on the REINFORCE algorithm, where the reward `R_i` for each trajectory `i` is the negative total route length. A shared baseline `b` (average reward over all trajectories) is used to reduce gradient variance. The entire framework is optimized jointly end-to-end.

**Training and Inference:** The model is trained on 100,000 instances uniformly distributed across 16 VRP variants derived from combinations of B, L, O, and TW constraints. The best model is selected based on validation performance on CVRP. Testing involves 48 VRP variants, including 16 in-distribution tasks and 32 out-of-distribution tasks (featuring unseen constraints like Mixed Backhaul (MB) and Multi-Depot (MD), which are held out during training for zero-shot evaluation).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of CCL was conducted on 48 diverse VRP variants, including 16 in-distribution (seen during training) and 32 out-of-distribution (unseen constraints) tasks, with problem sizes N=50 and N=100. Performance was measured by total routing length (Obj.), performance gap (Gap) to a strong heuristic baseline (HGS-PyVRP), and inference time.

#### 4.1. In-Distribution Evaluation

On the 16 in-distribution tasks, CCL demonstrated favorable performance against state-of-the-art multi-task VRP solvers, including MTPOMO, MVMoE, RF-TE, and CaDA.
- **Superior Performance:** CCL achieved the best performance (lowest objective values and performance gaps) on all 16 in-distribution tasks for both N=50 and N=100 problem sizes.
- **Impact of ReLD:** When combined with ReLD (a context-aware module), denoted as CCL†, the performance was further enhanced, achieving the best overall results across all in-distribution tasks. This suggests a complementary relationship where ReLD excels in extracting global constraint signals, while CCL's dynamic node refinement offers finer-grained adaptation to local, node-specific constraints, particularly on Time Window (TW) tasks.
- **Statistical Significance:** Statistical t-tests indicated that the performance gains of CCL† over CaDA† were statistically significant, with p-values below 0.001 across all 16 tasks, and average gap reductions ranging from 7% to over 50%.

#### 4.2. Out-of-Distribution Evaluation (Generalization)

For the 32 unseen out-of-distribution tasks, CCL also exhibited strong generalization capabilities.
- **Consistent Outperformance:** CCL† consistently outperformed other methods for the N=50 setting, achieving the lowest average gap.
- **N=100 Performance:** For N=100, CCL† maintained competitive performance but showed a slightly higher gap on TW tasks compared to standalone CCL. The authors hypothesized this might be due to a lower test-time update rate in CCL†, potentially leading to overfitting to static constraint structures and under-adaptation to time-sensitive variations. Nevertheless, CCL (with or without ReLD) exhibited superior overall performance compared to CaDA and its variants. CCL and CCL† collectively outperformed baselines on 27 out of 32 tasks for N=50 and 24 out of 32 tasks for N=100.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies

Ablation experiments were conducted to assess the contribution of RGCR and TSNR within CCL†.
- **RGCR and TSNR Effectiveness:** Removing either RGCR or TSNR individually led to an increase in the performance gap. Removing both modules resulted in the highest gap, indicating their complementary effectiveness.
- **RGCR Components:** Within RGCR, using the dot product for correlation computing achieved the smallest gap on tasks with TW constraints, outperforming direct concatenation of attributes/embeddings and other correlation scores (random, cosine similarity). This suggests that RGCR's dynamic constraint prioritization is particularly beneficial for complex, time-sensitive constraints.
- **TSNR Components:** Within TSNR, the combination of node-level attention, embedding updates, and the distance bias was found to be essential for optimal performance, as removing any of these elements degraded results. Cross-attention was observed to be more computationally efficient than self-attention in this context while maintaining comparable performance.
- **Independence from ReLD:** Additional ablation studies confirmed that RGCR and TSNR remained effective even without the ReLD module, indicating that the core performance gains primarily originate from CCL's intrinsic design.

#### 4.4. Complexity Analysis

Compared to baselines like CaDA and CaDA†, CCL and CCL† introduced a moderate increase in memory usage and parameter count. The inference time was generally longer due to the additional computations involved in dynamic context reformulation and node re-embedding. However, the authors also compared CCL to a "heavy decoder" variant of CaDA (CaDA†-HD) with comparable inference time. In this comparison, CCL achieved a lower performance gap (0.25% reduction), suggesting that its effectiveness stems from its architectural design rather than simply increased computational capacity. A naive step-wise refinement strategy (ASW-TAM) was shown to incur significantly higher gaps, longer inference times, and larger memory consumption, further validating the efficiency of CCL's approach.

#### 4.5. Performance-Cost Trade-off and Real-World Practicality

Sensitivity analyses on the probabilistic update rates `P_tr` (training) and `P_ts` (testing) demonstrated a trade-off between solution quality and inference efficiency. Optimal performance was generally observed when `P_ts` was slightly higher than `P_tr`. A lightweight version of CCL† (using `P_tr`=0.25 and `P_ts`=0.25) achieved a competitive average gap with significantly reduced inference time, enabling flexibility for practical deployment.
The method also demonstrated zero-shot generalization on large-scale real-world VRPTW instances (N=600), achieving the lowest average gap while maintaining comparable inference times when applying a low update probability (`P_ts`=0.1). On other real-world CVRP and VRPTW benchmarks, CCL consistently performed well, outperforming existing SOTA methods on a majority of instances, indicating its potential for practical applications.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces a method that directly addresses a identified limitation in current neural multi-task VRP solvers: the static representation of node states and the overlooked dynamics of constraint relevance during sequential decision-making. By proposing Chain-of-Context Learning (CCL), the authors present a framework that dynamically adapts to the evolving context, leading to a more accurate understanding of constraint requirements and node priorities at each step of route construction.

The significance of this work lies in several areas:
- **Improved Solution Quality:** The experimental results demonstrate that CCL consistently achieves superior performance on a diverse set of multi-task VRP variants, including both in-distribution and challenging out-of-distribution scenarios. This improvement in solution quality can translate directly into more optimized routes, reduced transportation costs, and enhanced service efficiency in practical logistics and supply chain operations.
- **Enhanced Generalization:** The ability of CCL to generalize effectively to unseen constraint combinations and larger problem instances without re-training is a notable advancement. This "zero-shot" generalization capability is crucial for real-world deployment, where VRPs often present novel or dynamically changing constraints.
- **Finer-Grained Constraint Understanding:** The Relevance-Guided Context Reformulation (RGCR) module allows the model to adaptively focus on the most critical constraints at any given moment, moving beyond uniform attention. This mechanism provides a more nuanced approach to constraint satisfaction, which is particularly beneficial for complex constraints like time windows.
- **Efficient Dynamic Node Representations:** The Trajectory-Shared Node Re-embedding (TSNR) module offers an efficient way to update node embeddings based on the evolving context across multiple trajectories. This avoids the computational burden of naive re-embedding while enabling the model to capture step-wise dependencies, aligning the state representation with the Markov property.
- **Practical Applicability:** While acknowledging a trade-off with inference time, the work demonstrates the potential for configuring CCL for different performance-cost requirements. Its performance on large-scale real-world instances indicates its suitability for practical deployment in complex logistical environments.

The potential impact of this research is in advancing the capabilities of neural combinatorial optimization for real-world applications. By providing a more robust and adaptive framework for multi-task VRPs, CCL contributes to the development of intelligent systems that can make more informed and efficient routing decisions, even in the presence of dynamic and diverse operational constraints. Future work on further improving inference efficiency while maintaining high solution quality could broaden its applicability across various industries.
