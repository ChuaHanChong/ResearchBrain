# Predicting the Benefit of Retrieval Augmentation in Open-Domain Question Answering

> [!summary] Summary
> This paper studies predicting, per question, whether retrieval augmentation will improve answer quality over closed-book generation. It benchmarks pre-retrieval, post-retrieval, and post-generation gain predictors, introduces Bert-Gen supervised cross-encoders and Internal Prober, a dual-LoRA probe of frozen-LLM hidden states needing no decoding. Predicted gain drives selective RAG that beats always-retrieving across three QA datasets, two LLMs, two retrievers.

## Problem

- Retrieval augmentation is not universally beneficial: distracting or irrelevant passages can mislead the LLM, yet systems still apply retrieval uniformly to every question.
- Prior adaptive-RAG work learns retrieval policies from pre-retrieval question complexity, so it ignores actual retrieval quality and the model's own parametric knowledge.
- Existing gain predictors are either weak score-distribution heuristics or post-generation methods that require decoding full answers, which is too costly for latency-sensitive pipelines.

## Method

- Formalizes RAG gain as a log-ratio of shallow semantic answer-quality metrics (E5 cosine, cross-encoder, NLI entailment) with and without retrieved context, predicted without reference answers.
- Bert-Ret and Bert-Gen supervised regressors fine-tune ModernBERT-large end-to-end with MSE over question, top-5 passages, and one or both generated answers.
- Internal Prober attaches two LoRA adapters to one frozen LLM, probing final hidden states of question-only and question-plus-passages streams, with regression heads jointly predicting three gains.

## Results

- Pre-retrieval predictors correlate near zero (0-0.1) with true gain; unsupervised post-retrieval ones reach only 0.1-0.2, while supervised Bert-Ret reaches 0.45-0.49 on NQ-Open.
- Bert-Gen-2A is best overall, peaking at Pearson 0.87-0.89 on TriviaQA; Internal Prober trails it but beats Bert-Ret and uncertainty, cutting generated-token compute 82-95%.
- Selective RAG beats always-RAG: Bert-Gen-2A recovers up to 84% of the always-RAG-to-oracle gap, and Internal Prober wins under an unseen GPT-4o judge metric.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Classical query-performance-prediction signals transfer poorly to generation: retrieval-list score statistics say little about whether context actually helps the model answer.
> - A model's pre-generation hidden states already encode whether external knowledge is needed, so probing can substitute for most of the cost of speculative decoding.
> - Training a predictor jointly on several quality metrics improves robustness under evaluation metrics it never saw, though the probe needs white-box model access.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) has become an established paradigm for enhancing Large Language Models (LLMs) in knowledge-intensive tasks, particularly in open-domain question answering. RAG typically involves integrating external knowledge retrieved from a corpus into the LLM's prompt to ground responses and mitigate issues such as hallucinations. However, the efficacy of RAG is not universal; in certain instances, retrieved information may be unhelpful, redundant, or even detrimental, potentially leading to degraded answer quality. This observation motivates research into predicting the utility of retrieval augmentation for individual questions.

Previous work has explored the concept of "RAG Gain" primarily in the context of text completion, as introduced by Huly et al., who evaluated retrieval and generation-based signals for gain prediction. For question answering, prior research has largely focused on estimating retrieval utility, examining factors such as context length, the positioning of relevant passages, and conflicts between retrieved evidence and the LLM's internal knowledge. Dai et al. proposed assessing RAG effect through LLM uncertainty during generation, an approach that is revisited and extended in the present work. Lee et al. investigated probing LLM internal states for difficulty perception, providing a foundation for the novel "Internal Prober" introduced in this paper.

Furthermore, adaptive and selective RAG approaches, such as Self-RAG and Active-RAG, aim to dynamically determine when retrieval should be invoked. These methods, along with frameworks like Adaptive-RAG and approaches for conversational QA, primarily focus on policy learning and generation control rather than explicitly quantifying the predicted RAG gain. These systems often operate before retrieval, limiting their ability to incorporate retrieval quality or the LLM’s internal knowledge.

The most directly related work by Tian et al. also addressed predicting RAG gain for question answering, studying several pre- and post-retrieval predictors and a single post-generation predictor based on uncertainty. The current study differentiates itself by introducing novel post-generation predictors and an internal state probing method that aim to significantly narrow the performance gap between less computationally intensive pre-generation methods and more effective post-generation approaches. Unlike Tian et al., who used supervision to integrate existing predictors, this work focuses on training novel methods specifically designed to predict RAG gain directly.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to investigate whether the benefit of applying Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) for a given question, compared to generating an answer without retrieval, can be accurately predicted. A reliable prediction of RAG gain holds practical implications for optimizing LLM-based question answering systems.

The motivation for this study stems from several observations:
1. **Inconsistent RAG Benefits:** While RAG generally enhances LLM performance by grounding responses in external knowledge and reducing hallucinations, it does not consistently yield improvements. In many cases, retrieved content can be unhelpful, distracting, or even detrimental, leading to a degradation in answer quality.
2. **Computational Efficiency:** Indiscriminately applying RAG to all questions introduces unnecessary latency and computational overhead. Retrieving, processing, and integrating external passages, followed by potentially longer answer generation, consumes significant resources regardless of whether it actually improves the answer.
3. **Enhanced System Effectiveness:** A robust RAG gain predictor would enable selective, instance-level application of RAG. This would allow a system to dynamically decide whether to augment an LLM with retrieved information for a specific query, thereby optimizing both answer quality and resource utilization.
4. **Bridging Performance Gaps:** The study is motivated to explore and develop prediction methods that can effectively estimate RAG gain. This includes evaluating existing pre-retrieval and post-retrieval predictors, proposing novel post-generation predictors, and critically, devising an efficient predictor ("Internal Prober") that can achieve performance comparable to computationally expensive post-generation methods while incurring significantly lower computational cost. This aims to provide a practical solution for real-world, latency-sensitive applications.
5. **Establishing a Selective RAG Framework:** Ultimately, the research aims to utilize these predictors within a selective RAG framework, where the decision to employ retrieval augmentation is made dynamically on a per-question basis, based on the predicted gain. This framework is intended to demonstrate that selective application of RAG can lead to higher average answer quality compared to a uniform "always RAG" strategy.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research defines the problem as predicting the RAG gain, which is formalized as the relative (log) gain of using retrieved context `C` for answering question `q`. The gain is calculated as the logarithm of the ratio of answer quality with RAG to answer quality without RAG, plus a small epsilon for numerical stability. The task is to predict this gain without knowledge of the reference answer.

#### 3.1. Quality Metrics

To evaluate the "actual gain," the study addresses limitations of traditional factoid QA metrics like Exact Match (EM) and token-level F1, which may not fully capture semantic quality, redundancy, or misinformation. While LLM-as-a-judge approaches correlate highly with human judgments, they are computationally costly and sensitive to the choice of the judging LLM. Therefore, the study employs three shallow, BERT-based semantic alignment metrics to approximate LLM-as-a-judge quality:
- **Q_E5:** Cosine similarity between E5-large-v2 embeddings of the generated and reference answers.
- **Q_CE:** Semantic association score computed by a RoBERTa-large cross-encoder, normalized to.
- **Q_NLI:** Entailment probability, framing answer quality as a natural language inference problem, using a DeBERTa-v3-large-NLI model.
These metrics are shown to have strong pairwise Pearson correlations, indicating agreement, yet capture complementary aspects of quality.

#### 3.2. RAG Gain Analysis

An analysis of RAG gain distribution across three QA datasets (NQ-Open, HotpotQA, TriviaQA) shows that while average RAG gain is positive, a significant portion of questions yield little or no improvement, or even negative gain (e.g., >30% negative gain in HotpotQA). This empirical evidence further motivates the need for selective RAG.

#### 3.3. Gain Prediction Methods

The study evaluates and proposes various predictors categorized by the information they leverage:

- **Pre-Retrieval Predictors (Unsupervised):** Analyze only the question and corpus-level statistics, operating before retrieval.
 - **SCQ (Similarity to Collection Query):** Sum of TF-IDF weights of query terms.
 - **IDF (Inverse Document Frequency):** Average IDF values of query terms.
 - **VAR (Variance):** Average of variances of query term TF-IDF weights across documents.

- **Post-Retrieval Predictors:** Analyze the retrieved passages in addition to the question and corpus statistics.
 - **Unsupervised:**
 - **WIG (Weighted Information Gain):** Difference between average retrieval score of top-k passages and corpus retrieval score. A variant, U_WIG, is also used.
 - **NQC (Normalized Query Clarity):** Standard deviation of retrieval scores of top-k passages normalized by corpus score. A variant, QC, is also used.
 - **SMV (Score Magnitude and Variance):** Combines mean and variance of top-k retrieval scores, normalized by corpus score. A variant, U_SMV, is also used.
 - **REF (Reference List):** Rank Biased Overlap (RBO) between the original retrieved list and a re-ranked list (using BAAI/bge-reranker-v2-m3) as a high-quality reference.
 - **Supervised:**
 - **Bert-Ret:** A BERT-based model (ModernBERT-large) fine-tuned with a linear regression layer to predict RAG gain. Input includes the question and top-5 retrieved passages. Trained end-to-end with Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss.

- **Post-Generation Predictors:** Leverage the LLM's output (with and without RAG) in addition to pre-generation information.
 - **Unsupervised:**
 - **Uncertainty:** Measures the reduction in answer generation uncertainty, calculated as the difference between the maximum token-level entropy (from LLM's probability distribution) for no-RAG and RAG generated answers.
 - **Entailment:** Based on the assumption that higher faithfulness of the generated answer to the RAG context indicates better quality. Uses an NLI model (ModernBERT-large) to compute the difference in entailment scores between RAG-generated and no-RAG answers, given the question and retrieved passages as premise.
 - **Supervised:**
 - **Bert-Gen:** Similar architecture to Bert-Ret, but also incorporates generated answers. Three variants:
 - **Bert-Gen-1A_no-rag:** Input: question, retrieved passages, no-RAG answer.
 - **Bert-Gen-1A_rag:** Input: question, retrieved passages, RAG answer.
 - **Bert-Gen-2A:** Input: question, retrieved passages, both no-RAG and RAG answers.

- **Semi-Generation (Internal Prober – Novel):**
 - This novel predictor directly estimates RAG gain from the LLM's internal representations without requiring full auto-regressive generation.
 - **Architecture:** Utilizes a single frozen LLM backbone with two independent Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) adapters. One branch processes the question augmented with retrieved passages (`q; p1...p5`), and the other processes the question alone (`q`).
 - **Mechanism:** Each branch performs a single forward pass, extracting the final hidden state representation. These representations are fed into linear regression heads to produce predicted "quality" scores for each of the three semantic metrics (Q_E5, Q_CE, Q_NLI). The predicted gains are then computed as the log-ratio of these quality scores (similar to Equation 1).
 - **Training:** Trained using MSE loss against ground-truth gains, jointly predicting gains for all three quality metrics as a form of multi-task regularization. LoRA adapters enable task-specific reshaping of internal hidden states.
 - **Efficiency:** Significantly reduces computational cost by replacing two full auto-regressive generation processes (required by Bert-Gen-2A) with two parallel forward passes.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

- **LLMs:** Falcon-3-10B-Instruct and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct.
- **Retrieval:** Okapi BM25 (sparse) and E5-large-v2 (dense) over a Wikipedia dump (Dec. 20, 2018). For RAG, the top-5 retrieved passages (out of 100) augment the question.
- **Datasets:** NQ-Open, HotpotQA, and TriviaQA. Each dataset used 30,000 questions (24,000 for training supervised predictors, 6,000 for validation, and 3,600 sampled for testing).
- **Evaluation:** Pearson correlation (ρ) between predicted and actual gain. Williams’ two-tailed t-test with Bonferroni correction for statistical significance.

#### 3.5. Selective RAG Framework

A selective RAG framework is implemented using a threshold-based policy: if the predicted gain (`ˆG`) is positive, the RAG-augmented answer is chosen; otherwise, the no-RAG answer is selected. This framework is evaluated by measuring the average quality of selected answers and the accuracy of RAG decisions, comparing against "No RAG," "Always RAG," and "Oracle" baselines.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study presents a comprehensive evaluation of various RAG gain prediction methods, highlighting their performance in terms of Pearson correlation with actual gain and their effectiveness in a selective RAG setting.

#### 4.1. RAG Gain Prediction Quality (Table 1)

- **Pre-Retrieval Predictors:** These methods (IDF, SCQ, VAR) consistently demonstrated low to negligible correlations (approximately 0-0.1) with actual RAG gain across all experimental configurations (LLMs, retrievers, datasets, quality metrics). This suggests that query- and corpus-level statistics alone are insufficient for predicting RAG benefits.

- **Post-Retrieval Unsupervised Predictors:** Methods like NQC, WIG, and SMV, which analyze the distribution of retrieval scores, showed limited improvement, achieving low correlations in the range of approximately 0.1-0.2. The REF predictor, based on re-ranking and RBO, generally outperformed other unsupervised post-retrieval methods, reaching correlations of 0.25-0.30 on NQ-Open with BM25 retrieval, particularly when the re-ranker was effective.

- **Post-Retrieval Supervised Predictors:** The Bert-Ret predictor, which is a BERT-based model trained specifically for RAG gain prediction, significantly outperformed all unsupervised pre- and post-retrieval methods. It achieved correlations of up to 0.45-0.49 on NQ-Open and showed statistically significant improvements in most configurations (34 out of 36), indicating the advantage of a supervised approach in capturing complex linguistic and semantic relationships.

- **Post-Generation Unsupervised Predictors:**
 - **Uncertainty:** The reduction in answer generation uncertainty between no-RAG and RAG answers proved to be a strong signal, achieving correlations of 0.35-0.55 across most configurations. This suggests that the LLM's internal uncertainty is a valuable indicator of RAG utility.
 - **Entailment:** This predictor exhibited mixed results, performing well in some configurations (e.g., TriviaQA with BM25) but poorly in others. Its performance was generally less consistent than Uncertainty, possibly due to its reliance solely on faithfulness to context without leveraging the LLM's internal knowledge in the same implicit way as Uncertainty.

- **Post-Generation Supervised Predictors:**
 - **Bert-Gen-1A (no-rag/rag):** These variants, which incorporate a single generated answer, generally outperformed Bert-Ret, with statistically significant differences in most cases. This highlights the value of including generated answer information. No single variant consistently outperformed the other.
 - **Bert-Gen-2A:** This predictor, which leverages both the no-RAG and RAG generated answers, consistently achieved the highest prediction performance. It was statistically significantly better than all other predictors across all configurations, with correlations peaking at 0.87-0.89 on TriviaQA and 0.71-0.79 on NQ-Open. The study identifies Bert-Gen-2A as a state-of-the-art method for predicting RAG gain.

- **Semi-Generation (Internal Prober):** The novel Internal Prober demonstrated performance that statistically significantly transcended that of Bert-Ret and often surpassed three post-generation predictors (Uncertainty, Entailment, and Bert-Gen-1A). While it was statistically significantly outperformed by Bert-Gen-2A, Internal Prober achieved this performance with a substantial reduction in computational cost, reducing generated-token computation by 82-95% (averaged across datasets and LLMs). This positions Internal Prober as a practical alternative, especially for latency-sensitive systems.
 - **Effect of Partial Generation (Figure 4):** Further analysis revealed that allowing Internal Prober to generate even a small number of tokens (e.g., K=8) prior to probing substantially improved its prediction quality, gradually approaching that of Bert-Gen-2A. The most significant gains were observed within the first few generated tokens.

#### 4.2. Cross-Architectural and Cross-Metric Consistency

The observed correlation trends exhibited high consistency across different LLMs (Falcon and Llama), retrieval methods (BM25 and E5), and answer quality metrics (Q_E5, Q_CE, Q_NLI). The relative ranking of predictors remained largely stable. It was noted that Q_E5 often yielded higher correlation numbers, which the authors attribute to its smoother distribution of cosine values (empirically in the range [0.7, 1]), compared to the broader and more variable distributions of Q_CE and Q_NLI.

#### 4.3. Selective RAG Performance (Table 2)

The evaluated predictors were applied within a selective RAG framework, aiming to choose RAG only when an improvement in answer quality was predicted.
- **Baselines:** The "Always RAG" strategy statistically significantly outperformed "No RAG," confirming the general effectiveness of RAG. However, a notable performance gap remained between "Always RAG" and the "Oracle" (optimal selection) baseline, which prediction-based selectors aimed to reduce.
- **Predictor-Based Selection:** All prediction-based selectors (Bert-Ret, Internal Prober, Bert-Gen-2A) consistently outperformed the "Always RAG" strategy in terms of both average answer quality and accuracy across nearly all configurations.
- **Bert-Gen-2A:** This predictor consistently delivered the strongest improvements, achieving statistically significant gains over baselines and often recovering a substantial portion of the performance gap relative to the Oracle.
- **Internal Prober:** Performed competitively, sometimes matching or exceeding Bert-Gen-2A under the Q_NLI metric, aligning with its strong gain-prediction capabilities. Its performance was less consistent across retrievers, suggesting increased sensitivity to retrieval noise.
- **Retrieval Method Impact:** Selective RAG approaches generally yielded larger relative improvements when paired with BM25 retrieval compared to E5-based retrieval. This is attributed to the noisier nature of lexical retrieval, where selectively filtering out distracting context is more critical.
- **Unified Evaluation with Q_LLM (Table 3):** When all selectors were evaluated using an LLM-as-a-judge metric (Q_LLM) for Falcon/BM25 configurations, Internal Prober was observed to outperform Bert-Gen-2A. The authors suggest this might be due to Internal Prober's multi-task regularization, as it is trained to jointly predict multiple quality metrics, potentially making it more robust when evaluated with a metric it was not explicitly optimized for.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research makes several contributions to the field of Retrieval-Augmented Generation and its application in open-domain question answering:

1. **Systematic Investigation of RAG Gain Prediction:** The study provides a systematic and comprehensive evaluation of various RAG gain prediction strategies, encompassing pre-retrieval, post-retrieval, and post-generation methods. This clarifies the relative strengths and weaknesses of different approaches and identifies the most effective predictive signals.

2. **Introduction of Novel, High-Performing Predictors:**
 - **Bert-Gen-2A:** This supervised post-generation predictor achieves state-of-the-art performance in RAG gain prediction by explicitly modeling the semantic relationships among the question, retrieved passages, and both RAG-augmented and non-RAG generated answers. Its high correlation with actual gain establishes a new benchmark for prediction accuracy.
 - **Internal Prober:** This novel semi-generation predictor represents a significant advancement in balancing prediction accuracy with computational efficiency. By probing the LLM’s internal representations without full auto-regressive generation, it substantially reduces computational cost (82-95% fewer generated tokens) while delivering performance that often surpasses other predictors and significantly narrows the gap to computationally intensive post-generation methods. This makes it a practical solution for real-world, latency-sensitive QA systems.

3. **Enabling Selective RAG Frameworks:** The experimental results demonstrate that selective RAG, guided by the leading gain predictors (particularly Bert-Gen-2A and Internal Prober), consistently outperforms the strategy of uniformly applying RAG to all questions. This indicates that dynamically deciding when to invoke retrieval can lead to improved overall answer quality and potentially more efficient resource utilization. This has direct implications for the design of more intelligent and adaptive LLM-based systems.

4. **Advancement in Understanding RAG Dynamics:** The study's analysis of RAG gain distribution highlights that RAG is not universally beneficial, and that a significant portion of queries may not benefit or even suffer from retrieval augmentation. This reinforces the need for predictive mechanisms and contributes to a deeper understanding of when and why RAG is effective.

The potential impact of this work includes:
- **Optimized RAG Deployment:** Developers can integrate these gain predictors into RAG systems to make intelligent, query-specific decisions about retrieval augmentation, leading to higher quality and more reliable answers.
- **Resource Efficiency:** For applications with high query throughput or strict latency requirements, predictors like Internal Prober offer a pathway to maintain high performance while significantly reducing the computational load associated with unnecessary retrieval and generation.
- **Improved User Experience:** By avoiding the delivery of low-quality or distracting RAG-augmented answers, these methods can contribute to a more consistent and positive user experience in conversational AI and search applications.

**Limitations:** The authors acknowledge three main limitations: Internal Prober requires white-box access to the LLM, restricting its use with proprietary black-box models; experiments were limited to mid-sized (8–10B parameter) LLMs due to hardware constraints; and Bert-Gen-2A, while highly accurate, is computationally expensive and primarily serves as a strong reference point rather than a practical deployment solution.
