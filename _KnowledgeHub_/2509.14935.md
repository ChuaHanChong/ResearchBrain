# CAD-Driven Co-Design for Flight-Ready Jet-Powered Humanoids

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the Istituto Italiano di Tecnologia developed a CAD-driven co-design framework for jet-powered aerial humanoids, optimizing both robot morphology and control parameters to enhance flight performance. The framework systematically identifies structurally feasible robot configurations that minimize trajectory tracking error and energy consumption for dynamically constrained aerial tasks.

## Problem

- Designing aerial humanoids presents unique challenges due to the tight coupling between morphological characteristics (mass distribution, thrust placement) and dynamic capabilities/control performance, making traditional sequential design suboptimal.
- The vast, high-dimensional design space of aerial humanoids, where each configuration requires structural validation and dynamic evaluation, poses a significant computational hurdle for comprehensive exploration.
- Existing co-design frameworks often generate theoretically optimal robot designs that are not practically manufacturable or structurally sound, requiring a method to inherently ensure physical realizability.

## Method

- A CAD-driven co-design framework was developed, integrating parametric geometry generation, ==Uniform Latin Hypercube== sampling for 5,000 designs, and Finite Element Method (FEM) analysis to ensure structural integrity and manufacturability.
- K-means clustering was applied to reduce the extensive design space to a manageable set of representative centroid configurations, making the multi-objective optimization computationally tractable.
- A momentum-based Model Predictive Control (MPC) scheme was used for trajectory tracking, and the NSGA-II algorithm performed multi-objective optimization to jointly minimize trajectory tracking error and energy expenditure across selected robot morphologies and MPC control weights.

## Results

- The 100-cluster configuration consistently achieved a lower Pareto front compared to 50 and 250-cluster cases, indicating superior trade-offs between trajectory tracking error and energy consumption.
- Specific centroid models, such as Model 1188 (high turbine angle, large offset) achieved low trajectory tracking error at high energy cost, while Model 2586 (low turbine angle, large hip distance) minimized energy consumption at the expense of tracking accuracy.
- Adaptable models like 44, 4082, and 4256 were found in the "knee" region of the Pareto front, demonstrating their versatility in achieving balanced solutions across various control parameter tunings.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - An intermediate clustering granularity, such as 100 clusters, optimally balances design diversity and computational tractability, leading to more effective convergence and improved Pareto fronts in the co-design process.
> - Certain robot morphologies exhibit greater adaptability, allowing for a broader range of performance trade-offs (e.g., between tracking accuracy and energy efficiency) through the tuning of MPC control parameters.
> - The integrated framework successfully identifies structurally sound and dynamically optimized "flight-ready" aerial humanoid configurations that can execute complex aerial trajectories with validated control strategies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The design and control of robotic systems, particularly complex ones like aerial humanoids, often involve tightly coupled problems where morphological characteristics directly influence dynamic capabilities and control performance. This paper positions itself within the growing field of co-design, which aims to jointly optimize robot morphology and control strategies to enhance task execution.

Previous research in co-design has explored various robotic systems, including manipulators, grippers, and legged robots. Examples include optimizing modular manipulator morphology for task-specific manipulability, co-evolving robotic hand structures and grasping behaviors using reinforcement learning, and integrating parallel compliance in quadrupeds for improved motion robustness. These efforts generally demonstrate that co-optimization can lead to improved performance compared to optimizing design and control independently.

However, the authors identify several limitations in existing co-design frameworks when applied to aerial humanoid robots. Firstly, many current approaches focus on systems with limited dynamic complexity or low-dimensional design spaces, whereas aerial humanoids present challenges due to mass distribution, thrust interface placement, underactuation, and complex flight dynamics. Upper-body co-design frameworks for actuator configurations or mobile robot design integrating perception and planning are not directly applicable to jet-powered humanoids. Secondly, the computational cost of exploring high-dimensional design spaces, where each configuration must satisfy structural and dynamic feasibility, remains a significant hurdle. While techniques like parallel training, differentiable simulation, or trajectory optimization address this, they may not consistently guarantee manufacturable outcomes. Prior work on integrating CAD-informed geometry in jet-powered humanoids has primarily focused on component-level optimization and filtering non-viable candidates through FEM analysis, rather than incorporating trajectory-level performance metrics across extensive morphological variations.

This work addresses these gaps by proposing a framework that extends the design space exploration to full-body CAD-derived configurations and evaluates them using flight-based performance metrics. Unlike approaches that optimize isolated links, this research varies whole-body geometry across a large number of models and assesses each using a predictive controller in dynamic flight scenarios. The objective is to identify not only structurally feasible robots but also those capable of executing energy-efficient, dynamically constrained aerial trajectories, integrating manufacturability and dynamic performance at a comprehensive system level.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a CAD-driven co-design framework for optimizing jet-powered aerial humanoid robots to execute dynamically constrained trajectories. The framework aims to systematically explore the interplay between robot morphology and control parameters to identify configurations that offer favorable trade-offs in flight performance.

The motivation for this work stems from the inherent challenges in designing aerial humanoids:
- **Unique Design Challenges:** Aerial humanoids differ significantly from both conventional aerial vehicles and ground-based humanoids. They must satisfy both flight tracking and stability requirements while adhering to anthropomorphic geometry and accommodating complex actuation schemes like jet propulsion.
- **Coupled Design and Control:** The placement of thrust interfaces and the distribution of mass profoundly affect flight dynamics, control authority, and mechanical feasibility. Consequently, the design and control of these systems are tightly coupled problems requiring a coordinated exploration of both structural and control parameter spaces. Optimizing them independently may lead to suboptimal or unfeasible solutions.
- **Computational Intractability of High-Dimensional Spaces:** Exploring the vast design space for aerial humanoids, where each configuration must be structurally valid and dynamically evaluable, is computationally intensive. Traditional iterative design-test cycles are impractical for thousands of potential designs.
- **Ensuring Manufacturability and Feasibility:** Many co-design frameworks may generate theoretically optimal designs that are not practically manufacturable or structurally sound. There is a need for a framework that inherently ensures structural validity and compatibility with real-world prototyping constraints.
- **Performance-Driven Optimization:** The goal is not merely to create functional aerial humanoids, but to optimize their performance in terms of trajectory tracking accuracy and energy efficiency during dynamically constrained flight tasks. This requires integrating trajectory-level performance metrics into the design selection process.

By addressing these motivations, the paper seeks to provide a structured, efficient, and robust method for engineering jet-powered aerial humanoids that are "flight-ready" – meaning they are both mechanically feasible and capable of executing specific aerial tasks with validated control parameters and optimized performance.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed co-design framework integrates parametric geometry generation, structured sampling via Design of Experiments (DoE), and trajectory-based evaluation using model predictive control (MPC), followed by multi-objective optimization.

**A. Geometric Variation and Model Sampling**
- **Baseline Model:** The iRonCub-Mk3 aerial humanoid platform serves as the starting point.
- **Parametric Variation:** Eight geometric parameters are systematically varied, selected for their influence on thrust orientation, mass distribution, and moment of inertia. These parameters include:
 - Jetpack Turbine angle, offset distance, and height.
 - Forearm length, Shoulder width, Hip distance, Ankle link height, and Foot length.
- **Design Generation:** A Design of Experiments (DoE) approach, specifically Uniform Latin Hypercube (ULH) sampling, is used to generate 5,000 unique robot configurations. This ensures a broad and well-distributed exploration of the multidimensional parameter space.
- **Structural and Simulation Validity:** For each of the 5,000 configurations:
 - Updated physical properties (mass, center of mass, link inertia) are automatically derived from CAD assemblies (SolidWorks®).
 - These properties are propagated to URDF and MuJoCo XML files for compatibility with kinematic and physics-based simulation environments.
 - Finite Element Method (FEM) analysis is applied at the assembly level to validate structural integrity and manufacturability under nominal loading conditions. Models failing these constraints are discarded, ensuring only mechanically feasible designs proceed.
- **Indexed Pool:** Each validated model is assigned a unique index, creating a pool from which the optimization algorithm can select, rather than directly optimizing continuous geometric parameters.

**B. Design Space Reduction via Geometric Clustering**
- **Computational Challenge:** Evaluating all 5,000 structurally feasible models in the optimization pipeline is computationally intensive.
- **Clustering Application:** K-means clustering is applied to the eight-dimensional geometric parameter space. Each robot is represented by a normalized feature vector of its geometric inputs.
- **Centroid Selection:** The clustering identifies a reduced set of representative configurations, or centroids, that encapsulate the diversity of the original dataset. These centroids are then used in the multi-objective optimization.
- **Benefits:** This reduction makes the optimization tractable while preserving representative coverage of the design space. It also supports sensitivity analysis by examining parameter spread within clusters.

**C. Trajectory Generation**
- **Reference Path:** A minimum-jerk trajectory is defined through a sequence of 3D spatial waypoints. These waypoints specify desired robot positions.
- **Smooth Interpolation:** The trajectory is generated by interpolating between waypoints, incorporating local directional cues to ensure smooth and dynamically feasible position and velocity references for the robot's center of mass (CoM).
- **Consistency:** The same fixed trajectory is used for evaluating all centroid models and control parameter combinations, ensuring fair comparison of performance.

**D. Robot Model and Flight Control**
- **Robot Model:** The iRonCub-Mk3 is modeled as a floating-base multibody system with n+1 rigid links and n actuated joints. Turbine thrusters provide forces during flight. The system configuration includes base position, orientation, and joint angles.
- **Centroidal Momentum Dynamics:** System dynamics are formulated in terms of centroidal momentum, accounting for external forces including gravity and thrusts.
- **Model Predictive Control (MPC):** A linearized momentum-based MPC scheme is implemented for trajectory tracking.
 - **State Vector:** Includes CoM position, linear momentum, orientation, angular momentum, turbine thrust, thrust rate, and integral errors for CoM position and orientation.
 - **Input Vector:** Composed of joint displacement increments and turbine throttle commands.
 - **Cost Function:** A quadratic cost function is minimized, penalizing deviations from reference states (CoM position, linear momentum, orientation, angular momentum, integral errors) and control effort (joint motion, throttle rate change).
 - **Tunable Weights:** Eight weight matrices in the MPC cost function are tunable, defining priorities for state regulation versus input smoothness. These include weights for CoM position error ($W_x$), linear momentum error ($W_l$), orientation error ($W_\phi$), angular momentum error ($W_\omega$), integral position error ($W_{ex}$), integral orientation error ($W_{e\phi}$), joint motion ($W_{\Delta s}$), and throttle rate change ($W_{u_{th}}$).

**E. Design–Control Optimization**
- **Optimization Algorithm:** The NSGA-II (Nondominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm II) is used for multi-objective optimization.
- **Search Space:** The optimization jointly searches over:
 - A centroid index (selecting a valid robot morphology from the clustered set).
 - A vector of eight MPC cost weights.
- **Objectives:** The algorithm aims to minimize two objectives simultaneously:
 1) **Trajectory Tracking Error:** Measured as the mean squared error (MSE) of the robot's CoM position relative to the reference trajectory, computed across x, y, and z directions.
 2) **Energy Expenditure:** Calculated as the time integral of the absolute mechanical power exerted by the turbine thrusts (translational and rotational contributions) over the full trajectory duration.
- **Simulation and Constraints:**
 - Each candidate solution (centroid-controller pair) is simulated over the defined trajectory.
 - Simulations must complete without MPC solver failure.
 - Tracking error must remain below a threshold of 2.5 m.
 - Candidates violating these constraints are penalized or excluded from the Pareto front.
- **Computational Setup:** The framework runs on an Intel i7-10875H CPU with 32 GB RAM (Windows 11). SolidWorks handles geometric modeling, MuJoCo performs physics simulations, the controller is C++ with Python bindings, and modeFRONTIER manages the optimization, with simulations executed in a WSL2-based Conda environment.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The results section evaluates the effectiveness of the co-design framework by examining the trade-offs between trajectory tracking error and energy consumption for different clustering granularities and by analyzing the contributions of specific centroid models.

**A. Effect of Clustering Granularity on Optimization Performance**
- **Comparison:** NSGA-II was executed with different numbers of clusters: 50, 100, and 250. Population sizes and generation counts were scaled to maintain comparable total evaluations across these configurations (e.g., 60 generations for 25 initial population size for 50 clusters, versus 60 generations for 100 initial population size for 250 clusters).
- **Pareto Front Analysis:**
 - The **100-cluster case** demonstrated a consistently lower Pareto front compared to the 50 and 250-cluster cases. This indicates that the 100-cluster resolution yielded improved trade-offs, achieving lower trajectory tracking errors and/or lower energy consumption for comparable levels of the other objective.
 - The **50-cluster case** exhibited a less converged Pareto front, suggesting that a smaller number of clusters might lack sufficient morphological diversity to explore the optimal design space effectively.
 - The **250-cluster case** also showed a less converged front, with wider spread and higher objective values. This implies that increasing the number of clusters beyond a certain point can introduce excessive search complexity, potentially diluting the computational effort and hindering effective convergence towards a robust Pareto front.
- **Conclusion on Granularity:** The findings suggest that an intermediate clustering resolution, such as 100, provides a balance between design diversity and optimization tractability, leading to more effective co-design outcomes.

**B. Influence of Individual Designs and Control Parameter Tuning**
- **Candidate Solutions Distribution (100-cluster):** An analysis of all candidate solutions from the 100-cluster configuration, color-coded by centroid model index, revealed that certain morphologies consistently yielded high-performing solutions across various control gain settings. This was indicated by the repetition and spatial grouping of specific models in the objective space.
- **Pareto-Optimal Models:** Five specific centroid models (indices 44, 1188, 2586, 4082, and 4256) were identified as significant contributors to the final Pareto front.
 - **Extremes of the Pareto Front:**
 - **Model 1188 (Cyan):** Characterized by a high jetpack turbine angle (14°), large offset (30mm), and small forearm (15mm), tended to achieve low trajectory tracking error but at a high energy cost.
 - **Model 2586 (Green):** With a lower jetpack turbine angle (4°), large offset (30mm), and large hip distance (40mm), minimized energy consumption but at the expense of tracking accuracy. These models demonstrated specialization in optimizing a single objective.
 - **"Knee" Region (Balanced Solutions):**
 - **Models 44 (Blue), 4082 (Red), and 4256 (Dark Red):** These models appeared in the central "knee" region of the trade-off curve, indicating adaptability. Tuning the MPC control parameters for these fixed morphologies resulted in different trade-offs between tracking error and energy consumption, allowing them to provide well-balanced solutions. The presence of multiple colors and overlapping contributions in this region demonstrated their versatility.
- **Geometric Parameter Analysis:** While the specific geometric parameters of these models were tabulated (e.g., Model 44: angle 8°, offset 5mm, forearm 35mm, foot length 90mm), the research did not find a direct or consistent correlation between individual geometric features and specific performance outcomes. However, the repeated appearance of certain morphologies in key regions of the Pareto front underscored their importance in the co-design process.
- **Control Parameter Role:** The analysis highlighted that for a given morphology, adjusting MPC controller weights played a crucial role in shifting the performance trade-offs, demonstrating that high-performing solutions can arise from the same design through effective control tuning.

In summary, the framework successfully identified robot designs offering favorable trade-offs between tracking accuracy and energy. The clustering approach enabled efficient exploration of a large design space, leading to the isolation of a subset of centroid morphologies that are both structurally feasible and adaptable through control gain tuning, making them candidates for deployment.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of aerial humanoid robotics by addressing the complex co-design problem through a structured and computationally efficient framework. The development of a CAD-driven co-design methodology offers several areas of significance and potential impact:

- **Systematic Co-Design for Complex Systems:** The framework provides a systematic approach for the joint optimization of morphology and control for jet-powered aerial humanoids. This is particularly impactful for systems where these aspects are tightly coupled, and traditional sequential design-and-control methods often lead to suboptimal solutions. It moves beyond component-level optimization to full-body, trajectory-level performance evaluation.
- **Guaranteed Mechanical and Structural Feasibility:** By integrating CAD assemblies, automatic property extraction, and Finite Element Method (FEM) validation directly into the design generation phase, the framework ensures that all explored robot configurations are structurally sound and manufacturable. This addresses a common limitation in purely algorithmic design optimization, where outputs might not be physically realizable.
- **Computational Efficiency through Design Space Reduction:** The application of K-means clustering to reduce the vast design space of 5,000 unique models to a manageable set of centroids is critical. This strategy makes the multi-objective optimization computationally tractable, enabling a thorough exploration of morphology-control trade-offs without requiring excessive computational resources for every single design variant.
- **Performance-Driven Design Selection:** The multi-objective optimization using NSGA-II, focused on minimizing trajectory tracking error and energy expenditure, guides the selection towards designs that are not only functional but also optimized for specific performance metrics in dynamic flight scenarios. This allows for the identification of "flight-ready" configurations that balance accuracy and efficiency.
- **Identification of Adaptable Morphologies:** The results demonstrate that certain robot morphologies are more adaptable to various control strategies, offering a broader range of trade-offs between performance and energy consumption through controller gain tuning. This insight is valuable for designers seeking versatile robotic platforms.
- **Foundation for Future Robot Development:** This framework can serve as a foundational tool for the next generation of aerial humanoids. By providing a method to rapidly evaluate and select optimal designs for specific tasks, it can accelerate the development cycle for these complex robots.
- **Generalizability and Future Work:** The authors highlight potential extensions, such as supporting different geometric variations and applying the framework to a wider range of locomotion and manipulation tasks. This suggests the methodology could be generalized to various robot morphologies and mission profiles, leading to more flexible and efficient design workflows in robotics. Future work could also involve parallelizing evaluation to support even higher-resolution clustering and explore a broader range of optimal solutions.
