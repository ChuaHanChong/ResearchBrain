# Gradient Correlation Subspace Learning against Catastrophic Forgetting

> [!summary] Summary
> Gradient Correlation Subspace Learning (GCSL) was developed to combat catastrophic forgetting in neural networks during incremental class learning. This regularization-based method achieved a 2.5 to 3-fold improvement in average accuracy over baselines on MNIST and Fashion MNIST, demonstrating effective knowledge preservation in compact architectures.

## Problem

- Neural networks experience catastrophic forgetting when sequentially learning new tasks, severely degrading performance on previously acquired knowledge.
- Traditional deep learning paradigms struggle with continuous adaptation, limiting their applicability in dynamic, evolving environments.
- Retraining networks from scratch or fine-tuning them on new data is computationally expensive and often leads to the loss of prior learning.

## Method

- Introduces **GCSL** (==Gradient Correlation Subspace Learning==), which freezes previous task weights and trains new weights within a specific ==subspace==.
- The subspace is derived from the ==correlation matrix of gradients== from previous tasks, identifying directions least significant to existing knowledge.
- Employs ==Binary Cross Entropy loss==, calculated only for current task labels, to avoid implicitly modifying logits for previously learned classes.

## Results

- **GCSL** significantly reduced catastrophic forgetting, improving average accuracy by **2.5-3x** over baselines on MNIST and Fashion MNIST datasets.
- The method consistently surpassed Gradient Projection Memory (GPM) on MNIST across all tested network architectures.
- On Fashion MNIST, GCSL showed comparable or slightly better performance than GPM for larger network sizes, demonstrating competitive capabilities on more challenging datasets.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Constraining new learning to a subspace derived from the correlation of previous task gradients allows for knowledge preservation with minimal interference.
> - The optimal configuration of GCSL, including subspace size and application layer, is task-dependent, with simpler tasks benefiting from smaller subspaces and harder tasks requiring larger ones.
> - Applying GCSL to different layers (e.g., first vs. second hidden layer) can yield varied performance based on whether feature extraction or classification is more critical for a given dataset.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research addresses the problem of catastrophic forgetting within the field of Continual Learning (CL) or Lifelong Learning. Continual Learning aims to enable artificial neural networks to sequentially learn new tasks or classes without significantly degrading performance on previously learned ones. Traditionally, neural networks trained on new data often overwrite parameters critical for older tasks, leading to a severe decline in their ability to perform those tasks.

The paper identifies three primary scenarios of incremental learning: Incremental Task Learning, Incremental Domain Learning, and Incremental Class Learning. This work specifically focuses on Incremental Class Learning, which is characterized as the most challenging scenario. In this setting, the network works with a predefined maximum number of labels and learns each new task by mapping its labels to the next available logits, requiring the network to expand its classification capabilities over time.

Existing approaches to mitigating catastrophic forgetting broadly fall into three categories:
1. **Architectural Methods:** These methods involve expanding the network size (e.g., Progressive Neural Networks, Dynamically Expandable Networks) or adding compression steps to manage growth after each task (e.g., PackNet, Progress and Compress).
2. **Replay Methods:** These approaches store or generate samples from previous tasks to interleave with new task training (e.g., Meta-Experience Replay, Deep Generative Replay). They rely on additional memory for samples or generative models.
3. **Regularization-Based Methods:** These methods impose constraints on weight updates during new task learning to preserve knowledge from previous tasks. Examples include Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC), which uses the Fisher information matrix to identify and protect important weights.

Gradient Correlation Subspace Learning (GCSL) is positioned within the regularization-based methods, specifically those that incorporate gradient calculations into their approach. Other methods in this subcategory include Orthogonal Gradient Descent (OGD), Orthogonal Weight Modulation (OWM), Gradient Episodic Memory (GEM), and Gradient Projection Memory (GPM). GCSL differentiates itself from these related methods by operating in the weight space rather than directly altering gradients during back-propagation. It focuses on projecting new trainable weights into a specific subspace defined by the correlation matrix of gradients. Unlike OWM, which uses recursive least squares, GCSL leverages the correlation matrix to capture relationships between logits and loss, aiming to identify a subspace that minimally interferes with existing knowledge. Unlike GEM and GPM, GCSL temporarily initializes new weights and freezes original weights, allowing compatibility with any optimizer.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce and evaluate Gradient Correlation Subspace Learning (GCSL) as a novel method to reduce catastrophic forgetting in neural networks, particularly within the challenging context of incremental class learning.

The motivation for this work stems from several limitations of current deep learning paradigms:
1. **Inability to Continuously Adapt:** Traditional artificial neural networks are designed for static environments and often exhibit severe performance degradation when faced with sequential learning of new tasks or classes. This "catastrophic forgetting" renders them unsuitable for dynamic, evolving environments.
2. **Resource Inefficiency:** When new tasks or data become available, it is often necessary to retrain a network from scratch or fine-tune it. Retraining from scratch is computationally expensive and time-consuming, especially for complex deep learning models trained on large datasets. Fine-tuning often leads to a decline in performance on previously learned tasks if those tasks are not adequately represented in the fine-tuning data.
3. **Desire for Lifelong Learning:** There is a growing interest in developing artificial intelligence systems that can continuously accumulate knowledge over their lifetime, similar to biological learning. Enabling networks to learn new information incrementally while retaining old knowledge is a critical step towards achieving such lifelong learning capabilities.

Specifically, the authors aim to develop a method that allows networks to learn new tasks with minimal interference on previously learned tasks. GCSL seeks to achieve this by intelligently allocating network capacity in a way that preserves learned representations. The method proposes to identify and utilize subspaces within the network's weight space that are least significant to prior tasks, thereby allowing new learning to occur without substantially altering critical parameters. This approach intends to make previously trained deep networks more adaptable and reusable when new requirements arise, without the need for extensive retraining.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Gradient Correlation Subspace Learning (GCSL) method is designed to mitigate catastrophic forgetting in incremental class learning by constraining the learning of new tasks to a specific subspace of the network's weights. The method can be applied to one or more fully connected layers within a network, and the size of the subspace can be adjusted per layer and per task.

**Core Mechanism:**
GCSL operates by freezing the weights of previously learned tasks and introducing new, trainable weights for the current task. These new weights are projected into a subspace derived from the correlation matrix of gradients of the previous tasks.

**Detailed Steps of GCSL for an Incremental Task `i` (as per Algorithm 1):**

1. **Initialization:**
 - If it is the first task (i=0), the network weights are randomly initialized, and training proceeds normally.
 - For subsequent tasks (i>0), for each layer where GCSL is applied:
 - New trainable weights ($W^l_{trainable}$) are randomly initialized (e.g., using Xavier initialization).
 - The weights ($W^l_{i-1}$) learned from the previous task are frozen.
 - The effective weights for the current task ($W^l_i$) are initialized as a combination of the frozen weights and the new trainable weights projected onto a subspace: $W^l_i \leftarrow W^l_{i-1} + V^l_{i-1} \cdot W^l_{trainable}$. The term $V^l_{i-1}$ represents the projection operator (a matrix of eigenvectors) derived from the previous task.

2. **Training Phase:**
 - The network performs forward and backward passes using the combined weights $W^l_i$ for mini-batches of the current task's dataset ($D_i$).
 - Crucially, only the newly initialized trainable weights ($W^l_{trainable}$) are updated during this phase. The frozen weights ($W^l_{i-1}$) remain constant.

3. **Subspace Calculation (Post-Training):**
 - After training the current task `i`, a correlation matrix ($C^l_g$) is computed for each layer where GCSL is applied. This matrix is based on the outer product of gradients of the task-specific loss ($L_{BCE}$) with respect to the layer's activations ($h^l$) for each sample in the current training set (or a subset thereof): $C^l_g = \frac{dL_{BCE}}{dh^l}^T \frac{dL_{BCE}}{dh^l}$. An accumulated average of these matrices is used.
 - Eigenvectors ($V^l_i$) of this averaged correlation matrix ($C^l_g$) are then computed and sorted by their corresponding eigenvalues.

**Ambiguity in Eigenvector Selection:**
The paper contains a discrepancy regarding the selection of eigenvectors for the projection matrix $V$:
- For the projection operator $V^l_{t_{k-1}}$ used during the training of task $t_k$ (i.e., in the formula $W^l_i \leftarrow W^l_{i-1} + V^l_{i-1} \cdot W^l_{trainable}$), the paper states it is constructed from "the $n$ smallest eigenvalues" of the gradient correlation matrix from the previous task. This aligns with the stated goal of learning in a "least significant" or "least affected" subspace.
- However, when describing the calculation of the eigenvectors $V^l_{t_k}$ for *storage* after the current task $t_k$ (which will then become $V^l_{t_{k-1}}$ for the *next* task $t_{k+1}$), the paper states that $V^l_{t_k}$ is composed of "the $n$ eigenvectors with the largest corresponding eigenvalues." If this stored $V^l_{t_k}$ (representing the most significant directions of task $t_k$) is subsequently used for projection in task $t_{k+1}$, it would imply training in the most significant subspace of the preceding task, which contradicts the core principle of minimizing interference. This discrepancy is present in the provided text.

**Task-Specific Binary Cross Entropy Loss:**
To prevent catastrophic forgetting, GCSL utilizes a Binary Cross Entropy (BCE) loss function instead of the traditional Cross Entropy (CE) loss. The BCE loss is calculated only for the labels present in the current training session. This approach prevents the loss from propagating through logits corresponding to labels from previous tasks that are not part of the current training set. This effectively avoids implicitly teaching the network to "mute" or de-emphasize previous task logits.

**Output and Storage:**
After task `i` is complete, the updated network weights ($W_i$) and the newly computed eigenvectors ($V_i$) are stored. Only these elements are carried forward for the next task's training.

**Flexibility:**
GCSL is designed to be flexible, allowing for:
- Application to one or multiple layers of a neural network.
- Adjustment of the subspace size (number of eigenvectors `n`) per layer and per task.
- Compatibility with any standard optimizer, as it modifies the weight update mechanism rather than the gradient calculation itself.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents findings from two sets of experiments: GCSL experiments exploring different configurations of the method, and comparison experiments against a state-of-the-art method.

#### 4.1. GCSL Experiments

These experiments were conducted on the MNIST and Fashion MNIST datasets, using empirically determined minimal fully connected network architectures to challenge the method's ability to find orthogonal gradient spaces rather than simply utilizing unused network capacity. Tasks were learned incrementally in pairs of labels (e.g., 0,1 then 2,3, etc.). Accuracy was measured on an accumulative validation set across all tasks learned so far.

#### 4.1.1. Results for MNIST Dataset

- **Forgetting Mitigation:** GCSL demonstrated a substantial improvement in mitigating catastrophic forgetting. Without GCSL, incremental learning resulted in an average accuracy of approximately 30%. With GCSL, the top average accuracy reached about 80%, indicating a significant reduction in forgetting (over 2.5 times improvement).
- **Subspace Size:** The largest possible subspace configuration (e.g., for a hidden layer architecture) did not yield the best results. Smaller subspace sizes, such as, generally performed better, suggesting an optimal balance between learning new tasks and preserving old ones.
- **Layer Specificity:** Applying GCSL to both hidden layers did not necessarily lead to the best performance. The highest accuracy was achieved when the method was applied only to the first hidden layer (L1). Applying GCSL only to the second hidden layer (L2) resulted in significantly worse performance. This suggests that for MNIST, preserving feature extraction in the first layer is more critical for robust continual learning with GCSL.

#### 4.1.2. Results for Fashion MNIST Dataset

- **Forgetting Mitigation:** Similar to MNIST, GCSL improved forgetting behavior considerably. Without GCSL, accuracy was around 15%, while GCSL achieved a top average accuracy of approximately 55% (over 3 times improvement).
- **Challenging Task:** The baseline accuracy for Fashion MNIST was lower than MNIST, indicating it is a more challenging dataset for the chosen network architecture.
- **Subspace Size:** In contrast to MNIST, larger subspace sizes generally led to better average accuracy for Fashion MNIST. This suggests that for a more complex task, the network requires more "room" or flexibility to learn new features without overly constraining itself, even if it might risk some interference.
- **Layer Specificity:** For Fashion MNIST, the best results were obtained when GCSL was applied only to the second hidden layer (L2). Applying it solely to the first hidden layer (L1) performed worse. This indicates that for Fashion MNIST, preserving the classification segment of the network (L2) by learning orthogonally in its space is more beneficial, while the feature extraction in earlier layers might benefit from more unconstrained updates or share features.

#### 4.1.3. Comparison Between Datasets

- **Task Difficulty and Subspace Size:** The easier MNIST task benefited from smaller subspaces that prioritized preventing forgetting. The harder Fashion MNIST task required larger subspaces, indicating a greater need to learn new information, even at a potential cost of some forgetting.
- **Optimal Layer:** The optimal layer for GCSL application differed significantly. For MNIST, applying GCSL to the first layer (feature extraction) was most effective, possibly due to distinct features for handwritten digits. For Fashion MNIST, applying GCSL to the second layer (classification) was more effective, suggesting that for more nuanced and overlapping image categories, preserving the classification layer is paramount.

GCSL was compared against Gradient Projection Memory (GPM), a related state-of-the-art continual learning method, across varying network architectures.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This paper introduces Gradient Correlation Subspace Learning (GCSL) as a new contribution to the family of orthogonal gradient-based approaches in continual learning. The proposed method demonstrates a robust ability to reduce catastrophic forgetting, particularly when applied to compact network architectures.

The significance and potential impact of GCSL include:

1. **Effective Forgetting Mitigation:** The experimental results show that GCSL substantially improves performance on previously learned tasks compared to naive incremental learning, reducing catastrophic forgetting by a factor of 2.5 to 3 on the tested datasets.
2. **Low Collateral Storage:** GCSL requires storing only the network weights and a subset of eigenvectors (V matrix) for each layer, which represents a relatively minor overhead compared to methods that require storing entire datasets (replay methods) or larger network expansions (architectural methods). This makes it suitable for applications with memory or computational resource constraints.
3. **Customizability and Integration:** The method offers high customizability, allowing users to select specific layers for GCSL application and adjust the subspace size dynamically. Its integration into standard training schemes is straightforward, as it primarily involves freezing specific weights and defining new trainable components, remaining compatible with any optimizer.
4. **Novel Orthogonal Subspace Learning:** By leveraging the gradient correlation matrix to define subspaces for new learning, GCSL offers an alternative perspective within gradient-based continual learning methods, potentially opening new avenues for research into how network capacity can be intelligently managed.
5. **Potential for Broader Application:** The method's effectiveness on lean networks suggests its potential for use in resource-constrained environments or for incrementally adapting pre-trained models. Future work may explore its application to self-supervised networks, combination with replay-based approaches, and integration with convolutional neural network (CNN) architectures.
6. **Insights into Layer Importance:** The varying optimal layer for GCSL application across different datasets (first layer for MNIST, second for Fashion MNIST) provides insights into how task characteristics might influence the most effective strategy for preserving feature extraction versus classification capabilities in continual learning.

The paper serves as an initial exploration of GCSL, demonstrating its promise as a competitive method in continual learning. Further research is necessary to fully understand its implications, optimize its parameters dynamically, and evaluate its performance across a wider range of benchmarks and network types.
