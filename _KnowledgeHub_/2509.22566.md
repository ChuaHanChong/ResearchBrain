# From Parameters to Behaviors: Unsupervised Compression of the Policy Space

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Politecnico di Milano and Technion introduced a two-stage framework that compresses high-dimensional Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) policies into a low-dimensional, behaviorally aligned latent space. This approach achieved compression ratios up to 121801:1 and enabled faster fine-tuning convergence on various control tasks compared to state-of-the-art DRL baselines.

## Problem

- Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) faces sample inefficiency, particularly in multi-task settings, due to policy optimization in high-dimensional, redundant parameter spaces.
- Many distinct policy parameter configurations can map to functionally similar behaviors, necessitating extensive exploration and computational resources.
- Traditional policy optimization methods often struggle to balance the expressivity of large networks with the efficiency of learning in high-dimensional spaces.

## Method

- A two-phase framework combining ==unsupervised pre-training== and supervised fine-tuning is proposed to learn and optimize policies in a low-dimensional latent space.
- A Novelty Search algorithm generates a dataset of behaviorally diverse policies, which an autoencoder with a novel 'Behavioral Reconstruction Loss' compresses into a compact latent representation by minimizing behavioral divergence (not parameter-level error), addressing parameter-space symmetries.
- Latent Policy Gradient with Parameter-based Exploration (Latent PGPE) is employed for efficient, gradient-based fine-tuning directly within the learned low-dimensional latent space.

## Results

- The proposed pipeline successfully learned low-dimensional latent representations, demonstrating substantial compression ratios up to **121801**:1 for policy parameters.
- Visualizations and performance recovery metrics indicated that latent spaces consistently encoded diverse and high-performing policies, with quality depending on latent dimension and initial dataset diversity.
- Latent PGPE achieved faster convergence than most state-of-the-art DRL baselines (7 out of 8 tasks) during fine-tuning, often reaching comparable or superior final performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - It is feasible to learn a low-dimensional, behaviorally meaningful latent representation of high-dimensional policy parameter spaces in an unsupervised fashion, achieving substantial compression.
> - The intrinsic dimensionality of a policy's behavior manifold is primarily determined by the environment's complexity, not solely by the policy network's parameter count, supporting the Manifold Hypothesis in DRL.
> - Optimizing policies within a compact, behavior-aligned latent space enables faster convergence and competitive performance compared to optimizing in the full parameter space, allowing simpler algorithms to control complex networks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) has demonstrated capabilities in various domains. However, a persistent challenge is its sample inefficiency, particularly when agents must learn in multi-task environments. This inefficiency is often attributed to the common practice of optimizing policies directly within a high-dimensional and often redundant parameter space. In this space, numerous distinct weight configurations can map to a comparatively smaller set of effective behaviors.

Existing research has attempted to mitigate this limitation through various approaches. Some works have focused on explicitly learning diverse behaviors (e.g., Eysenbach et al., 2018; Zahavy et al., 2023), while others have explored enforcing smaller policy networks within asymmetric actor-critic architectures (e.g., Degrave et al., 2022; Mastikhina et al., 2025). The broader framework of Unsupervised Reinforcement Learning (URL) (Laskin et al., 2021) also seeks to acquire general-purpose knowledge in a pre-training phase without specific reward signals, which can then be leveraged for downstream tasks.

This work addresses the problem by drawing inspiration from the Manifold Hypothesis (Cayton et al., 2005), positing that the manifold of realizable behaviors is intrinsically low-dimensional and largely independent of the network's parameter count. This contrasts with approaches like Policy Space Compression (Mutti et al., 2022), which aims to reduce the cardinality rather than the dimensionality of the policy space and involves an NP-hard optimization problem with stricter constraints.

Furthermore, this research relates to the field of Weight Space Learning (WSL), which focuses on learning latent representations of neural network parameters (Schürholt et al., 2021; 2022; 2024). Unlike some WSL approaches in RL that use task-specific generative models conditioned on goals or performance checkpoints (Faccio et al., 2023; Peebles et al., 2022), the present work employs a fully unsupervised approach. A key distinction is the use of a "behavioral reconstruction loss," which allows the autoencoder to compress policies based on their functional similarity rather than parameter proximity. This method inherently bypasses challenges associated with parameter-space symmetries (e.g., neuron permutations), which often complicate traditional WSL methods that rely on parameter-reconstruction losses. This also differentiates it from prior works using VAEs for policy compression or diversity, such as those by Chang et al. (2019) and Hegde et al. (2023), which typically use parameter-reconstruction objectives and achieve more moderate compression ratios.

The present paper proposes a paradigm shift towards learning and optimizing policies directly in a low-dimensional, behavior-aligned latent space. It integrates concepts from URL, manifold learning, and policy optimization to offer a novel two-stage framework that aims to provide a more efficient and scalable solution to DRL's sample inefficiency.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from the recognized sample inefficiency of Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL), which becomes particularly pronounced in multi-task scenarios. The authors contend that this inefficiency arises from the conventional approach of optimizing policies within a high-dimensional and often redundant parameter space (Θ). In this space, numerous distinct parameter configurations can result in functionally similar or identical agent behaviors. This redundancy necessitates extensive exploration and computation, leading to increased sample complexity.

To address this, the authors propose leveraging the Manifold Hypothesis, which posits that the manifold of realizable behaviors is intrinsically low-dimensional and its complexity is primarily a function of the environment, rather than the number of parameters in the policy network. Based on this hypothesis, the primary objective is to transition from optimizing policies in the high-dimensional parameter space to optimizing within a compact, low-dimensional latent space (Z) that is organized by functional similarity. This shift is hypothesized to enable more efficient learning and adaptation to new tasks.

The research specifies three core questions:

- **Q1: Is it possible to learn a low-dimensional latent representation of a high-dimensional policy parameter space in an unsupervised fashion?** This question probes the fundamental feasibility of compressing complex policy networks into a simpler, task-agnostic latent representation. The unsupervised nature of this learning is critical, as it aims to capture general environmental dynamics rather than task-specific reward structures.
- **Q2: What are the properties of such a latent representation, if it exists? Is its intrinsic dimension a function of the behavioral complexity rather than the size of the parameter space?** This objective focuses on characterizing the nature of the learned latent space. It seeks to ascertain whether the dimensionality of this space truly reflects the inherent complexity of the behaviors achievable within an environment, as opposed to simply mirroring the size of the neural network used to parameterize the policies.
- **Q3: How can we fine-tune on specific tasks leveraging the low-dimensional space? Does this offer any advantages?** This question addresses the practical utility of the compressed latent space. It investigates whether optimizing policies within this reduced-dimensional representation for specific tasks can yield benefits such as faster convergence or improved performance compared to traditional DRL methods operating in the full parameter space.

Collectively, these objectives aim to develop a framework that can learn a compact, behaviorally meaningful representation of policies and subsequently exploit this representation for more efficient and scalable DRL.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology is structured as a two-phase framework: an unsupervised pre-training phase followed by a supervised fine-tuning phase. This framework comprises three sequential steps: Policy Dataset Generation, Latent Behavior Compression, and Latent Behavior Optimization.

#### 3.1. Policy Dataset Generation

The initial step involves creating a dataset, denoted D_Θ, consisting of policies that exhibit a wide range of diverse behaviors. The authors identify that merely sampling policy parameters randomly from a uniform distribution is insufficient, as it tends to yield policies that are functionally similar or lack exploratory capacity. To counteract this, a Novelty Search algorithm is employed.

- **Behavioral Divergence Proxy:** Instead of directly estimating the divergence between complex state-action distributions (d_sa_π_θ), which would be computationally intensive, the method uses a proxy: the L2 distance between the action distributions of two policies (π_θ, π_θ') evaluated over a finite subset of states (M). This is formalized as D(π_θ || π_θ') = sqrt( Σ_{i=1 to M} (π_θ(· | s_i) - π_θ'(· | s_i))^2 ).
- **Novelty Scoring:** For each generated policy, a novelty score ρ(π_θ) is computed as the average divergence from its k_n-nearest neighbors in this behavioral space. Policies with higher novelty scores are considered behaviorally unique.
- **Dataset Formation:** The final dataset D_Θ is curated by selecting a top percentile of policies based on their novelty scores, thus ensuring a collection of behaviorally diverse policies. In experiments, k_n was set to 15, and the distance used for training the autoencoder was the mean squared error over sampled states.

#### 3.2. Latent Behavior Compression

In the second stage, the method learns a low-dimensional latent manifold from the behaviorally diverse policy dataset D_Θ using a generative model. An autoencoder architecture is selected for this purpose, comprising an encoder f_ξ: Θ → Z and a decoder g_ζ: Z → Θ.

- **Behavioral Reconstruction Loss:** A key innovation is the introduction of a novel Behavioral Reconstruction Loss (L_B(ξ, ζ)). Unlike standard autoencoders that minimize parameter-level reconstruction error, this loss minimizes the expected behavioral divergence between the original policy (π_θ) and its reconstruction (π_{(g_ζ ◦ f_ξ)(θ)}): L_B(ξ, ζ) = E_{θ~D_Θ} [D(π_θ || π_{(g_ζ ◦ f_ξ)(θ)})].
- **Functional Similarity:** This objective encourages the decoder to generate any parameterization that reproduces the original policy's behavior, thereby organizing the latent space (Z) purely based on functional similarity. This circumvents the issue of parameter-space symmetries.
- **Empirical Loss:** Practically, the loss is estimated as the Mean Squared Error between action vectors over a subset of sampled states: ˆL_B(ξ, ζ) = (1/NM) Σ_{i=1 to N} Σ_{j=1 to M'} ||π_θ_i(s_j) - π_{(g_ζ ◦ f_ξ)(θ_i)}(s_j)||^2_2, where N is the number of policies and M' is the number of sampled states.
- **Autoencoder Details:** A fully-connected MLP is used for the autoencoder. The encoder has two hidden layers (25 and 10 neurons), and the decoder is mirrored. ELU non-linearities are used. Training is performed using Adam optimizer for 50 epochs with a learning rate of 0.0001 and a batch size of 64, incorporating a learning rate scheduler.

#### 3.3. Latent Behavior Optimization

The final stage leverages the learned latent manifold for efficient, task-specific fine-tuning. Once the autoencoder is trained, the decoder g_ζ* (with frozen parameters ζ*) acts as a deterministic and differentiable function that maps latent codes (z) to policy parameters (θ).

- **Policy Gradient in Latent Space:** This structure enables the adaptation of Policy Gradient (PG) methods to operate directly in the latent space. By applying the chain rule, the gradient of the task-specific objective (J_R(z)) with respect to the latent code (z) can be computed as ∇_z J_R(z) = ∇_z g_ζ*(z)^T ∇_θ J_R(θ). Here, ∇_θ J_R(θ) is the conventional policy gradient, and ∇_z g_ζ*(z) is the Jacobian of the decoder.
- **Latent PGPE:** The paper specifically adapts Policy Gradient with Parameter-based Exploration (PGPE) to the latent space, referred to as Latent PGPE. This is particularly advantageous because PGPE typically struggles with high-dimensional parameter spaces but can be effective in a low-dimensional latent space while retaining the expressivity of the original large network. The authors note that for PGPE, the Jacobian does not need to be explicitly computed because the decoder is treated as a deterministic part of the black-box evaluation process of policies sampled from a Gaussian hyper-policy in the latent space.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

- **Environments:** Experiments are conducted in continuous control domains: Mountain Car Continuous (MC), Reacher (RC), Hopper (HP), and HalfCheetah (HC). Multiple downstream tasks are defined for each environment.
- **Policy Networks:** Fully-connected, feed-forward, deterministic MLPs are used. For MC, policies vary in size (Small: ~10^1 parameters, Medium: ~10^3, Large: ~10^5). Medium policies are used for HP and HC, and a specific medium size for RC.
- **Latent Dimensions:** The latent space dimensionality (k) is varied for different environments (e.g., MC: 1D, 2D, 3D; RC: 1D, 2D, 3D, 5D, 8D; HP/HC: 5D, 8D, 16D).
- **Baselines:** For fine-tuning, Latent PGPE is compared against state-of-the-art DRL algorithms (PPO, DDPG, TD3, SAC) and a Policy Gradient with Parameter-based Exploration (Parameter PGPE) operating in the original high-dimensional parameter space.
- **Evaluation:** The quality of latent spaces is assessed by discretizing or randomly sampling latent codes and evaluating the performance of the decoded policies, calculating a "performance recovery" metric relative to the original dataset. Fine-tuning performance is evaluated by tracking average returns over time.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments address the three research questions, providing empirical evidence for the proposed framework's capabilities.

#### 4.1. Unsupervised Pre-training

**Q1: Is it possible to learn a low-dimensional latent representation of a high-dimensional policy parameter space in an unsupervised fashion?**
The findings indicate a positive answer. The proposed unsupervised pipeline successfully learned low-dimensional latent representations capable of encoding behaviorally meaningful policies. This process achieved substantial compression ratios, up to five orders of magnitude (specifically, a 121801:1 compression rate at peak), demonstrating the feasibility of unsupervised behavioral compression.

**Q2: What are the properties of such a latent representation, if it exists? Is its intrinsic dimension a function of the behavioral complexity rather than the size of the parameter space?**
The experiments provide evidence for the properties of the learned latent representation:
- **Visual Inspection of Latent Manifolds (Fig. 3):** Visualizations revealed that the latent spaces, across varying encoding dimensions (1D, 2D, 3D) and policy sizes (Small, Medium, Large), consistently encoded behaviorally diverse and high-performing policies. Larger policy sizes tended to result in more complex latent landscapes, suggesting that these policies might encompass a broader range of behaviors or that compressing them into very low dimensions is more challenging. Increasing the latent dimension generally led to larger and better-shaped regions of high performance in the manifold, which can facilitate optimization.
- **Influence of Environment vs. Network Size:** The results support the hypothesis that the intrinsic dimensionality of the policy behavior manifold is primarily determined by the environment's complexity rather than the policy network's parameter count. Even a one-dimensional latent space was observed to capture a significant range of behaviors in some settings.
- **Dependency on Dataset Quality:** The quality of the learned representation was found to be dependent on the diversity of the initial policy dataset. If specific behaviors were underrepresented in the pre-training data (e.g., the "height" task in Mountain Car), the latent space struggled to encode them effectively.
- **Quality of Latent Behavior Compression (Table 1, Table 2):**
 - **Performance Recovery:** Generally, increasing the number of latent dimensions or the size of the policy network led to improved performance recovery. Some configurations exhibited recovery rates greater than 1, suggesting that the autoencoder could generalize and produce policies with higher performance than some of those in the training dataset, potentially due to the autoencoder's generalization capacity or evaluation variance.
 - **Instability in Low Dimensions:** For Small policies, 1D latent spaces often failed to learn meaningful encodings, collapsing to uniform representations, attributed to instability in insufficient latent dimensions. This issue was mitigated by increasing the latent dimensions or using larger policy networks.
 - **Generalization to Complex Environments:** Similar trends were observed in more complex MuJoCo environments (Hopper, HalfCheetah). Increasing latent dimensions consistently improved performance recovery in HalfCheetah. Task complexity also affected generalization, with lower recovery rates for more challenging tasks.
 - **Dataset Size:** The size of the policy dataset (10k, 50k, 100k) did not show a consistent or strong influence on performance recovery in the evaluated settings.

#### 4.2. Supervised Fine-tuning

**Q3: How can we fine-tune on specific tasks leveraging the low-dimensional space? Does this offer any advantages?**
The results provide a positive answer, indicating that fine-tuning in the learned low-dimensional latent space offers advantages.
- **Convergence Rate and Performance (Fig. 4, Fig. 5):** Latent PGPE, operating in the compressed latent space, demonstrated faster convergence than most state-of-the-art DRL baselines (7 out of 8 tasks). Its performance was often comparable to or better than the baselines, even for complex tasks like "speed" in Mountain Car and Reacher.
- **Latent Dimension Correlation:** Both the convergence rate and the final performance of Latent PGPE were positively correlated with the number of latent dimensions. This reinforces the observation that larger latent spaces tend to be better structured, offering more amenable optimization landscapes.
- **Dependency on Pre-training Quality:** The performance during fine-tuning was directly linked to the quality of the learned latent representation. For instance, Latent PGPE failed to solve the "height" task in Mountain Car, a limitation attributed to the insufficient representation of high-performing policies for this specific behavior within the initial unsupervised policy dataset.
- **Baseline Performance:** Traditional DRL algorithms (PPO, DDPG, TD3, SAC) typically struggled with smaller policy networks but performed better with larger ones. Conversely, Parameter PGPE (operating in the original parameter space) benefited from fewer parameters but suffered from high sample complexity. Latent PGPE successfully leveraged the advantages of both: controlling a small set of parameters while retaining the expressivity of a large network.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces a novel framework that shifts the paradigm of Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) by moving policy optimization from the high-dimensional, redundant parameter space to a low-dimensional, behaviorally-aligned latent space. This shift is significant for several reasons:

- **Addressing Sample Inefficiency:** The core contribution directly tackles the notorious sample inefficiency of DRL, particularly in multi-task settings. By compressing policy parameterizations by several orders of magnitude (up to 121801:1), the framework offers a method to operate in a significantly more compact and efficient representation, potentially reducing the computational resources and data required for learning.
- **Empirical Validation of Manifold Hypothesis:** The work provides empirical evidence supporting the "Manifold Hypothesis" in the context of DRL. It suggests that the inherent dimensionality of a policy's behavior manifold is predominantly dictated by the environment's complexity rather than the size of the underlying neural network, which could influence future network design and theoretical understanding in DRL.
- **Enhanced Optimization Efficiency:** The ability to perform gradient-based optimization directly in the low-dimensional latent space enables faster convergence and competitive performance for task-specific fine-tuning. This allows simpler policy optimization algorithms, like PGPE, to become effective even when controlling the expressive power of large neural networks, which traditionally suffer in high-dimensional spaces.
- **Modular and Extensible Framework:** The proposed two-phase (unsupervised pre-training, supervised fine-tuning) and three-step (dataset generation, compression, optimization) pipeline is intentionally modular. This design encourages future research into alternative behavioral divergence metrics, more advanced generative architectures for compression, and the adaptation of various optimization algorithms for latent behavior.
- **Broader Applicability in RL:** The learned compact and semantically meaningful representation of policies could have broader applications beyond the current scope. It may benefit other RL approaches that condition value functions or meta-learners directly on policy parameters, providing a more robust and efficient input than raw parameter vectors.
- **Contributions to Reproducibility:** The paper contributes to scientific reproducibility by providing detailed experimental settings, hyperparameters, and making the source code publicly available, which allows other researchers to verify and build upon the reported findings.

In summary, this work presents a foundational step towards more efficient and scalable DRL by proposing a principled method for unsupervised compression of the policy space based on functional behavior rather than parametric proximity.
