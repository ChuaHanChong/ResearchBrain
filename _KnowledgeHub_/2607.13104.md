# Self-Improvements in Modern Agentic Systems: A Survey

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Jilin University, KAUST, University of Alberta, and IDSIA/USI/SUPSI present a comprehensive survey that unifies the fragmented field of self-improving AI agents, providing a formal framework and a systematic taxonomy for understanding modern foundation-model-based systems. The work distinguishes between foundation model parameter updates and scaffold component modifications, identifying diverse self-induced learning signals and outlining future research directions.

## Problem

- The rapidly evolving field of self-improving AI agents suffers from fragmented terminology and scope.
- A lack of a unified formal perspective or a systematic connection between modern FM-based agents and classical AI self-improvement concepts exists.
- Existing surveys often treat foundation model fine-tuning and agent scaffolding as isolated topics, without a holistic view.

## Method

- Introduces a unified system-level framework that defines a modern agent as a ==foundation model== coupled with a dynamic ==operational scaffold==.
- Formalizes self-improvement as a ==self-induced update operator== that modifies either the foundation model parameters or the scaffold components.
- Develops a systematic ==taxonomy== classifying self-improvement methods based on the target of modification and the nature of the ==self-induced learning signals==.

## Results

- The survey details various methods for foundation model improvement, including using intrinsic generative demonstrations, evaluative feedback, and extrinsic exploratory experience with grounded or simulated environments.
- It comprehensively categorizes scaffolding improvement techniques, covering prompt optimization, memory evolution (objects, structure, processing), and autonomous tool governance (routing, refinement, creation).
- Practical applications across domains like software engineering, web navigation, gaming, and robotics leverage these mechanisms, with evaluation requiring continuous tracking of **performance trajectories**, **resource efficiency**, and **stability**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Self-improvement can be distinctly categorized into updates to the underlying foundation model parameters (slow adaptation) or modifications to the agent's operational scaffold (fast adaptation).
> - Self-induced learning signals, such as generative demonstrations, evaluative feedback, and extrinsic exploratory experience, are crucial drivers for continuous agent evolution.
> - Effective self-improvement requires a balance between different adaptation loops and robust evaluation protocols to ensure continuous performance gains and address safety challenges.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses the rapidly evolving field of autonomous AI agents, particularly those powered by large-scale foundation models (FMs) such as large language models (LLMs) and vision-language models (VLMs). These modern agentic systems, which leverage natural language as a unified interface for perception, reasoning, and control, are transitioning from research prototypes to deployed systems. A core challenge and objective within this landscape is the development of AI systems capable of self-improvement—autonomous adaptation and evolution based on experience with minimal or no human intervention.

Historically, the concept of self-improvement in AI dates back to early optimization frameworks, self-reproducing automata theory, symbolic AI's heuristic self-modification, and connectionism's meta-learning approaches, including self-referential learning and Gödel Machines. The paper situates modern FM-based agents as a continuation of this intellectual lineage, arguing that FMs alleviate historical bottlenecks by providing a highly expressive, general-purpose semantic medium for self-modification.

Despite the rapid proliferation of empirical frameworks for self-evolving agents, the broader research landscape has remained fragmented in terminology and scope. Existing surveys often treat foundation model fine-tuning and agent scaffolding as isolated topics, lacking a unified formal perspective or tracing the conceptual roots to classical AI. This survey aims to bridge these gaps by providing a rigorous and systematic positioning of modern self-improving agentic systems within a unified taxonomy and formalization, thereby clarifying their historical evolution and offering a forward-looking roadmap. It distinguishes itself by providing a unified formalization, tracing historical roots, and offering a signal-based lens for classifying improvement mechanisms.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this survey is to frame modern self-improving agents as adaptive systems that convert experience into accumulated capability gains, with the ultimate goal of achieving controllable evolution or adaptation from experience with minimal to no human input.

The detailed motivations and objectives are as follows:

- **Unify the Field:** The research field surrounding self-improving agents is characterized by fragmented terminology, with similar concepts often described under different labels (e.g., self-correction, meta-prompting, self-play). A key motivation is to provide a common language and a unified system-level framework to clarify these underlying mechanistic similarities.
- **Establish a Formal Framework:** The paper aims to formalize self-improvement as a self-induced update operator. This involves defining a modern agent as a configuration coupling a foundation model with an operational scaffold (comprising prompts, memory, tools, and control logic) and formalizing how this configuration updates itself.
- **Develop a Systematic Taxonomy:** A major objective is to organize prior work into a comprehensive taxonomy. This taxonomy categorizes existing methods based on the target of modification (foundation model parameters or scaffold components) and the signals that drive these changes.
- **Trace Historical Context:** The survey is motivated by a desire to connect modern FM-based self-improvement to its historical and theoretical foundations in classical AI, providing a clear trajectory of how foundational learning mechanisms have adapted over time.
- **Review Empirical Landscape and Applications:** The authors seek to integrate their classification system with practical applications across various domains, such as software engineering, web navigation, gaming, scientific discovery, embodied AI, robotics, and general computer control.
- **Analyze Evaluation Protocols and Identify Open Problems:** The paper aims to critically analyze current evaluation protocols and benchmarks for self-improving agents, discussing the challenges of measuring and benchmarking continuous improvement, and outlining open problems and future research directions, particularly concerning safety and reliability.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology of this survey involves developing a system-level framework and a unified formalization to systematically categorize and analyze self-improving agents.

**A. Agent Formulation:**
The paper defines a modern foundation-model-based agent at time step *t* as a configuration A_t = (θ_t, Σ_t), where:
- θ_t represents the neural parameters of the foundation model (the cognitive core).
- Σ_t denotes the agent's dynamic operational scaffold, which specifies how the FM is conditioned, grounded, and connected to the external world. Σ_t is further decomposed into (p_t, m_t, T_t, g_t), where p_t are prompts, m_t is memory, T_t is the set of tools, and g_t is control logic.
The agent's behavior is governed by an induced policy π_{θ_t, Σ_t}(A_t | X_t), where X_t is ephemeral execution state.

**B. Formal Definition of Self-Improvement:**
Self-improvement is formalized as a self-induced update operator U that modifies the agent's intrinsic configuration. The process involves an execution phase (E) where the agent generates learning signals (e.g., trajectories, critiques) and an update phase where U applies these signals to modify θ or Σ.
Two primary modes of self-reference are identified:
1. **Foundation Model Improvement:** The agent's policy indirectly induces improvement by generating experience or auxiliary artifacts (learning signals) used by an external optimization procedure to update the model parameters θ_t. This is expressed as θ_{t+1} = U_θ(θ_{1:t}, E(π_{θ_t, Σ_t}; Σ_t, C_t)), with Σ_{t+1} = Σ_t.
2. **Scaffolding Improvement:** The agent's policy directly implements improvements by modifying components of its own operational definition, updating the scaffold Σ_t while keeping θ fixed. This is expressed as Σ_{t+1} = U_Σ(Σ_{1:t}, E(π_{θ_t, Σ_t}; Σ_t, C_t)), with θ_{t+1} = θ_t.
The concept of a "skill" is introduced as a reusable instance of the self-induced update operator, which can be serialized into any of the agent's substrates (tool, instruction, memory entry, consolidated weights, or control logic).

**C. Taxonomy of Approaches:**
The paper's core methodological contribution is its taxonomy, which classifies approaches based on the update target and the form of the self-induced learning signal (S_t):
- **Foundation Model Improvement (Section 5):** Subdivided by signal form:
 - Intrinsic Generative Demonstrations (S_t ≈ D_t): Agent synthesizes training instances.
 - Intrinsic Evaluative Feedback (S_t ≈ e_t): Agent constructs supervisory signals (scores, preferences, critiques).
 - Extrinsic Exploratory Experience (S_t ≈ τ_t): Agent learns from interaction trajectories and environment outcomes, further split into Grounded Task Environments and Simulated Proxy Environments.
- **Scaffolding Improvement (Section 6):** Subdivided by the scaffold component targeted:
 - Prompt Optimization (p_t → p_{t+1}): Edits to prompts or instructions.
 - Memory Evolution (m_t → m_{t+1}): Updates to how experience is stored, consolidated, and retrieved.
 - Tool Governance (T_t → T_{t+1}): Refinement or expansion of the agent's action space through tool creation or selection.
 - Full Scaffolding Update (Σ_t → Σ_{t+1}): Holistic reconfiguration of the agent’s operational architecture, including its own source code.

**D. Survey Structure:**
The remainder of the paper elaborates on these categories:
- Section 2 provides historical context.
- Section 3 defines the agentic system and self-improvement formally.
- Section 4 presents the unified taxonomy.
- Sections 5 and 6 detail each improvement pathway and its subcategories, discussing generation strategies, data formats, trade-offs, and safeguards.
- Section 7 reviews practical applications of these mechanisms across various domains.
- Section 8 discusses evaluation protocols and benchmarks.
- Section 9 presents implications for system design and future directions.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper's main findings are presented through its comprehensive categorization and analysis of existing work, identifying common patterns, trade-offs, challenges, and safeguards across various self-improvement paradigms.

**A. Unified Framework for Self-Improvement:**
The survey successfully unifies diverse self-improvement methods under a common formalism (A_t = (θ_t, Σ_t) and the self-induced update operator U). This framework clearly distinguishes between **Foundation Model Improvement** (parametric updates to θ) and **Scaffolding Improvement** (non-parametric updates to Σ).

**B. Foundation Model Improvement (Section 5):**
- **Intrinsic Generative Demonstrations:** Agents can act as data synthesizers, generating instruction-response pairs, reasoning traces, or multimodal samples. Strategies include bootstrapping, filtering with self-consistency or verifiers, and curriculum learning. Challenges include model collapse, diversity loss, and the need for robust quality control. Safeguards involve retaining benchmark data, using strong checkers, and diversity-aware pooling.
- **Intrinsic Evaluative Feedback:** Agents can self-supervise by generating evaluative feedback (scalar scores, preferences, critiques). This includes rubric-based judging, consistency-based signals (e.g., majority voting, entropy), and corrective feedback (identifying flaws, proposing revisions). Trade-offs exist between flexibility, reliability, and the risk of over-optimizing to biased judges or reinforcing existing errors. Safeguards include separating generator and evaluator, using external anchors, and heterogeneous critique models.
- **Extrinsic Exploratory Experience:** Learning signals are derived from interaction with environments. This occurs either through **Grounded Task Environments** (real, sandboxed, or rule-based settings providing programmatic verifiers, learned reward models, or self-generated tasks) or **Simulated Proxy Environments** (learned world models predicting environment responses). Challenges include sparse rewards, reward hacking, capability regression, hallucinated dynamics in world models, and context window limitations.

**C. Scaffolding Improvement (Section 6):**
- **Prompt Optimization:** This paradigm involves refining system prompts or in-context exemplars. Methods range from **Scalar-Feedback Optimization** (using quantitative metrics) to **Qualitative-Feedback Refinement** (using textual critiques), **Population-Based Evolution** (applying evolutionary algorithms to prompts), and **Textual Gradient Optimization** (using mathematically-inspired directional feedback). The trend is towards richer, more structured learning signals for more targeted updates.
- **Memory Evolution:** Memory is treated as an actively evolving scaffold. The survey decomposes this into:
 - **Memory Objects:** Explicit (processed interaction trails, curated raw content, integrated external knowledge, human-readable) and Implicit (latent embeddings, machine-native). Trade-offs involve interpretability vs. compactness.
 - **Memory Structure:** Flat (temporal), Hierarchical (multi-level abstraction), Graph-Based (nodes and edges encoding relations), and Vector Retrieval (dense embeddings). Each structure offers different trade-offs in scalability, expressiveness, and retrieval latency.
 - **Memory Processing:** Signal-driven operations (Create, Read, Update, Delete) are continuously adjusted based on utility and feedback to manage information flow, prevent noise, and ensure long-term coherence.
- **Tool Governance:** This involves the agent autonomously reasoning about, managing, and creating its tools. It comprises:
 - **Dynamic Tool Routing:** Efficiently selecting, sequencing, and coordinating tools. Paradigms include retrieval- and graph-based routing, policy-learning routing, and proactive/interactive routing (e.g., for clarification or repair).
 - **Iterative Tool Refinement:** Debugging and adapting existing tools using execution feedback, critique specialization, API abstraction, and interface alignment.
 - **Autonomous Tool Creation:** Synthesizing new executable functions when needed, triggered by immediate needs or open-ended curiosity, with emphasis on lifecycle automation and standardized integration protocols.
- **Full Scaffolding Update:** This is the deepest level of intervention, where the agent modifies its entire operational logic or codebase, making the update self-referential. Examples include evolutionary coding agents that rewrite their own algorithms and validate changes on benchmarks, moving towards truly recursive self-improvement within defined safety objectives.

**D. Applications (Section 7):**
The survey details how these self-improvement mechanisms are instantiated in:
- **Software Engineering:** Utilizing compilers, tests, and CI/CD for feedback to update scaffolds (self-modifying code) or fine-tune models.
- **Web Navigation and Automation:** Improving grounding, planning, and error recovery in dynamic web environments through scaffold updates (memory, action policies) or FM fine-tuning.
- **Games and Strategic Reasoning:** Leveraging self-play and outcome feedback for FM improvement (policy optimization) or scaffold improvement (curricula, skill libraries).
- **Scientific Discovery:** Implementing tool-augmented research loops, workflow evolution (scaffolding), and parameter updates from experimental outcomes.
- **Embodied AI and Robotics:** Focusing on open-ended skill acquisition via data flywheels (FM improvement) and curricula/safety logic (scaffolding).
- **General Computer Control:** Acquiring reusable procedures for new applications, with gains from scaffold evolution (memory, planning) or FM updates from interaction traces.

**E. Evaluation (Section 8):**
The paper emphasizes that evaluation of self-improving agents must be a continuous process, tracking performance trajectories, resource efficiency, and stability over time. It differentiates:
- **Metric-Based Measurement:** Using deterministic, executable evaluators for tasks with formal success criteria, requiring reporting of full performance trajectories, transfer capabilities on held-out data, resource costs, and regression rates.
- **Judge-Based Measurement:** Employing parameterized judges (e.g., LLM-as-a-Judge) for open-ended tasks, necessitating transparency about judge identity and rubric, separate judging budgets, and safeguards against over-optimization to judge biases.
- **Benchmarking:** Distinguishes **Mechanism Benchmarks** (isolating specific update channels like memory or prompt refinement) from **Domain Benchmarks** (instantiating improvement under realistic task and feedback constraints).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The significance and potential impact of this survey are multi-faceted, addressing both the conceptual understanding and practical development of self-improving AI.

**A. Conceptual Clarity and Unification:**
By providing a unified formalization and a systematic taxonomy, the survey significantly reduces the terminological and conceptual fragmentation in the field. This clarity is crucial for fostering a shared understanding, facilitating comparison across disparate methods, and accelerating research progress by establishing a common language for discussing architectural components, update targets, and learning signals.

**B. Roadmap for Future Research:**
The comprehensive analysis of current approaches, their trade-offs, and identified challenges naturally culminates in a detailed roadmap for future research directions. These include:
- **Algorithmic Paradigms:** Developing test-time continual adaptation, active exploration driven by curiosity, and more sophisticated parametric distillation and joint optimization methods to bridge fast (scaffold) and slow (FM) improvement loops.
- **Complexity and Robustness:** Addressing resource-constrained improvement dynamics, fostering multi-agent cooperative co-evolution, and ensuring robustness against open-world distribution drift, including architectural shifts towards neural-computer formulations.

**C. Implications for System Design and Safety:**
The survey's discussion on system design highlights critical implications:
- **Balanced Adaptation:** It underscores the need to strategically leverage both fast (scaffold) and slow (parametric) adaptation loops, confining updates to the scaffold when feedback is noisy and deferring parametric consolidation until new behaviors are stable.
- **Governed Criticism:** It reframes the role of critics as governed infrastructure rather than passive benchmarks, emphasizing the need for decoupled evaluators to prevent reward hacking and self-confirming loops.
- **Layered Gating for Safety:** It stresses that self-improvement exacerbates AI alignment challenges, necessitating strict layered gating mechanisms for self-modifications. This means treating agents as untrusted code in protected environments and enforcing continuous auditing and verification to prevent persistent architectural vulnerabilities and ensure safety.

**D. Advancement of AI Autonomy:**
By systematically outlining how modern agents can autonomously inspect, evaluate, and modify their own operational logic, the survey contributes directly to the vision of increasingly autonomous AI systems. It moves beyond stateless AI tools to designing systems capable of continuous self-evolution, expanding the frontiers of machine autonomy in a measurable and controlled manner.

In conclusion, this survey provides a foundational reference for researchers and practitioners in the field of agentic AI, offering both a historical perspective and a forward-looking guide to building robust, safe, and continuously self-improving intelligent systems.
