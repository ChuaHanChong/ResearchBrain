# TapSampling: Inference-Time Sampling with a Task-Progress-Understanding Verifier for Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> TapSampling, an inference-time framework from Harbin Institute of Technology and Tsinghua University, enhances robotic manipulation by employing efficient action sampling via a learned posterior and an interpretable verifier that understands task progress. The system improved average success length on CALVIN benchmarks by up to 0.21, increased average success rates on LIBERO-Long by 1.2%, and achieved a 5.0% average success rate improvement in real-world manipulation tasks.

## Problem

- Non-deterministic generalist robotic policies (e.g., diffusion models, LLMs) often exhibit instability and inconsistent performance under identical environmental conditions.
- The prevalent 'single-shot inference' paradigm commits to a single action at each step, lacking mechanisms for self-correction or recovery from stochastic or incorrect outputs.
- Efficiently generating multiple plausible action candidates and constructing a robust, interpretable verifier for low-level robotic actions remain significant challenges for inference-time sampling strategies.

## Method

- Introduces **TapSampling**, a 'plug-and-play' inference-time framework designed to improve various pretrained generalist robotic policies without requiring fine-tuning.
- Develops an Action-VAE to learn a compressed posterior distribution for actions, enabling efficient generation of multiple diverse and high-quality candidate actions from a small initial set.
- Formulates a task-progress-understanding verifier, trained self-supervisingly on the intrinsic sequential structure of expert trajectories, to predict an action's impact on task completion, facilitating interpretable action selection.

## Results

- TapSampling improved average success length on CALVIN benchmarks from **2.41** to **2.58** (+**0.17**) for Diffusion Policy and from **3.30** to **3.51** (+**0.21**) for OpenVLA.
- The framework increased the average success rate across 10 challenging LIBERO-Long tasks by **1.2%** (from **96.8%** to **98.0%**) and showed a **5.0%** average success rate improvement in real-world manipulation tasks on a Franka robot.
- Learned posterior sampling incurred negligible latency (less than **0.01** s) while providing better fidelity than Gaussian sampling (lower MMD), and the verifier achieved an approximate **12x** speedup compared to a baseline verifier when evaluating 16 candidates.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Inference-time computation serves as an effective complementary axis for improving robotic policy performance, offering benefits beyond solely scaling training data or model size.
> - A learned posterior distribution, modeled by an Action-VAE, can efficiently generate high-fidelity and diverse action candidates that approximate the policy's distribution, addressing the trade-off between speed and fidelity.
> - Task progress can be self-supervisedly extracted from sequential robotic datasets and used as an interpretable metric for verifying actions, distinguishing beneficial from detrimental low-level robot behaviors.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of embodied control has recently witnessed considerable advancements, largely driven by the scaling of training data and model sizes. Non-deterministic generative models, such as diffusion models and autoregressive Large Language Models (LLMs), have been integrated into robotic manipulation systems. These models, often leveraging pretrained foundation models like Video Diffusion Models (VDMs) and Vision-Language Models (VLMs), enable robots to perceive, understand, and generate multimodal information for complex tasks. Efforts involve expanding action vocabularies or incorporating action heads to adapt these models for robotic control. While these policies demonstrate capabilities in various tasks, they frequently exhibit instability, succeeding in some instances and failing in others under identical environmental conditions. This variability is attributed to their non-deterministic nature and the prevalent "single-shot inference" paradigm, where a single action is committed at each decision step, lacking a mechanism for self-correction or recovery from stochastic outputs.

Recognizing these limitations, research in other domains, particularly LLMs and diffusion models, has explored inference-time strategies, such as repeated sampling or verifier-guided selection, to enhance output quality. This paper positions itself within the emerging trend of applying similar inference-time sampling techniques to robotic policies. The primary challenge in adapting these strategies to robotics lies in two areas: (1) efficiently generating multiple plausible action candidates, as direct policy sampling can be computationally expensive and simple Gaussian sampling may disregard critical action correlations, and (2) effectively verifying these candidates. Unlike high-level semantic tasks, low-level robotic actions lack readily available evaluators or semantic metrics, making the construction of a robust verifier challenging. Existing robotic verification methods often produce scores with limited interpretability, are tightly coupled to specific policy architectures, or evaluate progress solely from the current state without modeling the impact of candidate actions. This work aims to address these specific gaps by proposing an inference-time sampling framework tailored for robotic manipulation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the observed instability and performance ceilings of existing non-deterministic generalist robotic policies. Despite their advanced capabilities, models leveraging diffusion or next-token-prediction paradigms exhibit inconsistent behavior, meaning they can succeed or fail under identical environmental conditions. This instability is a direct consequence of the single-shot inference paradigm, where the model outputs a single action without considering alternatives or evaluating its potential outcome. Empirical evidence, such as retry experiments where allowing multiple attempts significantly increases task success, suggests that these policies often generate correct actions alongside incorrect ones, but the system lacks a mechanism to discern and select the optimal action in a single attempt. This motivates the exploration of inference-time computation as a complementary axis to improve performance, rather than solely relying on scaling training data or model size.

Building on this motivation, the key objectives of the TapSampling framework are:

1. **Develop a Policy-Agnostic Inference-Time Framework:** The core objective is to propose a "plug-and-play" framework, TapSampling, that can enhance the performance of various pretrained generalist robotic policies without requiring further fine-tuning of these underlying policies. This emphasizes broad applicability and ease of integration.
2. **Introduce Efficient Action Sampling with a Learned Posterior:** To address the computational cost and fidelity issues of existing sampling methods, the paper aims to develop an Action-VAE. This component is designed to represent actions in a low-dimensional latent space, allowing for the efficient generation of multiple diverse and high-quality candidate actions that closely approximate the true action distribution, building upon a small set of initial policy-generated actions.
3. **Formulate Action Verification through Task Progress Understanding:** A critical objective is to create a semantically grounded verifier for action selection. This verifier is designed to interpret low-level actions by predicting their impact on task progress. It leverages the intrinsic sequential structure inherent in robotic datasets for training, thereby avoiding the need for additional data synthesis or manual annotation. The goal is for this verifier to assign interpretable values to candidate actions, facilitating informed selection.
4. **Enable Interpretable Action Selection:** The framework aims to use the predicted task progress scores for interpretable action selection. By understanding whether an action is likely to advance or impede task completion, the system can discard detrimental actions and combine beneficial ones, leading to more reliable and stable policy execution.
5. **Demonstrate Effectiveness across Diverse Scenarios:** The research seeks to empirically validate that TapSampling can substantially improve multiple generalist policies in both simulated and real-world environments, showcasing its practical utility and broad impact.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

TapSampling operates as a plug-and-play inference-time sampling framework structured around two core components: Efficient Action Sampling with Learned Posterior and Interpretable Action Selection via Task Progress Verification.

#### 3.1. Efficient Action Sampling with Learned Posterior

This component addresses the need for generating multiple action candidates efficiently and with high fidelity.

- **Approximate Posterior Learning (Action-VAE):** The framework introduces an Action-VAE (Variational Autoencoder) with a transformer-based architecture for its encoder and decoder.
 - **Training:** During training, an encoder `E` takes an action chunk `a` as input and compresses it into a latent Gaussian distribution, defined by a mean (μ) and diagonal covariance (diag(σ²)). A low-dimensional latent variable `z` is sampled from this distribution. A decoder `D` then reconstructs the original action `a` from `z`. The Action-VAE is trained with a reconstruction loss (to minimize the difference between original and reconstructed actions) and a Kullback–Leibler (KL) regularization term, which penalizes the divergence between the learned posterior and a standard normal prior. This process encourages the latent space to capture underlying action patterns.
 - **Inference (Latent Space Sampling):** At inference time, a small initial set of actions, `A_π = {a_i}`, is sampled from the pretrained policy `π(a | s, l)` (where `s` is the current state and `l` is the task description).
 1. Each policy action `a_i` is encoded by `E` into its corresponding latent Gaussian distribution `q_E(z | a_i)`.
 2. These individual latent distributions are combined to form a mixed posterior distribution `q_mix(z | A_π)`.
 3. An arbitrary number of new latent samples `z_i` are efficiently drawn from this `q_mix`.
 4. These latent samples are then decoded by `D` into diverse and high-quality candidate actions `A*`.
- **Rationale:** This approach aims to circumvent the high computational cost of repeatedly sampling from the policy directly and the fidelity issues of simpler methods like Gaussian sampling, which may neglect inter-dimensional action correlations. By learning a compressed posterior, the Action-VAE is intended to generate candidates that remain consistent with the original policy's action distribution but with greater diversity and efficiency. The authors note this strategy is primarily for efficiency, offering a favorable trade-off between speed and performance compared to direct policy sampling.

#### 3.2. Action Selection via Task Progress Verification

This component provides a mechanism to evaluate the generated action candidates and select the most appropriate one.

- **Training Data Construction:** A key innovation is the self-supervised construction of training data for the verifier, leveraging the intrinsic sequential structure of expert robotic trajectories `τ = {l, (s_1, a_1)..., (s_t, a_t)}`.
 - **Task Progress Value:** The paper assumes task progress grows linearly over time within a trajectory, assigning a task progress value `p_i = i/t` to each timestep `i`.
 - **Positive Samples:** A positive training sample is formed by `(l, s_i, a_i:i+k-1)`, where `a_i:i+k-1` is an action sequence that results in a positive task progress increment `(p_i+k - p_i) = k/t`.
 - **Negative Samples:** A corresponding negative sample is constructed using a *reversed* action sequence `a_r_i:i-k+1` from an earlier state `s_i-k`, yielding a negative task progress change `(p_i - p_i-k) = -k/t`. This signifies that such an action would impede task completion. The semantic representation of actions (e.g., joint angles, end-effector poses) allows for direct derivation of these reversed actions from the dataset. This data construction avoids manual annotation and is applicable to most existing robotic datasets.
- **Verifier Architecture:** The verifier `V` is built upon a Vision-Language-Action (VLA) architecture, specifically VLA-Adapter, which uses Qwen2.5-0.5B as its backbone for processing visual inputs and text instructions.
 - The VLM backbone extracts shared features from the current state `s` and task instruction `l`.
 - The action head, typically used for action generation, is modified to accept a query action `a` as input and predict a scalar value representing the expected task progress change `Δp`.
 - The model is optimized using an L1 loss function (`L_tap = ||V(s, l, a) - Δp||_1`), treating task-progress prediction as a regression problem.
- **Action Selection in Inference:**
 - The predicted scores from the verifier carry concrete semantic meaning, indicating an action's expected impact on task progress. Negative scores suggest impedance, small positive scores indicate stable execution, and larger positive scores imply rapid progress.
 - During inference, candidate actions (from `A_π` and `A*`) are evaluated by the verifier.
 - Actions with scores below a predefined threshold are discarded.
 - The final action `a*` is computed as a score-weighted average of the remaining, higher-scoring candidates. If no actions remain, the highest-scoring action is chosen.
- **Inference Efficiency:** The verifier is designed for efficiency. The VLM backbone processes the observation-instruction pair only once to obtain shared hidden states. These states are then batched and passed through the lightweight action head to evaluate multiple candidate actions in parallel, minimizing verification latency.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates TapSampling across various simulation benchmarks (CALVIN and LIBERO) and real-world environments, employing a range of representative generalist robotic policies.

#### 4.1. Overall Performance Improvement (Q1)

TapSampling consistently enhanced the performance of diverse non-deterministic policies:

- **CALVIN ABC→D Benchmark:**
 - **Diffusion Policy:** The average success length increased from 2.41 to 2.58 (+0.17). Task success rates improved across all 5 sequential tasks (e.g., Task 1: 82.1% to 83.9%; Task 5: 20.5% to 24.7%).
 - **OpenVLA:** The average success length improved from 3.30 to 3.51 (+0.21). Task success rates increased (e.g., Task 1: 93.4% to 94.5%; Task 5: 42.4% to 48.8%).
 - **VPP:** The average success length rose from 4.39 to 4.46 (+0.07). Task success rates showed smaller but consistent gains, particularly in later tasks (e.g., Task 1: 96.4% to 96.5%; Task 5: 78.3% to 81.1%).
- **LIBERO-Long Benchmark:**
 - **π_0.5:** The average success rate across 10 challenging tasks improved from 96.8% to 98.0% (+1.2%). Significant improvements were observed in specific tasks (e.g., Task 4: 96.0% to 100.0%, +4.0%; Task 5: 96.0% to 100.0%, +4.0%).
- **Real-World Experiments (Franka Research 3):**
 - **π_0:** Across three categories of manipulation tasks (Knock Down, Pick and Place, Stack), TapSampling improved the average success rate from 78.3% to 83.3% (+5.0%). Notably, it showed greater improvements in "unseen" scenarios and allowed for more precise manipulation (e.g., Stack unseen: 75.0% to 85.0%, +10.0%).

These results indicate that TapSampling is effective in improving manipulation capabilities across different environments, tasks, and policy architectures in a plug-and-play manner.

#### 4.2. Sampling Efficiency and Fidelity (Q2)

Analysis of sampling strategies revealed trade-offs between latency and performance:

- **Latency of Action Sampling:** Direct "Policy Sampling" (sampling many actions from the policy) significantly increased latency (approximately 8x for 16 samples). In contrast, "Gaussian Sampling" and "Learned Posterior Sampling" (TapSampling's method) incurred negligible additional latency (less than 0.01 s) after constructing a distribution from a small set (n=4) of initial policy actions.
- **Sampling Fidelity and Performance:**
 - **Policy Sampling** achieved the highest performance gains, but at a substantial increase in inference latency (approximately 20x the baseline).
 - **Gaussian Sampling** provided limited performance improvements, attributed to its inability to capture inter-dimensional correlations, leading to samples that deviated from the true action distribution.
 - **Learned Posterior Sampling** demonstrated a balance between efficiency and effectiveness. It achieved better performance than Gaussian Sampling (e.g., +0.07 average success length for VPP vs. +0.04 for Gaussian Sampling) while maintaining low latency comparable to Gaussian Sampling.
- **Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) Comparison:** Quantitative assessment showed that Learned Posterior Sampling consistently yielded lower MMD values compared to Gaussian Sampling across multiple kernel bandwidths, indicating a closer statistical alignment with the reference policy action distribution.

#### 4.3. Verification Efficiency and Effectiveness (Q3)

The verifier component of TapSampling also underwent analysis regarding its efficiency and impact on task completion:

- **Latency of Action Verification:** TapSampling's verifier demonstrated higher efficiency compared to a baseline verifier (RoboMonkey). For evaluating 16 candidates, TapSampling achieved an approximate 12x speedup. This is attributed to its architecture, where the VLM backbone is executed only once, and multiple candidate actions are evaluated in parallel through a lightweight action head.
- **Effectiveness of Task Progress Verification:**
 - The verifier's predictions correlated with task outcomes. When high-scoring actions (indicating positive task progress) were selected, tasks were completed in fewer steps.
 - Conversely, selecting low-scoring actions (indicating negative task progress) substantially increased the number of steps required for task completion or led to task failure.
 - Visual examples illustrated that low-scoring actions resulted in incorrect collisions or scene disruptions, while high-scoring actions led to successful task execution.
- **Generalization to Out-of-Distribution Actions:** The verifier, trained on CALVIN ABC, showed a degree of generalization when evaluated on unseen environment D. Forward expert actions received the highest average task progress scores, reversed actions received the lowest negative scores, and randomly generated or gripper-corrupted actions yielded scores close to zero, demonstrating its ability to distinguish between beneficial and detrimental actions even for OOD inputs.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies

- **Number of Samples:** Increasing the number of samples supplied to the verifier generally led to improved average success length, with diminishing returns beyond a certain point.
- **Latent Dimension:** For the Action-VAE, compressing action chunks (10x7 dimensions) into a 24-dimensional latent space yielded a good balance, maintaining low reconstruction error while achieving sufficient compression.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The TapSampling framework presents a significant contribution to the field of robotic manipulation, primarily by offering a practical and generalizable method to enhance the reliability and performance of existing robotic policies. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Addressing Policy Instability:** The work directly addresses the inherent instability observed in non-deterministic generative policies when operating under a single-shot inference paradigm. By providing a mechanism for multi-sample generation and intelligent verification, TapSampling mitigates failures caused by stochastic variance, leading to more robust policy execution. This is particularly crucial for real-world robotic deployments where single errors can lead to costly failures and environment resets are often unavailable.
- **Policy-Agnostic "Plug-and-Play" Enhancement:** A key strength of TapSampling is its design as a policy-agnostic framework. It improves the performance of various pretrained generalist policies (e.g., Diffusion Policy, OpenVLA, VPP, π_0.5) without requiring any architectural modifications or fine-tuning of the base policies themselves. This "plug-and-play" capability makes it broadly applicable and reduces the development overhead for integrating robustness improvements into diverse robotic systems.
- **Efficient and High-Fidelity Action Sampling:** The introduction of the Action-VAE for learned posterior sampling provides a novel approach to generate a diverse set of high-quality action candidates efficiently. This method strikes a favorable balance between computational cost (compared to direct policy sampling) and fidelity to the underlying action distribution (compared to simpler Gaussian sampling), offering a practical solution for inference-time candidate generation.
- **Interpretable and Self-Supervised Action Verification:** The task-progress-understanding verifier is a notable advancement. By framing action verification as an outcome prediction problem, it provides interpretable scores that explicitly indicate an action's expected impact on task progress. Crucially, the verifier is trained using the intrinsic sequential structure of expert trajectories, eliminating the need for manual annotations or external reward functions, which can be expensive and difficult to obtain. This self-supervised approach enhances scalability and applicability.
- **Improved Performance Across Diverse Scenarios:** The extensive experimental validation in both simulated environments (CALVIN, LIBERO) and real-world settings demonstrates consistent performance gains across various task complexities and policy architectures. This empirical evidence underscores the framework's practical utility and its potential to improve long-horizon manipulation capabilities and precision in robotic control.
- **Shift in Computational Strategy:** The work champions inference-time computation as a viable and effective complementary axis for improving robotic policy performance. This paradigm shift suggests that intelligent allocation of computational resources during deployment can yield substantial benefits, offering an alternative or supplement to solely scaling training data or model size.

In summary, TapSampling contributes a comprehensive, efficient, and interpretable framework for enhancing generalist robotic manipulation policies. Its ability to improve robustness and success rates without modifying or fine-tuning existing policies, coupled with its innovative sampling and verification mechanisms, holds significant promise for advancing the deployment of more reliable and capable robots in complex, unstructured environments.
