# Rapid On-Robot Learning for Dynamic Manipulation Skills: Robot Juggling

> [!summary] Summary
> An online learning framework lets a bimanual robot with multi-fingered hands and onboard vision acquire five three-ball juggling patterns directly on hardware within minutes. A regularized memory-based learner blends accumulated real-world experience with the gradients of a badly inaccurate prior model, while a precomputed mutually reachable set keeps successive throws and catches inside joint and actuator limits. Cascade juggling is learned in 53 seconds of physical interaction.

## Problem

- Prior juggling systems used paddles or cups to make contact predictable; multi-fingered hands add friction, compliance, impact and release-timing effects that models cannot capture accurately.
- Existing on-robot juggling learners need 40 to 500 attempts, or a dedicated random exploration phase, before command updates become informative enough to improve behavior.
- Satisfying instantaneous joint position, velocity and acceleration limits does not guarantee a throw leaves the arm able to execute the next catch.

## Method

- **Regularized Memory-Based Learner (RMBL)** fits an ==RBF-weighted local linear regression== over k-nearest memory tuples, regularizing its Jacobians toward the prior model's first-order derivatives with coefficient gamma.
- Command update solves a ==Levenberg-Marquardt== style regularized inversion of the fitted local Jacobian, penalizing deviation from the kernel-weighted reference command to avoid unstable adaptation steps.
- **Mutually Reachable Set (MRS)**, built offline from B-spline forward and backward reachable sets, enters the task-level planner as a ==polytope constraint== guaranteeing bidirectionally feasible skill transitions.

## Results

- Cascade learned in **53 seconds** of interaction, reaching five cycles by the eighth attempt in all **five** from-scratch trials, roughly five minutes wall-clock including resets.
- Retrospective ablation over **7,578** planner queries: without the reachable-set constraint **99.1%** of solutions fall outside it and **89.0%** are unreachable or non-viable; with it, **100%** are safe.
- Skill reuse gives the tennis, half-shower, cascade sequence in **75 seconds**, later patterns needing only **2 to 3** extra runs; shower and box plateau below five cycles.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A prior's value lies in its local gradients, not its accuracy: a model failing every zero-shot cycle still bootstraps learning without any exploration phase.
> - Conditioning reusable skills on observed state lets experience transfer across patterns, whereas open-loop per-throw corrections would force relearning separate skills for each pattern.
> - Safety for dynamic motion must constrain composition, not instantaneous limits; sensing latency exceeding flight time still caps precision no amount of adaptation recovers.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Broader Research Landscape

This work is positioned within the field of robotic manipulation, specifically addressing the "sim2real gap," which describes the discrepancies encountered when transferring robot behaviors from simulated or model-based environments to physical reality. For high-performance dynamic manipulation, such as juggling, even minor mismatches can significantly impair task execution, particularly when motions are fast, contacts are intermittent, and precise spatiotemporal coordination is required.

Historically, robotic juggling has served as a benchmark for dynamic manipulation. Early systems demonstrated robust juggling through meticulously designed mechanics (open-loop) or continuous visual feedback for stabilization (closed-loop). Many of these systems relied on specialized end-effectors, like paddles or cups, which simplify object interaction and reduce uncertainty during contact. However, employing multi-fingered hands, as in this research, introduces complex contact dynamics involving friction, compliance, impact, and precise release timing, which are challenging to model accurately.

On-robot learning has been explored in juggling, with approaches including:
- Iterative learning at the task level for improving open-loop throwing.
- Memory-based, nonparametric methods that retain and reuse physical experience to enhance closed-loop behavior, a principle that forms a foundation for the current study.
- Reinforcement learning techniques applied to juggling tasks.
- More recently, a closely related method by Ploeger and Peters utilized a local model regularized by an analytical prior. The current work distinguishes itself by applying this learning principle to reusable skills whose commands are conditioned on observed states, enabling continuous online learning during ongoing juggling, as opposed to episodic updates of open-loop sequences.

This research aims to advance the capabilities of multi-fingered robots in dynamic manipulation by developing a framework that overcomes the sim2real gap. It does so by enabling rapid and safe on-robot learning that *builds upon* imperfect prior knowledge rather than requiring an accurate initial model or discarding prior information as experience accumulates. The work seeks to bridge the gap between predictable interactions facilitated by specialized end-effectors and the complexities of general manipulation with multi-fingered hands.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations for this research are articulated as follows:

- **Overcoming the Sim2Real Gap:** The central motivation is to address the difficulty of deploying robot behaviors from simulation or analytical models to the physical world, especially for high-performance dynamic manipulation where subtle real-world deviations can cause significant failures.
- **Enabling Rapid On-Robot Learning:** The research seeks to develop an online learning framework that allows a bimanual robot to acquire complex manipulation skills, specifically diverse juggling patterns, directly on physical hardware within a short timeframe (i.e., minutes).
- **Leveraging Imperfect Prior Knowledge:** A core philosophy is that an imperfect prior model, even one incapable of successful zero-shot execution, can still provide valuable guidance for learning. The objective is to design a system that continually refines its behavior by building upon this imperfect prior knowledge rather than attempting to replace it entirely.
- **Efficient Use of Physical Experience:** Given the time-consuming and potentially risky nature of real-world robot interactions, a key motivation is to enable efficient learning from each new experience. This involves avoiding extensive, uninformed exploration across a vast space of possible behaviors.
- **Ensuring Safety During Continual Learning:** For dynamic, high-performance behaviors, conventional safety restrictions (e.g., limits on workspace or speed) can preclude task execution. The research aims to implement robust safety mechanisms that permit the robot to repeatedly practice and improve complex motions near its physical limits without risking damage or violating kinodynamic constraints.
- **Facilitating Skill Reusability and Composition:** The work is motivated by enabling the learning and composition of multiple distinct, long-horizon juggling patterns (e.g., cascade, tennis, half-shower, shower, and box). Decomposing the task into reusable skills allows experience gained from one pattern to be transferred and adapted for others.
- **Adapting to Real-World Variability:** Observations during development indicated that subtle, unforeseen changes in the physical environment or robot (e.g., dust, humidity, wear) could degrade previously learned behaviors. A motivation is to engineer systems capable of continually adapting to such changes to maintain robust performance over extended operational periods.
- **Addressing Challenges of Multi-Fingered Manipulation:** The research specifically targets dynamic manipulation with multi-fingered hands, which inherently involves complex contact dynamics that are difficult to model. This choice of hardware provides a challenging testbed for the proposed learning and safety framework.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a hierarchical, skill-based system architecture to enable rapid, safe, and online learning of dynamic manipulation skills on a bimanual robot platform.

**Robot Platform:** The experimental platform is AthenaZero, a custom-built, low-inertia bimanual manipulator developed at the RAI Institute. Each arm is equipped with a three-fingered gripper, where each underactuated finger features two degrees of freedom and is driven by a remote actuator via Bowden cables, allowing for a full open/close cycle in approximately 80 ms. The juggling objects are commercially available soft, deformable beanbags, weighing 130g and measuring 63.5mm in diameter.

**Perception System:**
A head-mounted sensor suite, comprising a Lucid Helios2 Wide Time-of-Flight camera for depth and a Lucid Triton RGB camera, operates synchronously at 30 Hz. The total latency from physical scene capture to 3-DoF tracked ball position is approximately 0.1 seconds. The perception pipeline is designed for object-agnostic tracking:
1. **Instance Segmentation:** The Efficient Track Anything Model (EfficientTAM) is utilized. It is initialized by user-selected point prompts on a few example images of the target objects, allowing for segmentation without task-specific data collection or retraining.
2. **3D Point Cloud Segmentation:** Segmented instance masks are used to extract the 3D point cloud of each object from the depth camera data.
3. **Pose Estimation:** Fast Generalized Iterative Closest Point (FastGICP) performs scan-to-model registration, aligning a standard CAD model (e.g., a sphere) to the observed point cloud to estimate the object's 3D position.
4. **Ballistic Trajectory Prediction:** Consecutive 3D positions gathered during a ball's flight phase are aggregated. A parabolic trajectory, assuming unperturbed ballistic flight under gravity, is fitted to these observations to predict the ball's position at a future, orchestrator-specified absolute time. This prediction serves as the target catching state for the control system.

**Control System Architecture (Figure 1):**
The system architecture is hierarchical and skill-based, implemented within ROS2 Humble, with core blocks operating as independent ROS nodes to support asynchronous data flows and multi-rate execution.
- **Orchestrator:** The highest-level component, which asynchronously initiates a predefined sequence of juggling "skills" to the robot's arms, adhering to a global wall-clock schedule. It manages the temporal phase of the juggling pattern and monitors for conditions that would terminate a juggling attempt (e.g., inability to detect a ball, infeasible planned motion).
- **Skill Executor:** Receives skill descriptions (including Type, Object ID, Task Identifier, and Duration) from the Orchestrator and decodes the desired outcome (`y_d`).
- **Regularized Memory-Based Learner (RMBL):** This is a core contribution.
 - **Inputs:** The current state `x` (comprising the commanded catch position for the preceding ball `p_catch-` and the latest observed landing position `p_land-`), the desired outcome `y_d` (target landing position for the current throw), and an accumulated experience memory `D = {(x_i, u_i, y_i)}`.
 - **Prior Model (`f_0`):** For the throwing skill, an identity mapping `f_0(x, u) = u` is used as the prior, meaning the commanded landing position (`u`) is assumed to be the actual outcome (`y`). Even though this prior is initially inaccurate, its first-order gradients (`∂f0/∂u = I`, `∂f0/∂x = 0`) are retained as useful guidance.
 - **Memory Integration:** The learner retrieves `k`-nearest neighbors from `D` in the joint `(x, y)` space and assigns weights `w_i` using a localized Radial Basis Function (RBF) kernel. A nominal reference task command (`u_bar`) is computed as a weighted average of these neighbors.
 - **Local Model Fitting and Regularization:** A local linear model (with Jacobians `C, D` and offset `d`) is fitted to the weighted `k`-NN experiences. This fitting is regularized towards the prior model's derivatives and offset, controlled by a regularization hyperparameter `γ`. This regularization provides stability and guides extrapolation when local experience is sparse.
 - **Command Output:** The optimal task-level command `u*` is computed by solving a regularized quadratic optimization problem. This problem penalizes large adaptation steps (similar to a Levenberg-Marquardt update), ensuring stable and controlled adaptation.
 - **Online Adaptation:** Each throw's observed outcome (`y`) is immediately stored as an experience tuple `(x_i, u_i, y_i)` in `D`, making it available to influence subsequent throws and facilitating real-time learning.
- **Task-Level Planner:** Translates the `u*` command into a target joint transition state `s_f = (q_f, q_dot_f, q_ddot_f)`. This involves solving a nonlinear constrained optimization problem that defines the task-space conditions for throwing or catching.
 - **Mutually Reachable Set (MRS) Constraint:** A critical safety mechanism. The planner's solutions for `s_f` are strictly constrained to a precomputed convex polytope `M_S = {s | Ms <= m}`. The MRS is defined as a set of transition states within which any two states can be connected in either direction by a feasible jerk-constrained trajectory, respecting the robot's physical limits (position, velocity, acceleration, jerk). This set is constructed offline using B-splines and polyhedral projections as a conservative inner approximation of the true maximally mutually reachable set. Its purpose is to prevent the robot from commanding states from which it cannot safely recover or execute subsequent actions.
- **Joint-Level Trajectory Generator:** Utilizes Ruckig, a jerk-limited minimum-duration trajectory generation algorithm. It computes trajectories between the current joint state and the target `s_f`, adhering to all kinodynamic limits. If the target cannot be reached within the scheduled duration, Ruckig computes the fastest feasible trajectory, introducing a timing error.

**Skill Decomposition and Reusability:**
The continuous multi-ball juggling task is decomposed into discrete, reusable "throw" and "catch" skills. This structure enables the transfer of learned experience. For instance, experience acquired while learning the tennis pattern can be reused for cascade and half-shower patterns that share similar underlying skills, with the learner adapting to new state distributions (`x`).

**Handling Sensorimotor Latency:**
For juggling patterns like shower and box, which involve short flight times for side throws, the 0.1-second sensorimotor and visual processing latency makes real-time catch correction infeasible. In these scenarios, catches operate open-loop. However, the learner still measures the observed landing position of the thrown ball, and this information is utilized to improve the *precision* of subsequent side throws, progressively increasing the accuracy for the open-loop catches.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study yielded several key findings demonstrating the efficacy and characteristics of the proposed online learning framework for dynamic manipulation.

- **Rapid On-Robot Skill Acquisition:** The bimanual AthenaZero robot successfully learned to continuously juggle five distinct three-ball patterns—cascade, tennis, half-shower, shower, and box—within minutes of real-world interaction.
 - Cascade juggling was acquired in an average of 53 seconds of physical interaction, requiring approximately 7 manual resets across five independent trials to consistently achieve five consecutive juggling cycles.
 - Learning a continuous sequence transitioning through tennis, half-shower, and cascade patterns was achieved in an average of 75 seconds of physical interaction.
 - For the more challenging shower and box patterns, performance reached its observed plateau after approximately 30 and 60 seconds of physical interaction, respectively.
- **Demonstrated Learning Efficacy:** Visualizations of 3D ball trajectories and landing distributions (Figure 3) illustrated the rapid improvement in throwing accuracy. Initially, ball landing locations were significantly dispersed, reflecting the inaccuracy of the prior model. With accumulating physical experience, these distributions became tightly clustered around the designated targets, demonstrating sample-efficient adaptation.
- **Utility of Imperfect Prior Knowledge:** The analytical prior model used was demonstrably inaccurate, unable to sustain even a single juggling cycle without adaptation. Nevertheless, the research found that this imperfect prior provided crucial first-order information (gradients) that guided the learning process from its inception. This allowed the robot to begin improving its behavior immediately, without requiring a dedicated initial exploration phase, which is often necessary for memory-based learning without informative priors.
- **Impact of Prior Regularization:** Experiments investigating the regularization factor `γ` (Figure 4) highlighted its critical role in balancing reliance on the prior model versus real-world experience. Excessive regularization (larger `γ`) significantly slowed adaptation, while insufficient regularization (smaller `γ`) led to more variable and inconsistent learning outcomes, making the system susceptible to sparse or noisy observations. A `γ` value of 0.001 was identified as providing an effective balance for the experiments.
- **Reusability of Learned Skills:** The decomposition of juggling into reusable skills facilitated efficient knowledge transfer. During continuous learning across tennis, half-shower, and cascade patterns, the robot rapidly achieved proficiency in the subsequent patterns (within 2-3 additional runs). This indicates that experience acquired for shared underlying skills could be reused, with the learner adapting to shifts in the state (`x`) distribution, validating the benefit of a closed-loop stateful policy over open-loop mappings.
- **Limitations with High Precision Demands (Shower and Box):** For the shower and box patterns, which involve short flight times that necessitate open-loop catching due to sensorimotor latency, the learning system successfully converged the throwing precision. Ball landing distributions became highly concentrated. However, these patterns did not consistently achieve the same level of deterministic, zero-failure convergence across all trials (e.g., completing five consecutive cycles) as the cascade, half-shower, or tennis patterns (Figures 2C,D). This suggests that the purely open-loop catching policy may impose a robustness limitation for these more complex, precision-demanding behaviors.
- **Effectiveness of Mutually Reachable Set (MRS) for Safety:**
 - A retrospective ablation study (Table 3) confirmed the practical relevance and necessity of the MRS constraint. Without the MRS, 99.1% of the task-level planner solutions retrospectively computed were found to lie outside the precomputed safe region.
 - A high proportion, 89.0%, of these unconstrained solutions were classified as unsafe (either unreachable from the preceding transition state, non-viable for subsequent motion, or both).
 - In contrast, all solutions generated with the MRS constraint remained within the safe set and were consequently 100% reachable and viable by construction.
 - The dominant failure modes varied across patterns: cascade and continuous tennis–half-shower–cascade primarily exhibited "reachable, not viable" issues, while the box pattern largely showed "viable, not reachable" failures. The MRS effectively prevented the robot from attempting these unsafe transitions (Figure 5).
 - The MRS is a conservative inner approximation; some states outside it might still be safe for particular transitions, though for most behaviors, the MRS boundary closely separated safe and unsafe solutions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework with several significant implications for the development and deployment of robotic systems capable of dynamic manipulation:

- **Enabling Practical On-Robot Learning:** The work demonstrates a practical and efficient approach for robots to acquire complex, dynamic manipulation skills directly on physical hardware within a short operational timeframe. This capability reduces the reliance on extensive simulation tuning or perfect analytical models, accelerating the development and deployment of new robotic behaviors.
- **Effective Utilization of Imperfect Prior Knowledge:** The finding that even an initially inaccurate prior model can effectively guide learning is a key contribution. This philosophy suggests that researchers can leverage existing, imperfect models as powerful starting points for adaptation, significantly reducing the burden of achieving perfect initial modeling and making real-world learning more accessible.
- **Enhanced Safety for Dynamic Tasks:** The introduction and validation of the Mutually Reachable Set (MRS) provide a robust mechanism for ensuring kinodynamic safety during online learning of high-performance, dynamic behaviors. This allows robots to operate closer to their physical limits without risking damage, which is crucial for tasks requiring agile and energetic motions, thereby expanding the envelope of safe robotic operation.
- **Improved Sample Efficiency through Skill Reusability:** The decomposition of complex tasks into reusable skills, combined with memory-based learning that adapts to changes in observed states, enhances sample efficiency. This approach facilitates the transfer of learned experience across different, related tasks or variations, reducing the learning time for new competencies and contributing to more generalized robotic intelligence.
- **Addressing Real-World Variability:** The observed ability of the system to adapt to subtle, unmodeled changes in physical conditions (e.g., effects of dust, humidity, or minor wear-and-tear on contacts) highlights its potential for maintaining robust performance over extended periods in dynamic and unpredictable real-world environments.
- **Guidance for Future Robot Design and Control:** The research offers insights into the synergistic relationship between mechanical design, low-level control strategies, and task-level learning. It suggests that designing low-level behaviors that produce repeatable outcomes can significantly facilitate faster and easier adaptation at the task level, influencing future directions in robot system engineering.
- **Foundation for More Dexterous and Complex Manipulation:** The framework establishes a foundation for extending to more advanced and dexterous manipulation behaviors, such as club juggling (which necessitates control over both position and orientation) or other contact-rich tasks. It also points towards integrating goal-conditioned reinforcement learning policies for low-level behaviors with high-level memory-based learners, and addressing continuous adaptation across longer sequences of skills.
