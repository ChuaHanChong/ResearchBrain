# From Intention to Execution: Probing the Generalization Boundaries of Vision-Language-Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> NYU researchers develop INT-ACT, a comprehensive 50-task simulation benchmark for systematically evaluating Vision-Language-Action model generalization, revealing a persistent "intention-action gap" where state-of-the-art VLA models like π0 and SpatialVLA achieve 80-100% intention correctness (understanding what to do) but suffer dramatic task success drops under distribution shifts, with additional findings that VLA fine-tuning erodes the sophisticated linguistic capabilities of underlying VLM backbones even when the base VLM (PaliGemma) demonstrates robust understanding of complex instructions before robotics training, demonstrating through evaluation across object diversity, language complexity, and vision-language thinking categories that current approaches fail to reliably translate high-level semantic understanding into precise motor execution especially under multimodal ambiguity where linguistic priors can override correct visual grounding.

## Problem

- Lack of comprehensive, standardized benchmarks for evaluating VLA model generalization beyond simple task success rates, leaving no systematic understanding of where and why VLA models fail under distribution shifts
- Unclear how much VLM pre-training actually contributes to robotic policy generalization capabilities
- Real-world robot evaluation barriers limit reproducible research across institutions

## Method

- ==Develop INT-ACT==, an open-sourced benchmark with 50 tailored tasks across 10 subcategories testing object diversity, language complexity, and vision-language thinking, to enable reproducible VLA research
- Introduce "Intention Correct Rate" metric to decouple high-level understanding from low-level execution
- Systematically evaluate four prominent VLA architectures (π0, SpatialVLA, Magma, Octo) using consistent protocols

## Results

- Most VLA models achieve 80-**100%** intention correctness but dramatic task success drops under distribution shifts; π0-scratch performed best overall but still showed significant intention-action gaps across all generalization categories
- VLA models lose linguistic robustness compared to their VLM backbones, with performance drops across language variation tasks
- Multimodal ambiguity scenarios cause systematic misbehavior with increased wrong object attempt rates, especially for π0-finetune

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - VLA models exhibit a persistent "intention-action gap" where semantic understanding doesn't translate to reliable execution, with performance degradation under multimodal ambiguity revealing a brittle coupling between perception and action systems
> - VLA fine-tuning erodes sophisticated linguistic capabilities present in base VLM backbones through catastrophic forgetting
> - Object identity impacts grasping success more than out-of-distribution status, suggesting dataset-specific biases

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated at the intersection of large Vision-Language Models (VLMs) and robotics, specifically focusing on Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. The broader landscape is characterized by a paradigm shift towards "foundation models" – large, pre-trained architectures designed for broad applicability across numerous downstream tasks. This trend, originating in Natural Language Processing (NLP) with models like BERT and GPT, and extending to computer vision with models like CLIP and DINOv2, has recently spurred the development of "Robotic Foundation Models." These models, trained on large, diverse robotics datasets (e.g., Open-X Embodiment), aim to equip embodied agents with generalist capabilities, moving away from task-specific, specialized models.

Early Robotic Foundation Models, such as RT-1, Octo, and RDT, typically leveraged separate pre-trained encoders for language (e.g., T5) and vision (e.g., SigLIP) to inform imitation learning policies. However, a more recent wave, including models like RT-2, OpenVLA, SpatialVLA, and π0, directly integrates powerful pre-trained VLMs, aiming to transfer their broad generalization abilities from web-scale data to robotic control. These VLA models represent the state-of-the-art in pursuing versatile, "generalist" robot policies capable of interpreting natural language instructions and performing complex manipulations in diverse environments, often in few- or zero-shot settings.

Despite this rapid progress, a significant gap exists in the systematic evaluation of VLA models' generalization capabilities. Traditional robotics benchmarks (e.g., RLBench, RoboMimic) primarily focus on low-level control and lack language instructions, making them unsuitable for VLA evaluation. Newer benchmarks like CALVIN and LIBERO incorporate language but offer limited task variety, failing to deeply probe generalization. SimplerEnv made strides in bridging the sim2real gap but is limited by a small set of tasks. While real-world VLA evaluations (e.g., by the π0 and OpenVLA teams) offer rich testing environments, their high time and monetary costs create barriers to reproducibility and accessibility for the broader research community.

This paper directly addresses this critical evaluation deficit. By introducing INT-ACT, a comprehensive, simulation-based probing suite that significantly extends SimplerEnv, the authors provide a much-needed standardized tool to systematically measure VLA generalization. This work aims to clarify how much VLM pre-training truly contributes to the generalization of downstream robotic policies, scrutinizing whether the impressive perceptual and linguistic understanding of VLMs reliably translates into robust physical action in robotics.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to **systematically measure and understand the generalization capabilities of state-of-the-art Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models**, particularly focusing on how effectively they leverage the inherent broad generalization of their underlying Vision-Language Model (VLM) backbones.

The motivation stems from several critical gaps and unfulfilled promises in the current landscape of VLA research:
1. **Insufficient Evaluation Benchmarks:** Current robotics benchmarks are either designed for traditional imitation learning without language support, or, if they include language, they offer a limited variety of tasks that do not adequately stress-test the generalization potential that VLMs are hypothesized to bring to robotics.
2. **Lack of Clarity on VLM Contribution:** There is an open question regarding how much VLM pre-training genuinely contributes to the generalization capabilities of downstream robotic policies. The assumption is that VLMs provide a strong foundation for generalist robots, but systematic empirical evidence is lacking.
3. **Barriers to Reproducibility and Accessibility:** Real-world robot setups, while ideal for evaluation, are prohibitively expensive and time-consuming, hindering systematic and reproducible studies across different research institutions.

To address these motivations, the authors set forth two main contributions:
- **Introduction and Open-Sourcing of INT-ACT:** To fill the void of comprehensive VLA generalization benchmarks, the paper introduces INT-ACT (Intention-Action), a unified probing suite. This suite comprises 50 simulation-based tasks across 10 subcategories, meticulously designed to evaluate VLA models along three critical dimensions: object diversity, language complexity, and vision-language thinking. By open-sourcing this suite, the authors aim to establish a new standard for VLA evaluation, promoting accessibility and reproducibility in the field.
- **Extensive Benchmarking and Uncovering Failure Modes:** Through systematic evaluation of prominent VLA architectures (π0, SpatialVLA, Magma, Octo) on the INT-ACT suite, the research aims to uncover and characterize key failure modes. Specifically, they seek to identify whether strong semantic understanding translates into precise motor execution and how multimodal generalization holds up under various distribution shifts. This diagnostic approach is crucial for guiding future architectural designs and training strategies for VLA models.

In essence, the research is motivated by the desire to move beyond anecdotal successes of VLA models to a rigorous, principled understanding of their current limitations, especially concerning their ability to generalize from "intention" (high-level understanding) to "execution" (precise robotic action).

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of this research's methodology lies in the design and application of **INT-ACT**, a novel and comprehensive VLA generalization probing suite.

#### 3.1. Testbed Choice

The authors chose to build INT-ACT entirely in **simulation** using the **SimplerEnv benchmark**, which is built on the **ManiSkill2 simulator**. This choice was driven by the desire to minimize deployment barriers and maximize accessibility. SimplerEnv is particularly notable because it is designed to closely match model performance in the real world, mitigating some of the traditional sim2real gap concerns of other simulation environments. The original SimplerEnv had only 4 tasks per dataset; INT-ACT significantly expands this to **50 tailored tasks for the BridgeV2 dataset**, substantially enhancing its generalization testing potential.

#### 3.2. Design Principles for Generalization Probing

INT-ACT's tasks are systematically organized into three major categories to probe different facets of VLA generalization:

- **Object Diversity:** This category assesses VLA policies' ability to generalize to objects unseen during their fine-tuning (Out-Of-Distribution, OOD).
 - **OOD Objects:** Incorporates household items from other benchmarks (e.g., LIBERO) and entirely new domains (e.g., industrial tools) that are visually dissimilar to objects in the BridgeV2 fine-tuning dataset. Tasks are of the `PUT {Source} ON {Target}` format, with variations where the source, target, or both objects are OOD.
 - **OOD Relations:** Introduces novel relationships between objects already seen in the BridgeV2 dataset (e.g., "put a carrot on a sponge") to test memorization vs. genuine relational understanding.

- **Language Complexity:** This category evaluates how well VLAs retain the sophisticated linguistic understanding of their underlying VLMs when faced with non-templated or nuanced instructions.
 - **Language Action:** Paraphrases action verbs to be compositional and less frequent in the BridgeV2 dataset.
 - **Language Negation:** Adds negative qualifiers (e.g., "not," "don't") to irrelevant objects, requiring precise semantic understanding.
 - **Language Appearance:** Replaces object names with descriptive words (e.g., "a purple object" instead of "an eggplant"), challenging visual-language grounding.
 - **Commonsense:** Introduces instructions requiring commonsense reasoning (e.g., "put rabbit's favorite vegetable on the plate").

- **Vision-Language Thinking:** This category probes the robustness of VLAs in complex visual environments and their ability to resolve ambiguity.
 - **Object Distractors:** Adds irrelevant objects to scenes to simulate clutter and occlusion, testing the model's ability to focus on salient information.
 - **Semantically Challenging Distractors:** Introduces distractors that require commonsense reasoning to disambiguate (e.g., an actual orange fruit when instructed to pick up an "orange juice box"), testing fine-grained visual-language binding.
 - The authors acknowledge that SimplerEnv's green-screen compositing limits the ability to vary lighting, texture, and camera pose. While recent VLA models are relatively robust to lighting/background, camera pose variation remains a known challenge from prior work.

#### 3.3. Model Selection and Training Protocol

The study evaluates four prominent VLA models representing diverse architectural paradigms:
- **π0:** A diffusion/flow-based model, evaluated in two variants: `π0-finetune` (from released checkpoint) and `π0-scratch` (trained from scratch using its VLM backbone PaliGemma and BridgeV2 data).
- **SpatialVLA:** A leading autoregressive VLA model, used directly from an official BridgeV2-finetuned checkpoint.
- **Magma:** An autoregressive model that employs a co-training regime with vision-language data, evaluated in a zero-shot configuration.
- **Octo:** A large multi-task imitation learning baseline, evaluated in zero-shot mode for its Small (27M parameters) and Base (93M parameters) variants, as zero-shot performance showed slightly better results in BridgeV2 scenes compared to fine-tuned variants.

All fine-tuning experiments for models requiring it were conducted on the BridgeV2 dataset, consistent with SimplerEnv's environment, adhering to the respective papers' training protocols for fair comparison.

#### 3.4. Evaluation Procedure

For each of the 50 tasks, each model was evaluated across 24 distinct episodes, covering all possible scene and object configurations predefined by ManiSkill2. Each configuration was repeated across 3 random seeds. All reported metrics were averaged across these episodes and seeds for robust statistical analysis.

#### 3.5. Metrics

To provide a fine-grained understanding of policy behavior, the study reports three primary metrics:
- **Grasp Success Rate:** Binary indicator of whether the robot's gripper successfully grasps the correct source object at any point during the episode (natively supported by SimplerEnv).
- **Intention Correct Rate (New):** A novel metric indicating whether the gripper moves within a small radius (5cm) of the *correct* source object at any frame. This metric is crucial for decoupling high-level semantic understanding (intent) from low-level motor execution (action).
- **Task Success Rate:** Binary indicator of successful task completion (natively supported by SimplerEnv).
- **Wrong Object Attempt Rate (Additional for Case Studies):** Records if any non-source object is moved during the episode, particularly relevant for evaluating models under distraction.

This meticulous methodology allows for a systematic and granular assessment of VLA models' generalization boundaries, going beyond simple task success rates to diagnose the underlying causes of failures.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The extensive benchmarking on the INT-ACT suite revealed several critical insights into the generalization capabilities and fundamental limitations of current state-of-the-art VLA models.

#### 4.1. The Persistent and Pronounced Intention-Action Gap

The most striking finding is the pervasive **Intention-Action Gap**. While most VLM-initialized VLA models demonstrated high (80-100%) "Intention Correctness" across nearly all generalization categories, indicating robust semantic understanding and high-level planning (i.e., knowing "what to do"), their "Task Success Rates" dropped drastically. Even the `π0-scratch` model, which generally performed best, fell far short of translating its high intention into reliable task completion. This gap persisted even when comparing VLM-based VLAs against non-VLM baselines like Octo. This suggests that the limitation lies not in the VLM's ability to ground instructions or perceive the correct target, but rather in the VLA's capacity to translate that high-level intent into precise and robust low-level motor execution, especially under out-of-distribution (OOD) observations.

#### 4.2. Generalization to Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Objects

VLA models show strong intention correctness when faced with OOD objects, whether as source or target. However, this strong intention does not guarantee successful execution. Interestingly, grasping success rates varied significantly not just by OOD status, but by the identity of the object itself. For instance, an OOD "coke can" sometimes yielded higher grasping success than a common "carrot" from the BridgeV2 dataset. Furthermore, changing only the *target* object could still lead to a substantial drop (up to 40%) in the grasping success rate of the *source* object, even if the source remained unchanged. This highlights a brittle coupling between high-level perception/planning and low-level action, where changes in one part of the scene can unexpectedly impact ostensibly independent motor primitives.

#### 4.3. Fragile Linguistic Capability

Contrary to expectations that VLAs would inherit and preserve the robust linguistic understanding of their VLM backbones, the results showed that current VLA models *do not* maintain this capability when facing complex language instructions. Performance drops were observed across all tested language variation categories (Language Action, Language Negation, Language Appearance). Even when `π0-finetune` was additionally fine-tuned with paraphrased instructions (`π0-finetune+rephrase`), while its intention for "Language Action" tasks improved, this did not reliably translate into more robust action execution. Magma, possibly due to its joint vision-language co-training, exhibited the best robustness to variations in action words, but overall, the loss of linguistic generalizability after end-to-end VLA training was significant.

#### 4.4. Breakdown under Compounded Vision-Language Thinking

VLAs showed *some* robustness to isolated visual distractions or simple commonsense reasoning tasks. However, their performance became highly brittle under conditions of **multimodal ambiguity**, where visual and linguistic OOD factors compounded. Case studies (e.g., "Put rabbit's favorite vegetable on plate" with a toy bunny distractor, or "Put juice squeezed from orange on plate" with an actual orange fruit distractor) revealed a breakdown in correctly binding linguistic intent to the visual scene. In such scenarios, the "Wrong Object Attempt Rate" surged, especially for models like `π0-finetune`, indicating that linguistic priors could override correct visual grounding, leading to systematic misbehavior. Crucially, auxiliary VQA probing of the underlying PaliGemma VLM (the backbone for π0) demonstrated that the VLM itself *did* possess considerable understanding of these complex instructions *before* fine-tuning for VLA, suggesting that the VLA training process might be inadvertently eroding or "catastrophically forgetting" the VLM's generalist reasoning abilities.

In summary, the results consistently point to a fundamental challenge: current VLA models are adept at interpreting instructions and identifying targets, but this "good intention" frequently fails to translate into precise, generalizable robotic execution, especially when faced with novel objects, complex language, or multimodal ambiguities.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research holds significant importance for the advancement of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models and the broader field of embodied AI and robotics.

#### 5.1. Establishing a Standardized, Comprehensive Benchmark

The most immediate and tangible impact is the introduction and open-sourcing of **INT-ACT**. Prior to this work, a critical gap existed in standardized, comprehensive benchmarks for evaluating VLA generalization. Existing benchmarks were either too limited in task variety, lacked language conditioning, or were prohibitively expensive/difficult to reproduce. INT-ACT directly addresses this by providing a unified, simulation-based probing suite of 50 tasks across diverse categories (object, language, vision-language). By releasing the task suite and evaluation code, the authors provide the research community with a vital tool for consistent, reproducible, and rigorous evaluation of future VLA models. This will accelerate research by enabling clearer comparisons between different architectures and training methodologies.

#### 5.2. Uncovering Fundamental Limitations and Guiding Future Research

The systematic evaluation conducted using INT-ACT revealed two critical, previously undiagnosed or underemphasized failure modes in current VLA models:
- **The Intention-Action Gap:** This finding is highly significant as it pinpoints a core challenge: VLAs can understand *what* to do (high intention) but struggle with *how* to do it precisely and robustly (reliable execution), especially under out-of-distribution conditions. This goes beyond merely reporting low task success rates; it offers a diagnostic metric (Intention Correct Rate) to understand the *nature* of the failure. This insight will prompt future research to focus on closing this gap, potentially through better low-level control mechanisms, robust action representation learning, or more sophisticated methods for grounding high-level symbolic intent in continuous motor commands.
- **Fragile Multimodal Generalization and Erosion of VLM Capabilities:** The observation that VLA fine-tuning can *erode* the sophisticated linguistic and reasoning capabilities of their powerful VLM backbones is a crucial and counter-intuitive finding. It suggests that current end-to-end VLA training paradigms might inadvertently disrupt the valuable pre-trained knowledge within VLMs. This calls for fundamental rethinking of VLA architectural designs and training protocols. Future work might explore modularized approaches (separating perception/planning from control), techniques that "freeze" or more selectively fine-tune VLM components (drawing inspiration from fields like image generation), or more advanced strategies for knowledge distillation and transfer from VLMs to action policies.

#### 5.3. Driving Progress Towards Truly Generalist Robots

The ultimate promise of VLA models is to create versatile, "generalist" robots. By rigorously defining and probing the "generalization boundaries," this work provides a clearer roadmap for achieving this goal. It highlights that simply integrating large VLMs is not enough; the way they are integrated and fine-tuned for action is paramount. The insights from INT-ACT will encourage the development of VLA models that are not only capable of interpreting diverse instructions but can also reliably execute complex behaviors in novel and ambiguous real-world scenarios.

#### 5.4. Implications for Data Collection and Training Strategies

The findings also have implications for data collection and training strategies. The observed fragility under language variations and compounded visual-language shifts suggests a need for more diverse and challenging robotics datasets that explicitly include such variations, rather than relying solely on templated commands or clean scenes. Furthermore, training methods that explicitly preserve VLM generalization, perhaps through multi-task learning with both vision-language and robotics data, or novel regularization techniques, will become increasingly important.

In conclusion, "From Intention to Execution" is a pivotal contribution that not only provides a much-needed evaluation framework but also delivers actionable insights into the core limitations of current VLA models, setting a clear agenda for future research to bridge the critical "perception-to-action" gap in robotics.
