# Multi-level Supervised Contrastive Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> The Multi-level Supervised Contrastive Learning (MLCL) framework enhances supervised contrastive learning by incorporating multiple projection heads with adaptive temperatures to capture multi-level similarity for multi-label and hierarchical classification. It achieves improved performance, notably up to 10% higher accuracy than SupCon with limited training data, and converges four times faster.

## Problem

- Standard supervised contrastive learning methods (like SupCon) define similarity based on a single, atomic label, which oversimplifies complex relationships in real-world data.
- Existing SCL approaches are inadequate for datasets with multi-label or hierarchical structures, often requiring specialized, disjointed solutions.
- A lack of a unified framework to simultaneously leverage various levels of similarity for robust representation learning in complex classification tasks.

## Method

- Introduces ==Multi-level Supervised Contrastive Learning (MLCL)== which employs multiple distinct ==projection heads==, each dedicated to capturing a specific aspect or level of similarity.
- Each projection head is trained with its own supervised contrastive loss and a unique ==temperature hyperparameter==, allowing for adaptive learning based on the granularity of similarity (e.g., lower temperature for subclasses, higher for superclasses).
- For multi-label tasks, MLCL uses label-specific heads and an additional ==global projection head== that incorporates Jaccard similarity to capture overall semantic relationships.

## Results

- MLCL achieved approximately **1%** accuracy improvement over SupCon on full CIFAR-100 and DeepFashion datasets, and significantly outperformed baselines under limited data conditions (e.g., **9-10%** higher accuracy on CIFAR-100 with **1,000** samples).
- Demonstrated superior training efficiency by converging **four times faster** than SupCon (**250** vs. **1,000** epochs) on CIFAR-100.
- Outperformed BERT with cross-entropy loss on multi-label text classification benchmarks (TripAdvisor, BeerAdvocate), showing improved accuracy across various limited training sample sizes.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly modeling different levels of similarity (e.g., subclass and superclass) through separate projection heads leads to richer, more structured, and semantically meaningful representations.
> - Optimizing temperature parameters independently for each projection head is crucial, with lower temperatures effectively distinguishing fine-grained similarities and higher temperatures suitable for broader categories.
> - A global projection head, particularly in multi-label scenarios, can regularize the learning process, capturing overall semantic relatedness and improving robustness to label noise.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper significantly contributes to the field of **representation learning**, specifically within the paradigm of **contrastive learning**. Contrastive learning methods aim to learn meaningful representations by maximizing agreement between different augmented "views" of the same data instance while pushing away representations of dissimilar instances.

The research landscape of contrastive learning broadly divides into two main streams:
- **Self-supervised Contrastive Learning (e.g., SimCLR, MoCo, BYOL):** These methods learn representations from unlabeled data by constructing positive pairs (different augmentations of the same image) and negative pairs (augmentations of different images). SimCLR (Chen et al., 2020a) is a foundational work in this area, demonstrating impressive performance without relying on specialized architectures or memory banks. Subsequent works like BYOL (Grill et al., 2020) and methods focusing on hard negatives (Kalantidis et al., 2020) further refined self-supervised approaches.
- **Supervised Contrastive Learning (SCL) (e.g., SupCon):** SupCon (Khosla et al., 2020) extended the contrastive learning paradigm to supervised settings by defining "similarity" based on ground-truth labels. In SupCon, samples sharing the same class label are treated as positive pairs, while those from different classes are negative. This approach has shown superior performance over traditional cross-entropy loss in many scenarios, particularly when labeled data is scarce.

The present work directly builds upon SupCon but addresses its inherent limitation: **SupCon typically assumes a single, atomic label defines similarity.** However, many real-world datasets possess more complex label structures, such as:
- **Multi-label classification:** Where a single instance can be associated with multiple labels simultaneously (e.g., an image containing a "cat" and a "dog").
- **Hierarchical classification:** Where labels are organized in a tree-like structure, with parent-child relationships (e.g., an animal classified as "dog" (subclass) and "mammal" (superclass)).

Existing research has attempted to tackle these complex label structures, but often in a disjointed manner:
- For **multi-label classification**, methods like Dao et al. (2021a) and Zhang and Wu (2024) have explored learning label-level embeddings or using multiple positive sets. However, these often involve multi-stage training or specific loss functions that might not generalize well.
- For **hierarchical classification**, approaches such as Guided (Landrieu and Garnot, 2021) and HiMulConE (Zhang et al., 2022) integrate class distances or specialized loss functions tailored for hierarchical structures.

This paper, "Multi-level Supervised Contrastive Learning" (MLCL), carves out a unique niche by introducing a **unified framework** that can simultaneously handle both multi-label and hierarchical aspects of data within a single, coherent representation space. It acknowledges that "various aspects of similarity" can exist between samples, going beyond a single, monolithic definition. By proposing multiple projection heads, each capturing a distinct level or aspect of similarity, MLCL offers a more granular and flexible approach than its predecessors, addressing a critical gap in the existing supervised contrastive learning literature.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a novel supervised contrastive learning method that can effectively capture and utilize **multi-level aspects of similarity** inherent in complex datasets, thereby generalizing standard supervised contrastive learning to a broader range of real-world problems.

The key motivations driving this research are rooted in the limitations of existing supervised contrastive learning (SCL) methods, particularly SupCon:
1. **Simplistic Similarity Definition:** Standard SCL frameworks, like SupCon, rely on a single projection head and define similarity based on a single ground-truth label per sample. This simplistic definition fails to encompass the nuanced and multi-faceted ways samples can be similar. For instance, two images might belong to different "subclasses" (e.g., "trout" vs. "shark") but share a common "superclass" (e.g., "fish"). A single projection head struggles to differentiate these multiple levels of relatedness.
2. **Inadequacy for Complex Label Structures:** The single-label assumption makes standard SCL suboptimal for common real-world tasks such as multi-label classification (where an instance has multiple, non-exclusive labels) and hierarchical classification (where labels are organized in a taxonomy). Current solutions for these tasks are often tailored, leading to fragmented approaches.
3. **Suboptimal Performance with Limited Data:** The inability of single-projection-head models to capture the full complexity of sample similarity leads to suboptimal performance, especially in scenarios where training data is limited. In such cases, a richer understanding of similarity relationships can significantly improve representation quality.
4. **Lack of a Unified Framework:** There is a need for a unified framework that can simultaneously address both multi-label and hierarchical aspects without requiring task-specific architectural changes or two-stage training processes, making the learned representations more versatile.

By addressing these motivations, MLCL aims to learn richer, more semantically meaningful representations that are robust and performant across diverse downstream tasks, particularly under challenging conditions like limited training data or noisy labels.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed **Multi-level Contrastive Learning (MLCL)** framework extends standard supervised contrastive learning by incorporating multiple projection heads, each dedicated to capturing a specific aspect or level of similarity.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The method adopts a unified formulation for both multi-label and hierarchical classification. For each input sample $x_k$, there are $L$ associated levels of labels, denoted as $\{x_k, y_l^k\}$, where $y_l^k$ corresponds to either a specific label at level $l$ (for hierarchical) or a multi-hot vector of labels (for multi-label). The goal is to train an encoder $f(.)$ that generates semantically meaningful representations $f(x)$.

#### 3.2. Framework Architecture

MLCL follows the core structure of supervised contrastive learning with a crucial modification:
1. **Data Augmentation:** For each sample $x_k$, two random augmentations, $\tilde{x}_{2k}$ and $\tilde{x}_{2k-1}$, are generated, maintaining the original label (or labels).
2. **Encoder Network:** An encoder network $f(.)$ processes the augmented samples $\tilde{x}_i$ to produce embedding vectors $f(\tilde{x}_i)$.
3. **Multiple Projection Heads:** Unlike standard SupCon which uses a single projection head, MLCL employs $H$ distinct projection networks, $g_h$, where $h \in \{1, \dots, H\}$. Each $g_h$ maps the encoder's embedding $f(\tilde{x}_i)$ into a specific projection space, resulting in $z_i^h = g_h(f(\tilde{x}_i))$. These heads are connected to the encoder's output.
4. **Supervised Contrastive Loss per Head:** For each projection space $h$, a supervised contrastive loss $L_h$ is computed. This loss function, inspired by Khosla et al. (2020), pulls positive sample pairs closer together and pushes negative sample pairs apart. The definition of "positive pairs" is *independent* for each projection head, based on a specific similarity criterion relevant to that head. The loss for each head $h$ also includes a unique temperature hyperparameter, $\tau_h$.
 $$ L_h = \sum_{i \in I} \frac{-1}{|P_h(i)|} \sum_{p \in P_h(i)} \log \frac{\exp(z_i^h \cdot z_p^h / \tau_h)}{\sum_{a \in A(i)} \exp(z_i^h \cdot z_a^h / \tau_h)} $$
 where $P_h(i)$ is the set of positive samples for anchor $i$ in projection head $h$, and $A(i)$ is the set of all other samples in the batch.
5. **Final Loss Function:** The overall loss $L$ is a weighted sum of the individual projection head losses:
 $$ L = \sum_{h=1}^H \alpha_h L_h $$
 where $\sum_{h=1}^H \alpha_h = 1$. The weights $\alpha_h$ control the contribution of each similarity aspect.

#### 3.3. Application to Hierarchical Classification

For hierarchical tasks (e.g., CIFAR-100, DeepFashion), the framework uses two projection heads:
- One head focuses on the **subclass** labels, enforcing fine-grained separation.
- The second head focuses on **superclass** labels, ensuring that samples from related broad categories remain close.
A crucial aspect is the temperature setting: a **lower temperature ($\tau_1 = 0.1$)** is used for the subclass head to promote distinct representations for specific classes, while a **higher temperature ($\tau_2 = 0.5$)** is used for the superclass head. This allows more uniform learning from all negative samples at the superclass level, preventing excessive penalty for 'hard negatives' that are distinct subclasses but share the same superclass. During inference, projection heads are discarded, and a linear classifier is trained on the frozen encoder representations.

#### 3.4. Application to Multi-label Classification

For multi-label tasks (e.g., TripAdvisor, BeerAdvocate), the architecture is adapted:
- **Label-Specific Heads:** A distinct projection head is assigned for each individual label (aspect) in the dataset.
- **Global Projection Head:** An *additional* global projection head is introduced to capture overall semantic relationships between samples, beyond individual label overlaps. This global similarity is often quantified using metrics like Jaccard similarity between the multi-hot label vectors of samples. The loss for this head incorporates the Jaccard similarity score as a weight, emphasizing samples with higher overall label overlap.
- **Combined Loss:** For text datasets, the multi-level contrastive loss is combined with a standard cross-entropy loss (L_ce) for linear classification, where the final loss is $L = \sum_{h=1}^H \alpha_h L_h + (1 - \sum_{h=1}^H \alpha_h)L_{ce}$. This is a common practice for fine-tuning pretrained language models.

#### 3.5. Temperature Analysis

The paper provides a detailed analysis of the temperature parameter ($\tau$).
- As $\tau \rightarrow 0^+$, the loss function primarily focuses on the **hardest negative sample** (the one most similar to the anchor), applying a strong penalty to it. This is beneficial for fine-grained distinctions (like subclasses).
- As $\tau \rightarrow +\infty$, the loss treats all negative samples more uniformly, distributing the penalty more evenly. This is useful for broader categories (like superclasses) where some 'negative' samples might still share high-level similarities.
This analysis justifies the use of different temperature values for different projection heads, allowing for adaptive learning based on the nature of similarity at each level.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted on both image and text datasets demonstrate that the Multi-level Contrastive Learning (MLCL) framework consistently outperforms state-of-the-art supervised contrastive learning methods and other baselines.

#### 4.1. Hierarchical Classification (Image Datasets)

- **Datasets:** CIFAR-100 (100 classes, 2 levels) and DeepFashion (50 categories, 3 superclasses, using 2 levels).
- **Performance:** MLCL achieved marginal improvements of approximately 1% over SupCon (e.g., 77.70% vs 76.50% on CIFAR-100; 73.90% vs 72.82% on DeepFashion) when trained on full datasets. It also outperformed other specialized hierarchical methods like Guided and HiMulConE.
- **Data Efficiency:** A significant finding is MLCL's superior performance in **limited training data scenarios**. For CIFAR-100, with only 1,000 training samples, MLCL achieved 34.74% accuracy, a substantial improvement over SupCon's 26.82% and Cross-Entropy's 20.50%. This advantage persisted with 5,000 and 10,000 samples, showing improvements of 9-10% over SupCon.
- **Training Efficiency:** MLCL converged significantly faster, requiring only 250 epochs compared to SupCon's 1,000 epochs, indicating better learning efficiency.

#### 4.2. Multi-label Classification (Text Datasets)

- **Datasets:** TripAdvisor (7 aspect-based sentiment labels) and BeerAdvocate (5 aspect-based sentiment labels).
- **Performance:** MLCL, particularly with the global projection head, consistently outperformed a fine-tuned BERT encoder with standard cross-entropy loss across various limited training sample sizes (from 30 to 360 samples). For instance, on TripAdvisor with 360 samples, MLCL achieved 79.0% accuracy compared to BERT CE's 78.10%.
- **Impact of Global Head:** The inclusion of the global projection head (MLCL with $h_{global}$) consistently yielded higher accuracy compared to MLCL without it, confirming its effectiveness in capturing broader semantic similarities and acting as a regularizer.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies and Insights

- **t-SNE Visualizations:** Qualitative analysis using t-SNE demonstrated that MLCL learns a more structured and semantically meaningful representation space. Unlike SupCon, which might place semantically unrelated classes close together (e.g., "camel" near "bottle"), MLCL correctly groups related classes (e.g., different animal types together, container types together) while maintaining separation from distinct categories.
- **Temperature Impact:** The ablation study on superclass projection head temperature showed that an optimal intermediate temperature (e.g., $\tau = 0.5$) significantly improved performance. Both excessively low or high temperatures were detrimental, supporting the theoretical analysis that different levels of hierarchy require different sensitivities to hard negatives.
- **Robustness to Label Noise:** The global projection head showed a beneficial regularizing effect. In experiments with uniform label noise (30-80%), models trained with a higher weight for the global projection loss exhibited increased robustness and higher classification accuracy compared to cross-entropy baselines.
- **Transfer Learning:** Features learned by MLCL on CIFAR-100 demonstrated better transferability to CIFAR-10, yielding over 1% accuracy improvement compared to SupCon across various training sample sizes.

In summary, MLCL is a robust and efficient method that learns superior representations, especially under data scarcity, by explicitly modeling multi-level similarity information through multiple, adaptively-tempered projection heads.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Multi-level Supervised Contrastive Learning" (MLCL) framework presents a significant advancement in the field of representation learning, with several important implications and potential impacts:

#### 5.1. Significance

1. **Unified Framework:** MLCL introduces a unified, flexible framework that can simultaneously address complex data structures found in multi-label and hierarchical classification tasks. This unification simplifies the development of models for diverse real-world datasets, which often exhibit such complexities, by providing a single conceptual and architectural approach.
2. **Enhanced Representation Quality:** By explicitly modeling various aspects of similarity through multiple projection heads, MLCL learns richer, more granular, and semantically consistent representations. This moves beyond the simplistic "same-class-different-class" dichotomy of standard SupCon, capturing the full spectrum of relationships between samples.
3. **Improved Data Efficiency:** A crucial finding is MLCL's superior performance in scenarios with limited training data. This is highly significant for applications where labeled data is expensive or scarce, making MLCL a valuable tool for effective representation learning even in challenging, low-resource environments.
4. **Faster Convergence:** The demonstrated ability of MLCL to converge significantly faster than SupCon (e.g., 250 vs. 1,000 epochs) translates into reduced computational costs and quicker model development cycles, which is a practical benefit for researchers and practitioners.
5. **Robustness and Generalizability:** The framework's enhanced robustness to label noise (via the global projection head as a regularizer) and improved transfer learning capabilities highlight its ability to learn more generalizable and resilient features.
6. **Flexibility and Compatibility:** MLCL integrates seamlessly with existing contrastive learning methods and common encoder architectures (ResNet, BERT) without requiring specialized network designs. This broad applicability makes it easy to adopt and adapt across various domains (computer vision, NLP) and downstream tasks.

#### 5.2. Potential Impact

- **Real-world Applications:** MLCL can be directly applied to a wide array of real-world problems with complex label structures, such as medical image diagnosis (hierarchical disease classifications), product categorization (multi-label product attributes), sentiment analysis (multi-aspect reviews), and scientific document analysis (hierarchical topic modeling).
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The concept of multi-level similarity with adaptive temperature control opens new avenues for research in contrastive learning. Future work could explore more sophisticated ways to define and combine similarity notions, automatically learn optimal temperature parameters, or extend the framework to handle other complex data relationships (e.g., graph-structured data, temporal hierarchies).
- **Resource-Constrained Environments:** For domains with limited data, such as rare disease diagnosis or specialized scientific fields, MLCL offers a powerful approach to leverage what little data is available more effectively, potentially accelerating discovery and application.

While the paper does not explicitly detail limitations beyond pointing to future work on interpretability, potential considerations could include the increased number of hyperparameters (weights $\alpha_h$ and temperatures $\tau_h$) to tune, which might add complexity for initial setup, although the paper provides sensible defaults and analysis.

In conclusion, MLCL represents a compelling step forward in supervised representation learning, offering a principled and effective way to harness the rich, multi-faceted similarity information present in many real-world datasets. Its improvements in data efficiency, convergence speed, and robustness underscore its potential to significantly impact the development of more intelligent and adaptable AI systems.
