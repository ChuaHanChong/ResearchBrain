# Spectral Normalization for Lipschitz-Constrained Policies on Learning Humanoid Locomotion

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Seoul National University developed a computationally efficient method using Spectral Normalization for training reinforcement learning policies, enabling smoother, more stable control for humanoid robots in real-world deployment by significantly reducing GPU memory consumption compared to gradient penalty methods. The approach produced stable walking on a physical humanoid robot while requiring only 47% of GPU memory compared to 74% for previous Lipschitz-constrained policies.

## Problem

- Reinforcement learning policies trained in simulation often produce high-frequency, abrupt control commands that real-world robot actuators cannot execute, leading to instability and failure (sim-to-real gap).
- Traditional methods like regularization rewards require extensive hyperparameter tuning, while Gradient Penalty-based Lipschitz-Constrained Policies (GP-LCP) consume excessive GPU memory, limiting parallel training environments.
- The inability to efficiently generate smooth policies hinders the practical deployment of complex locomotion behaviors learned in simulation to physical robots.

## Method

- The authors propose applying ==Spectral Normalization (SN)== to policy networks as an efficient method to constrain the Lipschitz constant of the neural network.
- SN normalizes each weight matrix by its spectral norm (largest singular value), ensuring that each layer and thus the overall network is 1-Lipschitz continuous, promoting smooth control.
- A scaling coefficient is introduced for the final layer's weights to provide flexibility in tuning the effective Lipschitz constant, balancing smoothness and task performance.

## Results

- The Spectral Normalization approach reduced GPU memory consumption to approximately **47%** of available memory, similar to a baseline, significantly less than GP-LCP's **74%** usage.
- In simulation, SN achieved comparable control quality to GP-LCP, with significantly lower joint velocity, acceleration, and torque difference metrics compared to baseline policies (e.g., torque difference **9.25** for SN vs. **27.36** for baseline).
- When deployed on the physical TOCABI humanoid robot, SN policies enabled stable walking across various command inputs, outperforming baseline policies which consistently failed due to vibrations.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Spectral Normalization (SN) offers a computationally efficient alternative to gradient penalty methods for enforcing Lipschitz continuity in policy networks.
> - By ensuring a 1-Lipschitz continuous policy network, SN inherently promotes smooth control commands, which are critical for real-world robotic systems with finite actuator bandwidth.
> - A tunable scaling coefficient for SN allows balancing policy smoothness with task performance and learning convergence.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Introduction

Reinforcement learning has shown remarkable success in training robots to perform complex locomotion tasks in simulation. However, transferring these learned behaviors to real-world robots remains a significant challenge. This paper addresses a critical aspect of this "sim-to-real gap" by proposing a computationally efficient method to generate smoother, more stable control policies for humanoid robots.

The research, conducted by teams from Seoul National University, focuses on the fundamental problem that policies trained in idealized simulations often produce high-frequency, abrupt control commands that real actuators with finite bandwidth cannot execute. This mismatch leads to vibrations, instability, and failure when deployed on physical hardware.

### 2. The Problem with Simulation to Real Transfer

In simulation environments, actuators are typically modeled as having infinite bandwidth, meaning they can instantly respond to any torque command regardless of how rapidly it changes. Real actuators, however, have significant limitations in how quickly they can change their output. When a policy trained in simulation commands rapid torque changes, real motors cannot keep up, resulting in oscillations and unstable behavior.

Traditional approaches to address this issue include:

1. **Regularization rewards** that penalize high joint velocities or accelerations, but require extensive hyperparameter tuning
2. **Gradient Penalty-based Lipschitz-Constrained Policies (GP-LCP)** that mathematically enforce smooth control by constraining the Lipschitz constant of the policy network

While GP-LCP provides a principled solution, it comes with a critical limitation: excessive GPU memory consumption that severely limits the number of parallel training environments, slowing down the learning process.

### 3. Spectral Normalization Approach

The core innovation of this work is applying **Spectral Normalization (SN)** to policy networks as an efficient alternative to gradient penalties. Originally developed for stabilizing Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), spectral normalization constrains the Lipschitz constant of neural networks by normalizing the spectral norm (largest singular value) of each weight matrix.

The key insight is that by ensuring each layer of the network has a Lipschitz constant of at most 1, the overall network becomes 1-Lipschitz continuous. This mathematical property guarantees that small changes in the robot's state lead to proportionally small changes in its actions, inherently promoting smooth control.

For each weight matrix $\mathbf{W}$ in the network, spectral normalization applies:

$$\hat{\mathbf{W}} = \frac{\mathbf{W}}{\sigma(\mathbf{W})}$$

where $\sigma(\mathbf{W})$ is the spectral norm (largest singular value) of the matrix.

### 4. Mathematical Foundation

The theoretical foundation relies on the relationship between Lipschitz continuity and policy gradients. For a Gaussian policy $\pi(a|s) = \mathcal{N}(\mu(s), \sigma^2)$, the authors show that:

$$\max_{s,a} \|\nabla_s \log \pi(a|s)\|_2^2 \approx \frac{4}{\sigma^2} \|\nabla_s \mu(s)\|_2^2$$

By constraining the spectral norm of the policy network that produces $\mu(s)$, they effectively control $\|\nabla_s \mu(s)\|$, ensuring the policy gradient remains bounded and promoting stable learning.

To provide flexibility in tuning the effective Lipschitz constant, the authors introduce a scaling coefficient $\lambda_{\text{SN}}$ applied to the final layer's weights, allowing practitioners to balance smoothness and task performance.

### 5. Experimental Setup and Methodology

The experiments were conducted using the TOCABI humanoid robot, a 1.8-meter tall, 100-kilogram bipedal robot with 12 actuated lower body joints. The training employed the Adversarial Motion Prior (AMP) framework combined with Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO).

Key aspects of the experimental setup include:

- **Observation space**: 49-dimensional state including base height, orientation, velocities, joint positions, and previous actions
- **Action space**: 12 torque commands for lower body joints
- **Training environment**: Isaac Gym with 4096 parallel environments
- **Hardware**: NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPU with 24GB VRAM
- **Domain randomization**: Comprehensive randomization of physical parameters to improve sim-to-real transfer

The policy and value networks used 2-hidden layer MLPs with 512 neurons and ReLU activations, with spectral normalization applied to all layers of the actor network.

### 6. Results and Findings

The experiments revealed several key findings:

**Memory Efficiency**: The most significant result is the dramatic reduction in GPU memory usage. While GP-LCP consumed approximately 74% of the available 24GB GPU memory, the spectral normalization approach required only 47% - comparable to the unregularized baseline policy. This efficiency gain allows for scaling up the number of parallel training environments, accelerating the learning process.

**Control Quality**: In simulation, spectral normalization achieved comparable regularization effects to GP-LCP, with significantly lower joint velocity, joint acceleration, and torque difference metrics compared to baseline approaches. The torque difference metric for SN was 9.25 compared to GP-LCP's 9.80 and the baseline's 27.36.

**Real-World Performance**: When deployed on the physical TOCABI robot, both GP-LCP and SN policies achieved stable walking across various command inputs, while baseline policies consistently failed due to vibrations. Notably, the SN approach often produced even smoother motions than GP-LCP, with lower joint velocity and acceleration values.

**Hyperparameter Sensitivity**: The study found that the SN coefficient $\lambda_{\text{SN}} = 0.2$ provided the optimal balance between smoothness and task performance. Values too high (1.0) resulted in jittery motions, while values too low (0.1) hindered learning convergence.

### 7. Significance and Impact

This research makes several important contributions to robotics and reinforcement learning:

**Computational Efficiency**: By reducing GPU memory overhead from 74% to 47%, the method enables larger-scale parallel training, which is crucial for modern RL approaches that rely on massive data collection from thousands of simulated environments.

**Practical Deployment**: The work directly addresses one of the most pressing challenges in robotics - reliable sim-to-real transfer. By generating finite-bandwidth control signals, the approach makes learned locomotion policies more suitable for real-world deployment.

**Principled Regularization**: Unlike heuristic regularization methods that require extensive tuning, spectral normalization provides a mathematically grounded approach to enforcing smoothness constraints based on Lipschitz continuity theory.

**Broad Applicability**: While demonstrated on humanoid locomotion, the technique is generally applicable to other robotic systems and control tasks where smooth, realizable control is critical.

The method represents a practical advancement in making reinforcement learning more viable for complex robotic systems by addressing both theoretical soundness and computational efficiency. By enabling more efficient training while maintaining the mathematical guarantees of Lipschitz-constrained policies, this work helps bridge the gap between laboratory demonstrations and real-world robotic deployment.
