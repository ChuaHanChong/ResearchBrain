# SIR: Structured Image Representations for Explainable Robot Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the Intuitive Robots Lab, Karlsruhe Institute of Technology, developed Structured Image Representations (SIR) using Scene Graphs (SGs) to enable intrinsically explainable robot learning. This approach achieved an average success rate of 19.5% on the RoboCasa benchmark, outperforming image-based baselines at 14.81%, while demonstrating robustness to distractor objects and uncovering latent dataset biases through sparse, learned sub-graphs.

## Problem

- Robot learning policies often rely on opaque visual embeddings, making decision-making uninterpretable and difficult to debug.
- Existing graph-based robot learning methods typically use high-level information or auxiliary graphs, lacking direct, intrinsically explainable structured scene representations.
- Robot policies based on visual embeddings are often sensitive to irrelevant visual distractions, hindering robustness in complex environments.

## Method

- **SIR** integrates ==Scene Graphs (SGs)== as a direct, structured intermediate representation into a ==Goal-Conditioned Imitation Learning (GCIL)== framework.
- Develops an ==end-to-end learnable sparsification module== to prune the SGs into minimal, task-relevant sub-graphs that explain robot actions.
- Employs a ==Graph Neural Network (GNN)== to process the sparse SGs, generating an embedding fed into ==diffusion== or ==behavior cloning== action generation models.

## Results

- Achieved an average success rate of **19.5%** on the RoboCasa benchmark, surpassing image-based baselines which scored **14.81%**.
- Demonstrated high robustness to distractor objects, with graph-based models showing almost no performance degradation, unlike image baselines which decreased by **3.3%**.
- Identified cropped-image features, often combined with bounding box coordinates, as the most effective initial node representations for Scene Graphs.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Learning sparse, task-relevant sub-graphs provides intrinsic explainability, directly revealing the model's understanding of the scene and decision-making criteria.
> - Analyzing the learned sub-graphs effectively uncovers latent dataset biases, such as spurious correlations and positional biases, which influence robot behavior.
> - Structured Scene Graph representations significantly enhance model robustness to novel distractor objects, unlike traditional image embeddings which show performance degradation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot learning, particularly Imitation Learning (IL) and Goal-Conditioned Imitation Learning (GCIL), has seen significant progress with the adoption of attention-based and diffusion-based models. These advancements enable robots to perform complex tasks based on visual observations and language goals in increasingly intricate environments. However, a prevalent challenge in these approaches is the reliance on learned visual embeddings. While effective for compact information encoding, these embeddings typically lack explicit structure and are often opaque. This opacity limits the interpretability of robot decision-making, making it difficult to understand the rationale behind generated actions.

This work addresses the growing need for structured and expressive scene representations in robot learning. Current graph-based IL methods often integrate graphs as auxiliary input or rely on planning algorithms (e.g., Task and Motion Planning). These methods typically use high-level information graphs that require specific key-points for updates during execution. In contrast, this research investigates the direct use of structured scene representations, specifically Scene Graphs (SGs), as the primary state input for robot learning in a step-based GCIL framework, without requiring explicit planning. This approach distinguishes itself from previous work that might use graphs within diffusion frameworks but restrict them to specific modalities like point-cloud embeddings or simpler, limited-node graphs.

Furthermore, the paper situates itself within the context of learning graph sparsification. While existing graph reduction techniques aim to reduce graph size for storage, runtime, or outlier handling, many of these methods (like condensation or coarsening) do not preserve interpretability. Traditional sparsification often focuses on removing edges rather than nodes, which is less effective for interpretability in fully-connected graphs, especially when information from unselected nodes might still propagate. This work introduces an end-to-end learnable node removal process *before* message passing, aiming to ensure that only task-relevant nodes influence the final graph embedding, thereby enhancing interpretability.

In summary, this research contributes to the development of intrinsically explainable robot learning by proposing a novel method for generating and sparsifying Scene Graphs as direct, structured observations, thereby moving beyond opaque visual embeddings and addressing limitations in current graph-based and graph sparsification techniques for robotics.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the limitations of existing robot policies based on learned visual embeddings. These policies often operate without explicit structure, rendering their decision-making processes opaque and sensitive to visual distractions. This opacity makes it challenging to interpret *why* a robot makes a particular decision, debug its behavior, or identify underlying issues. The authors aim to overcome these challenges by introducing explicit structure and intrinsic explainability into robot learning.

The key objectives of this work are articulated through the introduction of Structured Image Representations (SIR) for Explainable Robot Learning, which leverages Scene Graphs (SGs) as an intermediate representation for robot policy learning. Specifically, the paper outlines two main contributions and four research questions:

**Main Contributions/Objectives:**

1. **Investigation of SGs as effective scene representations:** The research aims to thoroughly examine the utility of SGs in robot learning, including a systematic analysis of different image modalities (e.g., symbolic labels, geometric cues, visual features) as initial node representations within these graphs. This objective seeks to determine which types of perceptual information are most effective when structured as a graph.
2. **Proposal and analysis of learnable SG sparsification:** A core objective is to develop a method for end-to-end learnable sparsification of SGs. This involves creating a task-relevant sub-graph that offers direct insight into the objects and interactions the model considers critical for its decisions. The subsequent analysis of these resulting sub-graphs is intended to provide insights into the model's decision-making process, specifically by observing which objects are selected or excluded by the model.

**Research Questions (RQs):**

- **RQ1: How do SG embeddings compare to image embeddings as model input in GCIL?** This question motivates a direct performance comparison between graph-based and traditional image-based observation inputs in goal-conditioned imitation learning.
- **RQ2: How does the choice of the initial node representation (e.g., symbolic labels, geometric cues, or visual features) affect the performance of SG-based models?** This objective drives an ablation study to understand the impact of different perceptual feature modalities on the effectiveness of graph representations.
- **RQ3: How robust are models that use SG embeddings compared to image embeddings in regard to distractor objects?** This question aims to evaluate the resilience of graph-based representations to irrelevant visual information, a common challenge for image-based methods.
- **RQ4: Do sparse SGs facilitate the interpretability and analysis of the decision-making process of the respective behaviour model?** This central question directly addresses the core motivation for explainability, investigating whether the learned sub-graphs can effectively reveal insights into how the model perceives a scene and makes decisions.

Overall, the motivation is to develop robot learning models that are not only capable but also transparent, allowing researchers and practitioners to understand, trust, and debug robotic behavior by providing clear, interpretable intermediate representations that directly inform action generation. The intrinsic explainability of SIR, meaning the explanation is part of the learning process rather than a post-hoc analysis, is a key driving factor.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, Structured Image Representations (SIR), integrates Scene Graphs (SGs) into a Goal-Conditioned Imitation Learning (GCIL) framework to achieve intrinsically explainable robot learning. The architecture comprises four main components, with the SG extraction step being frozen, and the subsequent three modules trained end-to-end within the GCIL model:

1. **Scene Graph Generation:**
 - **Object Detection and Node Creation:** The process begins by extracting all objects from a given RGB or RGB-D image, using either ground-truth or predicted segmentation masks. Each detected object forms a node in a Fully-Connected Graph (FC-Graph).
 - **Initial Node Representations:** Four primary feature modalities are investigated for initial node embeddings:
 - **Label:** A one-hot encoding of the object's symbolic label.
 - **Cropped-Image-Feature:** A visual embedding derived from a pre-trained ResNet backbone applied to the object's cropped bounding box region.
 - **BB-Coordinates:** Normalized 2D bounding box corner and center coordinates in pixel space.
 - **Point-Cloud-Feature:** An embedding from a pre-trained PointNet architecture applied to the object's associated point cloud.
 - **Edge Features:** These are initialized based on geometric distance between nodes if bounding box or point cloud information is used; otherwise, they are set to 1 to facilitate message passing. Different modalities can be concatenated to form the initial node embedding.

2. **Scene Graph Sparsification:**
 - **Objective:** To prune the FC-Graph into a minimal, task-relevant sub-graph, which then serves as the explanation for the generated actions.
 - **Node Score Prediction:** A two-layer Transformer-Decoder (termed FiLMDecoder) with four heads per layer is used to predict a Node Score (NS) for every node. This module employs Adaptive Layer Normalization (AdaLN) to condition node embeddings on the language goal.
 - **Node Weight (NW) Definition:** Based on the NS, a Node Weight NW(n) is defined as NS(n) if the node is selected for the sub-graph, and 0 otherwise.
 - **Loss Functions:**
 - **Soft Histogram Loss:** Applied to encourage NS values to be uniformly distributed within, preventing all scores from converging. This loss uses a Gaussian kernel for soft binning and MSE against a uniform target distribution, weighted at 0.1 during training.
 - **L1 Loss:** Applied to node weights, promoting high NW(n) for instruction-relevant nodes and low for irrelevant ones.
 - **Node Selection:** The top-k highest-scoring nodes are chosen to form the sparse sub-graph, where `k` is a task-specific parameter representing the number of task-relevant objects.

3. **Scene Graph Embedding Generation:**
 - **Graph Neural Network (GNN):** A GNN consisting of two Graph Attention v2 (GATv2) residual layers is used. These layers update node features by weighting information from neighbors using learned attention scores.
 - **Global Average Pooling:** After message passing, a global average pooling layer aggregates features from all nodes into a final graph-level representation, which serves as the state representation for the action generation model.
 - **Differentiability Adaptations:** To enable end-to-end learning of the FiLMDecoder, specific adaptations are made: the gradient of NS(n) is maintained for NW(n), edge weights are calculated as the product of node weights (EdgeWeight(u, v) = NW(u) · NW(v)), and node scores NS(n) are explicitly included in the pooling step (GraphEmbedding = Σn∈V NW(n) ∗ Xn / Σn∈V 1_NW(n)>0). This ensures that only selected nodes contribute to the final embedding and aids gradient flow.

4. **Action Generation:**
 - The generated SG embedding and a CLIP-embedded language goal are fed into a downstream action generation model.
 - Two models are evaluated: Multimodal Diffusion Transformer (MDT) for state-of-the-art performance and a Behavior Cloning (BC)-Transformer as a baseline.
 - The action generator produces 10 future actions from a single observation.

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Benchmarks:** RoboCasa (24 atomic tasks) and CALVIN (language-conditioned long-horizon manipulation, D→D setting).
- **Evaluation Protocol:** Each model configuration trained twice with different seeds, evaluated over 100 rollouts.
- **Sensory Information:** A subset from static cameras (left and right for RoboCasa, single for CALVIN) is used to ensure controlled comparison.
- **Multi-View Handling (RoboCasa):** Two strategies: "Split-View Graph" (separate GNNs for each view) or "Fusion Graph" (concatenating node features from both views into a single graph).
- **Baselines:** Image-based models (ResNet18, fine-tuned), models using point clouds (FPV-Net patch-wise input), or combinations thereof.
- **Explainability Evaluation:** Qualitative analysis of the "explanation sub-graphs" (G_expl) constructed as the mean presence of nodes/edges across rollouts (Equation 3). This metric quantifies the consistency of node selection and highlights deviations from human expectations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of SIR was conducted across various task settings and compared against image-based baselines, providing insights into the research questions.

**RQ1: How do SG embeddings compare to image embeddings as model input in GCIL?**
- **Performance Superiority:** On the RoboCasa benchmark, sparse graph policies (SIR) achieved an average success rate of 19.5%, outperforming image-based baselines (14.81%) and even fully-connected graph models (16.98%). This indicates that graph embeddings generally surpass image embeddings.
- **Task-Specific Performance:** While graph-based models showed significant improvements in categories such as Doors, Levers, Knobs, and Buttons, they did not consistently outperform image-based baselines in tasks like Drawers or Pick and Place. This suggests potential dataset biases influencing performance in certain tasks.
- **Point Cloud Integration:** Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) were observed to be a more effective architecture for integrating point cloud information as node features (11.08% for Point-Cloud-Features alone, 15.04% with Cropped-Image-Features) compared to directly inputting point clouds into the action generation model (4.13% alone, 13.25% with images).

**RQ2: How does the choice of the initial node representation affect the performance of SG-based models?**
- **Optimal Node Features:** Cropped-Image-Features, either individually (16.65%) or combined with BB-Coordinates (15.90% for Split-View, 16.98% for Fusion Graph), were identified as the best-performing initial node representations. These combinations consistently surpassed standard image baselines.
- **Diverse Modality Integration:** The results demonstrated that graph-based models can effectively integrate diverse node representations, including symbolic labels, geometric cues, and visual features, leading to performance superior to baselines using corresponding individual feature types.

**RQ3: How robust are models that use SG embeddings compared to image embeddings in regard to distractor objects?**
- **Image Baseline Sensitivity:** The image-based baseline exhibited a notable performance decrease (3.3% on average) when novel distractor objects were introduced during inference.
- **Graph-based Robustness:** In contrast, SIR, FC-Graph, and Threshold sparsification models showed high robustness to distractor objects, with almost no performance degradation on average. Some categories (Drawers, Knobs, Levers) even displayed slight performance increases. This indicates that structured graph representations are significantly more resilient to irrelevant visual information.

**RQ4: Do sparse SGs facilitate the interpretability and analysis of the decision-making process of the respective behaviour model?**
- **Intrinsic Explainability:** SIR's method of learning sub-graphs as an intermediate representation provides intrinsic explainability, allowing for direct analysis of the model's scene understanding during action generation.
- **Revealing Dataset Biases:** The analysis of learned sparse graphs, particularly deviations from human-expected sub-graphs, proved effective in uncovering critical dataset and model characteristics:
 - **Spurious Correlations:** Models sometimes included "distractor nodes" (e.g., Wall, Counter, Oven) that were seemingly irrelevant to the task but consistently selected. This suggests the model exploits spurious correlations within the training data, where these objects might provide sufficient contextual information to achieve success without direct causal relevance. For example, in a high-performing "CloseDrawer" task, the model consistently included the Oven and Microwave.
 - **Positional Biases:** In tasks like "CloseSingleDoor," the instruction-grounded SIR model sometimes selected only the robot's gripper and mobile base nodes, completely omitting the target door objects, yet still succeeded with high performance. This indicates a strong positional bias in the dataset, where the robot has learned to execute a fixed trajectory based on its own state, rendering the target object's specific location less critical for task completion.
- **Comparison to Post-Hoc Methods:** The end-to-end nature of SIR allows it to expose these biases, which methods employing Vision-Language Models (VLMs) or Large Language Models (LLMs) for pre-filtering would likely mask by always selecting "logically correct" objects.

**Ablation Studies:**
- SIR with instruction-grounded sparsification generally outperformed other sparsification methods (Random Node Removal, Naive NR, Threshold, TopK), with the soft histogram loss contributing significantly to performance.
- Behavior Cloning (BC)-Transformer-based models showed lower overall performance compared to MDT, but graph-based observations still resulted in higher average success rates than image-based BC models.
- Preliminary experiments on CALVIN showed image-based models slightly outperforming sparse graph methods, suggesting further fine-tuning may be required for complex, long-horizon tasks.
- Using imperfect, generated graphs (predicted by a fine-tuned DETR) for inference resulted in performance drops, indicating a distribution shift challenge for models trained on ground truth.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces Structured Image Representations (SIR) as a significant advancement in explainable robot learning, leveraging Scene Graphs (SGs) to provide transparent and interpretable robot policies. The findings demonstrate several key areas of significance and potential impact:

1. **Enhanced Performance and Robustness:** SIR-based policies achieve higher average success rates compared to traditional image-based baselines in Goal-Conditioned Imitation Learning (GCIL). Crucially, these graph-based representations exhibit significant robustness to novel distractor objects introduced during inference, a critical advantage over image-based methods that often suffer performance degradation. This improved robustness can lead to more reliable and deployable robotic systems in unstructured environments.

2. **Effective Multi-Modality Integration:** The work highlights the efficacy of Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) in integrating diverse perceptual modalities, such as image features, bounding box coordinates, and point cloud features. This demonstrates a flexible and powerful architecture for combining various forms of visual information into a coherent and structured scene understanding, which can be critical for robots operating with rich sensory inputs.

3. **Intrinsic Explainability for Debugging and Analysis:** The most significant impact lies in SIR's capacity for intrinsic explainability. By learning a sparse, task-relevant sub-graph as an intermediate representation during the end-to-end training process, SIR provides direct insights into the model's decision-making. This allows researchers to:
 - **Uncover Dataset Biases:** The deviations of the learned sub-graphs from human-expected selections (e.g., including irrelevant "distractor nodes" or omitting key task-relevant objects) effectively expose latent dataset biases, such as spurious correlations and positional biases. This capability is invaluable for debugging training data, improving data collection strategies, and building more robust datasets.
 - **Interpret Model Behavior:** Understanding *why* a model succeeds or fails, or *what* features it focuses on, moves beyond simple performance metrics. This level of interpretability is crucial for developing trustworthy AI in robotics, as it enables humans to diagnose issues and build confidence in autonomous systems.

4. **Foundation for Future Research:** The paper lays the groundwork for several promising future directions. These include developing methods for the model to learn the optimal number of task-relevant nodes (rather than relying on heuristics for 'k') and reducing the reliance on ground-truth data for graph generation by fine-tuning 2D foundation models to predict object masks. Such advancements could further enhance the autonomy and scalability of SIR-like approaches.

5. **Broader Impact on AI Interpretability:** By demonstrating an end-to-end learned, intrinsically explainable representation that can effectively reveal hidden biases in complex datasets, this research offers a valuable paradigm for AI interpretability beyond robotics. It underscores the potential of structured intermediate representations to provide actionable insights that traditional opaque neural networks or post-hoc explanation methods might fail to deliver.

In conclusion, SIR provides a method that not only enhances robot learning performance and robustness but, more importantly, offers a powerful, intrinsically explainable tool for understanding and debugging the complex interplay between visual perception, decision-making, and underlying data characteristics. This contributes significantly to the development of more transparent, reliable, and intelligent robotic systems.
