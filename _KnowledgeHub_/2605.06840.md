# Extracting Search Trees from LLM Reasoning Traces Reveals Myopic Planning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at NYU and Generality Inc. introduced a method to extract formal search trees from large language models' (LLMs) natural language reasoning traces in a four-in-a-row game. The study found that while LLMs generate deep lookahead in their traces, their final move decisions are predominantly driven by myopic, shallow evaluations of immediate consequences, revealing a distinct planning mechanism compared to human strategic behavior.

## Problem

- The true planning capabilities of LLMs, especially those using chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning, remain debated; it's unclear if their multi-step reasoning constitutes genuine planning or merely pattern completion.
- Previous studies primarily assessed LLM planning through behavioral outcomes, lacking extensive examination of the internal structure of their reasoning processes.
- There was a need to investigate whether LLM reasoning traces structurally resemble systematic search and, critically, whether such search causally drives their eventual decisions.

## Method

- The team developed a method to extract and quantify formal ==search tree structures== from unstructured natural language ==chain-of-thought (CoT)== reasoning traces generated by LLMs in a ==four-in-a-row== game.
- ==Computational cognitive models==, adapted from human planning research, were fitted to LLM move choices to determine how search tree information is integrated into decisions.
- A ==causal intervention== study was performed by surgically editing parts of the reasoning traces to assess the direct influence of different search depths on the model's final move selection.

## Results

- Computational modeling showed that a myopic model, which ignores deeper lookahead, consistently achieved higher prediction accuracy for LLM move choices than models that integrated full search tree information.
- Causal intervention revealed that restoring only depth-1 reasoning paragraphs significantly reduced the rate of move changes (to **4.1%**), while adding back deeper paragraphs had only a marginal further effect, indicating the predominant influence of shallow evaluations.
- LLMs exhibited substantially shallower search (average max depth **1.00-3.48 plies**) compared to typical human planners (**4-6 plies**) in the four-in-a-row domain, even when generating longer reasoning chains.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - LLMs' decision-making in strategic tasks is best characterized as 'myopic planning,' where choices are primarily influenced by the immediate consequences of first-ply moves, largely disregarding deeper lookahead explicitly generated in their reasoning traces.
> - Contrary to human strategic planning where deeper search often correlates with expertise, LLMs' planning performance (winning rate) was significantly predicted by search breadth (considering more initial candidate moves) rather than search depth.
> - There is a fundamental dissociation between the surface-level generation of deep reasoning traces by LLMs and the actual, shallower information used to drive their final decisions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of large language models (LLMs) has led to significant advancements in their ability to generate complex, extended chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning traces. These traces often include explicit deliberation over hypothetical future outcomes, which bears resemblance to the mental simulation observed in human planning. In both artificial intelligence (AI) and cognitive science, planning has been frequently formalized as a tree search process, where deep forward search is considered a fundamental driver of planning capability. Game-playing AI, such as AlphaGo, achieves superhuman performance through systematic deep search, and human cognitive models also characterize planning as mental simulation of action sequences, with expertise correlating with deeper simulation.

However, a significant debate exists regarding whether LLMs genuinely engage in search-based planning. One perspective suggests that LLMs, due to their autoregressive nature, fundamentally lack the capacity for systematic search and backtracking. This view is supported by studies indicating LLMs may fail at multi-step planning, with their outputs being more consistent with pattern completion. Conversely, an opposing perspective highlights LLMs' strong performance on tasks seemingly requiring multi-step planning, such as competitive programming, mathematical reasoning, and strategic gameplay.

The conclusions in existing literature have largely been derived from analyzing behavioral outcomes, such as solution accuracy, rather than investigating the internal structure of the reasoning processes that generate these outcomes. This gap means that whether LLM reasoning traces structurally resemble systematic search and, more critically, whether any apparent search genuinely drives their decisions, remains largely unexplored. Previous work on extracting structured graphs from reasoning traces has predominantly focused on single-answer tasks like mathematics or coding, rather than planning tasks which necessitate evaluating sequences of future actions and their consequences. This paper addresses this lacuna by developing a method to analyze the structural characteristics of LLM planning and its causal influence on decisions in a strategic game context.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this research were twofold:
1. **To determine if LLM reasoning traces exhibit the structural hallmarks of systematic search.** This objective aimed to move beyond mere behavioral evaluation of LLM planning by developing a method to extract and quantify formal search trees from the unstructured natural language of Chain-of-Thought reasoning. This was a necessary precursor to understanding the underlying mechanisms of LLM planning.
2. **To investigate whether the search process evident in LLM reasoning traces causally drives their final decisions.** Even if reasoning traces appear to contain search-like structures, it is not immediately clear if this simulated search is genuinely integrated into the decision-making process, or if it is merely a generated artifact that does not influence the final output. This objective sought to establish a causal link between the deliberated search and the chosen action.

The motivation for these objectives stemmed from the ongoing controversy surrounding LLMs' planning capabilities. Existing behavioral benchmarks have yielded inconsistent conclusions, partly because they evaluate only outcomes without probing the internal processes. By analyzing the structure of reasoning traces and their impact on decisions, the researchers aimed to provide a more mechanistic characterization of LLM planning, offering a more granular understanding than outcome-based assessments alone. The "four-in-a-row" board game was selected as a suitable domain due to its well-defined rules, which facilitate tractable and verifiable tree extraction, and the existence of an established computational cognitive model for human planning in this game, providing a rigorous baseline for comparison. Additionally, four-in-a-row is less likely to be overrepresented in LLM training data compared to games like chess or Go, offering a cleaner testbed for emergent planning abilities rather than memorized solutions.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed a multi-faceted approach involving a game tournament with LLMs, the extraction of search trees from their reasoning traces, quantification of search effort, computational modeling of move decisions, and a causal intervention study.

**Game Setup and Tournament:**
Twenty-seven LLMs participated in a round-robin tournament of "four-in-a-row" on a 4x9 board. The game is a two-player, zero-sum game where players alternate placing pieces to achieve four in a row horizontally, vertically, or diagonally. LLMs received a system prompt outlining the rules and board representation (FEN-style notation). User prompts provided the current board state and active player, and models responded with a move in `m <row> <col>` format. Only 14 models with fully accessible reasoning traces were included in subsequent analyses, yielding 9696 reasoning traces across 1092 games.

**Search Tree Extraction:**
Reasoning traces, being unstructured natural language, were converted into formal search trees using an LLM judge (GPT-5). The judge was prompted to identify and extract every move explicitly deliberated in the trace. A nested list format was used to represent the tree: `[("r,c"), [child_node_1], [child_node_2]...]`. Depth-1 nodes represented candidate first-ply moves by the model, depth-2 nodes were anticipated opponent replies, and so on, maintaining strict player alternation. Only explicitly named moves were included. The extraction prompt was optimized using a human-annotated validation set.

**Quantifying Search Trees:**
For each model, the following metrics were calculated:
- **Tree size:** Total number of nodes in an extracted search tree.
- **Winning rate:** Overall performance in the tournament.
- **Search depth:** The maximum number of alternating moves simulated ahead (max ply).
- **Search breadth:** The number of candidate first-ply moves considered by the model.
Relationships between these metrics were analyzed using correlations and linear regression to understand how search effort dimensions relate to game performance.

**Computational Modeling to Predict Moves:**
Computational cognitive models, adapted from the heuristic search model of Van Opheusden et al., were fitted to predict LLM move decisions from the extracted search trees.
- **Heuristic Function:** A fixed heuristic function `h(s)` was used, defined as a linear combination of spatial pattern features on the board state `s`. Features included inverse Euclidean distance to the board center (ϕ_centre) and counts of connected two-in-a-row, unconnected two-in-a-row, three-in-a-row, and four-in-a-row patterns (ϕ_i). A scaling factor `C` allowed for an offensive (`C > 1`) or defensive (`C < 1`) bias.
- **Value Backup Rules:** Four model variants were compared based on how they propagated heuristic values up the search tree:
 - **Full-tree model:** Evaluated `h(s)` at all leaf nodes and recursively propagated values upward using minimax (maximizing for the model, minimizing for the opponent).
 - **Myopic model:** Applied `h(s)` directly to the depth-1 states resulting from each first-ply move, entirely ignoring deeper nodes.
 - **Discount model:** Interpolated between full-tree and myopic models with a discount factor `γ ∈`, where `γ=1` is full-tree and `γ=0` is myopic.
 - **No-tree model:** A baseline that ignored the extracted search tree, scoring all legal first-ply moves using `h(s)` alone.
- **Model Fitting:** Move choices were modeled as a softmax over the backed-up values of candidate moves. Parameters (heuristic weights, `C`, `γ`) were fitted by minimizing the negative log-likelihood over all observed moves using L-BFGS-B, with 20 random restarts. Models with fewer than 20 parseable reasoning trees were excluded.

**Causal Intervention Study:**
To directly test the causal role of reasoning traces, a causal intervention study was performed on Qwen3-Next-80B-Thinking.
- **Paragraph-Level Labeling:** An LLM judge (Claude Opus 4.7) labeled each paragraph of the reasoning trace with a structural type (e.g., preamble, branch, final decision, meta) and annotated all moves mentioned within each paragraph, along with their search depths.
- **Trace Editing:** Different portions of the trace were surgically removed:
 - Removal of only "final decision" paragraphs.
 - Removal of "final decision" paragraphs plus the entire branch corresponding to the chosen move.
 - Progressive re-addition of paragraphs from the chosen branch: first, only depth-1 paragraphs (immediate consequences), then also depth-2 paragraphs (opponent replies).
- **Model Rerunning:** The pruned traces were fed back to the LLM as a prefill (at temperature 0). The new move output was then compared to the original move to measure the move change rate.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

**Search Effort and Game Performance:**
A positive relationship was observed between the average size of extracted search trees (search effort) and overall winning rate across LLMs (Figure 2A). This correlation was evident both across different models and within model families. Causal evidence was provided by GPT-OSS-120B, where a "high" reasoning effort setting (allocated more tokens) led to larger search trees and a higher winning rate compared to a "medium" setting with identical model architecture. This suggests that increased deliberation causally contributes to performance gains.

**LLMs' Search Characteristics Compared to Humans:**
LLMs exhibited shallower search than human planners. The average maximum search depth for LLMs ranged from 1.00 to 3.48 plies, which is considerably shallower than the 4 to 6 plies inferred for human players, where deeper search correlates with expertise. In the linear regression predicting winning rate, search depth was not a significant predictor when controlling for search breadth (p = 0.82), while breadth remained significant (p = 0.0025). Models with a higher breadth-to-depth ratio tended to achieve higher winning rates (Figure 2C). These findings indicate that LLMs' performance advantage from increased search effort is primarily driven by considering a broader range of first-ply candidate moves rather than deeper lookahead.

**LLMs Do Not Act on Deeper Search:**
Computational modeling revealed that LLMs' move decisions were best explained by a myopic strategy.
- **Model Comparison:** Tree-based models (full-tree, myopic, discount) consistently outperformed the no-tree baseline, confirming that extracted search trees contain predictive information. However, the **myopic model consistently achieved lower negative log-likelihood and higher prediction accuracy than the full-tree model across all LLMs** (Figure 3C-D). When the models disagreed, the myopic model was correct more than twice as often.
- **Depth Harm:** The "depth harm," defined as the increase in negative log-likelihood when using the full-tree model over the myopic model, was positive for every model and increased with search depth (Figure 3E). This suggests that backing up values from deeper nodes actually impaired, rather than improved, prediction of LLM moves.
- **Discount Factor:** For the discount model, the discount factor `γ` converged to near zero for every model, effectively collapsing to the myopic model. This indicates that deeper nodes contributed negligibly to the value backup process.
These results collectively suggest that while LLMs *generate* deeper nodes in their reasoning traces, they do not propagate value information from these deeper nodes to influence their first-ply move decisions in a manner consistent with minimax or similar human-like planning.

**Impact of Search Tree Information:**
The "candidate gain," defined as the decrease in negative log-likelihood from the no-tree model to the myopic model, decreased significantly with search breadth (Figure 3F). This implies that for models considering only a few candidates (narrow breadth), the specific moves considered in the candidate set are highly predictive of the final move. For models considering many candidates (wide breadth), the candidate set adds less information beyond the raw heuristic value. This supports the observation that breadth, not depth, is more impactful for LLM performance.
Regarding heuristic parameters, an offensive bias parameter `C` (weighting own threats more heavily) was the only normalized feature weight that significantly predicted winning rate (Figure 3G).

**Causal Role of Reasoning Traces and Depth:**
The causal intervention study confirmed that reasoning traces causally drive move selection, but primarily through shallow content.
- **Final Decision Removal:** Removing only the "final decision" paragraphs resulted in a minor 2.1% move change, indicating these concluding statements were not critical drivers.
- **Whole Branch Removal:** Removing the entire chosen branch (including final decision and branch analysis) caused a substantial 32.0% move change. This provides direct causal evidence that the reasoning trace influenced the decision. Removing an unchosen branch resulted in only a 1.2% change.
- **Depth-Specific Re-addition:** When starting from full branch removal and progressively adding back paragraphs by depth:
 - Adding back only depth-1 paragraphs (immediate consequences) reduced the move change rate to 4.1%.
 - Further adding back deeper paragraphs (depth-2 and beyond) only marginally reduced the rate to 3.7%, a negligible difference.
These results demonstrate that LLM move selection is predominantly driven by the shallowest evaluation of candidate moves, with deeper lookahead content in the reasoning trace having a negligible causal effect on the final decision, despite being generated.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research provides a refined understanding of LLM planning by shifting the focus from merely "can LLMs plan?" to "what kind of planning algorithm do their decisions reflect?" By analyzing the internal structure of reasoning traces rather than just behavioral outcomes, the study reveals a fundamental dissociation: LLMs generate the surface structure of tree search, but their decisions are driven by a myopic mechanism that largely ignores deeper lookahead. This mechanistic insight would be invisible to behavioral benchmarks alone, emphasizing the necessity of interpretability methods for understanding complex LLM behaviors.

The findings have significant implications for **interpretability and scalable oversight** of LLMs. The observed disconnect between generated reasoning traces and actual decision processes suggests that LLM-generated explanations or reasoning steps may not always be faithful representations of their internal deliberation. If models can produce structured search without relying on it for decisions, oversight methods based solely on reasoning traces might fail to detect the true drivers of behavior. This raises concerns for developing reliable methods for assessing and controlling advanced AI systems.

Regarding **why LLMs exhibit myopia**, several non-exclusive explanations are proposed: an algorithmic bottleneck in Transformer attention for implementing value backup operations over long contexts, the learning of alternative heuristics that prioritize salient threats over full minimax propagation, or an adaptive strategy where models treat increasingly unreliable predictions at greater depths as untrustworthy, thus relying on shallow, more certain information.

The study offers **targeted guidance for improving LLM planning capabilities**. Current approaches often assume that increasing test-time compute, lengthening reasoning traces, or prompting for deeper search will inherently lead to better decisions. However, these results suggest that the primary bottleneck is not the *generation* of deep content, but the model's *ability to act upon it*. Therefore, future development may require training signals that explicitly reward the effective use and integration of deep lookahead information, moving beyond outcome-based supervision.

While the study offers profound insights, it also acknowledges limitations. The analyses were conducted within a single game domain (four-in-a-row), and the generalizability of these conclusions to tasks with different structures, complexities, and planning demands remains an open question. Additionally, the reliance on a specific parametric heuristic function, although validated for human behavior, may not fully capture all nuances of LLM decision-making, suggesting avenues for future exploration with alternative feature sets or value architectures.
