# Stackelberg Learning from Human Feedback: Preference Optimization as a Sequential Game

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at ETH Zürich developed Stackelberg Learning from Human Feedback (SLHF), a framework that reframes LLM alignment as a sequential-move game. This approach addresses the limitations of scalar reward models and stochastic equilibria, enabling principled inference-time refinement where a Follower policy significantly enhances initial model responses by 60.5% over the Leader's output.

## Problem

- Existing Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) methods struggle to capture complex and intransitive human preferences using scalar reward functions, leading to potential misalignment.
- Nash Learning from Human Feedback (NLHF) can result in undesirable stochastic equilibrium policies and stability issues during simultaneous-move training.
- There is a lack of principled mechanisms that allow Large Language Models (LLMs) to dynamically improve or refine their outputs at inference time.

## Method

- The ==Stackelberg== Learning from Human Feedback (**SLHF**) framework formulates LLM alignment as a sequential-move Stackelberg game, involving a Leader policy that generates an initial response and a Follower policy that refines it.
- The STACKELBERGGDA algorithm, a two-timescale Gradient Descent-Ascent method, is proposed to optimize the Leader and Follower policies, with the Follower adapting faster.
- A memory-efficient implementation shares the underlying LLM parameters for both the Leader and Follower, leveraging distinct prompt templates for their respective tasks.

## Results

- The STACKELBERGGDA-FOLLOWER policy significantly enhanced responses, achieving **60.5%** preference over its own Leader's outputs and outperforming RLHF and NLHF baselines by up to **60%** in human preference comparisons on a **0.5B** parameter model.
- The Follower policy demonstrated strong generalization capabilities, consistently improving the quality of responses generated by other diverse, independently trained LLMs.
- SLHF successfully scaled to an **8B** parameter model (LLAMA-3.1-TULU-3-**8B**-SFT), achieving improved AlpacaEval 2.0 win rates and demonstrating stronger performance in general chat applications compared to similar-scale DPO baselines.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Sequential game theory, specifically Stackelberg dynamics, offers a robust framework for preference optimization that naturally accommodates intransitive human preferences and can yield deterministic equilibrium policies.
> - Decomposing the alignment problem into distinct Leader and Follower roles simplifies learning, as the Follower optimizes its response against a committed action from the Leader.
> - The inherent sequential structure of the Stackelberg game provides a principled and effective mechanism for dynamic, inference-time self-improvement of LLM outputs without additional training.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper addresses the critical and rapidly evolving challenge of aligning Large Language Models (LLMs) with human preferences, a cornerstone for developing safe, helpful, and reliable AI systems. The work is situated at the intersection of reinforcement learning, game theory, and natural language processing.

The dominant paradigm for LLM alignment has been **Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)**. RLHF typically involves training a reward model on pairwise human comparisons and then optimizing an LLM policy to maximize this scalar reward using techniques like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). While empirically successful, RLHF suffers from several fundamental limitations:
1. **Scalar Reward Assumption:** It assumes that diverse and complex human preferences can be adequately captured by a single, real-valued reward function, often relying on the Bradley-Terry model. This assumption frequently breaks down in practice, especially with intransitive preference structures observed in human feedback.
2. **Sensitivity to Data:** Learned reward models can be sensitive to the distribution and biases of the training comparison data, potentially leading to misranked preferences or undesirable outcomes like mode collapse.
3. **Intransitivity:** Scalar reward models are inherently limited in representing cyclic or intransitive preferences, which are common in real-world human judgment.

To address these limitations, a new wave of **Preference Optimization** methods has emerged, largely shifting towards direct optimization over pairwise preferences without an explicit scalar reward model. Within this landscape, **Nash Learning from Human Feedback (NLHF)** stands out by framing preference optimization as a two-player simultaneous-move game, seeking a Nash Equilibrium. NLHF offers advantages by avoiding the Bradley-Terry assumption and accommodating intransitive preferences. However, it too has drawbacks:
1. **Stochasticity:** Nash equilibria often involve mixed (stochastic) strategies, which can be undesirable in applications requiring consistent and deterministic outputs.
2. **Convergence:** Optimizing against a non-stationary opponent in simultaneous-move games can hinder convergence and stability during training.

**Stackelberg Learning from Human Feedback (SLHF)** positions itself as a principled advancement in this game-theoretic direction. By adopting a sequential-move game (Stackelberg dynamics), where a Leader commits to an action before a Follower responds, SLHF directly tackles the inherent stochasticity of NLHF and offers a more stable optimization landscape. Furthermore, it inherently provides a mechanism for **inference-time refinement**, a growing area of interest where models improve their outputs at test time through additional computation rather than further training. This differentiates it from existing self-correction algorithms that often rely on external feedback or predefined reward functions.

In summary, SLHF enters a research space grappling with the limitations of current LLM alignment techniques, offering a robust, game-theoretic alternative that explicitly addresses intransitive preferences, fosters stable learning, and enables dynamic self-improvement at inference time, thereby pushing the boundaries of what aligned LLMs can achieve.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce and rigorously evaluate **Stackelberg Learning from Human Feedback (SLHF)**, a novel preference optimization framework. This objective is driven by several key motivations stemming from the shortcomings of existing LLM alignment methodologies:

1. **Overcoming RLHF's Limitations with Intransitive Preferences:** The most significant motivation is to move beyond the restrictive assumption of scalar reward functions, prevalent in RLHF. The authors highlight that reward models, particularly those based on the Bradley-Terry model, struggle to represent cyclic or intransitive human preferences, which are empirically observed in feedback data. SLHF aims to directly optimize over pairwise preferences, thereby circumventing the need for an explicit reward model and handling intransitivities more naturally.

2. **Enhancing Stability and Determinism Compared to NLHF:** While NLHF addresses some of RLHF's limitations by framing alignment as a two-player game, its simultaneous-move nature often leads to mixed (stochastic) strategies in equilibrium. This inherent stochasticity can be undesirable in applications demanding consistent and reliable outputs. Furthermore, simultaneous optimization against a moving opponent can pose convergence challenges. SLHF, through its sequential design, aims to yield more stable learning and, in certain cases, admit deterministic equilibrium policies for the Leader and Follower, offering greater consistency.

3. **Enabling Principled Inference-Time Refinement:** A crucial motivation is to provide a systematic method for **inference-time refinement**, allowing LLMs to improve their outputs at the point of generation without requiring further training or external feedback. This capability is particularly valuable when underlying preferences might shift or be highly individualized at test time. The sequential Leader-Follower structure of SLHF is designed to naturally support this iterative self-correction, enabling the Follower to refine the Leader's initial response.

4. **Decomposing the Alignment Problem for Better Learning:** The Stackelberg framework decomposes the complex preference optimization problem into distinct, complementary roles. The Follower's task becomes a "refinement problem" – finding the best response to a *known* (committed) action from the Leader. This simplifies the Follower's learning. The Leader, in turn, learns to produce initial actions that are robust to the Follower's anticipated refinements. This decomposition is hypothesized to lead to more stable and efficient learning dynamics.

In essence, the authors are motivated to create a more robust, flexible, and practically useful framework for LLM alignment that addresses the theoretical and empirical limitations of current state-of-the-art methods, with a particular focus on dynamic self-improvement at inference time.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core methodology of this paper revolves around re-conceptualizing the LLM alignment problem as a **sequential-move game**, specifically a Stackelberg game. This game-theoretic approach is central to developing the SLHF framework and its associated optimization algorithm.

#### 3.1. Stackelberg Game Formulation

- **Players:** The game involves two policies: a **Leader** (π) and a **Follower** (ω).
- **Sequence of Play:** Given a context `x` (e.g., a user prompt), the Leader first commits to and produces an action `y` (an LLM response). The Follower then observes both the context `x` and the Leader's *realized action* `y`, and subsequently generates its own refined action `y'` (an improved response).
- **Objective Function:** The Leader aims to maximize its expected preference for its action `y` over the Follower's refined action `y'`, while the Follower aims to minimize this preference (effectively, to generate a `y'` that is preferred over `y`). The objective function (Equation 5) incorporates Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence regularization terms for both policies against fixed reference policies (π_ref and ω_ref), ensuring policy exploration and staying close to the initial model.
- **Informational Advantage:** A key distinction from standard Stackelberg settings is that the Follower conditions on the *realized action* `y`, not just the Leader's policy. This provides the Follower with more specific information, simplifying its best-response problem.
- **Solution Concept:** The solution to this game is a **Stackelberg equilibrium (π*, ω*)**. The authors prove the existence and uniqueness of this equilibrium under standard regularity assumptions (Proposition 1). Importantly, they also show that, unlike Nash Equilibrium, the Stackelberg equilibrium can admit *deterministic* policies for both Leader and Follower in the non-regularized limit (Remark 2), which addresses the stochasticity issue of NLHF.

#### 3.2. STACKELBERGGDA Algorithm

- **Optimization Strategy:** To approximate the Stackelberg equilibrium, the paper proposes **STACKELBERGGDA** (Algorithm 1), a two-timescale Gradient Descent-Ascent (GDA) algorithm.
 - **Leader Update:** The Leader's policy (π) is updated via gradient *ascent* to maximize its objective.
 - **Follower Update:** The Follower's policy (ω) is updated via gradient *descent* to minimize the Leader's objective (which is equivalent to maximizing the Follower's preference for its own response).
 - **Two-timescale Learning:** A key aspect is the use of different learning rates (η_F > η_L, governed by coefficient κ), allowing the Follower to adapt faster than the Leader. This is motivated by stronger convergence guarantees in more general nonconvex-concave regimes and empirical success in other adversarial training settings.
 - **Projection:** After each update, policies are projected onto their respective probability simplices to maintain valid probability distributions.
- **Theoretical Basis:** The objective function `f(π, ω)` is shown to be concave in π and convex in ω (Section A.3), a property that supports the ergodic convergence guarantees of two-timescale GDA algorithms.

#### 3.3. Scalable Implementation for LLM Fine-Tuning (Algorithm 2)

- **Parameterization:** For large-scale LLM fine-tuning, tabular policies are infeasible. Both Leader (π_θ) and Follower (ω_ϕ) policies are parameterized by neural networks (transformer models).
- **Shared Model Architecture:** Crucially, the authors implement a memory-efficient strategy where the Leader and Follower *share the same underlying LLM parameters (θ)*. This is achieved by using distinct prompt templates (Figure 1):
 - **Leader Prompt:** Just the user prompt (`<user_prompt>`).
 - **Follower Prompt:** User prompt + Leader's initial response + an instruction to "Improve the previous answer!" (`<user_prompt>\nAssistant: <leader_response>\nUser: Improve the previous answer!\nAssistant:`).
- **Loss Function:** A unified loss function (Equation 10) is defined for the shared parameters `θ`, incorporating both the Leader's and Follower's optimization objectives, weighted by the two-timescale coefficient κ.
- **Gradient Estimation:** Unbiased gradient estimates are derived using policy gradient methods (REINFORCE-style) from batches of sampled (context, leader response, follower response, preference feedback) tuples.
- **Computational Efficiency:** This approach requires two samples per prompt, making its computational cost comparable to other game-theoretic NLHF methods, and avoids the high memory cost associated with PPO-based RLHF methods (e.g., value function estimation).

This comprehensive methodology allows SLHF to be both theoretically grounded in game theory and practically scalable for aligning large language models, setting the stage for its empirical validation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of SLHF provides compelling evidence for its effectiveness and unique advantages over existing preference optimization frameworks. The key findings are categorized as follows:

#### 4.1. Empirical Comparison of Solution Concepts (0.5B Parameter Model)

- **Initial Performance (Leader vs. Baselines):** In a round-robin tournament using the QWEN2.5-0.5B model, STACKELBERGGDA-LEADER performed comparably to NASH-MD-PG (an NLHF baseline). Both significantly outperformed the base QWEN2.5-0.5B model and the RLOO (RLHF baseline). For instance, both Leader and Nash achieved approximately 73% preference over the base model and 61% over RLOO, tying at 50% when compared directly. This suggests that the Leader policy is competitive in generating strong initial responses.
- **Inference-Time Refinement via Follower:** A pivotal finding is the substantial performance gain achieved by the STACKELBERGGDA-FOLLOWER. When the Follower refined its *own* Leader's initial responses, it achieved an impressive 80% preference over QWEN2.5-0.5B, 66% over RLOO, 60% over NASH-MD-PG, and even outperformed the Leader's responses by 60.5%. This demonstrates that the two-turn inference procedure provides significant gains with minimal additional computational cost (just one extra generation).

#### 4.2. Generalization of Inference-Time Refinement

- **Cross-Model Improvement:** STACKELBERGGDA (acting as a Follower) consistently improved the outputs generated by *other independently trained models* (QWEN2.5-0.5B, RLOO, NASH-MD-PG). For example, it achieved gains of up to 60% even when refining outputs from NASH-MD-PG. This highlights the Follower's robust ability to generalize and enhance responses from diverse initial sources.
- **Lack of Generalization in Baselines:** In contrast, other models (QWEN2.5-0.5B, RLOO, NASH-MD-PG) showed limited to no ability to act as effective Followers for other models, often degrading quality or only improving on the base model. This indicates that explicitly training for a refinement task, as SLHF does, is crucial for effective inference-time self-improvement; mere instruction prompting is insufficient.

#### 4.3. General Purpose Fine-tuning and Scalability

- **LLAMA-3.1-TULU-3-8B-SFT Alignment:** STACKELBERGGDA was successfully applied to fine-tune a larger LLM (LLAMA-3.1-TULU-3-8B-SFT) using "LLM-as-a-judge" feedback.
- **Benchmark Performance:** Both STACKELBERGGDA-LEADER and STACKELBERGGDA-FOLLOWER substantially improved the AlpacaEval 2.0 win rates over the base model, outperforming other models of similar scale (e.g., LLAMA-3.1-TULU-3-8B-DPO) in terms of human preference proxy scores. This demonstrates scalability from 0.5B to 8B parameters and efficacy in general chat applications.
- **Instruction Following (IFEval):** While STACKELBERGGDA showed strong performance on human preferences (AlpacaEval), the IFEval scores (measuring verifiable instruction following) were lower than the DPO baseline. This suggests a trade-off or different focus, but also points to future research directions in combining verifiable and comparative feedback within SLHF.

#### 4.4. Additional Findings (from Appendix)

- **Intransitivity:** Analysis of the HELPSTEER2 dataset confirmed significant intransitivity in aggregated human preferences, with 57% of prompt-completion graphs containing cycles, underscoring the necessity of frameworks like SLHF.
- **Iterative Improvement:** Extended experiments showed that with increased inference-time samples (N=5), RLOO's performance collapsed due to mode-collapse, and NASH-MD-PG showed limited gains compared to the base model. Conversely, STACKELBERGGDA consistently improved across various attributes (coherence, correctness, helpfulness) as N increased, demonstrating effective scaling with test-time compute and a more balanced improvement profile.
- **Two-Timescale Coefficient Ablation:** Ablation studies on the `κ` parameter (follower weight) confirmed that moderate values (e.g., κ=5) optimally balance Leader and Follower performance, indicating the importance of this hyperparameter for stable optimization.

In summary, the main findings robustly demonstrate that SLHF is a competitive and often superior framework for LLM alignment, particularly excelling in its ability to enable principled and generalized inference-time refinement, and scaling effectively to larger models.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of Stackelberg Learning from Human Feedback (SLHF) represents a significant advancement in the field of Large Language Model (LLM) alignment, with profound implications for both research and practical applications of AI.

#### 5.1. Addressing Fundamental Limitations of Existing Paradigms

- **Beyond Scalar Rewards:** SLHF directly tackles the core limitation of Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) by moving beyond scalar reward models, which notoriously struggle with capturing the complexity and intransitivity of human preferences. This is crucial for building LLMs that are truly aligned with nuanced and diverse human values, rather than simplified, potentially misaligned proxies.
- **Stable and Deterministic Solutions:** By framing alignment as a sequential-move game, SLHF offers a compelling alternative to Nash Learning from Human Feedback (NLHF). It avoids the inherent stochasticity often associated with Nash Equilibria, potentially yielding more consistent and predictable model outputs where a deterministic best response exists. The sequential nature also fosters more stable learning dynamics, as the Follower optimizes against observed actions rather than a constantly shifting opponent.

#### 5.2. Pioneering Principled Inference-Time Refinement

- **Dynamic Self-Improvement:** One of the most impactful contributions of SLHF is its principled and built-in mechanism for **inference-time refinement**. This means that LLMs trained with SLHF can dynamically improve their responses at the moment of interaction, without requiring additional training or external human feedback. This capability is invaluable in dynamic environments where user preferences might be highly individual, contextual, or evolve over time.
- **Generalization of Refinement:** The demonstrated ability of the SLHF Follower to improve outputs from *any* initial model, even those it wasn't specifically trained with, highlights its robustness and generalizability. This opens doors for creating more adaptable and universally applicable refinement agents.

#### 5.3. Enhanced Robustness and Consistency

- **Handling Intransitivity:** The game-theoretic framework inherently handles intransitive preferences, which are common in real-world human feedback. This makes SLHF-aligned models more robust to complex preference structures that often lead RLHF into suboptimal or collapsed states.
- **Reliable Outputs:** For applications requiring high consistency and reliability (e.g., factual information generation, sensitive content moderation), the potential for deterministic policies in SLHF offers a significant advantage over inherently stochastic Nash solutions.

#### 5.4. Practical Implications and Scalability

- **Improved User Experience:** For end-users, the ability of LLMs to self-refine means more personalized and satisfactory interactions. Users could iteratively prompt for improvements, knowing the model is designed to refine its responses in an aligned manner.
- **Efficient Alignment:** SLHF offers a computationally efficient training paradigm, particularly its single-model instantiation and batched gradient estimation, making it scalable for large LLMs (demonstrated up to 8B parameters). Its memory efficiency compared to PPO-based methods also reduces the computational barrier for widespread adoption.
- **Foundation for Advanced AI Assistants:** The framework lays a strong foundation for developing highly capable AI assistants that can not only generate initial responses but also iteratively refine them to match a user's evolving needs or specific context, moving towards more collaborative human-AI interaction patterns.

#### 5.5. Future Research Directions

- SLHF also outlines clear avenues for future research, such as integrating active preference elicitation and personalized refinement to adapt dynamically to individual users, and developing algorithms with stronger (last-iterate) convergence guarantees, building upon the theoretical foundation laid in this work.

In conclusion, SLHF represents a significant step forward in aligning LLMs with human preferences by leveraging the power of sequential game theory. Its ability to robustly handle complex preference structures, enable principled inference-time self-improvement, and scale to large models positions it as a highly impactful framework for the future development of more intelligent, adaptable, and user-centric AI systems.
