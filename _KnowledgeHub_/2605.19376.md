# Generative Recursive Reasoning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from KAIST, Mila, New York University, and Université de Montréal introduced Generative Recursive Reasoning Models (GRAM), a framework that injects stochasticity into recursive neural networks to enable multi-hypothesis exploration and generative capabilities. GRAM achieves improved performance on complex structured reasoning and multi-solution constraint satisfaction problems, outperforming deterministic recursive baselines and demonstrating high-quality unconditional generation of diverse data.

## Problem

- Existing Recursive Reasoning Models (RRMs) are predominantly deterministic, following a single latent trajectory and producing a single prediction.
- This deterministic nature limits their capacity to maintain uncertainty, explore alternative hypotheses, or address inherent ambiguity in complex reasoning problems.
- Deterministic RRMs struggle with multi-solution tasks, often exhibiting mode collapse and low coverage of possible solutions.

## Method

- **GRAM** formulates recursive reasoning as a stochastic latent trajectory, allowing for probabilistic, multi-hypothesis exploration.
- It employs a hierarchical recursive core with an inner loop for low-level refinement and an outer loop for high-level stochastic state transitions.
- Inference-time computation is scaled through both recursive depth and parallel sampling of multiple latent trajectories, with a Latent Process Reward Model (LPRM) used for effective candidate selection.

## Results

- GRAM consistently outperformed deterministic recursive baselines on structured reasoning tasks, achieving **97.0%** accuracy on Sudoku-Extreme and **52.0%** on ARC-AGI-1.
- It demonstrated superior performance and coverage on multi-solution constraint satisfaction problems (e.g., **99.7%** accuracy and **90.3%** coverage on N-Queens 8x8), where deterministic models showed mode collapse.
- GRAM achieved high-quality unconditional generation, producing **99.05%** valid Sudoku boards and diverse Binarized MNIST digits with competitive Inception and FID scores, showing improved efficiency over diffusion models.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Introducing stochasticity into recursive latent state transitions enables the exploration of multiple reasoning paths and the maintenance of uncertainty, improving robustness in complex problems.
> - Computational scaling can occur not only through recursive depth but also by exploring multiple parallel latent trajectories at inference time, enhancing solution quality and robustness.
> - The proposed variational framework allows recursive models to function as effective unconditional generative models for structured data, producing diverse and constrained outputs beyond conditional reasoning tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in "Generative Recursive Reasoning" addresses how extended computation should be implemented in neural reasoning systems, positioning itself within the landscape of both autoregressive models and Recursive Reasoning Models (RRMs).

Large autoregressive models, such as large language models, typically scale reasoning by extending a sequence-generation process. This can involve explicit intermediate computation (e.g., chain-of-thought prompting) or implicit representations within hidden states. While effective, these models often tie computational depth to output length or token generation, which can lead to verbosity and inefficiency.

A complementary approach is explored by Recursive Reasoning Models (RRMs), which perform iterative refinement of a persistent latent state using shared transition functions. This design decouples reasoning depth from both parameter scale and output length, allowing a compact model to execute many steps of internal computation. Previous RRMs, such as Universal Transformers, Looped Transformers, Hierarchical Reasoning Models (HRM), and Tiny Recursive Models (TRM), have demonstrated potential in structured reasoning tasks requiring constraint propagation, state tracking, and iterative correction. They illustrate that reasoning capability can arise from the organization of computation itself, not solely from model size or output length.

However, existing RRMs are predominantly deterministic. Given an input and initialization, they follow a single latent trajectory and converge to a single prediction. This determinism restricts their ability to maintain uncertainty, consider alternative hypotheses, or explore multiple solution strategies, especially in problems with inherent ambiguity or multiple valid solutions. This limitation motivates the current research.

GRAM introduces a probabilistic dimension to recursive reasoning, drawing inspiration from probabilistic latent state-space models. While these models have been used in sequential generative modeling and video prediction to capture uncertainty and multimodal dynamics, GRAM reinterprets stochastic dynamics as a computational process rather than mere temporal observation modeling. This reinterpretation allows GRAM to define possible reasoning trajectories, supporting multi-hypothesis exploration and both conditional and unconditional generation within the RRM paradigm, addressing the determinism inherent in prior recursive approaches.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central question motivating this work is whether recursive latent computation can support probabilistic, generative, and multi-hypothesis reasoning while retaining the computational efficiency characteristic of compact recurrent models. The paper identifies a fundamental limitation in existing Recursive Reasoning Models (RRMs): their deterministic nature. Prior RRMs follow a single, fixed latent trajectory, leading to a single prediction. This determinism restricts the model's capacity to:
- **Maintain uncertainty:** In many reasoning problems, especially those with combinatorial complexity or partial information, a single deterministic path may be insufficient or prone to getting trapped in suboptimal solutions.
- **Explore alternative hypotheses or solution strategies:** When multiple valid solutions exist, or when the optimal path is not immediately obvious, deterministic models struggle to explore the solution space comprehensively.
- **Address intrinsic ambiguity:** Problems where the correct output is not uniquely determined by the input benefit from a model that can represent and explore diverse possibilities.

To address these limitations, the key objectives for developing Generative Recursive Reasoning Models (GRAM) are:
1. **Formulate recursive reasoning as a stochastic latent trajectory:** This involves treating the reasoning process itself as probabilistic, where each recursive step involves a sampled transition rather than a deterministic update. This enables the model to explore multiple hypotheses and solution strategies.
2. **Enable multi-trajectory computation and parallel inference-time scaling:** By allowing multiple stochastic trajectories to be sampled in parallel, GRAM aims to scale inference not only by increasing recursive depth but also by increasing the number of concurrently explored paths.
3. **Develop a latent-variable generative model:** The framework is designed to model conditional distributions `p_θ(y|x)` by marginalizing over stochastic latent reasoning trajectories, and also to support unconditional generation `p_θ(x)` when input is absent or fixed.
4. **Improve performance on structured reasoning and multi-solution constraint satisfaction tasks:** The probabilistic and multi-trajectory capabilities are hypothesized to lead to improved performance on tasks that require iterative refinement and exploration of complex solution spaces.
5. **Demonstrate unconditional generation capability:** To validate the generative nature of the model beyond conditional reasoning, the paper aims to show its ability to generate novel instances of structured data from an empty input.

These objectives aim to extend the efficiency and depth-scaling benefits of RRMs with the robustness and explorative capacity of probabilistic generative models.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

GRAM models conditional distributions `p_θ(y|x)` by marginalizing over stochastic latent reasoning trajectories, and can also define an unconditional generative model `p_θ(x)` by replacing the input with an empty conditioning embedding.

**Architecture Overview:**
GRAM comprises an Encoder, a Recursive Core, and a Decoder.
- **Encoder (`f_enc`):** An input `x` is first processed by an encoder `f_enc` to produce a fixed embedding `e_x` that is used throughout the recursive computation.
- **Recursive Core:** This is the central component where latent states are evolved through learned stochastic transitions. It operates with a hierarchical structure:
 - **Latent State (`z`):** The latent state `z` is composed of two interacting components: a high-level component `h` (abstract reasoning state) and a low-level component `l` (fine-grained intermediate computation).
 - **Inner Loop (Low-level Refinement):** Within a single latent transition step `t`, the low-level component `l` is refined `K` times (e.g., K=4 or K=6) using a function `f_L`. This process `l_{t,k} = f_L(h_{t-1}, l_{t,k-1}, e_x; θ)` updates `l` while `h` remains fixed, separating the time scales of `h` and `l`.
 - **Outer Loop (High-level Stochastic Transition):** After `K` low-level refinements, the high-level component `h` is updated. This is where stochasticity is introduced. The model first computes a deterministic proposal `u_t = f_H(h_{t-1}, l_t; θ)`. Then, a conditional perturbation `ϵ_t` is sampled from a state-dependent Gaussian distribution `p_θ(ϵ_t | u_t) = N(μ_θ(u_t), σ_θ^2(u_t)I)`. The new high-level state is `h_t = u_t + ϵ_t`. The combined state `z_t = (h_t, l_t)` forms the next latent state. This process is repeated for `T` transitions, forming a "supervision step."
 - **Nested Recursion:** The full recursive computation applies `N_sup` supervision steps, where the terminal state of one step (`z^{(n)}_T`) becomes the initial state of the next (`z^{(n+1)}_0`).
- **Decoder (`f_dec`):** After `T` transitions, the decoder `f_dec` produces a prediction `ŷ = arg max f_dec(h_T; θ)` based on the final high-level latent state.

**Training Procedure:**
GRAM is trained using amortized variational inference to maximize the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO).
- **Latent Variable Modeling:** The model defines a conditional likelihood `p_θ(y|x)` by marginalizing over a full latent trajectory `τ = (z_0 → ... → z_{T_Total})`, where `T_Total = T × N_sup`.
- **Variational Inference:** Since direct maximum likelihood is intractable, a variational posterior `q_ϕ(τ|x,y)` is introduced. The training objective maximizes the ELBO: `log p_θ(y|x) ≥ E_{q_ϕ(τ|x,y)}[log p_θ(y|τ,x)] - KL(q_ϕ(τ|x,y) || p_θ(τ|x))`.
- **Prior vs. Posterior:** Both the generative prior `p_θ(τ|x)` and the variational posterior `q_ϕ(τ|x,y)` are modeled as conditional Markov processes. The posterior differs by sampling `ϵ_t` from a target-conditioned distribution `q_ϕ(ϵ_t | u_t, y)`, giving it access to the ground-truth target `y` during training.
- **Practical Implementation:** Deep supervision is applied across `N_sup` consecutive supervision steps, providing dense learning signals. To manage computational cost and enable training with constant memory, truncated gradient propagation is used, where gradients are computed only through the final transition of each supervision step. This results in a surrogate objective `L^{(n)}_GRAM` for each step, which is a biased but memory-efficient approximation of the full ELBO. A KL balancing coefficient is used to prevent posterior collapse.

**Inference-Time Scaling:**
GRAM supports two complementary axes for scaling computation at inference:
- **Depth:** Adaptive Computation Time (ACT) is optionally employed, allowing each trajectory to terminate at a learned halting depth rather than a fixed number of steps.
- **Width:** The model can sample `N` multiple latent reasoning trajectories (`τ^{(i)}`) in parallel from the learned prior `p_θ(τ|x)`. Each trajectory's terminal state is decoded into a candidate output `ŷ^{(i)}`.
- **Candidate Selection:** The final prediction can be selected by either majority voting among the `N` candidates or by using a Latent Process Reward Model (LPRM). The LPRM is a value head `v_ψ(z_t)` trained to predict the final quality (accuracy) of a trajectory from its latent state, and it selects the candidate with the highest predicted value. This parallel exploration enhances robustness and solution quality without increasing sequential recursion length.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrate that GRAM offers advantages over deterministic recursive models and exhibits capabilities in both conditional reasoning and unconditional generation.

**Challenging Puzzle Tasks (Sudoku-Extreme, ARC-AGI):**
- GRAM consistently outperformed deterministic recursive baselines such as Looped Transformers, HRM, and TRM on both Sudoku-Extreme and ARC-AGI benchmarks. For instance, on Sudoku-Extreme, GRAM achieved 97.0% accuracy compared to TRM's 87.4%. On ARC-AGI-1, GRAM scored 52.0% versus TRM's 44.6%. This improvement is attributed to GRAM's use of stochastic latent transitions, which enable exploration of diverse reasoning trajectories, leading to more robust reasoning capabilities.
- Direct prediction models, even with high parameter counts, showed no success (0% accuracy) on Sudoku-Extreme and ARC-AGI-2, confirming the necessity of recursive computation for these tasks.

**Parallel Sampling and Inference-Time Scaling:**
- GRAM exhibited a new axis of inference-time scaling: width, through parallel trajectory sampling. Increasing the number of parallel samples (`N`) consistently improved performance.
- On Sudoku-Extreme, GRAM with `N=20` samples at 16 iterations achieved 97.0% accuracy, surpassing all deterministic baselines, including TRM, which reached 90.5% even with 320 iterations. This indicates that parallel exploration can achieve superior performance with comparable computational budgets by bypassing latency bottlenecks associated with depth-only scaling. The Latent Process Reward Model (LPRM) was used for effective candidate selection.
- On ARC-AGI-1, without data augmentation, GRAM showed robust scaling with parallel samples, outperforming TRM, which did not benefit similarly. An ablation also showed that the benefits of parallel sampling diminish with increased data augmentation, suggesting they play complementary roles in achieving solution diversity.

**Multi-solution Puzzle Tasks (N-Queens, Graph Coloring):**
- Deterministic recursive models (Looped TF, HRM, TRM) demonstrated structural limitations in capturing multiple valid solutions. Their coverage was low (e.g., at most 36.1% on N-Queens) and their accuracy degraded significantly as the number of possible solutions increased.
- GRAM, in contrast, maintained consistent performance in terms of accuracy and improved coverage with parallel sampling. For N-Queens (8x8), GRAM achieved 99.7% accuracy and 90.3% coverage.
- GRAM also exhibited sharper constraint satisfaction compared to other generative models like Autoregressive (AR) Transformers and Masked Diffusion Language Models (MDLM). For Graph Coloring, GRAM significantly reduced conflict edges (e.g., 2.7 for 8-vertex) compared to AR (19.0), while achieving comparable diversity. This highlights that recursive refinement in GRAM enables stricter adherence to constraints.

**Unconditional Generative Capabilities (Binarized MNIST, Sudoku):**
- GRAM demonstrated its ability to function as an unconditional generative model.
- On Sudoku generation from empty boards, GRAM achieved a 99.05% validity rate using 10.9M parameters and 16 supervision steps, surpassing D3PM baselines that used larger models (up to 55.1M parameters) and more denoising steps (1000). The generated boards were diverse and unique.
- For binarized MNIST image generation, GRAM produced recognizable digits with Inception Score (IS) and FID scores comparable to D3PM, whereas the deterministic TRM baseline exhibited mode collapse (FID 303.29).
- The generative quality on MNIST improved monotonically with increased recursive depth at inference time, even beyond the steps used during training, demonstrating the transferability of iterative refinement.

**Ablation Studies:**
- Stochastic guidance (SG) was shown to be a consistent architectural advantage, improving performance across various base architectures (Looped TF, deep supervision, hierarchical recursion). The full GRAM, integrating all components, achieved the best results.
- Both stochasticity and learned guidance were essential. Removing guidance (pure stochasticity) maintained Sudoku performance but caused mode collapse on N-Queens (50.27% accuracy). Removing stochasticity entirely (pure deterministic guidance) resulted in complete failure (0.0% on both tasks) due to severe overfitting.
- Naive attempts to add stochasticity to TRM (stochastic decoding or random initialization) did not yield performance improvements, suggesting that GRAM's gains stem from its specific variational framework rather than general randomness.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces Generative Recursive Reasoning Models (GRAM), offering a new framework for neural reasoning systems that integrates probabilistic computation into recursive architectures.

**Key Contributions:**
- **Probabilistic Multi-Trajectory Recursion:** GRAM establishes a design principle for recurrent and recursive reasoning architectures by transforming deterministic latent state refinement into a stochastic process. This enables models to explore multiple reasoning paths and maintain uncertainty, addressing a limitation of prior deterministic RRMs.
- **Width-Based Inference-Time Scaling:** The framework introduces and empirically validates a novel dimension for scaling computation at inference time—width—by sampling multiple latent trajectories in parallel. This complements traditional depth-based scaling and offers a way to improve solution quality and robustness without solely relying on longer sequential computation, potentially mitigating latency bottlenecks.
- **Improved Structured Reasoning and Constraint Satisfaction:** GRAM demonstrates superior performance over deterministic recursive baselines on complex structured reasoning tasks like Sudoku-Extreme and ARC-AGI. Its ability to explore diverse solution spaces also enables it to effectively handle multi-solution constraint satisfaction problems (e.g., N-Queens, Graph Coloring) where deterministic models suffer from mode collapse.
- **Generative Modeling through Recursion:** The work illustrates GRAM's capability as an unconditional generative model, capable of producing high-quality, diverse, and constrained outputs (e.g., valid Sudoku boards, binarized MNIST images) by leveraging its iterative refinement process. The quality of generation improves monotonically with recursive depth, suggesting new directions for generative modeling beyond conditional reasoning.

**Potential Impact:**
The principles introduced by GRAM could benefit various fields requiring sophisticated reasoning and multi-hypothesis exploration. This includes constraint satisfaction problems, scientific discovery, and decision-making systems where maintaining uncertainty and considering alternative solutions are crucial. By showing that increased inference-time computation, through architectural innovation rather than just parameter scaling, can enhance reasoning, GRAM suggests a path for developing more capable and efficient AI systems.

**Limitations:**
Despite its contributions, the paper identifies certain limitations:
- **Training Efficiency:** The sequential nature of deep supervision in GRAM can limit its training efficiency compared to highly parallelizable Transformer architectures, posing a barrier to scaling the model to the size of modern large foundation models.
- **Risk of Plausible but Invalid Generations:** In real-world or high-stakes applications, the model's ability to generate plausible but potentially invalid solutions could pose risks if not accompanied by rigorous validation, uncertainty calibration, and domain-specific safeguards.
- **Computational Cost of Multi-Sample Inference:** While parallel sampling offers benefits, it increases the overall computational and energy costs at scale, which needs to be considered for practical deployment.
