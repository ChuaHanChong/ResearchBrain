# Adaptive NMS: Refining Pedestrian Detection in a Crowd

> [!summary] Summary
> An adaptive Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS) algorithm was developed to dynamically adjust suppression thresholds based on local pedestrian density, improving detection performance in crowded environments. This method reduced the miss rate by 1.6% for Faster R-CNN on the CityPersons dataset and achieved 10.8% MR-2 on the CityPersons validation set when integrated with AggLoss and larger input scales.

## Problem

- State-of-the-art pedestrian detectors experience significant performance degradation in highly crowded environments.
- Traditional greedy-NMS with a fixed Intersection over Union (IoU) threshold faces a dilemma in crowds, either suppressing true positives (low threshold) or introducing many false positives (high threshold).
- Existing NMS enhancements like Soft-NMS or learning-based methods do not fully resolve the specific issue of preserving highly-overlapped true positives in dense crowds.

## Method

- Introduces **Adaptive-NMS**, which dynamically adjusts the ==NMS suppression threshold== (NMN_MNM) by taking the maximum of a base threshold (NtN_tNt) and a predicted instance-specific object density (dMd_MdM).
- An auxiliary, dedicated ==density prediction subnet== predicts the object density (dMd_MdM) for each bounding box proposal, framed as a regression task using ==Smooth-L1 loss==.
- The density prediction subnet incorporates contextual information from surrounding objects using a large 5x5 convolutional kernel to accurately estimate local crowd density.

## Results

- Adaptive-NMS consistently reduced the log-average Miss Rate (MR−2^{-2}−2): for Faster R-CNN on CityPersons, MR−2^{-2}−2 improved from **14.5%** to **12.9%** (**1.6%** reduction).
- Performance gains were concentrated in high-density pedestrian regions, confirming the method's specific efficacy in crowded scenarios while maintaining baseline performance in sparse areas.
- When combined with AggLoss and increased input scale, adaptive-NMS achieved **10.8%** MR−2^{-2}−2 on the CityPersons validation set, reporting a competitive performance against state-of-the-art methods.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Fixed NMS thresholds are fundamentally inadequate for crowded pedestrian scenes, necessitating a context-aware approach to avoid suppressing legitimate detections.
> - Local object density provides critical information for adaptively setting the NMS threshold, allowing for more lenient suppression in genuinely crowded regions while remaining strict in sparse areas.
> - A separate, learnable density prediction module, integrated into existing detection pipelines, can effectively capture the necessary contextual cues to guide adaptive NMS.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Pedestrian detection constitutes a specialized branch within the broader field of general object detection. Historically, this area has progressed from methods relying on hand-crafted features to sophisticated deep learning architectures, particularly Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs). These CNN-based approaches have substantially advanced performance on public benchmarks.

Despite significant progress in general object detection, a specific and persistent challenge remains: detecting pedestrians in highly crowded environments. Real-world scenarios such as airports, train stations, and shopping malls frequently present high-density pedestrian scenes where existing state-of-the-art detectors exhibit notable performance degradation. This problem is distinct from general object occlusion, as crowded pedestrians often share similar appearances and experience significant mutual occlusion. For instance, a method like OR-CNN, which performs well on datasets like Caltech (4.1% Miss Rate), sees its performance degrade to 11.0% Miss Rate on CityPersons, where a substantial portion of pedestrians are highly overlapped.

A critical post-processing step in nearly all modern object detection pipelines, irrespective of whether they are single-stage (e.g., SSD, YOLO, RetinaNet) or two-stage (e.g., Faster R-CNN, R-FCN) architectures, is Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS). NMS is employed to filter out redundant bounding box predictions and reduce false positives. However, traditional greedy-NMS, which uses a fixed Intersection over Union (IoU) threshold, encounters a fundamental limitation in crowded scenarios. A low NMS threshold may inadvertently suppress true positive detections that are highly overlapped (common in crowds), leading to an increased miss rate. Conversely, a high NMS threshold, while potentially preserving more true positives, can introduce a greater number of false positives due to the correlated scores often assigned by CNNs to neighboring regions around an object.

Previous research efforts have attempted to address these issues. Some approaches, such as Repulsion Loss and aggregation penalties, focus on modifying the detection loss function to encourage detectors to produce more compact bounding boxes, thereby making them less sensitive to the NMS threshold. Other methods, like Soft-NMS, aim to mitigate the harshness of greedy-NMS by gradually decreasing the scores of overlapping boxes rather than immediately discarding them. There have also been attempts to learn the NMS function directly from data using dedicated neural networks or to formulate NMS as an optimization problem. However, the authors argue that these existing solutions often do not fully resolve the specific dilemma posed by highly-overlapped true positives in dense crowds, as Soft-NMS still penalizes overlapped boxes and learning-based methods can be complex or inefficient in scenarios with a vast number of highly-overlapped proposals.

This work specifically positions itself to address the NMS problem in crowded pedestrian scenes by proposing an adaptive NMS mechanism. This mechanism dynamically adjusts the suppression threshold based on the local density of targets, aiming to retain legitimate highly-overlapped detections while continuing to suppress false positives effectively. This approach seeks to overcome the limitations of fixed-threshold NMS, Soft-NMS, and certain learning-based NMS variants by explicitly accounting for the crowd context.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to improve pedestrian detection performance, particularly in highly crowded scenes, by developing a refined Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS) algorithm. The motivation for this work stems from several challenges identified within current pedestrian detection systems:

- **Performance Degradation in Crowds:** State-of-the-art pedestrian detectors, despite their general effectiveness, experience significant performance drops when applied to crowded environments. This is a crucial limitation for real-world applications that frequently involve high pedestrian densities, such as in urban surveillance, autonomous driving, or public safety monitoring.
- **Limitations of Fixed-Threshold NMS:** The widely adopted greedy-NMS algorithm employs a fixed Intersection over Union (IoU) threshold to filter redundant bounding boxes. This fixed threshold presents a fundamental dilemma in crowded scenes:
 - **Low Threshold Issue:** A low threshold (e.g., 0.5) can lead to the erroneous suppression of true positive bounding boxes that legitimately overlap significantly due to the presence of multiple, closely packed individuals. This results in an increased miss rate for crowded pedestrians.
 - **High Threshold Issue:** Conversely, a high threshold (e.g., 0.7) might preserve these highly-overlapped true positives but simultaneously introduce a substantial number of false positives. This occurs because deep learning detectors often assign correlated high confidence scores to multiple overlapping proposals around an object, making it difficult for a high threshold to differentiate true positives from their associated redundant detections.
- **Inadequacy of Existing NMS Enhancements:**
 - Methods that encourage compact bounding box predictions aim to reduce sensitivity to NMS thresholds. However, achieving perfectly compact and discriminative predictions for all instances in a crowd remains challenging, as CNN features for neighboring individuals can be highly similar.
 - Soft-NMS attempts to alleviate the harshness of greedy-NMS by gradually decaying scores rather than abruptly discarding boxes. However, the core hypothesis of Soft-NMS—that highly overlapped boxes are more likely to be false positives—still assigns a greater penalty to highly overlapped boxes, which is problematic when those overlaps represent distinct individuals in a crowd.
 - Learning-based NMS methods or optimization-based approaches, while effective in general object detection, may be inefficient or rely on discriminative features that are hard to obtain for highly similar and occluded individuals in crowds.
- **Need for Context-Aware Suppression:** The authors argue that an ideal NMS strategy for crowds should adapt its behavior. It should be lenient with highly overlapped detections in genuinely crowded regions (where high overlap indicates multiple true positives) but remain strict in sparse regions (where high overlap is more indicative of redundant false positives).

Therefore, the key motivation is to develop an NMS algorithm, termed "adaptive-NMS," that dynamically adjusts its suppression threshold based on the local density of objects. This adaptive strategy aims to retain more true positives in crowded conditions while simultaneously minimizing false positives across varying scene densities, thereby overcoming the limitations of fixed, global NMS thresholds.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology introduces "adaptive-NMS," a novel Non-Maximum Suppression algorithm designed to dynamically adjust its suppression threshold based on the perceived density of objects in a given region.

#### 3.1. Adaptive-NMS Algorithm

The core concept of adaptive-NMS modifies the conventional greedy-NMS by replacing the static NMS threshold ($N_t$) with a dynamic, instance-specific threshold ($N_M$). This adaptive threshold is determined by:
$N_M := \max(N_t, d_M)$,
where $N_t$ is a predefined base NMS threshold (e.g., 0.5), and $d_M$ represents the "object density" associated with the bounding box $M$ that currently has the highest confidence score.

The object density, $d_i$, for a given ground truth object $i$, is quantitatively defined as the maximum Intersection over Union (IoU) between that object's bounding box ($b_i$) and any other ground truth object's bounding box ($b_j$) in the set $G$:
$d_i := \max_{b_j \in G, i \neq j} \operatorname{iou}(b_i, b_j)$.
This definition allows the algorithm to gauge the level of crowd occlusion surrounding an individual.

The adaptive suppression strategy operates as follows:
- For any candidate bounding box $b_i$ that is being considered for suppression relative to the highest-scoring box $M$:
 - If $\operatorname{iou}(M, b_i) < N_t$, the box $b_i$ is retained without modification (its score is unchanged or it is passed to the next stage). This is consistent with standard NMS behavior for distantly located boxes.
 - If $M$ is located in a crowded region, meaning its associated density $d_M$ is greater than the base threshold $N_t$, then $N_M$ becomes $d_M$. In this scenario, the algorithm applies a lighter penalty or retains neighboring proposals that have an IoU with $M$ greater than or equal to $N_t$ but less than $d_M$. This helps preserve other true positives that are highly overlapped with $M$.
 - If $M$ is in a sparse region, where $d_M \leq N_t$, then $N_M$ defaults to $N_t$. The suppression process then behaves identically to original greedy-NMS, effectively removing very close boxes as false positives.

The authors state that adaptive-NMS maintains the same computational complexity as traditional greedy-NMS and Soft-NMS. The only additional cost is storing an N-element list for predicted densities, which is considered negligible. It is also designed to be compatible with both greedy-NMS (default for comparisons) and Soft-NMS re-scoring functions.

#### 3.2. Density Prediction Subnet

To implement adaptive-NMS, the density $d_M$ for each bounding box proposal must be predicted. This is framed as a regression task, with training optimized using the Smooth-L1 loss.

A dedicated, auxiliary sub-network is designed for this density prediction. The rationale for a separate subnet, rather than simply adding a head layer to existing detection features, is that density prediction requires contextual information about surrounding objects, which individual object features typically lack.

The architecture of the density prediction subnet consists of three convolutional layers and is designed for compatibility with both two-stage and one-stage detectors:
- **For two-stage detectors (e.g., Faster R-CNN):** The subnet is integrated after the Region Proposal Network (RPN). It takes as input a reduced-dimension convolutional feature map (obtained via a 1x1 convolution), along with the objectness predictions and bounding box predictions from the RPN.
- **For one-stage detectors:** The subnet is deployed behind the final detection network, consuming similar types of feature maps and predictions.
- **Contextual Information:** A key design element is the use of a large kernel (5x5) in the final convolutional layer of the density subnet. This larger receptive field enables the network to effectively capture and incorporate information from the surrounding regions, which is crucial for accurately estimating the local object density.

#### 3.3. Experimental Setup

Experiments were conducted on two pedestrian detection datasets known for their crowded scenes: CityPersons and CrowdHuman.
- **Evaluation Metric:** The primary metric used is the log-average Miss Rate (MR or MR$^{-2}$) over a False Positive Per Image (FPPI) range of $[10^{-2}, 10^0]$, consistent with pedestrian detection benchmarks.
- **Baseline Detectors:**
 - **Two-stage:** Adapted Faster R-CNN with a VGG-16 backbone (for CityPersons) and Faster R-CNN with Feature Pyramid Network (FPN) and ResNet-50 backbone (for CrowdHuman).
 - **One-stage:** Modified RFB Net with a VGG-16 backbone.
- **Training Details:** Standard SGD optimization with specific learning rate schedules, momentum, and weight decay tailored to each detector and dataset. All new convolutional layers in the density subnet were initialized using the MSRA method.
- **Ablation Studies:** Comparisons were performed against greedy-NMS and Soft-NMS (linear method) with various fixed thresholds and parameters to isolate the contribution of adaptive-NMS.
- **State-of-the-Art Comparisons:** For competitive benchmarking, adaptive-NMS was also combined with an "AggLoss" term (which encourages compact proposals) and evaluated with larger input image scales.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation demonstrated the effectiveness of the proposed adaptive-NMS method in improving pedestrian detection, particularly in crowded scenes.

#### 4.1. Ablation Study on Adaptive-NMS

- **CityPersons Dataset:**
 - For the Faster R-CNN detector, adaptive-NMS reduced the MR$^{-2}$ from 14.5% (with greedy-NMS) to 12.9%, representing a 1.6% improvement.
 - For the RFB Net detector, adaptive-NMS reduced the MR$^{-2}$ from 13.9% (with greedy-NMS) to 12.7%, a 1.2% improvement.
 - Soft-NMS, when evaluated with its "linear" method, showed only minor or negative improvements on the MR$^{-2}$ metric (e.g., 14.2% for Faster R-CNN, 14.2% for RFB Net), which the authors attributed to Soft-NMS retaining low-score detections that may fall outside the relevant FPPI range for evaluation.
- **CrowdHuman Dataset:**
 - For the FPN detector, adaptive-NMS reduced the MR$^{-2}$ from a baseline of 50.42% to 49.73%, an improvement of 2.62% compared to the FPN baseline reported in the dataset.
 - For the RFB Net detector, adaptive-NMS reduced the MR$^{-2}$ from 65.22% to 63.03%, an improvement of 2.19%.

These results indicate that adaptive-NMS consistently reduces the miss rate across different detector architectures and highly crowded datasets, suggesting its efficacy as a post-processing algorithm for pedestrian detection in dense scenarios.

#### 4.2. Analysis of Performance Across Density Levels

To further understand the impact of adaptive-NMS, pedestrians on the CityPersons validation set were categorized into five density subsets based on their maximum IoU with other ground truth objects.
- For sparse pedestrians (density $\leq$ 0.4), adaptive-NMS showed similar performance to greedy-NMS and Soft-NMS.
- As the pedestrian density increased (e.g., density > 0.4 up to > 0.7), adaptive-NMS significantly reduced the miss rate compared to both greedy-NMS and Soft-NMS. This finding confirms that the proposed method specifically targets and improves detection performance in crowded regions by effectively preserving more highly-overlapped true positives.
- Visual comparisons (Figure 5) illustrate that adaptive-NMS successfully retains more crowded true positives while simultaneously removing false positives in sparse areas.

#### 4.3. Comparison to State-of-the-Art

The authors demonstrate that adaptive-NMS can be combined with existing techniques to achieve competitive results.
- **CityPersons Validation Set:**
 - With a standard input scale (×1), adaptive-NMS improved baseline detectors to achieve results comparable to other advanced pedestrian detectors without requiring additional complex modules.
 - When combined with AggLoss (designed to enforce compact proposals), the Faster R-CNN detector with adaptive-NMS achieved 11.9% MR$^{-2}$, outperforming OR-CNN (12.8% MR$^{-2}$) which uses a similar backbone (VGG-16) and scale.
 - With an increased input image scale (×1.3) and AggLoss, the Faster R-CNN detector with adaptive-NMS achieved 10.8% MR$^{-2}$, which was reported as a state-of-the-art performance on the CityPersons validation set at the time of publication.
- **CityPersons Test Set:**
 - With a ×1.3 scale and AggLoss, the Faster R-CNN detector achieved 11.79% MR$^{-2}$. Adaptive-NMS further improved this to 11.40% MR$^{-2}$. This result is competitive with other methods that use stronger backbones (e.g., Repulsion Loss with ResNet-50) or specialized occlusion-aware modules (e.g., OR-CNN).

#### 4.4. Identified Limitations

The authors noted that while adaptive-NMS effectively handles crowds, it can still preserve more false positives if the initial bounding box proposals from the detector are not sufficiently compact. This observation (Figure 6) suggests that the quality of initial detector predictions remains an important factor, particularly when the NMS threshold is adaptively raised for crowded instances.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research makes several contributions that have implications for the field of computer vision, particularly in robust object detection.

- **Addresses a Critical Problem in Pedestrian Detection:** The work directly confronts the known weakness of pedestrian detectors in crowded environments, a scenario frequently encountered in real-world applications. By specifically tackling the limitations of Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS) in dense crowds, the adaptive-NMS approach offers a targeted solution to a persistent challenge.
- **Enhanced Accuracy in Crowded Scenes:** The demonstrated significant reductions in miss rate, particularly in high-density areas, indicate that adaptive-NMS can lead to more reliable and comprehensive detection of individuals within congested scenes. This improvement is crucial for applications requiring high recall in challenging visual conditions.
- **Generalizability and Ease of Integration:** The adaptive-NMS algorithm is presented as a post-processing step that is compatible with both single-stage and two-stage object detection architectures. Its integration requires an auxiliary, learnable sub-network for density prediction, but the core NMS mechanism remains computationally efficient, akin to traditional greedy-NMS. This ease of integration and low computational overhead suggest that it can be readily adopted by existing and future detection systems without extensive architectural modifications or significant performance penalties.
- **Novel Approach to NMS:** By introducing the concept of a dynamic, instance-specific NMS threshold learned from local object density, this work moves beyond static global thresholds and the uniform penalty applications of methods like Soft-NMS. This context-aware approach to suppression offers a more nuanced way to handle overlapping detections.
- **Advancement of State-of-the-Art:** The achievement of state-of-the-art or highly competitive results on challenging crowd pedestrian benchmarks such as CityPersons and CrowdHuman validates the practical utility and effectiveness of the proposed method, pushing the performance boundaries in this specialized domain.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The method of learning and leveraging local density information to guide post-processing opens avenues for further research. This principle could potentially be extended to other object detection tasks where inter-object occlusion or high density presents similar challenges, or to inform other stages of the detection pipeline.
