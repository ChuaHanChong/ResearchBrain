# Fast Non-Episodic Adaptive Tuning of Robot Controllers with Online Policy Optimization

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from UC Santa Barbara and Caltech developed and validated M-GAPS, a model-based, non-episodic online policy optimization algorithm, enabling robot controllers to continuously adapt in real-time to unpredictable environmental changes and rapidly tune parameters on hardware. The algorithm demonstrated fast convergence, reducing tracking errors by over 5x in ~10 seconds on an Ackermann car and showing robust adaptation to wind and payload disturbances on a quadrotor.

## Problem

- Robot controllers struggle to continuously adapt to unpredictable and rapidly changing real-world environments and task requirements (e.g., wind, payload changes).
- Existing adaptive control methods are often too restrictive, requiring specific policy structures and limited to basic cost functions.
- Model-free reinforcement learning is data-inefficient, often relies on stationary dynamics or artificial episodes, and doesn't leverage available approximate models for robotics.

## Method

- Proposed and empirically validated **M-GAPS**, a ==non-episodic, model-based online policy optimization== algorithm, which computes ==approximate gradients of a surrogate cost== in O(1) time per step.
- Implemented **M-GAPS** on a quadrotor and an Ackermann-steered car, utilizing an approximate dynamics model, a standard ==nonlinear geometric control policy==, and a ==logarithmic reparameterization of gains==.
- Compared **M-GAPS** against episodic model-based (==DiffTune==) and model-free (==OPRF==) policy optimization algorithms on real hardware, assessing performance in detuned, disturbed, and rapidly tuning scenarios.

## Results

- **M-GAPS** achieved the fastest convergence from detuned initialization, reaching near-optimal performance within **8-12 seconds** for quadrotor trajectory tracking.
- The algorithm demonstrated robust adaptation to large disturbances, significantly reducing position tracking error in both time-varying wind and a **60%** quadrotor mass increase, outperforming fixed expert controllers.
- **M-GAPS** rapidly tuned a nonlinear controller for an Ackermann-steered car, reducing tracking error by over **5x** within approximately **10 seconds** on a new robotic platform.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Non-episodic, model-based online policy optimization (M-GAPS) offers a superior balance of flexibility, data efficiency, and real-time adaptivity compared to both restrictive adaptive control and data-hungry RL.
> - Continuous, single-trajectory adaptation (M-GAPS) avoids the pitfalls of episodic methods, such as sensitivity to episode length and large, discrete parameter updates that can induce instability.
> - Utilizing approximate models with efficient analytic gradient computation (enabled by tools like SymForce) makes complex online optimization feasible for real-time execution on resource-constrained embedded systems.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work addresses the problem of online optimization for parameterized nonlinear robot control policies in dynamic and unpredictable environments. The broader research landscape for robot control policies includes traditional adaptive control, various forms of reinforcement learning (RL), and emerging approaches from online optimization theory. This paper positions itself at the intersection of these fields, aiming to overcome limitations of existing methods.

Traditional adaptive control methods often require a co-designed policy and adaptive law, restricting their applicability to specific structures of unknown time-varying dynamics and limiting their ability to optimize general cost functions beyond quadratic tracking. While some advancements have relaxed these conditions or focused on specific robot types like quadrotors, the policy class often remains prescribed, which is a key limitation.

Reinforcement learning (RL) offers greater generality, capable of optimizing a wide range of policy classes without explicit model knowledge. However, many popular RL methods, particularly those relying on replay memory, assume stationarity in dynamics or costs, making them less suitable for rapidly changing, non-episodic scenarios. Policy gradient RL, while avoiding replay memory issues, often assumes unknown dynamics and costs, leading to slow and data-inefficient learning that can hinder rapid adaptivity in real-world applications. For many robotic systems, engineers possess approximate dynamics models and design explicit cost functions, rendering a purely model-free approach suboptimal in terms of data efficiency.

The recent application of ideas from online optimization to control problems has shown promise for handling time-varying dynamics and costs. Initial work in this area primarily focused on linear time-invariant systems or prescribed specialized policy classes. More recent efforts have explored optimizing arbitrary policy classes, particularly when closed-loop dynamics exhibit contractiveness. These theoretical developments, however, often provide local regret guarantees for nonlinear systems, leaving the practical performance in complex robotic systems to be empirically validated. Furthermore, many online optimization approaches implicitly assume an episodic structure or require computational resources that are not feasible for onboard real-time execution.

This research aims to bridge the gap between the restrictiveness of classical adaptive control and the data inefficiency and computational demands of general RL, especially in non-episodic, continuously adapting settings. It explores a class of online policy optimization algorithms that can leverage approximate models (where available) while operating in real-time on hardware, without relying on artificial episodes or stationarity assumptions. The work specifically investigates whether such methods can provide both the flexibility needed for general nonlinear dynamics and costs, and the efficiency and stability required for practical robotic deployment.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to study and experimentally evaluate online algorithms for tuning the parameters of a robot controller in a demanding scenario where the system dynamics, the policy class, and the optimality objective can all vary over time and are not known in advance. The core constraint is that the system operates on a single, continuous trajectory without artificial episodes or state resets, necessitating continuous and immediate adaptation.

The motivations for this work are multi-faceted:

- **Addressing Unpredictable Real-World Changes:** Robots deployed in real-world environments frequently encounter unpredictable changes in their surroundings (e.g., wind, terrain, payload variations) and task requirements. These changes necessitate immediate and continuous adaptation of their control policies. Traditional controllers, often tuned for specific nominal conditions, may exhibit degraded performance or instability when faced with significant deviations from these conditions.
- **Limitations of Existing Control Paradigms:**
 - **Adaptive Control:** While designed for unknown parameters, classical adaptive control is often too restrictive. It typically requires co-design of the policy and the adaptation law, limiting its application to specific control policy structures and primarily optimizing quadratic tracking errors. This constraint makes it unsuitable for the general nonlinear dynamics and complex cost functions prevalent in modern robotics.
 - **Reinforcement Learning (RL):** Model-free RL, while general, often suffers from data inefficiency, requiring extensive interaction with the environment. Many RL algorithms rely on replay memories or stationarity assumptions, which are incompatible with rapidly time-varying dynamics or the single-trajectory, non-episodic nature of continuous robot operation. Policy gradient methods can avoid some of these issues but still typically assume unknown dynamics and costs, leading to slower learning compared to model-based approaches.
- **Leveraging Available Information:** For many practical robotic systems, engineers often possess an approximate dynamics model and a well-defined cost function. Model-free RL methods often fail to utilize this valuable information, resulting in suboptimal learning speed and data efficiency. This work seeks methods that can incorporate such partial knowledge.
- **Hardware Feasibility:** The algorithms must be efficient enough to run onboard a microcontroller in real-time, which is a constraint that often rules out computationally intensive methods common in simulation-based RL or complex optimal control synthesis.
- **Empirical Validation of Online Optimization Theory:** While theoretical frameworks for online policy optimization exist, especially for nonlinear systems, they often provide local guarantees (e.g., local regret bounds) or rely on strong assumptions (e.g., contractiveness) that are challenging to verify or may not preclude practical issues like getting stuck in local minima. Empirical validation on hardware is therefore crucial to confirm the practical utility and robustness of these theoretical concepts in complex robotic settings.
- **Specific Goal for Quadrotor Control:** As a case study, the research focuses on nonlinear trajectory tracking control for quadrotors, a common and challenging problem that benefits significantly from adaptable controllers. The objective is to propose a practical implementation of a single-trajectory model-based online policy optimization algorithm, M-GAPS, for this specific application, alongside reparameterizations of the quadrotor state space and policy class to improve the optimization landscape.

In summary, the motivation is to develop a class of online policy optimization algorithms that offer a balance between the flexibility of general policy classes, the data efficiency of model-based approaches, and the real-time adaptivity required for robust robot operation in unpredicted, continuously changing environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on implementing and comparing three online policy optimization algorithms—M-GAPS, DiffTune, and OPRF—on hardware, using a quadrotor and an Ackermann-steered car as test platforms.

**Online Protocol:**
The research defines a discrete-time, time-varying online process. At each timestep `t`, the learner observes the current state `x_t`. It then selects a policy parameter `θ_t` to determine an action `u_t = π_t(x_t, θ_t)`. Subsequently, it incurs a cost `c_t = f_t(x_t, u_t)` and the state evolves according to `x_{t+1} = g_t(x_t, u_t)`. Crucially, the learner also observes the partial derivatives of `π_t`, `f_t`, and `g_t` with respect to `(x_t, u_t, θ_t)`. This process repeats along a single trajectory for `T` timesteps, without any state resets or episodes. The objective is to minimize the total cumulative cost.

**Algorithms Implemented and Compared:**

1. **M-GAPS (Model-based, Non-episodic):** This algorithm is a practical implementation of the theoretical framework from.
 - **Theoretical Foundation:** It aims to perform online gradient descent (OGD) on a "surrogate cost" `F_t(θ)`, which represents the cost at time `t` if a constant policy parameter `θ` had been applied for all previous timesteps.
 - **Computational Challenge:** Directly computing the gradient `∇F_t(θ)` requires re-simulating the entire state trajectory from `t=0` to `t`, leading to `Ω(t)` computational cost per step and requiring oracle access to past system functions.
 - **M-GAPS Approximation:** To overcome this, M-GAPS maintains an internal state `y_t` (an `n x d` matrix) that approximates the sensitivity `∂x_t/∂θ_t`, which is updated recursively in `O(1)` time per step. This approximation assumes that the closed-loop dynamics are "contractive" and that `θ_t` changes slowly enough.
 - **Update Rule:** `θ_{t+1} = θ_t - ηG_t`, where `G_t` is the approximate gradient computed using `y_t` and local derivatives.

2. **DiffTune (Model-based, Episodic):**
 - **Approach:** This method imposes artificial episodes of length `H`. Within each episode, a constant parameter `θ_k` is deployed.
 - **Gradient Computation:** It computes the gradient of the cumulative cost within the episode analytically, using the model for derivatives. Similar to M-GAPS, it leverages a sensitivity state `y` to efficiently compute this gradient over the episode's duration, assuming the initial state of each episode is fixed (ignoring the influence of previous policies).
 - **Update Rule:** `θ_{k+1} = θ_k - ηG_pg`, where `G_pg` is the computed episodic gradient.

3. **OPRF (Model-free, Episodic):**
 - **Approach:** This algorithm is a model-free, parameter-space policy gradient method, also using artificial episodes of length `H`.
 - **Gradient Estimation:** It uses "One-Point Residual Feedback" (OPRF) to estimate the gradient. For each episode, a perturbed parameter `θ'_k = θ_{k-1} + εh_k` is sampled and deployed, and the cumulative cost `J_k` is observed. The gradient estimate uses the difference between current and past cumulative costs, `(J_k - J_{k-1})h_k / ε`.
 - **Applicability:** Chosen for its potential to reduce variance in single-point queries for gradually changing costs, and suitability for low-dimensional parameter spaces.

**Quadrotor Case Study Details:**

- **Dynamics Model:** The quadrotor state includes position (`p`), velocity (`v`), logarithmic rotation coordinate (`r` on so(3)), and angular velocity (`ω` on so(3)). Dynamics are discretized using Lie group integration for rotation and forward Euler for other states. The model assumes unit mass and identity moment of inertia.
- **Policy Class:** A standard nonlinear geometric control structure is used, featuring an outer-loop position controller (generating a desired thrust vector `z`) and an inner-loop attitude controller (orienting the body towards `z`). Key parameters (`K_i, K_p, K_v, K_r, K_ω`) are diagonal gain matrices. A `softclamp` function is applied to angular acceleration commands for practical actuation limits.
- **Reparameterization:** To improve the optimization landscape, a logarithmic reparameterization (`θ = log(ϑ)`) is used for the positive-definite feedback gains, addressing their potentially wide range of magnitudes and non-uniform sensitivities.
- **Cost Function:** The objective function primarily penalizes squared position tracking error, with smaller regularization terms for velocity error, angular velocity error, torque, and thrust to prevent oscillations and excessive control effort.
- **Hardware and Software:** Experiments were conducted on a Bitcraze Crazyflie 2.0 quadrotor running at 500 Hz on its onboard microcontroller. Analytic derivatives for model-based methods were generated using the SymForce package, which facilitates symbolic differentiation of Lie algebraic operations and C++ code generation.

**Experimental Scenarios:**

1. **Detuned Initialization:** Algorithms were initialized with a suboptimal parameter set (halved gains compared to an expert-tuned baseline, `θ_m - log 2`). Performance was evaluated on an aggressive figure-8 trajectory, comparing quasi-regret against the expert `θ_m`. This scenario aimed to test initial convergence speed and the impact of episode length for episodic methods.
2. **Large Disturbances:**
 - **Time-varying wind:** A periodic wind disturbance was generated using fans, and a cardboard panel was attached to the quadrotor to amplify the effect. The quadrotor flew a linear back-and-forth pattern. This tested short-term adaptivity and robustness to unmodeled dynamics.
 - **Heavy payload:** A 23g steel weight was attached to the 39g quadrotor (60% mass increase) while the dynamics model remained nominal. A horizontally-oriented circular trajectory was flown. This tested robustness to significant, constant modeling errors.
3. **Ackermann-Steered Car Experiment:** M-GAPS was deployed on a 1:6-scale Ackermann-steered car, tuning a nonlinear controller for circular trajectory tracking on grass and concrete. This aimed to demonstrate the versatility of M-GAPS across different robotic platforms and its utility for automatic tuning on new hardware.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation on both quadrotor and Ackermann-steered car platforms yielded several findings regarding the performance of online policy optimization algorithms.

**Detuned Initialization (Quadrotor Figure-8 Tracking):**
- **M-GAPS Performance:** M-GAPS demonstrated the fastest convergence to near-optimal performance. When initialized with suboptimal parameters, it reduced tracking costs significantly, achieving a quasi-regret close to zero relative to the expert-tuned controller within approximately 8-12 seconds (2-3 laps of the figure-8 trajectory). Its trajectory visibly improved, approaching the expert's path rapidly. The parameter evolution for M-GAPS showed a relatively smooth adaptation towards improved gains.
- **DiffTune Sensitivity to Episode Length:** The performance of DiffTune was highly dependent on the chosen episode length. With an optimal, hindsight-determined episode length, DiffTune performed comparably to M-GAPS in terms of overall cumulative cost, though it still exhibited a larger constant quasi-regret. However, with suboptimal episode lengths, its performance degraded significantly, and the quasi-regret continued to increase towards the end of the experiment. DiffTune's parameter updates were characterized by large, step-like changes at episode boundaries, which sometimes induced visible jitter in the quadrotor's attitude.
- **OPRF Performance:** The model-free OPRF algorithm consistently lagged behind both model-based methods (M-GAPS and DiffTune). It showed some improvement from the detuned baseline but exhibited substantially higher quasi-regret and slower convergence. Its parameter evolution was noisy due to its reliance on randomized queries for gradient estimation.

**Large-Disturbance Experiments (Quadrotor):**
- **Time-Varying Wind Adaptivity:** In the presence of a periodic wind disturbance (fans toggled on/off), M-GAPS significantly outperformed the fixed expert-tuned controller (`θ_m`). It reduced position tracking error in both fan-on and fan-off phases. The algorithm demonstrated rapid adaptivity, with several policy parameters showing clear transient responses and adjustments when the fan phase switched, indicating an active response to the changing environmental conditions despite using a nominal dynamics model.
- **Heavy Payload Robustness:** When the quadrotor's mass was increased by 60% with an attached payload, M-GAPS substantially reduced the tracking error compared to the expert-tuned `θ_m` (which was not designed for such conditions). This outcome occurred even though the dynamics model used by M-GAPS was not updated to account for the increased mass, demonstrating robustness to significant and constant unmodeled dynamics.
- **Instance-Specific Adaptation:** A comparison of parameter evolution between the time-varying wind and heavy payload scenarios revealed distinct adaptation patterns. For instance, the vertical position gain (`k_z_p`) showed a large change in the payload scenario but minimal change in the wind scenario. This indicates that M-GAPS adapts specifically to the type of disturbance encountered, rather than making generic adjustments.

**Ackermann-Steered Car Experiment:**
- **Versatility and Rapid Tuning:** M-GAPS was successfully applied to an Ackermann-steered 1:6-scale off-road car. It effectively tuned a nonlinear controller, reducing the tracking error by a factor greater than 5 within approximately 10 seconds, which was less than one full period of the circular trajectory. This result indicates the algorithm's versatility across different robot platforms and its utility for rapid, automated controller tuning for new hardware or controller designs, even when continuous online adaptivity is not the primary objective.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of robotic control by demonstrating the practical applicability and advantages of a specific class of online policy optimization algorithms for real-world robotic systems.

**Hardware Practicality and Real-Time Adaptivity:** A primary significance is the empirical validation of the non-episodic, model-based M-GAPS algorithm on actual hardware (Crazyflie quadrotor and an Ackermann-steered car) operating in real-time. This demonstrates that such computationally efficient algorithms can be implemented onboard microcontrollers and perform complex controller tuning and adaptation at high frequencies (e.g., 500 Hz). The ability of M-GAPS to rapidly adapt to unpredictable, time-varying disturbances like wind and payload changes, and to continuously tune parameters without artificial episodes, offers a notable advancement over controllers requiring manual re-tuning or those based on offline optimization.

**Bridging Gaps in Control Paradigms:** The work positions M-GAPS as an approach that effectively addresses limitations inherent in both classical adaptive control and model-free reinforcement learning. It provides more flexibility than typical adaptive control by optimizing general nonlinear policy classes and cost functions, without rigid structural requirements. Simultaneously, it offers improved data efficiency and stability compared to model-free RL by leveraging approximate dynamics models and operating in a continuous, non-episodic manner, avoiding the data-hungry nature and stationarity assumptions often associated with RL. This suggests a middle ground that is suitable for many practical robotic applications where some model knowledge is available but perfect models are not.

**Automated and General-Purpose Controller Tuning:** Beyond online adaptivity, the experiments on detuned initialization and the Ackermann-steered car demonstrate M-GAPS's utility as a general tool for automated controller tuning. It can quickly bring a poorly initialized or newly designed controller to a high-performance state, significantly reducing the manual effort typically involved in tuning complex robotic systems. This has potential impact on the development cycle for new robotic platforms and control strategies.

**Robustness to Model Mismatch:** The results in the large-disturbance scenarios (wind and heavy payload) highlight M-GAPS's robustness. The algorithm achieved substantial performance improvements even when the actual system dynamics deviated significantly from the nominal model (e.g., 60% mass increase). This suggests that the model-based approach can tolerate certain levels of model error, which is crucial for real-world deployments where perfect models are rarely attainable.

**Leveraging Differentiable Computing:** The use of tools like SymForce for symbolic differentiation and C++ code generation underscores the growing impact of differentiable programming and simulation techniques in robotics. These tools enable the efficient computation of analytical gradients on embedded systems, making sophisticated model-based online optimization methods practically viable.

**Future Research Directions:** The paper acknowledges limitations, such as the applicability to systems with discontinuities (e.g., walking robots) and the theoretical challenges in verifying contractiveness for complex nonlinear systems. These points suggest avenues for future research, including extending the framework to handle non-smooth dynamics and developing more practical methods for theoretical guarantees or empirical validation in highly complex scenarios. The local regret guarantees, while valuable, do not preclude poor local minima, which necessitates continued empirical testing in diverse applications.

In conclusion, this research provides strong empirical evidence for the utility of fast, non-episodic, model-based online policy optimization for robust and adaptive robot control. Its impact lies in offering a more flexible, data-efficient, and practically deployable solution for a wide range of real-world robotic challenges characterized by time-varying and unpredictable conditions.
