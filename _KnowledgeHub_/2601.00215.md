# From Sight to Insight: Improving Visual Reasoning Capabilities of Multimodal Models via Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers identified that visual perception, not algorithmic reasoning, primarily limits multimodal large language models (MLLMs) on visual puzzles. They enhanced MLLMs' visual reasoning through a reinforcement learning framework using specially designed reward functions, successfully inducing multi-step, visually-grounded thought processes without reliance on costly human annotations.

## Problem

- Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) frequently underperform on visual reasoning tasks, such as solving visual puzzles, that demand precise visual perception and complex multi-step reasoning.
- MLLMs often exhibit 'weakly grounded' reasoning, failing to consistently interpret visual information and bypassing visual details in favor of abstract reasoning or direct answers.
- Existing RL-based approaches for multimodal reasoning often require dense intermediate supervision like executable tool outputs or human-annotated reasoning traces, which are expensive to obtain for complex visual tasks.

## Method

- Empirically determined that ==visual perception== is the primary bottleneck for MLLMs in solving visual puzzles by comparing performance on multimodal versus text-only inputs.
- Utilized ==Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)== to fine-tune the Qwen-2.5-VL-7B MLLM using synthetically generated, diverse visual puzzle datasets.
- Designed and evaluated ==six distinct reward functions== within a reinforcement learning framework to incentivize structured, multi-step, and explicitly visually-grounded reasoning without requiring costly human-annotated reasoning traces.

## Results

- MLLMs (Claude-3.5/3.7) showed substantial average performance gains of **23.6 to 26.7 percentage points** when visual information was converted to textual descriptions, confirming visual perception as the primary bottleneck.
- The 'Mixture' reward function achieved the highest average accuracy (**15.56%**) on the `Diverse-8k` dataset and successfully encouraged the MLLM to generate longer, structured, and visually-grounded reasoning trajectories across various categories.
- RL training on a diverse dataset significantly improved generalization across both in-domain and out-of-domain visual puzzle categories compared to the no-RL baseline, albeit with overall accuracy remaining modest on highly complex tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Visual perception is the fundamental bottleneck limiting MLLM performance on complex visual puzzles, as converting images to text significantly boosts their reasoning accuracy.
> - Reward-driven reinforcement learning, especially with 'Mixture' and 'Continuous' reward functions, can effectively induce longer, structured, and visually-grounded reasoning chains ('think with image') in MLLMs.
> - It is possible to foster emergent, multi-step reasoning capabilities in MLLMs purely through careful reward design, reducing dependency on expensive, dense intermediate supervision for complex multimodal tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research paper makes a significant contribution to the rapidly evolving field of multimodal artificial intelligence, particularly at the intersection of large language models, computer vision, and reinforcement learning. MLLMs have recently demonstrated impressive capabilities across a spectrum of tasks by integrating textual and visual information. However, their performance on tasks requiring deep visual perception and complex multi-step reasoning, such as solving visual puzzles, has consistently lagged behind their language-only counterparts or simpler vision tasks.

Prior work has highlighted that MLLMs often struggle with visual puzzles (Chia et al., 2024; Ghosal et al., 2025; Song et al., 2025). The broader landscape has seen the emergence of "chain-of-thought" (CoT) prompting (Wei et al., 2022; Kojima et al., 2022) to elicit reasoning in language models, and attempts have been made to extend these ideas to visual domains (Dong et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025a). However, these efforts often reveal that MLLMs generate reasoning that is "weakly grounded" in visual inputs (Fan et al., 2025a; Zhang et al., 2024). This paper contributes by systematically diagnosing this problem, empirically demonstrating that *visual perception* is the primary bottleneck, rather than solely algorithmic reasoning. This diagnostic insight is crucial for directing future research efforts more effectively.

Furthermore, the paper situates itself within the growing trend of using reinforcement learning (RL) to enhance the reasoning capabilities of large language models (Lightman et al., 2023; DeepSeek-AI, 2025; OpenAI, 2024). While RL has shown promise in eliciting reasoning chains in text-only models, its application to complex, visually-grounded reasoning in MLLMs is still an emerging area (Su et al., 2025). Many existing RL-based approaches for multimodal reasoning (e.g., Wang et al., 2025b; Ong et al., 2025; Fan et al., 2025b; Zheng et al., 2025c) rely on some form of intermediate supervision, such as executable tool outputs, bounding box annotations, or human-annotated reasoning traces. This work distinguishes itself by exploring whether *long, structured visual reasoning can be induced purely through reward design*, without requiring such costly, dense intermediate supervision. This is a significant research question, particularly for tasks like visual puzzles where high-quality, multi-hop, and visually-grounded reasoning annotations are challenging and expensive to obtain. By focusing on reward-driven RL and designing a suite of reward functions, the authors aim to unlock emergent reasoning behaviors in MLLMs, addressing a key limitation in the current paradigm of multimodal reasoning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind this research stems from the observed limitations of Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) when confronted with visual reasoning tasks that demand precise visual perception. While MLLMs have advanced considerably in many domains, they frequently underperform on tasks like visual puzzles, which necessitate both accurate visual interpretation and sophisticated algorithmic thinking. The authors identify a "dual bottleneck": MLLMs not only inadequately interpret visual information but also often fail to maintain consistent visual grounding throughout their reasoning processes, frequently bypassing visual details in favor of more abstract, algorithmic reasoning or jumping directly to an answer.

To address these challenges, the paper outlines three key research questions and objectives:

1. **RQ1: Identify the Bottleneck of MLLMs for Solving Visual Puzzles.** The first objective is to empirically determine whether the primary limitation of MLLMs in solving visual puzzles lies in their visual perception capabilities or their algorithmic reasoning abilities. This diagnostic aim is crucial for guiding targeted improvements. The hypothesis is that visual perception is the key bottleneck, and the authors sought to validate this by comparing MLLM performance on multimodal inputs versus text-only inputs (where images are converted into descriptive text).
2. **RQ2: Incentivize Multi-step, Structured Long Visual Reasoning using Reward-Driven Reinforcement Learning.** Given the observation that MLLMs struggle with visually-grounded reasoning and that longer reasoning trajectories can sometimes improve performance, the second objective is to explore how to encourage MLLMs to generate multi-step, structured reasoning that is explicitly grounded in visual information. Critically, this objective aims to achieve this *without requiring costly human-annotated reasoning traces* or external tools. The core approach involves designing and systematically evaluating various reward functions within a reinforcement learning framework to elicit this desired reasoning behavior.
3. **RQ3: Evaluate the Generalization Capabilities of RL-Trained Models.** The third objective is to assess how well the RL-trained MLLMs generalize their enhanced visual reasoning capabilities to both in-domain (seen during training) and out-of-domain (unseen during training) visual puzzle categories. This evaluates the robustness and transferability of the induced reasoning skills.

In essence, the paper is motivated by a desire to understand and overcome the current shortcomings of MLLMs in visual reasoning, proposing a novel RL-centric approach focused on intrinsic reward design to foster more robust and visually-grounded reasoning processes.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors employ a comprehensive methodology to investigate MLLM reasoning capabilities, focusing on bottleneck identification and the application of reward-driven reinforcement learning (RL) to enhance visual reasoning.

#### 3.1. Models and Datasets

For bottleneck analysis, the study utilizes closed-source commercial models: Claude-3.5-Sonnet and Claude-3.7-Sonnet, operating in "thinking mode." For RL training, the open-source Qwen-2.5-VL-7B (instruct) model is selected due to its strong visual reasoning capabilities among publicly available MLLMs.

The primary dataset is AlgoPuzzleVQA (Ghosal et al., 2025), a benchmark designed to evaluate MLLMs on challenging visual puzzles requiring visual perception, language comprehension, and algorithmic reasoning. Nine diverse categories are chosen for experiments: Clock, Checker Move, Maze, Move Box, N-Queens, Number Slide, Tower of Hanoi, Rotting Kiwi, and Water Jugs. To overcome the limited size of the original dataset (100 samples/category) for RL training, the authors synthetically generate 9,000 additional training samples across four categories (Clock, Maze, Move Box, N-Queens), ensuring no overlap with the original AlgoPuzzleVQA test set. Two training splits are created: `Clock-3k` (3,000 samples from the Clock category) and `Diverse-8k` (8,000 samples evenly distributed across the four selected categories).

#### 3.2. Bottleneck Experiment Setup

To isolate the source of MLLM limitations, experiments are conducted in two settings:
1. **Multimodal (<image, question>):** Standard MLLM input where both the image and question are provided.
2. **Text-only (<text-representing image, question>):** The visual information from the image is converted into a structured textual description (e.g., character grids for mazes, 'R'/'G' for checkers, time strings for clocks) and provided to the model alongside the question.
A unified prompt structure (Figure 2 in the paper) is used across both settings. Evaluation is strictly in an open-ended format using exact match accuracy to avoid models guessing from multiple-choice options.

#### 3.3. Reinforcement Learning Training

The Qwen-2.5-VL-7B model is fine-tuned using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) (Shao et al., 2024), an RL-based optimization method.

**Reward Function Design:** A core component of the methodology is the design and evaluation of six distinct reward functions, summarized in Table 1 and detailed in Section 2 of the paper:
1. **Vanilla:** A baseline balancing rewards for generating formatted reasoning (`<think>`) and answer accuracy (`<answer>`).
2. **Only-Accuracy:** Rewards solely for correct final answer, ignoring intermediate reasoning structure or content.
3. **Mixture:** A composite reward that incentivizes structured, multi-step reasoning by rewarding content within `<image description>`, `<think>`, `<rethink>` tags, alongside answer accuracy. It specifically measures the amount of *unique* reasoning content and penalizes repetition.
4. **Continuous:** Extends the Mixture reward by introducing partial credit for "near-correct" answers, particularly for numerical or time-based outputs, to guide reasoning more subtly.
5. **Visual-Fusion:** Focuses on explicit visual grounding by requiring and rewarding unique descriptions within a `<visual>` tag nested inside the `<think>` block.
6. **No-Accuracy:** An extreme setting that removes ground-truth supervision for the final answer. It rewards for generating unique content in reasoning tags and the *presence* of an `<answer>` tag, without checking its correctness, to assess emergent reasoning.

These reward functions are implemented within a modular scoring framework, using structured prompts with specific tags (Table 9) to guide the model's output format. Hyperparameters for GRPO training include a learning rate of 1e-6, batch size of 128, maximum prompt and response lengths of 2048 tokens, and 500 training steps, with a KL divergence coefficient of 0.00 to encourage diverse reasoning.

#### 3.4. Evaluation Metrics

Model performance is evaluated using exact match accuracy between the model's final answer (extracted from the `<answer>` tag) and the ground truth. Qualitative analysis is also performed to examine the structure, content, and visual grounding of the generated reasoning chains.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several key findings that shed light on MLLM capabilities and the effectiveness of reward-driven reinforcement learning for visual reasoning.

#### 4.1. Bottleneck of MLLMs (RQ1)

The empirical evaluation strongly supports the hypothesis that visual perception is the primary bottleneck for MLLMs in solving visual puzzles. When images were converted into textual descriptions, Claude-3.5-Sonnet and Claude-3.7-Sonnet showed substantial average performance gains of 26.7 percentage points and 23.6 percentage points, respectively. For instance, Claude-3.7's accuracy on the 'Clock' category jumped from 7% to 83% in the text-only setting, and both models achieved near-perfect scores on 'N-Queens' when provided with textual representations. Qualitative analysis further corroborated this, revealing that initial errors frequently stemmed from misinterpreting visual cues (e.g., misreading clock hands, incorrect spatial identification of objects) which then propagated through subsequent algorithmic reasoning steps.

#### 4.2. Reward Function Ablation and Long Reasoning (RQ2)

The RL training experiments demonstrated that reward function design significantly impacts the elicitation of long, structured visual reasoning.
- **Overall Performance:** Training with the `Diverse-8k` dataset consistently led to better performance across all reward functions compared to training solely on the `Clock-3k` dataset, indicating the importance of diverse exposure for generalizable improvements.
- **Mixture Reward Effectiveness:** The `Mixture` reward function achieved the highest average accuracy (15.56%) on the `Diverse-8k` dataset. Crucially, the `Mixture` and `Continuous` rewards successfully incentivized the MLLM to generate longer, more structured reasoning trajectories that explicitly referenced and explained visual details, a phenomenon termed "think with image." This emergent behavior, including step-by-step self-evaluation and referencing fine-grained image details (e.g., distinct colors of clock hands), was observed across both in-domain and out-of-domain categories, demonstrating the ability of these rewards to unlock long reasoning capabilities without dense CoT annotations.
- **Unexpected Behaviors:** The `Only-Accuracy` reward, despite its simplicity, performed competitively with or even surpassed the `Mixture` reward in about half of the classes, suggesting that while long reasoning can be induced, it does not unilaterally guarantee superior accuracy. Furthermore, the `No-Accuracy` reward, which provides no ground-truth supervision for the final answer, surprisingly outperformed the baseline and even some other reward functions, indicating that incentivizing internal reasoning structure alone can induce some level of useful generalization.
- **Persistent Challenges:** Even with RL, certain categories like 'Clock' and 'Tower of Hanoi' remained intrinsically difficult, aligning with prior research on their complex temporal and recursive reasoning demands.

#### 4.3. Generalization (RQ3)

Generalization performance was notably improved by training on a diverse dataset. While the `Clock-3k` training showed limited generalization beyond its specific domain, the `Diverse-8k` dataset substantially improved performance across nearly all classes, both in-domain and out-of-domain, relative to the no-RL baseline. A trade-off between in-domain accuracy and out-of-domain generalization was observed, potentially due to overfitting during RL training, yet OOD performance with RL consistently exceeded the baseline. The study also found that no single reward function universally dominated, with different functions excelling in specific categories, highlighting the heterogeneity of visual puzzle tasks.

#### 4.4. Error Analysis

Despite the improvements from RL training, the overall accuracy remained modest (best model at 15.56%). A qualitative error analysis identified four primary failure modes:
1. **Visual Understanding Errors:** Models still incorrectly identified object positions or attributes even after generating extensive visual reasoning.
2. **Algorithmic Reasoning Errors:** Failures in intermediate computations or decision steps.
3. **Inconsistencies:** The model's final answer contradicted its own prior reasoning steps.
4. **Incomplete Generation:** Failure to produce a valid answer tag or reach a definitive conclusion.
These errors suggest that while the current reward functions encourage *the act* of long reasoning, future work might require more explicit reward signals that evaluate the *accuracy* and *consistency* of intermediate visual grounding, potentially leveraging LLM-based judges.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research carries significant implications for the development of more capable and genuinely intelligent multimodal AI systems, particularly those designed for complex reasoning tasks.

Firstly, the paper's **systematic identification of visual perception as the primary bottleneck** in MLLMs for solving visual puzzles is a crucial diagnostic contribution. This finding redirects research efforts from solely focusing on algorithmic reasoning to enhancing MLLMs' fundamental capacity for accurate visual interpretation and grounding. Understanding this core limitation is paramount for developing more robust and reliable MLLMs. By empirically validating this bottleneck through the compelling "image-to-text" conversion experiments, the authors provide clear guidance for future model architectural designs and training strategies.

Secondly, the work demonstrates a **methodological breakthrough in inducing long, structured visual reasoning through reward-driven reinforcement learning, without requiring costly, dense human annotations or external tools.** This is a major step forward, as the scarcity and expense of high-quality, step-by-step reasoning annotations have been significant barriers to training truly reasoning MLLMs. The design and evaluation of six distinct reward functions provide a comprehensive analysis of how different reward signals can shape emergent reasoning behaviors. The success of the "Mixture" and "Continuous" rewards in eliciting visually-grounded, multi-step thought processes ("thinking with images") highlights the power of carefully crafted intrinsic rewards. This paves the way for more autonomous and scalable methods for improving MLLM reasoning.

Thirdly, the research contributes to **improving MLLM capabilities** by enhancing their visual perception and mitigating the tendency to bypass visual information. While overall accuracy improvements on highly complex visual puzzles remain modest, the emergence of structured, self-reflective reasoning chains is a qualitative leap. This emergent behavior, even if not yet leading to near-perfect performance on all challenging puzzles, signifies a deeper understanding and processing of multimodal inputs by the models.

Finally, this study opens several **promising avenues for future research**. The detailed error analysis, revealing issues like persistent visual understanding errors, algorithmic flaws, and internal inconsistencies, suggests that future reward designs could explicitly penalize these specific failure modes. Incorporating advanced LLM-based judges to evaluate the accuracy and consistency of intermediate visual grounding steps within the reasoning chain could lead to more precise and effective reward signals. Moreover, the observed trade-off between in-domain and out-of-domain performance implies a need for further research into regularization techniques or more sophisticated curriculum learning strategies within RL for MLLMs to enhance generalization. Ultimately, this work is a foundational step towards building MLLMs that can not only "see" but also truly "understand" and "reason" about the visual world, which is critical for real-world applications requiring complex multimodal intelligence.
