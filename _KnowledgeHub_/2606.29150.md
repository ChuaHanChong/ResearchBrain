# Flow Reasoning Models: Turning Flows Into Efficient Recurrent Reasoners

> [!summary] Summary
> Flow Reasoning Models (FRMs) turn continuous flows over discrete outputs into recurrent reasoners by self-conditioning a flow denoiser on its own clean prediction, making each pass a decodable refinement of the candidate solution. Fixed-Point Forcing trains that carry channel on rollout-derived states instead of one-pass ones, yielding 99.5%, 100.0% and 99.9% solve rates on Sudoku-Extreme, Zebra and Maze-Unique.

## Problem

- Autoregressive reasoners commit tokens in a fixed order and provide no mechanism to revise earlier decisions once their global consequences become apparent.
- Masked diffusion updates tokens conditionally independently within a step, so aggressive parallel decoding creates inconsistencies and needs conservative schedules or remasking machinery.
- Continuous discrete flows model all token interdependencies jointly but are untested on structured reasoning; naively applied they solve only about 13% of Sudoku-Extreme.

## Method

- **Flow Reasoning Models** feed the clean-prediction denoiser output back through a ==self-conditioning carry==, creating a ==recurrent reasoning depth== separate from flow time, with no ==backpropagation through time==.
- **Fixed-Point Forcing** builds the detached carry from a ==self-conditioned rollout== starting at a time sampled uniformly before the supervision time, targeting carry-side ==exposure bias==.
- Both stages keep the canonical ==linear interpolant path== and ==tokenwise cross-entropy== endpoint objective on a non-causal ==DiT== backbone; Stage A trains self-conditioning, Stage B applies **FPF**.

## Results

- Fixed-Point Forcing lifts the three-seed mean Sudoku-Extreme solve rate from **32.6%** to **99.2%** and Maze-Unique from **96.2%** to **98.0%**, with Zebra averaging **99.9%**.
- At **7M** parameters FRM matches the next-best reasoner's **98.7%** peak Sudoku solve rate using an estimated **44 times** fewer inference FLOPs, and leads all three accuracy-compute frontiers.
- The adjacent-state symmetric-KL residual predicts correctness at **AUROC 1.00** under FPF versus chance-level **0.50** under conventional self-conditioning, whose gold-target loss instead rises with depth.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Recurrent reasoning need not require a bespoke recurrent architecture; it can emerge from the refinement dynamics of a general-purpose generative denoiser.
> - Self-conditioned recurrence silently fails through exposure bias, stabilizing confidently wrong fixed points, so training on model-induced states is what makes extra depth productive.
> - When training aligns convergence with correctness, the residual between successive states becomes a usable, though not formally certified, correctness signal for adaptive compute.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a central challenge in artificial intelligence: structured reasoning, which involves coordinating multiple interdependent decisions to arrive at a globally consistent solution. Existing generative models face limitations in this domain.

**Autoregressive models** process tokens sequentially, committing to early decisions without the capacity for revision. This fixed order can be problematic for tasks where later information might necessitate a change to a previous choice.

**Masked diffusion models** offer more flexibility by allowing parallel token updates. However, tokens updated within a single step are conditionally independent given the current state. This characteristic can lead to inconsistencies when aggressive parallel updates are applied, particularly in tightly constrained problems. Correcting errors often requires additional mechanisms such, as remasking, and many conservative updates.

**Continuous flow models over discrete data** represent an alternative approach, capable of modeling all tokens and their interdependencies simultaneously. While these models have demonstrated efficacy in unconditional generation of natural language, their application to structured reasoning tasks has been less explored. Previous attempts using a naive application of such continuous flows to problems like Sudoku-Extreme yielded limited success, achieving approximately a 13% solve rate.

This paper introduces Flow Reasoning Models (FRMs) as a novel framework that bridges this gap. FRMs leverage continuous flows for discrete structured outputs and enhance them with a recurrent refinement mechanism. This approach aims to combine the benefits of continuous flow models (simultaneous interdependency modeling) with iterative refinement capabilities, addressing the revision limitations of autoregressive models and the coordination challenges of masked diffusion models. The work also fits into the broader field of recurrent and fixed-point reasoning, where models are designed to scale test-time computation through weight-tied recurrence and learn stable attractor dynamics for solutions.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a generative modeling framework that effectively performs structured reasoning by efficiently coordinating mutually constraining decisions and achieving high accuracy. This overarching goal is driven by several specific motivations:

1. **Addressing Limitations of Autoregressive Models:** Autoregressive models commit to predictions sequentially, lacking a mechanism to revise earlier choices. For complex structured reasoning problems where the global consistency of a solution might only become apparent after many steps, this inability to revise can lead to suboptimal or incorrect outcomes. The authors aim to create a model that can make and revise decisions in parallel.

2. **Overcoming Coordination Challenges in Masked Diffusion Models:** Masked diffusion models allow parallel updates, but the conditional independence of tokens updated within the same step can lead to inconsistencies. This necessitates conservative update strategies or additional remasking mechanisms to correct errors, which can increase computational cost and complexity. The research seeks a method that can efficiently coordinate interdependent choices to avoid such inconsistencies.

3. **Enhancing Continuous Flow Models for Structured Reasoning:** While continuous flow models have shown promise in unconditional generation, their direct application to structured reasoning tasks has exhibited limited performance (e.g., 13% solve rate on Sudoku-Extreme). A key motivation is to adapt these models to become effective and efficient reasoners for such problems.

4. **Developing an Iterative Refinement Mechanism:** The authors propose that structured reasoning benefits from iterative refinement. They aim to incorporate a mechanism that turns one-shot denoising into iterative solution refinement, allowing the model to self-condition on its own past outputs to progressively improve candidate solutions.

5. **Mitigating Exposure Bias in Recurrent Self-Conditioning:** Initial experiments with self-conditioning improved performance but revealed a limitation: conventional self-conditioning becomes unreliable at greater recurrent depth. This is attributed to exposure bias, a mismatch between the one-step training predictions and the recursively generated inference states. This bias can cause the model to converge to "spurious fixed points"—confidently incorrect solutions. A significant motivation is to develop a training method to address this specific mismatch, ensuring that additional recurrent depth leads to productive refinement rather than stabilization of errors.

6. **Achieving State-of-the-Art Accuracy and Efficiency:** The ultimate motivation is to develop a model that not only addresses the methodological challenges but also demonstrates competitive or superior performance in terms of both accuracy (solve rate) and computational efficiency (inference FLOPs) across various structured reasoning benchmarks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on Flow Reasoning Models (FRMs), which adapt continuous flows over discrete structured outputs into recurrent reasoners, coupled with a novel training method called Fixed-Point Forcing (FPF).

### 4. Discrete Flows for Conditional Reasoning (FRM Foundation)

- **Problem Formulation:** Reasoning is framed as conditional generation over data pairs (c, y), where 'c' is the problem specification (e.g., Sudoku clues) and 'y' is its discrete solution (y1..., yL) over a vocabulary V. The solution 'y' is noised and generated, while 'c' serves as a fixed conditioning input.
- **Representation:** The discrete solution 'y' is encoded as a one-hot endpoint x1 ∈ {0, 1}L×|V|. Predictions are decoded by a position-wise argmax.
- **Flow Matching:** A linear interpolant, xt = (1 - t)ε + tx1, connects Gaussian noise ε ∼ N(0, I) to the one-hot endpoint x1, where t ∈.
- **Denoiser and Velocity Prediction:** A denoiser Dtθ(xt | c) predicts a categorical distribution over the clean token at each position. This clean endpoint prediction determines the probability-flow velocity: vtθ(xt | c) = (Dtθ(xt | c) - xt) / (1 - t). The denoiser's output serves as both a directly decodable candidate solution and the quantity used to advance the flow.
- **Training Objective:** The model is trained using token-wise cross-entropy loss: LCE(θ) = Et,c,y,ε [- Σi=1 to L log Dtθ(xt | c)i,yi]. This standard discrete-flow model serves as the baseline FLM (Flow Language Model).

### 5. Self-Conditioning as Recurrent Reasoning

- **Mechanism:** The denoiser is augmented to Dtθ(xt | c, s), where 's' is an additional input carrying a previous clean-solution prediction. A null carry (s = ∅) is used initially (e.g., zero-valued).
- **Training (Conventional Self-Conditioning):** During training, two passes are used. A null-carry pass produces a detached prediction es = stopgrad[Dtθ(xt | c, ∅)]. The supervised pass then receives either 'es' or the null carry. Gradients do not pass through 'es'.
- **Inference:** FRMs iteratively feed the latest clean prediction back through 's'. This feedback mechanism enables the model to preserve correct decisions, revise inconsistencies, and correct earlier errors across discrete reasoning depth 'k'. The recurrent refinement process is defined as s(0)t = ∅, s(k+1)t = Dtθ(xt | c, s(k)t). Each carry directly estimates the clean solution, allowing detached training without backpropagation through time.

### 6. Fixed-Point Forcing (FPF)

- **Problem Identification (Exposure Bias):** While self-conditioning improves performance, its gains saturate with depth on challenging problems. This is attributed to exposure bias: the distribution of carries 's' encountered during one-pass training (derived from ground truth) differs from the distribution of recursively generated carries during multi-step inference. This mismatch can lead to the denoiser becoming overconfident in incorrect predictions and converging to spurious fixed points.
- **FPF Solution:** FPF addresses this by training the denoiser on rollout-derived conditioning states, making the training-time carry distribution more aligned with inference-time dynamics.
 - **Rollout-Derived Carry Construction:** For a given supervision time 't', a rollout start time tstart is sampled uniformly from U(0, t). The inference-time self-conditioned integrator is then run from tstart to 't'. The final prediction, sFPF, from this rollout is detached (stopgrad(sFPF)) and used as the carry for the loss-bearing prediction Dtθ(xt | c, stopgrad(sFPF)).
 - **Objective Preservation:** Crucially, FPF preserves the canonical flow-matching path. The loss-bearing input remains xt = (1 - t)ε + ty, and the target remains 'y'. The rollout prediction only influences the self-conditioning channel, not the state at which the flow objective is supervised. This ensures that the model learns to correct states produced by its own dynamics while adhering to the core flow-matching objective.

### 7. Experiments and Evaluation

- **Benchmarks:** FRMs were evaluated on three structured prediction tasks:
 - **Sudoku-Extreme:** 9x9 puzzles requiring constraint satisfaction and extended deduction.
 - **Zebra:** Relational deduction puzzles, testing transferability across heterogeneous reasoning.
 - **Maze-Unique:** 30x30 path-finding benchmark with unique solution paths, allowing direct exact-grid accuracy evaluation.
- **Metrics:** Primary metric is exact solve rate. Inference efficiency is measured by total FLOPs per instance and Number of Function Evaluations (NFE).
- **Baseline Comparisons:** The performance was compared against various diffusion and flow language models (FMLM, MDLM, ReMDM, FLM, Adaptive MDLM) and specialized recurrent reasoners (HRM, TRM, FPRM, EqR).
- **Training Configuration:** All experiments used a non-causal DiT backbone and the categorical clean-prediction objective with AdamW optimizer. Training involves Stage A (self-conditioning from scratch) followed by Stage B (initializing from Stage-A weights and applying FPF).

### 8. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of Flow Reasoning Models (FRMs) demonstrated their effectiveness and efficiency across multiple structured reasoning tasks.

### 9. Accuracy and Efficiency Across Structured Reasoning Tasks

- **Near-Perfect Solving:** FRMs achieved high solve rates across diverse reasoning domains:
 - **Sudoku-Extreme:** 99.5%
 - **Zebra:** 100.0%
 - **Maze-Unique:** 99.9%
- **Accuracy-Compute Frontier Dominance:** FRMs established a superior accuracy-compute frontier compared to diffusion language models and specialized recurrent reasoners on all three tasks.
 - On **Sudoku-Extreme**, FRMs attained the highest solve rate (99.5%) and matched a leading baseline (EqR's 98.7% solve rate) with approximately 44 times fewer inference FLOPs.
 - On **Zebra**, FRMs reached 100.0% solve rate, surpassing reported peaks for comparison methods.
 - On **Maze-Unique**, FRMs achieved a 99.9% peak, which was comparable to the highest observed performance on this benchmark.
- **Generalizability:** The consistent high performance across tasks with different constraint types (constraint satisfaction, relational deduction, path finding, and varying sequence lengths/vocabularies) suggests that recurrent flow refinement is not specific to a particular task representation.

### 10. Impact of Self-Conditioning and Fixed-Point Forcing

- **Limited Performance of Vanilla Flows:** The baseline vanilla Flow Language Model (FLM) exhibited limited peak performance and did not scale effectively with increased inference compute. For example, on Sudoku-Extreme, FLM achieved only a 13.9% solve rate, and additional forward passes did not significantly improve performance.
- **Self-Conditioning Enables Recurrent Refinement:** The introduction of self-conditioning significantly improved solve rates over plain discrete flows.
 - On **Sudoku-Extreme**, solve rate increased from 13.1% to 32.6%.
 - On **Maze-Unique**, solve rate improved from 77.6% to 96.2%.
 - This demonstrates that recurrent computation, enabled by self-conditioning, allows for the preservation of correct assignments and the revision of earlier mistakes.
 - However, conventional self-conditioning proved unreliable at depth, with its solve rate plateauing on Sudoku-Extreme, indicating that it frequently converged to incorrect solutions.
- **Fixed-Point Forcing (FPF) Creates Healthy Reasoning Dynamics:** FPF effectively mitigated the exposure bias observed with conventional self-conditioning, leading to substantial performance gains and robust recurrent scaling.
 - **Dramatic Performance Improvement:** FPF raised the mean solve rate on Sudoku-Extreme from 32.6% to 99.2%, on Maze-Unique from 96.2% to 98.0%, and on Zebra from 36.9% to 99.9%.
 - **Recurrent Scaling:** Unlike conventional self-conditioning, FPF allowed for continued improvement in solve rate with additional recurrent depth and NFE, indicating that the model converted additional computation into corrected solutions.
 - **Alignment of Convergence with Correctness:** Under FPF, additional recurrent updates reduced gold-target cross-entropy as solve rate increased, implying that convergent trajectories were directed toward the correct solution.
 - **Predictive Convergence:** The residual (token-averaged symmetric KL between adjacent predictive distributions) became a strong indicator of correctness under FPF (AUROC 1.00 on Sudoku-Extreme), in contrast to conventional self-conditioning where it was uninformative (AUROC 0.50). This indicates that FPF reshapes the model's closed-loop dynamics, making correct solutions behave as stable, error-correcting attractors, while incorrect states remain dynamically active or unstable.

### 11. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents Flow Reasoning Models (FRMs) as a contribution to the field of structured reasoning in artificial intelligence, demonstrating a method for efficient and accurate problem-solving.

**Generalizability and Robustness:** The consistent near-perfect performance of FRMs across diverse structured reasoning tasks—Sudoku-Extreme (constraint satisfaction), Zebra (relational deduction), and Maze-Unique (path finding)—indicates that the framework is not limited to a specific problem type. This suggests that the principles of recurrent flow refinement can be broadly applicable to problems requiring complex, interdependent decision-making.

**Computational Efficiency:** FRMs demonstrate a favorable accuracy-compute tradeoff. The achievement of high solve rates with significantly fewer inference FLOPs compared to existing masked diffusion models and specialized recurrent reasoners (e.g., 44x fewer FLOPs than a competitive baseline on Sudoku-Extreme) highlights the potential for more efficient AI systems for complex reasoning. This efficiency could be particularly impactful in resource-constrained environments or applications requiring rapid inference.

**Methodological Advancement in Generative Models:** The introduction of FRMs, which turn continuous flows over discrete outputs into recurrent reasoners, represents an advancement in generative modeling. By using the flow denoiser's clean prediction as both the recurrent input and an explicit solution candidate, the method integrates iterative refinement within a general-purpose generative architecture. This contrasts with approaches that rely on specialized latent-state architectures for recurrent reasoning.

**Addressing Exposure Bias:** The development of Fixed-Point Forcing (FPF) addresses a known challenge in recurrent self-conditioned models: exposure bias. By training on rollout-derived conditioning states, FPF ensures that the model learns to correct states produced by its own inference dynamics. This intervention enables productive scaling of recurrent depth, leading to improved performance and aligning model convergence with solution correctness. This mechanism could have implications for other self-conditioned and recurrent learning paradigms where train-inference mismatch poses a problem.

**Implications for AI Systems:** The findings suggest that effective recurrent reasoning can emerge from the learned refinement dynamics of general-purpose generative models, rather than exclusively from bespoke recurrent architectures. This could simplify the design of AI systems capable of structured reasoning by leveraging existing, scalable generative model backbones. The ability of FRMs to effectively make and revise interdependent decisions in parallel contributes to enhancing the problem-solving capabilities of AI.

**Future Directions:** The paper identifies scaling this approach to larger models and less structured tasks as an important direction for future work. This implies that the FRM framework, with its demonstrated efficiency and robustness on structured problems, could potentially be extended to a wider array of complex AI challenges.
