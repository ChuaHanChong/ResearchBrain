# Dynamic Evaluation of Classical and Control-Aware Optimal Trajectory Planning in Robot Manipulators

> [!summary] Summary
> This work introduces a control-aware optimal trajectory planning framework, explicitly incorporating manipulator dynamics and actuator control effort during trajectory generation. Through a unified nonlinear evaluation, the proposed method reduces RMS tracking error by 22-73% and RMS corrective torque by 28-41% compared to classical kinematic planners.

## Problem

- Classical kinematic trajectory planners overlook manipulator dynamics and control effort, leading to inefficient execution with high corrective control actions.
- Existing optimal control methods often have high computational complexity, making them less suitable for certain industrial applications.
- Previous comparative studies of trajectory planners lacked a unified evaluation framework, making it difficult to isolate the true impact of different planning methods on closed-loop execution.

## Method

- Developed a **control-aware optimal trajectory planning framework** using ==midpoint linearization== of manipulator dynamics and formulating the problem as a ==finite-horizon quadratic program (QP)==.
- Incorporated system dynamics and actuator constraints directly into the cost function and terminal constraints of the QP.
- Established a ==unified nonlinear evaluation framework== that subjects all planners to identical nonlinear manipulator dynamics, a ==feedforward-assisted PID controller==, and actuator saturation limits for fair comparison.

## Results

- The control-aware optimal planner achieved an RMS tracking error of **2.825 × 10⁻⁴ rad**, outperforming cubic, quintic, and trapezoidal planners by **22%**, **50%**, and **73%** respectively.
- It reduced RMS corrective torque by approximately **28.35%** (vs. cubic), **41.29%** (vs. quintic), and **30.27%** (vs. trapezoidal).
- The proposed method maintained the lowest cumulative executed cost, reducing it by **48.13%** relative to the cubic planner and over **62%** relative to both quintic and trapezoidal planners.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Kinematic smoothness alone does not guarantee dynamically efficient or optimal execution on a nonlinear robotic manipulator, as shown by the poorer performance of high-order classical methods.
> - Explicitly considering manipulator dynamics and actuator control effort during trajectory planning significantly reduces tracking errors and the need for corrective feedback control.
> - A rigorous, unified evaluation environment is essential for accurately comparing trajectory planning strategies and isolating the performance benefits of dynamically informed methods.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Trajectory planning is a foundational aspect of robotic manipulator operation, directly influencing tracking accuracy, actuator demand, and overall execution characteristics. The broader research landscape in this domain is generally bifurcated into classical kinematic approaches and more advanced optimal control methods.

Classical trajectory planning techniques, such as cubic and quintic polynomials or trapezoidal velocity profiles, are widely adopted due to their computational simplicity and the kinematic smoothness they offer. These methods generate trajectories based on joint positions, velocities, and accelerations, often ensuring continuity and smoothness in joint space. However, a significant limitation of these approaches is their purely kinematic nature; they do not explicitly incorporate the complex, nonlinear dynamics of the robotic manipulator or the associated actuator control effort during the trajectory generation phase. This omission can result in trajectories that, despite appearing smooth kinematically, may lead to inefficient or aggressive execution when subjected to real-world nonlinear dynamics and feedback control, necessitating substantial corrective control action and increasing actuator burden.

In response to these limitations, optimal control methods have emerged, designed to generate dynamically feasible trajectories by integrating system dynamics and control objectives directly into the optimization process. While advanced nonlinear and receding-horizon optimization techniques have demonstrated strong performance in theory, their computational complexity can be a barrier for certain industrial applications where offline trajectory generation followed by feedback tracking is sufficient. Moreover, existing research, including studies on actuator-aware trajectory optimization, suggests that kinematic smoothness alone does not consistently translate to dynamically efficient manipulator execution.

A critical gap identified in previous comparative studies is the lack of a unified evaluation framework. Many comparisons between classical and optimal planners have been conducted under differing control strategies, simulation environments, or dynamic models, which makes it challenging to isolate the specific impact of the trajectory generation method itself on closed-loop execution performance.

This work addresses this gap by developing a control-aware optimal trajectory planning framework and, critically, a unified nonlinear evaluation framework. By comparing classical and the proposed optimal planning methods under identical nonlinear dynamics, controller structures, and actuator constraints, the research aims to provide a direct and isolated assessment of how different trajectory generation approaches influence corrective control effort and overall nonlinear execution behavior. This contribution enhances the understanding of trajectory planning efficacy in realistic robotic operational contexts.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and evaluate a control-aware optimal trajectory planning framework for robotic manipulators. This framework explicitly incorporates manipulator dynamics and actuator control effort during trajectory generation, aiming to overcome the limitations of purely kinematic methods. A further key objective is to conduct a fair and isolated comparison of this proposed optimal planner against widely used classical kinematic trajectory planners (cubic, quintic, and trapezoidal) under identical closed-loop nonlinear execution conditions.

The detailed motivations driving this work are as follows:
- **Addressing the Dynamic Inefficiency of Classical Planners:** Classical trajectory generation methods prioritize kinematic smoothness and computational efficiency but overlook manipulator dynamics and actuator control effort. This neglect often leads to trajectories that, when executed on a physical robot with nonlinear dynamics, necessitate significant corrective control actions, leading to increased actuator demand, energy consumption, and potential wear. The research is motivated by the need to generate trajectories that are inherently more compatible with the robot's dynamic capabilities.
- **Improving Execution Performance under Realistic Conditions:** In practical robotic systems, trajectories are executed through feedback controllers acting upon nonlinear manipulator dynamics. The quality of a trajectory, therefore, cannot be solely judged by its kinematic smoothness. The authors are motivated to investigate whether dynamically informed trajectory generation can reduce tracking errors, minimize corrective control burden, and lower overall execution costs under these realistic, nonlinear operating conditions.
- **Providing a Fair Comparative Analysis:** Previous comparative studies often utilized different feedback controllers, simulation environments, or simplified dynamic models for various trajectory planners. This inconsistency makes it difficult to definitively attribute performance differences solely to the trajectory generation method. A central motivation is to establish a unified nonlinear evaluation framework that subjects all planners to the same nonlinear dynamics, controller structure, controller gains, and actuator constraints. This ensures that any observed differences in execution behavior can be directly linked to the trajectory planning strategy itself, providing a more robust and credible comparison.
- **Validating the Importance of Control-Awareness:** The work is motivated by the hypothesis that explicitly considering control effort and system dynamics during planning can lead to substantial improvements in closed-loop execution, even for structured industrial tasks where offline planning is sufficient. This aims to demonstrate that higher-order kinematic smoothness alone may not guarantee dynamically efficient execution, thereby challenging conventional assumptions in the field.
- **Developing a Practical Optimal Planning Approach:** While advanced optimal control techniques exist, some involve significant computational overhead. This research aims to develop a finite-horizon, control-effort-aware optimal formulation suitable for offline trajectory generation, providing a balance between dynamic awareness and computational tractability for certain applications.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology involves developing a control-aware optimal trajectory planning framework and establishing a unified evaluation environment for comparing it with classical planners.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation for Control-Aware Planning

The dynamics of an n-DoF robotic manipulator are described by the equation τ = D(q)¨q + C(q, ˙q) ˙q + G(q), where q represents joint positions, ˙q joint velocities, ¨q joint accelerations, τ actuator torques, D(q) the mass matrix, C(q, ˙q) the Coriolis and centrifugal forces matrix, and G(q) the gravity vector. By incorporating gravity compensation (τ = G(q) + u), the dynamics simplify to ¨q = D(q)⁻¹(u − C(q, ˙q) ˙q). The system state is defined as x = [qᵀ, ˙qᵀ]ᵀ, leading to the nonlinear state-space representation ˙x = f(x, u).

#### 3.2. Midpoint Linearization of Manipulator Dynamics

To facilitate optimal control, the nonlinear dynamics f(x, u) are linearized around a midpoint operating pair (¯x, ¯u) using deviation variables δx = x − ¯x and δu = u − ¯u. This yields a first-order approximation δ ˙x(t) = A_c δx(t) + B_c δu(t), where A_c and B_c are Jacobian matrices of f with respect to x and u, respectively, evaluated at (¯x, ¯u). This midpoint linearization strategy is employed to improve approximation accuracy over large point-to-point motions. The continuous-time linear model is then discretized using a Zero-Order Hold method with a sampling time T_s, resulting in the discrete-time matrices A_d and B_d.

#### 3.3. Control-Aware Optimal Control Framework

The trajectory planning problem is framed as a finite-horizon regulation problem.
- **Regulation-State Representation:** A desired goal state x_g = [q_gᵀ, ˙q_gᵀ]ᵀ is defined. The regulation-state vector at step k is x_k = [(q_k - q_g)ᵀ, (˙q_k - ˙q_g)ᵀ]ᵀ, which describes the deviation from the goal. The discrete-time dynamics are then x_k+1 = A_d x_k + B_d u_k.
- **Finite-Horizon Prediction Model:** Stacked state and control vectors (X and U) are constructed over a prediction horizon N. The prediction model is expressed as X = ¯A x(0) + ¯B U, where ¯A and ¯B are stacked prediction matrices derived from A_d and B_d.
- **Cost Function Formulation:** The objective function J is defined as a quadratic cost penalizing state deviation and actuator effort over the finite horizon:
 J = ½ Σ_{k=1}^N x(k)ᵀ Q x(k) + ½ Σ_{k=0}^{N-1} u(k)ᵀ R u(k) + ½ x(0)ᵀ Q x(0)
 Here, Q is a positive semi-definite weighting matrix for state deviation, and R is a positive definite weighting matrix for actuator effort. This cost function is transformed into a quadratic programming (QP) form: J(x(0), U) = constant(x(0)) + ½ Uᵀ H U + x(0)ᵀ Fᵀ U.
- **Terminal Constraint:** To ensure convergence to the desired goal state, a terminal constraint x(N) = 0 is imposed. This is translated using the prediction model into B_N U = -A_N x(0).
- **Optimal Control Law:** The problem is formulated as a constrained quadratic program:
 min_U (½ Uᵀ H U + x(0)ᵀ Fᵀ U)
 subject to actuator constraints u_min ≤ U ≤ u_max and the terminal constraint B_N U = -A_N x(0). This QP is solved using MATLAB's `quadprog` function to obtain the optimal control sequence U*.

#### 3.4. Baseline Trajectory Planning Methods

The proposed framework is compared against three classical kinematic planners:
- **Cubic Polynomial:** q(t) = a_0 + a_1 t + a_2 t² + a_3 t³.
- **Quintic Polynomial:** q(t) = a_0 + a_1 t + a_2 t² + a_3 t³ + a_4 t⁴ + a_5 t⁵.
- **Trapezoidal Velocity Profile:** A piecewise function characterized by constant acceleration, constant velocity, and constant deceleration phases.
These methods derive trajectory coefficients by satisfying boundary conditions (initial/final position, velocity, and for quintic, acceleration) without considering manipulator dynamics or control effort.

#### 3.5. Experimental Setup (Unified Evaluation Framework)

- **Manipulator Model:** A reduced 3-DoF UR5 robotic manipulator model is used, operating under nonlinear rigid-body dynamics.
- **Task:** A fixed-duration point-to-point joint-space motion task is defined.
- **Simulation Parameters:** All simulations utilize a total trajectory time T_f = 2 seconds, a sampling time ∆t = 0.02 seconds, and a horizon N = 100.
- **Execution Model:** Crucially, all planned trajectories (from the proposed optimal planner and the classical methods) are executed using the *same nonlinear RK4 integration model* of the UR5 manipulator. This ensures that the dynamic execution environment is identical for all planners.
- **Controller:** A feedforward-assisted PID tracking controller is employed for all methods. The applied control input is ∆τ_cmd = ∆τ_ff + ∆τ_fb, where the feedback term ∆τ_fb = K_p e + K_i ∫e dt + K_d ė, and e = q_ref − q is the tracking error. Identical PID gains and actuator saturation limits (|∆τ_i| ≤ 60 Nm) are applied across all planners, preventing controller variations from influencing the comparison.
- **Evaluation Metrics:**
 - **Tracking Accuracy:** Quantified by the Root Mean Square (RMS) tracking error.
 - **Corrective Actuator Demand:** Assessed using RMS corrective torque and the cumulative corrective-control activity J_∆τ = ∫ Σ|∆τ_i(t)| dt, which measures the total absolute corrective effort.
 - **Overall Execution Performance:** Evaluated using the cumulative executed cost J_exec = Σ (x_kᵀ Q_exec x_k + u_kᵀ R_exec u_k), where Q_exec and R_exec penalize tracking deviation and corrective control effort during nonlinear execution, respectively.

This unified framework ensures that any observed performance differences are directly attributable to the trajectory generation method, providing a fair and isolated evaluation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presented a comprehensive comparison of the proposed control-aware optimal trajectory planner against classical cubic, quintic, and trapezoidal planners under identical nonlinear execution conditions on a 3-DoF UR5 manipulator.

#### 4.1. Planned Trajectory Characteristics

Analysis of the planned joint acceleration profiles (Fig. 2) revealed distinct behaviors. The trapezoidal planner exhibited abrupt acceleration transitions, characteristic of its piecewise velocity profile. The quintic planner, despite its higher-order smoothness formulation, displayed comparatively large acceleration variations. In contrast, the proposed control-aware optimal planner produced smoother and more gradually distributed acceleration profiles across all joints, suggesting a greater compatibility with the manipulator's nonlinear dynamics during execution.

#### 4.2. Tracking Performance Under Nonlinear Execution

The proposed control-aware optimal planner consistently achieved lower tracking error throughout most of the trajectory execution (Fig. 3). The trapezoidal planner exhibited the largest tracking deviations, particularly near acceleration transition regions. Quantitatively, the RMS tracking error for the proposed optimal method was 2.825 × 10⁻⁴ rad. Compared to this, the RMS tracking errors for the cubic, quintic, and trapezoidal planners were 3.622 × 10⁻⁴ rad, 5.653 × 10⁻⁴ rad, and 1.059 × 10⁻³ rad, respectively. This indicated relative increases in RMS tracking error of approximately 22% for cubic, 50% for quintic, and 73% for trapezoidal planners when compared to the proposed method (Table I).

#### 4.3. Corrective Control Effort Analysis

The proposed control-aware optimal planner consistently required lower corrective control effort during nonlinear execution (Fig. 4). The cumulative corrective-control activity (J_∆τ) further demonstrated that the proposed planner generated the lowest total corrective actuator effort among all methods (Fig. 5). Specifically, the RMS corrective torque for the optimal planner was 1.893 Nm. This compared favorably to 2.629 Nm for cubic, 3.074 Nm for quintic, and 3.066 Nm for trapezoidal planners. These figures represent reductions in RMS corrective torque of approximately 28.35% (cubic), 41.29% (quintic), and 30.27% (trapezoidal) when using the optimal planner. Similarly, the integrated corrective-control activity for the optimal planner was 4.535 Nms, substantially lower than 6.329 Nms (cubic), 7.723 Nms (quintic), and 6.504 Nms (trapezoidal) (Table I).

#### 4.4. Closed-Loop Execution Cost Comparison

Throughout the motion duration, the proposed control-aware planner maintained the lowest cumulative executed cost (Fig. 6). The final executed cost for the optimal method was 3.622. In comparison, the cubic, quintic, and trapezoidal planners resulted in final executed costs of 6.983, 9.562, and 9.697, respectively. These values indicate that the proposed method reduced the executed cost by approximately 48.13% relative to the cubic planner, and by over 62% relative to both the quintic and trapezoidal planners (Table I).

#### 4.5. Quantitative Performance Summary

Overall, the results consistently demonstrated the superior nonlinear execution performance of the proposed control-aware optimal planner across all evaluated metrics. The quantitative data underscored that while classical trajectory planners produce kinematically smooth trajectories, they can lead to substantially higher corrective actuator demand and execution costs under realistic nonlinear manipulator dynamics. The findings indicate that kinematic smoothness alone does not ensure dynamically efficient nonlinear manipulator execution.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of robotic manipulation by providing empirical evidence for the advantages of control-aware trajectory planning over classical kinematic methods, particularly under realistic nonlinear execution conditions. The significance and potential impact of this work can be understood through several key aspects:

- **Validation of Dynamic Awareness in Planning:** The study robustly validates the premise that explicitly incorporating manipulator dynamics and actuator effort during trajectory generation significantly enhances execution performance. The consistent reductions in tracking error, corrective control effort, and overall execution cost observed with the proposed method underscore the importance of dynamic awareness in robot motion planning, moving beyond purely kinematic considerations.
- **Establishment of a Unified Evaluation Framework:** A notable contribution is the development and implementation of a unified nonlinear evaluation framework. By ensuring identical nonlinear dynamics, controller structures (feedforward-assisted PID), controller gains, and actuator constraints across all evaluated trajectory planners, the research effectively isolates the impact of the trajectory generation method itself. This approach addresses a common limitation in prior comparative studies and provides a more rigorous and fair basis for assessing planning algorithms. This framework can serve as a benchmark for future research in trajectory planning evaluation.
- **Challenging Conventional Wisdom on Kinematic Smoothness:** The findings directly challenge the assumption that higher-order kinematic smoothness (e.g., quintic polynomials) inherently leads to dynamically more efficient or superior execution. The results demonstrate that while kinematic smoothness is a desirable property, it does not guarantee optimal dynamic behavior or minimal control effort when executed on a nonlinear physical system. This suggests that the focus should shift towards dynamic feasibility and control-effort minimization rather than solely relying on the order of kinematic polynomials.
- **Practical Implications for Industrial Robotics:**
 - **Improved Efficiency and Longevity:** By significantly reducing corrective actuator demand and execution cost, the proposed control-aware planning approach can lead to more energy-efficient robot operation and potentially extend the lifespan of actuators and mechanical components by reducing stress and wear.
 - **Enhanced Precision and Robustness:** Lower tracking errors and smoother actuator commands contribute to higher precision in task execution and potentially greater robustness against disturbances, as the feedback controller has less corrective work to perform.
 - **Reduced Burden on Feedback Control:** Dynamically consistent trajectories generated by control-aware planners provide a better feedforward signal, thereby reducing the burden on the feedback controller. This can simplify controller tuning or enable the use of simpler, less computationally intensive feedback controllers while maintaining performance.
- **Guidance for Future Research and Development:** The work encourages further exploration into computationally efficient control-aware planning methods that can be integrated into various robotic applications. It highlights the potential for developing offline trajectory generators that are not only kinematically smooth but also inherently dynamically optimized, which could be particularly beneficial for industrial manipulation tasks requiring high precision and efficiency. The approach of midpoint linearization combined with a finite-horizon QP offers a practical avenue for achieving this.

In conclusion, this research provides strong evidence that integrating manipulator dynamics and actuator constraints into the trajectory planning process yields substantial benefits in terms of tracking accuracy, control effort, and overall operational efficiency. Its rigorous comparative methodology and clear findings have the potential to influence the design and evaluation of future trajectory planning algorithms in robotics.
