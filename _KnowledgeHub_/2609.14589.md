# Robotic Servo Tracking of Moving Targets with Dynamic Imitation Constraints

> [!summary] Summary
> A visual servoing method that tracks moving targets under explicit trajectory constraints. It models end-effector approach as a time-varying second-order system with proven convergence, then generates Dynamic Constrained Trajectories in real time via temporal scaling and shape-amplitude decoupled Gaussian modulation learned from demonstrations. A constraint-aware multi-feature controller follows them, reaching 4.0 mm pose error, 87% grasp success, and zero overshoot.

## Problem

- Image-based servoing maps feature residuals directly to velocities, constraining only start and end states, so intermediate motion oscillates, jumps, or collides in cluttered scenes.
- Predictive and optimization servoing needs prior target velocity and degrades on non-differentiable target motion, while global planners such as CHOMP and STOMP are slow and only centimeter accurate.
- Imitation and reinforcement approaches encode trajectory constraints implicitly through rewards or post-processing, and classic movement-primitive modulation degenerates when start and goal coincide, assuming static targets.

## Method

- A ==time-varying second-order state-space model== of end-effector approach, with ==Lyapunov analysis== proving bounded convergence to the target under bounded, non-analytic target motion input.
- The **Dynamic Constrained Trajectory**: temporal scaling of the phase and Gaussian bases, ==shape-amplitude decoupled modulation== fitted by ==Locally Weighted Regression==, and control-point selection enforcing monotone distance-to-goal decrease.
- A ==constraint-aware multi-feature controller== converting differential pose transforms or least-squares keypoint velocities into spatial velocity, then joint velocity via adjoint transform and ==Jacobian pseudo-inverse==.

## Results

- Across **20** scenarios and **150** attempts on a Kinova arm, the method reached **4.0** plus or minus **1.2 mm** pose error and **87%** success, versus **8.0 mm** and **83%** for RTMPose4VSG.
- It recorded **zero** overshoot or oscillation events beyond the **10 mm** threshold, while RTMPose4VSG logged **two** and TSCTO **one**, the latter also lagging with **8.3 s** settling.
- Global planners trailed badly: OMPL-CHOMP at **14 mm** error and **23.4 s**, OMPL-STOMP at **18 mm** and **75%** success, against the proposed **7.0 s** settling time.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Distributing target motion across a sequence of control points with enforced monotone progress suppresses oscillation caused by noisy feature residuals in any servo loop.
> - Decoupling shape from amplitude in trajectory modulation removes the degeneracy where identical start and goal points collapse the learned shape term to zero.
> - Explicit constraints buy zero overshoot and roughly double precision for about 0.6 s extra settling; demonstrations were static-target only, and the avoidance ablation is qualitative.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

Moving-target grasping asks a robot arm to keep up with an object while moving through clutter. Ordinary visual servoing turns the gap between what the camera sees and what it wants to see into velocity commands. That can make the arm responsive, but it leaves the arm's route between the current pose and the target weakly constrained. The authors are concerned that this intermediate motion can be jerky, oscillatory, or unsafe around obstacles, rather than simply that the arm needs to reach the target faster.

Their approach works differently. Instead of steering only toward the latest visual error, it repeatedly reshapes a demonstrated path around the moving target, selects the next point along that path, and commands the arm to follow it. In real-world trials, the method reported lower pose error and higher grasp success than a direct visual-servo baseline. It did not have a shorter duration.

### 2. From demonstration to moving constraint

Visual servoing is closed-loop control that uses visual features or target pose to guide robot motion. The familiar version converts current-versus-desired feature differences into velocity commands. Because it acts on the immediate error, it places relatively weak constraints on the intermediate path.

The proposed mechanism proceeds in steps:

1. It learns a smooth demonstration using Gaussian basis functions.
1. It adjusts the demonstration's timing with a time-scaling transformation.
1. It separates the path's learned shape from the start-to-target displacement. This allows different intermediate paths even when the start and end points coincide.
1. At each update, it generates a generalized trajectory using the current target information.
1. From that trajectory, it selects the next control point. The chosen point is closer to the desired target than the current point, while its neighboring point is farther away.

Repeating this selection forms the **dynamic constrained trajectory (DCT)**. The DCT is a sequence of waypoints that constrains intermediate motion while adapting to the target.

Schematic of dynamic imitation-constrained trajectory generation. It shows the demonstration and trajectory-generation process, the resulting generalized and dynamic constrained trajectory, start and end points, and selected control points.

The controller turns the next point into a velocity using either the differential target pose or corresponding tracked features. This requires the target pose or features to be available in real time. The convergence argument assumes a stable final target state and target motion that approaches a bounded neighborhood of it. Under those conditions, it yields convergence to a small neighborhood, not perfect tracking of any arbitrarily changing target.

### 3. What the tests show

The ablation compares two approaches. Feature-guided visual servoing directly maps feature residuals to velocities. The constrained version uses a learned obstacle-avoidance trajectory and adapts constraints to target motion. According to the authors, the former tends to collide. The ablation illustrates the route constraint's intended role, but it does not establish obstacle safety across environments.

The real-world tests used a Kinova arm, a RealSense depth camera, and a 100 Hz controller. They covered 20 scenarios with the same initial configurations, motion scripts, and replayed disturbances across methods. Each method had 150 attempts. A successful trial reaches the graspable pose and completes the grasp without collision or target loss.

For moving-target grasping, the proposed method reported a pose error (PE) of **4.0 ± 1.2 mm** and a **87%** success rate. RTMPose4VSG reported **8.0 ± 2.1 mm** and **83%**. Durations went the other way: **7.0 ± 0.8 s** for the proposed method versus **6.4 ± 1.0 s** for RTMPose4VSG. The paper evaluates PE as error between the expected and actual wrist-camera poses. The table does not specify how PE aggregates its components or what the ± denotes. Overshoot/oscillation (OS/OC) was 0 for the proposed method versus 2 for RTMPose4VSG. Fluctuations exceeding the ±10 mm grasp tolerance count as OS/OC. The authors attribute RTMPose's oscillation to feature fluctuations being passed into velocity commands. The experiment does not isolate which DCT component caused the improvements.

### 4. A route, not a universal guarantee

The contribution is to constrain the intermediate route while still adapting to a moving target. In these grasping trials, it improved reported pose error, success, and OS/OC, but not elapsed time. The obstacle avoidance shown uses a trajectory learned from demonstrations and is not a general collision-proof guarantee.
