# Physically Embodied Gaussian Splatting: A Realtime Correctable World Model for Robotics

> [!summary] Summary
> Physically Embodied Gaussian Splatting develops a real-time, self-correcting world model for robotics by coupling 3D Gaussians for visual representation with particles for physics simulation. This approach tracks and predicts dynamic scenes at 30 Hz, achieving lower tracking error than physics-only or visual-only baselines in diverse manipulation and interaction scenarios.

## Problem

- Traditional robotics world models struggle to bridge the gap between accurate visual representation and physics-based reasoning, often lacking real-time self-correction.
- Existing neural scene representations (e.g., NeRF, D3DGS) are either computationally intensive or lack predictive physical understanding.
- Physics-only simulations accumulate error over time without visual feedback, while visual tracking alone struggles with occlusions or rapid movements.

## Method

- A **Gaussian-Particle** representation is introduced, coupling ==3D Gaussians== for visual rendering with ==particles== for ==physics simulation==.
- A continuous ==prediction-correction loop== is employed: ==Position-Based Dynamics (PBD)== predicts particle movement, followed by ==visual optimization== to correct Gaussian positions based on camera observations.
- ==Visual forces== are computed from ==photometric error== during visual optimization and applied back to the particles, driving corrections into the physics simulation.

## Results

- The system operates at **30 Hz** using three cameras, enabling real-time world modeling for dynamic scenarios.
- Demonstrates significantly lower tracking error compared to physics-only simulations, Dynamic 3D Gaussians (D3DGS), and CoTracker baselines across various simulated and real-world tasks.
- Successfully handles diverse dynamic scenarios including object manipulation, falling, picking up, and deformable objects like ropes in both simulated and real environments.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The dual Gaussian-Particle representation allows leveraging both visual accuracy of 3D Gaussians and the predictive power of particle-based physics.
> - Real-time visual feedback via "visual forces" enables continuous correction of physics simulations, ensuring the world model remains synchronized with the actual environment.
> - Combining real-time Gaussian Splatting with efficient Position-Based Dynamics facilitates practical, high-frequency world modeling for dynamic scenes.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Creating accurate and useful world models is a fundamental challenge in robotics. Robots need to understand their environment, predict how it will change, and adapt when those predictions don't match reality. The research paper "Physically Embodied Gaussian Splatting" introduces a novel approach that combines visual representation with physics simulation to create a world model that operates in real-time while continuously correcting itself based on visual feedback.

Traditional robotics approaches often struggle to bridge the gap between perception and physics-based reasoning. Most representations either focus on visual accuracy without physical understanding or implement physics without a proper visual grounding. This work addresses this gap by introducing a "Gaussian-Particle" representation that combines 3D Gaussians for visual rendering with particles for physics simulation.

#### 1.1. Background and Related Work

This research builds upon several key areas in computer vision and robotics:

**Neural Scene Representations**: Recent advances like Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) and 3D Gaussian Splatting have revolutionized how we represent scenes visually. Gaussian Splatting, in particular, offers real-time rendering capabilities that make it suitable for robotics applications, unlike the slower NeRF models.

**Particle-Based Physics**: Position-Based Dynamics (PBD) has emerged as a versatile approach for simulating physics in computer graphics and robotics. It uses particles connected by constraints to model various physical behaviors and can handle collisions, deformations, and material properties efficiently.

**World Models in Robotics**: Creating models that can predict environmental changes is crucial for robot planning and control. Previous approaches often failed to integrate visual and physical representations effectively or required extensive training data.

Previous research like ParticleNeRF attempted to combine NeRF with physics simulation but suffered from computational inefficiency. Similarly, Dynamic 3D Gaussians (D3DGS) incorporated physical priors for tracking but couldn't predict future states. This paper's approach combines the strengths of these methods while addressing their limitations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The system runs at 30 Hz using only three cameras, making it practical for real-world robotic applications. It can handle various dynamic scenarios including objects being pushed, falling, picked up, and even deformable objects like ropes, significantly outperforming approaches that use only physics simulation or visual tracking alone.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. The Gaussian-Particle Representation

The core innovation in this research is the "Gaussian-Particle" representation, which consists of two coupled components:

1. **3D Gaussians**: These handle visual representation and rendering. Each Gaussian is defined by its position, covariance matrix (which determines its size and orientation), and color. They can be efficiently rendered using Gaussian Splatting techniques.

2. **Particles**: These handle physical simulation. Particles follow physical laws like gravity and respond to collisions. They are connected by constraints to maintain object shape.

The key insight is that these components are coupled: each Gaussian is associated with one or more particles, and the positions of the Gaussians are influenced by both the physics simulation of particles and visual correction based on camera observations.

This dual representation allows the system to leverage the strengths of both approaches: the visual accuracy of Gaussian Splatting and the predictive capability of physics simulation.

#### 3.2. System Architecture

The system operates in a continuous cycle with two main phases:

1. **Prediction Phase**: The system uses Position-Based Dynamics (PBD) to simulate how particles (and their associated Gaussians) will move based on physical laws. This provides a physics-based prediction of the future state.

2. **Correction Phase**: The predicted state is rendered from the viewpoints of the available cameras, and the rendered images are compared to the actual camera images. The difference drives corrections to the Gaussians' positions, which then influence the particles' positions through "visual forces."

This prediction-correction loop runs at 30 Hz, allowing the system to continuously update its world model based on real-time observations. The visual forces provide a mechanism to correct the physics simulation when its predictions don't match reality, ensuring the world model remains synchronized with the actual environment.

#### 3.3. Initialization Process

Before the system can track objects, it needs to initialize the Gaussian-Particle representation. This process involves several steps:

1. **Pointcloud Initialization**: Create an initial pointcloud from RGBD cameras.

2. **Segmentation**: Segment the pointcloud using instance masks to identify individual objects.

3. **Gaussian Initialization**: Fill the bounding box of each object with spherical Gaussians and remove those outside the object mask.

4. **Initial Training**: Train the Gaussians using a reconstruction loss to improve visual quality.

5. **Particle Initialization**: Initialize particles at Gaussian locations and create shape-matching constraints to maintain object structure.

6. **Final Training**: Continue training the combined representation with additional constraints for collision avoidance.

This initialization process creates a physically-aware visual representation for each object in the scene, which serves as the foundation for subsequent tracking and interaction.

#### 3.4. Online Prediction and Correction

Once initialized, the system enters its real-time operation cycle, which consists of:

1. **Physics Prediction**: The PBD simulator advances the particle positions based on physical laws, including gravity, collisions, and shape constraints.

2. **Gaussian Update**: The Gaussians' positions are updated based on their associated particles.

3. **Visual Rendering**: The scene is rendered from the viewpoints of the available cameras.

4. **Visual Optimization**: The Gaussians are optimized to minimize the photometric error between rendered and observed images.

5. **Visual Force Computation**: The displacement of Gaussians during optimization is used to compute "visual forces" that are applied to the particles.

6. **Force Integration**: The visual forces are integrated into the PBD simulation, adjusting the particles' positions.

The visual forces are a key innovation that allows the system to correct physics-based predictions when they deviate from reality. This is particularly important in scenarios where the physics model may be incomplete or inaccurate, such as when dealing with complex interactions or unknown material properties.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Experimental Results

The authors evaluated their system on a wide range of scenarios, both in simulation and real-world settings:

**Simulated Scenarios**:
- Single object manipulation
- Multiple object interactions
- Objects being pushed over
- Objects being picked up
- Deformable rope manipulation

**Real-World Scenarios**:
- Similar set of tasks performed with a real robot and physical objects

The results demonstrate that the proposed system significantly outperforms baselines:
- **Physics Only**: A system that uses only physics simulation without visual correction
- **D3DGS**: Dynamic 3D Gaussian Splatting, which uses visual tracking without physics
- **CoTracker**: A state-of-the-art visual tracking method

The combined approach shows dramatically lower tracking error, especially in challenging scenarios like objects being pushed or falling where physics-only approaches tend to accumulate error over time. The system also handles cases where visual tracking alone would fail, such as occlusions or rapid movements.

#### 4.2. Parameter Sensitivity Analysis

The authors conducted extensive experiments to understand how different parameters affect system performance:

Key findings include:
- **Number of Cameras**: Performance improves with more cameras, but even with just 3 cameras, the system performs well.
- **Image Resolution**: Higher resolutions generally improve performance, but the system is robust even at lower resolutions.
- **Visual Gain**: This parameter controls how strongly visual forces influence the physics simulation. Moderate values (60-90) perform best.
- **Learning Rate**: The learning rate for Gaussian position optimization affects tracking accuracy, with values around 2.0e-3 providing good results.
- **Optimization Steps**: More optimization steps per physics step improve accuracy, with diminishing returns after about 7 steps.

The authors also analyzed the impact of different physical priors:

The system performs best when all physical priors are included, but remains functional even when some priors are removed, demonstrating robustness to model limitations.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

#### 5.1. Limitations and Future Work

Despite its impressive performance, the system has several limitations:

1. **Initialization Requirements**: The system requires good initialization with RGBD data and instance segmentation, which might not always be available.

2. **Handling of Physical Failures**: When the physics prediction fails dramatically, the system may struggle to recover, as shown in some failure cases.

3. **Computational Requirements**: While running at 30 Hz, the system still requires significant computational resources.

4. **Material Properties**: The current system doesn't model complex material properties or non-rigid deformations in detail.

Future work could address these limitations by:
- Developing more sophisticated initialization methods
- Incorporating learning-based approaches to improve physics prediction
- Extending the representation to handle more complex material properties
- Integrating with robot planning and control systems

#### 5.2. Conclusion

"Physically Embodied Gaussian Splatting" represents a significant advancement in world modeling for robotics. By combining the visual fidelity of Gaussian Splatting with the predictive power of particle-based physics and a novel visual correction mechanism, the system achieves accurate, real-time tracking and prediction of dynamic scenes.

The system's ability to continually correct itself based on visual feedback makes it particularly valuable for robotics applications, where the environment is constantly changing and perfect physics models are rarely available. The comprehensive experiments demonstrate that this approach significantly outperforms both pure physics simulation and pure visual tracking methods across a wide range of scenarios.

This work bridges an important gap between perception and physical reasoning in robotics, providing a foundation for more sophisticated robot interaction with dynamic environments. The real-time performance and robustness to various scenarios suggest that this approach could be integrated into practical robotics systems for manipulation, navigation, and human-robot interaction.
