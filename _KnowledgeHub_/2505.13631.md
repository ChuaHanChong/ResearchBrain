# Learning (Approximately) Equivariant Networks via Constrained Optimization

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of Stuttgart and École Polytechnique developed Adaptive Constrained Equivariance (ACE), a framework that automatically balances model performance with the degree of equivariance enforced in neural networks. This method improves accuracy, sample efficiency, and robustness on datasets with partial or imperfect symmetries by reformulating training as a constrained optimization problem.

## Problem

- Strictly equivariant neural networks (ENNs) struggle with complex loss landscapes and perform suboptimally when real-world data deviates from perfect symmetry due to noise or variations.
- Existing relaxation methods for ENNs are often heuristic, requiring extensive manual tuning of hyperparameters, penalty weights, or schedules, limiting their general applicability.
- Prior approaches may not guarantee specific equivariance levels in the final model or effectively adapt to partial symmetries present in the data.

## Method

- The ==Adaptive Constrained Equivariance== (ACE) framework approaches ENN training as a constrained optimization problem, leveraging dual methods to automatically and adaptively adjust the degree of equivariance.
- A homotopic network architecture is introduced, where each layer consists of an equivariant component and a non-equivariant component, scaled by learnable parameters (modulation coefficients γi). When all γi=0γi=0, the network is strictly equivariant.
- For strictly equivariant data, equality constraints (γi=0γi=0) are enforced through gradient descent-ascent; for partially symmetric data, resilient inequality constraints (∣γi∣≤ui| ≤ u_i∣γi∣≤ui) with learnable slack variables uiu_iui adaptively relax equivariance based on data.

## Results

- ACE-trained models showed improved final performance and sample efficiency, for example, achieving lower MSE on N-Body simulations with approximately **44%** fewer training samples and reducing MAE across all QM9 molecular property targets.
- The framework enhanced robustness, with ACE-trained equivariant models converging rapidly and maintaining significantly higher test accuracy under severe input degradation (e.g., 0-**85%** random point dropout on ModelNet40) where baselines failed.
- Analysis of modulation coefficients and slack variables revealed adaptive training dynamics, where early layers might acquire more flexibility while later layers remain tightly constrained, demonstrating the method's ability to negotiate equivariance based on data characteristics.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Formulating equivariance control as a constrained optimization problem allows for automated, data-driven adaptation of equivariance without the need for manual tuning of penalty weights or schedules.
> - The homotopic architecture, combined with dual variable optimization, enables a smooth, annealing-like process, guiding the model from an initially flexible state to a more constrained, equivariant one.
> - The framework can adaptively allocate flexibility in specific layers (via slack variables uiu_iui for resilient constraints) to account for local symmetry-breaking effects, thereby optimizing performance while preserving as much equivariance as possible.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Equivariant neural networks (ENNs) represent a class of models designed to integrate known symmetries into their architectural design. This integration often leads to improved generalization capabilities and enhanced sample efficiency, particularly when the underlying data distribution exhibits such symmetries. ENNs have found applications across various domains, including computer vision, graph learning, and 3D structure modeling. Architectures such as group equivariant convolutional networks, SchNet for molecular systems, and Tensor Field Networks for 3D point clouds exemplify this approach.

Despite their theoretical advantages and empirical successes, training strictly equivariant models can present challenges. Their inherent symmetries can lead to complex loss landscapes, which may hinder optimization even when data perfectly aligns with the assumed symmetries. A more prevalent issue in real-world scenarios is that data frequently deviates from perfect symmetry due to factors such as noise, measurement biases, structural variations, or physical phenomena like dynamical phase transitions. In such cases, strictly equivariant models may struggle to accurately fit the data, potentially leading to suboptimal performance.

To address these limitations, a line of research has explored methods for relaxing or approximating equivariance during training. These approaches can be broadly categorized into architectural modifications and training-time relaxation strategies. Architectural modifications involve softening weight sharing or incorporating non-equivariant components alongside equivariant ones, such as Residual Pathway Priors or methods using weighted filter banks. Training-time relaxations often involve adding penalties for equivariance violations to the loss function, with penalty weights adjusted throughout training, or perturbing the underlying architecture with non-equivariant components that are gradually reduced according to a predefined schedule.

However, existing relaxation strategies frequently rely on heuristic approaches, requiring domain-specific knowledge or extensive manual tuning of hyperparameters, penalty weights, or schedules. This manual intervention can limit their practical applicability and may not guarantee the desired balance between model performance and the degree of equivariance achieved. Some methods also fail to guarantee that the final solution will be strictly equivariant, or struggle to adapt to partial symmetries present in the data.

This work introduces a novel framework that seeks to overcome these limitations by offering an automated, general-purpose strategy for navigating the trade-offs between strict equivariance and model flexibility. It aims to provide a principled method for relaxing and tightening equivariance constraints without requiring manual tuning or domain-specific penalties, thereby fitting into the landscape by enhancing the robustness and applicability of equivariant neural networks to real-world, imperfectly symmetric data.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from the inherent tension between the theoretical benefits of strictly equivariant neural networks and the practical challenges encountered when applying them to real-world data. While ENNs leverage symmetries to improve sample efficiency and generalization, their strict adherence to these symmetries can lead to:
1. **Complex Loss Landscapes:** The constrained nature of equivariant architectures can create intricate optimization landscapes, making training difficult even when the data perfectly aligns with the model's symmetries.
2. **Mismatched Symmetries with Real-World Data:** Real-world datasets are often imperfectly symmetric due to noise, measurement errors, structural variations, or underlying physical processes that break symmetries. Strictly equivariant models may not generalize well or achieve optimal performance when these ideal conditions are violated.
3. **Limitations of Existing Relaxation Methods:** Prior approaches to relax equivariance, whether through architectural modifications or training objective penalties, often depend on sensitive hyperparameters, require manual tuning of schedules, or demand domain-specific knowledge to design effective intermediate stages. Such methods may not guarantee specific levels of equivariance in the final model or effectively adapt to partial symmetries.

Given these challenges, the authors aim to address the following core question: Is it possible to develop an automated, general-purpose strategy that can effectively navigate the trade-offs between enforcing equivariance and allowing model flexibility, without requiring manual tuning or domain-specific interventions?

To answer this, the paper draws motivation from homotopy (or continuation) principles in optimization. This strategy involves solving a complex problem by gradually transforming a simpler, surrogate problem into the target one, tracking solutions along the way. Applied to ENNs, this suggests that gradually introducing equivariance, rather than strictly imposing it from the start, could smooth the training process and guide the model towards a more effective solution.

The key objectives of this work are thus:
- To develop a principled, automated framework for training equivariant models that can adaptively balance downstream performance with equivariance.
- To eliminate the need for manually tuned penalties, weights, or schedules, which are common in existing relaxation methods.
- To enable the model to automatically identify and account for symmetry-breaking effects in data, relaxing equivariance where necessary to mitigate its impact on performance while still exploiting partial symmetries.
- To provide theoretical guarantees regarding the approximation error and equivariance level of the resulting models.
- To empirically evaluate the proposed framework across diverse architectures and tasks, demonstrating improvements in performance metrics, sample efficiency, and robustness.

By achieving these objectives, the research seeks to offer a practical middle ground between strictly equivariant and entirely unrestricted models, making the benefits of symmetry more broadly accessible in machine learning applications.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology, termed Adaptive Constrained Equivariance (ACE), approaches the training of equivariant neural networks as a constrained optimization problem. This framework leverages dual methods, which are conceptually related to homotopy optimization, to automatically and adaptively adjust the degree of equivariance enforced during training.

**Homotopic Architecture:**
The core architectural modification involves defining a network $f_{\theta,\gamma}$ parameterized by standard weights $\theta$ and a set of homotopic parameters $\gamma \in \mathbb{R}^L$, where $L$ is the number of layers. Each layer $i$ is constructed as a sum of an equivariant component $f_{\text{eq},i}$ and a non-equivariant component $f_{\text{neq},i}$, scaled by $\gamma_i$:
$f_{\theta,\gamma} = f^L_{\theta,\gamma} \circ \cdots \circ f^1_{\theta,\gamma}$ with $f^i_{\theta,\gamma} = f^i_{\text{eq},\theta} + \gamma_i f^i_{\text{neq},\theta}$.
When $\gamma_i = 0$ for all layers, the network $f_{\theta,0}$ is strictly equivariant. As $|\gamma_i|$ increases, the model's deviation from strict equivariance is allowed to grow. The non-equivariant components $f^i_{\text{neq},\theta}$ can be simple linear layers or small multi-layer perceptrons (MLPs). The method's general applicability is noted, extending beyond this specific architecture to any $f_{\theta,\gamma}$ differentiable with respect to $\theta$ and $\gamma$, where $f_{\theta,0}$ is equivariant.

**Training for Strictly Equivariant Data (Equality Constraints - Algorithm 1):**
For scenarios where the data is assumed to be perfectly equivariant, the training objective is formulated as a constrained optimization problem (PI):
$\text{minimize}_{\theta,\gamma} \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}} \left[ \ell_0 \left( f_{\theta,\gamma}(x), y \right) \right] \quad \text{subject to } \gamma_i = 0, \text{ for } i = 1, \ldots, L.$
This formulation is solved using its empirical dual problem, which involves optimizing an empirical Lagrangian $\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta, \gamma, \lambda) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=1}^N \ell_0(f_{\theta,\gamma}(x_n), y_n) + \sum_{i=1}^L \lambda_i \gamma_i$, where $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}^L$ are Lagrange multipliers (dual variables).

Algorithm 1 performs a gradient descent-ascent optimization:
1. **Primal Update:** $\theta$ and $\gamma$ are updated via gradient descent on $\hat{\mathcal{L}}$. Critically, $\gamma_i^{(0)}$ are initialized to 1, starting with a flexible, non-equivariant model.
2. **Dual Update:** $\lambda_i$ are updated via gradient ascent. The update rule $\lambda_i^{(t+1)} = \lambda_i^{(t)} + \eta_d \gamma_i^{(t)}$ shows that $\lambda_i$ accumulates positive or negative values proportional to the constraint violations (i.e., non-zero $\gamma_i$).
This mechanism acts as an annealing process. As training progresses, the dual variables $\lambda_i$ exert pressure on $\gamma_i$ to move towards zero (due to the $\lambda_i \gamma_i$ term in the Lagrangian and the gradient of $\hat{\mathcal{L}}$ with respect to $\gamma_i$ including $\lambda_i$). This data-driven process allows the model to transition from an initially flexible state to a strictly equivariant one, balancing the primary objective function $\ell_0$ with the equivariance requirement without manual schedules.
Theorem 4.1 provides a bound on the approximation error between $f_{\theta,\gamma}(x)$ and $f_{\theta,0}(x)$, indicating that if $\gamma_i$ values are small at convergence, the error introduced by setting them to zero (to obtain a strictly equivariant model) is negligible.

**Training for Partially Equivariant Data (Resilient Constraints - Algorithm 2):**
When data exhibits partial or imperfect symmetries, the strict equality constraints ($\gamma_i=0$) can be too restrictive. In such cases, Algorithm 1 might lead to oscillations in $\gamma_i$ or non-vanishing $\lambda_i$, signaling that the imposed symmetry is not fully aligned with the data. Theorem 4.2 formally establishes that the magnitude of $\gamma$ provides a measure of non-equivariance, enabling control over the model's equivariance level.

To adapt to partial symmetries, the paper introduces a resilient constrained learning framework, replacing (PI) with (PII):
$\text{minimize}_{\theta,\gamma,u} \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}} \left[ \ell_0 \left( f_{\theta,\gamma}(x), y \right) \right] + \frac{\rho}{2} \|u\|^2 \quad \text{subject to } |\gamma_i| \le u_i, \text{ for } i = 1, \ldots, L.$
Here, $u \in \mathbb{R}^L_+$ represents slack variables, which are also optimized. They allow adaptive relaxation of the equivariance constraints. The term $\frac{\rho}{2} \|u\|^2$ penalizes large slack values, creating a trade-off: increasing $u_i$ (relaxing the constraint) improves $\ell_0$ but increases the penalty. It is shown that optimal slack $u_i^*$ is proportional to the corresponding Lagrange multiplier $\lambda_i^*$.

Algorithm 2 extends Algorithm 1 by:
1. **Slack Update:** An additional gradient descent step is included for the slack variables $u_i$.
2. **Dual Update (Inequality):** The dual variables $\lambda_i$ are updated with a projection onto the non-negative orthant (i.e., $[\lambda_i + \eta_d (|\gamma_i| - u_i)]_+$) to reflect the inequality constraints.
This resilient approach allows the model to automatically determine which layers require more flexibility (larger $u_i$) to achieve better performance, while still preserving as much equivariance as possible. Spectral Normalization is employed on the non-equivariant layers $f^i_{\text{neq},\theta}$ to control their scale and ensure bounded equivariance error. The method thus adaptively balances the strictness of equivariance with performance, guided by the data itself.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical evaluation of Adaptive Constrained Equivariance (ACE) addresses three primary research questions across various architectural types and datasets.

**RQ1: Final Performance and Sample Efficiency**
- **N-Body Simulations (SEGNN):** When applied to the N-Body dataset, the SEGNN model trained with ACE's equality constraint (`f_eq`) demonstrated faster convergence and achieved a lower final Mean Squared Error (MSE) compared to the vanilla SEGNN baseline. Furthermore, the ACE-trained `f_eq` model achieved performance comparable to or exceeding the vanilla SEGNN using approximately 44% fewer training samples (5000 samples vs. 9000 samples). It also outperformed other baselines, including a heuristic-based relaxation method and a non-equivariant SEGNN relying on data augmentation.
- **Molecular Property Regression (QM9):** For the invariant SchNet architecture on the QM9 dataset, incorporating ACE led to a reduction in Mean Absolute Error (MAE) across all quantum-chemical targets, indicating improved optimization and predictive accuracy. When applied to the more flexible SEGNN on QM9, ACE provided an additional performance boost for certain molecular properties, suggesting its utility even when the base architecture is already well-aligned with data symmetries.
- **3D Shape Classification (ModelNet40, VN-DGCNN):** On the ModelNet40 benchmark, the ACE-trained equivariant model (`f_eq`) achieved notable gains of +2.78 and +1.77 percentage points in class and instance accuracy, respectively, over normal training. A partially equivariant ACE variant (`f_eq + f_neq`) also yielded comparable improvements.

**RQ2: Training Dynamics and Robustness**
- **Noisy 3D Shape Classification (ModelNet40):** Under input degradation (0-85% random point dropout), the standard equivariant baseline failed to converge. In contrast, the ACE-trained equivariant projection (`f_eq`) converged rapidly and consistently achieved significantly higher test accuracy, demonstrating enhanced robustness to input perturbations.
- **Relaxed Equivariance for Motion Capture (CMU MoCap, EGNO):** On the CMU Motion Capture dataset, the performance of the ACE-trained equivariant projection (`f_eq`) was similar to the vanilla EGNO. However, the partially equivariant model (`f_eq + f_neq`) trained with an equality constraint outperformed both, indicating that strict equivariance might be overly restrictive for this dataset. The most significant improvement was observed with the ACE resilient inequality constraint approach (`f_eq + f_neq`), which further reduced test MSE on both "Run" and "Walk" tasks. This suggests that the adaptive allocation of non-equivariant capacity by resilient constraints led to better predictive performance.
- **Modulation Coefficients Dynamics ($\gamma_k$ and $u_k$):**
 - **ModelNet40 (VN-DGCNN, equality constraints):** The modulation coefficients $\gamma_k$ for early layers decayed rapidly to zero within the first third of training, enforcing strict equivariance. Deeper layers, however, exhibited brief oscillations around zero before converging, suggesting an initial exploration of non-equivariant directions that ultimately led to an improved strictly equivariant solution.
 - **CMU MoCap (EGNO, equality constraints):** In contrast, on the MoCap dataset, the $\gamma_k$ coefficients for EGNO oscillated around zero throughout training, indicating that imposing strict equivariance was consistently restrictive and that the optimizer continually explored non-equivariant paths.
 - **CMU MoCap (EGNO, resilient constraints):** The learned slack values $u_k$ in the resilient model showed an adaptive behavior. Early layers acquired larger slack values, allowing for more flexibility in representing local structures, while later layers remained more tightly constrained, maintaining a higher degree of equivariance. All constraints gradually tightened as training progressed. These dynamics illustrate ACE's ability to guide the model towards suitable approximately equivariant behavior, stabilize training under degradation, and improve performance.

**RQ3: Theory-Empirics Alignment**
- **Equivariance Deviation:** Empirical measurements of equivariance deviation for the ACE resilient model on the CMU MoCap dataset showed a steady decrease, approaching values near zero by the end of training. This observation aligns with the theoretical bounds provided in Theorem 4.2, confirming that the equivariance constraint is effectively enforced in practice and that partial equivariance can be preserved in a stable and interpretable manner.

Regarding computational overhead, ACE introduces additional (non-equivariant) MLP layers during training, which modestly increases parameter counts and training/validation times compared to baselines (Table 4). However, at inference, these non-equivariant layers are dropped for `f_eq` models, resulting in virtually identical runtimes to the pure equivariant models.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work introduces Adaptive Constrained Equivariance (ACE), a framework that offers a principled and automated approach to training equivariant neural networks. Its significance lies in addressing several critical limitations of existing methods for incorporating symmetries into deep learning models.

**Key Contributions and Impact:**
- **Automated and Principled Equivariance Control:** ACE moves beyond heuristic relaxation strategies by formulating equivariant training as a constrained optimization problem. By leveraging dual methods and homotopy principles, it automatically balances model performance with the degree of equivariance enforced, eliminating the need for manual tuning of penalties, weights, or complex schedules. This automation makes equivariant networks more accessible and robust for practitioners.
- **Enhanced Performance and Efficiency:** The empirical results consistently demonstrate that ACE improves accuracy, sample efficiency, and convergence speed across diverse tasks (N-body simulations, molecular property regression, 3D shape classification, motion forecasting) and architectures (SEGNN, SchNet, VN-DGCNN, EGNO). The ability to achieve similar or better performance with fewer training samples is particularly impactful in data-scarce domains.
- **Robustness to Imperfect Symmetries:** Real-world data often deviates from perfect symmetry. ACE's ability to accommodate partial equivariance through resilient constraints allows models to adaptively relax strict symmetry requirements where data irregularities exist, leading to improved predictive performance and robustness to input degradation (e.g., noisy point clouds). The dynamic evolution of the modulation coefficients ($\gamma$) and slack variables ($u$) provides insights into where and how much flexibility is beneficial, offering a data-driven equilibrium between equivariance and non-equivariance.
- **Theoretical Grounding:** The provision of explicit theoretical bounds on approximation error for strictly equivariant models and equivariance error for partially equivariant models offers a deeper understanding of the method's behavior and provides guarantees on the model's properties.
- **Practical Middle Ground:** ACE offers a flexible conceptualization of symmetry as a "negotiable" inductive bias. It allows models to exploit generalization benefits of symmetry when aligned with data, while providing necessary flexibility for optimization or when symmetries are imperfect. This provides a practical and effective middle ground between rigid strictly equivariant models and unconstrained, symmetry-agnostic models.

**Future Directions and Broader Impact:**
The paper acknowledges minor computational overhead during training due to auxiliary non-equivariant layers. Future work could focus on designing architectures or update rules that break equivariance without adding parameters, improving runtime efficiency. Extending the framework to other symmetry groups and data modalities beyond point clouds, molecules, and motion trajectories (e.g., 2D image convolutions or graph neural networks) is a natural next step, with initial toy experiments suggesting feasibility. Co-designing network architectures that anticipate dynamic constraint tightening and loosening could further accelerate convergence and reduce data requirements. Furthermore, exploiting the information encoded in the learned dual variables for adaptive pruning or sparsification of non-equivariant components could lead to more efficient models.

In a broader societal context, the advancements in training equivariant networks, particularly those capable of handling real-world data imperfections, have potential benefits in scientific fields such as drug discovery (e.g., more accurate molecular property prediction) and materials science (e.g., modeling physical systems with complex symmetries). The authors do not foresee any negative societal consequences from this research.
