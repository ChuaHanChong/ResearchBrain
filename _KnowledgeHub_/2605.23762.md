# Direct Dynamic Retargeting for Humanoid Imitation Learning from Videos

> [!summary] Summary
> The Direct Dynamic Retargeting (DDR) framework, developed by researchers from LAAS-CNRS and ANITI, converts noisy human video demonstrations into dynamically feasible humanoid robot trajectories using a single-stage Model Predictive Control approach. This significantly improves the physical consistency and tracking accuracy of robot reference motions, leading to faster and more robust learning for reinforcement learning policies, demonstrated by successful zero-shot sim-to-real transfer on a Unitree H1-2 robot.

## Problem

- Humanoid imitation learning from videos is hindered by noisy and physically inconsistent pose extraction from monocular demonstrations.
- Traditional motion retargeting methods (Geometric and Indirect Dynamic Retargeting) introduce a 'geometric bias' from kinematic projections, leading to physically infeasible or suboptimal dynamic robot trajectories.
- The morphological mismatch between humans and robots, combined with biased references, results in slow reinforcement learning convergence or suboptimal robot behaviors.

## Method

- Introduced Direct Dynamic Retargeting (DDR), a single-stage ==Model Predictive Control== (MPC) framework that directly computes dynamically feasible robot trajectories from noisy expert video keypoints.
- DDR minimizes an augmented distance function, combining spatial tracking and relative shape-matching via a Laplacian matrix, between robot keypoints and expert keypoints within a physics simulator.
- The optimization problem is solved using the derivative-free Cross-Entropy Method (CEM) within a receding horizon MPC scheme, which is robust to ambiguities in contact sequence identification from video data.

## Results

- DDR-generated trajectories demonstrated superior physical feasibility (e.g., **0%** infeasibility for simpler motions) and higher contact sequence accuracy (e.g., **13.71%** error for one-foot balance vs. GR's **32.12%**), while mitigating foot slippage.
- Reinforcement learning agents trained with DDR references achieved faster convergence and higher final cumulative rewards (e.g., pistol squat policies converged **37%** faster and achieved **12%** higher reward than IDR).
- DDR policies exhibited superior reference tracking fidelity (e.g., pistol squat: **0.729** rad joint RMSE vs. GR's **0.976** rad), enabling robust zero-shot sim-to-real transfer of agile motions onto a Unitree H1-2 humanoid robot.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Eliminating the intermediate kinematic projection (geometric bias) in motion retargeting is essential for generating truly dynamically optimal and feasible robot trajectories from human demonstrations.
> - Directly incorporating dynamic constraints and physics simulation from the outset yields higher fidelity references, which are critical for efficient and robust reinforcement learning.
> - Derivative-free optimization methods like CEM can effectively handle noisy video data and implicitly identify complex contact sequences without requiring explicit prior knowledge.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Humanoid robotics has advanced significantly, particularly through the application of Reinforcement Learning (RL) for complex locomotion and loco-manipulation tasks. However, training RL agents for intricate skills often requires extensive and challenging manual reward engineering. Imitation Learning (IL) offers an alternative by enabling robots to acquire skills directly from expert demonstrations.

A primary challenge in IL for humanoids is obtaining high-quality expert data. While traditional methods rely on precise Motion Capture (MoCap) systems, recent efforts have focused on extracting human poses from more scalable sources such as monocular videos. However, video-based pose extraction frequently yields noisy and physically inconsistent trajectories.

A further complication arises from the morphological mismatch between humans and humanoid robots, encompassing kinematic differences (e.g., limb proportions) and dynamic discrepancies (e.g., mass distribution, actuation limits). This necessitates "motion retargeting" to adapt human demonstrations for robotic platforms.

Existing motion retargeting strategies typically fall into two categories:
- **Geometric Retargeting (GR):** This approach maps human keypoints or joint angles to the robot's configuration space using Inverse Kinematics (IK). While effective for spatial matching and joint limit adherence, GR methods inherently disregard dynamic constraints, often producing physically infeasible robot trajectories.
- **Indirect Dynamic Retargeting (IDR):** These methods attempt to address GR's limitations by introducing a second stage where the kinematically retargeted GR reference is tracked by a controller or RL agent within a physics simulator. While ensuring dynamic feasibility, this two-stage pipeline is hypothesized to be constrained by the initial kinematic projection, potentially leading to a "geometric bias" that restricts the search space for dynamically optimal motions.

This research addresses the identified geometric bias of GR and IDR by proposing a single-stage, direct dynamic retargeting approach. It aims to generate dynamically feasible trajectories from video demonstrations without an intermediate kinematic projection, thereby seeking to provide more accurate and physically viable references for downstream imitation learning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to introduce Direct Dynamic Retargeting (DDR), a novel single-stage framework designed to generate high-fidelity, dynamically feasible trajectories for humanoid robots directly from expert video demonstrations.

The motivation for pursuing this objective stems from several identified limitations in current imitation learning pipelines for humanoid robots:
- **Scalability of Demonstrations:** While monocular video demonstrations offer a scalable source for teaching complex skills, current extraction tools often produce noisy and physically inconsistent human trajectories.
- **Morphological Mismatch:** A significant challenge is the inherent kinematic and dynamic differences between human bodies and humanoid robots. Raw human motion cannot be directly applied to robots without adaptation.
- **Limitations of Existing Retargeting Methods:**
 - **Geometric Retargeting (GR):** These methods prioritize kinematic similarity but ignore dynamic constraints, leading to physically infeasible robot motions that violate balance, torque, or contact dynamics. These references are unsuitable for direct robot execution or for robust training of RL agents.
 - **Indirect Dynamic Retargeting (IDR):** While IDR methods incorporate physics simulation to achieve dynamic feasibility, they typically refine a pre-computed geometric reference. The authors hypothesize that this intermediate kinematic projection introduces a "geometric bias." This bias restricts the search space for dynamically optimal behaviors, potentially yielding suboptimal dynamic solutions and reducing the overall accuracy of tracking the original human demonstration.
- **Impact on Imitation Learning:** The quality and physical viability of reference trajectories are critical for the efficiency and performance of subsequent imitation learning policies. Dynamically infeasible or biased references can lead to slow training convergence, suboptimal robot behaviors, undesirable artifacts, or outright learning failures.

By proposing DDR, the authors aim to bypass the geometric bias inherent in GR and IDR. Their goal is to directly compute dynamically feasible trajectories in the robot's task space, ensuring that the generated references are physically viable from the outset. This is intended to accelerate the training convergence of RL agents and enhance their ability to execute agile and balancing behaviors on real humanoid robots.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology introduces Direct Dynamic Retargeting (DDR), a Model Predictive Control (MPC) framework that computes dynamically feasible trajectories directly from noisy video data.

#### 3.1. Approach Overview

The core idea of DDR is to directly minimize the distance between the robot's keypoint trajectory and the expert demonstration's keypoint trajectory, *while simultaneously ensuring the robot's trajectory is dynamically feasible*.
- **Robot Model:** The robot's configuration ($q$) includes a floating base and $n_q$ actuated joints. A trajectory ($Q$) is a sequence of configurations, and a control sequence ($U$) represents joint torques or target positions.
- **Rollout Function:** A physics simulator's rollout function ($S_{q_0}: U \to Q$) simulates the robot's dynamics from an initial state ($q_0$) under controls $U$, accounting for contacts. This defines the "Feasibility Set" ($F_{q_0}$), which contains all physically attainable trajectories.
- **Keypoint Space:** An extended forward kinematics function ($FK: Q \to R^{3m \times T}$) maps a robot trajectory to its corresponding 3D keypoint trajectories ($x$) for $m$ keypoints over $T$ timesteps.
- **Expert Demonstration ($x_{ref}$):** This is the target keypoint trajectory extracted from the human video.
- **Retargeting Problem Formulation:**
 - **Geometric Retargeting (GR):** Finds $(Q_{GR}, x_{GR})$ minimizing $d(x_{GR}, x_{ref})$ and minimizing distance to $F_{q_0}$ (though it often produces infeasible $Q_{GR}$).
 - **Indirect Dynamic Retargeting (IDR):** Finds $(Q_{IDR}, x_{IDR}) \in F_{q_0} \times \tilde{F}_{q_0}$ by tracking $x_{GR}$ (i.e., minimizing $d(x_{IDR}, x_{GR})$) within a physics simulator.
 - **Direct Dynamic Retargeting (DDR):** Finds $(Q_{DDR}, x_{DDR}) \in F_{q_0} \times \tilde{F}_{q_0}$ by directly minimizing $d(x_{DDR}, x_{ref})$ (Equation 2). This formulation establishes that $d(x_{DDR}, x_{ref}) \le d(x_{IDR}, x_{ref})$, mathematically demonstrating that DDR provides a lower bound on the tracking error by avoiding the geometric bias introduced by $x_{GR}$.

#### 3.2. SMPL Keypoints Extraction

Human motion is reconstructed from monocular video inputs using the SMPL model, leveraging a feature extraction pipeline similar to VideoMimic. The reference signal ($x_{ref}$) for the robot consists of the trajectories of the torso, shoulders, hands, and feet keypoints.

#### 3.3. GR and IDR References

- **GR Reference:** Generated using Pyroki, which performs kinematic optimization from the extracted SMPL keypoints.
- **IDR Reference:** Computed using the same solver as DDR, but instead of tracking $x_{ref}$, it tracks the GR keypoint trajectory ($x_{GR}$). This allows for a direct comparison of the impact of the initial geometric bias.

#### 3.4. Dynamically Feasible Trajectory (DDR Core)

The generation of dynamically feasible trajectories involves minimizing an augmented distance function that considers both spatial tracking and relative shape-matching:
- **Spatial Tracking ($E_p$):** Standard Euclidean distance between robot keypoints $f_k(q_i)$ and reference keypoints $\tilde{x}_i$.
- **Relative Shape-Matching ($E_l$):** Uses a Laplacian matrix ($L$) to penalize structural deformations between neighboring keypoints, thereby preserving the local structural shape of the demonstration while being invariant to global translation and rotation. The total error $d$ is a combination of $E_p$ and $E_l$.

The optimization problem (Equation 2) is solved using the **Cross-Entropy Method (CEM)**. CEM is a derivative-free sampling approach, which is well-suited for handling ambiguities in contact sequence identification from noisy video data, unlike gradient-based solvers that often require predefined contact sequences. The implementation leverages the framework introduced in and operates within a **Model Predictive Control (MPC)** scheme with a receding horizon.

#### 3.5. Imitation via Reinforcement Learning

The offline-computed retargeted trajectories are used as references for training closed-loop RL policies. This approach aims to create policies that are robust to variations in initialization and external disturbances, facilitating sim-to-real transfer.
- **Problem Formulation:** The task is framed as a Constrained Markov Decision Process (CMDP). Policies are trained to maximize a discounted sum of future rewards while adhering to hardware safety constraints.
- **Framework:** An actor-critic architecture is used, specifically the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm integrated with the "Constraints-as-Terminations" (CAT) framework within IsaacLab.
- **Observation Space:** Includes joint positions, joint velocities, previous actions, base linear and angular velocities, base height, projected gravity, and a phase variable parameterizing the reference motion.
- **Action Space:** Desired joint positions, which are then scaled and tracked by a PD-controller.
- **Reward Function:** Inspired by the DeepMimic approach, it includes terms for tracking joint positions, joint velocities, root pose, root velocity, and end-effector positions. Penalty terms for joint acceleration, torques, action rate, joint velocity, and foot slip are also included to promote stable and physically plausible behavior.
- **Training:** Techniques such as reference state initialization and early termination are employed.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of DDR focused on two main areas: the quality of the retargeted trajectories themselves and the performance of downstream imitation learning policies. A dataset of five agile motions (squat, kung fu, one-foot balance, pistol squat, balancing stick) was collected from monocular videos of three human subjects. GR, IDR, and DDR references were generated for comparison.

#### 4.1. Retargeted Trajectories

- **Feasibility:** GR trajectories exhibited a high percentage of physically infeasible segments (3.08% to 28.38%). In contrast, IDR and DDR significantly improved feasibility, often achieving 0% infeasibility for simpler motions like squat and pistol squat, and much lower rates for complex ones (e.g., IDR 2.86%, DDR 3.36% for one-foot balance). This indicates that dynamic retargeting methods are effective at producing physically viable motions, with slight discrepancies noted due to different physics engines used for generation (Mujoco) and evaluation (Pinocchio).
- **Contact Sequence Accuracy:** DDR consistently produced contact sequences that more closely matched the manually labeled ground truth from videos (error rates ranging from 0.00% for squat to 13.71% for one-foot balance). GR frequently demonstrated contact breakage (e.g., 32.12% error for squat), while IDR often performed worse than DDR despite similar cost functions, suggesting the geometric bias from GR negatively impacts contact consistency (e.g., IDR 24.72% vs. DDR 13.71% for one-foot balance).
- **Feet Slippage:** GR trajectories showed frequent sliding artifacts due to the absence of a dynamic contact model. DDR and IDR, by incorporating dynamic consistency, effectively mitigated these slippage issues, enforcing stationary contact points.
- **Success Rate:** For stochastic methods, DDR consistently achieved higher success rates in generating complete trajectories without significant deviations or falls compared to IDR (e.g., DDR 100% vs. IDR 66.67% for kung fu; DDR 66.67% vs. IDR 0.00% for balancing stick). This indicates that the geometric bias in IDR's initialization can lead the solver towards unstable regions, resulting in failures.
- **Reference Tracking Accuracy:** When evaluating tracking accuracy using the Laplacian distance, GR typically achieved the lowest pelvis tracking error due to its disregard for physical feasibility. However, DDR generally outperformed IDR in aggregate keypoint tracking across all motions (e.g., mean Laplacian error of 0.055 for DDR vs. 0.055 for GR and 0.055 for IDR on squat; 0.065 for DDR vs. 0.062 for GR and 0.072 for IDR on kung fu). This suggests that while GR provides a kinematically close reference, its unphysical nature is detrimental, and IDR's reliance on this biased GR reference limits its ability to find truly optimal dynamic solutions. DDR achieved comparable or superior tracking to GR while maintaining dynamic feasibility.

#### 4.2. Imitation Policies

- **Learning Performance:** RL agents trained with DDR references demonstrated faster convergence and achieved higher final cumulative rewards across most motions compared to those trained with GR or IDR references. For instance, in the pistol squat, DDR policies achieved a final reward of 383.7 and 90% convergence at 79.6k environment steps, significantly outperforming GR (200.7 final, 128.4k steps) and IDR (340.8 final, 93.5k steps). This indicates that dynamically feasible and unbiased references provide a more coherent and effective learning signal for RL agents.
- **Policy Reference Tracking:** Policies trained on DDR references exhibited the highest fidelity in tracking their respective retargeted trajectories, resulting in lower Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) for joint configurations, mean Cartesian position error, and mean Laplacian error. For example, in the pistol squat, DDR policies had a joint RMSE of 0.729 rad, mean position error of 0.316 m, and mean Laplacian error of 0.244 m, which were lower than both GR (0.976 rad, 0.541 m, 0.329 m) and IDR (0.807 rad, 0.486 m, 0.284 m). This further supports the conclusion that DDR's direct dynamic optimization avoids the physical impossibility of GR targets and the kinematic bias of IDR, enabling the RL agent to track the reference more accurately and robustly.

#### 4.3. Real-World Experiments

The trained control policies were successfully deployed on a human-sized Unitree H1-2 humanoid robot. The experiments demonstrated zero-shot sim-to-real transfer for all evaluated motions (squat, pistol squat, kung fu, one-foot balance, and balancing stick) without task-specific manual tuning. The robot was observed to execute the target motions effectively, including a one-foot recovery jump during a pistol squat, indicating the robustness and practical utility of the framework.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces Direct Dynamic Retargeting (DDR), a method with implications for the field of humanoid robotics and imitation learning.

**Key Contributions and Significance:**
- **Elimination of Geometric Bias:** The work identifies and addresses a fundamental limitation in previous motion retargeting methods (Geometric Retargeting and Indirect Dynamic Retargeting) by eliminating the intermediate kinematic projection. This "geometric bias" has been shown to restrict the search space for dynamically optimal solutions. By directly optimizing for dynamic feasibility in task space, DDR generates trajectories that are both closer to the original human demonstration and physically viable for the robot.
- **High-Fidelity, Dynamically Feasible References:** DDR consistently produces retargeted trajectories with superior physical consistency, higher contact sequence accuracy, and reduced foot slippage compared to existing baselines. These high-quality references are crucial for effective imitation learning.
- **Improved Imitation Learning Performance:** The provision of physically coherent and unbiased reference trajectories by DDR leads to significant downstream benefits for Reinforcement Learning. RL agents trained with DDR references demonstrate faster convergence rates, achieve higher cumulative rewards, and exhibit improved reference tracking fidelity. This translates to more robust and accurate execution of complex, agile, and balancing behaviors.
- **Scalability for Video Demonstrations:** By leveraging human pose extraction from monocular videos and directly processing these noisy inputs into dynamically feasible robot motions, DDR contributes to making imitation learning more scalable. It paves the way for utilizing large, diverse online video datasets for teaching humanoid robots new skills.
- **Validation through Real-World Deployment:** The successful zero-shot sim-to-real transfer of DDR-trained policies onto a physical humanoid robot (Unitree H1-2) validates the practical utility and robustness of the framework. This demonstrates the potential for deploying these learned skills in real-world applications.

**Potential Impact:**
The DDR framework has the potential to accelerate the development of advanced humanoid robot capabilities. By providing a reliable method for generating high-quality training data, it could enable robots to learn a wider range of complex and dynamic skills from human examples more efficiently. This could foster breakthroughs in areas such as agile locomotion, complex manipulation, and human-robot interaction in unstructured environments. The method's ability to handle noisy video data also opens avenues for creating extensive, publicly available datasets of dynamically viable humanoid motions, which could benefit the broader research community.

However, the work acknowledges limitations, including the potential for ill-conditioned solutions due to a purely task-space objective and sensitivity to manual tuning of task-space weighting and initialization. Future research aims to address these limitations and further scale the framework for even larger and more diverse datasets from "in-the-wild" web videos.
