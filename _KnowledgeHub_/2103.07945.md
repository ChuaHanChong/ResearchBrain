# Learning One Representation to Optimize All Rewards

> [!summary] Summary
> By learning a universal Forward-Backward (FB) representation of environment dynamics, agents can instantly derive near-optimal policies for arbitrary, previously unseen reward functions after a single unsupervised exploration phase. This enables performance comparable to or surpassing specialized goal-oriented methods in multi-task scenarios, including complex environments like Ms. Pacman.

## Problem

- Reinforcement learning agents typically require costly retraining for each new reward function or task, limiting their adaptability.
- Existing model-based RL methods struggle with accurate long-term trajectory synthesis and necessitate computationally intensive planning for new tasks.
- Goal-oriented RL paradigms are often restricted to predefined sets of goals or features, failing to generalize to arbitrary or unobserved reward structures.

## Method

- The core solution is the **Forward-Backward (FB) representation**, which learns two functions (F and B) summarizing ==environment dynamics== in a compact form.
- The process involves an ==unsupervised, reward-free learning phase== where F and B are trained via a ==Temporal Difference==-like algorithm.
- Once F and B are learned, optimal policies for any new reward function can be instantly derived by computing a ==reward-specific vector z_R== and applying a ==direct policy formula==, eliminating the need for planning.

## Results

- The FB representation achieves performance comparable to or exceeding specialized goal-oriented baselines like **DQN** and **DQN+HER** across various environments, including pixel-based Ms. Pacman.
- It enables qualitative adaptation to complex, novel reward functions, such as avoiding forbidden states or reaching the nearest of multiple goals, without retraining.
- Visualizations confirm that the learned F and B embeddings capture the topological and dynamic structure of environments, reflecting meaningful relationships and reachability.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A compact Forward-Backward representation can universally encode environment dynamics, enabling the derivation of optimal policies for any reward function.
> - Optimal policies for arbitrary rewards can be directly computed from the learned representation and the reward, bypassing explicit planning.
> - The sub-optimality of policies derived from the FB representation is directly proportional to its approximation error, indicating theoretical robustness.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research sits at the intersection of unsupervised reinforcement learning (RL), representation learning, and multi-task/goal-oriented RL. It addresses a core challenge in RL: creating agents that can adapt to new tasks quickly and efficiently without retraining or extensive planning.

Traditionally, RL agents are trained for a specific reward function. When the task changes, the agent typically needs to be retrained, which is computationally expensive and data-intensive. This paper aims to overcome this limitation by learning a *single, universal representation* of the environment dynamics that can then be used to derive optimal policies for *any* reward function specified *a posteriori*, with minimal additional computation or planning.

The work differentiates itself from several existing paradigms:

- **Goal-oriented RL (e.g., HER, Universal Value Function Approximators)**: Methods like Hindsight Experience Replay (HER) [Andrychowicz et al., 2017] allow agents to learn to reach multiple goals efficiently, often by re-labeling past experiences. Universal Value Function Approximators (UVFAs) [Schaul et al., 2015] learn a value function conditioned on both the state and the goal. While effective for predefined sets of goals (e.g., reaching specific states), they cannot generalize to arbitrary, unobserved reward functions (e.g., dense rewards, weighted combinations of goals, or tasks involving "avoidance" without prior specification). This paper explicitly addresses this limitation by offering immediate adaptation to *any* reward, not just a pre-specified class.
- **Model-based RL**: Learning a full model of the world's dynamics (transition probabilities) is a common approach to enable planning for new tasks. However, synthesizing accurate long-term state trajectories is notoriously difficult in complex or high-dimensional environments [Talvitie, 2017]. Even with a perfect model, explicit planning (e.g., Monte Carlo Tree Search, value iteration) is often required for each new reward, which can be computationally intensive at test time. The proposed Forward-Backward (FB) representation avoids synthesizing state trajectories and eliminates the need for explicit planning during the test phase, directly providing optimal policies.
- **Successor Representations (SR) and Successor Features (SF)**: First introduced by Dayan, SRs represent the expected future occupancy of states. Successor Features (SF) [Barreto et al., 2017] generalize this by learning expected future cumulative discounted *features* rather than states. Universal Successor Feature Approximators (USFA) [Borsa et al., 2018] extend this to learn optimal policies for rewards that are linear combinations of a *finite, user-provided set of feature functions*. The current work reuses the policy parameterization from USFA but significantly departs by:
 - Introducing a *novel representation* (FB) that learns the successor measure directly, without relying on user-provided features for the rewards. This allows it to handle *any* reward function, including those not linearly spanned by predefined features, addressing a key limitation of USFA in continuous spaces where goal states are infinite.
 - Leveraging recent theoretical advances in *successor state learning* [Blier et al., 2021], which provide pointwise occupancy map estimates even in continuous spaces, bypassing the need for sparse rewards (like 1_s=goal).

In essence, this work aims to bridge the gap towards truly general-purpose RL agents that can explore an environment once in an unsupervised manner and then instantaneously solve any task within that environment, regardless of the reward structure, without further costly training or planning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to develop a method for **unsupervised reinforcement learning** that can:

1. **Learn a compact, unified representation of an MDP's dynamics**: This "summary" should encode enough information about the environment's transitions to derive optimal behaviors for any future task.
2. **Enable immediate policy derivation for arbitrary rewards**: Once the environment is explored and the representation is learned (in an unsupervised, reward-free phase), a policy for *any* newly specified reward function should be obtainable with minimal additional computation and *without* explicit planning or further training.
3. **Achieve near-optimal performance**: The derived policies should be provably near-optimal, with sub-optimality directly proportional to the representation's approximation error.
4. **Work in diverse environments**: The method should be applicable to both discrete and continuous state/action spaces, including high-dimensional, pixel-based inputs.

The primary motivation stems from the limitations of current RL paradigms:
- The **task-specific nature** of most RL algorithms necessitates expensive retraining for every new reward function.
- **Model-based RL's difficulty** in accurately synthesizing long-range trajectories and its inherent need for a planning phase.
- **Goal-oriented RL's inability** to adapt to general reward structures beyond a predefined set of goals or features.

By addressing these issues, the authors aim to take a significant step towards creating **controllable agents in arbitrary black-box stochastic environments**, where an agent can learn about its world first and then immediately respond to diverse tasks presented later.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the proposed methodology is the **Forward-Backward (FB) representation**, which comprises two functions, $F$ and $B$, that effectively summarize the environment's dynamics.

**Core Idea and Theoretical Foundation:**
The central theoretical insight is that for any policy $\pi_z$ parameterized by a vector $z$, its Q-function $Q^{\pi_z}_r$ (for a given reward $r$) can be expressed as a linear combination involving a "successor measure" $M^{\pi_z}$ and the reward $r$. The paper postulates the existence of $F: S \times A \times Z \to Z$ and $B: S \times A \to Z$ such that $F(s_0, a_0, z)^T B(s', a')$ approximates $M^{\pi_z}(s_0, a_0, s', a')$, the discounted probability of visiting $(s', a')$ from $(s_0, a_0)$ under $\pi_z$. If such functions exist and are learned perfectly, then the optimal policy for any reward $r$ is simply $\pi_{z_R}(s) = \text{arg max}_a F(s, a, z_R)^T z_R$, where $z_R = \mathbb{E}_{(s,a) \sim \rho}[r(s,a)B(s,a)]$ (Theorem 2). This means that once $F$ and $B$ are learned, there is no need for planning; the optimal policy is an explicit formula.

**Two-Phase Approach:**

1. **Unsupervised Learning Phase (Reward-Free Exploration):**
 - **Objective**: To learn $F$ and $B$ from reward-free interactions with the environment.
 - **Mechanism**: The learning is based on a Temporal Difference (TD)-like algorithm adapted from recent successor state learning methods [Blier et al., 2021]. The core idea is to train $F$ and $B$ to satisfy a Bellman-like equation for the successor measure $M^{\pi_z}$. The target for $F(s_0, a_0, z)^T B(s', a')$ is $1_{(s_0,a_0)=(s',a')} + \gamma \mathbb{E}_{s_1 \sim P(\cdot|s_0,a_0)}[F(s_1, \pi_z(s_1), z)^T B(s', a')]$.
 - **Loss Function**: The unsupervised loss aims to minimize the discrepancy between the predicted successor measure ($F^T B$) and the true successor measure of the policy $\pi_z$, averaged over a distribution of state-actions $\rho$ and randomly sampled $z$ values. An auxiliary loss is used to regularize $B$ (e.g., towards orthonormality) to address identifiability issues.
 - **Policy Parameterization**: The policies $\pi_z(s)$ are defined directly from $F$ and $z$ as $\text{arg max}_a F(s, a, z)^T z$. During training, a soft (Boltzmann) version of this greedy policy is used for stability.
 - **Exploration**: The method assumes access to an exploration scheme or replay buffer. In experiments, agents either use a uniform policy or the learned policies $\pi_z$ with randomly sampled $z$ values, indicating that the exploration itself is unsupervised.
 - **Function Approximation**: Deep learning methods (neural networks) are used for $F$ and $B$, allowing for operation in high-dimensional and continuous state spaces.

2. **Reward Estimation and Exploitation Phase (Test Time):**
 - **Reward Representation ($z_R$)**: Once a reward function $r(s,a)$ is specified, the policy parameter $z_R$ is computed as the expectation of $r(s,a)B(s,a)$ over the training data distribution $\rho$. This can be done by:
 - Explicit calculation if $r(s,a)$ is known (e.g., target state, where $z_R = B(\text{target state})$).
 - Averaging $r(s,a)B(s,a)$ over sampled reward observations.
 - Using a learned reward model $\hat{r}(s,a)$ and averaging $\hat{r}(s,a)B(s,a)$ over the replay buffer.
 - **Policy Derivation**: The optimal policy is directly obtained as $\pi_{z_R}(s) = \text{arg max}_a F(s, a, z_R)^T z_R$. No planning or further learning is required.
 - **Optional Fine-tuning**: While not strictly necessary in experiments, the Q-function could be fine-tuned with actual rewards via standard Q-learning if needed.
 - **Incorporating Prior Information**: The framework allows for incorporating prior knowledge about reward dependence (e.g., if rewards only depend on specific features $\phi(s,a)$), by letting $B$ be a function of $\phi(s,a)$ instead of $(s,a)$. This reduces model complexity and focuses learning.

**Theoretical Guarantees:**
The paper provides strong theoretical guarantees: if training is perfect, the policies are provably optimal for *all* reward functions (Theorem 2). With imperfect training, the sub-optimality is directly proportional to the approximation error of $F^T B$ (Theorems 8 and 9), highlighting the robustness of the approach.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The authors evaluate the FB representation across a range of diverse environments, including discrete and continuous mazes, the FetchReach robotic arm, and the pixel-based Ms. Pacman game.

#### 4.1. Goal-Oriented Setting (Quantitative Comparisons)

- **Performance Comparison**: For goal-reaching tasks (single-state sparse rewards), FB representation is quantitatively compared against goal-oriented baselines, primarily DQN (and DQN+HER for Ms. Pacman).
 - **Mazes and FetchReach**: FB performs comparably to or slightly better than DQN. The performance of FB consistently increases with the dimension $d$ of the representation space (e.g., FB-50, FB-75, FB-100), with higher dimensions matching or exceeding DQN's success rates.
 - **Ms. Pacman**: In this challenging pixel-based environment, vanilla DQN fails, requiring HER to perform. FB-100 (using $d=100$) matches or even surpasses the performance of DQN+HER, demonstrating its effectiveness in high-dimensional visual inputs and complex dynamics.
- **Dimension $d$ Impact**: The dimension $d$ (e.g., FB-25, FB-50, FB-75, FB-100, FB-200) directly impacts performance. A smaller $d$ can lead to "spatial blurring" of rewards and Q-functions, especially evident in Discrete Maze where policies for small $d$ might stop near the goal instead of exactly on it due to the approximation. Increasing $d$ improves policy quality.
- **Sample Efficiency**: FB shows no loss in sample efficiency compared to goal-oriented baselines. In Ms. Pacman, it even learns faster than DQN+HER, suggesting that learning a general representation can be more efficient than task-specific learning for multi-goal settings.

#### 4.2. More Complex Rewards (Qualitative Results)

A key strength of FB is its ability to adapt to novel, more complex reward functions *a posteriori* that goal-oriented baselines cannot handle without retraining.
- **Goal with Forbidden States**: The agent successfully learns to reach a target while avoiding specified forbidden regions (e.g., setting $z_R = B(\text{goal}) - \lambda \sum B(\text{forbidden states})$). Trajectories and Q-value heatmaps confirm the agent's ability to navigate around obstacles.
- **Nearest of Two Goals**: For the task of reaching the closest of two equally rewarding positions ($z_R = B(\text{goal}_0) + B(\text{goal}_1)$), the agent correctly identifies and moves towards the nearer target. This demonstrates its ability to handle non-linear combinations of Q-functions.
- **Distant Large vs. Close Small Reward**: The agent successfully trades off proximity and reward magnitude, demonstrating a nuanced understanding of reward structure.
These qualitative results confirm the "optimize all rewards" claim, showcasing the method's flexibility.

#### 4.3. Embedding Visualizations

- **F and B Embeddings**: Visualizations of the learned $F$ and $B$ state embeddings (projected to 2D using t-SNE) for mazes and Ms. Pacman reveal that they capture the topological and dynamic structure of the environment. States on opposite sides of walls are separated in the embedding space, while states on the same side cluster together. This indicates that the learned representations indeed encode meaningful relationships and reachability information, effectively summarizing the MDP dynamics.
- **Z-dependence of F**: Visualization of F embeddings for different $z$ vectors shows how $F$ changes its representation of a state's future based on the target reward, allowing for flexible policy generation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The FB representation makes several significant contributions to reinforcement learning:

1. **Towards General-Purpose Controllable Agents**: The most impactful contribution is the advancement towards building truly controllable agents. By learning a universal representation of the environment's dynamics, an agent can explore once and then immediately derive optimal policies for any task, specified through a reward function, without further environmental interaction or costly planning. This shifts the paradigm from task-specific training to general competence acquisition.
2. **Elimination of Planning at Test Time**: Unlike model-based RL, which requires explicit planning for new tasks, FB directly computes near-optimal policies via a simple function evaluation. This significantly reduces computational overhead during deployment or when adapting to rapidly changing objectives.
3. **Generalization Beyond Predefined Features/Goals**: By learning its own reward-dependent representation $B$, the method overcomes a major limitation of prior work like USFA, which relies on user-provided features. This enables handling of *arbitrary* reward functions, including complex, non-linear ones, which is crucial for real-world applicability.
4. **Theoretical Soundness and Robustness**: The rigorous theoretical guarantees (provable optimality with perfect training and proportionality to approximation error with imperfect training) provide a strong foundation for the method. The robustness to approximation errors and the ability to work with various norms enhance its practical viability.
5. **Efficiency in Multi-Task Settings**: The empirical results demonstrate that this unsupervised, general-purpose approach can achieve comparable or even superior sample efficiency to specialized goal-oriented methods like HER in multi-task scenarios, suggesting a promising path for broad applicability.

**Potential Impact**:
- **Accelerated RL Deployment**: By reducing the need for retraining or extensive planning, FB could significantly accelerate the deployment of RL agents in dynamic environments where tasks change frequently.
- **Foundation for AI Agents**: This work lays a foundation for more intelligent and adaptable AI agents that can rapidly generalize to novel objectives, moving towards greater autonomy and flexibility.
- **Improved Representation Learning**: The learned $F$ and $B$ representations themselves could be valuable for understanding environmental dynamics, potentially informing other areas of AI research, such as predictive modeling or latent space exploration.

**Limitations Acknowledged by Authors**:
- **Exploration Challenge**: The method assumes access to a good exploration strategy or a sufficiently diverse replay buffer; it does not solve the exploration problem itself.
- **Approximation Limitations**: Representing "all rewards" with a finite-dimensional vector $z_R$ implies information loss. Rewards uncorrelated with $B$ are treated as zero. A large dimension $d$ might be required for complex environments, which scales linearly with algorithm complexity.
- **Bias Towards Long-Range Behavior**: The method may implicitly bias towards learning long-range, spatially smooth relationships over local details of rewards, potentially blurring fine-grained reward functions (as observed with small $d$).
- **Variance in Large Environments**: Learning successor measures can be challenging in very large state spaces due to the need to sample independent state-action pairs, potentially leading to high variance.

Despite these limitations, the FB representation represents a significant step towards learning versatile, controllable agents capable of adapting to a wide range of tasks in complex, unknown environments.
