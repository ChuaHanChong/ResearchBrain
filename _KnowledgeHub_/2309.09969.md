# Prompt a Robot to Walk with Large Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> A study by Wang et al. demonstrates that off-the-shelf Large Language Models, specifically GPT-4, can serve as low-level feedback controllers for high-dimensional, dynamic robot systems like quadrupedal robots. The research shows that carefully engineered text prompts, incorporating historical observation-action sequences and numerical normalization, enable robots to perform walking behaviors in simulation environments without task-specific fine-tuning.

## Problem

- Existing LLM applications in robotics are primarily for high-level planning, not direct low-level control of complex dynamic systems.
- Developing robot foundation models for low-level control requires extensive, robot-specific training data, which is often difficult and costly to acquire.
- Effectively grounding LLMs in physical environments to generate the precise, dynamic motions required for robotic tasks remains a challenge.

## Method

- A framework was developed that enables an off-the-shelf LLM (GPT-4) to function as a low-level feedback controller, directly outputting target joint positions for robotic locomotion.
- A multi-component text prompt design was introduced, combining static textual context (task description, I/O meaning, joint order, control pipeline) with dynamic few-shot examples of historical observation-action pairs.
- A numerical normalization method was implemented to convert floating-point sensor readings and actions into non-negative integers, optimizing for LLM tokenization and improving the model's ability to infer numerical relationships.

## Results

- Successfully prompted an A1 quadruped robot to walk on both flat ground and uneven terrain in simulation at 10 Hz, demonstrating a degree of robustness against environmental disturbances.
- Performance was highly dependent on prompt design, showing that a comprehensive description prompt, longer historical observation-action sequences (up to the 8k token limit), and rich observation inputs significantly improved walking time and success rate.
- The framework demonstrated generalizability by enabling an ANYmal quadruped robot to walk in a different simulation environment (Isaac Gym), indicating its potential applicability across various robotic platforms and simulation settings.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Off-the-shelf LLMs can generate dynamic robot motions by interpreting historical observation-action sequences through in-context learning, extending their role beyond high-level planning to direct low-level control.
> - Textual descriptions within the prompt are critical for grounding LLMs in physical environments, providing essential context that enables the LLM to interpret numerical data and generate appropriate control actions.
> - The method of normalizing numerical values to align with LLM tokenization schemes significantly enhances the model's capacity to process and utilize quantitative information for control tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Large language models (LLMs), pre-trained on extensive internet-scale data, have demonstrated capabilities across various domains, including natural language processing, computer vision, and code generation. Building on this, there has been increasing interest in deploying LLMs for embodied agents in robotics. This includes efforts to develop robot foundation models, which are typically trained on large volumes of robot-specific data.

The existing research landscape largely utilizes LLMs as high-level planners, providing symbolic instructions or abstract goals for robotic systems. For instance, LLMs have been employed for manipulation tasks, high-level locomotion planning, navigation, and as core components for language agents. Some works have used LLMs to instruct contact patterns for quadrupedal locomotion or to define reward parameters for robot walking. Specialized GPT models have also been trained using robot data to enable walking.

This paper distinguishes itself by exploring a novel paradigm: using off-the-shelf LLMs directly as *low-level feedback controllers* for high-dimensional, dynamic robot systems, specifically for robot walking. Unlike robot foundation models that require specific large-scale robotic datasets for training, this work investigates whether pre-trained LLMs, without any task-specific fine-tuning, can infer and output real-time control actions. It diverges from approaches that use LLMs for high-level planning or with iterative improvement mechanisms for control, instead proposing a direct feedback policy based on in-context learning from few-shot prompts. The core question addressed is whether LLMs, primarily trained on text, can effectively ground themselves in the physical world and generate dynamic robot motions by interpreting historical observation-action sequences.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind this research stems from the recognized capabilities of large language models and the escalating interest in applying these foundation models to real-world robotics. However, significant challenges persist in effectively grounding LLMs in physical environments and enabling them to generate the dynamic motions required for robotic tasks. A notable constraint in existing approaches, such as robot foundation models, is their reliance on large-scale, robot-specific training data, which is not as readily available as textual data.

Against this background, the authors pose a specific research question: Can off-the-shelf Large Language Models function as low-level controllers for high-dimensional dynamical systems, such as robots, without requiring any additional task-specific training or fine-tuning? The conventional application of LLMs in robotics has primarily been for high-level planning tasks. This work seeks to investigate the feasibility of LLMs directly predicting low-level control actions, such as target joint positions, autoregressively from a historical input-output sequence of a robotic system.

The key objectives of this research are threefold:
1. **Develop a Framework for LLM-based Feedback Control:** To introduce and evaluate a framework where LLMs operate as a feedback policy, outputting direct robot control actions (e.g., target joint positions), contrasting with their more common role as planners in robotics. This paradigm, termed "prompting a robot to walk," aims to leverage few-shot prompts collected from the physical environment.
2. **Design and Analyze Text Prompts for Robot Walking:** To systematically propose and analyze a specific text prompt design that enables LLMs to in-context learn robot walking behaviors. This involves structuring textual descriptions alongside historical observation and action data.
3. **Validate Generalizability Across Robots and Environments:** To extensively validate the proposed framework on diverse robotic platforms, various terrains, and multiple simulation environments, thereby assessing its generalizability and robustness.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on employing an off-the-shelf Large Language Model (GPT-4) as a low-level feedback controller for robotic locomotion without fine-tuning. The overall framework involves a one-time initialization, specific prompt engineering, and a method for grounding the LLM in a physical simulation environment.

**A. Data Collection for Initialization:**
The process begins with a one-time initialization of the LLM prompt. This involves collecting observation and action pairs from an existing, operational robot controller. This initial controller can be either model-based or learning-based. The collected data, comprising sensor readings (e.g., IMU, joint encoders) as observations and target joint positions as actions, serves to populate the initial historical context for the LLM. As the robot subsequently interacts with the environment based on LLM outputs, this initial offline data is replaced by the LLM-generated trajectory history.

**B. Prompt Engineering:**
A crucial component of the methodology is the design of the text prompt, which guides the LLM to function as a feedback policy. The prompt is structured into two main categories:
- **Description Prompt (P_Desc):** This static part provides textual context and instructions. It includes several sub-components:
 - `P_TD` (Task Description): A precise description of the robot walking task, including details like the desired operating frequency of the policy (e.g., 10 Hz).
 - `P_IO` (Meaning of Input and Output Space): Specifies the format and interpretation of both the robot's observation (input) space and the action (output) space. For example, it delineates the dimensions corresponding to linear velocity, angular velocity, gravity, joint positions, and joint velocities for the input, and target joint positions for the output.
 - `P_JO` (Joint Order): An explicit enumeration of the robot's joint order to ensure the LLM comprehends the robot's configuration.
 - `P_CP` (Full Control Pipeline): An overview of how the LLM's output integrates into the broader control system, such as the use of a higher-frequency Proportional-Derivative (PD) controller to track the LLM's target joint positions.
 - `P_AI` (Additional Illustration): Provides supplementary instructions regarding data processing methods and specific output format requirements (e.g., "Your output is only one line and starts with 'Output:'").
- **Observation and Action Prompt (P_Hist):** This dynamic part consists of a sequence of historical observation and action pairs, akin to few-shot examples. These pairs are generated from the robot's recent trajectory history. This component allows the LLM to infer system dynamics and learn reactive control in-context, with the observation prompt serving as the feedback signal. Both observations and actions are converted into a text format for LLM input.

**C. Numerical Value Normalization:**
To address the LLM's limitations in processing floating-point and negative numbers, a normalization approach is implemented. All numerical values within the observation and action prompts are linearly transformed into non-negative integers ranging from 0 to 200. This is based on the hypothesis that LLMs, primarily trained on text tokens, are more effective when numerical values are presented in a format that aligns with their tokenization scheme (e.g., integers within [-300, 300] are tokenized as single tokens in GPT-4). This facilitates the LLM's ability to discern implicit numerical relationships.

**D. Grounding LLMs in Physical Environments:**
The LLM is grounded within a physics-based simulator (MuJoCo or Isaac Gym). The pipeline operates as follows:
1. The LLM receives the constructed text prompt.
2. It generates target joint positions as a text output.
3. These target joint positions are then fed to a low-level Proportional-Derivative (PD) controller, which operates at a higher frequency (200 Hz) to track the LLM's 10 Hz targets.
4. The robot executes these actions in the simulator.
5. New observations from the environment are collected.
6. The historical observation-action prompt (`P_Hist`) is updated with the latest interaction, and the loop continues.
This work was conducted entirely in simulation, primarily due to the inference speed limitations of the LLM. GPT-4 was the primary LLM used, with a temperature setting of 0 to minimize output variance. Other LLMs were evaluated but found insufficient for the task. The A1 quadruped robot (12 actuated joints) was chosen as the initial testbed, with its initialization policy trained using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) in Isaac Gym. The ANYmal robot was used for generalization studies.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several key findings across a series of experiments designed to address the guiding questions (Q1-Q3).

**A. Robot Walking Capability (Q1):**
The study successfully demonstrated that an A1 quadruped robot could be prompted to walk using GPT-4. This was achieved on both flat ground and uneven terrain. During experiments on uneven terrain, the LLM policy allowed the robot to recover from disturbances and maintain walking posture, illustrating a degree of robustness. The LLM policy operated at 10 Hz, with a 200 Hz PD controller tracking its target joint positions. While the gait produced was noted to be "reasonably worse" compared to higher-frequency RL-based policies, the feasibility of LLM-driven low-level control was established. Analysis of joint trajectories (Figure 4) revealed that the LLM-generated trajectories, though initialized by an RL policy, diverged significantly, exhibiting more regular patterns. This suggests that the LLM engaged in a different form of in-context learning rather than merely imitating the initial behavior.

**B. Prompt Design Analysis (Q2):**
Extensive experiments were conducted to evaluate the impact of various prompt components:

- **Description Prompt Influence (Figure 5):**
 - Experiment E1 (no description prompt, only historical observations and actions) resulted in minimal walking success, demonstrating the necessity of textual context.
 - Progressively adding components to the description prompt, from only input/output meaning (E2), to joint order (E3), and then to task description and full control pipeline (E4), incrementally improved both normalized walking time and success rate.
 - The complete description prompt (E5) yielded the highest performance, indicating a synergy between descriptive text and historical data. This finding highlights text as a critical interface for providing context to the LLM.

- **Observation and Action Prompt Length (Figure 6):**
 - Varying the length of historical observation and action pairs (0, 10, 30, 50 steps, corresponding to approximately 348 to 7298 tokens) showed a positive correlation with performance.
 - Increased history length consistently led to enhanced normalized walking time and success rate, suggesting that longer sequences enable the LLM to better infer dynamics and generate reactive control actions. The practical limit was imposed by GPT-4's 8k token context window.

- **Observation Choice Impact (Figure 7):**
 - Providing no observation (E1, open-loop control) was insufficient for successful walking.
 - Gradually adding observation components (base linear/angular velocity, joint position/velocity) improved performance.
 - The full selected observation set (33 dimensions, E5), which included linear/angular velocity, gravity vector, joint positions, and joint velocities, achieved the best performance. While 33 dimensions were used compared to 48 for the RL policy (due to token limits), it was sufficient for enabling walking. This suggests the LLM can derive understanding of underlying physical principles from these inputs.

- **Normalization Method Benchmark (Table I):**
 - The method of normalizing numerical values proved critical. Direct use of original floating-point values (E1) or simple positive normalization (E2) resulted in very low performance.
 - Normalization to integer values (E3) and specifically to positive integer values (E5) significantly improved normalized walking time and success rate.
 - The most effective method (E5) mapped numerical values to positive integers such that they would likely be tokenized as single tokens by GPT-4. This suggests that presenting numerical data in a token-efficient manner aids the LLM in discerning implicit numerical relationships, crucial for control tasks.

**C. Generalization Across Robots and Environments (Q3):**
The framework's generalizability was validated by applying it to the ANYmal quadruped robot. The ANYmal, which differs from the A1 robot in size, mass, and mechanical design, was successfully prompted to walk on flat ground. Furthermore, this validation was performed in Isaac Gym, demonstrating robustness across different simulation environments (compared to MuJoCo used for the A1 robot). These results indicate that the proposed method can generalize to different robotic platforms and simulation settings.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a notable advancement in the application of Large Language Models (LLMs) to robotics, marking a departure from conventional paradigms. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Novel Paradigm for LLM-based Control:** The work introduces a paradigm where off-the-shelf LLMs function as low-level feedback controllers for dynamic robot movements, specifically walking. This contrasts with the prevalent use of LLMs as high-level planners in robotics. Demonstrating this capability for high-dimensional, dynamic systems like quadrupedal robots expands the perceived utility and scope of LLMs in physical domains.

- **Feasibility of Zero-shot/Few-shot Physical Grounding:** The study illustrates that LLMs can achieve complex physical behaviors like robot walking through few-shot in-context learning, without requiring task-specific fine-tuning on extensive robot data. This leverages the LLM's pre-trained world knowledge and pattern recognition abilities to interpret numerical sequences within a robotic context. This finding suggests a potential pathway for more data-efficient learning in robotics, circumventing the challenges associated with large-scale robotic data collection and model training.

- **Text as a Direct Interface for Control:** The research highlights the critical role of textual descriptions in enabling LLMs to interpret and act upon numerical observations and actions. This indicates that text can serve as an effective interface for conveying control design principles, system specifications, and task objectives to an LLM-based controller. This could simplify the programming and adaptation of robotic systems, potentially allowing non-expert users to communicate complex behaviors using natural language.

- **LLMs as Dynamic Feedback Controllers:** The authors propose that LLMs, with their evolving contextual state, act as dynamic feedback controllers. This conceptualization suggests that LLMs can adapt their "internal parameters" based on the history of interactions, analogous to adaptive controllers. This offers a new perspective on the control capabilities of LLMs, moving beyond static mappings of state to action.

- **Demonstrated Generalizability:** Validation across two distinct quadrupedal robots (A1 and ANYmal) and two different physics simulators (MuJoCo and Isaac Gym) indicates a degree of generalizability for the proposed method. This suggests that the approach is not confined to a single robot morphology or simulation environment, enhancing its broader applicability.

Despite these significant contributions, the paper also identifies critical limitations that represent areas for future development. These include the fragility of the current prompt design, its bias towards specific initialization policies, the prohibitive cost and low inference speed of current LLMs (necessitating paused simulations), and the limited token context length. Addressing these limitations, particularly the speed and cost, would be crucial for deploying such LLM-based controllers on real-world hardware. Ultimately, this work opens avenues for more flexible, adaptable, and intuitively programmable robotic systems, potentially bridging the gap between high-level human intent and low-level robot execution through natural language interaction.
