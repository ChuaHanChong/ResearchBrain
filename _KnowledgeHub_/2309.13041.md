# Robotic Offline RL from Internet Videos via Value-Function Pre-Training

> [!summary] Summary
> V-PTR is a three-phase system for robotic offline RL that pre-trains an intent-conditioned value function via temporal-difference learning on action-free human video (Ego4D), refines the representation with multi-task conservative Q-learning on Bridge robot data, then fine-tunes on a handful of target demonstrations. On real WidowX manipulation, it yields smoother value functions, better robustness to distractors, and large success-rate gains over video-representation baselines.

## Problem

- Internet video lacks action and reward labels, creating a type mismatch with offline RL methods that need annotated transitions to learn value functions.
- Self-supervised visual pre-training on video extracts appearance features but no functional knowledge of dynamics, affordances, or achievable long-term outcomes.
- Action-relabeling and inverse-RL approaches only work when video and robot domains nearly match, failing across egocentric-human versus shoulder-view robot viewpoint gaps.

## Method

- Phase 1 trains an ==intent-conditioned value function== on action-free video by ==TD-learning==, factorized as an inner product of state, intent, and goal embeddings over a ResNet50-v2 backbone.
- Phase 2 refines that encoder with multi-task ==conservative Q-learning== on Bridge robot data, initializing both critic and actor from the video representation and conditioning on one-hot or language task vectors.
- Phase 3 fine-tunes the Q-function and policy on ten target demonstrations, treating the target as a new task with stratified sampling that draws a 0.1 fraction from target data.

## Results

- Across four pick-and-place tasks V-PTR reached **24/48** successes versus **6/48** for masked pre-training with behavior cloning, **5/48** without video, and **2/48** for contrastive value pre-training.
- With unseen distractors V-PTR held **20/48** successes and reached **3/9** on novel target objects; on complex tasks it scored **5/12** opening a microwave and **6/12** sweeping beans.
- Language-conditioned V-PTR hit **44/72** versus **21/72** for the no-video baseline; SARSA probing showed the lowest value-fitting error, and Grad-CAM localized attention onto gripper and objects.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Matching the pre-training objective to the downstream one matters: TD value learning on video transfers better to offline RL than contrastive or reconstructive representation objectives.
> - Action-free data can still teach functional structure, since value learning encodes achievable outcomes rather than just appearance, even across large embodiment and viewpoint gaps.
> - Gains show up as robustness rather than raw capability, and all such methods stay brittle to shifts in workspace height, camera angle, and robot configuration.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a central challenge in robotic learning: developing skills that generalize broadly to novel scenarios. This problem is recognized as significant in the broader machine learning community, where pre-training on large, diverse datasets has driven generalization in various domains. However, applying this paradigm to robotics is hindered by the nature of available data. While internet-scale video datasets (e.g., Ego4D) offer a vast repository of human behavior and interactions, they lack action and reward annotations critical for traditional Reinforcement Learning (RL) methods. This "type mismatch" limits their direct utility for robotic skill acquisition.

Prior research has attempted to integrate video data into robot learning pipelines through various approaches. One common method involves self-supervised visual representation learning on video data (e.g., using masked auto-encoders or time-contrastive learning), followed by downstream policy learning via behavioral cloning on these learned representations. The paper suggests that such approaches primarily extract visual features and may not capture a deeper "functional" understanding of the world, such as intentions, affordances, dynamics, or long-term outcomes, which are valuable for robotic agents despite embodiment differences.

Another class of methods modifies downstream RL algorithms to jointly train on video and robot data. These often involve training inverse dynamics models to generate action pseudo-labels for video transitions or using inverse RL to imitate state distributions. However, these methods typically assume a small domain gap between video and robot data, which is frequently not the case, especially when dealing with egocentric human video and robot data with different viewpoints or embodiments.

This paper positions itself by proposing a novel approach that explicitly addresses the functional understanding gap and the domain mismatch. By leveraging temporal-difference (TD) learning to pre-train value functions directly on action-free video data, it aims to extract knowledge about "what future outcomes can be achieved." This contrasts with prior works like Value-Implicit Pre-training (VIP), which also learns value functions during pre-training but uses time-contrastive prediction for reward shaping, whereas this work focuses on providing robust value function initializations for downstream offline RL. The proposed V-PTR system seeks to integrate the benefits of video pre-training with diverse robot data to enhance generalization and robustness in robotic offline RL, particularly in scenarios where a significant domain gap exists.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a system capable of acquiring robotic skills that exhibit broad generalization to new scenarios, a significant challenge in robotic learning. The authors are motivated by the success of pre-training on large, diverse datasets in other machine learning fields and hypothesize that a similar approach could benefit robotics.

The specific objectives and motivations are detailed as follows:
- **Leveraging Internet-Scale Data**: The primary motivation is to effectively utilize the vast amounts of readily available internet video data, such as Ego4D, which captures a wide diversity of human behaviors, skills, and interactions. This data is far more abundant than existing robot datasets, which are typically constrained to a limited number of tasks and scenarios.
- **Addressing Data "Type Mismatch"**: A key challenge is that internet video datasets inherently lack action and reward annotations required by conventional Reinforcement Learning (RL) methods. The work aims to overcome this "type mismatch" by developing a system that can meaningfully extract information from observation-only video.
- **Beyond Visual Features**: Current approaches that use self-supervised learning on video data for visual representation learning often focus on pixel-level features. The authors are motivated to move beyond this to extract a deeper "functional" understanding from videos. This includes discerning intents, affordances, environment dynamics, and long-term outcomes, which are crucial for an agent to perform complex tasks, even with a different embodiment (e.g., a robot observing human actions).
- **Improving Downstream Offline RL**: The overarching goal is to boost the robustness and generalization capabilities of robotic offline RL policies. This involves providing effective pre-training that leads to better-initialized value functions and policies, capable of adapting to novel objects, distractors, and variations in the workspace from a limited number of demonstrations.
- **Progressive Knowledge Integration**: The system is motivated by a staged approach to knowledge acquisition:
 1. Learning "what future outcomes can be achieved" from action-free video data.
 2. Learning "what robot actions lead to these outcomes" from multi-task robot data.
 3. Learning "how the desired task can be solved" through fine-tuning on a small target dataset.

In essence, the motivation is to unlock the potential of large-scale human video data for robotic learning, moving beyond mere visual feature extraction to a more profound understanding of goal-directed behavior, thereby enabling robots to learn more generalizable and robust skills with less task-specific robot data.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed system, **Video Pre-Training for Robots (V-PTR)**, is designed to leverage large-scale human video data and multi-task robotic data to improve robotic offline RL. It operates in three sequential phases: video pre-training, multi-task robot pre-training, and fine-tuning.

#### 3.1. System Overview

V-PTR begins by learning a general value function from action-free human video. This initial pre-training extracts a functional understanding of temporal dynamics and outcomes. Subsequently, the learned representations are refined using multi-task robot data, which introduces information about robot embodiment and action space. Finally, the system fine-tunes the value functions and policies on a small target dataset for specific downstream tasks.

#### 3.2. Phase 1: Video Pre-Training via TD-Learning

This phase focuses on extracting meaningful representations and a functional understanding from observation-only video data.
- **Intent-Conditioned Value Function (ICVF)**: V-PTR learns an ICVF, $V(\mathbf{s}_{\text{video}}, \mathbf{g}_{\text{video}}, \mathbf{z})$, which estimates the expected discounted future rewards of reaching a goal $\mathbf{g}_{\text{video}}$ from a state $\mathbf{s}_{\text{video}}$, assuming the policy *intended* to reach a different observation $\mathbf{z}$. This formulation allows for learning about goal-reaching without explicit action labels.
- **Temporal-Difference (TD) Learning**: The ICVF is learned using a TD-learning objective, which is a common approach for value function estimation in RL. The objective minimizes the squared difference between the current value estimate and a bootstrapped target value (computed using a delayed target network, $\bar{V}$).
 - The loss function incorporates an implied advantage term that influences the update based on whether the current state already matches the goal.
- **Goal and Intent Sampling**: To train the ICVF, various frames are sampled from the video dataset:
 - A start frame $\mathbf{s}$ is sampled.
 - A goal frame $\mathbf{g}$ can be sampled as the next observation, a future observation within the same video, or a random observation from a different video.
 - An intent frame $\mathbf{z}$ is sampled similarly to $\mathbf{g}$, and with a certain probability, $\mathbf{z}$ is set equal to $\mathbf{g}$.
- **Parameterization**: The ICVF is parameterized as $V(\mathbf{s}_{\text{video}}, \mathbf{g}_{\text{video}}, \mathbf{z}) := \phi(\mathbf{s}_{\text{video}})^{\top} T(\mathbf{z})\psi(\mathbf{g}_{\text{video}})$. Here, $\phi$ and $\psi$ are models (MLPs) that transform observations into low-dimensional representations, and $T$ is a learned mapping that aligns these representations. The encoder $\phi(\mathbf{s})$ serves as the visual representation backbone for subsequent phases.

#### 3.3. Phase 2: Multi-Task Robot Pre-Training via Offline RL

Following video pre-training, the system refines the learned visual encoder on a multi-task robot dataset ($\mathcal{D}_{\text{robot}}$). This phase bridges the domain gap between human video and robot observations and integrates information about the robot's action space.
- **Algorithm**: V-PTR uses multi-task Conservative Q-Learning (CQL) to pre-train a Q-function and a policy. CQL is an offline RL algorithm designed to address issues of extrapolation error when learning from static datasets.
- **Initialization**: The visual backbone $\phi_{\theta}$ for both the Q-function and the policy is initialized with the encoder obtained from Phase 1.
- **Inputs**: The Q-function and policy take the robot observation's representation ($\phi_{\theta}(\mathbf{s}_{\text{robot}})$) and a task vector $t$ (either a one-hot identifier or a language embedding) as input. The Q-function additionally takes the action vector.
- **Objectives**: The Q-function is trained to satisfy the Bellman equation, incorporating the CQL regularizer to mitigate overestimation. The policy is trained to maximize the Q-value, often with an entropy bonus. It is important to note that the tasks and workspaces in $\mathcal{D}_{\text{robot}}$ are explicitly disjoint from the target tasks used in downstream evaluation.

#### 3.4. Phase 3: Fine-Tuning to a Target Task

In the final phase, the pre-trained Q-function and policy are adapted to a specific target task using a small target dataset ($\mathcal{D}_{\text{target}}$).
- **Algorithm**: CQL is again employed for fine-tuning.
- **Joint Training**: The fine-tuning process involves continuing multi-task offline CQL on both the $\mathcal{D}_{\text{robot}}$ and $\mathcal{D}_{\text{target}}$ datasets jointly. The target task is treated as a new task with a distinct identifier.
- **Overfitting Mitigation**: To prevent overfitting to the small $\mathcal{D}_{\text{target}}$, a stratified sampling strategy is used, where a small proportion ($\tau=0.1$) of each training batch comes from $\mathcal{D}_{\text{target}}$ and the rest from $\mathcal{D}_{\text{robot}}$.

#### 3.5. Implementation Details

- **Visual Encoder**: A ResNetv2-50 backbone is used for visual feature extraction. Batch normalization layers are replaced with group normalization for stability in offline RL.
- **Network Architectures**: The ICVF heads are 3-layer MLPs. The CQL policy and Q-function use 2-layer MLP heads after the visual encoder, concatenating proprioceptive state and task information.
- **Training Parameters**: Video frames are 224x224. Data augmentation (random crops, color jitter), weight decay, and dropout are used during video pre-training. Training involves $2 \times 10^6$ gradient steps for Phase 1 and $2 \times 10^5$ for Phase 2, with Adam optimizer and learning rates of $10^{-4}$.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of V-PTR was conducted on a real WidowX robot and in simulation, comparing its performance against several prior methods across various manipulation tasks and generalization scenarios.

#### 4.1. Real-World Setup and Comparison

- **Datasets**: Ego4D (4M egocentric human video transitions) for Phase 1; a subset of the Bridge dataset (150K WidowX robot manipulation transitions) for Phase 2. Target tasks involved 10 teleoperated demonstrations each, collected on a WidowX robot in a novel toy-kitchen workspace.
- **Tasks**: Four pick-and-place tasks (e.g., "Croissant from bowl," "Knife in pot") and two more complex tasks ("Open microwave," "Sweep beans").
- **Comparison Methods**:
 - No video data: PTR (Pre-training for Robots), BC (Behavioral Cloning).
 - Video pre-training: R3M+BC, MVP+BC (using frozen representations with imitation learning); VIP+CQL, VIP$_{\text{frozen}}$+CQL, and VIP$_{\text{reward}}$ (reward-weighted imitation learning or downstream CQL with VIP representations).

#### 4.2. Quantitative Real-World Results

- **Scenario 1 (Initial Pose/Object Variation)**: V-PTR consistently outperformed all other methods. Across 48 trials, V-PTR achieved 24 successes, significantly higher than R3M+BC (0/48), MVP+BC (6/48), VIP+CQL (2/48), VIP$_{\text{frozen}}$+CQL (0/48), and PTR (5/48). In some tasks, V-PTR was the only method to achieve any successful trials.
- **Scenario 2 (Novel Distractor Objects)**: When novel distractor objects were introduced, all methods experienced performance degradation. However, V-PTR exhibited the smallest performance drop and maintained its lead (20/48 successes), outperforming MVP+BC (5/48), VIP+CQL (4/48), and PTR (1/48).
- **Scenario 3 (Novel Target Objects)**: For tasks involving previously unseen target objects, V-PTR achieved a 3/9 success rate, which was low overall but still equal to the best performing VIP+CQL variant and superior to most other baselines (0/9 or 1/9).
- **Complex Tasks (Table 2)**: On "Open Microwave" and "Sweep Beans," V-PTR again outperformed VIP variants and PTR. For instance, on "Open Microwave," V-PTR achieved 5/12 successes compared to VIP+CQL (2/12) and PTR (0/12).
- **Language-based Task Specification (Table 3)**: V-PTR demonstrated strong performance when task specifications were provided via natural language embeddings, outperforming language-conditioned BC and PTR by approximately 50% in total success rate (44/72 for V-PTR language vs. 8/72 for BC and 21/72 for PTR).
- **Reduced Demonstrations (Table 5)**: Even with only 10 demonstrations per target task, V-PTR maintained a significant performance advantage over baselines, indicating its data efficiency.

#### 4.3. Qualitative Observations

- **Behavioral Differences**: V-PTR policies exhibited more precise object localization, re-orientation of the gripper before grasping, and attempts to retry actions upon failure (e.g., re-grasping a dropped object). In contrast, some baseline policies, like VIP, made imprecise grasps without re-orientation, leading to failures.
- **Extended Actions**: On the "sweep beans" task, V-PTR's policy swept a larger area and consistently initiated a second sweep, which was not observed in the VIP policy that was often too slow to perform a second sweep.

#### 4.4. Visualizations and Diagnostic Experiments

- **Value Estimation Quality (Figure 6)**: V-PTR representations resulted in smoother and more monotonically increasing value functions over successful rollouts, especially when evaluated on held-out data with novel distractors. PTR and VIP often produced non-smooth and non-monotonic value functions in such scenarios. A quantitative analysis showed V-PTR achieved the smallest SARSA value fitting error on held-out datasets.
- **Visual Cues (Grad-CAM, Figure 7)**: Grad-CAM visualizations indicated that V-PTR policies focused on task-relevant visual cues, such as object and gripper positions, while largely ignoring background elements. In contrast, PTR and VIP exhibited higher attention on scene backgrounds. This suggests V-PTR learns more discriminative and relevant visual features for control.
- **Simulation Results (Figure 8)**: In a simulated pick-and-place task, V-PTR consistently demonstrated percentage improvements in success rates over PTR across various generalization challenges (novel target objects, novel distractor objects, novel workspaces), with only one exception (novel target container).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a notable advancement in robotic learning by demonstrating a method to effectively leverage vast, action-free human video data for robotic skill acquisition. The V-PTR system offers several significant contributions and potential impacts:

- **Pioneering Value Function Pre-training from Video**: The work represents the first large-scale demonstration that temporal-difference (TD) learning alone can be an effective strategy for pre-training from action-free video data to enhance robotic Reinforcement Learning. This establishes a precedent for using RL-like objectives directly on passive observational data.
- **Enhanced Generalization and Robustness**: V-PTR policies exhibit significantly improved zero-shot generalization capabilities. This includes robustness to variations in initial object placements, novel distractor objects, and the ability to manipulate previously unseen target objects. Such generalization is crucial for deploying robots in real-world, unstructured environments where conditions inevitably vary from training scenarios.
- **Deeper "Functional" Understanding**: By learning intent-conditioned value functions, V-PTR goes beyond extracting superficial visual features. It implicitly captures a "functional" understanding of long-term outcomes, affordances, and dynamics from human videos, which is a more profound form of knowledge for an agent than mere visual representations. This functional understanding allows the robot to infer meaningful behavioral strategies.
- **Bridging the Data Modality Gap**: The system successfully addresses the "type mismatch" between abundant observation-only video data and the action/reward-annotated data required for traditional robotic RL. This opens up opportunities for robots to benefit from the immense amount of human interaction data available online, overcoming a major bottleneck in scaling robotic intelligence.
- **Improved Data Efficiency**: The ability of V-PTR to yield robust performance from a small number of target task demonstrations (even as few as 10) suggests significant improvements in data efficiency. This reduces the laborious and costly requirements for collecting large, task-specific robot datasets, accelerating the development and deployment of new robotic skills.
- **Adaptability to Language Specifications**: The demonstration that V-PTR can effectively leverage natural language for task specification further broadens its applicability, enabling more intuitive and flexible human-robot interaction.

While the paper acknowledges current limitations, such as sensitivity to variations in workspace height, camera angle, and robot configurations, it clearly establishes the promise of value-based video pre-training. Future work could explore scaling to multi-robot datasets, even larger human video datasets with richer annotations (e.g., language), and larger neural network models. Overall, this research provides a foundational step towards more general-purpose robot learning algorithms, moving closer to robots that can learn complex skills by observing the world through human actions.
