# Improving Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture with Diffusion Noise

> [!summary] Summary
> N-JEPA enhances the Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture by integrating diffusion noise into masked token position embeddings during self-supervised pre-training. This method yields a +1.5% Top-1 accuracy improvement over the I-JEPA baseline on ImageNet-1K linear evaluation and delivers significant gains in low-shot learning settings.

## Problem

- Traditional self-supervised learning methods often rely on hand-crafted data augmentations, which can limit generalization and be sensitive to human prior knowledge.
- Masked Image Modeling (MIM) can sometimes produce lower-level representations that hinder performance in downstream tasks compared to more semantic features.
- Existing efforts to leverage generative diffusion models for representation learning have generally lagged behind dedicated self-supervised methods in learning robust semantic features.

## Method

- **N-JEPA** modifies the JEPA framework by introducing an additional predictor that forecasts ==masked== information from student features augmented with diffusion noise.
- It employs the EDM (Elucidating the Design Space of Diffusion-Based Generative Models) noise schedule to inject multi-level Gaussian noise into the position embeddings of masked tokens, preserving Vision Transformer architecture compatibility.
- The model is trained using a composite loss function, combining the standard JEPA prediction loss with auxiliary noise-to-teacher and context-to-noise denoising objectives.

## Results

- N-JEPA achieved a consistent +**1.5%** Top-1 accuracy improvement over the I-JEPA baseline on ImageNet-**1K** linear evaluation (**73.6%** vs. **72.1%** at 600 epochs).
- The method demonstrated substantial gains in low-shot ImageNet-**1K** classification, improving Top-1 accuracy by +**2.5%** with **1%** labels (**60.0%**) and +**3.1%** with **10%** labels (**66.1%**) compared to the baseline.
- The multi-level noise schedule consistently outperformed the single-level noise schedule, yielding a **0.2**-**0.4%** higher Top-1 accuracy, validating its role in robust feature learning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Diffusion noise can effectively act as a feature-level augmentation within self-supervised learning frameworks like JEPA, enhancing representation capacity without relying on hand-crafted transformations.
> - A multi-level noise schedule, applying distinct noise samples to each masked block, proves more effective than a single-level schedule for promoting diverse and robust feature learning.
> - Careful weighting of the auxiliary noise-related losses is critical, indicating their role in complementing the primary prediction objective to maximize performance gains.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is positioned within the rapidly evolving fields of self-supervised learning (SSL) and generative models, particularly denoising diffusion probabilistic models (DDPMs). Self-supervised learning has achieved notable success in learning high-quality feature representations from unlabeled data, demonstrating strong performance in discriminative tasks such as image classification. Methodologies within SSL include contrastive learning, which relies on data augmentation invariance, and Masked Image Modeling (MIM), which involves predicting masked pixels or tokens.

Concurrently, generative models, especially diffusion models, have emerged as a dominant paradigm in image generation and detail enhancement, capable of producing high-fidelity samples by approximating complex data distributions. This success has led to explorations into leveraging generative models for representation learning, with some studies demonstrating their potential as strong pre-trained representations for downstream tasks. However, in these instances, the performance often lags behind that of dedicated SSL methods for semantic representations.

The paper identifies a conceptual connection between MIM and diffusion models, noting that the "add noise and then denoise" philosophy of diffusion models aligns with SSL's "corruption first and then reconstruction" approach. Prior work like DiffMAE has attempted to establish this link by viewing Masked Autoencoders (MAE) as a single-step, patch-conditioned diffusion model. Other efforts, such as DreamTeacher, have explored distilling knowledge from generative models into image backbones, while DDAE and L-DAE deconstruct diffusion models to explore their components for self-supervised representation learning.

This research specifically builds upon the Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA), a recent advancement in SSL that emphasizes learning semantic understanding by predicting masked patches in a high-level representation space, thus reducing reliance on hand-crafted augmentations. Previous extensions to JEPA, such as FlexPredict, have introduced complexities by requiring the learning of additional matrices to manage location uncertainty.

The presented work aims to bridge the gap between these two powerful paradigms by integrating the core principle of diffusion models—diffusion noise—into a JEPA-based SSL framework. By doing so, it seeks to enhance the representation capacity and robustness of SSL models for recognition tasks, potentially leading towards a unified vision pretraining approach for both recognition and generation. This approach attempts to capitalize on the semantic understanding capabilities inherent in generative models to improve discriminative feature learning, without introducing the complexities or performance limitations observed in some earlier integration attempts.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the representation capacity and robustness of self-supervised learning (SSL) models, particularly for recognition tasks, by integrating principles derived from denoising diffusion models.

The motivation for this objective stems from several observations:
- **Complementary Strengths of SSL and Generative Models**: SSL has proven highly effective for discriminative tasks, while generative models excel in image generation and detail enhancement. The authors propose that the semantic understanding derived by generative models through approximating data distributions could also benefit recognition tasks.
- **Conceptual Alignment of Corruption-Reconstruction**: The core mechanism of diffusion models involves adding noise and subsequently denoising, which is analogous to the "corruption first and then reconstruction" principle inherent in many SSL methods, including Masked Image Modeling (MIM). This parallel suggests a natural synergy between the two paradigms.
- **Bridging MIM and Diffusion Models**: The authors view diffusion noise as a specific form of "mask," indicating a direct connection between diffusion processes and MIM. This insight motivates injecting diffusion noise into MIM frameworks to improve their performance.
- **Limitations of Existing SSL/MIM Approaches**:
 - **Hand-crafted Data Augmentations**: Traditional SSL methods, particularly contrastive learning, heavily rely on hand-crafted data augmentations. These augmentations are often limited by human prior knowledge and may not generalize effectively across different modalities.
 - **Low-level Representations in MIM**: While MIM alleviates some issues with hand-crafted augmentations, it can sometimes yield low-level representations that hinder performance in downstream tasks, especially in linear probing or transfer learning settings with limited supervision.
 - **Lagging Performance of Diffusion for SSL**: While some studies have explored using generative diffusion models as pre-trained representations for downstream tasks, their performance in learning semantic representations has generally lagged behind dedicated SSL methods.
- **Building upon Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA)**: The research specifically targets JEPA, an SSL method that aims to predict masked patches within a high-level representation space, thereby focusing on semantic features. The motivation is to improve JEPA's pre-training process without relying on explicit image transformations for robustness.
- **Addressing Complexity of Prior JEPA Extensions**: Previous efforts to enhance JEPA, such as FlexPredict, introduced additional complexities like learning an elaborated matrix to balance location certainty and context influence. The current work aims for a more straightforward integration of noise.

To achieve this, the paper proposes **N-JEPA (Noise-based JEPA)**. The key idea is to incorporate diffusion noise directly into the position embedding of masked tokens within the JEPA framework. The use of the EDM (Elucidating the Design Space of Diffusion-Based Generative Models) noise schedule is motivated by its ability to preserve the standard Vision Transformer (ViT) architecture, thus avoiding additional computational costs associated with timestep embeddings. Furthermore, the introduction of a multi-level noise schedule is posited as a form of feature-level augmentation, intended to enhance model robustness without introducing strong biases inherent in hand-crafted augmentations.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed N-JEPA method integrates diffusion noise into the Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA) to enhance the self-supervised pre-training process. The methodology can be broken down into the underlying diffusion model principles, the architectural modifications to JEPA, the noise injection mechanism, and the loss functions employed.

#### 3.1. Preliminary: Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPMs) and EDM

The foundation for noise injection is rooted in denoising diffusion models. Standard DDPMs involve a forward noising process, where Gaussian noise is gradually added to data ($x_0$ to $x_T$), and a reverse denoising process that learns to predict and remove this noise to generate new samples.
N-JEPA specifically adopts the **EDM (Elucidating the Design Space of Diffusion-Based Generative Models)** framework for its noise schedule. EDM utilizes a "Variance Exploding" parameterization, where Gaussian noise $n \sim N(0, \sigma^2I)$ is added directly to the data distribution $P_{\text{data}}(x)$, resulting in diffused data $x_\sigma = x_0 + n$. This choice is motivated by two factors:
1. **Compatibility**: EDM provides a unified framework for various diffusion models.
2. **Architectural Preservation**: By using $P_\sigma(x)$ instead of $P_t(x)$, EDM avoids the need for explicit timestep embeddings, thereby preserving the Vision Transformer (ViT) framework without introducing extra computational costs. The objective in EDM is to estimate the denoising function $D(x; \sigma)$ directly, which transforms noisy input back to the original clean sample $x_0$.

#### 3.2. Overall Architecture (N-JEPA)

The N-JEPA architecture builds upon I-JEPA, which uses a predictor network to forecast masked information in a high-level representation space rather than directly reconstructing pixels.
- **Encoders**: An input image $X$ is converted into a sequence of non-overlapping patches. These patches are fed into a **teacher encoder** and a **student encoder**. The teacher encoder's parameters are an Exponentially Moving Average (EMA) of the student encoder's parameters.
- **Masking Strategy**: The model employs a multi-block masking approach inspired by I-JEPA. For each training iteration, multiple target blocks (e.g., 4) are randomly sampled from the teacher's patches, defining regions whose representations are to be predicted. A separate student block mask is also sampled. Visible patches are fed to the student encoder, while the teacher encoder processes all patches to generate target representations.
- **Predictors**: N-JEPA incorporates **two predictor networks**, both narrow ViT architectures. They take the student encoder's output (representations of visible patches) as input and are conditioned on position embeddings corresponding to the target mask locations.
 - **Predictor 1 (Context Predictor)**: This predictor operates similarly to the original JEPA, aiming to predict the teacher features from the context features of visible patches. It does **not** have noise injected into its masked token position embeddings.
 - **Predictor 2 (Noise Predictor)**: This is the novel component. It predicts the teacher features from student features that have been augmented with diffusion noise. Crucially, diffusion noise (from the EDM schedule) is injected into the *position embedding* of the masked tokens for this predictor.

#### 3.3. Diffusion Noise Injection

The core innovation is the injection of EDM noise into the position embeddings of the masked tokens.
- **Multi-level Noise Schedule**: For each set of $L$ masked blocks (e.g., 4), the model initializes $L$ *different* noise samples, all drawn from the same EDM noise distribution ($N(0, \sigma_{\text{data}}^2I)$). This "multi-level" approach is intended to serve as a form of feature-level augmentation, promoting robustness and learning more diverse features by disturbing the deterministic positional information.
- **Implementation**: For predictor 2, the position embeddings $\psi_s^N$ for the masked tokens are generated by adding a noise sample $n_j$ to the standard position embeddings $\psi_s^C$ for each masked block $j$: $\psi_s^N = \psi_s^C + n_j$.

#### 3.4. Loss Functions

N-JEPA utilizes a composite loss function combining three components:
1. **Context-Teacher Prediction Loss ($L_{C-T}$)**: This is the primary JEPA-style loss. It measures the discrepancy between the representations predicted by Predictor 1 (context-based, $z_s^C(i)$) and the corresponding target representations from the teacher encoder ($\hat{z}_t(i)$). A Smooth-L1 loss is used:
 $L_{C-T} = \frac{1}{L} \sum_{i=1}^{L} D(\hat{z}_t(i), z_s^C(i))$
2. **Noise-Teacher Prediction Loss ($L_{N-T}$)**: This loss measures the discrepancy between the representations predicted by Predictor 2 (noise-augmented, $z_s^N(i)$) and the teacher's target representations ($\hat{z}_t(i)$). Smooth-L1 loss is also used here:
 $L_{N-T} = \frac{1}{L} \sum_{i=1}^{L} D(\hat{z}_t(i), z_s^N(i))$
3. **Context-Noise Denoise Loss ($L_{C-N}$)**: This loss encourages the noise-augmented predictions to be consistent with the context-only predictions, effectively acting as a denoising objective. It is calculated as the mean squared error (MSE) between the outputs of Predictor 1 ($z_s^C(i)$) and Predictor 2 ($z_s^N(i)$):
 $L_{C-N} = \frac{1}{L} \sum_{i=1}^{L} \sum_{j \in L_i} \|z_{s,j}^N - z_{s,j}^C\|^2_2$

The **Overall Loss ($L_{total}$)** is a weighted sum of these three components:
$L_{total} = L_{C-T} + \lambda_1 L_{N-T} + \lambda_2 L_{C-N}$
Hyperparameters $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$ control the contribution of the auxiliary noise-related losses.

#### 3.5. Training and Evaluation Details

- **Dataset**: Pre-trained on ImageNet-1K with 224x224 resolution.
- **Architecture**: ViT-Base for student/teacher encoders (depth 12, width 768), and ViT-based predictors (depth 6, embedding dim 384).
- **Training Schedule**: 100 or 600 epochs, AdamW optimizer, linear learning rate warmup and constant decay, cosine weight decay. Batch sizes of 128 (100 epochs) or 1024 (600 epochs).
- **Evaluation**: Linear probing on ImageNet-1K (100%, 1%, and 10% labels). The student encoder's global image representation (averaged output, not [cls] token) is used.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of N-JEPA demonstrates consistent improvements in self-supervised representation learning, particularly in classification tasks. The findings are primarily based on linear probing accuracy on ImageNet-1K.

#### 4.1. Overall Performance Comparison

N-JEPA consistently outperforms previous baselines, including I-JEPA, on ImageNet-1K linear evaluation:
- **100 Epochs**: N-JEPA achieved a Top-1 accuracy of **68.3%**, surpassing the reproduced I-JEPA baseline of **66.8%** by **+1.5%**.
- **600 Epochs**: N-JEPA achieved a Top-1 accuracy of **73.6%**, improving upon the reproduced I-JEPA baseline of **72.1%** by **+1.5%**.
These results indicate that the integration of diffusion noise into JEPA yields substantial and consistent performance gains across different training durations.

#### 4.2. Loss Function Selection

An ablation study on loss function choices (Table 1) revealed:
- Using only $L_{C-T}$ (I-JEPA baseline) yielded 66.8% Top-1 accuracy.
- Adding only $L_{N-T}$ or $L_{C-N}$ to $L_{C-T}$ (with equal weights) resulted in a slight performance decrease (65.3% and 66.4% respectively).
- However, when all three losses ($L_{C-T} + L_{N-T} + L_{C-N}$) were combined with equal weights, the performance improved to **67.2%**, an increase of +0.4% over the $L_{C-T}$ baseline. This suggests that the combined effect of noisy prediction and denoising contributes positively to the learning process.

#### 4.3. Loss Weight Hyperparameter Tuning

Further investigation into the weights of the auxiliary losses ($L_{N-T}$ and $L_{C-N}$) revealed their optimal configuration (Table 2):
- Setting $\lambda_1 = 0.1$ and $\lambda_2 = 0.1$ for $L_{N-T}$ and $L_{C-N}$ respectively, while $L_{C-T}$ had a weight of 1, produced the best results.
- For 100 epochs, this configuration achieved **67.9%** (a +1.1% improvement over the equally-weighted total loss).
- For 600 epochs, it achieved **73.4%** (a +1.3% improvement over the equally-weighted total loss).
These findings suggest that $L_{C-T}$ remains the primary objective, with $L_{N-T}$ and $L_{C-N}$ serving as crucial auxiliary components that, when appropriately weighted, enhance model robustness.

#### 4.4. Multi-level Noise Schedule

The multi-level noise schedule, which involves sampling different noise for each masked block, demonstrated superior performance compared to a single-level noise schedule (Table 3):
- For 100 epochs, multi-level noise achieved **68.3%** vs. single-level noise at **67.9%** (+0.4%).
- For 600 epochs, multi-level noise achieved **73.6%** vs. single-level noise at **73.4%** (+0.2%).
This confirms the hypothesis that diverse noise injection acts as an effective feature augmentation, contributing to better representation learning.

#### 4.5. Ablation Studies on Model Components

- **Mask Token Sharing (Table 5b)**: Using unshared mask tokens between the two predictor networks resulted in a Top-1 accuracy of **67.9%**, outperforming shared mask tokens which yielded 66.5%.
- **Predictor Parameter Sharing (Table 6a)**: Unshared predictor network parameters achieved **67.9%**, slightly better than shared parameters at 67.3%.
These studies suggest that maintaining distinct pathways or parameters for processing context-based and noise-augmented information provides a marginal benefit.

#### 4.6. Low-Shot ImageNet-1K Evaluation

N-JEPA demonstrated significant improvements in low-shot learning settings, using only a fraction of available labels (Table 6b):
- When using the last four layers for representation and 1% of ImageNet labels, N-JEPA achieved **60.0%** Top-1 accuracy, a **+2.5%** improvement over the baseline (57.5%).
- With 10% of ImageNet labels, N-JEPA achieved **66.1%**, a **+3.1%** improvement over the baseline (63.0%).
These results indicate that N-JEPA learns more semantically rich and robust representations that generalize effectively even with limited supervision.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research makes several significant contributions to the fields of self-supervised learning (SSL) and deep learning for computer vision, with potential impacts on future research directions and applications.

#### 5.1. Enhanced Self-Supervised Representation Learning

The primary significance lies in demonstrating that integrating diffusion noise, a core principle from generative modeling, can substantially enhance the quality and robustness of representations learned by an existing SSL framework (JEPA). The consistent improvement of +1.5% in linear probing accuracy on ImageNet-1K over the I-JEPA baseline, across both 100 and 600 training epochs, indicates a measurable step forward in SSL performance for discriminative tasks. This suggests that the model can learn more useful and robust features for classification.

#### 5.2. Bridging Generative and Discriminative Models

The work provides a concrete and effective method for combining elements of denoising diffusion models with Masked Image Modeling (MIM) within a joint-embedding predictive architecture. This integration represents a move towards unifying the strengths of generative models (semantic understanding derived from data distribution approximation) with the efficacy of SSL for discriminative tasks. By establishing a connection where diffusion noise acts as a specific form of mask, the research contributes to bridging two prominent paradigms in vision pretraining.

#### 5.3. Improved Robustness through Feature Augmentation

The proposed multi-level noise schedule, where different noise samples are applied to the position embeddings of multiple masked blocks, functions as a novel form of feature-level augmentation. This approach avoids the limitations and biases of traditional hand-crafted data augmentations. The empirical results, showing better performance with multi-level noise compared to single-level noise, confirm that this strategy enhances the model's robustness and its ability to learn diverse features. This can lead to models that are more resilient to variations in input data.

#### 5.4. Stronger Performance in Low-Shot Learning

The significant improvements observed in low-shot ImageNet-1K evaluations (e.g., +2.5% for 1% labels and +3.1% for 10% labels) highlight that N-JEPA learns representations that are not only effective for full-dataset classification but also generalize exceptionally well under conditions of limited labeled data. This has substantial practical implications for applications where obtaining large labeled datasets is difficult or costly, such as in specialized medical imaging or niche industrial inspections.

#### 5.5. Architectural Efficiency

By adopting the EDM framework for diffusion noise, N-JEPA manages to integrate generative model principles without requiring extensive modifications to the standard Vision Transformer (ViT) architecture or introducing significant additional computational overhead. This efficiency makes the proposed method more practical for adoption and scaling in real-world scenarios.

#### 5.6. Catalyst for Future Research

The paper concludes by explicitly suggesting that its work is a step towards exploring the combination of diffusion models and self-supervised methods, and expresses hope that it will "rekindle interest in the unified vision pretraining paradigm for recognition and generation." This indicates a potential impact on fostering new research directions that explore dual-capability models, capable of both high-performance recognition and high-quality generation from a single pre-training framework. The unexplored capacity for generation tasks in N-JEPA itself is a clear avenue for future investigation.
