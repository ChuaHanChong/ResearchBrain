# VRA: Grounding Discrete-Time Joint Acceleration in Voltage-Constrained Actuation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at The Chinese University of Hong Kong developed Voltage-Realizable Acceleration (VRA), a method to ground discrete-time joint acceleration commands in the physics of voltage-constrained electric actuators. VRA derives acceleration bounds directly from actuator voltage limits, significantly reducing voltage saturation and improving execution consistency in robotic systems.

## Problem

- Kinematically admissible joint acceleration commands, generated by discrete-time robot motion controllers, can be physically unrealizable by voltage-constrained electric actuators.
- Decoupled feasibility evaluation in current kinematic reasoning methods often leads to acceleration envelopes that include commands exceeding physical capabilities, particularly at high joint velocities.
- A structural mismatch exists between discrete-time kinematic reasoning and the continuous-time dynamics governing physical feasibility at the actuator level.

## Method

- Proposed **Voltage-Realizable Acceleration (VRA)**, a joint-level acceleration interface that explicitly connects kinematic reasoning with actuator dynamics.
- Derived joint acceleration bounds directly from actuator voltage constraints, considering both ==transient and quasi-steady voltage feasibility==, and compensating for ==residual voltage==.
- Integrated **VRA** with existing ==kinematic envelopes== while implicitly encoding velocity limits through motor voltage feasibility, rather than relying on explicit discrete-time velocity constraints.

## Results

- VRA **significantly reduced voltage saturation events** across different motors and operating conditions, demonstrating improved physical realizability of commanded accelerations.
- In high-speed deceleration scenarios, VRA **maintained torque-realizable behavior**, resulting in smoother braking, reduced oscillations, and more consistent execution near kinematic boundaries.
- On a real quadruped robot, VRA enabled **stable whole-body behaviors** for complex tasks, even at **longer kinematic reasoning time scales** where traditional methods led to instability and task failure.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Joint acceleration bounds that appear kinematically realizable can become physically unrealizable under voltage-constrained electric actuators due to back-EMF effects at high speeds.
> - Deriving acceleration bounds directly from voltage constraints ensures physical realizability and improves command-execution consistency.
> - Encoding velocity limits inherently within motor voltage feasibility offers a less conservative and more physically consistent approach compared to discrete-time velocity propagation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot motion control widely employs discrete-time kinematic reasoning for enforcing joint-level constraints such as position and velocity limits. These methods, often used as upstream modules for whole-body control frameworks (e.g.,), generate kinematically admissible joint motions. A fundamental assumption in this decoupled approach is that these kinematically admissible commands can be reliably realized by the underlying actuators.

However, the authors identify a structural mismatch in this assumption, particularly for robots with voltage-constrained electric actuators. Existing kinematic reasoning methods typically evaluate admissibility independently of actuator execution dynamics, leading to acceleration envelopes that may include commands physically unrealizable at high joint speeds. Furthermore, the discrete-time nature of kinematic reasoning contrasts with the continuous-time dynamics governing physical feasibility at the actuator level.

Current approaches to address actuator limits often fall into two categories:
1. **Viability-based joint-level control:** Methods like provide theoretical guarantees for future feasible trajectories but rely on discrete-time kinematic propagation and often state-independent acceleration limits. They define feasibility over future trajectories rather than instantaneous physical execution, which can mismatch with voltage-constrained hardware.
2. **Joint-level actuator-aware control:** These methods typically handle actuator limits post-hoc, such as clipping torque commands after high-level commands are generated, or focusing on maintaining stability after saturation has occurred. These approaches enforce feasibility at the command level, decoupled from kinematic constraints.

The present work positions itself by addressing a "missing execution-level abstraction" at the joint level. Rather than operating at the whole-body controller or treating saturation as a mere execution-side effect, this research focuses on mediating between kinematic reasoning and actuator voltage limits directly at the joint acceleration interface. It seeks to provide physically realizable acceleration bounds that explicitly account for voltage constraints, thereby serving as a precondition for any discrete-time kinematic reasoning method. This distinguishes it from methods that provide system-level constraint satisfaction (e.g., Control Barrier Functions or hierarchical quadratic programs), which operate at a higher abstraction level.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the observation that kinematically admissible joint acceleration commands, commonly generated by discrete-time robot motion controllers, can be physically unrealizable by voltage-constrained electric actuators. This discrepancy arises from two main factors:
1. **Decoupled Feasibility Evaluation:** Kinematic admissibility is often assessed without explicit consideration of the underlying actuator's execution dynamics. Consequently, acceleration envelopes may contain commands that exceed the physical capabilities of an actuator, particularly when operating at high joint velocities where voltage limits become more restrictive.
2. **Temporal Mismatch:** Kinematic reasoning typically operates in discrete time, predicting future states based on assumed acceleration limits. In contrast, the physical realizability of these accelerations is governed by continuous-time actuator dynamics and real-world voltage constraints, leading to a potential breakdown of the assumed state transitions upon physical execution.

This situation exposes a "missing abstraction" at the interface between higher-level kinematic reasoning and lower-level joint execution. When this interface admits physically unrealizable accelerations, the reliable closure between command and execution on physical hardware is compromised. The authors argue that addressing this issue at the joint level, rather than solely within whole-body controllers, is crucial for improving system robustness and performance.

Based on this motivation, the key objectives of this work are:
1. **To identify and formalize the missing execution-level abstraction:** Specifically, to highlight that joint acceleration bounds, while appearing kinematically realizable, can become physically unrealizable under voltage-constrained electric actuators.
2. **To propose Voltage-Realizable Acceleration (VRA):** This method aims to establish a joint-level acceleration interface that explicitly connects kinematic reasoning with actuator dynamics. VRA is designed to provide kinematic reasoning modules with acceleration bounds that are inherently realizable under voltage constraints.
3. **To filter unrealizable commands:** By restricting commanded accelerations to voltage-realizable constraints, VRA aims to eliminate acceleration commands that are physically unrealizable due to voltage limitations or model discrepancies.
4. **To conduct a systematic hardware study:** The research aims to empirically demonstrate the effects of the discretization–actuation mismatch and evaluate VRA's capacity to remove unrealizable accelerations, restore consistent execution near constraint boundaries, and reduce constraint-induced oscillations on physical robotic systems.
5. **To define a minimal realizability contract:** VRA intends to ensure that kinematic acceleration constraints are meaningful at execution time, acting as a precondition for discrete-time kinematic reasoning methods.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology, Voltage-Realizable Acceleration (VRA), aims to bridge the gap between discrete-time kinematic reasoning and voltage-constrained actuator physics by providing explicitly voltage-realizable acceleration bounds.

**Problem Setup:**
The problem considers a robotic system operating under discrete-time joint-level acceleration commands. Joint kinematics are defined by a set F comprising position (q) and velocity (˙q) bounds. Kinematic reasoning approximates this set by restricting commanded joint acceleration `¨q_k` to `[¨q_lb,k, ¨q_ub,k]`. The paper highlights that this kinematic admissibility does not guarantee physical realizability by actuators.
The actuator model assumes each joint is driven by a voltage-constrained electric motor, with the applied voltage `u_k = [u_d,k u_q,k]^T` satisfying `||u_k|| ≤ V_bus`. Under the common assumption of `i_d,k = 0`, the motor voltage is expressed as a function of `i_q,k`, `˙q_k`, and motor parameters. This implies that feasible motor torque, and consequently feasible joint acceleration, is state-dependent and decreases with increasing joint velocity.
The core problem is to characterize a joint-level interface that maps kinematically admissible accelerations to those physically realizable under voltage-constrained actuation, ensuring that for a given `¨q_k`, there exists an `i_q,k` satisfying the voltage limit and the kinematic/dynamic equations. This induces a state-dependent feasible torque interval `τ_k ∈ [τ_lb,k(˙q_k), τ_ub,k(˙q_k)]`.

**Acceleration Realizability from Voltage Constraints:**
VRA derives joint acceleration bounds directly from actuator voltage constraints. This is achieved under two mild assumptions:
1. **Electrical-mechanical time-scale separation:** The electrical speed is assumed constant over one control step, simplifying voltage feasibility to a one-dimensional quadratic constraint.
2. **Bounded model discrepancies:** Unmodeled effects (e.g., DC-link ripple, dead time, parameter mismatch) are lumped into a residual voltage term `u_res,k`, which is compensated to prevent model drift and ensure validity across operating conditions.

Voltage realizability is approximated by two complementary quadratic inequality constraints on the q-axis current `i_q,k`:
- A **transient voltage-realizable set (S_tr,k)**: Guarantees instantaneous realizability under discrete-time current dynamics.
- A **quasi-steady look-ahead set (S_st,k)**: Anticipates near-future back-EMF effects.
The intersection of these two sets, `I_k = S_tr,k ∩ S_st,k`, provides a state-dependent admissible q-axis current interval. Since joint torque is linearly related to `i_q,k`, this interval directly translates into a voltage-realizable torque and, subsequently, an acceleration interval `A_v,k`. Lemma 1 formally establishes that any acceleration produced by VRA admits realizability under voltage constraints by construction, assuming the stated actuator model and sampling conditions.

**Acceleration Realizability from Kinematic Constraints:**
VRA integrates with existing kinematic envelopes by considering position limits and the position-velocity relationship (Equations 8 and 9). A key difference is the treatment of velocity constraints:
- Unlike traditional methods that impose discrete-time velocity constraints (Equation 10) scaling with `1/∆t_p`, VRA **removes** these explicit constraints.
- Instead, the maximum admissible joint velocity is implicitly encoded within the motor voltage feasibility. At high speeds, back-EMF dominates, and the voltage constraint `p˙q_k-1φ ≤ V_limit` directly imposes an upper bound on achievable joint velocity. This allows the velocity bound to be structurally determined and adjusted by the available voltage budget. This continuous-time, physics-based approach to velocity limits is argued to be less conservative than discrete-time propagation.

**Combining Actuation and Kinematics:**
The full VRA process at each discrete-time step `k` involves:
1. **Residual Voltage Estimation:** `u_res,d,k` and `u_res,q,k` are computed from previous control steps using the nominal voltage model and measured actual voltages.
2. **Predictive Voltage Calculation:** `ˆu_d,k`, `ˆu_tr,q,k`, and `ˆu_st,q,k` are calculated for the current step `k` incorporating the residual voltages. `ˆu_st,q,k` includes a look-ahead step `s∆t` to anticipate future back-EMF.
3. **Current Set Determination:** `S_tr,k` and `S_st,k` are determined based on `ˆu_d,k`, `ˆu_tr,q,k`, `ˆu_st,q,k`, and `V_limit`. The intersection `I_k` is the final admissible q-axis current interval.
4. **Voltage-Realizable Acceleration Set (A_v,k):** `I_k` is converted to an acceleration interval `A_v,k` using the joint dynamics (`M¨q_k + h(˙q_k) = k_t i_q,k`).
5. **Kinematic Acceleration Set (A_k):** Kinematic acceleration bounds (`A_1,k`, `A_2,k`, `A_3,k`) are computed from position limits, taking `¨q_max,k` (from `A_v,k`) as the updated deceleration bounds. The discrete-time step for kinematic reasoning `∆t_p` is decoupled from the execution time step `∆t`.
6. **Commanded Acceleration Set (A_cmd,k):** The final acceleration command must lie within the intersection `A_cmd,k = A_v,k ∩ A_k`.
7. **Final Projection:** Due to potential model discrepancies, the commanded acceleration is projected back onto `I_k` to ensure execution realizability.

The computational order is explicitly stated as: States → `I_k` → `A_v,k` → `A_k` → `A_cmd,k` → `I_k` → Actuators.

**Experimental Setup:**
Experiments were conducted on actuated joints (Motor 8115, Motor 6210) and a wheel-legged quadruped robot. The control period (`∆t`) was fixed at 0.001 s, while the kinematic reasoning step (`∆t_p`) was varied (0.001, 0.005, 0.01 s). VRA was compared against Viability-Based Acceleration Control (VBAC) as a kinematic baseline and a Motor Operating Region (MOR) derived from dynamometer data as an actuator-level voltage-realizability baseline. Performance metrics included voltage saturation, velocity tracking conservation, braking smoothness, and oscillatory behavior (zero crossings).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of VRA focused on demonstrating its ability to synchronize kinematic reasoning with actuator voltage constraints and to propagate these benefits to whole-body robot behaviors.

**A. Validating Voltage-Realizable Acceleration from Actuator Constraints:**
Experiments on two distinct motors (Motor 8115 and Motor 6210) under varied operating conditions (nominal 24V/25°C and stressed 36V/45°C) showed that:
- Kinematic baselines (VBAC) frequently resulted in voltage saturation during aggressive step torque inputs, indicating that kinematically admissible commands were often physically unrealizable.
- VRA significantly reduced the occurrence of voltage saturation events across all tested configurations by explicitly restricting commanded accelerations to the admissible q-axis current set (`I_k`).
- Analysis indicated that `I_k` provided a more accurate characterization of voltage-realizable actuation compared to the Motor Operating Region (MOR) baseline. VRA integrated voltage-realizable bounds upstream, with residual clipping primarily attributable to model mismatch rather than fundamental design.

**B. Combining Kinematic Envelopes with Voltage-Realizable Acceleration:**
This set of experiments on a single Motor 8115 evaluated VRA's compatibility with discrete-time kinematic constraints while adhering to voltage-realizable bounds during a high-speed decelerating scenario towards position and velocity limits.
- **Torque Consistency:** VBAC exhibited significant discrepancies between commanded and feedback torques near kinematic bounds, indicating voltage infeasibility and torque distortion (Fig. 6). VRA, in contrast, maintained torque-realizable behavior both within and near the bounds.
- **Deceleration Profile:** The paper highlights that voltage-constrained actuators exhibit a "weak-then-strengthen" deceleration behavior at high speeds (due to back-EMF dominance at high velocity, then increasing current margin at lower velocity), which is incompatible with constant acceleration limits. VRA's method, by encoding velocity limits through motor voltage feasibility, resulted in less conservative and more continuous deceleration, avoiding the abrupt "switching-prone" behavior observed with VBAC (Fig. 5).
- **Near-boundary Performance:** Quantitative metrics (Table II) demonstrated that VRA, particularly with `∆t_p = 0.01s`, showed improved speed conservation during braking (smaller `∆˙q_max`), reduced velocity RMS error (`|∆˙q_RMS|`), lower torque RMS error (`|∆τ_RMS|`), and fewer zero crossings (`f_zc`) compared to VBAC. This indicates smoother braking, reduced oscillations, and more consistent execution near kinematic boundaries.

**C. Propagation of Joint-Level Realizability to Whole-Body Behaviors:**
The efficacy of VRA was further validated on a real wheel-legged quadruped robot, where joint-level acceleration interfaces (VRA or VBAC) were enforced transparently beneath a high-level whole-body reinforcement learning controller.
- **Two-leg Jumping Motion:** In a jumping task with position limits on swing and stance legs:
 - At a short kinematic reasoning time scale (`∆t_p = 0.01s`), both VBAC and VRA completed the motion but exhibited boundary-induced oscillations.
 - At a longer time scale (`∆t_p = 0.05s`), VBAC became unstable and failed the task. VRA, however, completed the motion with full constraint satisfaction and without noticeable oscillations (Fig. 7a), demonstrating robustness to reasoning-execution time-scale mismatch.
- **Bipedal Standing with Upper-Limb End-Effector Motion:** This task imposed maximum velocity limits on all actuated joints, requiring stringent velocity control during standing.
 - VBAC failed at `∆t_p = 0.05s`. When operating at its best-performing time scale (`∆t_p = 0.01s`), VBAC still induced strong oscillations in both lower and upper-limb joints, leading to constraint-induced pattern changes and task failure (Fig. 2c, Fig. 7b).
 - VRA, even at a longer `∆t_p = 0.05s`, maintained smooth upper-limb motion and stable bipedal standing, successfully completing the end-effector task.

Collectively, these experiments indicate that VRA effectively functions as a missing acceleration interface, restoring consistency between kinematic acceleration reasoning and voltage-limited actuation, thereby preventing adverse behaviors observed with traditional kinematic approaches.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research addresses a fundamental issue in robotic control concerning the implicit assumption of physical realizability for kinematically admissible commands. By introducing Voltage-Realizable Acceleration (VRA), the authors propose a principled method to explicitly ground discrete-time joint acceleration bounds in the physics of voltage-constrained electric actuators.

The significance and potential impact of this work include:

1. **Enhanced Execution Fidelity:** VRA improves the consistency between high-level kinematic commands and low-level actuator execution. This helps ensure that the robot's physical capabilities are accurately reflected in the acceleration bounds provided to controllers, leading to more predictable and reliable hardware performance.
2. **Reduced Constraint-Induced Instabilities:** The experimental results demonstrate that VRA effectively eliminates kinematically admissible but physically unrealizable acceleration commands, which frequently cause voltage saturation, torque distortion, and boundary-induced oscillations in conventional methods. This translates to smoother, more stable operation, particularly when robots are operating near joint position or velocity limits.
3. **Improved Robustness to Time-Scale Mismatch:** By integrating continuous-time actuator dynamics into discrete-time kinematic reasoning, VRA exhibits robustness to variations in the kinematic reasoning time step (`∆t_p`). This allows higher-level controllers to reason over longer time horizons without inadvertently generating physically impossible commands, which can lead to instability in traditional approaches.
4. **Complementary to Advanced Control Frameworks:** VRA is designed to be a foundational layer, complementing existing whole-body, viability-based, and safety-critical control methods (e.g., CBFs, hierarchical QPs, MPC). It provides an "execution-aware" acceleration interface, ensuring that the inputs to these higher-level frameworks are physically feasible, thus making their theoretical guarantees more meaningful in practical hardware deployments.
5. **Re-evaluation of Velocity Limits:** The approach highlights that velocity limits can be inherently encoded within motor voltage feasibility, arising from continuous-time motor physics rather than conservative discrete-time propagation. This can lead to less conservative and more natural velocity control.
6. **Broader Applicability:** While demonstrated on a wheel-legged quadruped, the underlying principles of connecting actuator voltage dynamics to joint acceleration bounds are applicable to a wide range of robots utilizing voltage-constrained electric actuators.

The acknowledged limitations, such as its current joint-level interval-based nature and focus on a specific motor model without considering effects like field weakening or thermal limits, indicate avenues for future research. Extending VRA to capture multi-joint feasibility and integrating it into system-level control frameworks could significantly expand its impact on developing more robust and capable autonomous robots.
