# ForceDelta-VLA: Distilling Force-Conditioned ActionCorrections for Contact-Rich Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> ForceDelta-VLA splits a force-aware VLA's output into a slow reference action plus fast learned corrections. A frozen teacher's force-conditioned and learned force-agnostic predictions, paired on shared context and noise, define force-correction targets; a separate delay target handles stale references. A 2.4 ms student updates corrections between chunks, reaching 82.2% mean success on nine real-robot contact-rich tasks versus 54.4% for ForceVLA.

## Problem

- Force-aware VLAs such as ForceVLA predict task motion and contact-dependent adjustment in one action output, so contact feedback waits for a full new action chunk.
- Demonstrations record only the final action, with no labels separating reference motion from correction; many reference-plus-correction splits sum to the same demonstrated action.
- Asynchronous multi-rate policies separate computation or sensor rates without defining correction supervision; residual RL and corrective imitation need rewards or extra on-policy interaction for correction signals.

## Method

- **Force-agnostic correction distillation**: a frozen ==ForceVLA==-based teacher with a causal ==TCN== wrench-history encoder gains a learned missing-force token plus ==low-rank adapter==; force-conditioned minus force-agnostic predictions, sharing context and flow noise, give force targets.
- **State-consistent dual-correction policy**: a shared attention module with a learned query runs twice, once with force token masked, feeding separate force and delay heads; delay target includes reference-state alignment.
- **Asynchronous schedule replay**: sampled query periods and inference latencies select the latest completed cached reference per correction time, training the student on delayed references and cached visual-language context it sees at deployment.

## Results

- Nine real-robot single-arm and bimanual tasks: **82.2%** mean success versus **54.4%** ForceVLA, **57.2%** TA-VLA, **45.0%** ImplicitRDP, and **70.6%** for directly executing the Stage-1 Temporal Teacher.
- Peak contact force drops about **26%** versus ForceVLA (**16.0** to **11.8 N** single-arm); correction pass takes **2.43 ms** versus **189.7 ms** reference; unseen objects **66.7%** versus **40.0%** teacher.
- Ablations: demonstration-derived correction targets cut success **12.5**/**17.5** points, single combined head **10** points, fast full-action distillation **17.5**/**22.5** points; **200 ms** extra reference delay costs **15** versus teacher's **25** points.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - When demonstrations lack decomposition labels, paired predictions from one frozen teacher with and without a modality give clean, noise-matched residual targets, beating demonstration-minus-prediction supervision.
> - Treat a missing modality as a learned null token, not zeros; zero-wrench queries misrepresent 'no sensor' as 'no contact' and degrade both reference and targets.
> - Let a fast student predict corrections to a reused slow chunk rather than full actions; train on replayed inference delays so stale references stay correctable.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position within the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models connect visual observations, language instructions, and robot actions. Recent VLA research has extended these models with force or torque information because many manipulation tasks cannot be solved reliably from vision alone. USB insertion, plug manipulation, button pressing, wiping, and drawer operation require a robot to recognize contact, friction, misalignment, or changes in resistance and adapt its motion accordingly.

Most force-aware VLA policies generate complete action chunks using visual, language, proprioceptive, and force inputs. This design combines two distinct functions: generating a task-level motion and making rapid local adjustments when contact conditions change. Since generative VLA inference is computationally expensive, the resulting policy may not react quickly enough between successive action-chunk updates.

ForceDelta-VLA addresses this issue through a slow–fast architecture. A relatively slow teacher generates a reusable reference action without measured force input. A lightweight student then modifies that reference using recent force history and robot state. The student does not regenerate the complete action trajectory; instead, it predicts force-dependent and delay-dependent corrections. This places the work at the intersection of force-aware manipulation, asynchronous robot control, residual policy learning, and policy distillation.

The method differs from residual reinforcement learning and corrective imitation learning because it does not require rewards, additional on-policy interaction, or human correction data. It also differs from conventional policy distillation, which generally transfers an entire policy or action predictor to a faster model. Here, only the correction component is distilled, while the generative teacher remains responsible for task-level reference motion.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

#### 2.1. Separating task motion from contact adaptation

The central motivation is that task-level motion and contact-dependent adjustment have different temporal requirements. During USB insertion, for example, the robot may need to maintain a general forward motion while correcting its position or orientation when the connector contacts the port rim. A complete action prediction generated several hundred milliseconds earlier may contain an appropriate approach direction but become unsuitable after a new contact state emerges.

The authors therefore aim to decompose the commanded pose into three components:

1. A reference action representing the reusable task motion.
2. A force correction responding to recent wrench measurements.
3. A delay correction compensating for the fact that the reference action was generated earlier and may no longer correspond exactly to the robot’s current state.

The intended result is a fast corrective pathway that can operate between reference-action updates.

#### 2.2. Addressing the lack of correction labels

Teleoperated demonstrations contain final commanded actions, but they do not identify which portion of each action is task-level motion and which portion is a contact-specific adjustment. Directly subtracting a reference from a demonstrated action is ambiguous because multiple decompositions can produce the same command.

The paper’s second objective is therefore to construct explicit correction targets from the existing demonstrations without collecting new labels. The proposed solution uses paired predictions from a frozen teacher: one prediction conditioned on force history and one prediction using a learned force-agnostic mode. Their difference defines a force-correction target.

#### 2.3. Handling asynchronous execution

A further objective is to train the student under the same stale-reference conditions that occur during deployment. If training always supplies a current reference action, the policy may fail when the deployed teacher is still computing a new action chunk. ForceDelta-VLA addresses this through asynchronous schedule replay, in which training simulates reference-query periods, inference delays, cached context, and the selection of the most recently completed reference.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Overall architecture

The method uses an offline dataset of teleoperated trajectories. Each trajectory contains images, a language instruction, the robot’s end-effector state, commanded actions, and a history of six-dimensional wrench measurements consisting of force and torque.

The commanded pose is represented as the sum of the reference pose, force correction, and delay correction. Corrections are applied to translation and a continuous six-dimensional rotation representation. The gripper command is retained from the reference action rather than separately corrected. For bimanual tasks, both arms’ inputs and pose actions are concatenated and predicted jointly.

#### 3.2. Stage 1: Temporal force-conditioned teacher

The first stage trains a force-aware teacher based on ForceVLA. Instead of using only an instantaneous force embedding, the teacher processes a 100-millisecond wrench history through a causal temporal convolutional network. The teacher predicts a 50-step action chunk conditioned on visual observations, language, robot state, and the encoded wrench history.

After training, the teacher parameters are frozen. This teacher provides a force-conditioned action distribution against which the correction targets can be defined.

#### 3.3. Stage 2: Force-agnostic reference policy

The reference policy is derived from the frozen teacher but is trained to operate without measured wrench input. The authors distinguish unavailable force input from a physically measured zero-force signal. A learned token represents unavailable force history, and a low-rank adapter modifies the pose output of the action expert only in this force-agnostic mode.

The resulting policy generates the reference action used during execution. This design is important because a zero wrench can carry physical meaning, whereas missing force measurements indicate a different operating condition.

#### 3.4. Stage 3: Paired correction-target construction

At a sampled correction time, three pieces of information are considered:

- The stored reference action generated earlier at state \(S_k\).
- A current force-agnostic prediction evaluated at the present state.
- A current force-conditioned prediction evaluated at the same present state and using the recent wrench history.

The current force-conditioned and force-agnostic predictions share the same cached visual-language context, robot state, flow noise, and sampling conditions. The pose difference between them is used as the force-correction target. Matching the context and sampling noise is intended to ensure that the difference primarily reflects the influence of force information rather than unrelated variation in the generative prediction.

The delay-correction target captures the remaining discrepancy between the current force-agnostic prediction and the previously stored reference. It includes both the difference between the two reference trajectories and an alignment term that converts the change from the old reference state to the current state into normalized action coordinates. Thus, the delay branch learns more than simple temporal interpolation; it also learns to compensate for state mismatch.

#### 3.5. Student correction policy

The student receives the cached task context, the current robot state, recent wrench history, the interpolated segment of the stored reference action, and timing features. Timing features encode information such as cache age and the phase of interpolation within the action chunk.

A shared attention module processes these inputs using a learned correction query. It is evaluated twice. The force-correction pass receives all inputs, including the force token. The delay-correction pass masks the force token, preventing the delay output from directly depending on wrench measurements. Separate output heads then predict five-step force and delay corrections.

The training objective is a temporally weighted mean-squared error between each student correction and its corresponding teacher-defined target. Earlier correction steps receive greater or equal weight according to a decay factor, and a separate coefficient balances the delay loss against the force loss.

#### 3.6. Asynchronous deployment

During deployment, the teacher periodically generates a new force-agnostic reference action and stores its action chunk, query state, and task context. The student runs independently and can issue multiple correction queries while the teacher is computing the next reference. At each control step, the executor selects the latest valid completed student result, interpolates the reference action, adds the two corrections, clips them separately, and converts the result into an absolute command.

The reference-action pathway requires approximately 189.7 milliseconds per forward pass, whereas the correction pathway requires only 2.43 milliseconds. This difference allows the student to respond at a rate compatible with the robot’s 100 Hz command-transmission limit without repeatedly running the full generative model.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Success rates

The evaluation covers nine real-robot tasks: five single-arm tasks and four bimanual tasks. Each task has 200 demonstrations, and the reported comparisons use 20 trials per task and method.

ForceDelta-VLA achieves an average success rate of 82.2%. This compares with 54.4% for ForceVLA, 57.2% for TA-VLA, 47.2% for the non-force VLA baseline \(\pi_{0.5}\), and 45.0% for ImplicitRDP. Direct execution of the Stage-1 Temporal Teacher reaches 70.6%.

ForceDelta-VLA improves over ForceVLA on all nine tasks. The largest improvements occur on USB Insertion, increasing from 40% to 80%; Button Pressing, increasing from 50% to 85%; and Plug Insertion, increasing from 35% to 70%. Relative to the Temporal Teacher, the complete system provides an additional average gain of 11.7 percentage points, with gains of 25 points on USB Insertion and 20 points on Plug Insertion.

#### 4.2. Contact force and completion time

Over successful trials, the mean peak contact force is 11.8 N on the single-arm platform and 12.5 N on the bimanual platform. ForceVLA produces corresponding values of 16.0 N and 16.8 N. The reported reductions are 4.2 N and 4.3 N, or approximately 26% on each platform.

Completion time also decreases relative to ForceVLA, by 5.2 seconds for single-arm tasks and 5.9 seconds for bimanual tasks. Compared with the Temporal Teacher, ForceDelta-VLA reduces peak force by 3.3 N and 2.2 N and reduces completion time by 3.9 and 3.4 seconds on the two platforms, respectively.

A Button Pressing example shows that correction magnitude is small before contact, increases during pressing, and decreases after contact release. This behavior is consistent with the intended role of the force branch.

#### 4.3. Delay sensitivity and ablations

When additional reference-action latency is increased from zero to 200 milliseconds on USB and Plug Insertion, ForceDelta-VLA’s success decreases by 15 percentage points, compared with a 25-point decrease for the Temporal Teacher. Its mean peak force increases by 3.7 N, compared with 9.6 N for the teacher. These results indicate that fast correction updates provide some robustness to delayed reference generation, although they do not eliminate the effect of delay.

Ablations support both correction components. Removing force correction decreases success by 15 and 17.5 percentage points on the single-arm and bimanual subsets and increases peak force. Removing learned delay correction decreases success by 7.5 points on both subsets. Replacing the paired teacher target with a demonstration-derived target also reduces success, as does using a zero-wrench teacher query.

A single head trained to predict the combined correction performs worse than the two-head design. Fast full-action distillation also underperforms correction distillation, supporting the decision to preserve the reference action and assign the student a narrower prediction problem. Omitting asynchronous schedule replay reduces success by 7.5 and 12.5 percentage points on the two subsets.

#### 4.4. Generalization and failure modes

On unseen objects in USB Insertion, Object Flipping, and bimanual Whiteboard Wiping, ForceDelta-VLA achieves 66.7% success, compared with 40.0% for the Temporal Teacher and 16.7% for ImplicitRDP. Its mean peak force over successful trials is 13.6 N, 8.2 N lower than the Temporal Teacher.

Failures remain when the task requires a substantial change in strategy. Insertion failures often involve jamming at a rim, after which the correction does not retreat sufficiently to retry. Drawer and cabinet failures can occur when pulling begins before stable contact. Wiping and flipping failures involve incorrect contact geometry. These cases suggest that the student is better suited to local correction than to replacing the reference trajectory or planning a new approach direction.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

ForceDelta-VLA provides a method for obtaining fast, force-responsive behavior from an existing VLA policy without requiring explicit correction labels or additional interactive data collection. Its main methodological contribution is to define force corrections through matched force-conditioned and force-agnostic teacher predictions, while treating reference delay and state mismatch as a separate learning problem.

The results suggest that decomposing action generation can improve both task success and contact behavior. The approach may be useful where generative policy inference is slower than the rate at which contact conditions change, particularly in insertion, pressing, and constrained-motion tasks. It also offers a general framework for distilling specialized reactive components while retaining a larger model for semantic and task-level reasoning.

The evidence is limited to nine real-robot tasks, two platforms, and wrench estimates derived from joint torques rather than dedicated force/torque sensors. The experiments also use relatively small evaluation sets, especially for unseen-object generalization. The failure analysis indicates that the method still depends on the reference trajectory when recovery requires retreating, changing direction, or re-establishing contact. Future work involving higher-precision sensing and reference policies capable of explicit recovery could address these limitations.
