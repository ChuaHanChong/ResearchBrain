# Dexterous Tactile World Model

> [!summary] Summary
> DTWM is a video world model that predicts future frames of egocentric bimanual manipulation from observed video plus whole-hand tactile glove signals. Each hand's tactile embedding is added as a zero-initialized residual at that hand's projected image location through a Gaussian footprint, under block-causal masking. Versus a matched vision-only model, it cuts hand-motion underestimation from 23% to 9% and hand-region perceptual error by 7.4%.

## Problem

- Video-trained world models must infer contact from ambiguous visual evidence, since hands occlude contact regions and light touch looks like firm pressing, yielding merged fingers and stalled hands.
- Prior visual-tactile world models represent touch as global features, extra tokens, or separate tactile images, never aligned with the image region where contact actually occurs.
- Existing visual-tactile world models train on robot fingertip or gripper sensors conditioned on actions or subgoals, leaving action-free prediction of egocentric human manipulation unaddressed.

## Method

- ==Hand-located tactile residual==: each hand's standardized pressure and flexion reading is embedded and spread over video tokens with a normalized ==Gaussian footprint== centered at the skeleton-projected hand position.
- ==Zero-initialized injection== into five of thirty blocks of a frozen, ==LoRA-adapted video diffusion transformer==, with a fixed scale buffer letting the tactile pathway be switched off at inference.
- ==Block-causal chunked flow-matching prediction==: observes thirteen clean frames with touch and hand skeletons, then denoises thirty-six future frames in one pass with no future pose, action, or touch.

## Results

- On EgoTouch held-out clips, **DTWM** reaches full-frame LPIPS **0.4157** in-distribution and **0.4774** out-of-distribution, beating the vision-only baseline (**0.4243**/**0.4882**), VT-WM, FeelWorld, and TouchWorld.
- Ablations: per-taxel contact without force raises in-distribution LPIPS to **0.4200**, and one contact bit per hand reaches **0.4307**, worse than vision-only, so force magnitude and location both matter.
- The gain grows over the horizon, about **4.1x** larger after the first predicted chunk; disabling touch at inference still beats the baseline (**0.4208** vs **0.4243** in-distribution LPIPS).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - How a modality is injected matters as much as having it: with identical glove data, spatially indexing touch at the hand location beat token, image, and global-feature conditioning.
> - Force trends carry predictive information beyond contact state: steady force predicts sustained grasps, while rising or decaying force makes release 1.8 to 2.5 times likelier.
> - Coarse tactile signals can hurt rather than help, yet training with rich touch leaves a residual visual benefit even when the sensor is absent at deployment.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A video model that predicts what hands will do next can see a hand on an object without knowing whether it is barely touching, pressing, or holding it. Contact can decide whether a grasp holds or an object moves, and the hand often hides that contact from the camera. DTWM addresses this by adding the touch readings from two gloves near the hands in the image, then predicting the future video.

In one held-out clip, both hands open a spray can. At frame 12 the model has video and touch. Its prediction for frame 44 is then compared against the ground truth. In this example, DTWM's hands appear more like the ground truth than the vision-only prediction.

The authors argue that better prediction could help generate training data, initialize policies, or provide simulated environments. The paper tests future-video prediction, not those downstream uses.

Dashed boxes mark ground-truth hand regions. Right: a radar chart compares TouchWorld, FeelWorld, VT-WM, and DTWM on six axes, including full-frame and hand-crop LPIPS (lower is better).

### 2. Put touch where the hand is

The two signals describe different things. Glove channels refer to patches of skin, while video tokens refer to image locations. DTWM bridges this gap in four steps:

- It embeds each observed hand's reading.
- It uses the observed rendered hand skeleton to locate that hand in the video-token grid.
- It spreads the embedding around that location with a normalized Gaussian footprint.
- It adds the result as a residual in five of the pretrained transformer's 30 blocks.

The residual projection starts at zero, so the pretrained predictor is unchanged at initialization. The model is trained on paired video and bilateral glove readings.

At inference, the model observes 13 frames and predicts the next 36 in one pass. A block-causal mask prevents predicted chunks from seeing later chunks. No future pose, action, or touch is provided.

Schematic of the pipeline. Observed frames, hand pose, touch, and task instruction feed a 30-block video diffusion transformer. A tactile embedding is placed at the hand with a footprint and injected into five blocks, and predicted frames come out.

### 3. Same predictor, touch removed

The key comparison is against a vision-only control. It is matched to DTWM in architecture, parameter count, and training. It keeps the tactile pathway, hand locations, and injection sites, but its tactile readings are zeroed.

The clearest gain is in hand motion. The test was the full 36-frame prediction on the 3,000-clip held-out set. The flow-magnitude ratio is generated total hand-region flow divided by ground-truth flow, with 1 ideal. On this measure, generated hand flow is 0.771 of ground truth for the control and 0.912 for DTWM. That means underestimation of hand motion falls from 23% to 9%.

A smaller three-run analysis measured perceptual quality. LPIPS is a learned perceptual distance, where lower is better. The hand crop is an enlarged region around the ground-truth hands and usually includes the manipulated object. Against the control, hand-crop LPIPS falls 7.4% and full-frame LPIPS falls 5.1%. On the held-out-episode split, mean hand-crop LPIPS is 0.3817 for the control and 0.3536 for DTWM. DTWM wins 7 of 9 independently trained run pairings on each of the two smaller splits.

The second comparison simplifies the touch input instead of removing it. Each variant was trained once and tested on the same splits.

- **Per-taxel binary contact** keeps contact locations and finger-flexion channels but removes force magnitude.
- **One contact bit per hand** also discards spatial location and flexion.

Both are worse than the full reading. The full reading gives full-frame / hand-crop LPIPS of 0.4157 / 0.3227 in-distribution and 0.4774 / 0.3668 on the held-out task split. Per-taxel contact gives 0.4200 / 0.3246 and 0.4823 / 0.3745. One bit per hand gives 0.4307 / 0.3318 and 0.5014 / 0.3864.

This supports the value of richer tactile input, especially force magnitude. The hand-level bit removes several kinds of information together, so it does not isolate spatial location as the sole cause.

### 4. Contact is the missing cue

Placing each hand's touch signal at the hand's location in video-token space lets a video predictor use contact that the camera cannot see. It improves predicted hand motion and perceptual quality against a matched control.

The scope is future-frame prediction of human manipulation on EgoTouch, a roughly 20-hour corpus. The work does not show a robot policy succeeding. In the 3,000-clip evaluation, 2,728 clips have tasks with a same, near-duplicate, or related counterpart in training. Only 272 clips come from three tasks with no close counterpart. The authors note that hand reconstruction misses some frames and that DTWM cannot yet be placed directly inside a robot planner or policy.
