# Steering Robustness into World Action Models via Mechanistic Interpretability and Optimal Control

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Georgia Tech developed a framework combining mechanistic interpretability with optimal control to enhance the robustness of World Action Models (WAMs) against out-of-distribution shifts. This approach introduced an adaptive, closed-loop activation steering method (WA-LQR) that intervenes during inference, achieving up to a 41% increase in WAM success rates without model retraining.

## Problem

- World Action Models (WAMs) often exhibit brittleness and degraded performance when encountering out-of-distribution (OOD) shifts, such as changes in camera viewpoint, robot initial states, or visual corruptions.
- Enhancing WAM robustness typically requires extensive data collection for training or computationally expensive retraining processes, which are impractical for real-world deployment scenarios.
- A lack of understanding exists regarding the internal representations of WAMs that contribute to these failures and how to effectively intervene to improve robustness without modifying model weights.

## Method

- Conducted a ==mechanistic interpretability== study to identify robustness-relevant nuisance features within WAM ==activation spaces==, determining if they are represented by approximately linear directions amenable to steering.
- Developed a training-free open-loop ==activation steering== method (**ActAdd**) that adds a scaled ==contrastive direction== to WAM activations during inference to bias model behavior.
- Introduced a novel closed-loop optimal control method, **World-Action Linear Quadratic Regulator (WA-LQR)**, which projects WAM activations into a low-dimensional ==contrastive subspace==, models ==local linear dynamics==, and synthesizes a ==feedback controller== to adaptively steer activations only when they deviate from desired feature strengths.

## Results

- WA-LQR substantially improved WAM robustness, increasing success rates by up to **41%** for models like Cosmos-Policy **2B** and DiT4DiT across various OOD perturbations (e.g., camera orientation, gripper position, Gaussian noise).
- Open-loop ActAdd also generally outperformed unsteered baselines, sometimes surpassing WA-LQR for perturbations with strong linear separability (e.g., camera Gaussian noise corruption) but showed high sensitivity to its scaling parameter.
- The mechanistic analysis, based on linear separability (SVM hinge loss), successfully predicted steerability: models/tasks exhibiting lower hinge loss (higher separability) achieved greater robustness improvements through steering.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Robustness-relevant nuisance factors in WAM activations can be identified in low-dimensional contrastive subspaces and often exhibit approximately linear separability, indicating their amenability to steering interventions.
> - WAM dynamics demonstrate local linearity within these identified latent subspaces, which validates the application of linear optimal control techniques like LQR for adaptive, closed-loop steering.
> - The effectiveness of activation steering is architecture-dependent; models like Cosmos-Policy and DiT4DiT show significant improvements, while others like LingBot-VA, with weaker linear separability, respond minimally to steering.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

Robot learning has seen advancements through the application of foundation models, which are capable of generating generalized policies across various objects, tasks, and environments. Within this domain, Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models directly map observations and instructions to actions. World Action Models (WAMs) represent a further development, integrating action prediction with future-state modeling, typically utilizing video-generative backbones. This integration aims to produce dynamically-coherent robot policies, offering advantages in long-horizon reasoning and potentially improved robustness. However, existing WAMs frequently exhibit brittleness when faced with out-of-distribution (OOD) shifts, such as variations in camera viewpoint, changes in robot initial states, or visual corruptions. These real-world perturbations are often unavoidable during deployment and cannot be exhaustively covered by training data alone, highlighting a need for methods to enhance robustness without extensive data collection or computationally expensive retraining.

Concurrently, mechanistic interpretability (MI) has emerged as a field focused on understanding the internal representations and computations within neural networks that govern model behavior. This approach has primarily been applied to large language models (LLMs) and, more recently, to image and video generative models. A key finding in LLMs is that semantic features often manifest as approximately linear directions within the activation space, leading to the development of "activation steering" techniques. These methods involve modifying hidden states during inference to alter model behavior without requiring retraining. While MI has begun to be explored in robotics foundation models, existing studies have predominantly concentrated on VLAs, investigating steerable activation directions, feature observability, attention head finetuning, and causal reliance on visual regions.

This research distinguishes itself by applying MI to WAMs, which, unlike VLAs, integrate video-generative backbones to jointly encode future visual states, action dynamics, and control outputs. The paper addresses the specific challenge of WAM brittleness under OOD shifts by leveraging MI to diagnose the nature of these failures within activation space and subsequently developing inference-time intervention methods. It extends activation steering techniques to the WAM context, proposing both open-loop and closed-loop control strategies. The work contributes to the growing body of research on enhancing the robustness and interpretability of complex robot learning models, particularly by bridging MI with model-based optimal control for practical application in robotics.

### 2. Key objectives and motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the observed brittleness of World Action Models (WAMs) when operating under distribution shifts. While WAMs offer a promising paradigm for robotic control by coupling action prediction with future-state modeling, their performance degrades under common real-world perturbations such as changes in camera orientation, variations in initial gripper position, and visual noise. These nuisance factors are inherent to deployment environments, and it is impractical to cover them comprehensively through training data or to continually retrain models for every new shift. Therefore, there is a clear need to improve WAM robustness without resorting to extensive data collection or slow, resource-intensive retraining processes.

Against this background, the research is guided by two fundamental questions:
1. Can the internal activations of WAMs provide insights into why these models fail under various perturbations?
2. If such insights are gained, can these internal representations be leveraged to enhance WAM robustness without necessitating finetuning?

These questions translate into two specific, detailed objectives:

**Problem 1: Linear feature discovery in WAM activation space.**
The objective is to mechanistically interpret WAM activations to determine whether robustness-relevant nuisance features are represented by approximately linear directions within the WAM's internal activation space. This involves conducting a systematic study across different WAM architectures (Cosmos-Policy, DiT4DiT, LingBot-VA) and various perturbation types (camera position, gripper position, Gaussian image noise). For each layer and denoising timestep of the WAM, the researchers aim to identify low-dimensional projections and linear directions that can effectively separate activations corresponding to nominal (e.g., successful or clean inputs) and perturbed (e.g., unsuccessful or noisy inputs) rollouts. The rationale is that if robustness-critical features exhibit such linear separability, they may be amenable to targeted steering interventions. This objective also involves quantitatively evaluating the degree of linear separability, for instance, by measuring the classification loss of a linear Support Vector Machine (SVM) on these projected activations, to predict the potential for steerability.

**Problem 2: Training-free robustness steering.**
Building upon the insights from Problem 1, the second objective is to synthesize inference-time steering policies that can modify WAM activations to modulate the robustness-relevant features identified. This objective specifically focuses on *training-free* methods, meaning interventions should occur at inference time without altering the model's learned weights. The research proposes two types of steering approaches:
- **Open-loop activation addition:** This involves adapting existing techniques, primarily from LLMs, to construct contrastive activation directions from examples with and without the target perturbation, and then adding a scaled version of this direction to the WAM's activations during inference.
- **Closed-loop feedback steering using optimal control:** This involves developing a novel method, World-Action Linear Quadratic Regulator (WA-LQR). WA-LQR aims to provide a more adaptive and minimally-invasive intervention by projecting activations into a low-dimensional contrastive subspace, modeling the local linearity of WAM activation dynamics within this subspace, and then using LQR to synthesize a feedback controller. This controller is designed to steer activations only when they deviate from a desired feature strength, thereby avoiding potential oversteering and degradation associated with purely open-loop methods.

By achieving these objectives, the research seeks to provide both a diagnostic tool for WAM robustness and practical, efficient methods for improving WAM performance under challenging OOD conditions.

### 3. Methodology and approach

The methodology of this research comprises a mechanistic study to identify robustness-relevant features in WAM activation space, followed by the development and evaluation of two activation steering methods: open-loop activation addition and a novel closed-loop optimal control approach, WA-LQR.

**Mechanistic Study for Interpreting WAMs (Section 4):**
1. **Model Selection and Perturbations:** The study investigated three WAM architectures: Cosmos-Policy 2B, DiT4DiT, and LingBot-VA. Three types of robustness-relevant perturbations were considered: initial gripper position changes, initial camera position changes, and Gaussian image noise corruption.
2. **Activation Collection:** For each perturbation type, contrastive datasets were created, consisting of inputs corresponding to nominal conditions (D+) and perturbed conditions (D-). For instance, for Gaussian noise, D+ contained clean inputs, and D- contained noised inputs. For gripper and camera perturbations, D+ could be unperturbed inputs or inputs leading to successful rollouts, while D- were perturbed inputs or inputs leading to unsuccessful rollouts. Activations were collected from a full model forward pass across all relevant layers and denoising timesteps. Due to the high dimensionality of activations (F, H, W, D), an average pooling was applied over token positions and robot action chunk timesteps to obtain a summarized activation vector `x̄ ∈ R^D`.
3. **Contrastive Direction Calculation:** For each paired input `(ξ+, ξ-)`, a contrastive direction `d_k = x̄+_k - x̄-_k` was computed.
4. **Linear Separability Evaluation:** To assess the presence of simple, interpretable geometric structure, a Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed on the set of contrastive directions `{d_k}`. Activations (`x̄+` and `x̄-`) were projected onto the top three principal components. A linear Support Vector Machine (SVM) was then trained on these 3D projected activations, and the average hinge loss per sample was used as a quantitative metric for linear separability. A loss of 0 indicated perfect classification, while a loss of 1 indicated random classification. This metric served as a predictor for steerability.

**Activation Steering for Robustifying WAMs (Section 5):**

**A. Open-Loop Activation Addition (ActAdd):**
1. **Contrastive Vector Construction:** Given paired inputs `(ξ+n, ξ-n)`, contrastive activation directions `d^(n)_l,t,τ` were computed by subtracting hidden states from the two forward passes for each layer `l`, denoising timestep `t`, and action-chunk timestep `τ`.
2. **Steering Vector Formation:** An average steering vector `a_l,t` was calculated by averaging `d^(n)_l,t,τ` over all `N` pairs and `H_a` action-chunk positions.
3. **Inference-Time Intervention:** During inference, the steering vector `a_l,t` was added to the WAM activations `x_l,t,τ` at each chunk and timestep with a scaling factor `γ`: `x_l,t,τ ← x_l,t,τ + γa_l,t`. Positive `γ` values pushed towards `ξ+` behavior, while negative values reversed the effect. This method is training-free and weight-preserving but open-loop, meaning it does not adapt to the current activation state or WAM dynamics, potentially leading to oversteering.

**B. Steering Latent World Activation Dynamics via WA-LQR (World-Action Linear Quadratic Regulator):**
WA-LQR aims to provide closed-loop feedback control by adapting to the WAM's activation state.
1. **Dimensionality Reduction:** For each layer-denoising pair `(l, t)`, a low-dimensional (e.g., 64-dimensional) contrastive subspace was constructed. This involved pooling contrastive directions and applying streaming randomized Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) to obtain an orthonormal basis `V_l,t`. A projection matrix `P_l,t = V^⊤_l,t` was used to map high-dimensional activations `x_l,t,τ` to a low-dimensional latent space `z_l,t,τ = P_l,t x_l,t,τ`.
2. **Local Linear Dynamics Assumption:** The research assumes that WAM dynamics are locally linear within this latent subspace. The reduced latent dynamics were approximated as `δz_(l+1),t,τ ≈ ẽA_l,t δz_l,t,τ + ẽB_l,t δu_l,t,τ`, where `ẽA_l,t` and `ẽB_l,t` are projected Jacobians of the controlled block dynamics. These were computed efficiently using Jacobian-vector products (JVPs) or vector-Jacobian products (VJPs), avoiding explicit materialization of high-dimensional Jacobians.
3. **Feature Setpoint Definition:** A latent contrastive direction `ẽz_l,t` was derived by averaging projected contrastive vectors. A desired feature strength `β*l,t = λ||ẽz_l,t||_2` was set, where `λ` controls steering strength. The feature tracking error `α_l,t,τ` was defined as the difference between desired and realized feature strength, and `δz_l,t,τ` represented the latent tracking error.
4. **LQR Controller Synthesis:** For each action chunk `τ` and denoising timestep `t`, an independent L-step LQR problem was solved over the transformer blocks. This minimizes a quadratic cost function of state deviations (`δz`) from the feature setpoint and control effort (`δu`). The control penalty `R_chunk_l,t(τ)` incorporated an action-decay schedule, making steering strongest for early chunks and gradually decaying. The solution yielded feedback gains `K_l,t,τ`.
5. **Inference-Time Control:** The optimal controller for intervention `u*_l,t,τ` was given by `¯u_l,t,τ + K_l,t,τ α_l,t,τ v_z_l,t`. When `¯u` is zero, the intervention magnitude is proportional to the online feature-tracking error. The full WA-LQR procedure chained these `T` per-timestep, `L`-layer controllers through WAM inference. This closed-loop nature allows WA-LQR to adapt to the realized latent activation at each layer and timestep, steering only when necessary.

### 4. Main findings and results

The research presents several findings regarding the mechanistic interpretability of WAMs and the effectiveness of activation steering methods for improving robustness.

**Activation Properties (Section 6.1):**
1. **Low-Dimensional Feature Representation:** Empirical validation supported the assumption that robustness-relevant factors in WAM activations reside in a low-dimensional contrastive subspace. For instance, the variance of contrastive vectors for camera orientation perturbations in Cosmos-Policy 2B was largely captured by a few dimensions when projected into a 64-dimensional latent subspace. This observation suggests that perturbations primarily influence a limited set of internal features rather than broadly affecting the entire activation space.
2. **Local Linearity of Dynamics:** WAMs were found to exhibit local linearity, an important property previously observed in LLM and T2V models. This was demonstrated by high cosine similarity and near-unity magnitude ratios between the actual latent activation and its linear approximation (using `ẽA_l,t`) under random perturbations. This finding justifies the use of linear optimal control techniques like LQR within the WAM context.
3. **Subspace Overlap:** The `ẽA_l,t` matrices, representing the linearized dynamics, showed significant overlap across different inputs and tasks. This suggests that the identified control mechanisms might be transferable, enabling the reuse of `ẽA_l,t` matrices computed from a single input to steer model behavior on other tasks.

**Steering to Improve Robustness (Section 6.2):**

1. **Predictive Power of Mechanistic Analysis:** The mechanistic analysis's prediction of steerability, based on low-dimensional linear separability (quantified by hinge loss), correlated with empirical intervention outcomes. A negative correlation was observed between hinge loss and robustness improvements through steering, indicating that models or tasks with clearer linear separation of features were more amenable to steering.
2. **Cosmos-Policy Performance:**
 - **WA-LQR Effectiveness:** On Cosmos-Policy 2B, WA-LQR demonstrated the most effective improvements in robustness against camera orientation and initial gripper position perturbations. It significantly increased success rates, in some cases by up to 41% (e.g., from 3.3% to 43.3% or 33.3% to 73.3% for Gaussian noise, from 56.7% to 83.3% for camera orientation for Task 0->Task 5, and from 83.3% to 100.0% for gripper position for Task 1->Task 2).
 - **ActAdd Performance:** Open-loop Activation Addition (ActAdd) also generally outperformed unsteered and prompt-steering baselines. Notably, ActAdd sometimes surpassed WA-LQR in effectiveness for perturbations with strong separability, such as camera Gaussian noise corruption.
 - **Sensitivity of ActAdd:** ActAdd's performance was highly sensitive to the scaling parameter `γ`, with optimal performance occurring within a narrow range and quickly degrading with smaller or larger values. WA-LQR, due to its closed-loop, adaptive nature, mitigated this sensitivity and avoided oversteering.
 - **Prompt Steering:** Prompt steering baselines were consistently ineffective, either matching unsteered performance or degrading it.
3. **DiT4DiT Performance:** On DiT4DiT, ActAdd provided substantial improvements on Gaussian noise but showed limited impact on initial gripper position. WA-LQR, however, outperformed both ActAdd and other baselines on both Gaussian noise and initial gripper position perturbations.
4. **LingBot-VA Performance:** Consistent with the mechanistic analysis, which showed weak linear separability (high SVM loss) for LingBot-VA activations, the steering interventions yielded minimal or no major robustness improvements. ActAdd often degraded performance due to oversteering, while WA-LQR, by adaptively modulating steering magnitude, managed to avoid degradation but still did not achieve substantial improvements. This highlights the architecture-dependent nature of steerability.

Overall, the findings indicate that mechanistic interpretability can diagnose WAM robustness issues and guide inference-time interventions. Both open-loop and closed-loop activation steering can effectively improve success rates for models exhibiting low separability loss, with closed-loop WA-LQR offering greater adaptive control and avoiding the pitfalls of fixed-magnitude interventions.

### 5. Significance and potential impact

This research makes several contributions to the fields of robot learning, mechanistic interpretability, and control theory, with notable significance and potential impact.

**Diagnostic Tool for WAM Robustness:** The study demonstrates that mechanistic interpretability can serve as an effective diagnostic tool to analyze how robustness-relevant perturbations are represented within WAM activation spaces. By evaluating low-dimensional linear separability through metrics like SVM hinge loss, the research provides a method to predict whether a particular WAM architecture or task is amenable to activation steering for robustness improvement. This diagnostic capability can guide model selection or provide insights into model design, focusing efforts on architectures that exhibit steerable internal representations.

**Novel Activation Steering Framework for WAMs:** The introduction of World-Action Linear Quadratic Regulator (WA-LQR) represents a significant advancement. It is, to the best of the authors' knowledge, one of the first closed-loop activation steering methods specifically designed for WAMs. By combining mechanistic insights into latent feature spaces with principles of optimal control, WA-LQR offers an adaptive, minimally-invasive approach to steering. Unlike open-loop methods, WA-LQR dynamically adjusts interventions based on the WAM's current activation state and deviation from desired feature strengths, which helps prevent oversteering and potential performance degradation. The adaptation of open-loop activation addition (ActAdd) to WAMs also provides a simpler baseline.

**Improved Out-of-Distribution Robustness:** The experimental results demonstrate that these training-free activation steering methods, particularly WA-LQR, can substantially improve the robustness of WAMs to critical out-of-distribution shifts. Success rates increased by up to 41% across various perturbation types (camera orientation, gripper position, Gaussian noise) on models like Cosmos-Policy and DiT4DiT. This is significant because it offers a practical, inference-time solution to a major deployment obstacle for robotic policies, avoiding the need for costly and time-consuming data augmentation or retraining.

**Broader Implications for Model Design:** The findings highlight that steerability is architecture-dependent, with models like Cosmos-Policy and DiT4DiT exhibiting clear linear structure for certain features, while LingBot-VA did not. This observation has implications for the design of future robotic foundation models, suggesting a need to understand and potentially engineer architectures that inherently promote steerable and interpretable representations. Such designs could lead to models that are not only performant but also more robust and easier to debug and control in diverse real-world scenarios.

**Limitations and Future Directions:** Acknowledged limitations include the current inability to mechanistically predict when representations are transferable across tasks or environments, requiring per-setting analysis. The strong dependence on model architecture also suggests that steerability is not universal. Future work is encouraged to develop more generalizable methods for predicting transferable representations and to explore WAM architectures explicitly designed to be steerable and to preserve useful representations from their base foundation models. This line of research could lead to more robust, transparent, and controllable AI systems for embodied agents.
