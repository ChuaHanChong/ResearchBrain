# Neural Robot Dynamics

> [!summary] Summary
> NVIDIA researchers developed Neural Robot Dynamics (NeRD), a generalizable neural simulator for articulated rigid-body robots, using a hybrid prediction framework and a robot-centric state representation. NeRD learns to predict robot dynamics, generalizing across tasks, environments, and controllers, and can be fine-tuned with real-world data to bridge the sim-to-real gap. Policies trained in NeRD transferred with comparable performance to ground-truth simulators, and a Franka robot policy achieved a 1.927 mm end-effector error on a real robot.

## Problem

- Traditional analytical robot simulators struggle with computational efficiency and modeling complex interactions for high degrees of freedom and intricate contact dynamics.
- Prior neural robotics simulators lack generalizability, often requiring application-specific training and failing to adapt to novel tasks, environments, or customized controllers due to inadequate state representation.
- A persistent challenge in robotics is bridging the simulation-to-reality gap, where policies learned in simulation often do not transfer effectively to physical robots.

## Method

- **NeRD** introduces a ==hybrid prediction framework== that selectively replaces only the low-level, application-agnostic forward dynamics and contact solvers of a classical simulator.
- The framework utilizes a ==robot-centric, spatially-invariant state representation== by transforming robot state, contact information, and joint torques into the robot's base frame, augmented with gravity.
- A ==causal Transformer== architecture is employed to predict relative state differences over a history window, trained on task-agnostic data from analytical simulations.

## Results

- NeRD demonstrated stable and accurate long-horizon predictions for diverse robots (e.g., Ant for **500 steps**, Cartpole for **1000 steps**) with low error margins.
- Policies trained entirely within NeRD simulators achieved high performance and transferred zero-shot to ground-truth analytical simulators with remarkably similar average rewards (typically **<5% error** across tasks).
- The model exhibited effective sim-to-real transfer, with a Franka policy trained in NeRD achieving a low steady-state end-effector error of **1.927 mm** on a real robot, and significantly outperformed analytical baselines when fine-tuned on real-world data for cube tossing.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Modularly replacing only core dynamics components allows leveraging generalizable intermediate quantities (like contact information and joint torques), enhancing model adaptability.
> - Enforcing spatial invariance through a robot-centric state representation reduces the effective state space and improves generalizability and data efficiency by eliminating the need to sample all global positions/orientations.
> - Predicting relative state changes and applying thorough input/output normalization are critical for maintaining long-horizon stability and accuracy in neural dynamics prediction.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Simulation is a cornerstone of modern robotics, facilitating policy learning, control evaluation, and design optimization. Traditionally, analytical simulators, built upon classical physics equations, have been the standard. However, these face challenges with high degrees of freedom, intricate contact dynamics, and computational efficiency for complex robots or large-scale data generation.

In recent years, neural simulators have emerged as a promising alternative. These data-driven models can efficiently predict robot dynamics and learn complex physics from real-world data, often outperforming analytical models in capturing hard-to-model interactions (e.g., compliant contact, soft bodies). Existing neural simulators, often integrated into model-based reinforcement learning (RL) or planning frameworks, typically operate in an end-to-end fashion. They substitute the entire classical simulator, directly mapping robot states and control actions to the next state.

However, a critical limitation of most prior neural robotics simulators is their lack of *generalizability*. They often require application-specific training, making implicit assumptions about fixed environments or specific controllers. This stems from their inadequate representation of the global simulation state, forcing them to "memorize" task and environment details rather than truly understanding general dynamics. Consequently, they struggle to adapt to novel tasks, unseen environments, customized controllers, or out-of-distribution state trajectories—challenges that classical simulators handle with relative ease due to their explicit physics models.

"Neural Robot Dynamics" (NeRD) directly addresses this generalizability gap for articulated rigid-body robots. It carves out a unique niche by proposing a *hybrid prediction framework* that selectively replaces only the application-agnostic components (low-level forward dynamics and contact solvers) of a traditional simulator. By leveraging intermediate simulation quantities (robot state, contact information, joint torques) and a novel *robot-centric, spatially-invariant state representation*, NeRD aims to achieve the best of both worlds: the efficiency and adaptability of neural networks combined with the generalizable structure of analytical physics. This positions NeRD as a foundational step towards robust, adaptable, and lifelong learning for robots in dynamic, real-world scenarios, setting it apart from end-to-end black-box neural world models.

The work also relates to:
- **Neural physics engines for single rigid bodies:** Previous works have modeled object-ground and inter-object interactions using neural networks (e.g., ContactNets, GNNs). NeRD extends this to articulated rigid bodies.
- **World models in model-based RL/planning:** Many model-based RL methods predict future states for policy learning, but often lack generalizability due to joint training with policies and limited state representations. NeRD decouples model training and emphasizes generalizability.
- **Hybrid neural simulation frameworks:** Some works integrate neural models for specific components (e.g., friction, passive forces, contact detection). NeRD integrates neural models for the core dynamics and contact solving, providing a more comprehensive replacement of the physics engine core.
- **Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs):** While PINNs incorporate physics laws, they often require expert-modeled equations for each system, limiting their application to complex, novel robot designs, whereas NeRD learns from data.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this work is to develop *generalizable neural simulators for articulated rigid-body robots*. The authors envision a future where every robot possesses a neural simulator, pre-trained from analytical simulations, capable of continuous fine-tuning from real-world data. This "digital twin" would accommodate wear-and-tear, environmental changes, and facilitate versatile skill learning throughout the robot's operational lifespan.

To achieve this goal, NeRD is specifically motivated by two key innovations:
1. **Hybrid Prediction Framework:** The motivation here is to overcome the generalization limitations of end-to-end neural simulators. Instead of replacing the entire classical simulator, NeRD selectively replaces only the low-level, application-agnostic forward dynamics and contact solvers. This allows NeRD to reuse intermediate, generalizable simulation quantities (like contact information and joint torques) that comprehensively describe the robot's interaction with the environment, regardless of the specific task, scene, or controller.
2. **Robot-Centric State Representation:** This innovation is driven by the desire to explicitly enforce spatial invariance in the learned dynamics. By transforming the simulation state (robot state and contact quantities) into the robot's body frame and augmenting it with the gravity vector, NeRD inherently learns dynamics that are invariant to global translation and rotation around the gravity axis. This not only enhances spatial generalizability but also significantly improves training and data efficiency by reducing the necessary state space exploration during model training.

The core motivation is to create neural simulators that are stable and accurate over long horizons, generalize across diverse tasks, environments, and controllers, enable policy learning purely within the neural engine, and, crucially, can be effectively fine-tuned with real-world data to bridge the simulation-to-reality gap—a major hurdle in contemporary robotics.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

NeRD (Neural Robot Dynamics) proposes a novel methodology for learning robot-specific dynamics models for articulated rigid bodies, characterized by a hybrid prediction framework and a robot-centric state representation.

#### 3.1. Preliminary: A Typical Robotics Simulation Workflow

The paper first outlines the standard workflow of a classical robotics simulator. At each timestep, the simulator takes a robot model, current robot state, action command, and scene configuration. It then performs collision detection to get contact information, executes a low-level controller to convert actions into joint torques, and passes these (along with robot state) to dynamics and contact solvers. Finally, time integration calculates the next robot state. Prior neural simulators often adopted an end-to-end approach, directly mapping `(state_t, action_t) -> state_(t+1)`, which memorizes scene and controller specifics, leading to poor generalization.

#### 3.2. Hybrid Prediction Framework

NeRD's core innovation is its hybrid prediction framework. Instead of replacing the entire simulator, NeRD uniquely replaces only the application-agnostic components: the low-level forward dynamics and contact solvers. This modular design allows NeRD to leverage intermediate simulation quantities—the robot state ($s_t$), contact information ($C_t$), and joint-space torques ($\tau_t$)—as a comprehensive, compact, and generalizable representation of the full simulation state.
- **Robot State ($s_t$):** Includes base position/orientation, joint angles, base spatial twist (6D velocity), and joint velocities.
- **Contact Information ($C_t$):** Reuses the collision detection module from the classical simulator. For pre-specified contact points on the robot, it generates contact event quantities (contact point on non-robot shape, normal, distance). Non-contact events are masked out.
- **Joint Torques ($\tau_t$):** Provided by the low-level controller, converting high-level action commands.

NeRD is trained as a parametric function, `NeRD_θ({s_k, C_k, τ_k}_(k=t-h+1 to t))`, that maps a history window (length `h`) of these quantities to the *relative state difference* $\Delta s_{t+1} = s_{t+1} \ominus s_t$. This relative prediction reduces the magnitude of the output values, stabilizing training. The model is trained by minimizing the mean squared error between predicted and ground-truth state differences. The next state $s_{t+1}$ is then computed by adding this predicted difference to the current state $s_t$.

#### 3.3. Robot-Centric State Representation

To further enhance generalizability and training efficiency, NeRD introduces a robot-centric state representation. The dynamics of a robot are inherently invariant under spatial translation and rotation around the gravity axis, assuming local interactions remain unchanged. NeRD exploits this by transforming the robot state ($s_t$) and contact-related quantities ($C_t$) into the robot's *base frame* ($B_t$).
- The robot's articulation (joint angles/velocities) is inherently spatially invariant.
- The base state (position, orientation, velocity) is transformed to be relative to its own base frame, effectively setting its position to zero and orientation to identity.
- Gravity is treated as an external force and expressed in the robot's base frame.
- The predicted state difference $\Delta s_{t+1}$ is also expressed in the *current* robot's base frame $B_t$. This ensures that the model learns how the robot's state *changes relative to itself* and its local environment, rather than absolute changes in a global frame. This implicitly enforces spatial invariance.

This representation reduces the effective state space and eliminates the need to exhaustively sample all spatial positions and orientations during training, significantly boosting spatial generalizability and data efficiency.

#### 3.4. Implementation and Training Details

- **Integration:** NeRD is implemented as an interchangeable backend solver within NVIDIA's high-performance Warp simulator, allowing seamless integration with existing robotics environments. Collision detection is GPU-parallelized, adapted from Warp.
- **Training Datasets:** Generated in a task-agnostic manner using Warp with the Featherstone solver. For each robot, 100K random trajectories (100 timesteps each) are collected, using randomized initial states, random joint torques, and optionally randomized environment configurations.
- **Network Architecture:** A causal Transformer architecture (lightweight GPT-2 implementation) is used, treating the history window as a sequence.
- **Hyperparameters:** A fixed set of hyperparameters (e.g., history window `h=10`, batch size 512, learning rate) is used across all six robotic systems, with only the embedding size varying based on robot DoF.
- **Training Process:** Uses teacher-forcing. Crucially, both *input and output normalization* are applied. Output normalization (using mean and std dev from dataset) prevents high-variance velocity terms from dominating the loss, enhancing accuracy and long-horizon stability.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of NeRD is demonstrated through extensive experiments across six diverse robotic systems (Cartpole, Double Pendulum, Ant, Franka, ANYmal, Cube Toss) in various scenarios.

#### 4.1. Long-Horizon Stability and Accuracy

- **Cartpole:** For a contact-free 2-DoF system, NeRD maintains high accuracy over 1000 simulation steps (16.67 seconds of passive motion), with revolute joint errors below 5 degrees and prismatic joint errors of 0.033m.
- **Ant:** For a complex 14-DoF floating-base robot with contacts, NeRD achieves accurate predictions over 500 steps, showing average base angular errors of 0.095 rad and positional errors of 0.057 m. These results confirm NeRD's capability for stable and accurate long-horizon dynamics prediction for diverse robotic systems.

#### 4.2. Contact Generalizability

- **Double Pendulum:** Evaluated on seven different contact configurations (one contact-free, six with varying planar ground setups). A single NeRD model generalized effectively across all scenarios, with maximum mean joint error of 0.056 rad (3.2 degrees) after 100 steps, proving its ability to handle diverse contact modes.

#### 4.3. Task, Controller, and Spatial Generalizability (RL Policy Learning)

- NeRD was used as the sole simulator to train PPO policies for diverse tasks (Cartpole swing-up, Franka reach, Ant running/spinning/spin tracking, ANYmal forward/sideways velocity tracking). These tasks explored specialized robot state distributions, custom low-level controllers (joint-torque vs. joint-position control), and extended spatial regions unseen during NeRD's training.
- Policies trained entirely within NeRD simulators achieved high performance. Crucially, when these policies were deployed *zero-shot* in the ground-truth analytical simulator, they yielded remarkably similar average rewards, with errors typically below 5% (e.g., Cartpole +0.01%, Franka +2.02%, Ant running +4.25%, ANYmal -0.02% to -0.07%). This strongly validates NeRD's generalizability across tasks, controllers, and spatial regions, as well as its long-horizon predictive accuracy.

#### 4.4. Sim-to-Real Transfer

- A Franka reach policy trained exclusively in the NeRD simulator was deployed zero-shot to a real Franka robot. It achieved a low steady-state end-effector error of 1.927 ± 0.699 mm, which was comparable to or even better than a policy trained in the ground-truth simulator (4.647 ± 2.667 mm). This critical finding demonstrates NeRD's accuracy and fidelity to real-world physics, effectively bridging the sim-to-real gap.

#### 4.5. Fine-tunability on Real-World Data

- **Cube Tossing:** A NeRD model pretrained on synthetic cube-tossing data was fine-tuned using a real-world cube-tossing dataset.
- The fine-tuned NeRD model significantly outperformed the base Warp simulator (position error: 0.018m vs. 0.036m; orientation error: 0.266 rad vs. 0.383 rad), showing its ability to adapt to real-world dynamics.
- Fine-tuning was highly efficient, converging 10 times faster (fewer than five epochs) than training a NeRD model from scratch on only the real data.
- NeRD achieved comparable or better performance to specialized baselines like ContactNets and GNN-Rigid for this specific dataset, while being a general-purpose model applicable to articulated bodies and fine-tuning much faster (minutes vs. hours).

#### 4.6. Ablation Studies (from Appendix C.5)

- **Network Architecture:** Transformer architecture was shown to be superior to MLP, GRU, and LSTM for modeling neural robot dynamics, especially for achieving ground-truth-comparable rewards in policy evaluation tasks.
- **Hybrid Prediction Framework:** The hybrid approach significantly outperformed an end-to-end (E2E) baseline, which showed large prediction errors due to its inability to generalize across varying contact configurations and out-of-distribution spatial regions.
- **Relative Robot State Prediction:** Predicting relative state changes was critical for training stability and accuracy, showing a 26x improvement over predicting absolute states for the Double Pendulum.
- **Robot-Centric State Representation:** This representation was crucial for spatial generalizability, particularly for robots like Ant that move extensively. The world-frame representation failed entirely when the robot moved outside the training data distribution.
- **Model Input and Output Normalization:** Both input and output normalizations were essential for stabilizing training and improving accuracy, especially for handling high-variance velocity terms.
- **History Window Size (h):** Increasing `h` up to 10 generally improved performance, confirming the benefit of sequential modeling.

#### 4.7. Computational Efficiency (from Appendix C.6)

- NeRD demonstrated higher frames per second (46K FPS) compared to the Warp analytical simulator (28K FPS) when simulating 512 parallel Ant environments. This highlights NeRD's efficiency for large-scale robot learning tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

NeRD marks a significant advancement in the field of neural robotics simulation, offering a compelling blend of the generalizability of analytical physics with the efficiency and adaptability of neural networks.

**Current Significance:**
- **Bridging the Sim-to-Real Gap:** The ability to fine-tune NeRD models from real-world data and demonstrate zero-shot sim-to-real transfer for a robot arm is a major breakthrough. This capability directly tackles one of the most persistent challenges in robotics, making learned policies more applicable in physical systems.
- **Enhanced Generalization:** NeRD's hybrid framework and robot-centric state representation overcome critical limitations of prior neural simulators, enabling generalization across diverse tasks, environment configurations, and low-level controllers. This means a single learned model can support a wider range of applications without re-training.
- **Efficient Policy Learning:** By providing a stable, accurate, and fast neural dynamics engine, NeRD facilitates efficient large-scale reinforcement learning. Policies learned entirely within the NeRD simulator can perform effectively in ground-truth analytical simulators, reducing the reliance on computationally expensive classical physics engines during training.
- **Modular and Interoperable Design:** NeRD's design as an interchangeable backend solver within a state-of-the-art simulator (NVIDIA Warp) makes it highly practical. Users can easily switch to NeRD, reusing existing policy-learning environments.

**Potential Impact and Future Directions:**
- **Lifelong Learning and Digital Twins:** NeRD lays the groundwork for creating adaptable digital twins that can continuously update their dynamics models based on real-world interactions, accounting for wear-and-tear or environmental changes. This could enable robots to maintain high performance over their operational lifetime.
- **Simulation of Complex Robots:** The methodology could be extended to even more complex robotic systems, such as humanoid robots with 20-50 degrees of freedom, where analytical modeling is exceptionally challenging. NeRD's efficiency and data-driven nature could offer a viable path for simulating these systems accurately.
- **Improved Data Sampling:** Currently, NeRD relies on random sampling for training data. Future work could explore more effective, state-aware data sampling strategies, particularly for high-dimensional and complex robots, to improve training efficiency without sacrificing task-agnostic generalizability.
- **Partial Observability:** Acknowledging the limitation of assuming full state observability for fine-tuning, future research could focus on methods for fine-tuning NeRD from partially observable real-world data, which is more representative of typical robot sensing capabilities. This would further enhance its practical applicability.

In conclusion, NeRD presents a robust and highly impactful approach to neural robot dynamics. By strategically integrating neural models within the structured framework of classical physics, it pushes the boundaries of neural simulation towards truly generalizable and practical tools for robotics research and development.
