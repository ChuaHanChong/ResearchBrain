# VIA: Visual Interface Agent for Robot Control

> [!summary] Summary
> Stanford University researchers introduce VIA (Visual Interface Agent), a framework enabling off-the-shelf visual agents to control robots for manipulation tasks by perceiving a browser-based 3D interface via screenshots and issuing commands through low-level kinematic tools. This approach achieves zero-shot success rates up to 88% on diverse tasks, including complex long-horizon assembly, and shows performance improvements with stronger foundation models and textual demonstrations.

## Problem

- Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, when fine-tuned for robot control, are often orders of magnitude smaller than frontier foundation models, limiting their general capabilities in physical reasoning and long-horizon planning.
- Code-as-Policies (CaP) approaches rely on human-crafted perception APIs and skill primitives, creating abstraction bottlenecks and preventing the foundation model from directly perceiving the scene or performing low-level control.
- Effectively translating the strong visual understanding, reasoning, and long-term planning abilities of general-purpose foundation models (FMs) to robust robot control remains a challenge for existing methodologies.

## Method

- **VIA** (Visual Interface Agent) reframes robot control as a visual tool-use task for off-the-shelf, frontier foundation models (FMs), bypassing robot-specific fine-tuning.
- The system utilizes a custom ==browser-based 3D user interface== that renders point cloud scenes and camera feeds, allowing the FM agent to perceive the environment solely through screenshots.
- A small set of human-legible ==Model Context Protocol (MCP)== tools provides direct kinematic control over the robot's end-effector (6-DoF poses, gripper state) and camera manipulation, enabling an ==observe-act loop==.

## Results

- VIA achieved zero-shot average success rates ranging from **60%** to **88%** across six diverse tabletop manipulation tasks using frontier agents like Claude Code and Codex, including **100%** success on the complex "Rainbow" long-horizon assembly task for CC-Fable.
- Providing textual demonstrations (waypoint lists) significantly improved performance, boosting CC-Opus's success rate on LIBERO-Goal tasks from **77%** to **100%**.
- While effective, the approach incurred estimated API costs of **$4.1** to **$15.1** per successful episode, and the most challenging precision-demanding task, "T-block," still had success rates between **10%** and **40%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - General-purpose visual agents, without robot-specific fine-tuning, can directly leverage their visual perception, reasoning, and tool-use skills to control physical robots through a simple visual interface.
> - By operating at the level of raw visual input (screenshots) and low-level kinematic commands, the framework minimizes reliance on human-crafted perception or skill abstractions, deferring complex reasoning to the foundation model.
> - The performance of robot control through this agentic interface scales positively with the capabilities and scale of the underlying foundation model, demonstrating direct transfer of advancements in general AI.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot manipulation presents a complex challenge, necessitating capabilities in visual understanding, physical reasoning, long-term planning, and precise closed-loop control. Recent advancements in general-purpose foundation models (FMs), which are trained on vast internet-scale datasets, have demonstrated proficiency in areas such as visual perception and reasoning. This has spurred efforts to integrate these powerful models into robotic control systems.

Two primary approaches have emerged in this landscape. The first involves converting existing vision-language models into **Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models**. This typically entails fine-tuning FMs on robot-specific data to directly output low-level actions. However, a significant limitation of this approach is the discrepancy in scale: due to constraints in available robot data and computational resources for fine-tuning, VLAs are often orders of magnitude smaller than frontier FMs. This reduction in scale can limit their general capabilities, particularly in complex areas like physical reasoning and long-horizon planning, which are often lacking in current robot policies.

The second paradigm is **Code-as-Policies (CaP)**. In this approach, FMs are prompted to generate programs that interface with pre-defined perception APIs (e.g., object detectors) and hand-crafted skill primitives (e.g., grasp_pose). While CaP leverages the coding abilities of FMs and can offer better generalization by avoiding task-specific training, it is often bottlenecked by these human-crafted abstractions. The FM itself does not directly perceive the scene, and the system's success can be highly sensitive to the design and quality of these primitives. Studies have indicated that performance can degrade significantly when high-level primitives are replaced with more low-level ones.

This paper introduces VIA (Visual Interface Agent), which positions itself as an alternative to both VLA and CaP by drawing inspiration from the rapidly developing field of **computer-use agents**. These agents, often powered by frontier FMs, are designed to operate general software through visual interfaces using generic tools like taking screenshots, clicking, and typing. VIA explores whether this established competence in virtual environment operation can be directly transferred to robot control.

VIA's contribution to the research landscape is its proposal to reframe robot control as a visual tool-use task for off-the-shelf, FM-powered agents. Unlike VLAs, VIA utilizes frontier FMs without robot-specific fine-tuning, thereby aiming to leverage their full general capabilities. In contrast to CaP, VIA's agent directly perceives the visual scene via screenshots and interacts through a small set of human-legible, low-level Model Context Protocol (MCP) tools that provide kinematic control, rather than relying on abstract, pre-defined perception or skill primitives. This approach seeks to directly harness the robust perception, reasoning, and closed-loop control abilities of advanced FMs, allowing progress in general computer-use agents to directly translate to improvements in robot manipulation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching goal driving this research is the development of generalist robot policies capable of executing complex manipulation tasks that demand sophisticated visual understanding, physical reasoning, long-term planning, and precise closed-loop control. The authors observe that while general-purpose foundation models (FMs) have demonstrated significant capabilities in perception and reasoning, effectively translating these to robot control remains a challenge for existing methodologies.

The key motivations and objectives for developing VIA stem from identified limitations in current approaches:

1. **Addressing the Scale and Capability Gap in VLA Models:** Traditional Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models attempt to adapt FMs for robot control by fine-tuning them on robot data to output low-level actions. However, the scarcity of robot-specific data and the computational expense of fine-tuning often necessitate using models that are orders of magnitude smaller than frontier FMs. This compromises their general capabilities, particularly in areas like physical reasoning and long-horizon planning, which are critical for complex manipulation tasks. A primary motivation for VIA is to bypass this limitation by directly leveraging the full scale and general intelligence of frontier FMs without robot-specific fine-tuning, thus preserving their inherent reasoning and multimodal understanding.

2. **Overcoming Abstraction Bottlenecks in Code-as-Policies (CaP):** Code-as-Policies approaches enable FMs to generate code that calls pre-defined perception APIs and hand-crafted skill primitives. While effective for tapping into FMs' coding abilities, this paradigm suffers from several drawbacks. The FM typically does not directly perceive the scene; instead, it operates through abstract representations provided by the APIs. This dependency on human-crafted abstractions can become a bottleneck, as the system's success is highly sensitive to the quality and design of these primitives. VIA is motivated to remove this bottleneck by allowing the agent to directly perceive raw visual input and operate through a minimal set of generic, low-level kinematic tools, deferring perception and reasoning directly to the FM.

3. **Exploring Transferability of Agentic Competence:** A central question driving this work is whether the emerging competence of FMs to operate general software through visual interfaces and generic tools can be directly applied to robot control. The observed ability of frontier agents to perform long-horizon tasks in a closed-loop manner—iteratively observing, planning, and executing, and recovering from errors—bears a striking resemblance to the desired characteristics of robot policies. The authors hypothesize that with the right visual interface and human-legible tools, state-of-the-art agents could control a robot without any robot-specific adaptation.

Based on these motivations, the specific objectives of VIA include:

- **Framework Development:** To design and implement VIA, a framework that recasts robot control as an agentic task where an off-the-shelf FM-powered agent drives a manipulator through a browser-based 3D interface.
- **Interface Design:** To create a browser-based 3D interface that renders a point-cloud scene from RGB-D cameras, along with raw camera feeds, allowing the agent to perceive the environment solely through screenshots, without access to privileged state information.
- **Tool Creation:** To develop a small, human-legible set of Model Context Protocol (MCP) tools that enable intuitive control of the robot's end-effector (e.g., setting 6-DoF target poses, gripper state) and camera manipulation.
- **Zero-Shot Evaluation:** To evaluate VIA's capability to solve a diverse suite of tabletop manipulation tasks in a zero-shot manner, using popular frontier agents (Claude Code and Codex) without any robot-specific fine-tuning.
- **Performance Analysis:** To assess how VIA's performance scales with the strength and scale of the underlying foundation model and to investigate the impact of providing text-based demonstrations (waypoint lists) on task success.
- **Efficiency and Cost Assessment:** To quantify the efficiency of tool calls and the estimated API costs associated with successful task execution using different agent-model configurations.

Ultimately, VIA aims to demonstrate that robot control can be framed as another general agentic task, directly benefiting from advancements in frontier FMs and their ability to interact with visual interfaces.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

VIA (Visual Interface Agent) conceptualizes robot control as an agentic observe-act loop operating on a visual interface, mirroring how a human might interact with 3D design software. The core methodology involves an off-the-shelf agent perceiving a browser-based 3D interface via screenshots, reasoning about the visual information, issuing commands through a set of Model Context Protocol (MCP) tools, observing the outcome, and iteratively adjusting until the task is complete.

#### 3.1. Visual Interface

The central component of VIA is a custom-built, browser-based 3D robot-control user interface (UI). This UI is designed to present all necessary information and control mechanisms in a human-legible format, which the agent then interprets through screenshots.

- **Scene Representation:** The UI constructs a 3D point cloud of the scene using data from multiple calibrated RGB-D cameras. This point cloud serves as the main interactive workspace.
- **Camera Feeds:** In addition to the 3D point cloud, the UI displays two raw camera feeds: a third-person view for overall scene understanding and a wrist camera view for close-up analysis during fine manipulation.
- **Agent Perception:** Crucially, the agent's perception is entirely mediated by screenshots of this entire UI. It does not have access to privileged simulator state information, raw point cloud vectors, or any perception-assisting APIs (e.g., segmentation functions). Visual understanding and reasoning are solely performed by the agent's underlying foundation model.
- **Robot Control Metaphor:** Control is executed in a "waypoint-style" manner. Users (or the agent) manipulate a virtual blue "target gripper" mesh within the 3D scene to define a desired 6-DoF end-effector pose and a binary gripper open/close state. This is analogous to posing an object in 3D design software.
- **Execution:** Once the agent is satisfied with the target gripper's pose (waypoint), it invokes a simple PI (proportional-integral) controller via an MCP tool. This controller moves the real robot's gripper to the specified target pose using linear interpolation while adhering to physical constraints.

#### 3.2. Model Context Protocol (MCP) Tools

To enable agent interaction with the UI, the core human operations are wrapped into a small, defined set of MCP tools. These tools are categorized as follows:

- **Observation Tools:**
 - `screenshot()`: Captures an image of the entire UI.
 - `hover(u, v)`: Moves a crosshair to specified normalized pixel coordinates, returning a screenshot with the overlay and the 3D coordinates of the hit point in the point cloud. Used for verification and probing.
 - `gripper_get_pose()`: Returns the target gripper's pose (position, orientation, open/closed state) and visually indicates its approach axis.
 - `gripper_show_rotation_gizmo()`: Displays a rotation gizmo on the target gripper to aid in understanding rotation axes.
 - `camera_get_pose()`: Returns current virtual camera parameters (target, distance, azimuth, elevation).
- **Action Tools (Manipulate Target Gripper):** These tools modify the virtual target gripper's pose on the UI and return an updated screenshot, allowing the agent to observe and iteratively refine. They do not affect the real robot until `execute_waypoint()` is called.
 - `gripper_teleport_via_click(u, v)`: Teleports the target gripper near a clicked point in the UI.
 - `gripper_drag(u1, v1, u2, v2, [constraint], [steps])`: Drags the target gripper across the view plane, optionally constrained to robot-frame X, Y, or Z axes.
 - `gripper_translate([d_x], [d_y], [d_z])`: Translates the target gripper by exact metric offsets along robot-frame axes.
 - `gripper_advance_or_retreat(direction, [steps])`: Moves the target gripper along its approach axis.
 - `gripper_rotate(axis, angle)`: Rotates the target gripper about one of its local gizmo axes by a specified angle.
 - `gripper_toggle([state])`: Opens or closes the target gripper.
 - `gripper_reset()`: Resets the target gripper to the real robot's current pose.
- **Camera Tools:**
 - `camera_orbit_via_key(d_azimuth, d_elevation)`: Orbits the virtual camera by angular deltas.
 - `camera_pan_via_key(d_right, d_up)`: Pans the camera's look-at point by metric offsets.
 - `camera_zoom(u, v, [steps])`: Zooms the camera towards specified screen coordinates.
 - `camera_reset()`: Restores the virtual camera to its initial view.
- **Execution Tools:**
 - `execute_waypoint()`: Commands the real robot to move to the current target gripper pose.
 - `end_episode()`: Terminates the current task episode.

The tools adhere to two principles: **minimalism**, wrapping human operations with minimal abstraction, and **agent ergonomics**, adapting continuous human interactions (like dragging) into discrete, explicit agent commands (like `gripper_rotate` with exact angles) suitable for discrete screenshot-based observation.

#### 3.3. Agentic Loop

VIA drives the system using general-purpose, off-the-shelf agents, specifically Claude Code (CC) and Codex, equipped with their latest underlying models (e.g., CC-Opus, CC-Fable, Codex-5.5, Codex-5.6-Sol). These agents are chosen for their general visual understanding, reasoning, and tool-use capabilities, not for their code-writing functions.

- **Loop Structure:** The agent operates in a continuous cycle of observation, thinking, tool invocation, and observation of the returned (updated) screenshot. This closed-loop structure allows for real-time error detection and re-planning.
- **Prompting:** Agents receive a short system prompt outlining the interface basics, guiding principles (e.g., task decomposition, closed-loop verification, common failure modes), and the concept of waypoint sequences. Task-specific prompts are provided in two variants: a **minimal prompt** stating only the goal for zero-shot evaluation, and a **detailed prompt** that includes an example list of waypoints, analogous to a textual demonstration.
- **Memory Management:** The agents are largely delegated context and history management, leveraging their inherent long-horizon planning capabilities. For evaluation, cross-episode memory read/write is typically disabled to test zero-shot performance, though this is noted as a potential future research direction for learning via reflection.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

- **Tasks:** Six diverse tabletop manipulation tasks implemented in the `robosuite` simulator: Stack, Turn on stove, Open drawer, Put bowl on plate (from LIBERO-Goal), Rainbow (custom long-horizon assembly), and T-block (from BuilderBench, requiring precision and physical reasoning). These tasks assess various skills including pick-and-place, articulated object manipulation, precision, and long-term planning.
- **Evaluation Protocol:** Each agent-model-prompt configuration is evaluated over 10 randomized seeds per task. Episodes are capped at one hour of wall-clock time. Success is determined by binary detectors for most tasks, with Rainbow judged visually.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrate VIA's capacity to enable off-the-shelf visual agents to perform diverse robot manipulation tasks without robot-specific fine-tuning.

#### 4.1. Diverse Manipulation Tasks Solved Zero-Shot

- With only a minimal prompt, VIA achieved average success rates ranging from 60% (Codex-5.5) to 88% (CC-Fable). This indicates that existing general agents can directly transfer their visual perception, reasoning, tool-use, and UI-operation skills to robot control in a zero-shot manner.
- Claude Code (CC) agents generally outperformed Codex agents. CC-Fable, in particular, demonstrated strong performance, effectively solving most tasks except for T-block.
- Success rates on LIBERO-Goal tasks such as "Turn on stove" (CC-Fable and CC-Opus both 100%) and "Open drawer" (CC-Fable 90%, CC-Opus 70%) indicate capabilities beyond simple pick-and-place, extending to articulated object manipulation.
- The "Rainbow" task, designed to test long-horizon planning, showed CC-Opus achieving 80% and CC-Fable achieving 100% success. The agents exhibited planning behavior, determining optimal block ordering and assembly direction based on initial configurations.
- The "T-block" task, requiring high precision (8mm error tolerance) and physical reasoning (rotating the base block), remained the most challenging, with success rates between 10% and 40% across agents. While agents identified the correct strategy (rotating the blue block by 45 degrees), achieving the necessary placement precision proved difficult.

#### 4.2. Improvement with Text Demonstrations

- When provided with detailed prompts containing example waypoint lists for the three LIBERO-Goal tasks, CC-Opus demonstrated a significant improvement, increasing its success rate from an average of 77% to 100%. This suggests that VIA facilitates a low-cost method for generating "demonstrations" from plain text or screenshots, potentially enabling learning via reflection.
- Codex-5.5, however, did not show a similar benefit from the detailed prompts in this experimental setup.

#### 4.3. Scaling with Model Size and Capabilities

- **Claude Code Family:** Upgrading from CC-Opus (4.8) to CC-Fable (5) resulted in improved success rates on all tasks with remaining headroom. The overall success rate for CC agents increased from 70% to 88%. CC-Fable achieved 29 out of 30 successes on LIBERO-Goal tasks with only the minimal prompt, nearly matching the perfect score CC-Opus achieved with detailed instructions. This suggests that as underlying models improve, less prompt engineering is required to achieve high performance.
- **GPT Family (Codex):** The upgrade from Codex-5.5 to Codex-5.6-Sol improved performance on the precision-demanding "Put bowl on plate" task, doubling the success rate from 40% to 80%. Results for other tasks were mixed, with "Rainbow" remaining largely unchanged (60% to 50%) and "T-block" seeing a drop (40% to 20%). This indicates that general improvements in foundation models can directly translate to enhanced robot control capabilities through VIA.

#### 4.4. Tool Call Efficiency and Estimated Costs

- **Tool Calls:** For Claude Code, the newer CC-Fable generally used comparable or fewer tool calls than CC-Opus for higher success rates, with exceptions like "Stack" (where CC-Fable deliberately used more calls for stable grasping) and "T-block" (due to a noisy comparison from low success). For Codex, the newer Codex-5.6-Sol tended to use more tool calls than Codex-5.5, despite similar success rates.
- **API Costs:** API costs were estimated for successful episodes. Codex models were generally more cost-effective. Codex-5.6-Sol averaged $4.1 per successful episode, and Codex-5.5 averaged $7.2. In contrast, CC-Opus averaged $9.5, and CC-Fable, being more expensive per token, averaged $15.1 per successful episode. Differences in agent design, such as Codex's more aggressive context compaction, contributed to cost variations.

In summary, VIA demonstrates that frontier agents, when provided with an appropriate visual interface and generic tools, possess inherent capabilities that enable them to control robots for diverse manipulation tasks zero-shot. Performance scales positively with the strength of the underlying foundation model, and textual demonstrations can further enhance success rates for certain agent configurations.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The VIA framework presents a novel approach to robot control with several significant implications for the field of robotics and artificial intelligence.

#### 5.1. New Paradigm for Robot Control

VIA introduces a paradigm that recasts robot control as a visual agentic task, similar to general computer operation. This departs from traditional methods by directly leveraging off-the-shelf, frontier foundation models (FMs) without requiring robot-specific fine-tuning or extensive domain adaptation. This approach suggests that the broad visual understanding, reasoning, and tool-use capabilities developed by FMs for general computer interaction can be directly applied to physical robot control.

#### 5.2. Direct Transfer of Foundation Model Capabilities

A key significance of VIA is its ability to allow advancements in general-purpose FMs to directly translate to improvements in robot control. By avoiding the fine-tuning process inherent in Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, which often limits the scale and generalizability of deployed robot policies, VIA enables robots to benefit from the full spectrum of capabilities of the largest and most advanced FMs. As FMs continue to evolve in perception, reasoning, context management, and tool use, these improvements are automatically inherited by VIA-powered robots.

#### 5.3. Reduced Reliance on Hand-Crafted Abstractions

Compared to Code-as-Policies (CaP) methods, which often depend on pre-defined libraries of perception and control primitives, VIA operates at a lower level of abstraction. The agent perceives raw visual input (screenshots of 3D point clouds and camera feeds) and commands the robot through generic kinematic tools. This minimizes the reliance on human-engineered abstractions, shifting the burden of visual understanding and complex reasoning primarily to the FM agent itself. This can lead to greater flexibility and potentially reduce the amount of task-specific engineering required.

#### 5.4. Demonstrated Generalizability and Robustness

The successful execution of diverse manipulation tasks, including pick-and-place, articulated object manipulation, and long-horizon multi-object assembly, in a zero-shot manner, highlights the generalizability of the VIA framework. The closed-loop observe-act cycle, coupled with the agent's inherent reasoning, enables error recovery and dynamic re-planning based on observed outcomes, contributing to robust task completion. The high success rates on tasks like the seven-block "Rainbow" assembly underscore its capacity for complex, multi-step operations.

#### 5.5. Potential for Low-Cost Learning and Policy Improvement

The finding that providing textual demonstrations (example waypoint lists) can significantly boost performance for some agents (e.g., CC-Opus) indicates a promising avenue for low-cost policy improvement. This allows for creating "demonstrations" from plain text or screenshots rather than requiring costly and labor-intensive teleoperation. It also opens up possibilities for applying advanced learning techniques like automatic prompt optimization or learning via reflection, where agents could iteratively improve their task-specific prompts or strategies based on past experiences, without needing to update model weights.

#### 5.6. Practical Applications and Future Directions

While currently limited by inference speed and cost to quasi-static tasks, VIA offers several practical applications:
- **Assembly tasks:** Where reliability and precision are prioritized over speed.
- **Reset mechanisms for RL:** Providing a robust method for resetting complex real-world environments for reinforcement learning.
- **Data collection:** Generating high-quality demonstrations for training faster, specialized policies, where a slow but reliable interface agent can create valuable datasets.

Future work includes extending VIA to real robots, which would validate its real-world applicability, and integrating more sophisticated controllers to enhance performance on precision-demanding tasks and improve collision avoidance. The framework's core idea, that general-purpose agents can directly control robots, aligns with the broader trend of foundational models permeating various domains, suggesting a future where advances in general AI capabilities directly contribute to increasingly capable robotic systems.
