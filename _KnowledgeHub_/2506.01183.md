# Doubly Robust Alignment for Large Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from LSE, Tsinghua, LCC, and Oxford developed Doubly Robust Preference Optimization (DRPO), a novel RLHF algorithm for large language models that maintains consistency if either the human preference model or the reference policy is correctly specified. DRPO consistently outperformed or matched state-of-the-art RLHF methods on summarization and dialogue tasks while demonstrating improved robustness to model misspecification.

## Problem

- Existing Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) algorithms are sensitive to various forms of model misspecification in aligning Large Language Models (LLMs) with human preferences.
- Common issues include the violation of assumptions in the Bradley-Terry preference model, errors in estimated reward functions leading to "reward hacking," and reliance on accurately specified reference policies in Direct Preference Optimization (DPO).
- Such misspecifications hinder accurate preference evaluation and can lead to suboptimal or misaligned policy learning in LLMs.

## Method

- Developed ==Doubly Robust== Preference Optimization (**DRPO**), a novel RLHF algorithm that adopts a preference-based approach using principles from doubly robust estimation methods.
- Proposed a doubly robust preference evaluation estimator that combines elements of Direct Method (DM) and Importance Sampling (IS) estimators, using residuals for bias correction.
- The optimization framework maximizes this doubly robust preference estimate, regularized by KL divergence from an estimated reference policy, to iteratively fine-tune LLMs.

## Results

- Theoretical analysis confirmed the double robustness and semi-parametric efficiency of the proposed preference evaluation estimator, demonstrating its consistency and optimal asymptotic variance.
- Empirical evaluation on synthetic IMDb data validated the double robustness, showing significantly reduced Mean Squared Error (MSE) when either the preference model or reference policy was correctly specified, compared to when both were misspecified.
- On real-world datasets like TL;DR and Anthropic HH, DRPO consistently outperformed or matched state-of-the-art RLHF methods (PPO, DPO, and its variants), achieving win rates such as **98.5%** against IPO on TL;DR, particularly under conditions of misspecified reference policies or noisy human preferences.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The proposed preference evaluation estimator exhibits "double robustness," ensuring consistency if either the preference model or the reference policy is correctly specified, not requiring both.
> - This estimator is also "semi-parametrically efficient," asymptotically achieving the lowest possible mean squared error among a class of estimators when underlying models are sufficiently accurate.
> - DRPO's theoretical suboptimality gap for the optimized policy depends on the *product* of reference policy and reward model estimation errors, making it more resilient to misspecification than PPO or DPO algorithms.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of large language models (LLMs) has witnessed rapid advancements, leading to transformative capabilities across various natural language processing tasks, including text generation and complex reasoning. These models typically undergo a two-stage training process. The initial pre-training stage involves learning general linguistic patterns and common-sense knowledge from extensive unlabeled text data through autoregressive next-token prediction. However, models optimized solely for token prediction often exhibit an objective mismatch with real-world deployment requirements, which demand alignment with complex human values such such as helpfulness, honesty, and harmlessness.

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) has emerged as a prominent post-training paradigm to address this alignment challenge. RLHF adapts pre-trained models using reinforcement learning techniques, leveraging human preferences as a guiding signal. The existing RLHF literature broadly categorizes algorithms into reward-based and preference-based approaches. While RLHF has achieved notable success in diverse domains, including robotics, video games, and LLM fine-tuning, current algorithms frequently encounter issues stemming from various forms of model misspecification. These include:

1. **Preference model misspecification:** Many reward-based algorithms rely on the Bradley-Terry (BT) preference model, which assumes properties like transitivity, context-independence, and perfect relationality in human preferences. Empirical evidence suggests these assumptions are often violated. While some preference-based algorithms use more general preference models (GPMs), their effectiveness still depends on correct model specification.
2. **Reward model misspecification:** Reward-based algorithms typically involve estimating a reward function from human preference data, followed by policy optimization (e.g., using Proximal Policy Optimization, PPO). Policy learning through RL is highly sensitive to errors in the estimated reward function, potentially leading to phenomena such as "reward hacking" and misguidance in policy development.
3. **Reference policy misspecification:** Recent algorithms, such as Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), aim to mitigate reward model misspecification by directly expressing the reward in closed form using a reference policy for policy learning. However, these DPO-based approaches are sensitive to the accurate specification of this reference policy.

This paper introduces a novel RLHF algorithm that integrates principles from doubly robust (DR) estimation methods, a technique extensively studied in statistics, econometrics, and machine learning, particularly in contexts like missing data and causal inference. DR methods construct estimators that remain consistent if at least one of two component models (e.g., propensity score model and outcome regression model in causal inference) is correctly specified. By drawing upon this framework, the present work aims to bridge a gap by applying DR principles to LLM fine-tuning, seeking to develop an algorithm robust to the aforementioned model misspecifications prevalent in RLHF.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust and statistically efficient algorithm for aligning large language models (LLMs) with human preferences, specifically addressing the prevalent challenge of model misspecification in existing Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) methodologies. The authors identify three critical areas where current RLHF algorithms demonstrate sensitivity:

1. **Preference Model Misspecification:** Many reward-based RLHF algorithms assume the Bradley-Terry (BT) model for human preferences. This model posits specific, often unrealistic, properties such as transitivity, context-independence, and perfect relationality. Empirical studies have shown that human preferences frequently violate these assumptions, leading to inaccuracies when the BT model is used. Even more general preference models (GPMs) are only effective if correctly specified. The motivation here is to develop a method that does not strictly rely on the fidelity of a single preference model assumption.

2. **Reward Model Misspecification:** Traditional reward-based RLHF approaches involve a two-step process: first estimating a reward function from human feedback, and then using this estimated reward to train an optimal policy via reinforcement learning (e.g., PPO). The authors highlight that policy learning is highly susceptible to errors in the estimated reward function. Misspecified reward models can lead to undesirable outcomes such as "reward hacking," where the policy optimizes for flaws in the reward function rather than true human intent, or simply misguide the policy learning process. This necessitates an approach that is less dependent on a perfectly estimated reward.

3. **Reference Policy Misspecification:** Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) algorithms were developed to bypass the explicit reward modeling step by formulating the reward in a closed form that depends on a reference policy. While this addresses reward model sensitivity, DPO-based methods become critically reliant on the accurate specification of the reference policy. Errors or misspecifications in the reference policy can lead to suboptimal or misaligned policy learning. The motivation is to create an optimization framework that is resilient even when the reference policy is not perfectly known or modeled.

In response to these challenges, the paper's key objectives are:

- **Propose a robust and efficient estimator for preference evaluation:** This estimator should be capable of accurately evaluating the probability of a target policy being preferred over a reference policy. The goal is for this estimator to possess "double robustness," meaning it consistently converges to the true preference probability if *either* the preference model *or* the reference policy is correctly specified, without requiring both. Additionally, it aims for "semi-parametric efficiency," achieving the smallest possible mean squared error among a class of estimators.
- **Develop a preference optimization algorithm for LLM fine-tuning:** Leveraging the proposed preference estimator, the algorithm should maintain the double robustness property, ensuring consistent policy learning even when the strict assumptions of models like the Bradley-Terry model are violated. Furthermore, when these idealized assumptions do hold, the algorithm should theoretically demonstrate a smaller suboptimality gap compared to PPO- or DPO-based methods.

Ultimately, the motivation is to enhance the reliability and performance of LLM alignment by providing a framework that is less brittle to the inherent uncertainties and complexities of modeling human preferences and policy behaviors.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors propose a novel Doubly Robust Preference Optimization (DRPO) algorithm that departs from traditional reward-based RLHF by adopting a preference-based approach. The methodology centers on two main components: a doubly robust preference evaluation estimator and an optimization framework built upon it.

**Data Generating Process:**
The work assumes a dataset $D$ comprising $n$ independent and identically distributed tuples $(X, Y^{(1)}, Y^{(2)}, Z)$. Each tuple is generated from a prompt $X$, with two responses $(Y^{(1)}, Y^{(2)})$ sampled from a reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. A human expert then provides a binary preference $Z = I(Y^{(1)} \succ Y^{(2)})$, indicating if $Y^{(1)}$ is preferred over $Y^{(2)}$. A key aspect highlighted is that the reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is not always known or perfectly specified, and the preference function $g^*(X, Y^{(1)}, Y^{(2)}) = P(Y^{(1)} \succ Y^{(2)}|X)$ dictates the true preference probability.

**Preference Evaluation:**
The goal of preference evaluation is to accurately estimate $p^*(\pi) := P(\pi \succ \pi_{\text{ref}})$, the total preference of a target policy $\pi$ over the reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. The authors first introduce two baseline estimators, drawing parallels to the Off-Policy Evaluation (OPE) literature:

1. **Direct Method (DM) Estimator (Equation 6):** This estimator relies on directly modeling and estimating the preference function $g^*$, denoted as $\hat{g}$. It then plugs $\hat{g}$ into an expression derived from $p^*(\pi)$, essentially calculating $E_{X \sim D, y \sim \pi(\cdot|X)}[\hat{g}(X, y, Y^{(a)})]$. Its consistency is contingent on the correct specification of $\hat{g}$.

2. **Importance Sampling (IS) Estimator (Equation 7):** This estimator leverages an identity (Lemma 1) that expresses $p^*(\pi)$ using the Importance Sampling (IS) ratio $w(y, x) = \pi(y|x)/\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)$. The estimator computes an empirical average of terms involving the IS ratio and the observed preference $Z$. Its consistency critically depends on the correct specification of the reference policy, $\hat{\pi}_{\text{ref}}$.

**Doubly Robust (DR) Estimator (Equation 9):**
The core of the proposed preference evaluation is the DR estimator, $\hat{p}_{\text{DR}}(\pi)$. It combines elements of both DM and IS estimators. The DR estimator is defined using an estimating function $\psi$ (Equation 8). This function has two main parts:
- A term resembling the DM estimator, based on the estimated preference function $\hat{g}$.
- An "augmentation term" similar to IS, but where the observed preference $Z$ is replaced by its residual $Z - \hat{g}(X, Y^{(1)}, Y^{(2)})$. This residual term acts as a bias correction.
The DR estimator computes the empirical expectation of this $\psi$ function. A notable feature is that each data tuple $(X, Y^{(1)}, Y^{(2)}, Z)$ is utilized twice in the estimating function (once as $(X, Y^{(1)}, Y^{(2)}, Z)$ and once implicitly as $(X, Y^{(2)}, Y^{(1)}, 1-Z)$), which is noted to reduce variance.

**Preference Optimization (DRPO):**
For policy optimization, the objective is to find an optimal policy $\hat{\pi}$ that maximizes the estimated total preference $\hat{p}_{\text{DR}}(\pi)$, regularized by a Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence term between the target policy $\pi$ and the estimated reference policy $\hat{\pi}_{\text{ref}}$:
$\hat{\pi} = \arg \max_{\pi \in \Pi} \{ \hat{p}_{\text{DR}}(\pi) - \beta E_{X \sim D} D_{\text{KL}}[\pi(\cdot|X) \| \hat{\pi}_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|X)] \}$ (Equation 10).
This objective is referred to as Doubly Robust Preference Optimization (DRPO).

**Practical Implementation:**
The authors detail practical refinements for implementing DRPO:
- **Gradient Computation:** The gradient of the objective function is derived, showing how it guides the policy to favor responses preferred by $\hat{g}$ and adjusts based on observed preferences and IS ratios.
- **Empirical Loss Function:** An empirical loss function (Equation 40, and further refined in Equation 41) is constructed such that its negative gradient approximates the desired objective gradient. This involves Monte Carlo sampling from the current policy for the direct-method term and using a $k3$-type empirical KL divergence for regularization.
- **Augmented Dataset:** The original dataset is augmented with swapped pairs $(X, Y^{(2)}, Y^{(1)}, 1-Z)$ for each $(X, Y^{(1)}, Y^{(2)}, Z)$ to simplify the loss function.
- **IS Ratio Clipping:** To mitigate high variance issues associated with IS ratios, especially when $\pi$ and $\hat{\pi}_{\text{ref}}$ diverge significantly, the IS ratio is clipped within a specified range.
- **Decoupled Gradient Computation:** The calculation of the IS ratio is decoupled from the gradient computation using a stop-gradient operation and by multiplying the IS term by $\log \pi_\theta$, which stabilizes training by shrinking gradients in regions of small $\hat{\pi}_{\text{ref}}$.

The entire framework operates by pre-training the reference model $\hat{\pi}_{\text{ref}}$ and the preference model $\hat{g}$ independently. These models can be learned internally from data (e.g., via Supervised Fine-Tuning for $\hat{\pi}_{\text{ref}}$ or maximum likelihood for $\hat{g}$) or be external. The DRPO algorithm then iteratively updates the target policy $\pi$ based on the empirical loss.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper presents both theoretical analyses and empirical validations for the proposed Doubly Robust Preference Optimization (DRPO) algorithm.

**Theoretical Findings:**

The theoretical analysis establishes several properties for the DRPO preference evaluation estimator and the resulting optimized policy:

- **MSE of $\hat{p}_{\text{DR}}(\pi)$ (Theorem 2):** The Mean Squared Error (MSE) of the proposed DR estimator for preference evaluation is bounded. It consists of the semi-parametric efficiency bound (SEB), which scales as $O(n^{-1})$, and additional terms reflecting the estimation errors of the preference model $\hat{g}$ and the reference policy $\hat{\pi}_{\text{ref}}$. Crucially, the bias term in the MSE is a product of these two estimation errors.
- **Doubly Robust Evaluation (Corollary 3):** A direct consequence of Theorem 2, this corollary establishes that the DR estimator for preference evaluation (Eq. 9) is doubly robust. Its MSE decays to zero as the sample size $n$ approaches infinity if *either* the reference policy $\hat{\pi}_{\text{ref}}$ *or* the preference model $\hat{g}$ is correctly specified, without requiring both to be accurate.
- **Semi-parametric Efficiency (Corollary 4):** If both $\hat{\pi}_{\text{ref}}$ and $\hat{g}$ are "approximately correct" (i.e., their root MSEs decay to zero), and their product decays faster than $O(n^{-1/2})$, then the MSE of $\hat{p}_{\text{DR}}(\pi)$ asymptotically matches the SEB. This indicates that the estimator achieves the lowest possible MSE among regular and asymptotically linear estimators.
- **Performance Gap of $\hat{\pi}$ (Theorem 5):** When the Bradley-Terry model assumption is violated, the performance gap of the optimized policy $\hat{\pi}$ (defined as the difference in total preference between the best in-class policy and $\hat{\pi}$) is bounded. The bound shows dependence on the regularization parameter $\beta$, policy class complexity ($v$), sample size ($n$), and significantly, a product term of the estimation errors of $\hat{\pi}_{\text{ref}}$ and $\hat{g}$.
- **Doubly Robust Optimization (Corollary 6):** As a direct implication of Theorem 5, the DRPO algorithm itself exhibits double robustness. If the regularization parameter $\beta$ diminishes with $n$, and either $\hat{\pi}_{\text{ref}}$ or $\hat{g}$ is correctly specified, the performance gap of $\hat{\pi}$ converges to zero as $n \to \infty$.
- **Suboptimality Gap (Theorem 7):** In the ideal scenario where the Bradley-Terry model holds, the suboptimality gap of DRPO (the difference in expected reward between the true optimal policy $\pi^*$ and $\hat{\pi}$) is derived. The bound for DRPO is $O(\beta + \sqrt{v/n} + v/n + \|\hat{\pi}_{\text{ref}}/\pi_{\text{ref}} - 1\| \|\hat{r} - r^*\|)$, where $\|\hat{r} - r^*\|$ is the reward model estimation error.
 - This is compared to PPO-based algorithms ($O(\beta + \sqrt{v/n} + v/n + \|\hat{r} - r^*\|)$) and DPO-based algorithms ($O(\exp(-\bar{c}\beta^{-1}) + \beta^{-1}\sqrt{v/n} + \|\hat{\pi}_{\text{ref}}/\pi_{\text{ref}} - 1\|)$).
 - The crucial finding is that DRPO's suboptimality bound depends on the *product* of reference policy and reward model estimation errors, making it more robust. In contrast, PPO and DPO bounds are influenced by these errors in a first-order manner. This implies that DRPO can achieve strictly smaller suboptimality bounds when these estimation errors converge at a rate slower than $O(n^{-1/2})$.

**Empirical Results:**

The empirical evaluation was conducted on three datasets: IMDb (synthetic for evaluation), Too Long; Didn’t Read (TL;DR) (summarization), and Anthropic Helpful and Harmless (HH) (human dialogue).

- **Preference Evaluation on IMDb (Figure 3, Table 4):**
 - Using synthetic data where ground-truth models are known, the experiment demonstrated the double robustness of $\hat{p}_{\text{DR}}(\pi)$.
 - The MSE was significantly larger when both the preference model and reference policy were misspecified.
 - When *either* the preference model *or* the reference policy was correctly specified, the MSE was substantially reduced and decayed with increasing sample size, confirming Corollary 3.
 - The lowest MSE was achieved when both models were correctly specified, supporting semi-parametric efficiency (Corollary 4).

- **Preference Optimization on TL;DR (Figure 4 left, Table 2):**
 - This dataset features a misspecified reference policy (trained on a filtered subset).
 - DRPO-BT and DRPO-GPM (using Bradley-Terry and General Preference Model, respectively, for $\hat{g}$) consistently outperformed PPO, DPO, and seven DPO variants (Dr. DPO, rDPO, cDPO, CPO, ORPO, IPO, RSO) in pairwise win rates (e.g., DRPO vs IPO: 98.5% win rate).
 - The superior performance of DRPO, despite the misspecified reference policy, empirically validates its robustness to such misspecification as predicted by theory.

- **Preference Optimization on HH (Figure 4 right, Table 3):**
 - This dataset is noted for potentially containing unmodeled pairwise noise beyond the BT model, suggesting preference model misspecification.
 - **In-distribution evaluation:** DRPO-GPM achieved the best performance. DRPO-BT also outperformed PPO and matched DPO's performance. DRPO-BT's 57% win rate against PPO, despite using the same preference model for $\hat{g}$, further indicated its robustness.
 - **Out-of-distribution evaluation (AlpacaEval 2.0):** DRPO performed comparably to robust DPO variants (cDPO, rDPO, Dr. DPO) and showed higher win rates than other variants. The robust DPO variants are explicitly designed for noisy preferences, implying that incorporating similar noise-aware models into DRPO's preference model component could further enhance its performance in such settings.

In summary, the theoretical framework provides strong guarantees for DRPO's robustness and efficiency, which are consistently supported by empirical results across different tasks and types of model misspecification.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of Doubly Robust Preference Optimization (DRPO) represents a significant advancement in the field of Large Language Model (LLM) alignment through Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF). Its core contribution lies in enhancing the robustness of fine-tuning procedures against common and often unavoidable model misspecifications.

**Key Significance:**

- **Increased Robustness to Model Misspecification:** Traditional RLHF algorithms are highly sensitive to incorrect specifications of the preference model (e.g., Bradley-Terry model), the reward function derived from it, or the reference policy. DRPO mitigates this fragility by ensuring consistent performance even if *only one* of these component models (preference model or reference policy) is correctly specified. This "double robustness" is a critical feature, as achieving perfectly specified models in real-world human-AI interaction scenarios is often impractical.
- **Enhanced Statistical Efficiency:** Beyond robustness, the proposed preference evaluation estimator is shown to be semi-parametrically efficient. This implies that, when both underlying models are sufficiently accurate, DRPO achieves the lowest possible asymptotic variance, leading to more data-efficient and precise estimation of human preferences.
- **Improved Policy Learning Guarantees:** The theoretical analysis demonstrates that DRPO yields policies with a smaller suboptimality gap compared to established algorithms like PPO and DPO, particularly when model misspecifications are present. The dependence of DRPO's error bounds on the *product* of model estimation errors, rather than their sum, signifies a higher degree of resilience, leading to more reliable alignment outcomes.
- **Practical Performance Gains:** Empirical evaluations confirm these theoretical advantages. DRPO consistently outperforms or matches state-of-the-art RLHF methods across different tasks (summarization, human dialogue), especially when the reference policy or preference model is known to be misspecified. This suggests that DRPO can lead to more effective and stable LLM fine-tuning in diverse application contexts.

**Potential Impact:**

The development of DRPO has several potential impacts:

- **More Reliable and Stable LLM Alignment:** By making alignment less susceptible to imperfections in preference and reference models, DRPO can lead to LLMs that are more consistently helpful, harmless, and honest, even when human feedback data or underlying behavioral models are noisy or incomplete. This can improve user trust and satisfaction with LLM applications.
- **Reduced Development Costs and Iteration Time:** Developers may be able to rely on less precise initial models for preference and reference policies, reducing the effort and computational resources required for model training and iteration. This could accelerate the deployment of aligned LLMs.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The integration of doubly robust estimation principles into RLHF opens new avenues for research, particularly in exploring how other robust statistical techniques can be applied to complex machine learning problems like LLM alignment.
- **Broader Applicability:** While demonstrated for LLMs, the underlying principles of doubly robust preference optimization could be adapted to other domains requiring alignment with human feedback, such as robotics or personalized recommendation systems.

**Limitations and Broader Societal Impact Considerations:**

The paper acknowledges certain limitations, such as the potential for high variance in Importance Sampling (IS) ratios when target and behavior policies differ significantly (even with clipping) and the lack of evaluation on substantially larger-scale models due to hardware constraints.

From a broader societal perspective, while DRPO aims to improve LLM alignment with human preferences, the definition of "human preference" itself is critical. Improved alignment capabilities could be misused if models are trained to align with harmful, biased, or narrow preferences of specific groups. The authors explicitly note that the algorithm itself does not produce harmful content; rather, such outcomes arise from optimization towards problematic training data. Therefore, the successful and ethical deployment of DRPO, like any powerful alignment technique, relies heavily on careful curation and management of training datasets to ensure alignment with beneficial and ethical human values, preventing the propagation of harmful or undesirable behaviors.
