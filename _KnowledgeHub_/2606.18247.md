# Visual Verification Enables Inference-time Steering and Autonomous Policy Improvement

> [!summary] Summary
> VERITAS, a framework from Princeton University, enables generalist robot policies to improve autonomously by using an inference-time visual verifier to steer actions and collect high-quality data. It achieved an average 35% success rate improvement in real-world manipulation tasks through steering and demonstrated that autonomously collected data is as, or more, effective than human demonstrations for offline policy fine-tuning.

## Problem

- Generalist robot policies face a data bottleneck, relying on expensive and unscalable human-collected demonstrations.
- Existing robot learning paradigms often require costly human intervention (e.g., shared autonomy, expert relabeling) for corrections and data collection.
- Imitation learning is prone to covariate shift, where policy performance degrades as it deviates from the expert's state distribution, necessitating further expert queries.

## Method

- The **VERITAS** framework combines a stochastic generalist policy (generator) with a ==gradient-free visual verifier== that evaluates candidate actions.
- During inference, the policy samples multiple action chunks, the verifier scores them based on task alignment and geometric constraints, and the highest-scoring chunk is executed.
- Successful, verifier-guided trajectories are autonomously logged and used to fine-tune the base policy offline, distilling the verifier's reasoning into the policy weights for persistent improvement.

## Results

- Inference-time steering improved success rates by **12.6%** in simulation and **35%** in real-world manipulation tasks (e.g., placing a carrot on a plate, picking up a mouse) without any policy fine-tuning.
- Offline fine-tuning with autonomously collected, verified data led to an average **9.7%** policy performance improvement in simulation and consistent gains across real-world tasks.
- Verifier-curated autonomous rollouts demonstrated comparable or superior data efficiency to human expert demonstrations for policy fine-tuning, reducing reliance on costly human supervision.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Inference-time verification can significantly improve robot policy performance by trading computation for reduced data requirements, similar to advances in large language models.
> - Generating and filtering on-policy data autonomously through verification mitigates distribution shift and provides a scalable source for continuous policy improvement.
> - A visual verifier leveraging a VLM to create a static pixel-space 'reasoning trace' and scoring actions based on proximity to this trace is effective and efficient for steering.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of generalist robot policies, often referred to as robotic foundation models, represents a significant trend in contemporary robotics. These models, typically trained on extensive demonstration data, have demonstrated advanced capabilities across various domains, including manipulation and navigation. However, a fundamental bottleneck in their continued advancement is the cost and scalability of data collection. Unlike domains such as text or image processing, robotic data acquisition often involves expensive, slow processes limited by the availability of human experts. Current state-of-the-art models primarily rely on data collected by human experts, a method that scales linearly with human time and resources.

This work addresses this challenge by drawing inspiration from advances in large language models (LLMs), where inference-time computation and verification mechanisms have been successfully employed to improve reasoning performance without additional training. LLMs frequently generate multiple solution candidates and employ a "verifier" to select the most appropriate one. The paper positions its approach, VERITAS, as an analogous solution for robotics, enabling a robot policy to generate and verify multiple action candidates at inference time before committing to a physical action. This strategy allows for an exchange of computational resources for performance improvements, reducing the need for additional, costly data collection for coverage or recovery behaviors.

Existing robot learning paradigms often involve human-in-the-loop learning, shared autonomy, or expert interventions to correct policy mistakes and gather corrective data. Approaches like DAgger rely on expert relabeling, while shared autonomy blends human and autonomous control. More recent systems structure data collection by explicitly defining nominal execution and recovery phases, triggering human intervention during failures. VERITAS distinguishes itself from these methods by replacing human intervention with an autonomous, execution-time verification process. This verifier functions as a task-aligned preference signal, enabling the robot to improve performance and collect success-conditioned trajectories autonomously, thereby reducing reliance on direct human supervision.

Furthermore, previous research has explored integrating explicit reasoning into robot policies, often through training vision-language-action (VLA) models with supervision over intermediate visual and semantic representations. While these methods demonstrate embodied reasoning, they typically require extensive annotations during pre-training. Other directions involve sampling and steering online actions using learned verifiers or world models to predict reasonable actions. VERITAS contributes to this landscape by offering a novel perspective: it treats online verification not merely as a temporary performance boost but as a *data engine* for long-term policy improvement. The framework distills expensive inference-time reasoning into the base policy through autonomous verification and post-training, bypassing the need for training task-specific verifiers or relying on expensive annotations. By generating on-policy and kinematically feasible data, VERITAS also addresses the problem of covariate shift in imitation learning, which typically requires expert queries on robot-visited states to mitigate performance degradation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a scalable methodology for improving generalist robot policies without requiring a proportional increase in human-collected demonstration data. This overarching goal is driven by several motivations:

- **Addressing the Data Bottleneck in Robotics:** Robotic foundation models currently face a fundamental limitation in that their improvements are largely contingent on the availability of large-scale, high-quality human expert data. Collecting such data is expensive, time-consuming, and inherently difficult to scale. The authors seek to overcome this by enabling robots to learn from their own experiences in the real world with minimal external supervision and reduced cost.
- **Leveraging Inference-Time Computation for Performance:** Inspired by the success of large language models that use verification mechanisms to refine generated outputs, the researchers hypothesize that a similar approach can benefit robotics. In robotics, where physical data acquisition is significantly more costly than computational resources, trading increased inference-time computation for improved task performance represents an economically viable strategy. This allows the robot to "think" by sampling and evaluating multiple candidate actions before executing the most suitable one.
- **Enabling Autonomous Policy Steering:** A key motivation is to provide a mechanism for real-time, online improvement of existing generalist policies. By pairing a pre-trained policy with a gradient-free visual verifier, the framework aims to steer the policy's actions during deployment, yielding immediate performance gains without requiring any modifications to the policy's parameters or additional training.
- **Establishing a Self-Improvement Data Flywheel:** Beyond immediate steering, the work aims to create a sustainable self-improvement loop. The successful, verifier-guided trajectories generated during inference-time steering are inherently high-quality and on-policy. These trajectories can then be utilized as a source of autonomous supervision for offline policy fine-tuning. This "data flywheel" allows the robot to learn from its own successful executions, distilling the reasoning performed by the verifier back into the policy weights, thereby improving its capabilities persistently and reducing the need for verification in future deployments.
- **Mitigating Distribution Shift and Reducing Human Intervention:** Traditional imitation learning suffers from covariate shift, where the policy's performance degrades as it drifts from the expert's state distribution. Human-in-the-loop approaches address this by querying experts for labels on robot-visited states. A motivation for VERITAS is to automate this process. By generating data via the robot's own policy and filtering it with an automated verifier, the collected data is inherently on-policy and kinematically feasible, which can effectively mitigate distribution shift without requiring continuous human expert intervention.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The VERITAS framework is built upon a generator-verifier paradigm designed for generalist robot policies. The methodology comprises two main phases: inference-time policy steering and autonomous policy improvement through offline fine-tuning.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation and Policy Generation

The problem is formulated as language-guided robotic manipulation within a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP). The objective is to train a policy ($\pi_\theta$) that maximizes the expected success rate over a distribution of tasks, with a specific focus on enabling post-deployment self-improvement.

- **Generalist Robot Policies as Stochastic Generators:** The framework assumes access to a pre-trained "generalist policy" ($\pi_\theta$) that takes an observation ($o_t$) and a natural language instruction ($l$) to predict actions. Crucially, this policy is treated as a *stochastic generator*, capable of sampling diverse candidate actions. The authors specifically use generative policies trained with flow-matching or diffusion objectives, which can model complex, multi-modal action distributions, thus enabling exploration of various functional affordances.
- **Action Chunking:** To amortize the cost of verification and provide temporal context, the policy predicts sequences of actions, referred to as "action chunks" ($\mathbf{a}_{t:t+H}$), rather than single instantaneous movements.

#### 3.2. Verifier Interface

A verifier ($V$) is introduced to evaluate candidate action chunks. The verifier is defined as a plug-and-play, gradient-free function that maps an observation, a candidate action chunk, and a task instruction to a scalar score: $V(o_t, \mathbf{a}_{t:t+H}, l) \in \mathbb{R}$. This score conceptually represents the utility or alignment of executing the action chunk with the task instruction. The verifier operates solely at inference time and does not modify the policy parameters, allowing for flexible instantiation with various mechanisms (e.g., VLMs, geometric constraints, or learned value models) without retraining the base policy.

#### 3.3. Inference-Time Steering

The generator and verifier are combined using a Best-of-N selection strategy:
1. **Sampling:** At each decision step, the policy generates $N$ diverse candidate action chunks ($\{\mathbf{a}^{(i)}_{t:t+H}\}_{i=1}^N$).
2. **Verification:** The verifier scores each candidate ($v_i = V(o_t, \mathbf{a}^{(i)}_{t:t+H}, l)$).
3. **Selection:** The highest-scoring candidate is selected for execution ($\mathbf{a}^*_{t:t+H} = \mathbf{a}^{(i^*)}_{t:t+H}$, where $i^* = \operatorname{argmax}_i v_i$).
4. **Execution:** The robot executes the selected action chunk in the environment.

This process functions as a filter, prioritizing physically valid and task-aligned actions. The performance gains at this stage are purely derived from inference-time computation.

#### 3.4. Autonomous Self-Improvement

To translate inference-time gains into permanent policy improvements and amortize the computational cost, the successful verified trajectories are used for offline policy fine-tuning:
1. **Data Collection:** During policy deployment, successful verified trajectories generated via inference-time steering are logged into a dataset, $D_{auto}$.
2. **Policy Fine-tuning:** The original policy ($\pi_\theta$) is then fine-tuned on $D_{auto}$ using standard supervised behavior cloning to obtain updated parameters ($\theta'$). This distillation process ensures that the reasoning performed by the verifier is incorporated into the policy's weights.
3. **Mitigation of Distribution Shift:** A key aspect is that the data in $D_{auto}$ is generated by the robot's own policy and filtered by the verifier, making it inherently on-policy and kinematically feasible. This helps mitigate the distribution shift problem common in imitation learning by aligning the training distribution with the robot's exploration prior.

#### 3.5. Instantiating VERITAS for Robotic Manipulation

- **Base Policy:** The authors employ the $\pi_0$ architecture, an open-source Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model based on a mixture-of-transformers backbone with a flow-matching action head. This architecture is chosen for its ability to sample complex, multi-modal action distributions.
- **Visual Verifier Design (VERITAS Verifier):** The chosen verifier design, selected after ablation in simulation experiments, leverages a Vision-Language Model (VLM) to ground high-level semantic instructions into precise geometric constraints.
 1. **Trace Generation:** At the beginning of a task, the VLM is prompted with the initial observation and language instruction to generate a "visual reasoning trace"—a sequence of pixel-space waypoints that define an ideal end-effector trajectory. This trace remains static throughout the episode for temporal consistency.
 2. **Geometric Scoring:** During inference, for each candidate action chunk, the resulting end-effector/gripper positions are projected into the image plane. The verifier score is computed as the negative Euclidean distance between the candidate's projected trajectory and the nearest segment of the VLM-generated trace. This design allows for efficient verification, as the VLM is queried only once, and subsequent verification involves fast geometric consistency checks.
- **Implementation Details:** Experiments utilize an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5090 GPU. An action chunk horizon of $H$ steps is defined. For steering, $N=5$ candidate samples are used, maintaining a control frequency of 15 Hz. For offline improvement, the $\pi_0$-DROID checkpoint is fine-tuned on $D_{auto}$ using a learning rate of $5 \times 10^{-5}$ for 20,000 steps.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of VERITAS was evaluated through extensive experiments in both simulation and real-world settings, addressing inference-time steering, policy improvement, and data efficiency.

#### 4.1. Inference-Time Steering

- **Simulation Results:** Evaluation on 4 manipulation tasks from SimplerEnv showed that verifier-guided action selection consistently improved success rates compared to the base $\pi_0$-Bridge policy (Figure 4). All tested verifier designs (VERITAS, VLM-Constraints, Heuristic) outperformed the V-GPS baseline. Average success rate improvement was 12.6%. The largest gains were observed in more challenging tasks, such as "Stack Blocks," suggesting that the verifier has greater impact when the base policy is less confident and produces more diverse samples.
- **Real-World Results:** Similar trends were observed in real-world experiments using $\pi_0$-DROID and $\pi_{0.5}$-DROID policies across 4 challenging manipulation tasks (Figure 6). Inference-time steering significantly improved task success rates, achieving an average improvement of 35% without any policy fine-tuning. VERITAS consistently outperformed the V-GPS-DROID baseline. A comparison with PIVOT, which uses naive action primitives, demonstrated the importance of a strong action prior provided by the base policy.
- **Generalizability:** The framework demonstrated consistent improvements across different base policies and verifier implementations, highlighting its robustness and general applicability for inference-time steering.

#### 4.2. Offline Policy Improvement

- **Simulation Results:** Fine-tuning the $\pi_0$-Bridge policy on 656 autonomously collected, successful verified rollouts resulted in an average policy performance improvement of 9.7% across the 4 simulated tasks (Figure 5). Notably, the largest improvement occurred on "Stack Blocks," where the success rate increased by 27.9% (from 31.3% to 59.2%). This indicates that distilling verifier-guided behaviors into the policy weights effectively enhances its capabilities.
- **Real-World Results:** In real-world tasks, verified autonomous data consistently supported effective policy improvement across 4 challenging tasks for 2 policies (Figure 7). Performance reliably improved as more autonomously collected data was used for fine-tuning, demonstrating the sufficiency of autonomous rollouts for successful policy improvement.

#### 4.3. Autonomous vs. Human-Collected Data

- **Real-World Data Efficiency:** A crucial finding was the comparison of data efficiency between verifier-curated autonomous rollouts and human expert demonstrations for offline policy fine-tuning (Figure 7). Policies fine-tuned on datasets of 20, 30, 50, and 100 trajectories showed that autonomous data achieved comparable or, in several cases, superior data efficiency to human expert demonstrations. For instance, on "Carrot on plate," 50 autonomous demonstrations outperformed human demonstrations (0.70 vs. 0.65 success rate), and on "Pick up mouse," autonomous data consistently yielded higher success rates in the 20-50 demonstration range. At larger data budgets (e.g., 100 demonstrations), performance between the two data sources converged or remained competitive. These results suggest that autonomously collected, verifier-curated data can serve as a scalable and cost-effective alternative to human demonstrations for policy improvement.

#### 4.4. Implementation Hyperparameters

- **Action Execution Horizon:** Experiments indicated that the open-loop action execution horizon did not significantly degrade verification performance for up to 10 steps (Figure 8). An 8-step horizon was used for real-world evaluations.
- **Number of Samples (N):** Performance generally improved with an increased number of action samples $N$, but saturated beyond $N=8$ (Figure 9). This provides a practical guideline for deployment, balancing performance gains with computational cost; $N=5$ was utilized in the experiments.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The VERITAS framework presents several significant contributions to the field of robotic learning and holds potential for substantial impact:

- **Scalable Robot Self-Improvement:** The core impact is the provision of a practical and scalable mechanism for robots to continually learn and improve their capabilities from real-world experience without requiring continuous human expert supervision. By automating the data collection and filtering process, VERITAS bypasses the bottleneck of expensive and limited human demonstrations, enabling a "data flywheel" where deployment experience naturally translates into persistent capability enhancements.
- **Reduced Reliance on Human Intervention:** VERITAS significantly reduces the need for human-in-the-loop interventions, expert relabeling, or shared autonomy, which are common in existing robot learning paradigms. The visual verifier autonomously evaluates and selects high-quality actions, effectively replacing the role of a human supervisor in providing corrective signals and curating successful trajectories.
- **Enhanced Generalist Policies:** The framework demonstrates that pre-trained generalist robot policies can achieve substantial performance gains purely through inference-time computation. This extends the utility of existing foundation models by providing a method for online steering and improvement, thereby making these policies more robust and reliable in deployment.
- **Data-Efficient Learning:** A critical finding is that verifier-curated autonomous rollouts can match or even exceed the data efficiency of costly human expert demonstrations for offline policy fine-tuning. This offers a cost-effective and practical alternative for building training datasets, thereby making advanced robotic capabilities more accessible and scalable.
- **Mitigation of Distribution Shift:** By generating inherently on-policy and kinematically feasible training data through autonomous execution and verification, the framework effectively mitigates the distribution shift problem prevalent in imitation learning. This ensures that the policy learns from data within its reachable state-action space, leading to more robust and reliable improvements.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The work opens avenues for future research to address current limitations, such as the computational cost in latency-critical applications or the reliance on static visual traces in highly dynamic environments. Potential directions include distilling verifier logic into faster value functions, joint optimization of generators and verifiers for sample efficiency, and leveraging advancements in policy pre-training to further enhance the verification pipeline.

Overall, VERITAS offers a pathway for autonomous robots to develop greater intelligence and adaptability in real-world scenarios, shifting the paradigm from human-dependent data acquisition to robot-driven experiential learning.
