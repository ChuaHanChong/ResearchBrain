# MidasTouch: Monte-Carlo inference over distributions across sliding touch

> [!summary] Summary
> MidasTouch performs online global localization of a vision-based tactile sensor sliding on a known object surface. A tactile depth network turns each touch image into local 3D geometry, a sparse-convolution tactile code network compresses it into a 256-D embedding matched against a per-object codebook, and a Monte-Carlo particle filter disambiguates over time, reaching 0.74cm/9.43 degrees final pose error in simulation and 1.97cm/21.48 degrees in the real world at 10Hz.

## Problem

- Vision-based tactile images capture only local surface geometry and lack the global context needed to recover where the finger sits on an object.
- Prior tactile tracking is unimodal and needs good pose initialization, or is restricted to small parts comparable in size to the sensor footprint.
- Single touches are inherently ambiguous on symmetric or repeated geometry, and ICP-style matching is initialization-sensitive and intractable as a sampling-based measurement model.

## Method

- **MidasTouch** ==tactile depth network==: a ==ResNet-50 fully-convolutional model== trained on simulated renderings with lighting-calibration augmentation predicts heightmaps, masked and reprojected into local 3D point clouds.
- ==Tactile code network==: a ==sparse 3D convolution== place-recognition backbone, pretrained on LIDAR data and fine-tuned with ==triplet loss==, compresses geometry into 256-D codes stored in a 50k-pose per-object codebook.
- ==Monte-Carlo particle filter== on the object surface: kinematics-based motion model, ==cosine-similarity codebook lookup== as measurement model, ==low-variance resampling==, adaptive particle count, surface pruning, and ==DBSCAN== hypothesis clustering.

## Results

- Over **500** simulated trials on ten YCB objects, average final error is **0.74cm** and **9.43 degrees**; **500** real-world trials give **1.97cm** and **21.48 degrees**, running at **10Hz**.
- Closest-hypothesis metrics drop to median **0.28cm/2.03 degrees** in simulation and **1.11cm/10.76 degrees** in reality, showing the true mode is usually retained in the distribution.
- 3D geometry codes beat image embeddings on single-touch retrieval (**0.473** vs **0.546** normalized pose error) with a **300x** smaller codebook; small parts converge to roughly **4mm/5 degrees**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Geometry-space embeddings transfer better than image-space ones across sensors and scale far more compactly, since geometry normalizes away illumination and sensor-specific appearance.
> - Pairing a learned measurement model with nonparametric Monte-Carlo filtering resolves ambiguity that no single observation can, trading immediate accuracy for temporal disambiguation.
> - Localizability is a property of object geometry and contact area: featureless or symmetric surfaces fail, and deeper indentation yielding larger contact patches reduces error.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics increasingly relies on interactive perception, particularly for tasks involving contact-rich interactions such as sliding, in-hand manipulation, and finger-gaiting. These applications necessitate precise knowledge of the end-effector-object relative positioning. Traditional vision-based systems, while often effective for tracking, can be hindered by occlusions during contact, or by challenging object properties like transparency, specularity, or poor illumination.

Recent advancements in vision-based tactile sensors, such as GelSight and DIGIT, offer a localized view of object interactions by capturing high-resolution local surface geometry. However, these sensors inherently lack the global context required for comprehensive relative pose tracking across an entire object surface. Prior research with such sensors has often been limited to small parts or local pose tracking, frequently requiring good initial pose estimations or relying on unimodal likelihood distributions that struggle with ambiguities inherent to local contact. Some methods utilize binary contact sensors with particle filters for global estimation, but these typically demand a large number of discrete touches. The integration of vision and touch has also been explored to provide global context, but often involves external visual systems.

This work addresses the challenge of achieving global localization using solely tactile information from a vision-based sensor sliding over a known object. It positions itself as an analogue to mobile robot Monte-Carlo filtering, but applied to the surface manifold of an object. The framework leverages the concept that everyday objects are composed of distinct local geometries, and while a single tactile observation might be ambiguous (e.g., a sharp edge could be one of several similar features on an object), integrating observations over an extended sliding interaction can resolve these ambiguities. This approach is particularly relevant for environments where object models are available, such as in households, warehouses, or factories, and seeks to provide global pose estimation without the prerequisite of visual priors, though visual information can optionally serve as a bootstrap. The work builds upon developments in high-acuity tactile sensors, realistic tactile simulation for data generation, and learned models for 3D place recognition (e.g., from LIDAR data) adapted for tactile sensing.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop "MidasTouch," an online tactile perception system capable of performing global localization of a vision-based touch sensor as it slides across the surface of a known object. The system aims to output an evolving distribution of the sensor's 6D pose on the object's surface over time, critically without relying on visual priors.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges in robotics:
- **Information Gap in Contact-Rich Tasks**: While vision-based tactile sensors provide detailed local information about contact, they inherently lack global awareness. This limitation impedes robots from performing contact-rich tasks that require precise knowledge of the sensor's global position and orientation on an object.
- **Ambiguity of Local Tactile Signals**: A single tactile interaction often captures only a small patch of the object's surface geometry. This local information can be ambiguous, meaning the same tactile "feel" could correspond to multiple locations on an object, especially for objects with repetitive or symmetric features. The system is motivated to overcome this "contact non-uniqueness" problem by integrating information over time.
- **Need for Global Context without Vision**: In many practical robotic scenarios, visual tracking can be compromised due to occlusion by the end-effector itself, or by the inherent properties of objects (e.g., transparency, specularity). The research aims to provide a robust alternative or complement to vision by demonstrating that tactile sensing alone can establish global context through continuous interaction.
- **Enabling Advanced Manipulation**: Accurate and online global localization is crucial for advanced in-hand manipulation, re-grasping, and assembly tasks where precise control and planning depend on a reliable understanding of the robot's interaction with the object.
- **Benchmarking and Data for Tactile Sensing**: The tactile sensing community lacks comprehensive datasets for evaluating and benchmarking localization methods during dynamic interactions. A secondary objective is to release a dataset of real-world and simulated sliding interactions to facilitate future research in this domain.

Specifically, the research aimed to:
1. Develop an online particle filtering framework that operates on a stream of posed tactile images to estimate a distribution of finger-object poses.
2. Design learned embeddings that compactly represent local surface geometry extracted from vision-based tactile data.
3. Create and release the YCB-Slide dataset to support evaluation and benchmarking of tactile localization systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

MidasTouch is structured around a Monte-Carlo particle filter and comprises three main components: a Tactile Depth Network (TDN), a Tactile Code Network (TCN), and the particle filter itself. The system receives posed tactile images over time and outputs a continuously updating distribution of the sensor's pose on the object surface.

#### 3.1. Tactile Depth Network (TDN)

The TDN is responsible for converting an input tactile image into its corresponding local 3D geometry, represented as a heightmap.
- **Architecture**: A fully-convolutional residual network, leveraging a ResNet-50 backbone combined with up-projection blocks.
- **Training**: The network is trained in a supervised manner using data generated by the TACTO optical tactile simulator. This dataset comprises a large collection of DIGIT images paired with ground-truth heightmaps, generated from interactions with 40 YCB objects, reserving a subset for testing.
- **Sim2Real Transfer**: To enhance generalization to real-world scenarios, TACTO was calibrated using real-world DIGIT data, and random lighting augmentations were applied during training.
- **Operation**: At each timestep `t`, an RGB tactile image `I_t` is passed through the TDN to predict a heightmap `H_t`. A mask `C_t` is then computed via depth thresholding to isolate contact areas, yielding `Ĥ_t`. Finally, `Ĥ_t` is reprojected into a 3D point cloud `P_t` using the known camera perspective model.

#### 3.2. Tactile Code Network (TCN)

The TCN functions to summarize the high-dimensional 3D point clouds (`P_t`) generated by the TDN into a compact, low-dimensional embedding, referred to as a "tactile code" (`E_t`). This embedding is designed such that similar local geometries correspond to nearby codes in the embedding space.
- **Inspiration**: The design is inspired by 3D place recognition modules from the Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM) community, particularly those used for LIDAR data, such as MinkLoc3D, which employ sparse 3D convolutions.
- **Architecture**: It adopts the MinkLoc3D architecture, which includes voxelization of `P_t`, a feature pyramid network for per-voxel local features, and generalized-mean pooling to derive the 256-dimensional embedding vector `E_t`.
- **Training**: Pre-trained weights from LIDAR datasets are fine-tuned using TACTO-generated data from the 40 YCB objects. Training utilizes a contrastive triplet loss, accumulating positive and negative 3D geometries based on pose supervision.
- **Tactile Codebook**: For each known object, a tactile codebook `C_o` is pre-computed. This involves densely sampling 50,000 randomized sensor poses on the object's mesh. For each sampled pose, a tactile image is simulated using TACTO, processed by the TDN and TCN to obtain its corresponding tactile code `E_[m]`. A KD-Tree is constructed from these pose-code pairs for efficient nearest-neighbor search, allowing fast lookup of codes for candidate poses within the particle filter.

#### 3.3. Filtering over Posed Touch (Particle Filter)

The core of MidasTouch is a nonparametric Monte-Carlo particle filter that approximates the sensor's pose posterior distribution as a set of weighted particles.
- **Initialization**: The filter begins with an initial distribution `X_0` sampled from a coarse prior, which can optionally be sourced from visual measurements. This prior is intentionally uninformative, scattering particles widely across the object surface (e.g., 3σ_trans equals mesh diagonal, 3σ_rot equals 180°). Particles are then projected onto the nearest point on the object surface. A total of 50,000 particles (`N_0`) are initially used.
- **Motion Model**: Particles `x_[i]_t` are propagated forward based on noisy relative pose predictions (`∆p_t`) derived from robot kinematics or odometry. The update is performed by adding Gaussian noise (`N(∆p_t, Σ_∆p)`) to the previous particle pose (`x_[i]_(t-1)`).
- **Measurement Update**: Particle weights `w_[i]_t` are updated by comparing the current tactile code `E_t` (from the TCN) with the tactile code associated with each particle's pose (`C_o(x_[i]_t)`) in the pre-computed codebook. This comparison is based on cosine distance, and the resulting scores are normalized using a softmax function to produce weights ``.
- **Particle Resampling**: A new set of particles `X_t` is sampled from the current distribution with probabilities proportional to their weights, typically using low-variance resampling.
- **Hypothesis Clustering**: To provide distinct pose estimations, particles are hierarchically clustered in R^3 using DB-SCAN. The average position and quaternion within each cluster form a set of pose hypotheses `h_t`.
- **Implementation Details**: The system dynamically adjusts the number of particles based on the filter's convergence (standard deviation of hypotheses), injecting or removing particles while ensuring a minimum count of 1,000. Particles that drift beyond a 2mm threshold from the object surface are pruned (their weights set to zero). For real-world data, exponential smoothing is applied to heightmaps, and resampling frequency is reduced to every five iterations to enhance stability against noisy measurements.

#### 3.4. The YCB-Slide Dataset

The researchers released the YCB-Slide dataset to support further research. It includes real-world and simulated DIGIT sliding interactions with 10 standard YCB objects.
- **Simulated Data**: 50 trajectories (5 per object) of approximately 0.5m length, generated by TACTO, with noisy sensor poses.
- **Real-world Data**: 50 trajectories (5 per object), recorded at 30Hz for 60 seconds using a handheld DIGIT sensor on objects clamped in a vise. Ground-truth poses are provided by an OptiTrack motion capture system, with minor human error adjustments through post-processing.
- **Content**: DIGIT images, sensor poses, ground-truth mesh models, and ground-truth heightmaps + contact masks (for simulation data).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The MidasTouch system was evaluated in both simulated and real-world environments using the YCB-Slide dataset.

#### 4.1. Single-Touch Localization Performance

- Initial experiments demonstrated that tactile codes are effective proxies for pose prediction. When querying the tactile codebook with a single touch, the system performed significantly better than random touches across all tested objects.
- Objects with salient geometric features (e.g., tools like `power_drill`, `scissors`) were easier to localize, exhibiting lower pose errors. In contrast, objects with symmetric or regular structures (e.g., `sugar_box`, `mustard_bottle`, `mug`) exhibited larger "long-tail errors" due to inherent ambiguities.
- A comparison in Appendix B showed that the proposed 3D tactile codes yielded lower average normalized pose error (0.473) compared to image embeddings from prior work (0.546), while also being more compact.

#### 4.2. Simulated Global Localization Results

- Across 500 simulated trials, the particle filter demonstrated convergence to the most-likely pose hypothesis.
- The average final pose errors were 0.74 cm in translation and 9.43° in rotation. These errors generally correlated with the single-touch distinctiveness of the objects.
- The filter effectively managed multi-modal pose distributions, especially for symmetric objects, where multiple hypotheses could concurrently explain the observed tactile sequence before further unique features were encountered.
- An ablation study on initial pose uncertainty indicated that tighter priors (simulating better visual priors) led to lower average trajectory errors and reduced the prevalence of outliers. The system, however, was designed to function with very uninformative initial priors.

#### 4.3. Real-world Global Localization Results

- In real-world trials (500 runs), the system exhibited similar behavior, with hypotheses converging to the ground-truth pose, albeit with higher overall errors compared to simulation due to noisier tactile heightmaps. A tighter initial pose prior (β=0.5) was used to mitigate particle depletion.
- The averaged final pose errors were 1.97 cm in translation and 21.48° in rotation.
- Similar to simulation, tools and intricate objects were easier to localize, while symmetric objects presented more challenges.
- A notable failure mode occurred with the `baseball` object, where the system consistently failed to localize. This was attributed to a lack of sufficiently distinct geometry discernible from real tactile images, effectively presenting as a "featureless sphere."
- The full MidasTouch system operated at an average rate of 10Hz.

#### 4.4. Closest Hypothesis Error

- An additional metric, `min_cluster`, which calculated RMSE to the closest hypothesis to ground truth (assuming ground truth is known), showed significantly lower errors. In simulation, this was 0.28 cm and 2.03°, and in real-world experiments, 1.11 cm and 10.76°. This indicates that while the overall particle distribution might be spread across multiple modes, the correct pose is frequently captured within one of the distinct hypotheses maintained by the filter.

#### 4.5. Impact of Contact Patch Area

- A study on simulated `power_drill` trajectories demonstrated a correlation between larger average contact area and lower final pose error. Maintaining forceful contact, which increases the surface area impinging the sensor, provides more 3D surface geometry, leading to improved tracking accuracy.

#### 4.6. Experiments on Small Parts

- MidasTouch was also tested on smaller McMaster-Carr objects (`cotter_pin`, `eyebolt`, `steel_nail`). The system achieved higher accuracy (approx. 4 mm translation, 5° rotation) with shorter trajectories and fewer particles. This improved performance was attributed to the larger relative field-of-view provided by the sensor on smaller objects.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The MidasTouch framework represents a significant contribution to tactile perception and robotics by addressing the fundamental problem of global object localization using only vision-based tactile sensing during dynamic sliding interactions.

- **Novelty in Tactile Perception**: The work introduces a novel approach by integrating learned tactile embeddings, derived from local 3D geometry, within a nonparametric particle filter. This is a distinction from prior methods that often rely on image-based embeddings or struggle with multi-modal pose distributions.
- **Resolution of Ambiguity**: By leveraging a particle filter to disambiguate local tactile signals over extended sliding interactions, the system effectively overcomes the inherent ambiguity of single-touch localization, a long-standing challenge in tactile sensing. This "haptic apprehension" strategy enhances the robustness of pose estimation.
- **Reduced Dependency on Vision**: The ability to achieve global localization without requiring visual priors provides an alternative for scenarios where vision is occluded, compromised by object properties (e.g., transparency, specularity), or unavailable. This expands the operational envelope for robots engaged in contact-rich tasks.
- **Enabling Advanced Robotic Tasks**: Accurate and online global pose estimation is critical for a range of sophisticated manipulation tasks, including in-hand object reorientation, precise assembly, and adaptive sliding manipulation. MidasTouch can inform downstream planning and control modules by providing a continuous, multi-modal pose distribution.
- **Open-Source Contribution and Benchmarking**: The release of MidasTouch and the YCB-Slide dataset is a substantial contribution to the research community. The YCB-Slide dataset, encompassing both real-world and simulated interactions with ground truth, establishes a much-needed benchmark for tactile localization, mapping, object understanding, and learning dynamics models, thereby facilitating and accelerating future research in this area.
- **Foundation for Future Research**: The identified limitations also point to promising avenues for future work. These include extending the framework to dynamic objects (e.g., by incorporating visual measurements or local in-hand tracking), handling unknown object models (requiring on-the-fly reconstruction and codebook generation), scaling to multi-contact configurations, and modeling interactions with deformable or articulated objects. Furthermore, exploring end-to-end learning with differentiable filters could enhance real-world performance.
