# Imitation from Heterogeneous Demonstrations using Grounded Latent-Action World Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the University of Oxford developed Grounded Latent-Action World Models (GLAM), a framework to unify heterogeneous demonstration data into a shared, control-aware latent action space for robotic imitation learning. GLAM-aligned policies achieved up to +69% higher success rates on complex manipulation tasks and matched baseline performance using five times less target-robot data.

## Problem

- High cost and scarcity of target-robot specific demonstration data, hindering the scalability and deployment of visuomotor policies.
- Challenges in effectively leveraging abundant, cheaper heterogeneous data sources (e.g., handheld devices, simulations, human videos) due to action space mismatches, missing action labels, and visual domain gaps.
- Prior methods for cross-source data integration often rely on heuristic or hand-engineered alignment techniques that lack robustness and scalability.

## Method

- ****GLAM** Pretraining:** Learns a shared, ==source-invariant latent action space== via two coupled generative models: a Heterogeneous Generative Model (trained on all data with an Inverse Dynamics Model for latent action inference) and a Target Generative Model (trained on target data with an action encoder for control-aware latent actions).
- **Asymmetric Alignment Constraint:** An asymmetric KL divergence constraint aligns the Inverse Dynamics Model (IDM) posterior with the action encoder posterior on target data, allowing the IDM to absorb control semantics while maintaining source-invariance.
- **World-Model-Aligned Imitation Learning:** The pretrained GLAM's IDM relabels all heterogeneous data with inferred latent actions, which are then used to train a downstream behavioral cloning policy mapping observations to these source-agnostic, executable latent actions.

## Results

- GLAM-aligned policies consistently outperformed baselines across five manipulation tasks, achieving an average of **+35%** improvement in real-world tasks and up to **+69%** on a complex bimanual stacking task.
- The framework demonstrated improved data efficiency, matching the performance of a vanilla behavioral cloning baseline using five times fewer target-robot demonstrations.
- Auxiliary, unlabelled data effectively substituted for expensive target-robot teleoperation, with increasing amounts of auxiliary data yielding performance gains comparable to increasing target-robot data.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Action representations should be grounded in their predicted environmental effects, not their source, enabling a principled, learning-based approach to integrate heterogeneous data.
> - A frozen latent-action world model can function as a 'latent-action anchor' to provide per-step supervision, converting unlabelled auxiliary data into actionable behavioral cloning signals.
> - Utilizing object-level segmentation masks as observations further grounds latent actions in manipulated object dynamics, enhancing transferability and data efficiency across diverse embodiments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Modern imitation learning has facilitated the development of visuomotor manipulation policies; however, the generalisation and stability of these policies are often constrained by the volume and quality of demonstration data required. Collecting such data, particularly on target robots via teleoperation, is a time-consuming and expensive process, which presents a bottleneck for scaling policies to a wider range of real-world tasks.

To mitigate this dependence on costly target-robot data, current research explores leveraging alternative, more abundant, and cheaper data sources. These include trajectories from portable handheld devices, simulated environments, or human videos scraped from the internet. A primary challenge with these heterogeneous data sources is the mismatch in action spaces, the frequent absence of explicit action labels, and significant visual variations between embodiments and scenes that may not directly reflect underlying task semantics.

Prior efforts to integrate heterogeneous data often rely on heuristic or hand-engineered alignment techniques. For instance, co-training methods that combine simulated and real data frequently employ empirically tuned mixing ratios, source-specific tokenizers, or specific embodiment alignment strategies. These approaches typically address cross-source integration as a tuning problem rather than a fundamental learning problem.

This paper positions itself within the landscape of efforts to improve data efficiency and generalisation in imitation learning by proposing a principled approach to leverage heterogeneous data. It departs from heuristic alignment by positing that action representations should be grounded in their predicted environmental effects, irrespective of their origin. This perspective frames cross-source integration as a representation-learning challenge, using environment transitions as a shared, physically grounded supervisory signal.

The work also relates to world models and latent action models. World models are commonly used in robot learning as imagined simulators for policy optimization or as planners for trajectory optimization. However, these applications often necessitate substantial online interaction or extensive pretraining to ensure reliable rollouts for control. This paper utilizes a world model differently, as a "frozen latent-action anchor" that provides per-step labels, thereby circumventing the extensive online interaction requirements of reinforcement learning and the autoregressive drift challenges in trajectory optimization that contribute to high data demands.

Within the domain of latent action models (LAMs), prior work has focused on extracting pseudo-action labels from inter-frame transitions. Discrete latent approaches, such as LAPA, tokenize transitions for downstream policy learning, often requiring large-scale Vision-Language Model (VLM) finetuning. Continuous latent approaches, like CLAM, directly supervise downstream policies via regression, but are typically designed for single-embodiment settings. This research distinguishes itself from these LAMs by (i) developing a single Inverse Dynamics Model (IDM) trained across both target and auxiliary data to achieve a source-invariant latent action space, (ii) integrating a target-side action encoder aligned with the IDM to inject control semantics into the latent space, avoiding post-hoc grounding of pixel-reconstruction latents, and (iii) functioning as a frozen latent-action anchor to convert unlabelled auxiliary data into Behavioural Cloning (BC) supervision without requiring large-scale pretraining.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to enhance the data efficiency and generalisation capabilities of visuomotor policies in imitation learning by effectively leveraging heterogeneous demonstration data. The motivation stems from several critical limitations in current imitation learning paradigms:

1. **High Cost and Scarcity of Target-Robot Data:** Developing competent visuomotor policies typically necessitates large volumes (hundreds to thousands) of teleoperated trajectories collected directly on the target robot. This data acquisition process is inherently slow and expensive, forming a primary bottleneck for deploying advanced manipulation skills across a wide spectrum of real-world tasks.
2. **Abundance of Underutilized Auxiliary Data:** Concurrently, there is an increasing availability of alternative, cheaper data sources, such as demonstrations collected with portable handheld devices, simulations, or human videos. While these sources are abundant and cost-effective, their utility is limited by fundamental mismatches with target-robot data.
3. **Challenges with Heterogeneous Data Integration:** Integrating heterogeneous data presents several technical hurdles:
 - **Disparate Action Spaces:** Different data sources may operate with distinct action spaces (e.g., a floating handheld gripper versus a fixed-base robot arm).
 - **Absence of Action Labels:** Auxiliary data often lacks explicit action labels, which are crucial for traditional behavioural cloning.
 - **Visual Domain Gaps:** Substantial visual variations exist across embodiments and scenes (e.g., real-world vs. simulation), which can obscure the underlying task mechanics.
 - **Limitations of Existing Alignment Techniques:** Previous methods for bridging these gaps often rely on heuristic, hand-engineered, or empirically tuned alignment recipes that are not robust or scalable. These approaches tend to treat cross-source integration as a parameter tuning problem rather than a learning problem.

To address these motivations, the authors propose a core principle: **action representations should be grounded in their effect on the environment, not their source.** That is, actions that produce an equivalent effect on the manipulated object or environment should share a common representation, regardless of whether they originate from different embodiments, real environments, or simulated ones. This reframes cross-source data integration as a problem of learning a unified, physically grounded representation of actions.

The specific objectives derived from this motivation are:
- To instantiate this grounding principle by developing a **Grounded Latent-Action World Model (GLAM)**, comprising a pair of generative models. This model aims to learn a shared latent action space that is grounded by consistently predicting future observations across diverse data sources.
- To align actions inferred from auxiliary data (without labels) with actions from target data (with labels) within this unified latent space, thereby making the abundant auxiliary data usable for policy supervision.
- To utilize this aligned latent action space as a means to relabel all available heterogeneous data with source-agnostic, control-aware latent actions.
- To train downstream behavioural cloning (BC) policies using these relabeled data, mapping observations to latent actions and subsequently decoding them into executable robot actions.
- To empirically validate that GLAM successfully learns an aligned latent action space that facilitates effective action transfer across data sources, including those without explicit action labels.
- To demonstrate that GLAM-aligned policies achieve improved task success rates compared to baseline methods, particularly in data-scarce scenarios across various manipulation tasks in both simulation and the real world.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology involves a two-stage pipeline for learning visuomotor policies from heterogeneous demonstrations: GLAM Pretraining (Stage 1) and World-Model-Aligned Imitation Learning (Stage 2).

**Stage 1: GLAM Pretraining (World Model)**

This stage focuses on learning a shared, control-aware latent action space. Actions are treated as a continuous latent variable, $z_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$. The core of GLAM consists of two coupled generative models:

1. **Heterogeneous Generative Model (Equation 1):** This model is trained on the combined heterogeneous dataset $D = D_{tar} \cup D_{aux}$. $D_{tar}$ includes observations, proprioceptive states, and action labels ($\{(o_t, s_t, a_t)\}_t^T$), while $D_{aux}$ only contains observations ($\{o_t\}_t^T$). The model predicts future observations given current latent state ($x_t$) and latent action ($z_t$). It uses a visual encoder $q_\psi(x_t|o_t)$ to map observations to a latent state, and an Inverse Dynamics Model (IDM) $q_\phi(z_t|x_t, x_{t+1})$ to infer latent actions from observed state transitions. The critical design choice is the use of a *shared* IDM and a *shared* forward dynamics model $p_\theta(x_{t+1}|x_t, z_t)$ across both target and auxiliary datasets. This ensures that latent actions $z_t$ are inferred and used to predict transitions in a unified, source-invariant manner, regardless of the data source. This model is optimized using an Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO) objective, $J_H$.

2. **Target Generative Model (Equation 2):** This model is exclusively trained on the target dataset $D_{tar}$ and leverages the robot state $s_t$ and action labels $a_t$. It learns an action encoder $q_\eta(z_t|s_t, a_t)$ that infers latent actions from executable robot actions and proprioceptive states. This encoder, alongside a dynamics model $p_\theta(s_{t+1}|s_t, z_t)$ for proprioceptive states, injects control-awareness into the latent action space. An additional action reconstruction term, $\log p_\theta(a_t|z_t)$, is applied only to this branch, ensuring that the latent action $z_t$ can be decoded back into executable robot actions. This prevents the source-invariant IDM from being influenced by target-specific control details during its inference. This model is optimized using an ELBO objective, $J_T$.

3. **Asymmetric Alignment Constraint (Equation 5):** To bridge these two generative models and their respective approximate posteriors, an asymmetric Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence constraint, $C_{KL}$, is introduced. This constraint encourages the IDM posterior $q_\phi(z_t|x_t, x_{t+1})$ to align with the action encoder posterior $q_\eta(z_t|s_t, a_t)$ when applied to the target dataset $D_{tar}$. The "stop-gradient" operation on $q_\eta$ ensures that the action encoder, which has access to privileged ground-truth action labels, guides the IDM without being distorted by the potentially more ambiguous IDM signal. This allows the IDM to absorb executable-action semantics from target transitions and subsequently transfer them to auxiliary ones, preserving its source-invariance.

The overall training objective for GLAM is a joint minimization of the two negative ELBO losses and the alignment constraint: $L(\phi, \eta, \psi, \theta, D, D_{tar}) = -J_H(D) - J_T(D_{tar}) + \lambda C_{KL}(D_{tar})$, where $\lambda$ is a weighting factor.

**Object Masks as Observations (Variant):** A variant of GLAM is explored where binary segmentation masks of the manipulated object, extracted by an off-the-shelf segmentation model, are used as observations instead of raw RGB frames. This is hypothesized to further ground latent actions in the dynamics of the manipulated object.

**Stage 2: World-Model-Aligned Imitation Learning**

Once GLAM is pretrained, it serves as a frozen "latent-action anchor" for downstream imitation learning:

1. **Data Relabeling:** The pretrained GLAM's IDM is used to relabel every transition in the entire dataset ($D_{tar} \cup D_{aux}$) with its inferred latent action, $z_t \leftarrow \mu_{IDM_\phi}(x_t, x_{t+1})$. This process generates a large dataset of observation-latent action pairs, effectively converting unlabelled auxiliary data into actionable supervision signals.

2. **Downstream Behavioural Cloning (BC) Policy:** A BC policy is then trained to map observations to these newly generated latent actions.
 - **Policy Architecture:** The policy takes raw RGB images from two camera views (processed by a jointly trained DINOv2 backbone and a learned projector) concatenated with the proprioceptive state $s_t$ as input.
 - **Policy Type:** A lightweight, two-step regression policy called MIP (Multi-step Iterative Policy) is adopted to predict a chunk of latent actions, $\hat{z}_{t:t+H}$.
 - **Action Decoding:** These predicted latent actions are then decoded into executable robot actions $\hat{a}_{t:t+H}$ using a separate action decoder $h_a_\omega$.
 - **Training Objective (Equation 8):** The policy is trained end-to-end on $D_{tar} \cup D_{aux}$ with an objective function that includes two terms: one for regressing predicted latent actions towards the relabeled latent actions (from all data) and another for regressing predicted executable actions towards ground-truth executable actions (from target data only).

**Experimental Setup:** The methodology is validated across five manipulation tasks (three real-world: lifting, pick-and-place, knock-down; two simulated: 2-cube stacking, 3-cube stacking). Real-world tasks use a Kinova arm (target) and simulated UMI gripper (auxiliary). Simulated tasks use a Kinova model (target) and UMI gripper (auxiliary). Datasets consist of 100 target demonstrations and 400 auxiliary demonstrations. Baselines include standard MIP, CLAM (continuous latent action model), LAPA (VQ-VAE based latent action model), and their object-masked variants, all implemented with consistent network architectures and downstream policies for fair comparison.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental validation addressed two primary questions: whether GLAM learns a consistent shared latent action space across data sources, and whether auxiliary, unlabelled data can augment training datasets to improve BC performance.

#### 4.1. Cross-Source Transfer through a Shared Latent Action Space

- **Qualitative Validation:** Qualitative experiments (Figure 2 and Appendix A) demonstrate that GLAM successfully learns a shared latent action space that unifies different data sources. Latent actions inferred by the IDM from unlabelled auxiliary data (UMI gripper in simulation) and from real-world target data (Kinova arm) can be decoded by an action decoder trained *only* on target-robot actions (Kinova arm in simulation). When these decoded actions are replayed open-loop on the Kinova-sim robot, they reproduce the original manipulation trajectory effectively. This indicates robust cross-embodiment, in-distribution, and sim-real transfer capabilities of the learned latent action space.
- **Design Choices Impact:** The authors highlight that the asymmetric alignment constraint (Equation 5) and the selective application of the action-reconstruction term were crucial. Removing the asymmetric alignment led to the collapse of posteriors and failure of UMI latent transfer. Attaching the action-reconstruction term to the IDM (as in some prior works) also hindered transfer by injecting source-specific control particularities into the source-invariant IDM.

#### 4.2. World-Model Anchoring Enables BC to Generalize from Heterogeneous Data

- **Superior Performance of GLAM-aligned Policies:** GLAM(-O) policies consistently outperformed all baseline methods across all five manipulation tasks (three real-world, two simulated), as detailed in Figure 3.
 - On real-world tasks, GLAM-O achieved an average of +35% improvement over the best baseline.
 - On simulated tasks, it showed +44% on stack-two and a substantial +69% on the bimanual stack-three task. Notably, GLAM(-O) were the only methods to achieve non-trivial success on stack-three (72.7% versus ≤ 4% for baselines), a task typically requiring hundreds to thousands of demonstrations.
 - Statistical tests confirmed significant directional improvements across all tasks.
- **Limitations of Baselines:** Baselines like MIP and CLAM, designed for single-source learning, could not effectively leverage auxiliary data, often overfitting to the limited target data. LAPA, while trained on both sources, relied purely on visual reconstruction for latent action supervision, which proved insufficient for reliable cross-source transfer in this data-scarce setting. This underscores the effectiveness of GLAM’s control-aware and source-invariant latent space.
- **Benefits of Object-Mask Observations:** Utilizing binary segmentation masks of the manipulated object (GLAM-O) consistently improved task performance compared to using raw RGB input (GLAM) across all five tasks (Figure 3). Significant improvements were observed in tasks like knock-down (+25%) and bimanual stack-three (+16%). This supports the hypothesis that grounding latent actions at the object level enhances transfer across datasets.
- **Heterogeneous Data as a Substitute for Target-Robot Teleoperation:**
 - **Data Efficiency:** GLAM-O demonstrated improved data efficiency. It matched the performance of a vanilla BC baseline (MIP) that used five times more target-robot demonstrations (Figure 4a).
 - **Scalability with Auxiliary Data:** For GLAM-O, increasing the amount of auxiliary, unlabelled UMI data yielded performance gains comparable, trajectory-for-trajectory, to increasing the amount of target-robot Kinova data (Figure 4b). This indicates that auxiliary data can serve as a viable substitute for expensive target teleoperation when integrated via the GLAM framework.
 - **Latent Policy Efficiency:** The latent policies predicting GLAM latent actions were observed to be more data-efficient than vanilla BC policies, possibly due to GLAM's access to more diverse data during pretraining, leading to a smoother and more informative action space.
- **Motion Smoothness Corroboration (Appendix B):** Analysis of end-effector motion smoothness using jerk-based and FFT-based metrics (Figure 6) mirrored the data-efficiency trends. GLAM-O produced smoother motions with less target data, and auxiliary data contributed to motion smoothness similarly to target data. Comparisons of joint-action trajectories (Figure 7) showed GLAM-O policies following expert trends more closely and resulting in smoother, successful manipulation compared to baseline failures.
- **Scalability to Multiple Data Sources (Appendix C):** Augmenting the GLAM training mix with a third auxiliary data source (Kinova-sim trajectories) maintained or improved performance on all three real-robot tasks without requiring any architectural modifications (Table 1). This supports GLAM's claim of source-invariance and its potential to integrate even more diverse data sources.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces a methodology, Grounded Latent-Action World Models (GLAM), that addresses a fundamental challenge in robotic imitation learning: the dependence on large volumes of expensive, target-robot specific demonstration data. The findings indicate several areas of significance and potential impact:

1. **Alleviating Data Scarcity in Robotic Learning:** By demonstrating that abundant, unlabelled, and heterogeneous data can effectively substitute for costly target-robot teleoperation, GLAM provides a pathway to mitigate the data bottleneck in robotic manipulation. This could significantly reduce the resources and time required to develop and deploy capable robot policies, making advanced manipulation skills more accessible.

2. **Principled Cross-Source Data Integration:** The core principle of grounding action representations in their environmental effects, rather than their source, offers a more robust and scalable approach to integrating diverse data. This moves beyond heuristic alignment techniques prevalent in prior work, providing a learning-based solution to cross-domain generalization.

3. **Enhanced Generalisation and Robustness:** The observed performance improvements, particularly on complex tasks and in data-scarce settings, suggest that GLAM-aligned policies possess superior generalisation capabilities and stability. This is crucial for real-world robotic applications where policies need to perform reliably under varied conditions.

4. **Novel Application of World Models:** This work recontextualizes the application of world models in robot learning. Instead of using them for extensive imagined rollouts or trajectory optimization, GLAM leverages a frozen world model as a "latent-action anchor" for per-step supervision. This approach avoids the computational demands and potential for autoregressive drift associated with traditional world model uses, making it efficient for imitation learning from static datasets.

5. **Foundation for Future Research Directions:** The limitations outlined by the authors also highlight promising avenues for future work:
 - **Wider Generalisation to "In-the-Wild" Data:** Extending the framework to truly unconstrained web videos, which feature significant viewpoint and scene drift, could unlock vast amounts of training data.
 - **Adaptation to Diverse Embodiments:** Investigating the framework's applicability to morphologically distinct end-effectors, such as multi-fingered or dexterous hands, could broaden its utility across robot platforms. Bridging with human-hand demonstrations is a potential extension.
 - **Shared Skill Vocabularies:** Exploring how GLAM can learn a shared skill vocabulary across many tasks, enabling the transfer of primitive skills, could lead to more versatile and adaptable robotic systems.
 - **Multi-Modal Sensing Integration:** The potential to use GLAM as a multi-modal bridge, integrating tactile or force signals from instrumented target rollouts to enrich vision-only auxiliary data, could enhance policies for contact-rich manipulation tasks without explicit modality matching across sources.

In summary, GLAM represents a step toward more data-efficient and generalizable robotic manipulation by providing a principled method for unifying heterogeneous demonstrations into a shared, control-aware latent action space. This approach has the potential to accelerate the development and deployment of intelligent robotic systems in diverse real-world scenarios.
