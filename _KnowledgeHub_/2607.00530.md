# From Technical Metrics to User Perception: A User Study of a Multimodal Human-Robot Interaction System for Object Detection and Grasping

> [!summary] Summary
> This study explores whether objective technical improvements in a multimodal human-robot interaction system translate into measurable differences in user perception. Findings show that a 15-percentage-point gain in task success (from 75% to 90%) led to users perceiving the improved system as faster, more reliable, and more competent, with a strong preference for it.

## Problem

- Technical advancements in Human-Robot Interaction (HRI) systems often rely on objective metrics, without clear validation of their impact on user perception.
- A gap exists in understanding if quantitative improvements in robot performance are actually discernible or valued by human users during interaction.
- The lack of user-centered evaluation makes it difficult to confirm if technical gains lead to a more intuitive, reliable, or satisfying user experience.

## Method

- A ==within-subject user study== was conducted, comparing a baseline multimodal HRI system with a version technically improved through prior ablation studies.
- The study involved 24 participants interacting with both systems in a ==spoken-command object grasping== task, using a consistent hardware and software framework.
- User perception was measured through ==7-point Likert scales== for speed, reliability, and overall competence, alongside a direct preference question.

## Results

- **70.83%** of participants preferred the improved system over the baseline, with this preference being statistically significant (**p=0.043**).
- The improved system received significantly higher ratings for perceived speed (M=**5.69** vs. **4.61**), reliability (M=**4.89** vs. **3.95**), and overall competence/fluency (M=**5.01** vs. **3.76**), all with **p<0.001**.
- All three hypotheses were supported, demonstrating that previously identified technical improvements in visual grounding and language understanding translated into clear subjective user benefits.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A quantifiable technical improvement, specifically a 15-percentage-point increase in task success, was sufficient to generate consistent and measurable differences in user perception.
> - Users form holistic judgments of robot capability, with perceived competence and fluency showing the most pronounced subjective gain from local improvements.
> - Even with significant objective improvements, user perceptions of reliability may not reach scale ceilings, indicating high user expectations for system dependability.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of Human–Robot Interaction (HRI) is evolving from rigid, task-specific automation towards systems that can interpret human intent, adapt to context, and interact naturally with non-expert users. This shift is driven by the increasing integration of robots into diverse applications such as healthcare, domestic assistance, education, logistics, and collaborative manufacturing, where robots need to operate safely, transparently, and intuitively alongside humans. A central challenge in modern HRI is the accurate inference of human intention, which influences system efficiency, operational safety, and user satisfaction, particularly in safety-sensitive or close-proximity scenarios. The complexity further increases when robots must handle multimodal input, such as spoken instructions, visual context, and physical constraints simultaneously, requiring a balance of perception, language understanding, and control under uncertainty.

Recent advancements in foundation models, including large language models (LLMs) like LLaMA and GPT-4, have provided enhanced semantic parsing and structured reasoning capabilities. Vision–language models (VLMs) extend these capabilities to visual grounding, enabling joint reasoning over images and text. This has fueled research in robot navigation, manipulation, and interactive assistance, leading to interfaces that combine language and visual cues. While multimodal interaction has been shown to improve robot transparency and user acceptance, the HRI literature also cautions that powerful generative models alone are often insufficient. They can be brittle or misaligned with physical execution without grounding in the real world and deterministic downstream logic. Consequently, many effective robotic systems integrate learned foundation models with explicit perception and control modules.

This paper is presented as the third in a series of studies by the authors on multimodal spoken-command object manipulation. The initial work introduced a hybrid HRI pipeline combining speech recognition (Whisper), open-vocabulary visual perception (Florence-2), natural language understanding (LLaMA 3.1), and adaptive motion control (Interval Type-2 Fuzzy Logic Controller, IT2FLS). This baseline system demonstrated feasibility in a tabletop setting, achieving a 75% end-to-end task success rate but identified bottlenecks in object perception, robot execution, and issues in action extraction and motion generation. A subsequent ablation study systematically compared alternative modules within this framework, identifying a configuration that substantially improved technical performance. Specifically, replacing Florence-2 with Grounding DINO + SAM for perception and LLaMA 3.1 with Qwen 3.5 9B for action extraction resulted in a 90% success rate, with perception identified as the most influential factor.

The current paper addresses a critical gap left by these technical evaluations: whether such objective performance improvements are actually perceptible to human users during live interaction. It acknowledges that gains in technical metrics like success rate or latency do not automatically translate into user-detectable differences, particularly for short tasks or stable robot behaviors. Therefore, this work shifts the evaluation focus from purely technical benchmarking to assessing user perception of performance differences, thereby complementing existing evaluation paradigms in robotic manipulation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to investigate whether a quantifiable technical improvement in an end-to-end human–robot interaction system – specifically, a 15-percentage-point gain in task success (from 75% to 90%) – is sufficient to produce consistent and measurable differences in user perception. This study aims to determine if enhancements identified through technical ablation studies are meaningful from a human interaction standpoint, rather than solely from a benchmark perspective.

The motivation for this investigation stems from the observation that progress in the technical performance of HRI systems does not automatically guarantee a corresponding improvement in the user experience. While researchers often focus on objective metrics like success rates, accuracy, or latency, it is crucial to understand if these gains are salient and valued by human users interacting directly with the system. Without user-centered evaluation, it is challenging to ascertain whether technical advancements contribute to a more intuitive, reliable, or satisfying interaction.

The paper outlines three specific research questions guiding the user study:
1. Can participants reliably distinguish between the baseline and the improved HRI system after interacting with both in the same task?
2. Do participants perceive differences in key performance aspects such as speed, reliability, fluency, and overall competence between the two systems?
3. Does the system preferred by participants align with the configuration that demonstrated superior objective performance in prior technical evaluations?

Based on the findings from the previous technical studies, the authors formulated three hypotheses:
- **H1 (Perceived Speed):** Participants will rate the improved system as faster or more responsive than the baseline. This is based on the expectation that the improved configuration reduces delays in perception and control stages.
- **H2 (Perceived Reliability):** Participants will rate the improved system as more reliable and more likely to behave correctly during the task. This hypothesis is derived from the higher task success rate achieved by the improved system in the ablation study.
- **H3 (Overall Competence and Fluency/Preference):** Participants will judge the improved system as smoother and more competent overall, and will express a preference for it in a final choice question. This reflects the anticipated combined effect of better perception, more robust control, and stronger language grounding.

The contributions of this paper are threefold: first, it introduces a focused within-subject user study directly comparing the baseline multimodal manipulation system with its technically improved counterpart. Second, it analyzes whether participants can distinguish between the systems based on perceived speed, reliability, smoothness, and overall quality, and if these subjective judgments correlate with objective performance data. Third, it discusses the broader implications for HRI system evaluation, advocating for a joint assessment of technical metrics and user perception as complementary dimensions.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The study employed a within-subject design to compare two configurations of a multimodal human–robot interaction system for tabletop object grasping. Each of 24 participants interacted with both the baseline and the improved system under identical task conditions.

#### 3.1. Hardware Platform and Fixed Infrastructure

To isolate the effects of software changes, the study maintained a constant hardware platform and experimental setup:
- **Robot:** Dobot Magician robotic arm equipped with a suction-based end-effector.
- **Visual Sensing:** Intel RealSense D435i RGB-D camera.
- **Audio Input:** Samsung Buds2 wireless earbuds for spoken commands.
- **Computation:** Local processing on an Intel Core i9-14900HX laptop with an NVIDIA RTX 4070 GPU.
- **Workspace:** Fixed camera viewpoint, robot base pose, tabletop geometry, lighting conditions, and calibration procedure.
- **Software Framework:** The same graphical user interface, logging logic, and safety checks were used for both system configurations.

#### 3.2. Task Definition

The task involved spoken-command object grasping in a controlled tabletop environment. Participants issued natural-language requests for the robot to pick up, move, or spatially relate printed fruit images on the table (e.g., "pick up the apple," "move the banana to the left of the orange"). The command structure and object vocabulary were consistent with previous studies. A trial was deemed successful only if the system correctly interpreted the command, grounded the target object, and completed the manipulation without errors or control failures.

#### 3.3. Robotic System Configurations

Both systems implemented the same high-level multimodal pipeline (speech capture, transcription, action extraction, visual grounding, motion execution). The distinction lay in the specific models used for language and visual processing:

- **Baseline System:**
 - **Wake-word recognition:** Audio Spectrogram Transformer.
 - **Speech-to-text:** Whisper.
 - **Action Extraction (LLM):** LLaMA 3.1 7B, extracting structured command representations.
 - **Visual Grounding:** Florence-2 in open-vocabulary mode for object localization.
 - **Motion Control:** Interval Type-2 Fuzzy Logic Controller (IT2FLS) for execution and uncertainty handling.
 - **Prior Performance:** Achieved 75% end-to-end task success over 60 trials, with object detection and robot execution contributing most to latency, and action extraction and motion generation to failures.

- **Improved System:**
 - **Wake-word recognition & Speech-to-text:** Same as baseline (Audio Spectrogram Transformer, Whisper).
 - **Action Extraction (LLM):** Qwen 3.5 9B, identified as strongest in the ablation study for consistent structured outputs.
 - **Visual Grounding:** Grounding DINO + SAM, identified as the strongest perception pipeline in the ablation study.
 - **Motion Control:** Interval Type-2 Fuzzy Logic Controller (IT2FLS), retained as it remained the best controller in the ablation analysis.
 - **Prior Performance:** Achieved 90% end-to-end task success in the ablation study.

#### 3.4. Experimental Procedure

Each participant completed two runs with one system (e.g., "System A"), followed by a questionnaire. This procedure was then repeated with the other system ("System B"). The order of system presentation (A then B, or B then A) was randomized across participants to mitigate learning, fatigue, or expectation biases. The systems were presented neutrally as "System A" and "System B" without disclosing which was the baseline or improved version until after data collection. The entire session lasted approximately 30 minutes.

#### 3.5. Data Collection and Statistical Analysis

Participants provided subjective ratings on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree) for three constructs, each measured by two statements:
- **Perceived Speed:** "The system responded quickly to my commands," and "The system completed the task without unnecessary waiting or delay."
- **Perceived Reliability:** "The system selected the correct object reliably," and "The system behaved consistently during the task."
- **Overall Competence and Fluency:** "The system’s movements looked smooth and well coordinated," and "Overall, this system felt more capable for this task."
A final forced-choice question asked participants which system they preferred overall.

Statistical analysis involved:
- Calculating composite scores for each construct by averaging the two Likert items.
- Assessing normality of paired difference scores using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Given the non-rejection of normality for any construct (all W > 0.93, p > 0.05), a paired two-tailed t-test was used for the primary inferential analysis, as per the pre-specified sensitivity analysis.
- Reporting effect sizes using Cohen’s d_z for paired t-tests.
- Analyzing the final preference question with an exact binomial test against a 0.5 null proportion, with Cohen’s h for effect size.
- Applying Holm correction for multiple comparisons to control the family-wise error rate across the three construct-level comparisons.
- All hypothesis tests were two-tailed with a significance threshold of α = 0.05.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The user study involved 24 adult participants (mean age 24.6 years, SD = 4.36; 37.5% female). The findings are summarized as follows.

#### 4.1. Research Question 1: Ability to Distinguish the Two Systems

Participants demonstrated an ability to distinguish between the two system configurations. Seventeen out of 24 participants (70.83%) expressed an overall preference for the improved system. An exact two-tailed binomial test yielded a p-value of 0.043, which is statistically significant at the α = 0.05 level. The corresponding effect size was moderate (Cohen’s h = 0.43), with a 95% Clopper–Pearson confidence interval for the true proportion of [0.49, 0.87]. This outcome indicates that users could perceive a performance difference between the systems beyond random chance, providing support for the directional component of H3.

#### 4.2. Research Question 2: Perceived Performance Differences

For all three perceptual constructs, the improved system received significantly higher ratings than the baseline. Paired two-tailed t-tests were used, and p-values were Holm-corrected. Effect sizes are reported as Cohen's d_z.

- **Perceived Speed (H1):** The improved system was rated as substantially more responsive.
 - Baseline: M = 4.61, SD = 0.55
 - Improved: M = 5.69, SD = 0.61
 - Mean paired difference (Δ) = 1.08 scale points (95% CI: [0.83, 1.33]).
 - Effect Size: Very large (d_z = 1.85).
 - Statistical Significance: t(23) = 9.09, p_adj < 0.001. This finding confirms H1. The baseline's rating near the neutral point suggests its average completion time was noticeable as a delay, while the improved system was perceived as firmly responsive.

- **Perceived Reliability (H2):** The improved system was rated as significantly more reliable.
 - Baseline: M = 3.95, SD = 0.63
 - Improved: M = 4.89, SD = 0.59
 - Mean paired difference (Δ) = 0.94 scale points (95% CI: [0.68, 1.20]).
 - Effect Size: Large (d_z = 1.54).
 - Statistical Significance: t(23) = 7.54, p_adj < 0.001. This finding supports H2. The baseline was perceived as slightly unreliable (below midpoint), while the improved system, though better (above midpoint), did not reach the scale ceiling, which aligns with its 90% success rate (not 100% dependability).

- **Perceived Competence and Fluency (H3 - subjective dimension):** This construct showed the largest perceptual difference.
 - Baseline: M = 3.76, SD = 0.59
 - Improved: M = 5.01, SD = 0.64
 - Mean paired difference (Δ) = 1.25 scale points (95% CI: [0.99, 1.51]).
 - Effect Size: Very large (d_z = 2.03).
 - Statistical Significance: t(23) = 9.93, p_adj < 0.001. This strongly supports the subjective aspect of H3. The improved system was perceived as clearly more competent and fluent, indicating that users integrated improvements in visual grounding, action extraction, and control robustness into a holistic judgment.

#### 4.3. Research Question 3: Relationship Between Objective and Subjective Performance

The results indicate a strong alignment between the objective technical performance and subjective user perception. The improved system, which achieved a 90% task success rate in the ablation study (compared to 75% for the baseline), was preferred by a significant majority of participants (70.83%). Furthermore, it received significantly higher ratings across all three perceptual constructs (speed, reliability, competence/fluency), with large to very large effect sizes.

The 15-percentage-point gain in technical task success was demonstrably sufficient to generate consistent and measurable perceptual differences for users. Perceived competence and fluency exhibited the most pronounced subjective gain, suggesting that users could integrate various local improvements (e.g., fewer selection errors, more stable motion, reduced waiting) into a coherent global judgment of the robot's capability. While perceived reliability improved significantly, its absolute mean rating for the improved system (4.89) remained closer to the midpoint, indicating that users noticed the improvement but also still perceived some level of unreliability, consistent with the fact that even the best configuration still had occasional failures. This demonstrates the value of user studies in revealing nuanced perceptions that complement technical metrics.

**Summary of Findings:** All three pre-registered hypotheses were supported. Technical improvements previously identified through ablation studies translated into discernible and statistically significant differences in user perception across multiple dimensions during live interaction.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research offers several significant contributions and potential impacts, particularly for the evaluation and development of Human–Robot Interaction (HRI) systems.

#### 5.1. Methodological Implications for HRI Evaluation

The primary significance lies in underscoring that technical metrics, such as task success rates, per-stage accuracy, and completion times, while necessary for HRI system development, are insufficient on their own to fully assess an interaction system from a user's perspective. The study empirically demonstrates that objective performance gains can align with subjective user perception, but this alignment is not guaranteed and requires explicit validation. A 15-percentage-point increase in end-to-end task success was found to be a sufficient threshold for user perceptibility in this specific context. This finding advocates for the routine inclusion of user-centered evaluation, through methods such as subjective ratings, as a complementary step in validating improvements identified through technical ablation studies or benchmark comparisons. Relying solely on objective benchmarks risks developing systems that are technically superior but fail to deliver a perceptibly better or more satisfying user experience.

#### 5.2. Practical Implications for System Development

The study provides practical guidance for HRI system developers. By confirming that specific module upgrades (e.g., transitioning from LLaMA 3.1 to Qwen 3.5 9B for action extraction and from Florence-2 to Grounding DINO + SAM for visual grounding) lead to tangible user benefits, the research supports strategic investment in these areas. The results indicate that improvements in components like visual grounding and language understanding, which contribute to reduced failures and smoother operation, directly translate into user perceptions of increased speed, reliability, and overall competence. This can help prioritize development efforts towards components that not only improve benchmark scores but also enhance the quality of human interaction.

#### 5.3. Nuanced Understanding of User Perception

The research offers a nuanced understanding of user perception. While the improved system was clearly preferred and rated higher on all metrics, the reliability scores, even for the improved system, did not reach the highest end of the scale. This suggests that users, while appreciating improvements, still detect residual imperfections. This insight is crucial for understanding user expectations and for guiding future development toward higher levels of dependability and seamlessness required for real-world deployment, even after significant technical gains have been made. It highlights that users' threshold for "fully reliable" might be higher than current system capabilities, even at 90% objective success.

#### 5.4. Foundations for Future Research

The identified limitations of the study also lay a clear path for future research. The modest sample size, controlled laboratory environment, constrained object vocabulary, and brief interaction protocol mean that the conclusions are specific to this context. Future work can extend these findings by:
- Recruiting larger and more diverse participant populations, including individuals with varying levels of prior robotics experience.
- Designing longer interaction sessions and more complex instruction sets, incorporating multi-object manipulation or dynamic scene changes, to test system robustness and user acceptance over time.
- Expanding the evaluation framework beyond task-performance perception to include metrics such as user trust, perceived safety, cognitive workload, and long-term acceptance.
- Implementing longitudinal designs to assess whether initial perceptual advantages persist as users become more familiar with the systems and as novelty effects diminish.
- Enhancing ecological validity by using physically varied real-world objects instead of printed images, providing a more rigorous test for the visual grounding component, which was identified as a critical factor.

In conclusion, this work strengthens the argument for integrating user-centered methodologies into the standard evaluation pipeline for robotic systems, emphasizing that technical excellence and positive user experience are mutually dependent dimensions that should be jointly assessed for advancing HRI.
