# DataMIL: Selecting Data for Robot Imitation Learning with Datamodels

> [!summary] Summary
> DataMIL introduces a framework for selecting the most beneficial data from large, diverse datasets for robot imitation learning by using datamodels to estimate each data point's impact on target task performance. This method consistently outperforms heuristic-based selection strategies in both simulation and real-world robot tasks, achieving an average 61% success rate in real-world experiments while enabling cross-embodiment transfer.

## Problem

- Generalist robot policies, while trained on large datasets, frequently underperform on specific tasks requiring specialized skills.
- Selecting the most beneficial data for fine-tuning these policies from vast, diverse datasets is challenging, with existing heuristic methods (e.g., visual or state-action similarity) often failing to directly optimize for policy performance.
- Naive data combination strategies can degrade policy performance due to task-irrelevant information, distribution shifts between datasets, or variations in data quality.

## Method

- **DataMIL** introduces a method for selecting data for robot imitation learning by adapting the 'datamodels' framework, which predicts the influence of individual training data points on model performance.
- It uses a surrogate loss function, specifically validation loss on task-specific data, to estimate data point influence, thereby avoiding the need for expensive real-world policy rollouts during the selection process.
- The framework employs two main estimators for datamodels (regression and metagradient-based) and consists of three steps: datamodel estimation, selection of top-performing data points, and final policy training on the selected data combined with target task data.

## Results

- DataMIL consistently outperformed existing heuristic-based baselines in both simulated and real-world robot environments.
- On real robot tasks, DataMIL achieved an average success rate of **61%**, surpassing the next best method (STRAP) by 21 percentage points across diverse tasks and robot platforms.
- The framework effectively demonstrated cross-embodiment transfer, selecting data from one robot platform that improved performance on a different robot, and successfully scaled to larger policy networks like Octo using a metagradient-based estimator.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A surrogate loss function based on validation loss on task-specific data can effectively stand in for expensive real-world policy rollouts during data selection, accurately predicting data utility.
> - The datamodels framework can identify training data points that most improve policy performance on a target task, even selecting data from seemingly unrelated tasks or different robot embodiments, which is crucial for cross-embodiment transfer.
> - Performance-aware data selection, as implemented by DataMIL, consistently outperforms traditional heuristic-based methods, demonstrating that optimizing directly for policy improvement is more effective than relying on similarity metrics.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in this paper is situated within the contemporary landscape of robot learning, characterized by the emergence of large-scale "generalist policies" or "foundation policies" trained on extensive and diverse robotic datasets. This trend, inspired by successes in natural language processing (NLP) and computer vision (CV) with large models, aims to develop robust policies capable of performing a wide array of tasks across various environments and robot embodiments.

While these generalist policies demonstrate strong average performance across diverse tasks, a recognized limitation is their frequent underperformance on individual, specialized tasks. To address this, current practices often involve fine-tuning pre-trained foundation models with newly acquired task-specific demonstrations. However, acquiring such task-specific data is often expensive and time-consuming.

An alternative strategy involves leveraging existing large prior datasets by carefully selecting relevant subsets to augment smaller task-specific datasets, a process known as co-training or data curation. The challenge lies in performing this selection effectively; naive data inclusion can be detrimental to downstream policy performance. Previous data selection methods in robotics have primarily relied on heuristic-based approaches, such as filtering data based on semantic similarity, visual cues, motion patterns, or state-action pair closeness to target demonstrations. These heuristics, while intuitive in some contexts, do not directly assess the actual impact of a data point on the final policy's performance, potentially including data that degrades the policy or excluding data that could be beneficial.

The current work draws inspiration from the "datamodels" framework, which has been successfully applied in NLP and CV. Datamodels provide an efficient, performance-aware mechanism for data selection by directly estimating the behavior of a machine learning model as a function of its training data. This framework reduces the number of full model training and evaluation steps required for data selection compared to exhaustive search. However, directly applying datamodels to robotics faces a significant hurdle: the evaluation of robot policies often necessitates expensive and time-consuming real-world rollouts, making the direct application of existing datamodeling techniques computationally infeasible.

DataMIL aims to bridge this gap by extending the datamodels framework to imitation learning in robotics, specifically by addressing the challenges posed by real-world policy evaluation costs. It seeks to develop a method that can identify data points that improve policy performance while discarding those that degrade it, moving beyond heuristic-based selection to a direct, end-to-end, and performance-aware approach that is tractable in robotic settings.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust and efficient data selection framework, termed DataMIL (Datamodels for Imitation Learning), that enables robots to learn high-performing specialized policies by intelligently curating data from large, diverse prior datasets. This objective is driven by several key motivations:

1. **Enhancing Task-Specific Competence**: While large generalist robot policies achieve broad capabilities, they often exhibit suboptimal performance on individual, specialized tasks. Bridging this gap typically requires significant quantities of new task-specific data for fine-tuning, which is expensive and time-intensive to acquire. The motivation is to instead leverage the wealth of existing, diverse robotic datasets more effectively to boost performance on these specialized tasks.

2. **Addressing Limitations of Naive Data Use**: Simply aggregating all available data or randomly selecting subsets from large prior datasets can be counterproductive. Uncurated data may introduce noise, irrelevant information, or even conflicting demonstrations, thereby harming the performance of downstream policies. This highlights the need for a principled data selection mechanism.

3. **Overcoming Shortcomings of Heuristic-Based Selection**: Existing data selection methods in robotics frequently rely on similarity heuristics (e.g., visual, semantic, or motion similarity) to retrieve data relevant to a target task. While these methods are computationally less intensive, they operate on assumptions about what constitutes "useful" data and do not directly assess a data point's actual impact on the policy's success. A key motivation is to move beyond these indirect measures to a system that directly optimizes for policy performance.

4. **Tackling the Computational Challenge of Direct Optimization**: The ideal but unfeasible approach to data selection involves training and evaluating a policy on every possible data subset to identify the optimal set. This is prohibitively expensive, especially in robotics where policy evaluation often requires real-world interactions or high-fidelity simulations (rollouts). The motivation is to find a computationally tractable method that closely approximates this ideal objective without incurring such high costs.

5. **Adapting Datamodels for Robotics' Unique Constraints**: The datamodels framework has demonstrated success in other machine learning domains (NLP, CV) by estimating how individual data points influence model performance. However, its direct application to robotics is hampered by the high cost and non-differentiability of real-world policy evaluation. A core objective is to adapt this powerful framework to the unique challenges of robot imitation learning, specifically by developing methods that avoid expensive rollouts and integrate with differentiable learning processes.

In summary, the key objectives are to:
- Develop an end-to-end, performance-aware data selection framework for robot imitation learning.
- Introduce a computationally tractable surrogate objective that approximates true policy performance without requiring costly environmental rollouts.
- Extend the datamodels paradigm to robotic settings, enabling direct optimization of data selection for task success.
- Empirically validate DataMIL's ability to consistently improve policy performance on specialized tasks, including those involving heterogeneous datasets, different robot embodiments, and multi-task learning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

DataMIL is a data selection framework built on the datamodels paradigm, adapted to address the specific challenges of robot imitation learning. Its core idea is to estimate the influence of individual data points or clusters on a policy's performance on a target task, thereby enabling the selection of beneficial data and the exclusion of detrimental data.

The methodology can be broken down into the following key components:

#### 3.1. Estimating Datamodels

DataMIL leverages linear datamodels, where the predicted performance of a policy trained on a subset $D'$ is approximated as an additive sum of scores $\tau(z_i)$ for each data point $z_i \in D'$. The work explores two methods for estimating these $\tau(z_i)$ scores:

1. **Regression Estimator**: This method involves treating datamodeling as a supervised learning problem.
 - **Process**: A number of random subsets $D_j$ are sampled from the large prior dataset $D$. For each subset, a policy $A(D_j)$ is trained, and its performance $M(A(D_j))$ is evaluated using the target metric.
 - **Estimation**: A linear model is then fitted to these $(D_j, M(A(D_j)))$ pairs. The coefficients of this linear model, $\hat{\beta}$, represent the $\tau(z_i)$ scores for each data point $z_i \in D$, effectively quantifying its contribution to policy performance.
 - **Characteristics**: While conceptually straightforward and capable of high accuracy, this method is computationally expensive as it requires training and evaluating multiple policies.

2. **Metagradient-based Estimator**: This approach is designed for greater computational efficiency, particularly with complex policy models.
 - **Process**: The dataset is parameterized by a real-valued weight vector $w$, where $w_i$ denotes the influence weight of the $i$-th training sample. The method then approximates the target metric $M(A(w))$ using a first-order Taylor expansion around a baseline (e.g., $w_0$ as an all-ones vector).
 - **Influence Function**: The gradient of this approximation, $\nabla_w M(A(w_0))$, known as the influence function or metagradient, quantifies how changes in data weights affect model performance. These gradient values are used as the $\tau(z_i)$ scores.
 - **Computation**: Unlike traditional methods that approximate influence, DataMIL utilizes recent advances in computing metagradients exactly and efficiently by leveraging the iterative structure of standard optimization algorithms like stochastic gradient descent (SGD). This avoids the need for numerous full policy retrainings.
 - **Characteristics**: More complex but significantly more efficient for large models compared to the regression estimator, provided the evaluation objective is differentiable.

#### 3.2. Adapting Datamodels for Robotics

DataMIL introduces several key modifications to the datamodels framework to make it tractable and robust in robotic settings:

1. **Estimating Datamodels without Rollouts (Surrogate Loss Function)**:
 - **Challenge**: The true target metric $M$ (policy success rate from environment rollouts) is expensive to compute and typically non-differentiable. This prevents the efficient use of metagradient-based estimators.
 - **Solution**: DataMIL defines a proxy metric, $\tilde{M}(\pi, D_{target}) = \frac{1}{|D_{target}|} \sum_{(s,a) \in D_{target}} -L_{BC}(\pi(s), a)$. This is the negative average Behavior Cloning (BC) loss computed on a small, held-out set of task-specific target demonstrations $D_{target}$.
 - **Justification**: This proxy metric requires no additional environment rollouts and is fully differentiable, making it suitable for metagradient estimation. Empirical validation demonstrates that selecting data based on this proxy metric yields policies with only a marginal drop in true success rate compared to selection based on actual rollouts, while significantly outperforming policies trained on uncurated data. The BC loss function varies based on policy class (e.g., Negative Log-Likelihood or L1 loss for MLP policies, denoising score-matching for diffusion policies like Octo). The framework also allows for weighting specific states (e.g., pre-grasp states) in the BC loss to emphasize their importance.

2. **Clustering Training Examples**:
 - **Challenge**: Estimating influence at the level of individual state-action pairs can be noisy because each sample is seen few times during training.
 - **Solution**: DataMIL groups individual state-action pairs into temporal clusters. These clusters can range from sub-trajectories to entire trajectories or tasks.
 - **Heuristic**: The optimal cluster granularity depends on dataset size and computational budget. For mid-sized datasets (e.g., LIBERO), sub-trajectory clustering (e.g., length 15) offers a balance between precision and noise reduction. For very large, heterogeneous datasets (e.g., OXE), aggregating influence at the trajectory level proves more reliable.

3. **Reducing Distribution Shift**:
 - **Challenge**: Large, diverse robotic datasets often exhibit significant distribution shifts (e.g., varying embodiments, lighting, scenes). This can lead to inaccurate datamodel estimates if the training distribution for influence estimation is too different from the target task's distribution.
 - **Solution**: A small fraction of the target task data $D_{target}$ is included alongside the prior data during datamodel estimation. $D_{target}$ is split into two halves: one half is used for computing the proxy objective, and the other half is integrated into the training mix for the influence estimation process.
 - **Application**: This technique is particularly applied in real-world settings (e.g., OXE) where distribution shifts are more pronounced than in simulation.

#### 3.3. Data Selection and Policy Training

Once the per-cluster attribution scores $\tau(z_i)$ are estimated, DataMIL follows a clear procedure:
1. **Selection**: The top $x\%$ of prior examples with the highest positive influence scores are selected to form a curated dataset, $D_{sel}$.
2. **Co-training**: The final robot policy $\pi$ is trained using behavior cloning on a combination of the curated data $D_{sel}$ and the original target data $D_{target}$. A co-training strategy is employed where, at each training step, a batch is sampled from $D_{target}$ with probability $\alpha$ and from $D_{sel}$ with probability $(1-\alpha)$. A value of $\alpha=0.5$ was found to be effective.

In summary, DataMIL systematically applies datamodels to identify and select valuable data for robot imitation learning by overcoming computational and robustness challenges specific to robotics, leading to improved policy performance.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research validates DataMIL through extensive experiments across over 60 simulation and real-world manipulation tasks, demonstrating its efficacy compared to existing data selection baselines.

#### 4.1. Impact on Policy Performance (Simulation: MetaWorld)

- **Setup**: Evaluated on 50 manipulation tasks in MetaWorld. The prior dataset combined expert demonstrations with lower-quality exploration trajectories from a multi-task SAC agent. DataMIL used 5 expert demonstrations as target data and the regression-based datamodel estimator to select the top 10% of prior samples.
- **Results**: DataMIL achieved a notable performance boost, outperforming state-of-the-art baselines by approximately 10% in average success rate (Figure 3a). Similarity-based baselines (State Retrieval (SR), Action Retrieval (AR), Behavior Retrieval (BR), Flow, STRAP) often performed poorly. SR struggled to filter suboptimal actions, AR lacked task awareness by ignoring state information, and BR, combining both, inherited limitations. DataMIL’s direct, performance-aware selection effectively identified useful demonstrations and discarded harmful samples. Policies trained on DataMIL-selected data significantly outperformed those trained on all data (All-Data) or target data only (Target-Only), highlighting the critical role of data curation.
- **No-Goal Conditioning**: Even when goal information was masked during selection and training, DataMIL maintained a 10% lead over baselines, although overall performance for all methods decreased, indicating the benefit of goal context.
- **Qualitative Analysis**: DataMIL was observed to consistently select data from the correct task and effectively filter out noisy, suboptimal examples, in contrast to baselines that either included poor-quality data or retrieved from irrelevant tasks.

#### 4.2. Scalability to Complex Policies (Simulation: LIBERO)

- **Setup**: Tested on 10 long-horizon tasks from the LIBERO benchmark, using LIBERO-90 (4500 human-teleoperated demonstrations) as the prior dataset. The complex nature of these tasks and high-dimensional RGB observations necessitated the use of Octo, a transformer-based diffusion policy. The metagradient-based datamodel estimator was employed due to the high computational cost of training Octo.
- **Results**: DataMIL achieved the highest average success rate across the 10 tasks (Figure 3b). While visual similarity-based baselines (STRAP, BR, Flow) showed varying effectiveness, likely due to task-dependent suitability of their heuristics, DataMIL demonstrated consistent strong performance across all tasks.
- **Ablation Studies**:
 - **Selection Percentage**: DataMIL significantly outperformed baselines at lower selection ratios (5-10%), indicating its ability to prioritize high-quality samples. Performance gaps narrowed at higher ratios as more useful data was included by all methods.
 - **Cluster Size**: Finer-grained clustering (sub-trajectories of length 15) resulted in higher performance, suggesting that more precise control over data segments is beneficial.
 - **Co-training Ratio**: A 0.5 ratio (equal sampling from target and selected data) consistently yielded the best results.
 - **Extended Training for All-Data**: Longer training did not consistently benefit the All-Data baseline and could even degrade performance due to overfitting.

#### 4.3. Real-World Application and Heterogeneous Datasets (OXE)

- **Setup**: Demonstrated data selection from the Open X-Embodiment (OXE) datasets, one of the largest collections of robot datasets, and evaluated on four real-world tasks across different robot embodiments (Franka, Tiago, Droid). The target tasks were designed to be distinct from the OXE data in terms of scene, camera pose, or objects, testing transfer capabilities. The metagradient estimator was used, with distribution shift mitigation techniques.
- **Results**: DataMIL consistently achieved the highest success rates across all real-world tasks (Figure 4).
 - **Franka-Ball & Franka-Pouch**: While visual similarity baselines were competitive on simpler tasks, DataMIL maintained its advantage as the dataset diversity increased (Franka-Pouch), where heuristic-based methods started to break down.
 - **Tiago-Sink (Cross-Embodiment)**: DataMIL successfully selected useful data for the Tiago robot, an embodiment not present in the prior OXE data, significantly improving task success.
 - **Droid-Multitask**: DataMIL effectively supported multi-task learning, curating a single dataset that improved performance across three distinct tasks (bread in bowl, napkin in drawer, open drawer).
- **Qualitative Analysis of Selected Data**:
 - **Embodiment Relevance**: For tasks like Tiago-Sink (unseen embodiment), DataMIL selected data that was visually different but functionally relevant (e.g., table-top manipulation from ego-perspective, despite different robots), leading to positive transfer. Baselines often selected data from unrelated domains.
 - **Dataset Distribution**: DataMIL tended to select a balanced mix of demonstrations from several different prior datasets, promoting generalization. In contrast, baselines often disproportionately drew samples from a single source, potentially leading to overfitting to specific domain characteristics.
 - **Top/Bottom Samples**: High- and low-ranked data points often appeared visually similar but differed in their impact on policy performance. This suggests DataMIL's ability to discern subtle differences that make data helpful or harmful, aligning with findings in other ML domains where visually similar inputs can have different labels or effects.

#### 4.4. Computational Costs

- The regression estimator (MetaWorld) required around 8 GPU hours on 8 A100 GPUs (amortizing to 1-2 hours per task).
- The metagradient-based estimator (LIBERO, OXE) was 3-5x slower than standard policy training. For LIBERO (4 A5000 GPUs), it took 16-20 hours; for OXE (4 A100 GPUs), 3-4 days. While still significant, DataMIL avoids expensive online rollouts and runs passively.

Overall, the findings demonstrate that DataMIL provides a robust and effective framework for performance-aware data selection in robot imitation learning, offering consistent gains over existing methods across diverse simulation and real-world settings.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

DataMIL represents a significant advancement in the field of robot imitation learning, particularly in its approach to leveraging large, heterogeneous datasets. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Shift from Heuristics to Performance-Aware Selection**: DataMIL moves beyond traditional heuristic-based data selection methods (e.g., visual or semantic similarity) that rely on human notions of data quality. By directly optimizing for downstream policy performance using datamodels, it offers a principled, end-to-end approach to identify data that actively improves a policy and discard data that degrades it. This paradigm shift can lead to more robust and higher-performing robot policies.

2. **Unlocking the Potential of Large Robot Datasets**: The proliferation of large-scale robotic datasets (e.g., OXE) has created both opportunities and challenges. DataMIL provides a mechanism to effectively harness these diverse and often noisy collections, enabling positive transfer from seemingly unrelated prior data to specific target tasks. This capacity is crucial for scaling robot learning capabilities and reducing the dependency on costly, task-specific data collection.

3. **Tractability for Real-World Robotics**: A core innovation is the introduction of a differentiable surrogate loss function that replaces expensive real-world rollouts for datamodel estimation. This makes the powerful datamodels framework computationally tractable for robot learning, thereby overcoming a major barrier to its application in real-world robotic systems.

4. **Enhanced Generalization and Transfer Learning**: The empirical results, particularly on cross-embodiment tasks (Tiago-Sink) and multi-task learning (Droid-Multitask) from highly heterogeneous datasets (OXE), demonstrate DataMIL's ability to facilitate broader generalization. By intelligently curating data, it enables policies to adapt to new embodiments and perform multiple objectives simultaneously, even when the exact target scenarios are not present in the prior data.

5. **Informing Future Data Collection and Dataset Design**: The per-sample/per-cluster attribution scores generated by DataMIL offer valuable insights into which types of data are truly beneficial or detrimental. This can guide future data collection efforts, leading to more efficient and impactful dataset design. Understanding why visually similar data can have different impacts (helpful vs. harmful) is a promising area for further research.

**Potential Impact**:
- **Reduced Data Collection Costs**: By making more effective use of existing data, DataMIL can reduce the need for extensive new data collection for every specialized task, significantly lowering the cost and effort associated with developing new robot skills.
- **Accelerated Robot Skill Development**: More efficient data selection can accelerate the iteration cycle for developing and deploying robot capabilities, contributing to faster progress in robotics research and application.
- **More Robust and Reliable Robots**: Policies trained on carefully curated data are likely to be more robust, less prone to overfitting on irrelevant data, and more reliable in diverse real-world conditions.

**Limitations and Future Directions (as identified by the authors)**:
1. **Computational Cost**: Despite the use of efficient metagradient estimators, datamodel estimation still incurs computational costs several times higher than standard policy training. Future work could focus on accelerating this process, for instance, through the use of smaller proxy models.
2. **Hyperparameter Sensitivity**: The framework currently relies on various hyperparameters (e.g., selected dataset size, clustering granularity, co-training ratios) for which principled selection methods are lacking. Future research could aim to make these choices more automated or robust.
3. **Scaling to Truly Large-Scale Multi-Task Robotics**: While the Droid-Multitask setting provided a preliminary step, extending and evaluating DataMIL in truly massive, multi-task robotics settings remains an open and impactful research avenue.
4. **Understanding Fine-Grained Data Impact**: Further investigation into why visually similar data points can have vastly different impacts on policy performance is needed to refine data selection strategies and gain deeper mechanistic insights.

Addressing these limitations will be crucial for the widespread adoption and scaling of DataMIL in practical robotic applications, ultimately contributing to the development of more capable and intelligent robotic systems.
