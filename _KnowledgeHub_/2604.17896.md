# Can Explicit Physical Feasibility Benefit VLA Learning? An Empirical Study

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of Turku empirically studied integrating explicit physical feasibility supervision into diffusion-based Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies, demonstrating improvements in both robot safety and task accuracy. This additional supervision particularly enhanced learning efficiency in data-limited scenarios, with policies trained on 40 episodes achieving a Safe Success Rate of 29.00% on a challenging task, outperforming a baseline trained on 120 episodes.

## Problem

- Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies often implicitly infer physical feasibility from demonstrations, potentially leading to a lack of robust understanding or guarantees for safe robot interaction in real-world settings.
- Standard VLA training objectives prioritize matching demonstrated action sequences, potentially overlooking explicit geometric outcomes necessary for adhering to hard physical constraints like obstacle or self-collision avoidance.
- A systematic study on integrating explicit physical feasibility signals into the learning objective of VLA policies to bridge the gap between high-level reasoning and strict physical requirements was limited.

## Method

- Introduce an ==auxiliary, differentiable geometric feasibility loss== (==L_geo==) into the ==VLA== training objective, which explicitly supervises physical constraints by leveraging robot kinematics and obstacle geometry.
- The ==L_geo== is computed based on the ==signed distance of robot links to obstacles==, activating as a ==squared hinge loss== only when clearance falls below a defined safety margin.
- The total training objective combines the standard ==Mean Squared Error (MSE)== from ==imitation learning== with this ==L_geo==, weighted by a hyper-parameter, where geometric information provides a training-time inductive bias without requiring explicit geometry queries at deployment.

## Results

- Policies incorporating explicit feasibility supervision achieved significantly higher Safe Success Rates (SSR), demonstrating improved physical reliability and task accuracy compared to imitation-only baselines, e.g., SSR(0.02, 0.10) improved from **22.00%** to **43.50%** under small perturbations.
- The method significantly reduced the probability of low-clearance outcomes while simultaneously enhancing reaching accuracy, indicating a constructive impact on learned behaviors rather than just an avoidance penalty.
- Explicit feasibility supervision provided the largest efficiency gains in low-data regimes, enabling policies trained with only **40 episodes** to outperform baseline policies trained with three times more data (**120 episodes**) in some metrics; **40-episode** policies achieved a Safe Success Rate of **29.00%** on a challenging task.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicit physical feasibility supervision acts as a constructive learning signal, providing geometric guidance that helps VLA policies internalize reliable physical interaction and simultaneously improves both safety and task accuracy.
> - This explicit supervision offers maximal learning efficiency gains in low-data regimes, allowing policies trained with fewer demonstrations to outperform baselines trained with significantly more data.
> - The effectiveness of feasibility supervision is dependent on its strength, with appropriate (moderate) levels yielding optimal performance by balancing safety and precision, while overly strong supervision can introduce safety-accuracy trade-offs.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models represent a paradigm in robotics where multimodal inputs—language instructions and visual observations—are directly mapped to robot actions, aiming to integrate perception, reasoning, and control within a single policy. These models have demonstrated progress through large-scale imitation learning, showing generalization capabilities across various tasks, scenes, and robotic embodiments.

However, the increasing proximity of VLA policies to real-world deployment has highlighted a challenge: the necessity of satisfying hard physical constraints inherent to embodied interaction, such as kinematic limits, self-collision avoidance, and obstacle avoidance. Current VLA training procedures primarily focus on action imitation from demonstrations and typically do not explicitly supervise these geometric and physical constraints. Consequently, the geometric structure underlying physically feasible behavior is largely inferred implicitly from the demonstrations. This approach may lead to policies that achieve task completion but may not consistently adhere to physical safety and reliability requirements.

Recent research in VLA has begun to address these physical constraints. For instance, SafeVLA integrates constrained reinforcement learning for VLA safety, CoFreeVLA focuses on explicit risk estimation for collision-aware execution, and AEGIS/VLSA proposes a plug-and-play safety layer for constraint-aware deployment. Beyond VLA, the broader robotics field has long recognized physical feasibility as fundamental for robot control and planning, with techniques like configuration-space reasoning, collision checking, and feasibility-aware search. More recently, learned robot policies have also incorporated physical feasibility as a learning signal, including neural motion planning methods like MπNets and Avoid Everything, and generative visuomotor policies such as RK-Diffuser and KStar Diffuser. These works suggest that incorporating collision-aware or kinematics-aware signals can enhance policy reliability.

This paper positions itself within this evolving landscape by investigating the integration of explicit physical feasibility signals directly into the learning objective of VLA models, rather than relying solely on imitation or post-hoc safety layers. It aims to empirically study whether such explicit supervision can serve as a structured learning signal, thereby complementing the data-driven VLA learning paradigm and potentially improving policy reliability and learning efficiency.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to empirically investigate whether explicit physical feasibility supervision can serve as an effective structured learning signal for Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies. The authors aim to determine if introducing such supervision into the training process can provide structured guidance beyond what is implicitly learned from demonstrations alone.

The motivation for this study stems from several observations regarding current VLA learning paradigms:
- **Implicit Learning of Physical Feasibility**: While VLA models trained through large-scale imitation learning have achieved notable performance in mapping multimodal inputs to robot actions, their training objectives typically do not explicitly supervise hard physical constraints. These constraints include obstacle avoidance, kinematic limits, and self-collision avoidance, which are critical for safe and reliable real-world robot deployment.
- **Geometric Nature of Feasibility**: Physical feasibility is inherently geometric, depending on the relationships between robot kinematics, body geometry, and environmental obstacles. Classical robotics explicitly models and enforces this structure. In contrast, existing end-to-end VLA pipelines often lack an explicit mechanism to represent and enforce such geometry-related feasibility during action generation.
- **Limitations of Imitation Learning Objectives**: Standard action-learning objectives primarily supervise agreement with demonstrated action sequences. This means a policy might imitate successful behavior without explicitly internalizing the geometric regularities that contribute to its physical reliability. The policy may thus learn *what* actions to take but not necessarily *why* those actions are geometrically feasible or safe.
- **Bridging the Gap to Real-World Deployment**: As VLA policies move closer to practical application, task completion alone is insufficient. Robots must operate safely and reliably, adhering to physical constraints to avoid hazards like unintended collisions. Explicitly addressing physical feasibility during training could enhance the trustworthiness and deployability of VLA systems.

To systematically probe this question, the authors chose close-obstacle reaching as a controlled experimental setting. In this task, successful execution requires not only reaching a target but also maintaining sufficient clearance from nearby obstacles throughout the motion. This setting naturally couples policy performance with geometry-dependent physical feasibility, making it a suitable testbed for evaluating the impact of explicit feasibility supervision. The research aims to formulate a simple, geometry-grounded feasibility objective and integrate it into a diffusion-based VLA policy's training, providing an empirical study on its effects.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research investigates the integration of explicit physical feasibility supervision into the training of a diffusion-based VLA policy. This involves defining a specific task, adapting a standard VLA model, formulating a geometry-grounded feasibility objective, and generating a specialized dataset for evaluation.

#### 3.1. Task Definition

The study uses a close-obstacle reaching task to evaluate physical feasibility. In this scenario, a single-arm robot must reach a specified target while operating in the presence of a nearby obstacle. Task success requires both accurate target reaching within a tolerance and maintaining sufficient geometric clearance from the obstacle throughout the execution. This setup characterizes physical feasibility by robot-obstacle clearance, serving as a controlled environment for feasibility-aware VLA learning.

#### 3.2. Diffusion-based VLA Policy

The researchers adopt a standard diffusion-based VLA formulation, specifically fine-tuning RDT-1B, a large open-source diffusion foundation model (1.2 billion parameters) for robotic manipulation. The policy models the conditional distribution of a future action chunk, $p(a_{t:t+T_a-1} | \ell, o_t)$, given a language instruction $\ell$ and visual observations $o_t$. During training, a denoising network $F_\theta$ is trained to recover a clean expert action chunk $a_t^0$ from a noisy version $a_t^k$ (perturbed by Gaussian noise $\epsilon_k$ at diffusion step $k$). The standard learning objective is the Mean Squared Error (MSE) between the predicted and expert action chunks:
$L_{MSE} = E_{a_t^0, \epsilon_k, k, \ell, o_t} ||a_t^0 - F_\theta(a_t^k, \ell, o_t, k)||_2^2$.

#### 3.3. Physical Feasibility Supervision

To explicitly enforce physical feasibility, an auxiliary geometric supervision signal is introduced during training, leveraging robot kinematics and obstacle geometry.
- **Forward Kinematics Mapping**: Predicted action chunks (sequences of future joint states) are mapped to corresponding robot link poses in the workspace using forward kinematics, $T_{t+\tau}^{(l)} = f_{FK}^{(l)}(q_{t+\tau})$, where $q_{t+\tau}$ is the predicted joint state and $T_{t+\tau}^{(l)}$ is the pose of link $l$. This converts joint space outputs into geometric information.
- **Signed Distance to Obstacle**: For a small set of selected representative robot links $S$, their origins $p_{t+\tau}^{(l)}$ are used as representative points. Obstacles are modeled as Oriented Bounding Boxes (OBBs), $B_t$. The signed distance $d_{t+\tau}^{(l)}$ from each representative point to the OBB surface is computed, with a link-specific radius $r^{(l)}$ subtracted to account for body thickness: $d_{t+\tau}^{(l)} = SDF_{OBB}(p_{t+\tau}^{(l)}; B_t) - r^{(l)}$. Positive, zero, and negative values indicate clearance, contact, and penetration, respectively.
- **Geometric Feasibility Loss**: A squared hinge loss, $L_{geo}$, is applied. It activates when the surface clearance $d_{t+\tau}^{(l)}$ for any representative link point falls below a safety margin $\delta$. The loss is averaged only over active violations $V = \{(\tau, l) | d_{t+\tau}^{(l)} < \delta\}$:
 $L_{geo} = Avg_{d_{t+\tau}^{(l)} < \delta} [max(0, \delta - d_{t+\tau}^{(l)})]^2$.
 The overall training objective combines the MSE loss with this geometric feasibility loss: $L_{total} = L_{MSE} + \lambda L_{geo}$, where $\lambda$ controls the relative strength of feasibility supervision. Crucially, obstacle geometry and forward kinematics are only used during training; at inference, the policy acts solely on RGB observations and language instructions.

#### 3.4. Data Generation

A synthetic dataset was constructed using NVIDIA Isaac Sim with a Franka Arm robot.
- **Object Generation**: For each episode, a target and an obstacle (both cubes with randomized poses and colors) are generated. Scene generation enforces visibility and separability constraints: objects must not intersect in 3D, and both must be fully visible and clearly separable in at least two camera views.
- **Counterfactual Trajectory Generation**: This procedure ensures obstacles meaningfully interfere with robot motion. First, an obstacle-free reference trajectory $\pi^-$ is planned. Then, an obstacle is placed near the swept path of $\pi^-$ such that the minimum robot-obstacle clearance along $\pi^-$ falls below a small threshold $\epsilon = 0.10$ m, ensuring potential collision. With the obstacle fixed, a collision-free trajectory $\pi^+$ to the same goal is re-planned using MoveIt with OMPL. Only $\pi^+$ (the obstacle-avoidance trajectory) is retained in the dataset, along with three-view RGB observations, obstacle parameters, and a language instruction. The full dataset consists of 120 episodes, with subsets of 40, 80, and 120 episodes used to study data efficiency.

#### 3.5. Experimental Setup

- **Compared Methods**: The study compares two fine-tuning objectives: `MSE` (baseline, $L_{MSE}$ only) and `MSE+Feasibility` (ours, $L_{total} = L_{MSE} + \lambda L_{geo}$).
- **Evaluation under Obstacle Perturbations**: At test time, obstacle configurations are perturbed to assess the policy's adaptability.
 - **Small perturbations**: Local changes (0-0.10m translational shifts, slight size variations) around the original obstacle placement. Used for robustness under in-distribution configurations.
 - **Large perturbations**: Substantially different obstacle relocations that still interfere with the trajectory, creating larger visual changes. Used for local generalization under out-of-distribution configurations.
- **Evaluation Metrics**:
 - **Safety**: Minimum signed robot-obstacle clearance $d_{min}$ (negative values denote collision), and $Pr(d_{min} < 0.02)$ and $Pr(d_{min} < 0.05)$.
 - **Task Accuracy**: Final hand-to-target distance $d_{tgt}$, and $Pr(d_{tgt} < 0.10)$ and $Pr(d_{tgt} < 0.15)$.
 - **Combined Success**: Safe Success Rate $SSR(\alpha, \beta) = Pr(d_{min} > \alpha \land d_{tgt} < \beta)$, with two variants: $SSR(0.05, 0.15)$ (emphasizing safety) and $SSR(0.02, 0.10)$ (emphasizing precision).
- **Implementation Details**: RDT-1B is fine-tuned for 2000 epochs. Default hyperparameters for the feasibility objective are $\delta = 0.10$ m and $\lambda = 1.0$.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical study systematically investigated the effects of explicit physical feasibility supervision across various scenarios.

#### 4.1. RQ1: Behavior Change under Obstacle Perturbations

Policies trained on 40 episodes were evaluated under small and large obstacle perturbations.
- **Improved Task Performance**: The policy trained with feasibility supervision (`MSE+Feasibility`) demonstrated higher task-level performance, as measured by Safe Success Rate (SSR), compared to the baseline (`MSE` only). Under small perturbations, `MSE+Feasibility` achieved substantial gains in $SSR(0.02, 0.10)$ (43.50% vs. 22.00%) and $SSR(0.05, 0.15)$ (51.50% vs. 26.00%). Under large perturbations, where generalization was required, the gap remained significant, with `MSE+Feasibility` yielding $SSR(0.02, 0.10)$ of 29.00% compared to 8.25% for the baseline.
- **Enhanced Safety**: The `MSE+Feasibility` policy consistently preserved larger obstacle clearance. For instance, under large perturbations, the probability of low-clearance outcomes ($Pr(d_{min} < 0.05)$) was 45.00% for `MSE+Feasibility` versus 59.50% for the baseline, indicating that failures were shifted away from the obstacle boundary.
- **Simultaneous Safety and Accuracy Gains**: A key finding was that the benefits were not limited to safety; reaching accuracy also improved. The joint distribution of minimum clearance ($d_{min}$) and final target distance ($d_{tgt}$) showed that the `MSE+Feasibility` policy shifted the distribution towards both larger obstacle clearance and smaller target error (Figure 4). This suggested that the explicit feasibility signal acted as a constructive learning guidance, helping the policy better relate observed scene geometry to feasible robot motion, rather than merely introducing conservative avoidance behavior.

#### 4.2. RQ2: Learning Efficiency under Limited Data

The study compared policies trained with 40, 80, and 120 episodes under large perturbations.
- **Largest Gains in Low-Data Regime**: The improvement provided by feasibility supervision was most pronounced in the low-data regime. With only 40 training episodes, `MSE+Feasibility` showed the largest relative gain in SSR metrics. For example, its $SSR(0.02, 0.10)$ of 29.00% with 40 episodes surpassed the baseline's 19.00% even when the baseline was trained with 120 episodes. This indicates that explicit physical supervision enhances data efficiency by offering valuable geometric guidance when demonstration data is sparse.
- **Diminishing Returns with More Data**: As the amount of training data increased (to 80 and 120 episodes), the relative performance gain from feasibility supervision diminished. This suggests that with sufficient demonstration data, the imitation learning baseline begins to generalize more effectively, and the structured physical signal's complementary value decreases, potentially even competing with the information already present in the demonstrations if not carefully integrated.

#### 4.3. RQ3: Effect of Supervision Strength

An ablation study varied the safety margin $\delta$ and loss weight $\lambda$ under the 40-episode, large-perturbation setting.
- **Optimal Strength**: Explicit physical supervision was most effective when applied at an appropriate strength. Moderate supervision settings achieved the best balance between safety and accuracy, leading to the highest safe success rates.
- **Weak Supervision**: Under weak supervision (small $\delta$ and $\lambda$), the policy showed limited safety improvement but already exhibited improved reaching accuracy. This reinforced the observation that the physical signal serves as general learning guidance rather than solely an avoidance penalty.
- **Overly Strong Supervision**: Conversely, overly strong supervision, particularly with a large safety margin combined with a large loss weight, introduced a trade-off. While it pushed the policy further away from obstacles, it could also hinder precise target approach and consequently reduce overall task success rates. This highlights the importance of tuning supervision parameters to avoid overly conservative behavior that compromises task goals.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of robotic control, particularly within the domain of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, by empirically demonstrating that explicit physical feasibility supervision can serve as a beneficial structured learning signal. The findings indicate that augmenting VLA training with geometry-grounded feasibility objectives can improve both the physical reliability and overall task performance of policies, while also enhancing learning efficiency, especially in data-limited scenarios.

The primary significance of this work lies in challenging the prevailing imitation-only paradigm for VLA learning. By showing that policies benefit from explicit guidance on geometric and physical constraints, the paper suggests that VLA systems do not need to infer all physical regularities solely from demonstrations. This opens an avenue for incorporating prior physical knowledge directly into the learning process, potentially leading to more robust and trustworthy robotic behaviors.

The potential impacts include:
- **Improved Reliability and Safety**: Policies trained with explicit feasibility supervision demonstrate higher minimum clearance from obstacles and lower probabilities of collision, which is critical for real-world robot deployment where physical safety is paramount. This could reduce the risk of accidents and damage in practical applications.
- **Enhanced Task Performance**: Beyond just safety, the research shows that explicit feasibility supervision can also improve reaching accuracy. This suggests that understanding and adhering to physical constraints does not necessarily compromise task success but can, in fact, contribute to more precise and effective manipulation.
- **Increased Data Efficiency**: The observation that feasibility supervision yields the largest benefits in low-data regimes is significant. For scenarios where extensive demonstration datasets are difficult or costly to acquire, incorporating explicit physical priors could enable more efficient policy learning with fewer examples, accelerating development and deployment cycles.
- **Guidance for Future VLA Design**: The study provides empirical evidence for the utility of structured physical priors. This could guide future research in VLA model architecture and training methodologies, encouraging the exploration of broader task families, richer feasibility constraints (e.g., self-collision, kinematic limits), and more sophisticated formulations beyond simple distance-based objectives, such as learned geometric models or reinforcement learning-based objectives.
- **Reduced Reliance on Post-Hoc Safety Layers**: While existing work often relies on post-hoc safety layers or constrained reinforcement learning to address physical constraints during deployment, this research suggests that embedding feasibility awareness directly into the learning objective can lead to inherently safer policies, potentially simplifying the deployment stack and improving real-time performance by reducing the need for runtime safety checks.

The authors acknowledge limitations, including the focus on a single obstacle-aware manipulation setting, the instantiation of feasibility primarily through obstacle clearance, and the use of a relatively simple supervision signal. Addressing these limitations in future work could further validate and expand the applicability of explicit physical feasibility supervision in VLA learning.
