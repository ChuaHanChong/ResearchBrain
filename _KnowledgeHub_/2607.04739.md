# Spatial Attention: Adapting Execution Horizons for Diffusion Policies via Observation Sensitivity

> [!summary] Summary
> A framework for adaptively adjusting the execution horizon of diffusion policies in robotics is introduced, utilizing a mathematically derived "Spatial Attention" metric to balance responsiveness and computational efficiency. The approach consistently improved success rates across various manipulation tasks in simulation and real-world settings, including under external disturbances, while maintaining comparable average computational overhead.

## Problem

- Fixed execution horizons in action chunking for generative robot policies force a suboptimal trade-off between responsiveness and computational efficiency across diverse task phases.
- Existing action chunking methods are vulnerable to external disturbances and accumulating errors during open-loop execution, degrading performance in dynamic environments.
- Prior adaptive planning methods are often computationally prohibitive for real-time control with high-dimensional visual inputs, while heuristic policy approaches lack mathematical guarantees and robust evaluation under disturbances.

## Method

- Introduces **Spatial Attention** (Att(o)), a mathematically derived criterion quantifying the ==squared sensitivity== of a policy's action distribution to observation perturbations, to determine optimal resampling rates.
- Utilizes ==Bayes' rule== and two auxiliary ==score-based generative models== (for π(o|a) and π(o)) to practically compute the required gradients and thus Spatial Attention, even without an explicit policy density.
- Employs a ==sequence-to-sequence transformer== model to forecast Spatial Attention and dynamically adjust the execution horizon, assigning shorter horizons for high attention and longer for low attention phases, without requiring future observation generation.

## Results

- Spatial Attention-based adaptive policies consistently achieved improved success rates (e.g., **5-7%** for Square, **10-19%** for Tool Hang) across various Robomimic manipulation tasks compared to fixed-horizon baselines, in both state and vision observations.
- The framework demonstrated enhanced robustness, outperforming baselines in modified Lift and Can tasks with dynamic disturbances, indicating better responsiveness to unexpected environmental changes.
- In a real-world robot experiment, the adaptive policy attained a **0.92** success rate for grasping a moving block, substantially higher than the **0.42** achieved by a fixed-horizon baseline, while maintaining comparable average computational cost.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - An optimality criterion for adaptive resampling can be derived by minimizing the cumulative expected log-likelihood drop of sampled actions under observation perturbations, establishing a theoretical basis for dynamic execution horizons.
> - Spatial Attention, defined as the expected squared gradient of the policy's log-likelihood with respect to observation, directly quantifies the policy's sensitivity, providing a robust signal for when replanning is necessary.
> - Decomposition of the policy gradient using Bayes' rule allows practical computation of Spatial Attention for score-based generative models, making the adaptive strategy applicable to high-dimensional visual inputs in real-time.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

This work operates within the field of robot learning from demonstration, specifically focusing on policies built using generative models, such as diffusion models. These models commonly employ "action chunking," where a sequence of actions is sampled from a single observation. Action chunking offers advantages such as mitigating compounding errors and maintaining temporal consistency in long-horizon tasks, and enabling computationally intensive Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models to control robots by reducing query frequency.

A notable challenge with existing action chunking methods is the use of a fixed "execution horizon" (T<sub>a</sub>). This fixed horizon necessitates a trade-off between responsiveness (shorter T<sub>a</sub>, more frequent observations) and computational efficiency (longer T<sub>a</sub>, fewer generative model samplings). This compromise can be suboptimal because different phases of a task may require varying levels of responsiveness.

Previous attempts to adapt execution horizons have been explored in generative planning and policies. Generative planning methods (e.g., RDM, Jutras-Dubé et al., Punyamoorty et al.) often determine replanning triggers based on trajectory likelihood or predictive uncertainty. However, these approaches typically require generating future observations, which can be computationally prohibitive for real-time control with high-dimensional visual inputs. In generative policies, methods like Mixture of Horizons (MoH) attempt adaptive adjustment based on disagreement metrics, but these are often heuristic and have not been rigorously evaluated under external disturbances.

This paper addresses the limitation of fixed execution horizons by introducing a mathematically grounded approach to adaptively adjust the execution horizon. It distinguishes itself by:
1. Deriving an optimality criterion (Spatial Attention) from an optimization problem that minimizes cumulative likelihood drop, providing a theoretical basis lacking in some prior heuristic methods.
2. Proposing a method that does not require generating future observations, making it suitable for real-time control with high-dimensional inputs.
3. Evaluating its robustness against external disturbances, a scenario often overlooked by previous adaptive execution horizon policies.
4. Leveraging advancements in score-based generative models and Bayesian rule decomposition to practically compute the proposed Spatial Attention metric.

### 2. Key objectives and motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a framework that adaptively adjusts the execution horizon of action chunks sampled by generative robotic policies. This adaptation aims to balance the competing demands of responsiveness to environmental changes or disturbances and computational efficiency during task execution.

The motivation for this objective stems from several limitations observed in current robotic policies utilizing generative models for action chunking:

1. **Fixed Execution Horizon Compromise:** Existing action chunking methods typically employ a fixed execution horizon (T<sub>a</sub>), which is a user-specified value determined before deployment. This forces a single compromise between responsiveness and computational cost across the entire task.
2. **Vulnerability to Disturbances:** When an action chunk is executed for a fixed duration without incorporating new observations, the policy becomes susceptible to external disturbances and accumulating errors. These disturbances can degrade policy performance, especially in dynamic or uncertain environments.
3. **Computational Inefficiency:** While reducing the execution horizon improves responsiveness by allowing more frequent observation updates, each chunk sampling invokes a computationally intensive generative process. A fixed, short horizon across the entire task can lead to excessive computational overhead and increase overall task completion time, even during less critical phases. Conversely, a fixed, long horizon, while efficient, sacrifices responsiveness.
4. **Varying Task Demands:** Different phases of a robotic task often require different levels of precision and responsiveness. For instance, a grasping phase might demand high responsiveness to fine-tune gripper movements, while a transport phase might tolerate longer open-loop execution. A fixed execution horizon cannot accommodate these varying demands efficiently.
5. **Impracticality of Existing Adaptive Methods:** Some adaptive planning methods require generating future observations, which is computationally prohibitive for high-dimensional visual inputs common in robotic tasks. Other adaptive policy approaches rely on heuristic metrics that lack mathematical optimality guarantees and have not been validated under external disturbances.

To address these motivations, the authors introduce "Spatial Attention" as a theoretically grounded criterion for adaptive execution horizon adjustment. The overarching goal is to dynamically assign shorter execution horizons during phases requiring high responsiveness (high Spatial Attention) and longer horizons during less critical phases (low Spatial Attention), thereby minimizing the cumulative performance degradation due to disturbances under a fixed computational budget.

### 3. Methodology and approach

The methodology presented in this paper introduces Spatial Attention as a criterion for adaptively adjusting the execution horizon of action chunks generated by diffusion policies. The approach integrates a theoretical derivation for optimal resampling with practical methods for computing and forecasting Spatial Attention in real-time.

**A. Theoretical Formulation: Minimizing Likelihood Drop**

1. **Problem Definition:** The core problem is to minimize the expected log-likelihood drop of sampled actions when observations are perturbed by disturbances during open-loop execution of an action chunk. The disturbance `δo` is modeled as zero-mean Gaussian noise with variance growing as `ϵ^2 * τ^(2γ) * I`, where `τ` is the elapsed time since the last observation.
2. **Adaptive Resampling Rate:** A time-dependent resampling rate `ρ(t)` is introduced. The execution horizon `T_a` for a chunk sampled at time `t_k` is chosen such that the integral of `ρ(t)` over the chunk equals 1 (`∫ ρ(t) dt = 1`). The total number of resampling events `N` over a task horizon `T_max` is fixed as a budget (`∫ ρ(t) dt = N`).
3. **Optimization Problem:** The objective is to find `ρ(t)` that minimizes the cumulative expected log-likelihood drop over the task horizon, subject to the fixed sampling budget `N`. The expected log-likelihood drop `D(o, τ)` is approximated using a second-order Taylor expansion of `log π(a|o + δo)` around `o`.
4. **Spatial Attention Derivation:** Applying the Fisher-information identity (`E_a[||∇_o log π(a|o)||^2] = -E_a[tr ∇^2_o log π(a|o)]`), the per-step likelihood drop `D(o, τ)` is approximated as `(1/2) * Att(o) * ϵ^2 * τ^(2γ)`.
 - **Spatial Attention (Att(o))** is defined as `E_a~π(·|o)[||∇_o log π(a|o)||^2]`, quantifying the squared sensitivity of the policy's action distribution to observation perturbations.
 - By solving the constrained optimization problem using a Lagrangian argument, the optimal resampling rate `ρ(t)` is found to be proportional to `Att(o(t))^(1/(2γ+1))`. This implies that `ρ(t)` (and thus the frequency of resampling or inverse of execution horizon) should increase as Spatial Attention increases.

**B. Practical Implementation: Computing and Forecasting Spatial Attention**

1. **Computing `∇_o log π(a|o)`:** The primary challenge is that generative policies `π(a|o)` typically do not provide an explicit probability density function, making direct computation of `∇_o log π(a|o)` difficult.
 - The authors overcome this by applying Bayes' rule: `∇_o log π(a|o) = ∇_o log π(o|a) - ∇_o log π(o)`.
 - Two separate score-based generative models (specifically, Noise Conditional Score Networks or NCSN) are trained: one for the action-conditional observation distribution `π(o|a)` and another for the marginal observation distribution `π(o)`. Their respective score estimates are then used to compute the required gradients.
2. **Spatial Attention from Vision Input:** For high-dimensional image observations, directly computing gradients with respect to pixel-level perturbations is computationally expensive and potentially irrelevant to the task.
 - The approach evaluates Spatial Attention in the latent space of a Variational Autoencoder (VAE). The VAE encoder `f_ψ` maps image observation `o` to a latent representation `z` (`z = f_ψ(o)`).
 - Spatial Attention is then computed as `Att(z) = E_a~π(a|z)[||∇_z log π(a|z)||^2]`. This captures sensitivity to meaningful observation changes within the task-relevant latent space.
3. **Adaptive Execution Horizon Pipeline:**
 - **Forecasting Spatial Attention:** To determine future execution horizons without generating future observations, a sequence-to-sequence model `Att_ϕ` is trained. This model forecasts the Spatial Attention sequence for the length of the action chunk, given the current observation `o_t` and the sampled action chunk `A_t`. The model is a transformer architecture, trained offline on trajectories labeled with Spatial Attention values computed using the score networks.
 - **Horizon Selection:** The execution horizon `T_a` for the current chunk is determined by accumulating the forecasted Spatial Attention values. Specifically, `T_a` is the minimum `k` such that `sum(Att^(1/(2γ+1)) from t to t+k)` exceeds a predefined hyperparameter `C_att`. If no such `k` is found, the full chunk length `T_H` is executed.
 - This mechanism assigns shorter `T_a` values in regions of high forecasted Spatial Attention (implying higher sensitivity and need for responsiveness) and longer `T_a` values in regions of low Spatial Attention (allowing for greater computational efficiency).

This framework is designed to be independent of the specific architecture of the underlying generative policy, as Spatial Attention is estimated using auxiliary score networks trained on demonstration data.

### 4. Main findings and results

The experiments were conducted across simulation and real-world environments to evaluate the performance of the proposed Spatial Attention (SA) framework, denoted as "+SA," in adapting execution horizons for diffusion policies. The primary metric for evaluation was the success rate, while ensuring that the average execution horizon remained comparable between +SA methods and fixed-horizon baselines to maintain a fair comparison regarding computational cost.

**A. Baselines:**
The +SA framework was applied to and compared against four generative policy baselines:
- Diffusion Policy (DP) with DDPM (100 diffusion steps)
- Diffusion Policy (DP) with DDIM (16 diffusion steps)
- Consistency Policy (CP) with 3 diffusion steps
- Consistency Policy (CP) with 1 diffusion step

**B. Standard Simulation Experiments (Robomimic Benchmark):**
- **Tasks:** Four single-arm manipulation tasks from the Robomimic benchmark were used: Lift, Can (classified as "Simple" with T<sub>avg</sub>=16), Square, and Tool Hang (classified as "Complex" with T<sub>avg</sub>=32). Both state and vision observations were tested.
- **Results:**
 - The +SA methods consistently demonstrated improved success rates across all tasks and observation types compared to their corresponding fixed-horizon baselines.
 - On "Complex" tasks requiring higher precision, such as Square and Tool Hang, the performance gains were more pronounced, ranging from 5% to 7% for Square and 10% to 19% for Tool Hang.
 - The study showed that Spatial Attention values increased significantly during critical manipulation phases (e.g., grasping, inserting objects). By adaptively shortening T<sub>a</sub> in these high-attention regions, the policy could react more quickly to execution errors or minor disturbances, while maintaining computational efficiency by extending T<sub>a</sub> in low-attention phases. This dynamic adjustment led to higher success rates without increasing the average computational overhead.

**C. Responsiveness to External Disturbance (Modified Simulation):**
- **Tasks:** The Lift and Can tasks were modified to introduce dynamic disturbances: the target object would move orthogonally to the gripper's approach path once the gripper was within a certain distance.
- **Results:**
 - In these perturbed environments, +SA methods again outperformed all baselines in terms of success rate.
 - This finding supports the framework's ability to enhance robustness and responsiveness to unexpected environmental changes, which is a key design objective.

**D. Real-World Experiment:**
- **Task:** A real Franka Research 3 robot was tasked with lifting a red block moving on a camera slider at a constant velocity. The slider's orientation was varied across 5 directions to introduce dynamic disturbances.
- **Setup:** A DDIM-based policy was deployed, using state observations extracted from real-world vision via Foundation Pose. DDIM+SA was compared to vanilla DDIM, with both tuned to have comparable average execution horizons (DDIM: T<sub>a</sub>=8; DDIM+SA: T<sub>avg</sub> ∈ [7.5, 8.5]).
- **Results:**
 - DDIM+SA achieved a substantially higher success rate (0.92) compared to vanilla DDIM (0.42).
 - Vanilla DDIM frequently failed to grasp the moving cube because its fixed, long action chunk prevented timely reactions to the cube's movement.
 - DDIM+SA, by contrast, dynamically shortened its execution horizon when the gripper was close to the cube (a high-attention phase), enabling it to adjust its motion to the cube's updated position and execute successful grasps. This confirmed the real-time effectiveness and enhanced robustness of the approach on a physical robot.

In summary, the main findings consistently demonstrate that adaptively adjusting the execution horizon based on Spatial Attention significantly improves success rates across various manipulation tasks, including those with external disturbances, in both simulation and real-world scenarios. This improvement is achieved while maintaining or even slightly reducing the average computational cost compared to fixed-horizon baselines.

### 5. Significance and potential impact

The research presented in this paper offers a significant contribution to the field of robotic learning, particularly for systems employing generative models for policy control. The introduction of Spatial Attention as a mathematically grounded criterion for adaptively adjusting action chunk execution horizons addresses a fundamental trade-off between responsiveness and computational efficiency that has challenged existing methods.

**Key areas of significance and potential impact include:**

1. **Enhanced Robustness and Responsiveness:** By dynamically shortening the execution horizon in phases of high Spatial Attention (i.e., when the policy's action distribution is highly sensitive to observation changes), the robot can respond more quickly and precisely to external disturbances, environmental variations, or accumulated errors. This directly translates to improved robustness and higher success rates in dynamic and uncertain real-world environments, as demonstrated in the perturbed simulation and real-robot experiments.
2. **Improved Computational Efficiency:** Conversely, during task phases with low Spatial Attention, the framework allows for longer execution horizons, reducing the frequency of computationally expensive generative model inferences. This adaptive approach ensures that computational resources are allocated strategically, leading to more efficient task completion without sacrificing overall performance, as confirmed by maintaining comparable average execution horizons.
3. **Theoretically Grounded Adaptive Control:** Unlike prior heuristic approaches to adaptive execution horizons, this work derives Spatial Attention from an optimization problem aimed at minimizing cumulative likelihood drop under a fixed sampling budget. This mathematical grounding provides a robust and principled basis for the adaptive control strategy, enhancing its reliability and interpretability.
4. **Generality and Broad Applicability:** The framework is designed to be compatible with any generative policy that produces action chunks, not just diffusion policies. The estimation of Spatial Attention relies on auxiliary score networks trained on demonstration data, making it independent of the specific policy architecture. This modularity suggests broad applicability across various generative robotic control systems.
5. **Advancement in Real-Time Robotic Control:** The ability to adapt execution horizons without requiring the generation of future observations makes the framework practical for real-time control, even with high-dimensional visual inputs. The successful deployment on a real robot demonstrates its feasibility and effectiveness in physical systems, which is crucial for moving research from simulation to practical applications.

**Potential impacts:**

- **More Capable Robotic Systems:** Robots equipped with this adaptive control framework could perform complex manipulation tasks with greater reliability in unstructured or dynamic environments, such as manufacturing, logistics, or assistive robotics.
- **Resource Optimization:** The ability to manage computational resources adaptively could be particularly beneficial for robots with limited on-board processing power or those operating in scenarios where power consumption is a critical factor.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The concept of Spatial Attention and its derivation could inspire further research into sensitivity-based adaptive control strategies for other aspects of robotic learning, such as adaptive learning rates or adaptive exploration.

**Limitations and Future Directions (as identified by the authors):**

- **Discrete Action Chunks:** The current framework requires discrete action chunks and therefore does not extend to streaming-based policies that generate actions continuously. Relaxing this restriction is an important area for future work.
- **Scaling to Large VLA Models:** The framework has not yet been explored with the massive datasets and complex architectures used to train Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. However, the authors suggest that leveraging the latent representations already learned by VLM backbones could provide a natural space for evaluating Spatial Attention, indicating a promising avenue for extension.

Overall, this research provides a significant step towards more intelligent, robust, and efficient robotic systems by addressing a critical challenge in generative policy execution through a principled and practical adaptive control mechanism.
