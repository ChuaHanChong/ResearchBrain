# Evidence of an Emergent "Self" in Continual Robot Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Columbia University developed a method to quantify an emergent 'self' within a robot's deep reinforcement learning policy, observing that a stable, behavior-invariant subnetwork spontaneously forms in continually learning quadruped controllers and persists across diverse locomotion tasks.

## Problem

- Quantifying whether an intelligent system develops a concept of a 'self' and distinguishing it from other cognitive structures.
- Traditional robot self-modeling relies on explicit segregation, leaving open the question of spontaneous emergence within monolithic deep reinforcement learning policies.
- Interpreting and disentangling internal representations within complex, unified deep reinforcement learning policies remains a challenge.

## Method

- Trained a simulated quadruped robot using ==Soft Actor-Critic (SAC)== on a sequence of three distinct locomotion behaviors (walk, wiggle, bob) in a ==continual learning== paradigm.
- Identified subnetworks within the learned neural networks by analyzing ==neuron co-activation patterns== and quantified their ==structural persistence== across successive behavior learning phases.
- Compared these findings to baseline policies trained on a single, constant task to differentiate effects unique to multi-behavior continual learning.

## Results

- Continual learning policies developed a dominant subnetwork, primarily in the first hidden layer, that demonstrated significantly higher mean persistence (**16.9 percentage points**, p < **0.001**) across different locomotion behaviors compared to task-specific components.
- Constant task baseline policies did not exhibit a distinct, highly persistent 'self' subnetwork, showing diffuse cross-policy connections and more uniformly distributed persistence scores.
- The self-like subnetwork's size developed and solidified early in training, plateauing and consistently showing smaller reorganization (percent change) than task-like regions during behavior switches, across various architectures and thresholds.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The 'self' in an intelligent system may correspond to the invariant portion of its cognitive process, remaining stable while task-specific knowledge and skills are acquired and updated.
> - Continual learning, by requiring adaptation to diverse sequential tasks, can spontaneously induce a persistent, shared internal structure within a neural network, which functions as an emergent self-model.
> - A principled approach to analyzing neuronal persistence and structural integrity within deep neural networks allows for the identification and characterization of these emergent, stable internal representations without explicit architectural design.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper addresses a long-standing philosophical question concerning the nature of "selfhood" and its inner signature, extending this inquiry into the domains of robotics and artificial intelligence. Historically, philosophical accounts of selfhood, such as those by Locke, Ricoeur, and Parfit, emphasize continuity through change as a core aspect of identity. Merleau-Ponty additionally stresses the grounding of a basic sense of self in the lived body and action. This research operationalizes these philosophical concepts by proposing a definition of a "minimal embodied self" in intelligent systems: the invariant portion of a cognitive process that changes minimally compared to more rapidly acquired skills.

Within the field of robotics and AI, this work builds upon existing research in robot self-modeling and body-schema learning. Prior approaches often explicitly segregate the robot's self-model from the rest of its controller, employing techniques such as learning internal models of morphology for resilience to damage, visual reconstruction of the body, or inferring body schema from sensor data. In contrast, this study hypothesizes that a persistent, shared internal structure—a self-model—can implicitly emerge within a standard deep learning policy under specific training conditions, without explicit architectural segregation or self-modeling components.

The research also contributes to the understanding of deep reinforcement learning (RL) policies. Despite their impressive performance, these policies are frequently treated as monolithic functions, making their internal representations challenging to interpret. By analyzing internal neural network structures, the paper seeks to disentangle task-specific knowledge from more fundamental, body-related representations.

Furthermore, this work is situated at the intersection of multi-task and continual learning. While multi-task learning involves simultaneous training on different tasks to promote shared representations for generalization, continual learning entails sequential training, which can lead to catastrophic forgetting due to distribution shifts. The tension inherent in the continual learning regime—between retaining essential knowledge and adapting to new tasks—provides a suitable environment to test whether a stable, behavior-invariant representation of the robot's own body can survive successive behavior switches, while task-specific components reorganize. The paper aims to reveal how a controller learns to separate invariant knowledge (the "self") from transient, task-specific knowledge when faced with varied experiences.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate whether a concept of a "self" can emerge and be identified within the cognitive structure of robots, particularly under conditions of continual learning. The authors propose a quantifiable definition of "self" as the invariant portion of a cognitive process that exhibits significantly less change compared to rapidly acquired cognitive skills, based on the principle that the self represents the most persistent aspect of an agent's experiences.

The specific motivations and objectives guiding this study are detailed as follows:

- **Quantify and Differentiate the "Self"**: A key motivation is to overcome the challenge of principled quantification and differentiation of a "self" concept in intelligent systems. The paper seeks to provide an empirical method for isolating a "self" from other cognitive structures within a robot's controller.
- **Test Philosophical Concepts in Robotics**: The work is motivated by philosophical accounts of selfhood, particularly the idea that selfhood involves continuity through change. The authors aim to test this principle in a robotic context by examining if a portion of a robot's controller remains stable as its behaviors change over time.
- **Investigate Emergent Internal Structure**: Rather than explicitly segregating a robot's self-model, the research is motivated by the hypothesis that multi-behavior training in a continual learning setting could spontaneously induce a persistent, shared internal structure within a standard deep learning policy. This structure, it is theorized, would encode the robot's own body and its intrinsic dynamics.
- **Disentangle Internal Representations in Deep RL**: Current deep reinforcement learning policies are often viewed as black boxes. The study aims to move beyond this monolithic perspective by probing the internal representations of a robot's controller to identify which components are stable and which reorganize in response to new tasks. This objective contributes to the broader goal of making deep learning models more interpretable.
- **Define a Minimal Embodied Self**: The authors explicitly aim for a modest definition of self, not claiming human-like consciousness or introspection. Instead, they seek to determine if, when the same robot body learns different tasks, part of its controller behaves more like a stable, reusable structure tied to the agent itself, and less like a task-specific solution. This constitutes a definition of a minimal embodied self characterized by continuity through change within a physically grounded agent.
- **Assess Functional Importance**: Beyond identifying a stable subnetwork, the research aims to determine if this persistent structure is merely a stable chunk or if it plays a functionally important and generalizable role in the robot's ability to adapt and perform across different behaviors.

In essence, the research is driven by the ambition to empirically demonstrate the emergence of an implicit, functionally important "self-like" cognitive structure within robots, defined by its persistence across varied experiences and its role in organizing action for the same physical agent.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a systematic approach to train and analyze robot controllers under continual learning conditions, comparing them to single-task baselines. The methodology involves diverse robot morphologies, a specific training curriculum, and detailed analysis techniques to identify and validate emergent neural structures.

**Training Setup and Curriculum:**
- **Robots and Morphologies**: Three distinct robot systems were used:
 1. A quadruped (Ant-like, 8 degrees of freedom, 2 actuated joints per leg) from IsaacLab.
 2. A hexapod (6 legs, 18 degrees of freedom, 3 actuated joints per leg).
 3. A robotic manipulation arm (from the Continual World and Meta-World benchmarks).
- **Learning Algorithm**: All robots were trained using Soft Actor–Critic (SAC), a deep reinforcement learning algorithm.
- **Continual Learning Paradigm**: A single agent morphology was trained sequentially across distinct behaviors or tasks in a cyclical sequence.
 - **Locomotion Behaviors (Quadruped & Hexapod)**: Walk (forward translation), Wiggle (in-place rotation), and Bob (vertical hopping). These were chosen to cover qualitatively distinct whole-body repertoires.
 - **Manipulation Tasks (Robotic Arm)**: Sequences of distinct manipulation task families, spanning tool use, articulated-object interaction, obstacle-aware pushing, pressing, sliding, and alignment-sensitive extraction.
- **Training Phases**: Each behavior or task within the curriculum was treated as a separate training phase with its own replay buffer and step budget. Only the policy weights were transferred between phases. The learning algorithm and hyperparameters were identical in every phase, with only the reward function changing.
- **Phase Switching**: Transitions between behaviors were controlled by plateau detection on episodic returns. This involved a minimum training period before checks, comparing returns in back-to-back sliding windows, and declaring convergence when performance was stable and above a minimum threshold. A hard cap on training steps per phase was also implemented.
- **Control Condition**: For comparison, constant-task policies (e.g., walk-only) were trained for the same total number of training phases as the continual-learning policies.
- **Replication**: Experiments were repeated across multiple independent runs with different random seeds (8 for constant-task, 10 for continual-learning quadruped, 6 for hexapod, 3 for manipulation) to ensure robustness. Learned behaviors were also validated on a physical quadruped for kinematic validity.

**Analysis of Persistent and Task-Specific Neural Structure:**
- **Observation and Hidden Activations**: For each trained policy, rollouts were generated, and a shared set of reference states (observations) was collected from successful episodes. All policies were then evaluated on this identical input set to ensure consistent state visitation. Hidden unit activations from the first two fully connected layers (or first two for manipulation for comparability) were recorded, normalized, and dead units were excluded.
- **Co-activation Matrices**: A neuron–neuron cosine similarity matrix was computed for each behavior and hidden layer, capturing how pairs of neurons' activations correlated across the shared reference states. The absolute value of this matrix was used as an affinity matrix.
- **Subnetwork Identification (Block Diagonalization)**: Subnetworks were identified by block diagonalization of the co-activation matrix, thresholded at $\tau=0.70$ (robustness to $\tau$ choice was verified). Neurons were reordered using reverse Cuthill–McKee (RCM) for visual clarity of blocks.
- **Neuron Family Matching (Cross-Behavior Alignment)**: To track equivalent neurons across different behavior networks, the Hungarian algorithm was used to solve an optimal one-to-one assignment problem based on cosine similarity between unit activation vectors. This aligned units consistently across behaviors despite permutation symmetries.
- **Persistence Score**: For each matched neuron family, a persistence score was calculated as the average of two cross-behavior consistency terms:
 1. **Activation-similarity**: Cosine similarity between the family’s activation vectors across behavior pairs.
 2. **Connectivity-similarity**: Cosine similarity between the corresponding row of the family–family co-activation matrix across behavior pairs.
- **Subnetwork-Level Aggregation**: The largest subnetwork identified by block diagonalization in each hidden layer was defined as the "self-like" subnetwork. All remaining neuron groups were pooled into a "task-like" subnetwork. Mean persistence scores were computed for these aggregated subnetworks. Percent change (100% * (1 - persistence)) was used to visualize reorganization.

**Intervention Analyses (Freezing and Lesioning):**
- **Purpose**: To assess the functional importance of the persistent subnetwork during transfer to a new behavior.
- **Methodology**: Interventions were performed during the learning phase of a new behavior. A fixed number of neurons were selected, and the same-sized intervention was applied to either the "self-like" or "task-like" subnetwork.
 - **Freezing**: Incoming weights to the selected neurons were frozen during further learning.
 - **Lesioning**: Selected neurons were reinitialized while preserving their overall statistics, disrupting their learned function.
- **Comparison**: Comparisons were made on matched transfer runs (sharing transition type, random seed, run ID, and source training cycle) to isolate the effect of the intervention target. Reward differences and within-transition rank-normalized comparisons were used as metrics.

**Robustness Checks:**
The methodology included testing sensitivity to the block diagonalization threshold ($\tau$), replicating findings across various actor architectures (e.g., number of layers, units per layer, activation functions, normalization settings), and validating on diverse robot morphologies.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several key findings demonstrating the emergence and functional significance of a "self-like" subnetwork in continually learning robots.

**1. Emergence of a Persistent “Self”-like Subnetwork:**
- **Differentiation in Continual Learning**: Robots undergoing continual learning across multiple distinct behaviors (walk, wiggle, bob for locomotion; various task families for manipulation) developed an identifiable, persistent subnetwork. In contrast, control policies trained on a single, constant task exhibited a more fragmented internal organization without a clear, trackable, stable core.
- **Quantitative Stability**: The identified persistent subnetwork in continual learning policies was significantly more stable (p $\ll$ 0.001) compared to corresponding neural groupings observed in constant-task scenarios. Visualizations (alluvial flows) confirmed that neurons within this dominant subnetwork remained grouped and showed strong, stable cross-policy flows across behavior switches, unlike the dispersed patterns in single-task baselines.
- **Persistence Score Evidence**: Quantitative diagnostics confirmed that the largest subnetwork in continual learning consistently showed substantially higher mean persistence scores, indicating preserved co-activation and connectivity structure across different behaviors.

**2. Functional Importance of the Persistent Subnetwork:**
- **Enhanced Adaptation with Freezing**: During transfer to a new behavior, freezing the weights of the self-like subnetwork led to better performance compared to freezing an equally sized task-like subnetwork. This advantage was observed in 92.7% of 594 matched transfer pairs (p < 0.01).
- **Impaired Performance with Lesioning**: Conversely, lesioning (reinitializing) the self-like subnetwork resulted in a larger performance drop than lesioning an equally sized task-like subnetwork, with 91.3% of 504 matched transfer pairs showing worse performance for the self-lesioned condition.
- These intervention results indicate that the persistent subnetwork is not merely a stable, passive component but plays a functionally important role in facilitating cross-behavior adaptation.

**3. Self Emergence and Persistence Across Layers and Cycles:**
- **Sustained Persistence Gap**: The self-like subnetwork consistently exhibited a higher mean persistence score than the pooled task-like subnetwork across all hidden layers and training cycles in continual-learning agents. This mean separation was 16.9 percentage points with 99% confidence, indicating sustained stability as the robot learned new behaviors.
- **Development Over Training**: The number of neurons constituting the self-like subnetwork grew early in training and then plateaued. In the quadruped's 150x150 architecture, Layer 1's self subnetwork stabilized at approximately 80 neurons, while Layer 2's plateaued at about 30 neurons. This suggests that earlier layers tend to develop a larger, more pronounced self-like representation.
- **Contrast with Baselines**: Single-task baselines showed significantly weaker and less consistent separation between the largest subnetwork and the remaining units, especially in the first hidden layer. The inferred self subnetwork sizes in these controls remained very small, never expanding into a substantial shared core. This evidence supports that the self–task separation is a specific outcome of continual multi-behavior switching.

**4. Reorganization Concentrates in Task-like Regions:**
- Analysis of weight changes during behavior switches revealed that reorganization primarily occurred outside the dominant self-like subnetwork. This pattern is consistent with a stable core (the "self") supported by a more plastic "task-like" substrate that flexibly adapts to new behavioral demands.

**5. Robustness and Generalizability:**
- The qualitative patterns of self-like subnetwork emergence and functional importance were robust across various experimental conditions:
 - Different random seeds for training.
 - A broad range of block diagonalization threshold ($\tau$) settings.
 - Alternative actor architectures (varying widths, number of layers, activation functions, and normalization).
 - Different robot morphologies (quadruped, hexapod, and robotic manipulation arm across four distinct task families).
- Validation on a physical quadruped confirmed that the learned behaviors were kinematically valid, demonstrating the practical applicability of the simulated policies.

In summary, the results provide empirical evidence for an emergent, persistent, and functionally significant "self-like" subnetwork within the controllers of robots trained under continual learning conditions, differentiating it from task-specific components and showing its robustness across diverse robot platforms.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research provides a novel empirical perspective on the concept of "self" in artificial intelligence and robotics, with several significant implications:

**1. A Testable Definition of "Self" in Robots:**
The study offers a concrete, empirically testable definition of a "minimal embodied self" in deep control systems. By defining the self as the portion of the controller that remains stable across changing behaviors while still enabling the same body to act, the paper operationalizes philosophical concepts of continuity through change (Locke, Ricoeur) and embodied action (Merleau-Ponty). This avoids claims of consciousness or introspection, focusing instead on observable behavior and internal stability. This approach provides a foundation for future investigations into selfhood in artificial agents that is grounded in quantifiable computational properties.

**2. Advancement in Deep Reinforcement Learning Interpretability:**
Deep RL policies are often treated as monolithic. This work demonstrates a method for disentangling reusable body-related dynamics from behavior-specific control within these complex networks. Identifying stable and plastic subnetworks provides a diagnostic tool for understanding the internal organization of learned policies, moving beyond black-box interpretations. This could lead to more transparent and explainable AI systems.

**3. Implications for Continual Learning and Catastrophic Forgetting:**
The spontaneous emergence of a modular, reusable core structure under continual learning conditions suggests a mechanism by which intelligent systems might cope with catastrophic forgetting. The "self-like" subnetwork, being stable and functionally important across tasks, acts as a persistent backbone that can retain essential knowledge about the agent's body while other components adapt to new tasks. This finding could inform the design of more effective continual learning algorithms that explicitly preserve or leverage such emergent stable structures.

**4. Improving Transfer Efficiency and Robustness:**
The identified self-like subnetwork could have practical implications for robot learning and adaptation:
- **Learning New Behaviors**: It could serve as a stable foundation or "backbone" when training robots on novel tasks, potentially accelerating learning and improving transfer efficiency by reducing the need to relearn fundamental body dynamics.
- **Robustness to Perturbations**: Monitoring or explicitly freezing this self-like core during fine-tuning could prevent the overwriting of crucial body-related representations. This might enhance robustness to physical perturbations, such as joint damage, sensor failures, or even morphological changes, by preserving the agent's fundamental understanding of its own embodiment.
- **Scalable System Design**: The emergence of a modular, reusable core aligns with broader principles of scalable system design, suggesting that this inherent modularity could be exploited to build more adaptable and robust robotic systems.

**5. Generalizability Across Domains:**
The replication of findings across diverse robot morphologies (quadruped, hexapod, manipulation arm) and task domains (locomotion, manipulation) suggests that the principle of an emergent self-like subnetwork is not specific to a particular body plan or control task. This broad generalizability enhances the significance of the findings and opens avenues for exploring this phenomenon in a wider range of embodied AI systems.

**6. Future Research Directions:**
The research opens several future avenues, including testing the stability and utility of the self-like subnetwork with expanding behavior suites, deploying agents on physical hardware, especially for robots undergoing physical reconfiguration, and integrating complementary interpretability tools like causal interventions or sparsity-based decompositions to further elucidate the necessity and sufficiency of these subnetworks.

In conclusion, this work significantly contributes to the understanding of how fundamental self-representations can emerge in artificial agents without explicit design, offering both a theoretical framework for investigating rudimentary selfhood and practical implications for developing more intelligent, adaptable, and robust robotic systems in the future.
