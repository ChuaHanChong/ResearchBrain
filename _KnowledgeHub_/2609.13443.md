# Learning to Solve Hard Problems in RL for LLMs by Never Giving Up

> [!summary] Summary
> Never Give Up (NGU) is an adaptive sampling method for RL post-training of LLMs. Instead of a fixed number of completions per prompt, it keeps resampling any unsolved prompt with continuation probability p until a correct completion appears, then forms one large GRPO group anchored on the positives. Leveraging asynchronous RL, it shifts compute from easy to hard problems, improving pass@1 most on the hardest math and coding problems.

## Problem

- Practitioners assume RL trains uniformly across a difficulty range, but published RL-trained math, code and agentic models gain almost nothing on their hardest problems.
- The standard signal-loss remedy, raising completions-per-prompt K, fails: under a fixed batch size it merely admits more already-easy prompts into training batches.
- Explicit difficulty curricula and easy-prompt removal are brittle, hyperparameter-sensitive, and assume static difficulty, so models regress on problems they had previously solved.

## Method

- **Never Give Up**: sample K completions per prompt, stop at the first correct answer, otherwise requeue and continue with probability p, giving geometric sample counts averaging K/(1-p).
- ==Maintaining previous completions==: keep resampled negatives younger than T steps and merge them into one large ==GRPO group==, so rare correct answers get a much larger advantage.
- ==Anchoring the positives==: baseline over all sampled rewards, keep positive advantages intact, rescale remaining negatives by the positive-to-negative ratio so group advantages still sum to zero.

## Results

- On GSM8k Platinum with Qwen2.5 0.5B, NGU at **p=0.95** with **K=4** beats every fixed-K baseline from **K=4** to **K=32**, gains concentrated on extra-hard problems.
- Compute-matched at roughly **120 H100 hours** on Deepscaler with Qwen3 4B, NGU improves hard AIME and BRUMO problems beyond **K=16/32/64** baselines and a fixed-K curriculum.
- Ablations: staleness cap **T=4** beats **T=1, 8 and 16**; anchoring beats no-rescale and negative-downsampling; on Manufactoria GRPO stalls under **20%** on hard tests while NGU passes all tests.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Hard-problem progress is a compute-allocation problem, not a sampling-count problem: oversampling already-solved prompts wastes budget that adaptive early stopping recovers.
> - Problem difficulty shifts during training, so precomputed curricula hurt easy problems; online allocation tracks difficulty as the model itself changes.
> - LLM RL escapes the plasticity loss of from-scratch RL: 3000 steps under a poor reward still recovered once a better training signal arrived.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

This work addresses reinforcement learning (RL) for large language model (LLM) post-training, particularly the use of verifiable rewards for mathematical reasoning and code generation. Current LLM training pipelines commonly combine pre-training, supervised or instruction fine-tuning, and RL. RL is used to improve performance beyond the supervised data by reinforcing outputs that satisfy an external evaluator, such as a mathematical answer checker or software test suite.

A central algorithmic baseline in this area is Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). GRPO samples several completions for each prompt and calculates each completion’s advantage relative to the average reward within that group. This approach is computationally convenient, but it uses a fixed number of samples per prompt. The paper argues that fixed sampling is poorly matched to heterogeneous datasets containing both easy and difficult problems.

The authors identify a pattern they call the **Matthew Effect in RL for LLMs**. In the original sociological and economic usage, the Matthew Effect describes cumulative advantage: entities that already have advantages tend to receive more benefits. In this paper, the analogous observation is that RL improves problems in proportion to the model’s initial competence. Easy problems, which the model already solves relatively often, improve substantially, whereas difficult problems receive smaller gains or remain unsolved.

This issue is related to several existing research directions. Some prior methods address **signal loss**, in which all sampled completions receive the same reward and therefore produce no GRPO gradient. Other approaches use explicit difficulty curricula, remove easy prompts, increase the number of completions per prompt, or use external information to guide exploration. The present paper distinguishes its focus from these methods by emphasizing **signal efficiency**: the amount of useful learning signal obtained per unit of generation compute.

The authors also connect their method to asynchronous RL systems, which allow completed sampling jobs to be returned and replaced independently rather than forcing the entire batch to wait for the slowest prompt. This infrastructure is important because the proposed method reallocates computation dynamically across prompts.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

The paper has four main objectives.

First, it seeks to establish whether RL improves all problems in a dataset uniformly. The authors analyze RL-trained models in mathematics, code completion, and agentic coding. They classify examples by their difficulty for the initial model and compare initial and post-RL performance. Their objective is to determine whether the apparent success of RL is concentrated on tasks that were already relatively easy.

Second, the paper investigates why hard problems improve less. A straightforward explanation is that difficult problems require more samples before the model produces a correct solution. In GRPO, if all sampled completions are incorrect, the group contributes no useful relative-reward signal. The authors test whether increasing the number of completions per prompt is sufficient to address this problem.

Third, the paper evaluates an alternative explanation based on compute allocation. Fixed group sizes may waste computation on easy problems. For example, a large group can continue generating redundant samples for a problem that the model already solves reliably. Conversely, a small group may fail to discover a rare correct solution for a hard problem. The authors therefore aim to create an adaptive mechanism that samples easy problems briefly but continues sampling difficult problems when they remain unresolved.

Fourth, the paper tests whether this adaptive approach transfers beyond mathematical problems to coding tasks in which difficulty exists not only across prompts but also across individual test cases within a prompt. This setting is important because a program may pass many simple tests while failing a small number of difficult tests, causing per-test reward optimization to plateau before the full problem is solved.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Diagnosing the Matthew Effect

The authors first analyze three open-source RL-trained models:

- Olmo 3.1 RL-Zero Math on AIME mathematics problems.
- DeepCoder on LiveCodeBench version 5.
- DeepSWE-Preview on SWE-bench Verified.

They compare each RL-trained model with its initial model and divide evaluation examples into difficulty groups. For mathematics and code completion, difficulty is estimated from the initial model’s pass rate. For SWE-bench, the study uses human-time difficulty buckets supplied by the benchmark.

Across all three domains, the authors report that performance gains are largest on easy examples and smallest on hard examples. They interpret this as evidence that initial model competence strongly influences where RL produces improvements.

#### 3.2. GRPO Testbed and the Signal-Loss Hypothesis

To study the mechanism more carefully, the authors construct a controlled mathematical testbed using Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct and the cleaned GSM8K Platinum dataset. They create four evaluation categories—easy, medium, hard, and extra hard—based on the initial model’s sampling success rates. The categories correspond approximately to pass@1 values of 25%, 10%, 5%, and 0%, respectively. For some extra-hard problems, the model does not produce a correct answer even among 1,024 samples.

The model is trained with GRPO using different group sizes, \(K \in \{4, 8, 16, 32\}\). The number of prompts per batch is adjusted so that the total number of sampled completions remains constant. This makes it possible to compare the effect of group size without simply increasing total batch compute.

The results show that all group-size settings exhibit the Matthew Effect. Increasing \(K\) does not consistently improve difficult-problem learning; in the controlled GSM8K experiments, \(K=4\) performs best overall and particularly on the hardest subset. The authors argue that larger groups have two opposing effects. They increase the probability of finding a rare correct answer on a hard problem, but they also increase the probability that an easy problem contains at least one incorrect completion. That mixed group receives a gradient and remains in the training batch, causing more compute to be spent on already-solved prompts.

#### 3.3. Never Give Up Sampling

The paper introduces **Never Give Up (NGU)**, an adaptive sampling procedure designed to reallocate generation compute.

NGU initially samples a small group of completions, such as four, for a prompt. If all completions are correct, the prompt is treated as easy and removed from further sampling for that training instance. If all completions are incorrect, NGU resamples the same prompt rather than immediately discarding it. It continues this process with probability \(p_{\text{NGU}}\); otherwise, it gives up and moves to another prompt. This produces a geometric distribution over the number of samples allocated to a prompt. The expected number of sampled completions is approximately \(K/(1-p_{\text{NGU}})\).

The procedure depends on asynchronous RL. Easy prompts can be filtered quickly, and the available sampling capacity can immediately be used for other prompts. In a synchronous system, the batch would still need to wait for difficult prompts, reducing the compute-reallocation benefit.

NGU also stores previous completions for a prompt. When a correct completion is eventually found, the method can combine it with earlier incorrect completions to form a larger GRPO group. Because older completions may be off-policy, the authors test age limits \(T \in \{1,4,8,16\}\). They find that retaining completions up to approximately four training steps old is beneficial, while retaining substantially older samples degrades performance.

To use filtered older samples in the advantage calculation, the authors introduce **anchoring the positives**. Positive completions are retained with their original advantages, while the advantages of the remaining negative completions are rescaled so that the total advantage remains centered at zero. This outperforms simply discarding old samples or downsampling negatives to balance positive and negative examples.

#### 3.4. Scaling to Mathematics and Coding

For larger-scale mathematics experiments, the authors train Qwen3-4B-Base on a 10,000-example subset of DeepScaleR, using AIME 2025 and BRUMO November 2025 for evaluation. They compare GRPO baselines with \(K=16,32,64\) against NGU using \(K=16\) and continuation probabilities of 0.5, 0.75, and 0.875. Runs are compute-matched at approximately 120 H100 GPU-hours per setting.

For coding, they use Manufactoria, a task requiring models to construct programs that sort robots according to colored tape patterns. Each problem contains 14–30 tests with varying difficulty. The study uses Qwen3-4B-Instruct and compares standard GRPO with per-test rewards against GRPO augmented with NGU.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. The Matthew Effect Is Observed Across Domains

The initial cross-domain analysis shows that RL gains are unevenly distributed. On AIME, LiveCodeBench, and SWE-bench, easy examples receive substantially larger improvements than hard examples. The effect appears across different models and task types, suggesting that it is not limited to one benchmark or one implementation of RL.

The authors characterize this as a form of primacy bias arising from the model’s pretrained capabilities. However, they distinguish it from the plasticity-loss mechanism reported in some RL-from-scratch settings. In their coding experiments, an LLM can later recover from a suboptimal reward objective, indicating that the model is not permanently trapped by its initial training trajectory.

#### 4.2. Fixed Larger Groups Do Not Resolve Hard-Problem Learning

The GSM8K experiments indicate that increasing the number of completions per prompt is not a general solution. Under a fixed total sampling budget, larger \(K\) values can preserve easy prompts in the training batch because a single failure among many samples is sufficient to create a nonzero GRPO gradient. Smaller groups more aggressively filter easy prompts over time and eventually produce batches with a higher proportion of difficult examples.

However, the authors also show that \(K\) can be too small on very difficult tasks because the model may rarely discover a correct answer. This creates a trade-off between obtaining enough samples to find positives and avoiding redundant sampling of easy problems.

#### 4.3. NGU Improves Compute Allocation and Hard-Problem Performance

On the controlled GSM8K task, NGU outperforms fixed-\(K\) baselines, with the largest gains on extra-hard problems. Its training batches contain fewer easy prompts and more hard prompts than the fixed-group baselines. It therefore combines some advantages of small and large group sizes: it filters easy problems efficiently while allowing additional sampling for difficult ones.

The ablations show that three design choices are important. Setting \(p_{\text{NGU}}=1\) can cause the system to stall on prompts that are effectively unsolvable. Using synchronous rather than asynchronous RL removes much of the compute-allocation advantage. Finally, anchoring positive advantages performs better than downsampling negative completions.

On DeepScaleR, baseline Qwen3-4B performance averages 12.6% across AIME 2025 and BRUMO 2025. The GRPO baselines reach averages between 24.5% and 25.3%. NGU reaches 26.1–26.5%, depending on the continuation probability. Its main advantage is on hard examples: the best fixed-\(K\) baseline improves hard-problem pass@1 by approximately 2.5 percentage points over the initial model, whereas NGU settings achieve improvements of roughly 3.0–4.3 points. The \(p_{\text{NGU}}=0.875\) setting achieves a 26.5% combined average and a 4.3-point hard-subset improvement.

A curriculum baseline based on initial difficulty also improves hard examples, but it reduces performance on easy problems. The authors interpret this as evidence that problem difficulty changes during training and that static difficulty estimates are insufficient.

#### 4.4. NGU Addresses Test-Level Stagnation in Coding

In Manufactoria, standard GRPO with per-test rewards learns to pass many easy tests and some medium tests but largely stagnates on the hardest tests. The result is a high aggregate test pass rate without reliably solving all tests for any individual problem.

NGU initially performs slightly worse on the combined test metric, but it continues improving on hard tests. This improvement eventually transfers to the all-tests criterion, allowing the model to fully solve coding problems that standard GRPO does not complete. When compared with a two-stage approach that switches from per-test rewards to an all-tests reward after a warm-up period, per-test reward combined with NGU achieves approximately comparable compute efficiency.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The paper’s main contribution is to frame difficult-problem learning in LLM RL as a compute-allocation problem rather than solely as a failure to sample a positive example. This distinction has implications for the design of post-training systems. If training compute is fixed, allocating equal sampling effort to every prompt may systematically favor problems that are already easy for the model.

NGU provides a relatively simple implementation strategy that does not require an explicit difficulty model, a permanently altered training distribution, or extensive curriculum hyperparameters. It preserves the original prompt distribution while adaptively changing how many completions are generated for each prompt. The results suggest that this can improve performance per unit of compute, particularly on difficult mathematical problems and coding tasks with hard unresolved tests.

The method may be useful in other verifiable-reward settings, including theorem proving, structured planning, tool use, and software engineering. Its applicability is less clear when most prompts are difficult, when rewards are noisy, or when tasks require long multi-step interactions rather than single-response contextual-bandit optimization. The authors also note that extensive asynchronous resampling can make rollouts increasingly off-policy, potentially harming training stability.

Overall, the work highlights a limitation of evaluating RL progress using only aggregate pass rates. A model may show substantial average improvement while making little progress on the hardest cases. Tracking performance by difficulty and measuring compute efficiency can provide a more informative assessment of whether RL is expanding a model’s capabilities or primarily reinforcing existing strengths.
