# LLMPhy: Complex Physical Reasoning Using Large Language Models and World Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Mitsubishi Electric Research Labs (MERL) developed LLMPHY, a framework integrating Large Language Models (LLMs) with physics engines to perform complex physical reasoning. It uses LLMs for zero-shot black-box optimization, iteratively refining physical parameters and scene layouts based on simulator feedback, and achieved 62.0% mIoU accuracy on the new TraySim dataset.

## Problem

- Large Language Models (LLMs) struggle with precise, dynamic physical reasoning due to their inability to directly interact with the physical world or measure unobservable attributes like friction.
- Existing LLM-based methods often rely on 'intuitive physics' which is insufficient for exact predictions, while physics-informed models require extensive data or struggle with complex multi-body interactions.
- Traditional black-box optimization methods lack semantic understanding of physical parameters, making exploration less efficient in complex, non-differentiable physical systems.

## Method

- **LLMPHY** introduces a zero-shot black-box optimization framework that synergizes an LLM's program synthesis capabilities with a high-fidelity physics engine (**MuJoCo**).
- In Phase 1, the LLM iteratively infers unobservable physical parameters (e.g., friction, damping) by generating code for the simulator, receiving trajectory error feedback, and refining its parameter proposals.
- In Phase 2, the LLM reconstructs the task scene layout from images and then uses the inferred physical parameters to simulate object dynamics and answer physical reasoning questions about stability.

## Results

- LLMPHY achieved a mean Intersection-over-Union (mIoU) accuracy of 62.0% on the `TraySim` QA task, outperforming a pure LLM baseline (32.1% mIoU) and traditional black-box optimization methods (59.6-59.7% mIoU).
- LLM-based optimization showed faster initial convergence and produced more accurate physical parameter estimations compared to Bayesian Optimization and CMA-ES when minimizing trajectory errors.
- Providing the full optimization trace (history of past estimations and errors) to the LLM improved performance by 5% Avg. IoU, indicating the importance of historical context for effective iterative refinement.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The integration of LLMs with physics engines effectively provides LLMs with an interactive 'world model,' enabling them to perform grounded, dynamic physical reasoning beyond purely linguistic understanding.
> - LLMs can function as intelligent optimizers in black-box settings, leveraging their semantic understanding of physical attributes to achieve faster initial convergence and more accurate parameter estimation than purely numerical optimization methods.
> - The framework successfully handles both continuous optimization (for physical parameters) and discrete optimization (for scene layout and object properties), demonstrating versatility in complex physical reasoning tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The emergence of large language models (LLMs) has led to their application in various complex real-world problems, including robotic planning, code generation, mathematical reasoning, and scientific discovery. However, a consistent challenge identified in existing literature is the struggle of even advanced LLMs with reasoning about physical interactions, particularly their dynamic engagement with the physical world and the crucial problem of identifying unobservable physical parameters (e.g., mass, friction) that govern scene dynamics.

Prior work has explored integrating LLMs with external tools or simulators. Some approaches use LLMs as high-level reasoners or program synthesizers, where the LLM might query a physics engine for experimental outcomes without a continuous feedback loop for parameter refinement (e.g., Mind's Eye, Liu et al., 2022) or assume access to in-context examples for guidance (e.g., SimLM, Memery et al., 2023). Other related areas include scene de-rendering using analysis-by-synthesis with simulation (e.g., Galileo, Wu et al., 2015) to infer latent properties, and differentiable physics engines (e.g., GradSim, Murthy et al., 2020) that treat physical parameters as learnable variables for gradient-based optimization. Furthermore, LLMs have been investigated as optimizers for various problems.

This work, LLMPhy, positions itself by addressing the explicit identification of latent physical parameters within a black-box optimization framework that integrates LLMs with physics simulators. Unlike differentiable physics engines, LLMPhy operates in a gradient-free, black-box setting, allowing the use of any off-the-shelf physics engine without requiring explicit symbolic forms or differentiability. It extends prior LLM-simulator integrations by employing a closed-loop analysis-by-synthesis optimization, leveraging the LLM's program synthesis capabilities and inherent physics knowledge for iterative refinement. It also differentiates from existing physical reasoning benchmarks (e.g., Physion, PHYRE, CLEVRER, ComPhy) which primarily focus on outcome prediction or high-level causal reasoning, but typically do not supervise or evaluate the active recovery of latent physical parameters. Thus, LLMPhy introduces a novel problem setup and accompanying datasets specifically designed for parameter-identifiable physical reasoning in zero-shot settings.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable robust and parameter-identifiable physical reasoning in complex, contact-rich scenes by integrating large language models with physics engines. The work aims to overcome the current limitations of LLMs, which, despite possessing vast inherent knowledge, often struggle with dynamic engagement with the physical world and the inference of unobservable physical parameters essential for accurate predictions.

The core motivation stems from the recognition that real-world applications such as collision avoidance in autonomous systems and robotic manipulation necessitate precise world models. Such models rely on an accurate understanding and estimation of governing physical properties (e.g., mass, friction, restitution) and scene layouts. Humans typically approach such problems by constructing mental models and reasoning through underlying physics to predict outcomes. The research seeks to empower machine learning models with a similar capability to construct "digital twins" of physical scenes.

Specifically, the key objectives and motivations are detailed as follows:
- **Parameter Identification**: To address the crucial problem of identifying latent physical parameters (e.g., sliding friction, stiffness, damping, inertia, mass) that dynamically govern scene interactions. This is presented as a continuous optimization problem.
- **Scene Layout Estimation**: To reconstruct observable scene properties, such as object placements, types, and appearances, from input observations. This is framed as a discrete optimization problem.
- **Digital Twin Construction**: To combine parameter and layout estimations to build accurate digital twins of input scenes, which can then be used for precise outcome prediction and complex physical reasoning.
- **Leveraging LLM and Physics Engine Strengths**: To bridge the high-level reasoning and program synthesis capabilities of LLMs with the detailed, realistic world models implemented in modern physics engines. This integration is designed to overcome the "inability to dynamically engage with the world" often observed in LLMs.
- **Zero-Shot Reasoning**: To achieve parameter identifiability and physical reasoning in zero-shot settings, meaning without requiring extensive task-specific training data.
- **Creation of New Benchmarks**: To develop novel datasets and tasks that explicitly account for parameter identifiability, as existing physical reasoning benchmarks typically do not serve this purpose.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

LLMPhy operates as a black-box optimization framework that iteratively combines large language models with physics simulators. The fundamental approach involves using the LLM's program synthesis capabilities to generate executable code that configures a physics engine. The simulation results are then used as feedback to refine the LLM's subsequent parameter estimations, forming an implicit analysis-by-synthesis loop.

The methodology is structured into two distinct phases:

**Phase 1: Physical Parameter Estimation (Continuous Problem)**
This phase focuses on inferring the unobservable continuous physical attributes ($\Phi_c$) of object types present in a scene.
- **Input**: The LLM receives a task prompt describing the setup, in-context examples of simulator API usage and object trajectories, and a multi-view auxiliary video. From this video, 3D trajectories of object centers ($\tau(X_T)$) are extracted. In the synthetic datasets, these trajectories are directly provided from the simulator; for real-world experiments, computer vision methods are employed.
- **LLM Program Synthesis**: The LLM (e.g., OpenAI models, Gemma3, Phi-4) is prompted to generate Python code ($\pi(\Phi)$). This code encodes estimates for physical parameters such as sliding-friction, stiffness, damping, and inertia for each object type. Object masses are assumed fixed and known for TraySim, but estimated for CLEVRER-LLMPhy and Real-TraySim. The parameters are named using their physical interpretations to aid the LLM's reasoning.
- **Physics Simulation**: A physics simulator (MuJoCo for TraySim and Real-TraySim, PyBullet for CLEVRER-LLMPhy) executes the generated program. This produces simulated object trajectories.
- **Error Calculation and Feedback**: The L2 norm between the simulated trajectories and the ground-truth (or estimated from video) trajectories is computed as a reconstruction error. This error, along with the program trace from previous iterations, is fed back to the LLM as a refinement prompt. The process iterates for up to 30 steps, with parameters from the best-performing iteration selected.

**Phase 2: Scene Layout Estimation (Discrete Problem)**
This phase aims to reconstruct the observable discrete scene properties, such as object types, locations, and colors.
- **Input**: A vision-and-language model (LVLM, e.g., GPT-4o, GPT-4.1) receives multi-view query images ($x_g$) and a prompt introducing the Phase 2 APIs.
- **LVLM Program Synthesis**: The LVLM generates Python code ($\pi(\Psi)$) that describes the scene layout, including the type, location (on a grid), and color for each object.
- **Physics Simulation**: The simulator executes this program to render the reconstructed initial scene image.
- **Error Calculation and Feedback**: The Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR) between the rendered image and the input query image is used as a reconstruction error. This error, along with previous program traces and identification of misidentified objects (through image difference analysis and an auxiliary LVLM), is fed back to the LVLM. This phase typically runs for up to 5 iterations.

**Forward Simulation and Solution**
Once both the physical parameters ($\Phi^*$) from Phase 1 and the layout attributes ($\Psi^*$) from Phase 2 are estimated, a final forward simulation is performed. The query scene, configured with the inferred parameters and layout, is animated in the physics engine. The final state of objects (e.g., upright/fallen, involved in collisions) is extracted to derive the solution for the reasoning task.

**Datasets and Benchmarks**
The researchers introduce three new zero-shot datasets:
- **TraySim**: Synthetic dataset using MuJoCo. Features a circular tray with multiple objects (bottles, martini glasses, wine glasses) arranged on a grid, impacted by a pusher. Task: Predict which objects remain upright after impact. It evaluates inference of sliding friction, damping, inertia, and stiffness.
- **CLEVRER-LLMPhy**: Synthetic dataset using PyBullet, inspired by CLEVRER. Features spheres and cubes in multi-step interaction. Task: Predict objects involved in collisions when an initial sphere is set in motion. It evaluates inference of mass, sliding friction, rolling friction, and restitution.
- **Real-TraySim**: Real-world robotic manipulation task. A Kinova Gen3 robot pushes boxes on a tray. Computer vision techniques (MIDAS for depth, SAM for segmentation, AprilTags/classical trackers for 3D trajectories) are used to create a digital twin from RGB video. Task: Estimate tray-object physics from one video and transfer to predict outcomes under different push speeds.

**Evaluation Metrics and Baselines**
Performance is measured using mean Intersection-over-Union (mIoU) for reasoning task accuracy and mean absolute errors for parameter recovery. Baselines include Bayesian optimization, Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy (CMA-ES), random sampling, direct "intuitive physics" inference by an LVLM, and human performance.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of LLMPhy across three distinct datasets demonstrated its efficacy in parameter-identifiable physical reasoning.

- **Overall Performance and Baseline Comparison**:
 - LLMPhy consistently outperformed traditional black-box optimization baselines (Bayesian optimization, CMA-ES) and other comparative methods across all reasoning tasks.
 - On the **TraySim** task, LLMPhy achieved 6–12% higher accuracy on reasoning tasks compared to baselines. In combined Phase 1 (physics parameter estimation) and Phase 2 (scene layout estimation) evaluations, LLMPhy showed an approximately 7% improvement in accuracy (e.g., 66.3% with o3-mini+GPT-4.1 vs. 59.7% with CMA-ES).
 - Human subjects achieved a modest 54.2% accuracy on TraySim, and direct LVLM inference without the LLMPhy loop yielded only 32.1%, underscoring the complexity of the task and the necessity for deep physical reasoning.
 - On **CLEVRER-LLMPhy**, LLMPhy demonstrated a clear advantage, achieving 97.2% mIoU compared to 91.7% for Bayesian Optimization and 85.2% for CMA-ES in Phase 1 (with ground truth Phase 2). This indicated generalizability across different simulators and task types.

- **Parameter Recovery Accuracy**:
 - LLMPhy consistently achieved lower mean absolute errors for estimated physical parameters (e.g., sliding-friction, inertia, stiffness, damping) on TraySim compared to Bayesian optimization and CMA-ES (Table 3). This supports the hypothesis that LLMs leverage their embedded physical priors to propose more plausible and accurate parameter values.

- **Convergence Speed and Stability**:
 - Stronger LLMs (e.g., OpenAI o1, o1-mini) integrated within LLMPhy demonstrated faster and more stable convergence during the black-box optimization process for Phase 1 parameter estimation (Figures 5a, 5c). While some traditional methods like CMA-ES could achieve comparable error minimization over many iterations, LLMPhy often identified good parameter values earlier.
 - The iterative refinement mechanism in Phase 2 for layout estimation led to substantial improvements, with LVLM accuracy increasing by nearly 20% over 1–5 iterations (Table 2), highlighting the benefit of discrete optimization capabilities.

- **Impact of LLM/LVLM Strength**:
 - The performance of LLMPhy was observed to be directly correlated with the strength of the underlying LLM/LVLM. Stronger reasoning models (e.g., OpenAI o1, GPT-4.1) consistently delivered higher accuracy and more effective convergence than weaker or older models (e.g., Gemma3-12B, GPT-4o).

- **Real-World Application**:
 - Experiments on the **Real-TraySim** dataset confirmed the real-world applicability of LLMPhy. While performing comparably to baselines on input sequences, LLMPhy exhibited lower generalization error when parameters estimated from one push speed sequence were applied to predict outcomes for different push speeds (Figure 5d).
 - Inferred mass values from LLMPhy variants were found to be closer to the approximate true mass of the real-world objects compared to underestimations by CMA-ES or overestimations by Bayesian Optimization (Table 5), suggesting a more intuitive and accurate parameter inference.

- **Ablation Studies**:
 - LLMPhy demonstrated scalability to a higher number of object types, maintaining strong performance.
 - The framework showed robustness to noise injected into object trajectories during Phase 1 feedback, with only a minor drop in accuracy (approximately 5%).
 - Utilizing the full optimization trace (history of previous parameter choices and errors) significantly improved performance (approximately 5% mIoU increase), indicating its importance for guiding the LLM's parameter search.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The LLMPhy framework represents a notable advancement in the field of machine learning and artificial intelligence, particularly concerning physical reasoning and the integration of large language models with specialized tools.

- **Addressing a Critical Gap**: The work addresses a significant limitation of existing LLMs, which, despite their broad knowledge, often lack the capability for precise physical reasoning requiring the identification of latent physical parameters. By combining LLM-driven program synthesis with high-fidelity physics simulators, LLMPhy provides a systematic approach to inferring these unobservable properties.
- **Enabling Digital Twin Construction**: LLMPhy offers a principled methodology for constructing "digital twins" of physical scenes. This capability is foundational for accurate predictive modeling, counterfactual reasoning, and simulation-driven decision-making in complex environments.
- **Advancing Embodied AI and Robotics**: The ability to accurately estimate physical parameters and reconstruct scene layouts in a zero-shot manner has direct implications for embodied AI systems, such as robotics and autonomous vehicles. It could lead to more robust robotic manipulation, improved collision avoidance systems, and generally more intelligent agents capable of operating effectively in the physical world.
- **Novelty in Optimization**: The framework demonstrates a novel application of LLMs as black-box optimizers, capable of navigating both continuous (physical parameters) and discrete (scene layout) optimization spaces through program synthesis and iterative feedback. This extends the understanding of LLMs' capabilities beyond mere language generation or high-level planning.
- **New Benchmarks for Physical Reasoning**: The introduction of three new datasets (TraySim, CLEVRER-LLMPhy, and Real-TraySim) specifically designed to evaluate parameter identifiability and reasoning under partial observability provides valuable resources for future research in this area. These benchmarks encourage development beyond simple outcome prediction.
- **Implications for AI Development**: The findings suggest that integrating well-designed software modules (like physics engines) as tools for LLMs can significantly enhance their performance on complex problems. This approach, leveraging the LLM's inherent world knowledge and program synthesis abilities, points towards a broader paradigm for building more capable and adaptable AI systems.
- **Future Research Directions**: The work identifies several avenues for future research, including improving the computational efficiency of LLM-driven optimization, exploring integration with more advanced or specialized simulators, and extending the framework to tackle broader classes of reasoning tasks, such as causality and planning, or real-world robotic control.
