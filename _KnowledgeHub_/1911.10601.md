# Scaling active inference

> [!summary] Summary
> A scalable implementation of active inference is presented, enabling its application to high-dimensional continuous control tasks with superior sample efficiency. The approach integrates amortized inference with neural networks, Cross-Entropy Method for policy optimization, and particle-based trajectory sampling to handle complex environments, demonstrating improved exploration and faster learning compared to model-free baselines.

## Problem

- Active inference, despite its theoretical power, was largely confined to simplified, low-dimensional problems due to computational constraints.
- Previous implementations struggled with representing complex belief distributions and accurately quantifying uncertainty in high-dimensional continuous spaces.
- Evaluating policies through expected free energy calculations became computationally intractable in continuous action and state domains.

## Method

- Neural networks are used for ==amortized inference==, parameterizing both the generative model (transition and observation) and the ==recognition model== to handle high-dimensional spaces.
- Policy optimization for continuous action spaces is performed using the ==Cross-Entropy Method (CEM)==, which samples and refines action sequences based on ==expected free energy== evaluation.
- Uncertainty is propagated through time using a ==particle-based trajectory sampling== approach for more accurate estimation of expected outcomes during policy evaluation.

## Results

- The active inference agent demonstrated enhanced exploration in the MountainCar environment, exploring the state space more thoroughly than reward-driven or ε-greedy agents.
- It achieved superior sample efficiency, reaching optimal performance on the Pendulum task in fewer than **100 epochs**, compared to over **1000 epochs** for DDPG.
- The agent showed robust performance across various continuous control benchmarks, including Pendulum and Hopper, converging to high-reward policies with an **order of magnitude fewer samples** than strong model-free baselines.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Active inference, when scaled with neural networks and advanced planning methods, can effectively handle high-dimensional continuous control tasks.
> - The Expected Free Energy (EFE) framework naturally unifies exploration and exploitation under a single objective of free energy minimization.
> - Active inference provides a theoretically grounded approach to uncertainty-driven exploration by considering both pragmatic value (reward) and epistemic value (information gain).

## Detailed Report

### 1. Introduction

Active inference has emerged as an influential framework in computational neuroscience that attempts to unify perception, action, and learning under a single principle: the minimization of free energy. While theoretically powerful, active inference has largely been confined to simplified, low-dimensional problems due to computational constraints. This paper by Tschantz, Baltieri, Seth, and Buckley addresses this limitation by presenting a scalable implementation of active inference capable of handling high-dimensional continuous control tasks.

The research bridges the gap between the theoretical elegance of active inference and the practical demands of complex reinforcement learning problems. By demonstrating that active inference can be scaled to solve challenging continuous control tasks with remarkable sample efficiency, the authors provide a significant contribution to both the active inference literature and the broader field of reinforcement learning.

### 2. Active Inference Framework

Active inference posits that biological agents maintain internal models of their environment and select actions that minimize the difference between predicted and observed sensory inputs. This process can be formalized through the concept of free energy minimization, which comprises two key components:

1. **Variational Free Energy (VFE)**: Represents the discrepancy between the agent's beliefs about the world and its actual observations. Minimizing VFE leads to accurate perception.

2. **Expected Free Energy (EFE)**: Guides action selection by evaluating potential future states. It balances pragmatic value (achieving desired outcomes) and epistemic value (reducing uncertainty).

Mathematically, the EFE for a policy π can be expressed as:

$$G(\pi) = \underbrace{D_{KL}[Q(o_τ|π)||P(o_τ)]}_{\text{pragmatic value}} + \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{Q(s_τ|π)}[H[P(o_τ|s_τ)]]}_{\text{ambiguity}} - \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{Q(o_τ|π)}[H[Q(s_τ|o_τ,π)]]}_{\text{information gain}}$$

Where $Q$ represents the agent's beliefs, $P$ represents prior preferences or the generative model, and $H$ denotes entropy. The pragmatic value term drives the agent toward preferred outcomes, while the information gain term encourages exploration of uncertain areas.

### 3. Scaling Challenges

Previous implementations of active inference have been limited to discrete, low-dimensional problems due to several challenges:

1. **Representation of complex belief distributions**: Traditional methods struggle with high-dimensional, continuous spaces.
2. **Computational complexity of policy evaluation**: Evaluating EFE across potential action sequences becomes intractable in continuous domains.
3. **Uncertainty quantification**: Accurately representing uncertainty in complex environments is difficult but essential for effective exploration.

### 4. Methodology

The authors address these challenges through several key innovations:

#### 4.1. Amortized Inference with Neural Networks

The proposed model uses neural networks to parameterize both the generative model (predicting observations from states) and the recognition model (inferring states from observations). This amortization enables efficient inference in high-dimensional spaces.

The generative model consists of:
- A transition model $p_θ(s_{t+1}|s_t,a_t)$ implemented as a Bayesian neural network
- An observation model $p_φ(o_t|s_t)$ implemented as a deterministic neural network

The recognition model $q_ψ(s_t|o_t)$ infers the current state from observations.

#### 4.2. Policy Optimization via Cross-Entropy Method

To handle continuous action spaces, the authors employ the Cross-Entropy Method (CEM), which iteratively:
1. Samples action sequences from a proposal distribution
2. Evaluates the EFE for each sequence
3. Updates the proposal distribution based on the best-performing samples

This approach avoids the need to discretize the action space while enabling efficient exploration of the policy space.

#### 4.3. Particle-Based Trajectory Sampling

To propagate uncertainty through time when evaluating policies, the authors use a particle-based approach:
1. Sample initial states from the current belief distribution
2. For each particle, sample multiple possible future trajectories
3. Evaluate the EFE across all trajectories

This technique provides a more accurate estimation of expected outcomes compared to deterministic predictions.

### 5. Implementation Details

The authors implement their scalable active inference framework with the following components:

```python
def optimize_policy(current_state, model, iterations=5):
    # Initialize proposal distribution
    mu = np.zeros(action_dim * horizon)
    sigma = np.ones(action_dim * horizon)

    for _ in range(iterations):
        # Sample action sequences
        samples = np.random.normal(mu, sigma, (n_samples, action_dim * horizon))

        # Evaluate expected free energy
        values = []
        for sample in samples:
            actions = sample.reshape(horizon, action_dim)
            value = evaluate_efe(current_state, actions, model)
            values.append(value)

        # Update proposal distribution
        elite_idx = np.argsort(values)[:n_elite]
        elite_samples = samples[elite_idx]
        mu = np.mean(elite_samples, axis=0)
        sigma = np.std(elite_samples, axis=0)

    return mu.reshape(horizon, action_dim)[0]  # Return first action
```

Key implementation aspects include:

1. **Bayesian neural networks** for transition modeling, capturing both aleatoric uncertainty (inherent stochasticity) and epistemic uncertainty (model uncertainty)
2. **Nearest neighbor entropy estimation** for approximating information gain
3. **Variational training** to learn model parameters by minimizing free energy

### 6. Experimental Results

The authors evaluate their active inference agent on three continuous control benchmarks:

1. **MountainCar**: A classic problem where exploration is critical
2. **Inverted Pendulum**: Tests the ability to maintain balance
3. **Hopper**: A challenging locomotion task

The results demonstrate several key findings:

1. **Enhanced exploration**: In the MountainCar environment, the active inference agent explores the state space more thoroughly than reward-driven or ε-greedy alternatives (Figure 1).

2. **Superior sample efficiency**: The active inference agent achieves good performance with an order of magnitude fewer samples than DDPG, a strong model-free baseline (Figure 2).

3. **Robust performance**: The active inference agent converges to high-reward policies across all tasks, demonstrating the scalability of the framework.

In the Pendulum task, the active inference agent reaches optimal performance in fewer than 100 epochs, while DDPG requires over 1000 epochs. Similarly, in the Hopper task, the active inference agent achieves significant progress within 50 epochs, while DDPG shows minimal improvement even after 1500 epochs.

### 7. Comparison with Model-Based RL

The authors draw connections between their active inference implementation and existing model-based reinforcement learning approaches. Several operational homologies are identified:

1. **Model learning**: Both frameworks learn environment dynamics through experience
2. **Planning**: Both use learned models to simulate and evaluate potential future trajectories
3. **Exploration**: Both balance exploitation of known rewards with exploration of uncertain states

However, active inference offers distinct advantages:

1. **Unified objective**: Rather than treating exploration as a separate mechanism, active inference naturally balances exploration and exploitation through a single objective (EFE minimization)
2. **Principled uncertainty handling**: By considering both pragmatic and epistemic value, active inference provides a theoretically grounded approach to uncertainty-driven exploration
3. **Reward as prior**: Active inference reframes rewards as prior preferences, providing a Bayesian interpretation of the reward function

### 8. Theoretical Implications

The successful scaling of active inference to complex control problems has several theoretical implications:

1. **Biological plausibility**: The results support the hypothesis that biological systems might use active inference-like mechanisms for perception and action
2. **Free energy principle**: The effectiveness of free energy minimization in complex domains strengthens its position as a unifying principle for adaptive systems
3. **Predictive processing**: The implementation supports the view that prediction error minimization is a powerful computational principle

### 9. Limitations and Future Work

Despite its successes, the presented implementation has several limitations:

1. **Computational cost**: The particle-based approximation and policy optimization remain computationally intensive
2. **Model learning**: The efficiency of the approach depends on learning accurate world models, which can be challenging in highly complex environments
3. **Hyperparameter sensitivity**: Performance depends on appropriate settings for exploration-exploitation balance

Future work directions include:

1. Incorporating hierarchical representations to handle longer time horizons
2. Developing more efficient approximations of expected free energy
3. Integrating meta-learning to adjust exploration parameters adaptively
4. Applying the framework to partially observable environments

### 10. Conclusion

This paper makes a significant contribution by demonstrating that active inference can be successfully scaled to high-dimensional continuous control problems. The implementation achieves remarkable sample efficiency and exploration capabilities, outperforming strong model-free baselines.

By bridging the gap between the theoretical framework of active inference and practical reinforcement learning algorithms, this work opens new avenues for developing intelligent agents that combine the theoretical elegance of active inference with the scalability requirements of real-world applications.

The results suggest that the principles of free energy minimization and uncertainty-driven exploration provide a powerful foundation for building sample-efficient learning systems, with potential applications in robotics, autonomous systems, and artificial general intelligence.
