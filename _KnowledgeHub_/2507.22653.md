# UniLegs: Universal Multi-Legged Robot Control through Morphology-Agnostic Policy Distillation

> [!summary] Summary
> UniLegs, a two-stage policy distillation framework, enables a single universal controller to operate across diverse multi-legged robot morphologies while retaining high performance for individual designs. The Transformer-based student policy achieved an average of 94.47% of optimal teacher performance across five training morphologies and successfully demonstrated zero-shot sim-to-real transfer on an unseen physical robot.

## Problem

- Developing deep reinforcement learning controllers for legged robots that generalize effectively across diverse morphologies (e.g., varying leg counts or joint configurations) without compromising performance for individual designs.
- The inefficiency and resource intensiveness of training specialized controllers for every new robot morphology, requiring extensive retraining and hyperparameter tuning.
- Existing multi-morphology control approaches, such as those based on GNNs or directly trained Transformers, often lead to sub-optimal performance for specific robot designs.

## Method

- A two-stage teacher-student framework, **UniLegs**, is introduced, where optimal specialized teacher policies are first trained for individual robot morphologies using the PPO algorithm.
- Knowledge from these high-performing teacher policies is then distilled into a single, universal student policy, leveraging either an MLP or a Transformer architecture to mimic teacher actions.
- The framework employs an asymmetric actor-critic setup for teacher training and uses a Mean Squared Error loss function for the cross-morphology knowledge distillation process.

## Results

- The Transformer-based student policy achieved an average of **94.47%** of the optimal teacher performance across five training morphologies, outperforming the MLP baseline by **4.02** percentage points.
- For multi-legged robots (6-legged and 8-legged), the Transformer student policy achieved near-perfect performance, reproducing **99.36%** and **98.43%** of their respective optimal teacher policies.
- On the unseen Unitree Go2 quadruped, the Transformer policy demonstrated superior zero-shot generalization with **72.64%** of the specialized optimal performance and was successfully deployed for stable locomotion on the physical robot.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Policy distillation effectively transfers specialized optimal control knowledge from multiple expert policies into a single, generalized policy, mitigating the performance compromise typically seen in direct multi-morphology training.
> - Transformer architectures' self-attention mechanisms are crucial for dynamically modeling complex joint relationships across diverse kinematic structures, yielding superior cross-morphology generalization compared to MLP baselines.
> - Separating the specialized optimization for individual robot designs (teacher training) from the task of consolidating generalizable control principles (student distillation) enables both high morphology-specific performance and broad adaptability.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of robust locomotion controllers for legged robotics has seen advancements through deep reinforcement learning (RL), enabling robots to perform complex maneuvers in challenging environments. However, a significant challenge remains in developing controllers that can generalize across diverse robot morphologies while simultaneously achieving optimal performance for each specific design. Traditional approaches often involve creating specialized controllers for each morphology or training a single controller across multiple morphologies, which typically results in a compromise of performance for individual designs. This limitation necessitates extensive retraining and hyperparameter tuning for each new robot morphology, impeding practical deployment.

Previous research efforts to address cross-morphology control have explored various architectural approaches. Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) were introduced to model robot structures as graphs, representing joints as nodes and connections as edges. While GNNs represented an early attempt at structural generalization, they have demonstrated limitations in generalizing across significantly diverse morphologies. More recently, Transformer architectures have been employed to model interactions between joints, utilizing attention mechanisms for global information aggregation. However, when trained simultaneously on multiple morphologies, these Transformer-based methods have often converged to sub-optimal policies, sacrificing individual morphology performance for broader generalization.

Other strategies, such as hierarchical control frameworks, decompose tasks into morphology-agnostic high-level policies and morphology-specific low-level controllers, but these still require retraining low-level components for new robots. Domain randomization and adaptive learning approaches primarily focus on enhancing policy robustness and transfer within a single morphology or across similar domains, rather than broad morphological generalization.

Knowledge distillation has emerged as a method for transferring expertise between different models or domains. Prior works like TraKDis applied Transformer-based knowledge distillation in visual reinforcement learning, and other approaches have proposed multi-morphology controllers utilizing knowledge distillation. The concept of foundation models and methods such as DiffuseLoco also explore universal controllers with zero-shot transfer capabilities. Despite these advances, the dual objective of achieving both broad cross-morphology generalization and maintaining morphology-specific optimal performance within a single, unified control policy continues to be an unresolved challenge in legged robotics. This paper positions itself within this landscape by proposing a novel framework that seeks to reconcile these objectives through a two-stage policy distillation process.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the inherent difficulties and inefficiencies associated with current legged robot control methods when dealing with morphological diversity. Existing deep reinforcement learning approaches, while effective for specific robot designs, often struggle to generalize across different numbers of legs, joint configurations, and physical characteristics. Training a single policy across multiple morphologies frequently leads to sub-optimal performance for each individual robot, as the policy must compromise to accommodate the entire set. Conversely, training a specialized controller for every new morphology is resource-intensive, requiring significant time for retraining, hyperparameter tuning, and data collection. This bottleneck hinders the scalable deployment of legged robots in varied real-world applications.

The key objectives of this work are articulated as follows:
1. **Develop a unified morphology-agnostic control framework:** The overarching goal is to create a single control policy capable of effectively controlling a wide range of legged robot morphologies. This framework is designed to first identify optimal control strategies for individual robot designs and then consolidate this specialized knowledge into a universal controller.
2. **Preserve morphology-specific optimal behaviors:** A central objective is to ensure that the universal controller, despite its generality, can approximate the performance of specialized controllers tailored for each morphology. The aim is to overcome the performance compromise typically observed in multi-morphology training approaches. The proposed two-stage process, involving policy distillation, is intended to achieve this by transferring expertise from highly optimized teacher policies.
3. **Leverage Transformer architectures for effective knowledge transfer:** The research aims to evaluate and demonstrate the capability of Transformer-based architectures, specifically their attention mechanisms, in capturing and transferring optimal control principles across diverse kinematic structures. This is posited as a method to model joint relationships more effectively than simpler architectures like Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs), thereby enhancing generalization.
4. **Enable scalable integration of new robot designs:** By distilling knowledge into a single universal policy, the framework aims to reduce the need for extensive retraining when new robot morphologies are introduced. This contributes to a more scalable and efficient development process for future legged robot applications.
5. **Validate practical viability through real-world deployment:** A critical objective is to confirm the effectiveness and robustness of the proposed framework in a physical environment. This involves deploying the distilled policy on an actual robot to demonstrate its sim-to-real transferability and functional locomotion capabilities under real-world conditions.

In essence, the research aims to bridge the gap between specialized, high-performance control and broad, generalizable control by introducing an efficient, scalable, and practically viable methodology for universal legged robot locomotion.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology presented in this paper is a two-stage teacher-student framework designed for morphology-agnostic legged robot control through policy distillation. The approach addresses the problem formulated as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP), where observations and actions can vary in dimensionality across different robot morphologies, preventing direct policy transfer.

**A. Two-Stage Teacher-Student Framework**

1. **Overview:** The framework begins by training specialized teacher policies for each individual robot morphology using reinforcement learning. In the second stage, the knowledge from these specialized teacher policies is distilled into a single, universal student policy. This separation is intended to facilitate efficient training, allow for morphology-specific optimization, and enable the student policy to generalize across different designs. The teacher stage benefits from parallelization and tailored reward functions. The distillation stage aims to extract shared control principles, reduce overfitting, and enable generalization to unseen morphologies.

2. **Stage 1: Morphology-Specific Teacher Training:**
 - **Algorithm:** For each morphology $M_i$ in a training set $M_{train}$, a teacher policy $\pi_{\phi_i}$ is trained using the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm. The objective is to maximize the expected discounted return for each specific morphology.
 - **Asymmetric Actor-Critic Framework:** An asymmetric actor-critic architecture is employed. The actor network maps proprioceptive observations to actions, while the critic network evaluates states using additional privileged information available only during simulation training.
 - **State and Observation Space:** The actor's observation includes base angular velocity, gravity direction, command signals, joint positions, joint velocities, and previous actions. The critic's state augments these with privileged information such as base linear velocity and a terrain height map. A history buffer of length 5 is used to capture temporal dependencies.
 - **Action Space:** Actions are normalized target joint positions, mapped to physical joint limits via $q_{target} = q_{center} + a_t \cdot q_{range}$. A PD controller with uniform gains ($K_p = 20$, $K_d = 0.5$) tracks these targets.
 - **Reward Function:** A unified reward structure is applied across all morphologies, consisting of task-specific components (linear and angular velocity tracking) and regularization terms (e.g., body height, joint accelerations, power consumption, smoothness, orientation).
 - **Training Details:** Actor and critic networks share hidden layer dimensions of, with layer normalization after each hidden layer.
 - **Domain Randomization:** To enhance robustness and sim-to-real transferability, training incorporates randomness in physical parameters (mass, joint friction, actuator strength), terrain properties (friction, compliance), external disturbances (forces), and sensor noise.

3. **Stage 2: Cross-Morphology Knowledge Distillation:**
 - **Data Collection and Loss Function:** Datasets $D_i$ are collected for each morphology by executing the trained teacher policy under diverse conditions. The universal student policy $\pi_{\theta}$ is trained to mimic the teacher policies using a Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss function, quantifying the discrepancy between teacher and student actions for given observations. Training employs stochastic gradient descent with learning rate scheduling, gradient clipping, and early stopping.
 - **Student Model Architectures:** Two architectures are investigated for the student model, ensuring equivalent parameter counts for a fair comparison:
 - **Multi-layer perceptron (MLP) baseline:** Composed of four fully-connected layers with dimensions, using ReLU activations. This architecture directly maps concatenated morphology descriptors and state observations to action outputs.
 - **Transformer-based architecture:** Features 256-dimensional input and output embeddings with 8 parallel attention heads, processing information in 32-dimensional key, query, and value spaces. It comprises 3 Transformer encoder layers, each incorporating multi-head self-attention mechanisms and feed-forward networks.

**B. Setup and Implementation:**
- **Robot Morphologies:** The training set included five distinct morphologies: Unitree A1, Aliengo, ANYbotics ANYmal (all quadrupedal), a six-legged robot, and an eight-legged robot. For zero-shot generalization testing, the Unitree Go2 quadruped was used, explicitly excluded from training.
- **Environment:** Simulations were conducted in NVIDIA Isaac Gym with a physics frequency of 200 Hz and policy execution at 50 Hz. The environment included procedurally generated terrains (flat, slopes, steps, rough, gaps) and a progressive difficulty curriculum.
- **Distillation Process:** Knowledge distillation used 1 million state-action pairs per morphology, collected from expert teacher policies. Training involved 500 epochs with a batch size of 2048.
- **Evaluation Metrics:** Performance was measured using normalized reward scores, where each morphology's specialized teacher policy defined the optimal performance baseline (1.0). For the Go2, a specialized expert policy was trained for evaluation normalization only, not for distillation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation demonstrated the effectiveness of the proposed two-stage teacher-student framework and highlighted the comparative performance of Transformer-based architectures in morphology-agnostic robot control.

**A. Teacher Policy Training**
Initial training of specialized teacher policies for each morphology using PPO showed consistent and stable convergence. All five training morphologies (A1, Aliengo, Anymal, 6-legged, and 8-legged robots) achieved stable performance within approximately 3000 iterations. Multi-legged robots (6-legged and 8-legged) generally attained higher final reward scores compared to the quadrupedal robots. This outcome is attributed to the inherent stability advantages of platforms with a greater number of contact points and increased redundant degrees of freedom, which can simplify locomotion tasks.

**B. Performance Comparison of Student Policies**
The distillation process was evaluated by comparing the normalized reward scores of the universal student policies (Transformer and MLP) against the specialized teacher policies, which served as the optimal performance baseline (1.0).

1. **Distillation Effectiveness Across Training Morphologies:**
 - The Transformer-based student architecture achieved an average of 94.47% of the optimal teacher performance across the five training morphologies.
 - The MLP baseline achieved an average of 90.45% of the optimal teacher performance.
 - This indicates a 4.02 percentage point advantage for the Transformer architecture over the MLP baseline in preserving optimal control strategies.

2. **Morphology-Specific Performance:**
 - For the Unitree A1 quadruped, the Transformer captured 80.71% and the MLP 74.65% of the optimal policy.
 - For the Aliengo, the Transformer achieved 97.03% and the MLP 92.92%.
 - For the ANYmal, the Transformer reached 96.83% and the MLP 89.18%.
 - For multi-legged robots, the transfer of optimal strategies was particularly effective:
 - 6-legged robot: Transformer 99.36%, MLP 97.35%.
 - 8-legged robot: Transformer 98.43%, MLP 98.14%.
 These results suggest that the distillation process can effectively capture and transfer specialized optimal coordination patterns, especially for more complex multi-legged locomotion, achieving near-perfect reproductions of teacher performance in these cases.

3. **Zero-Shot Generalization to Unseen Morphology (Unitree Go2):**
 - When tested on the Unitree Go2 quadruped, a morphology entirely unseen during training, performance for both student architectures decreased compared to their performance on training morphologies.
 - The Transformer policy achieved 72.64% of the specialized optimal Go2 teacher performance.
 - The MLP policy achieved 69.29% of the specialized optimal Go2 teacher performance.
 - The Transformer maintained a 3.35 percentage point advantage over the MLP in this zero-shot scenario, indicating superior generalization capabilities.

**C. Architectural Analysis**
The consistent advantage of the Transformer architecture in preserving optimal strategies across all morphologies is attributed to its self-attention mechanism. This mechanism allows the Transformer to dynamically model complex relationships between different joints and body segments, providing greater flexibility in representing optimal control policies across diverse kinematic structures compared to the fixed connectivity patterns of an MLP. While the MLP demonstrated successful capture of many morphology-specific strategies, the attention layers provided a more adaptable framework for transferring control knowledge effectively. The performance drop in zero-shot generalization to the Go2 highlights the inherent challenge of transferring highly optimized strategies to entirely novel designs, yet achieving over 70% of specialized optimal performance suggests the potential of attention-based architectures for morphology-agnostic control.

**D. Real-World Deployment**
The distilled Transformer policy was successfully deployed on a physical Unitree Go2 quadruped robot. This robot was not part of the training set and served as a critical test case for zero-shot transfer in real-world conditions. The deployment involved transferring the trained policy weights directly to the robot's onboard computer without any additional fine-tuning. The physical experiments demonstrated stable gait patterns and effective locomotion capabilities in a laboratory environment, consistent with simulation predictions. The robot maintained balance and responded reliably to velocity commands, validating the sim-to-real transferability and practical viability of the morphology-agnostic control framework.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework that contributes to addressing a persistent challenge in robotics: developing universal controllers capable of operating across diverse robot morphologies without compromising performance. The work's significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Bridging the Generalization-Performance Gap:** The two-stage teacher-student framework effectively reconciles the need for broad cross-morphology generalization with the requirement for morphology-specific optimal performance. By first training specialized expert policies and then distilling their knowledge, the approach avoids the common pitfall of sub-optimal performance often associated with direct multi-morphology training. This is a step towards more versatile and high-performing legged robots.

2. **Scalable and Efficient Controller Development:** The proposed method offers a scalable solution for integrating new robot designs. Instead of requiring extensive retraining and hyperparameter tuning for each novel morphology, the expertise can be distilled into an existing universal policy. This efficiency can reduce development time and resources, accelerating the deployment of legged robots in various applications.

3. **Validation of Transformer Architectures in Robotics:** The consistent superior performance of the Transformer-based student policy over the MLP baseline highlights the efficacy of attention mechanisms for modeling complex joint relationships and transferring control knowledge across diverse kinematic structures. This reinforces the utility of Transformer architectures for advanced robotics control tasks, particularly those involving high-dimensional and variable morphological inputs.

4. **Demonstrated Sim-to-Real Transferability:** The successful real-world deployment on an unseen Unitree Go2 quadruped robot validates the practical applicability of the distilled policy. This confirmation of effective sim-to-real transfer is crucial for the adoption of such learning-based control methods in physical robotic systems, providing confidence that policies trained in simulation can perform robustly in reality.

5. **Potential for Broader Robotic Applications:** The ability to develop morphology-agnostic controllers has broad implications for fields such as exploration, search and rescue, logistics, and inspection. Robots could potentially adapt to unexpected damage (e.g., loss of a limb) or be quickly reconfigured for different tasks without requiring entirely new control software. This flexibility can lead to more robust, adaptable, and cost-effective robotic solutions.

In conclusion, this work offers a promising pathway towards truly universal legged robot controllers. By preserving morphology-specific optimal strategies while enabling generalization, it contributes to overcoming a significant bottleneck in legged robotics and paves the way for a new generation of adaptable and intelligent robotic systems.
