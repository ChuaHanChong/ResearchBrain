# CAC-VLA: Context-Gated Action Conditioning for Vision-Language-Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> CAC-VLA introduces a Vision-Language-Action framework that integrates a VLM-native latent-action interface and context-gated conditioning for continuous robot control. This approach achieved an 89.5% average success rate on the LIBERO-Plus benchmark, outperforming ACoT-VLA, and demonstrated a 48 percentage point improvement in full success rate on real-world pick-and-place tasks.

## Problem

- Existing Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models rely on implicit interfaces, forcing action experts to infer task structure and fine-grained motor commands from high-level VLM representations.
- Intermediate action-reasoning methods often use separate modules and provide action guidance as a fixed conditioning signal, which may be noisy or have varying relevance across task phases.
- There is a gap between VLM's semantic understanding and the precise motor control required for robot actions, leading to complexity for the action expert.

## Method

- **CAC-VLA** trains the Vision-Language Model (VLM) to predict "==latent actions==" which are encoded representations of future action segments, providing VLM-native, action-structured guidance.
- A **Context-Gated Action Conditioning** module adaptively calibrates the influence of the predicted latent actions on the action expert, modulating its contribution based on the current context and action-generation state.
- The framework combines a ==flow-matching== action expert loss with a ==latent alignment loss==, supervising the VLM-predicted latent actions with targets from an ==Ordered Action Tokenizer (OAT)==.

## Results

- CAC-VLA achieved an average success rate of **98.3%** on the LIBERO benchmark, ranking first in Object (**99.8%**) and Goal (**99.6%**) task suites.
- On the LIBERO-Plus benchmark for robustness to distribution shifts, CAC-VLA obtained an **89.5%** average success rate, surpassing ACoT-VLA's **88.0%**.
- Real-world experiments showed CAC-VLA improved pick-and-place full success rate by **48 percentage points** (**64%** vs **16%**) and block-stacking success rate by **16 percentage points** (**52%** vs **36%**) compared to the π0.5 baseline.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - An explicit latent-action interface, trained within the VLM using future action segments, can provide effective action-structured guidance for continuous robot control.
> - Adaptive calibration of latent-action guidance through a context gate is crucial for robustness, allowing the robot to dynamically adjust its reliance on potentially imperfect intermediate predictions.
> - A moderate latent-action horizon (e.g., 20) provides an optimal balance between capturing sufficient future context and maintaining relevance to the current action chunk.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models represent a paradigm in robot manipulation research, aiming to enable generalist robots to follow language instructions by translating multimodal understanding into physical actions. Current VLA architectures often integrate pre-trained Vision-Language Models (VLMs) with action experts responsible for generating continuous robot commands. These systems leverage the semantic knowledge and reasoning capabilities of VLMs, which has shown promise for generalization across various objects, scenes, and instructions.

However, a recurring challenge in many VLA models is the implicit nature of the interface between multimodal understanding and action generation. VLM representations, while rich, are not explicitly optimized or structured for action conditioning. This requires the downstream action expert to infer both the high-level task structure and the fine-grained motor commands from representations that do not inherently prioritize action-relevant information. Consequently, a significant gap often exists between semantic understanding and precise motor control, which the action expert must bridge.

To address this, a segment of research has focused on introducing intermediate reasoning signals. These include language-based approaches that decompose tasks into subgoals or textual plans, and vision-based methods that synthesize goal images or future observations. More recently, action-reasoning methods have emerged, generating intermediate representations closer to the control space, such as action plans, reference trajectories, or action-space priors. This body of work suggests that providing guidance explicitly tied to the action space can be beneficial for downstream action prediction, helping to bridge the semantic-to-control divide. Nevertheless, many existing action-reasoning methods typically rely on additional, often separate, action-generation or action-reasoning modules to obtain this guidance. Furthermore, the generated action guidance is frequently treated as a fixed conditioning signal, without adaptive mechanisms to adjust its influence based on dynamic factors such as the current scene, task phase, or action-generation state.

Within this landscape, CAC-VLA positions itself by proposing to integrate an explicit latent-action interface directly within the VLM itself, rather than through standalone modules. It aims to provide action-structured guidance that can be adaptively calibrated by the action expert during continuous control. Unlike some prior works that use latent actions for pretraining from unlabeled or heterogeneous videos, CAC-VLA utilizes ordered action latents encoded from *future action segments* as direct supervision for VLM-native action conditioning. The goal is to provide action-level abstraction and guidance without requiring the VLM to produce directly executable trajectories as intermediate steps, focusing instead on informing the continuous action expert in an adaptive manner.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate whether a Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model can be enhanced by equipping its Vision-Language Model (VLM) component with an explicit latent-action interface and by enabling the action expert to adaptively calibrate the influence of this action-structured guidance during continuous control.

The motivation for this work stems from observed limitations in existing VLA models and intermediate action-reasoning methods:

1. **Implicit Action Conditioning in Current VLAs:** Standard VLA models predominantly rely on high-level visual-language representations as the primary interface for action prediction. While these representations offer semantic understanding, they are not explicitly optimized for action conditioning. This design choice necessitates the action expert to implicitly infer task-level action structures and fine-grained motor commands from features that lack explicit organization around action-relevant information. This implicit mapping increases the complexity for the action expert in bridging the gap between multimodal understanding and precise motor control, potentially limiting accuracy and generalization.

2. **Limitations of Existing Action-Reasoning Methods:** A growing body of research introduces intermediate reasoning signals to provide more structured guidance, often moving closer to the control space by generating action plans or reference trajectories. While these methods demonstrate the benefit of action-level guidance, they frequently introduce additional action-generation or action-reasoning modules that operate separately from the core VLM. Furthermore, the action guidance provided by these methods is typically applied as a fixed conditioning signal. This fixed application is a limitation because the reliability and usefulness of such guidance can vary significantly across different task phases, environmental contexts, and the current state of action generation. For instance, a coarse action plan might be highly useful at the beginning of a task but less relevant for fine-grained adjustments.

To address these issues, CAC-VLA proposes a framework that trains the VLM itself to predict "latent actions." These latent actions are defined as encoded representations of future action segments, serving as intermediate action-structured guidance. This approach aims to provide action-relevant information directly from the VLM, without requiring the generation of executable trajectories as an intermediate step. A key challenge identified is that these latent actions, being predictions, might be incomplete, noisy, or not perfectly aligned with immediate control requirements. To mitigate this, the framework introduces a "context gate" module. The gate's purpose is to adaptively calibrate the residual contribution of the latent-action conditioning, allowing the action expert to modulate the influence of this guidance dynamically based on the current context and action-generation state.

In summary, the key objectives are to:
- Formulate and implement latent-action conditioning as an intermediate, action-structured interface connecting VLM representations with continuous robot control.
- Develop CAC-VLA, a framework that leverages VLM-predicted latent actions to adaptively guide the action expert via a context-gated mechanism.
- Validate the effectiveness of this approach on established robot manipulation benchmarks, demonstrating the importance of specific design choices such as the latent-action horizon and the adaptive context gate.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

CAC-VLA (Context-Gated Action Conditioning) is a framework designed to enhance Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models by integrating a VLM-native latent-action interface and context-gated conditioning for the action expert.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The traditional VLA policy directly maps a visual observation ($o_t$) and a language instruction ($l$) to an executable action chunk ($a_{t:t+H_e-1}$), where $H_e$ is the expert's action horizon: $a_{t:t+H_e-1} = \pi_{\theta}(o_t, l)$.
CAC-VLA introduces an intermediate latent-action representation ($z_t$). The policy first predicts this latent action from the multimodal context and then uses it to condition the action expert: $z_t = f_{\theta}(o_t, l)$, and $a_{t:t+H_e-1} = \pi_{\theta}(o_t, l, z_t)$. Here, $f_{\theta}$ is the VLM-side latent-action predictor, and $z_t$ provides action-structured conditioning for continuous expert control.

#### 3.2. VLM-Native Latent Action Prediction

To provide the action expert with structured action information, CAC-VLA trains dedicated VLM query tokens to predict latent actions instead of low-level executable commands. This design allows the VLM to learn an action-conditioned representation from the visual-language context, while delegating the generation of continuous control signals to the action expert.

Supervision for these latent actions is derived from an Ordered Action Tokenizer (OAT). Given a future action segment $a_{t:t+H_l-1}$, the OAT tokenizer encodes it into raw latent vectors $z^{oat}_t = \text{OAT}(a_{t:t+H_l-1})$. The latent-action horizon ($H_l$) can be configured independently of the expert action horizon ($H_e$), enabling the latent representation to summarize action structure over varying temporal ranges and offer broader temporal guidance.

Learnable latent query tokens ($q$) are appended to the VLM input. An expert-aware attention mask is employed to prevent action expert tokens from directly attending to these query tokens. This ensures that the influence of the query tokens on the expert is mediated solely through the predicted latent actions and the subsequent context-gated conditioning module. After processing the visual-language context and the latent queries, the VLM produces query hidden states ($h^q_t = \text{VLM}(o_t, l, q)$). These query states are then projected into the raw OAT latent space via a lightweight prediction head: $\hat{z}_t = W_z \text{LN}(h^q_t)$.

The predicted latent actions ($\hat{z}_t$) are supervised using a token-wise Smooth-L1 loss, $L_{align}$, calculated between $\hat{z}_t$ and $z^{oat}_t$.

#### 3.3. Context-Gated Action Conditioning

Latent actions, while structured, are predictions and may contain noise, be incomplete, or not perfectly align with immediate control needs. To address this, CAC-VLA introduces Context-Gated Action Conditioning, which adaptively calibrates the influence of latent-action guidance.

The module first projects the latent actions into expert-compatible conditioning tokens. During training, the OAT-encoded target $z^{oat}_t$ serves as the conditioning source; during inference, the VLM-predicted $\hat{z}_t$ is used. This source, denoted $z^c_t$, is projected by $\phi_c(\cdot)$ into conditioning tokens $c_t$ compatible with the action expert's hidden space.

This conditioning module is integrated into each action expert layer, specifically after self-attention and before the feed-forward block. Let $x_l$ be the action hidden states at layer $l$. These states are normalized, $\bar{x}_l = \text{Norm}(x_l)$, and then used as queries in a cross-attention mechanism with the latent-action conditioning tokens $c_t$: $u_l = \text{CrossAttn}(Q=\bar{x}_l, K=c_t, V=c_t)$. This operation retrieves latent-action information relevant to the current expert state.

To determine the appropriate strength for injecting this retrieved update, a context gate ($g_l$) is computed. This gate is conditioned on the current action-generation context, represented by the average-pooled normalized action state ($s^x_l$) and the average-pooled retrieved latent-action update ($s^u_l$). The gate is computed as $g_l = \sigma(W_g \tanh(W_x s^x_l + W_u s^u_l))$, where $g_l$ is a channel-wise gate shared across all action tokens. Finally, the retrieved latent-action update is injected into the expert through a gated residual connection: $x'_{l} = x_l + g_l \odot u_l$. This design ensures that the retrieval of latent-action information is separated from its adaptive injection, preserving the expert's continuous action modeling capacity.

#### 3.4. Training and Inference

The training objective combines the original flow-matching action expert loss ($L_{act}$) with the latent alignment loss ($L_{align}$): $L = L_{act} + \lambda_{align} L_{align}$, where $\lambda_{align}$ balances the latent-action supervision. During training, the frozen OAT tokenizer encodes ground-truth future action segments to provide both alignment targets for the VLM query tokens and the conditioning source for the expert. At inference time, the OAT tokenizer is removed, and the action expert is solely conditioned on the latent actions predicted by the VLM. This approach allows CAC-VLA to leverage future-action structure for supervision during training while remaining VLM-native during deployment.

For implementation, CAC-VLA extends the $\pi_{0.5}$ architecture. The expert action horizon ($H_e$) is fixed at 10. The number of latent query tokens ($N_q$) is 8, and the raw OAT latent dimension ($D_z$) is 4. The latent-action horizon ($H_l$) is 20 for LIBERO and 10 for LIBERO-Plus. Training uses AdamW optimizer, gradient clipping, and an exponential moving average (EMA) of model parameters. The learning rate follows a cosine schedule with warmup, and the latent alignment loss weight $\lambda_{align}$ is set to 0.1.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of CAC-VLA was conducted on two standard simulation benchmarks, LIBERO and LIBERO-Plus, and an initial real-world experiment.

#### 4.1. Simulation Experiments

**Results on LIBERO:**
On the LIBERO benchmark, which comprises four multi-task manipulation suites (Spatial, Object, Goal, Long), CAC-VLA achieved an average success rate of 98.3%. This places it as the second-highest performer among the compared methods, closely following ACoT-VLA (98.5%). Notably, CAC-VLA demonstrated strong performance in specific suites, achieving a 99.8% success rate on Object tasks and 99.6% on Goal tasks, ranking first in both categories. These results indicate that the proposed latent-action interface effectively supports continuous expert control across a range of standard manipulation tasks in simulation.

**Results on LIBERO-Plus:**
For the LIBERO-Plus benchmark, designed to evaluate robustness under various distribution shifts (camera viewpoint, robot embodiment, language instruction, lighting, background, observation noise, and scene layout), CAC-VLA was evaluated under a supervised fine-tuning setting. It achieved an average success rate of 89.5%. This performance surpassed ACoT-VLA (88.0%) in this specific evaluation setting. The framework demonstrated robust performance across different variations, with particularly strong results under lighting, background, noise, and layout shifts. This suggests that latent-action conditioning remains beneficial even when confronted with challenging visual and environmental changes.

#### 4.2. Ablation Studies

To understand the contribution of specific design choices, ablation studies were performed on the latent-action horizon and the context-gated conditioning mechanism.

**Effect of Latent-Action Horizon ($H_l$):**
Experiments varying the latent-action horizon showed that an $H_l = 20$ yielded the best average performance of 98.3% on LIBERO. Horizons of $H_l = 10$ and $H_l = 30$ resulted in slightly lower average success rates (98.1% for both). This suggests that a moderate latent-action horizon provides an optimal balance between capturing sufficient future-action context and maintaining relevance to the current action chunk.

**Effect of Context Gating:**
The ablation study on the conditioning design indicated that predicted latent actions contribute to improved performance. Removing latent-action conditioning entirely (reverting to a baseline that only uses VLM representations) reduced the average success rate from 98.3% (Full CAC-VLA) to 98.0%. Further, removing the adaptive context gate and instead using a fixed residual fusion mechanism lowered the success rate to 97.9%. These results demonstrate that the predicted latent actions are indeed useful for expert conditioning, and that the adaptive, context-gated injection method is more effective than a fixed residual fusion.

**Context Gate Behavior Analysis:**
Visualization of the learned context gate behavior in the appendix revealed that the gate values are not static but vary across flow steps (denoising steps) and task suites, indicating dynamic modulation of latent-action injection. Further analysis of the "effective latent-conditioning strength" (a ratio of the gated latent-action update magnitude to the current expert representation magnitude) showed different temporal conditioning patterns between LIBERO-Long and LIBERO-Goal tasks, suggesting adaptive guidance based on task families and temporal structures. Phase-level visualizations for individual manipulation tasks confirmed that the context gate adjusts latent-action guidance according to both manipulation progress and action-generation steps within a task.

#### 4.3. Real-World Experiments

An initial real-world evaluation was conducted on tabletop pick-and-place and block-stacking tasks using a UR7e robotic arm.

**Pick-and-Place Task:**
CAC-VLA achieved a Task Score of 72% and a Full Success Rate (Full SR) of 64% (16 out of 25 trials). In comparison, the $\pi_{0.5}$ baseline achieved a Task Score of 48% and a Full SR of 16% (4 out of 25 trials). This represents an improvement of 24 and 48 percentage points, respectively, over the baseline.

**Block-Stacking Task:**
For block stacking, CAC-VLA achieved a Stacking Success Rate (Stacking SR) of 52% (13 out of 25 trials), whereas the $\pi_{0.5}$ baseline achieved 36% (9 out of 25 trials). This indicates a 16 percentage point improvement for CAC-VLA.

These real-world results suggest that context-gated latent-action conditioning contributes to enhanced execution reliability on real-world manipulation tasks under the evaluated conditions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research addresses a fundamental challenge in the development of generalist robotic manipulation systems: effectively bridging the conceptual gap between high-level multimodal understanding from Vision-Language Models (VLMs) and the precise, continuous motor control required for robot actions. Current VLA models often rely on implicit interfaces, forcing the action expert to infer action-relevant structures from generalized visual-language representations. CAC-VLA offers a method to provide more explicit and structured guidance directly within the VLM architecture.

The introduction of a VLM-native latent-action interface represents a notable contribution. By training the VLM itself to predict coarse-to-fine latent actions—encoded representations of future action segments—the framework provides action-structured information at an intermediate level. This approach contrasts with systems that either rely solely on implicit VLM representations or introduce external, separate modules for action reasoning, which can add architectural complexity and computational overhead. The use of compact latent-action representations, rather than full executable trajectories, also has implications for efficiency in VLA design.

Furthermore, the proposed context-gated action conditioning mechanism is significant. It acknowledges and addresses the reality that intermediate guidance, especially when predicted, may be noisy, incomplete, or vary in relevance across different task phases or action-generation states. By adaptively calibrating the influence of latent-action guidance via a context gate, CAC-VLA enables the action expert to modulate its reliance on this signal, preserving its capacity for continuous action modeling while still benefiting from structured input. This adaptive mechanism enhances the robustness and flexibility of the robot's control policy.

The demonstrated performance improvements on both the LIBERO and LIBERO-Plus simulation benchmarks, achieving high success rates and outperforming several contemporary methods in specific settings, underscore the effectiveness of this approach. The strong performance on LIBERO-Plus, which evaluates robustness against distribution shifts, suggests that CAC-VLA contributes to more robust robot policies capable of operating under varied environmental conditions. The initial real-world validation, albeit on limited tasks, further indicates the practical applicability and potential for improved execution reliability in physical robot systems.

The work lays a foundation for future research in several areas, including exploring more sophisticated latent-action horizons (e.g., task-adaptive or hierarchical) and evaluating the framework on a broader range of real-world tasks and robot platforms. The findings suggest that explicit, adaptively modulated action-level guidance from within VLMs can be an effective strategy for advancing the capabilities of generalist robot manipulation, potentially leading to more capable and reliable autonomous systems.
