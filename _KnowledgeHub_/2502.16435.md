# Human Cognitive Benchmarks Reveal Foundational Visual Gaps in MLLMs

> [!summary] Summary
> The VISFACTOR benchmark, derived from human cognitive psychology tests, reveals that frontier Multimodal Large Language Models achieve only 30.17% accuracy on foundational visual cognition tasks, a substantial gap compared to human performance of 78.8%. This work identifies specific deficiencies in MLLMs' basic visual reasoning, including mental rotation, spatial relations, and figure-ground discrimination.

## Problem

- MLLMs demonstrate high performance on comprehensive benchmarks but paradoxically fail on seemingly simple visual reasoning tasks that humans find effortless.
- Existing evaluation methodologies primarily focus on overall task performance, obscuring MLLMs' deficiencies in foundational visual cognitive abilities.
- Current benchmarks lack psychometric rigor and are susceptible to overfitting, necessitating a new assessment approach grounded in human cognitive psychology.

## Method

- Introduces **VISFACTOR**, a benchmark adapting 20 vision-centric subtests from the ==Factor-Referenced Cognitive Test (FRCT)== battery, covering ==visualization==, ==perception==, ==memory==, and ==reasoning==.
- Modifies test formats (e.g., ==decomposed multiple choice==, ==grouped-consistency items==) and employs ==synthetic augmentation== to reduce chance-level accuracy and generate infinite, difficulty-controlled test cases.
- Evaluates 23 frontier MLLMs and a human baseline using ==zero-shot prompting==, assessing accuracy across 10 FRCT factors.

## Results

- The best-performing MLLM, GPT-5.1, achieved an overall accuracy of **30.17%**, while human participants scored **78.8%**, demonstrating a substantial performance gap.
- MLLMs consistently struggled with core visual tasks such as mental rotation, spatial relation inference, and figure–ground discrimination, regardless of model size or recency.
- The parametric generator successfully modulated task difficulty, with MLLM performance decreasing progressively across 'Easy,' 'Normal,' and 'Hard' generated subsets.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - MLLMs tend to rely on high-level concept recognition for visual tasks, struggling with robust low-level visual pattern memorization and comparison, especially with abstract stimuli.
> - Models exhibit fundamental limitations in visual attention and fine-grained detail perception, failing to discern subtle features or accurately process geometric properties like length, proportion, and continuous angular orientation.
> - Chain-of-Thought prompting provides only marginal overall gains for MLLMs on VISFACTOR, sometimes degrading performance on perceptual and spatial visualization tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) have demonstrated considerable advancements in multimodal artificial intelligence, achieving high accuracy in various tasks such as text recognition, mathematical reasoning, and clinical decision support. Holistic benchmarks, including MMBench, report high performance, with some frontier models reaching nearly 90% accuracy. This performance has fostered the perspective that direct, large-scale pretraining on complex tasks may already confer near-human visual cognition, which could benefit applications like embodied AI.

However, a discrepancy exists between MLLMs' performance on these general benchmarks and their abilities on targeted visual reasoning problems that humans can solve with ease. Human visual cognition develops through a hierarchical, bottom-up process, starting from basic primitives and Gestalt principles, and progressively building towards high-level semantic reasoning. In contrast, current MLLMs are primarily trained directly on complex downstream tasks, potentially bypassing these foundational visual capabilities. Prior research has indicated MLLMs' struggles with specific visual reasoning tasks, such as mental rotation and maze completion, despite their overall benchmark performance. This situation suggests a limitation in current evaluation paradigms, which tend to prioritize downstream task performance over the assessment of foundational visual faculties essential for human-like reasoning.

This paper addresses this perceived gap by introducing a benchmark grounded in cognitive psychology. It draws inspiration from the Factor-Referenced Cognitive Test (FRCT) battery, a well-established assessment that decomposes human cognition into measurable latent factors. By adapting FRCT, the work aims to provide a fine-grained cognitive profile of MLLMs' visual capacities, offering a diagnostic tool to pinpoint specific strengths and weaknesses beyond general task performance. This approach contrasts with many existing multimodal benchmarks that often rely on multiple-choice or yes-no formats, which can allow for inflated accuracy due to random guessing, thereby obscuring genuine performance disparities. The work thus positions itself as an effort to establish a more rigorous and cognitively informed evaluation standard for MLLMs' visual intelligence.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to systematically investigate and identify foundational visual gaps in Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) by comparing their capabilities to human visual cognition. This overarching objective is driven by several motivations:

Firstly, the authors observe a paradox in the current state of MLLMs: while these models achieve notable results on complex multimodal benchmarks, suggesting a high level of visual intelligence, targeted studies often reveal their failure on basic visual reasoning tasks that are effortlessly solved by humans. This discrepancy raises a fundamental question about whether MLLMs truly possess human-like cognitive visual abilities or if their apparent success is a consequence of pattern matching without genuine understanding. The motivation here is to ascertain the depth and nature of MLLMs' visual cognition.

Secondly, the researchers identify a limitation in existing evaluation paradigms. Most current benchmarks primarily focus on evaluating downstream task performance, such as object recognition or visual question answering, often neglecting the foundational visual faculties that underpin complex human reasoning. Traditional multiple-choice or yes/no formats in many benchmarks also introduce a significant probability of random guessing, which can obscure the true capabilities or limitations of models. This motivates the development of a benchmark with a more rigorous evaluation protocol that minimizes the impact of chance.

Thirdly, the work is motivated by principles from cognitive psychology, specifically the Factor-Referenced Cognitive Test (FRCT) battery. The FRCT operationalizes the decomposition of human cognition into distinct, measurable latent factors, providing a fine-grained profile of cognitive abilities. The authors seek to adapt this human-centric diagnostic approach to MLLMs, aiming to precisely identify which specific visual capacities are present or absent in these models, rather than relying on broad, holistic assessments.

Fourthly, a practical motivation for this work is the need for future-proof benchmarks. Traditional benchmarks with finite item sets face the risk of models overfitting to specific examples. To address this, the authors aim to implement a system that can automatically generate an unlimited supply of novel test cases with controllable difficulty. This ensures that the benchmark remains relevant and challenging as MLLM capabilities evolve, preventing saturation and enabling robust performance tracking over time. The ability to generate intermediate visual solution steps for some tasks also motivates potential applications in supervised fine-tuning for future model improvements.

Finally, the study is motivated by a desire to guide future research and development in MLLMs. By comprehensively analyzing the current capabilities and consistent failure modes of a wide range of frontier MLLMs across various foundational visual tasks, the authors aim to provide actionable insights. These insights are intended to inform the design of future MLLM architectures, direct training strategies towards building more genuinely human-like visual cognition, and contribute to a more accurate understanding of MLLMs' interpretability.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces **VISFACTOR**, a benchmark designed to evaluate foundational visual capabilities in MLLMs. VISFACTOR adapts 20 vision-centric subtests from the Factor-Referenced Cognitive Test (FRCT) battery, a cognitive psychology assessment, into an automated, multimodal evaluation framework.

**Test Selection:** The original FRCT battery contains 72 subtests. From these, 20 vision-centric subtests were selected that could be assessed with a vision-language interface producing text-only output. Excluded subtests included image-production tasks (e.g., drawing or tracing) and speech-dependent tasks (e.g., memory span based on auditory input). The selected 20 subtests cover 10 FRCT factors across four cognitive domains:
1. **Visualization & Spatial Reasoning:** Spatial Relations (S), Spatial Scanning (SS), and Visualization (VZ), which assess mental representation, manipulation, and navigation of spatial structures.
2. **Perceptual & Closure:** Closure Flexibility (CF), Closure Speed (CS), and Perceptual Speed (P), which evaluate processing, organizing, and interpreting visual stimuli under challenging conditions.
3. **Memory:** Visual Memory (MV) and Associative Memory (MA), which measure the capacity to encode, retain, and retrieve visual and relational information.
4. **Reasoning:** Induction (I) and Logical Reasoning (RL), which assess high-level logical synthesis, abstraction, and deduction.

**Digitization:** The human-oriented FRCT items underwent a digitization process:
- **Instructions:** GPT-4o and Gemini-2.5-Flash were used to summarize the original FRCT instructions into minimal, AI-friendly prompts, which were then reconciled by a human annotator.
- **Stimuli:** All images were captured at 300 dpi and cropped to contain only the task stimuli.
- **Ground Truth:** Answers were extracted verbatim from FRCT manuals.

**Evaluation Protocol:** To minimize the influence of random guessing, a rigorous evaluation protocol was developed, reducing the average chance-level accuracy from an estimated 22.47% to 2.89%. This involved:
- **Decomposed Multiple Choice:** For seven subtests with five options, each option was converted into a separate yes/no query. Models received credit only if all queries for a given item were answered correctly, reducing chance accuracy from 20% to approximately 3.13%.
- **Grouped-Consistency Items:** For subtests repeatedly probing the same latent feature, items were clustered, and credit was awarded only if all constituent items within a cluster were correct (e.g., for 8 binary items, chance became approximately 0.39%).
- **Symmetry Variants:** For binary comparison tasks, three additional variants were generated for each item to balance "yes" and "no" answers, preventing models from benefiting from a consistent bias. This reduced chance accuracy to approximately 6.25%.
- **Specialized Rewrites:** Specific modifications were made for SS3 (Map Planning Test), requiring correct answers for both directions of a shortest path query, and VZ3 (Surface Development Test), combining fill-in-the-blank with yes/no questions to ensure comprehensive understanding.
- **Output Processing:** Model outputs were normalized for yes/no questions, exact matching was required for multiple-choice and numeric fill-in-the-blank, and multiple acceptable ground-truth answers were permitted for some tasks (CS1, CS3). The total score was calculated as the average across all 20 tests.

**Parametric Generation:** To address the issue of benchmark saturation and enable continuous evaluation, automatic generation algorithms were implemented for 12 of the 20 subtests (CF1-3, CS1-3, MA1, S1-2, SS3, VZ1-2). These algorithms produce and validate an infinite supply of difficulty-controlled instances by parametrically modulating features such as grid size, noise severity, number of memorization pairs, rotation angles, and folding steps. This capability also allows for the generation of intermediate visual solution steps for some tasks, which could facilitate supervised fine-tuning.

**Model Evaluation:** A total of 39 frontier MLLMs were evaluated, including proprietary models (e.g., GPT, Gemini, Claude) and open-source models (e.g., LLaMA, Qwen, SEED). All evaluations were conducted in a zero-shot setting, with the temperature parameter set to 0 or minimal. Reasoning models were given a high thinking budget, and greedy decoding was the default sampling strategy. Models were accessed via official APIs or a Model-as-a-Service platform, with a retry count of 3 for each case.

**Human Baseline:** To contextualize MLLM performance, a human evaluation was conducted. Thirty-one university students completed 20 items per subtest (1,540 questions in total), following the identical digital protocol and scoring rules applied to the MLLMs. Each question was completed by three independent participants.

**Validity and Reliability Analysis:** The benchmark's internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s α coefficient. Construct validity was evaluated by performing Pearson correlation analysis between VISFACTOR's 10 cognitive factors and 20 subtests against 13 existing vision-language benchmarks. Case studies, particularly on MA1, were performed to investigate underlying mechanisms of model performance.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of 39 frontier Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) using VISFACTOR revealed several key findings regarding their foundational visual cognition.

**Overall Performance:** The highest-performing model, Gemini-3.1-Pro, achieved an overall accuracy of 54.0%. This score significantly trails the human baseline of 78.8%, established by university students on the identical benchmark protocol. The substantial performance gap indicates that current MLLMs lack genuine mastery of foundational visual cognitive abilities compared to humans.

**Performance by Subtests:** Models exhibited varied performance across the 20 subtests. They generally performed stronger on tasks related to memorization, such as Picture-Number Test (MA1) and Shape Memory Test (MV1–MV3), indicating robust capabilities in attending to and recalling relevant input context. Many models also performed well on Diagramming Relationships (RL2) and Figure Classification (I3), with the latter possibly benefiting from prior research focus on Bongard problems. Conversely, models consistently struggled with visualization and spatial reasoning subtests, particularly the VZ series (Form Board Test, Paper Folding Test, Surface Development Test), suggesting a fundamental deficiency in mentally representing, manipulating, and transforming spatial patterns.

**"Middle Score Anomaly":** A phenomenon termed the "Middle Score Anomaly" was observed, where MLLMs achieved intermediate performance (e.g., 30–60% accuracy) on tasks that are exceptionally easy for humans, like the Identical Pictures Test (P3), for which random guessing yielded only 3.13%. This outcome contrasts with human performance, which would typically be near-perfect or at chance. The researchers interpret this intermediate accuracy as evidence that current models may lack genuine reasoning capabilities, instead relying on concept-level recognition rather than deeper cognitive visual processing.

**Performance Trends by Models:** Analysis of model performance across different families and release dates showed that earlier models did not exhibit a strict correlation between performance and parameter size or release iteration. However, models released from late 2025 onwards, such as Gemini-3.0 and GPT-5.5, demonstrated a notable increase in performance, surpassing 40% accuracy, with Gemini-3.1-Pro peaking at 54.0%. This suggests a recent industry focus on enhancing foundational visual capabilities.

**Impact of Reasoning Efforts (Chain-of-Thought - CoT):** The application of Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting yielded marginal performance improvements for models in the GPT-4 family. Furthermore, a negative Pearson correlation was observed between CoT token count and accuracy for these models, indicating that increased verbalization did not necessarily translate to better reasoning. In contrast, GPT-5.1 showed substantial performance gains with increased reasoning effort. This suggests that "reasoning models" benefit from extended CoT, while models not specifically trained for reasoning may not. The study also observed instances where longer CoT degraded performance on perceptual and spatial visualization tasks (e.g., P3, CS2, SS3, VZ1), aligning with cognitive psychology findings that verbalization can sometimes hinder human performance in certain visual or holistic reasoning tasks. Conversely, CoT consistently improved performance on objective reasoning tasks like I3 and RL2.

**Influence of Temperatures:** Evaluations at different temperature settings (0.5 and 1.0) showed only marginal fluctuations in overall performance, indicating that the benchmark's conclusions are robust to decoding temperature variations.

**Performance on Generated Tests:** Using the parametric generation algorithms, models were tested on "Easy," "Normal," and "Hard" subsets. GPT-4.1's performance progressively decreased with increasing difficulty, validating the effectiveness of the generation system in modulating task complexity. For instance, increasing the number of memorized pairs in MA1 from 21 to 50 led to a substantial performance drop. Similarly, increasing the number of folds in VZ2 (Paper Folding Test) to five resulted in models failing to answer correctly. This confirms the benchmark's utility for evaluating increasingly capable models over time without saturation.

**Case Study: How Models Master MA1:** Investigations into the high accuracy on MA1 (Picture-Number Test) suggested that models primarily rely on translating visual cues into high-level, human-interpretable concepts (e.g., "soccer," "chair") and memorizing concept-number pairs. When presented with abstract, line-based figures (generated from CF2 or MV1) instead of semantically rich images, the models' accuracy sharply declined. This indicates that their success is heavily dependent on concept recognition rather than genuine low-level visual pattern perception or abstract visual memory. This hypothesis was further supported by tests with extreme yet verbalizable visual combinations (e.g., "a horse on the moon"), where models maintained high accuracy.

**Construct Validity and Internal Reliability:** The benchmark demonstrated strong convergent validity, showing high positive correlations with comprehensive multimodal benchmarks (e.g., MMMU-Pro, r=0.81; VisualPuzzles, r=0.93), confirming its measurement of foundational visual intelligence. It also exhibited excellent discriminant validity, with near-zero or negative correlations with orthogonal domains (e.g., OCRBench-v2, r=0.02; MathVista, r=-0.49). Moderate or low correlations with other matrix-style reasoning tasks (e.g., VisualSphinx, r=0.23) highlighted its non-redundant contribution. The overall reliability was high, with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.9392 across evaluated subjects, indicating strong internal consistency.

**Failure Analysis (Key Bottleneck):** A detailed failure analysis pinpointed critical bottlenecks:
- **Reliance on Accurate Textual Descriptions:** Models performed perfectly on tasks like CF3 when provided with textual descriptions of line segments, but performance dropped significantly when the same information had to be inferred from visual inputs.
- **Failure to Recognize Visual Details:** Models struggled with subtle visual details, such as identifying start points with small markers, distinguishing intersecting lines without explicit junction markers, and recognizing critical local features in obscured words.
- **Low Sensitivity to Length, Angle, and Proportion:** MLLMs demonstrated limitations in accurately assessing geometric properties like length, angle, and proportion, often misclassifying various directions as 45-degree angles and failing to select components of appropriate dimensions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research, through the introduction of VISFACTOR, holds several significant implications for the development, evaluation, and application of Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs).

Firstly, the study represents a **paradigm shift in MLLM training philosophy**. By exposing a substantial gap between MLLM performance on holistic benchmarks and their capabilities on foundational visual cognition tasks, the research challenges the notion that large-scale pretraining on complex tasks automatically confers genuine, human-like visual intelligence. The observed "castles in the air" phenomenon, where models excel at high-level tasks but lack mastery of basic primitives, argues for a more structured, bottom-up hierarchical training approach. This suggests that future MLLM development should focus on explicitly teaching foundational visual skills, such as mental rotation, spatial relation inference, and figure-ground discrimination, rather than solely relying on emergent abilities from broad data.

Secondly, the introduction of **VISFACTOR's parametric generator provides a scalable infrastructure for curriculum learning**. The ability to automatically generate an unlimited supply of difficulty-controlled test cases is a valuable resource for the research community. This can enable the implementation of curriculum-style training regimens, allowing MLLMs to incrementally acquire and master simple visual factors before progressing to more complex semantic reasoning. This capability is crucial for sustained progress in MLLM development, as it mitigates the risk of benchmark saturation and ensures continuous evaluation against novel challenges. The potential to generate intermediate visual solution steps for certain tasks further supports its use in supervised fine-tuning, offering a pathway for architectural improvements.

Thirdly, the findings have a direct impact on **calibrating user expectations and enhancing safety considerations** for MLLMs. The revelation that even frontier models achieve only 54.0% accuracy on foundational visual tests, significantly lower than the human baseline of 78.8%, highlights critical limitations. This underscores the inherent risks in deploying current MLLMs in safety-critical applications such as medical imaging analysis, autonomous robotics, or clinical decision support. In these domains, brittle spatial reasoning or inaccurate visual perception could lead to severe consequences, including misdiagnoses, navigation errors, or flawed decision-making. VISFACTOR provides a clearer map of these boundaries, urging caution and more robust validation for high-stakes scenarios.

Finally, this work offers **guidance for future research and development directions**. The detailed analysis of consistent failure modes—including reliance on textual descriptions over direct visual perception, inability to recognize subtle visual details, and low sensitivity to precise geometric properties—pinpoints specific areas for architectural innovation and algorithmic improvement. The benchmark's strong construct validity and internal reliability further establish it as a robust diagnostic tool. By identifying the missing substrate for genuine visual reasoning, VISFACTOR can catalyze efforts in MLLM visual reasoning, cognitive interpretability, and the principled generation of synthetic training data, ultimately driving the creation of more robust, reliable, and human-like artificial intelligence.
