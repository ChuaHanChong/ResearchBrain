# ContactDB: Analyzing and Predicting Grasp Contact via Thermal Imaging

> [!summary] Summary
> ContactDB is a dataset of hand-object contact maps captured by letting participants grasp 3D-printed household objects, then imaging residual heat with a calibrated RGB-D plus thermal rig and texture-mapping nine thermal views onto the object mesh. It contains 3750 textured meshes of 50 objects and 375K synchronized frames, and supports learned prediction of diverse contact maps from object shape.

## Problem

- Prior grasp datasets record joint configuration via data gloves, magnetic trackers, or hand-pose estimation, which interfere with natural grasping and never observe the actual contact surface.
- Contact regions are occluded from visible-light imaging, so existing contact estimates rely on low-resolution tactile sensors, crowdsourced self-report saliency, or error-prone hand-model intersection.
- Robotics grasp models treat contact as one point per fingertip, leaving open whether palm and proximal finger contact matter for everyday household objects.

## Method

- Thermal capture protocol: participants hold 3D-printed PLA objects 5 seconds with a stated post-grasp intent (use or hand-off), then a turntable rotates the object past a FLIR Boson thermal camera rigidly mounted on a Kinect v2.
- Contact-map pipeline: plane-fit plus color segmentation, ==ICP== 6D pose estimation across 9 views, circle-fit pose interpolation, then color-map optimization to texture the mesh with thermal intensity.
- Prediction models: a ==pix2pix== conditional ==GAN== on masked 4-channel RGB-D for single-view maps, plus **VoxNet** and **PointNet** trained with ==stochastic multiple choice learning== and ==DiverseNet== control variables for diverse 3D maps.

## Results

- Functional intent strongly shifts contact: scissors handle touched by **100%** of participants for use versus **38%** for hand-off, and binoculars barrels **93.88%** versus **12.50%**.
- Measured contact area exceeds a loose all-five-fingertip upper bound for many objects, showing palm and proximal finger contact dominate, and no bimanual grasps appeared at medium or small object scales.
- Diverse 3D prediction beats single-output baselines on held-out mug, pan, and wine glass: VoxNet sMCL error drops from **55.37%** at k=1 to **11.64%** at k=10, and DiverseNet reaches **8.72%** average on use intent.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Residual body heat on a low-conductivity material is a cheap, occlusion-free contact sensor, and thermal prints stay legible for over 35 seconds, enough for a full 360-degree scan.
> - Single-point-per-fingertip contact models understate real human grasps; soft manipulator design and grasp planners should account for palm and finger-shaft contact.
> - Shape-to-contact is one-to-many, so any single-output regressor averages incompatible grasps; multi-hypothesis training is required, and voxel grids outperform point clouds here.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Grasping and manipulating objects are fundamental human skills, and understanding the intricate interaction between the hand and an object, particularly the contact points, is crucial for advancing robotics and computer vision. The broader research landscape related to human grasping has seen a variety of approaches to record and analyze these interactions.

Previous datasets and methodologies have typically focused on aspects such other than direct, detailed contact. These include:
- **Hand joint configuration:** Methods such as data gloves, wired magnetic trackers, or model-based hand pose estimation have been used to record the kinematics of the hand. However, these methods often interfere with natural grasping or provide only indirect, coarse estimates of actual contact.
- **Grasp taxonomy and video annotation:** Observing grasps through third-person or first-person videos, with frames annotated according to a predefined grasp taxonomy, provides a higher-level classification of grasp types. This approach captures the overall grasp strategy but lacks specificity regarding contact locations.
- **Tactile sensors:** Embedding tactile sensors in gloves or objects can record contact points. These methods are often limited by the resolution and coverage of the sensors, providing sparse or coarse contact information.
- **Model-based estimations:** Some approaches involve manually configuring hand models to match observed grasps or using connected component analysis on intersecting hand and object vertices to estimate contact regions. These are vulnerable to inaccuracies in hand models and pose tracking, leading to speculative contact estimates.
- **Crowdsourced saliency:** Methods like tactile saliency maps gather preferences for touch points, which can be integrated into a map. However, these are based on subjective reporting rather than direct observation of physical contact.

The primary limitation of these existing approaches, as identified by the authors, is the difficulty in directly observing precise and rich hand-object contact due to factors like hand complexity, soft tissue deformation, and severe occlusion from external sensors. Consequently, fundamental questions about human grasping, such as the role of the palm in everyday object manipulation, have remained largely unanswered.

ContactDB addresses these limitations by introducing a novel method for directly observing and recording detailed contact maps on object surfaces using thermal imaging. By focusing on object-centric contact representations (textured 3D meshes), this work distinguishes itself from previous efforts that provided coarse or indirect contact estimates. This detailed contact information is intended to enable more granular analysis of grasping preferences and to serve as ground truth for training data-intensive deep learning models for grasp prediction, which have traditionally relied on simplified contact models (e.g., single points per digit). The paper positions itself as a foundational step toward a more accurate and comprehensive understanding of human grasping for applications in human-robot interaction and robotic manipulation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to create a detailed, object-centric dataset of human hand-object contact that occurs during functional grasping, and to utilize this data for analysis and prediction. This goal is driven by several specific motivations and aims:

1. **Overcome Contact Observation Challenges:** The primary motivation stems from the difficulty of accurately observing hand-object contact due to occlusion and the complex, deformable nature of the human hand. Traditional visual sensors cannot reliably capture the full extent and specific locations of contact. The authors sought a method to circumvent this challenge to provide direct, high-fidelity contact information.

2. **Enable Detailed Analysis of Human Grasping:** The researchers are motivated to provide a rich dataset that allows for in-depth analysis of factors influencing human grasping. This includes:
 - **Functional Intent:** Understanding how the purpose of a grasp (e.g., "use" an object versus "hand-off" an object) affects where and how contact is made. This is recognized as a significant, yet not fully explored, influence on grasping.
 - **Object Properties:** Investigating the impact of object shape, size, and semantic category on contact patterns.
 - **Non-Fingertip Contact:** Quantifying the role of the palm and proximal finger regions in grasping, as previous models often oversimplify contact to just fingertips, neglecting the soft tissue contact that is prevalent in human grasps.

3. **Support Grasp Prediction and Robotic Manipulation:** A key motivation is to provide data suitable for training machine learning models to predict diverse and robust contact patterns. This is intended to:
 - **Inform Robotic Gripper Design:** Provide empirical data to guide the design of soft robotic manipulators, which can leverage the extensive, non-fingertip contact observed in human hands.
 - **Improve Grasp Planning Algorithms:** Develop algorithms that can predict more human-like, stable, and functionally appropriate grasps by considering detailed contact maps, rather than relying on simplified contact models. This can lead to more robust and versatile robotic manipulation.
 - **Facilitate Grasp Retargeting:** Enable the transfer of human grasp knowledge to kinematically diverse robotic hands.

4. **Establish a Novel Dataset (ContactDB):** The core objective is to create "ContactDB," which is explicitly presented as the first large-scale dataset to record detailed contact maps for human grasps. This dataset aims to fill a gap in the existing research landscape by providing unprecedented fidelity in contact observation.

In summary, the key objectives were to develop a novel thermal imaging-based methodology for capturing detailed hand-object contact, assemble a large-scale dataset (ContactDB) using this method, analyze fundamental aspects of human grasping using the collected data, and finally, leverage this data to train models capable of predicting diverse contact maps from object shape for advancements in robotics and computer vision.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research centers on the development of ContactDB, a novel dataset capturing human hand-object contact using thermal imaging, followed by an analysis of the collected data and the training of predictive models.

#### 3.1. Contact Observation via Thermal Camera

The core innovation is the use of a thermal camera to observe contact. When a human hand grasps an object, heat transfers from the hand to the object surface. By using 3D printed objects made of PLA (polylactic acid), which retains thermal handprints effectively, and having participants hold objects for 5 seconds, distinct thermal patterns representing contact areas are captured after object release. The intensity in thermal images correlates with heat transfer, influenced by skin temperature, contact duration, heat conduction, and pressure. These factors were controlled (e.g., hand warmers provided, fixed grasp duration) to ensure heat conduction from contact was the dominant observable factor.

#### 3.2. Object Selection and Fabrication

- **Object Set:** 50 household objects were selected, drawing inspiration from the YCB dataset and adding common items. Objects that were overly similar, deformable, very small, or very large were excluded.
- **Abstract Shapes:** To study the influence of object size and shape independently of semantic meaning, primitive shapes (cube, cylinder, pyramid, torus, sphere) were included at three different scales (12, 8, and 4 cm principal axes).
- **Fabrication:** All objects were 3D printed using white PLA filament at 15% infill density. This ensured uniform heat dissipation properties, provided accurate 3D models for pose estimation, and allowed participants to focus on object geometry during grasping.

#### 3.3. Data Collection Protocol

- **Setup:** A FLIR Boson 640 thermal camera was rigidly mounted and calibrated with a Kinect v2 RGB-D sensor to register RGB and depth images accurately to thermal images.
- **Participants:** 50 able-bodied participants (mostly 20-25 years old, males and females) were recruited.
- **Grasping Procedure:**
 1. Objects were placed randomly on a table.
 2. Participants grasped each object with a specified functional intent, holding it for 5 seconds.
 3. The object was then handed to an experimenter (wearing an insulating glove) and placed on a rotating turntable.
 4. Chemical hand warmers were used by participants to enhance thermal handprints.
 5. Participants were instructed to avoid in-hand manipulation post-grasp to prevent smudging prints.
- **Multi-view Capture:** The turntable rotated 360 degrees, pausing at 9 equally spaced locations. A continuous stream of synchronized RGB, depth, and thermal images was recorded. Some objects were flipped and scanned again to capture unseen prints.
- **Functional Intents:** Two post-grasp functional intents were used:
 - ‘Hand-off’: Participants grasped 48 objects with the intent to hand them to the experimenter.
 - ‘Use’: Participants grasped a subset of 27 objects (after previous thermal prints dissipated) with the intent to use them (specific instructions provided for each object).

#### 3.4. Data Processing

The objective was to generate coherent, texture-mapped contact maps on 3D object meshes from multi-view thermal images.
1. **Image and Pose Extraction:** RGB, depth, and thermal images, along with turntable angles, were extracted at the 9 pause locations.
2. **Object Segmentation and Pose Estimation:** Depth maps were converted to pointclouds. Object segmentation was performed using a least-squares estimate of the turntable plane and white color segmentation. The Iterative Closest Point (ICP) algorithm was used to estimate the 6D pose of the object within the 9 segmented pointclouds, leveraging the known 3D object models.
3. **Pose Interpolation:** Object origins from the 9 views were used to estimate a 3D circle of the moving object. This allowed interpolation of object poses for views with noisy depth maps, hidden shape elements, or for symmetric objects rotating around their axis of symmetry.
4. **Texture Mapping:** The 3D mesh, 9 pose estimates, and thermal images were fed into a colormap optimization algorithm (implemented in Open3D) to produce a 3D mesh textured with a unified contact map. An example of the texture mapping accuracy showed a geometric error of 4.4 mm for a precisely heated spot.

#### 3.5. Data Analysis

Contact maps were processed by applying a sigmoid function to increase contrast.
- **Functional Intent Analysis:** Contact maps were clustered using k-medoids (k=3) based on XYZ values of contact points (intensity > 0.4). ‘Active areas’ on object surfaces were defined, and the fraction of participants touching these areas was quantified for each intent.
- **Object Size Analysis:** Dominant contact maps for same-shaped objects at different sizes were qualitatively compared. Grasps were manually labeled as bimanual or single-handed, and their relationship to participant hand size was investigated.
- **Non-Fingertip Contact:** Contact area was calculated by integrating the area of contacted faces (contact value > 0.4) on the mesh. This was compared against an upper bound estimated from participant palm prints with manually annotated fingertip regions, doubled for bimanual grasps.

#### 3.6. Contact Map Prediction

The task was to predict diverse contact patterns from object shape, acknowledging the one-to-many mapping.
- **Object Shape Representations:**
 - **Single-view RGB-D:** A 4-channel RGB-D image of the object.
 - **Full 3D:** Pointcloud (randomly sampled points with normalized XYZ and scale factor) or voxel occupancy grid (64³ voxelized space with binary occupancy and 4 additional features).
- **Diverse Prediction Strategies:**
 - **Stochastic Multiple Choice Learning (sMCL):** Trains an ensemble of *k* predictors. During training, loss is backpropagated to the network whose prediction is closest to the ground truth. A soft association and random dropout of predictions were used.
 - **DiverseNet:** Uses a single predictor network that generates diverse predictions by varying a one-hot encoded control variable *c* concatenated to internal feature maps. Each ground truth is associated with the closest prediction, and gradients routed through the appropriate *c*.
- **Prediction Models:**
 - **Single-view (RGB-D):** A modified pix2pix (conditional GAN) with a U-Net architecture (approx. 54M generator params, 3M discriminator params) for image-to-image translation. Input was RGB-D, output was a single-channel contact map. Images were cropped and masked by object silhouette.
 - **3D (Pointcloud):** PointNet (approx. 1.2M parameters) predicting contact for each voxel using cross-entropy loss on surface voxels.
 - **3D (Voxel Occupancy Grid):** VoxNet (approx. 1.2M parameters) using 3D convolutions, also predicting contact for each voxel with cross-entropy loss on surface voxels.
- **Training Details:** Models were trained separately for 'hand-off' and 'use' intents. Contact maps were thresholded at 0.4 to create ground truth for classification. Data augmentation (random yaw rotation, point scaling for PointNet) and weighted cross-entropy loss (10x for contacted voxels) were applied. SGD optimizer was used.
- **Evaluation:** Results were reported on held-out test objects (mug, pan, wine glass). Error was calculated by matching each ground truth contact map with the closest among *k* diverse predictions, discarding predictions with no contact.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study yielded several findings regarding human grasping behavior and the feasibility of predicting contact maps from object shape.

#### 4.1. ContactDB Dataset Characteristics

The ContactDB dataset comprises 3750 3D meshes textured with contact maps and 375,000 frames of synchronized RGB-D+thermal images. It includes 50 unique 3D printed household objects grasped by 50 participants under two functional intents ('use' and 'hand-off'). This is presented as the first large-scale dataset providing detailed, high-fidelity contact maps for human grasps.

#### 4.2. Analysis of Contact Maps

- **Influence of Functional Intent:** The functional intent significantly influenced the contact patterns. For example (Figure 3, Table 2):
 - A banana tip was touched by 22.45% of participants for ‘hand-off’ but 63.27% for ‘use’.
 - Binoculars barrels were touched by 12.50% for ‘hand-off’ but 93.88% for ‘use’.
 - Scissors handles were touched by 38.00% for ‘hand-off’ and 100.00% for ‘use’, while the blade was touched by 60.00% for ‘hand-off’ but 0.00% for ‘use’.
 - These quantitative and qualitative examples demonstrate that human grasps are adapted to the intended post-grasp action.

- **Influence of Object Size:** Object size was observed to impact grasping strategies (Figure 4).
 - Small objects typically resulted in two or three fingertip grasps.
 - Larger objects were often grasped with more fingers and more extensive non-fingertip contact.
 - Grasps for large objects exhibited bimodality: either bimanual full-hand grasps or single-handed fingertip grasps.
 - Specifically, participants with smaller hands showed a preference for bimanual grasps when handling large objects for ‘hand-off’ (Figure 6). No bimanual grasps were observed for medium or small objects.

- **Prevalence of Non-Fingertip Contact:** Analysis revealed that human grasps frequently involve substantial contact beyond just the fingertips (Figure 5).
 - The total contact area for many objects significantly exceeded an estimated upper bound for fingertip-only contact.
 - This indicates that the palm and proximal finger regions play a substantial role in human grasping and manipulation, challenging traditional robotic models that often simplify contact to single points.
 - Differences in average contact area for some objects (e.g., bowl, mug, PS controller, toothbrush) between functional intents further underlined the varied contact strategies.

#### 4.3. Prediction of Contact Maps

- **Overall Performance:**
 - The voxel occupancy grid representation (VoxNet) generally performed better than the pointcloud representation (PointNet) for 3D contact map prediction.
 - Models designed to predict diverse outputs (sMCL with k=10 and DiverseNet with k=10) consistently achieved lower error rates than models limited to a single prediction (sMCL with k=1), confirming the one-to-many nature of the problem (Table 3). This indicates that predicting multiple plausible contact patterns is essential for this task.

- **Single-View Prediction (RGB-D with Conditional GANs):**
 - The modified pix2pix model was able to generate realistic 2D contact map predictions for unseen object classes (e.g., mug, pan, wine glass) (Figure 8).
 - These predictions reflected the functional intent; for example, 'use' predictions for a mug showed contact on the handle, while 'hand-off' showed contact over the top. Pan 'use' predictions concentrated contact on the handle.

- **3D Prediction (VoxNet-DiverseNet):**
 - The VoxNet-DiverseNet model successfully generated diverse and realistic 3D contact map predictions for unseen object classes and unseen shapes of training object classes (Figure 10).
 - Predictions for a mug in 'use' intent showed horizontal grasps around the body.
 - Pan predictions concentrated at the handle, with some showing bimanual grasps.
 - Wine glass predictions indicated contact at the body-stem intersection.
 - For a camera, predictions showed contact at the shutter button and sides, and for a hammer, contact was predominantly at the handle. These results demonstrate the model's ability to learn and predict functionally appropriate contact patterns in 3D.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the fields of robotics, computer vision, and human-robot interaction through its novel dataset and the insights derived from it.

#### 5.1. Foundational Dataset (ContactDB)

The creation of ContactDB represents a significant contribution by providing the first large-scale dataset that records detailed, object-centric hand-object contact maps from functional human grasping. This directly addresses the long-standing challenge of observing contact due to occlusion and hand complexity. ContactDB offers a high-fidelity ground truth for contact regions, which was previously unavailable at this scale and detail.

#### 5.2. Enhanced Understanding of Human Grasping

The analysis of ContactDB data provides empirical evidence that challenges simplified models of human grasping.
- **Functional Intent:** The clear demonstration of how functional intent profoundly influences grasp contact patterns offers critical insights for designing more intelligent and adaptable robotic systems.
- **Object Properties:** The analysis of object size and shape affecting grasp strategy contributes to a richer understanding of human motor control and object interaction.
- **Role of Non-Fingertip Contact:** The quantification of extensive non-fingertip contact highlights the importance of the palm and proximal finger regions in human manipulation. This finding directly contradicts common simplifications in robotic grasp modeling, such as single-point contact, and emphasizes the need for more sophisticated contact models.

#### 5.3. Impact on Robotics

The findings and dataset have several potential impacts on robotics:
- **Soft Robotic Manipulator Design:** The detailed human contact patterns, particularly the prevalence of non-fingertip contact, can inform the design of future soft robotic grippers. By emulating human contact strategies, such manipulators could achieve more robust, compliant, and versatile grasps.
- **Improved Grasp Prediction and Planning:** ContactDB provides the necessary data to train advanced deep learning models capable of predicting detailed and diverse contact maps. This can lead to the development of grasp planning algorithms that move beyond simplistic contact points, enabling robots to:
 - Execute more human-like and stable grasps.
 - Adapt grasps based on functional intent and object properties.
 - Handle a wider range of objects with greater dexterity and robustness in unstructured environments, particularly household settings.
- **Grasp Retargeting:** The object-centric nature of contact maps could facilitate the retargeting of human grasp knowledge to various robotic hand models, improving the transferability of human demonstrations to robotic systems.

#### 5.4. Impact on Computer Vision

For computer vision, ContactDB provides a unique resource for:
- **Hand Pose Estimation:** The detailed contact maps can serve as additional constraints or ground truth for recovering and assisting in the prediction of hand poses during object manipulation, particularly in scenarios with significant self-occlusion.
- **Interaction Understanding:** The dataset contributes to a deeper understanding of human-object interaction, which is crucial for tasks like action recognition, scene analysis, and human-robot collaboration.

#### 5.5. Future Work Directions

The authors suggest future work including utilizing contact patterns to inform the design of soft robotic manipulators and exploring the use of contact maps to recover and/or assist in predicting hand pose in functional grasping.

In summary, ContactDB and its accompanying analysis provide a robust empirical foundation for understanding the intricacies of human hand-object contact. This work has the potential to significantly advance the development of more intelligent, adaptable, and human-centric robotic systems capable of complex manipulation tasks.
