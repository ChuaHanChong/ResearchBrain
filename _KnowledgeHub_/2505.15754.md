# Improving planning and MBRL with temporally-extended actions

> [!summary] Summary
> This research integrates Temporally-Extended Actions (TEAs) into model-based reinforcement learning and planning frameworks, allowing agents to optimize both actions and their durations. It introduces a multi-armed bandit mechanism for dynamic selection of maximum action duration, demonstrating improved performance and computational efficiency across various continuous control tasks, by reducing planning horizon requirements and mitigating compounding errors in learned dynamics models.

## Problem

- Discrete-time approximations of continuous systems often require small simulation step sizes, leading to computationally demanding planning problems with long horizons.
- Model-Based Reinforcement Learning (MBRL) agents rolling out learned dynamics models over long horizons are prone to accumulating prediction errors.
- Existing temporal abstraction techniques (e.g., action repeats) are often static heuristics or learned in a model-free context, lacking principled integration into model-based planning with continuous duration optimization.

## Method

- The framework introduces **Temporally-Extended Actions** (TEAs) where the planner optimizes both the primitive action and its continuous duration within a specified range.
- A neural network-based `Temporally-Extended Dynamics Function` (F^TE) is learned to predict next states and aggregated rewards for TEAs in constant time, avoiding iterative primitive model calls.
- A non-stationary Multi-Armed Bandit (MAB) framework, utilizing UCB with Exponential Moving Average, dynamically selects the optimal maximum action duration (δtmaxδ t_{max}δtmax) at the start of each episode.

## Results

- Temporally-extended actions (`A_TE`) solved sparse-reward planning problems (e.g., Mountain Car with DTE≥4D_{TE} ≥ 4DTE≥4 vs. ASTDA_{STD}ASTD with DSTD≥60D_{STD} ≥ 60DSTD≥**60**) and complex tasks like Multi-hill Mountain Car and Dubins Car where standard methods struggled or failed.
- In MBRL environments (e.g., MuJoCo tasks like Ant, Hopper, Walker), `A_TE` variants consistently outperformed `A_STD` and achieved speedups of up to 8 times faster while maintaining or improving performance.
- The MAB framework for dynamic δtmaxδ t_{max}δtmax selection (`A_TE(D)`) converged to performance comparable to manually tuned fixed durations (`A_TE(F)`), demonstrating its adaptability and effectiveness for automated hyperparameter selection.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Optimizing action duration as an explicit variable within planning significantly reduces the effective search space and computational load, enabling deeper exploration for the same computational budget.
> - Directly learning a temporally-extended dynamics model (F^TE) is crucial for both computational efficiency and prediction accuracy, as it prevents compounding errors that arise from iterating primitive one-step models.
> - A principled and adaptive mechanism, such as the Multi-Armed Bandit, can automatically tune critical hyperparameters like action duration ranges, enhancing the robustness and applicability of the method without manual intervention.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of reinforcement learning (RL) and robotics frequently models continuous-time physical systems using discrete-time approximations. This approach often necessitates a small simulation step size ($\delta_t$) to maintain accuracy. A consequence of a small $\delta_t$ is the requirement for a large planning horizon, which translates into computationally demanding planning problems and, potentially, reduced agent performance.

Previous research in model-free reinforcement learning (MFRL) has explored solutions such as "action repeats" or "frame-skips," where a single primitive action is executed multiple times for a fixed or learned duration. While these methods introduce a form of temporal abstraction, they often involve heuristically set frame-skip values or learn policies to determine discrete action durations (e.g., Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017; Sharma et al., 2017). Some work has explored continuous representations for timescale control in MFRL (e.g., Ni and Jang, 2022; Wang and Beltrame, 2024), but these approaches typically operate within a model-free context, may involve modified reward structures or constraints, and do not explicitly address the planning problem or its integration into model-based reinforcement learning (MBRL).

This paper addresses the limitations of standard discrete-time modeling by directly controlling the decision timescale within planning and MBRL frameworks. It extends the concept of temporally-extended actions (similar to macro-actions or options, but with a continuously optimizable duration) to model-based settings. By treating action duration as an explicit optimization variable, the work aims to enhance the efficiency and effectiveness of planning, particularly for shooting-based planners (e.g., Cross Entropy Method, CEM; Model Predictive Path Integral, MPPI) which are known to struggle with long planning horizons and sparse rewards. Furthermore, it seeks to mitigate compounding errors inherent in MBRL when predictions are made over extended primitive timesteps. The approach distinguishes itself by not learning a policy for action duration but rather integrating it as an optimization variable for the planner, and by providing a method for automatically selecting the optimal range for action durations using a multi-armed bandit (MAB) framework.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations for this research stem from the challenges associated with applying discrete-time models to inherently continuous real-world systems in planning and model-based reinforcement learning.

The key motivations are:

1. **Addressing Computational Demands of Long Planning Horizons:** When continuous systems are approximated discretely with a small timestep ($\delta_t$), achieving accurate local dynamics requires many sequential decisions to cover a meaningful time period. This leads to very long planning horizons (or rollouts) in algorithms like CEM or MPPI. Such long horizons significantly increase the computational burden of planning and can reduce its effectiveness, especially in environments with noisy dynamics or sparse rewards where the variance of estimates grows.

2. **Mitigating Compounding Errors in Model-Based RL:** In MBRL, agents learn a dynamics model to predict future states and rewards. When these models are rolled out over long horizons, small inaccuracies in the learned model can accumulate, leading to substantial prediction errors further down the trajectory. Temporally-extended actions, by effectively compressing many primitive steps into fewer decision steps, aim to reduce the number of model predictions and thus mitigate the compounding of errors.

3. **Enhancing Planning Search Efficiency:** Current planning methods, particularly shooting-based ones, explore a vast search space of primitive action sequences. By allowing the planner to optimize not just the action but also its duration, the proposed framework aims to:
 - **Reduce Search Space:** A given temporal duration can be covered by fewer, longer temporally-extended actions than by many short primitive actions. This effectively reduces the number of decision variables for the planner, simplifying the optimization problem.
 - **Enable Deeper Search:** A shallow planning horizon in terms of temporally-extended actions can correspond to a significantly deeper horizon in terms of primitive actions. This allows the agent to explore further into the future without incurring the typical computational cost of a deep primitive action search, which is crucial for problems with sparse or delayed rewards.

4. **Improving Model Learning and Training Time in MBRL:** Faster simulations and a reduced number of decision points (due to temporally-extended actions) can lead to quicker data collection and potentially smaller datasets of temporally-extended transitions. This can accelerate the training process for dynamics models in MBRL.

5. **Introducing a Principled and Adaptive Control of Decision Timescale:** Existing frame-skip or action-repeat mechanisms are often static heuristics or involve learning a policy in a model-free context. This work aims to directly control the continuous decision timescale by treating action duration as an optimizable variable within a planning framework. Furthermore, it addresses the challenge of selecting an appropriate range for these durations by proposing an automated mechanism based on a non-stationary multi-armed bandit formulation, thereby removing the need for manual hyperparameter tuning.

In summary, the core motivation is to develop a more efficient and robust framework for planning and MBRL by explicitly incorporating and optimizing the temporal extent of actions, thereby bridging the gap between continuous system dynamics and discrete decision-making processes.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology introduces Temporally-Extended Actions (TEAs) into planning and Model-Based Reinforcement Learning (MBRL) by treating the action duration ($\delta t$) as an additional optimization variable.

#### 3.1. Modeling Temporally-Extended Actions

- **Standard MDP vs. TEA Framework:** A standard discrete-time Markov Decision Process (MDP) involves a state $s_t$, an action $a_t$, a one-step transition $T(s_{t+1}|s_t, a_t)$, and a one-step reward $R(s_t, a_t)$, with a fixed environment timescale $\delta_{env}t$. In the proposed framework, at each decision step $k$, the planner outputs both a primitive action $a_k$ and its duration $\delta t_k \in [\delta t_{min}, \delta t_{max}]$.
- **Decision Steps vs. Execution Steps:** The framework distinguishes between "decision steps" (when the agent outputs an action and its duration) and "execution steps" (the number of primitive actions actually executed in the environment over the duration $\delta t_k$). The number of execution steps $e_k$ for a decision step $k$ is calculated as $e_k = \lfloor \delta t_k / \delta_{env}t \rfloor + \mathbb{I}(\delta t_k \pmod{\delta_{env}t})$.
- **Modified Return Calculation:** The discounted return for a trajectory $\tau$ is reformulated to account for the variable durations. The initial formulation ($J_2$) discounts rewards based on the cumulative number of primitive steps taken before the current temporally-extended action. A refined view ($J_3$) allows for two distinct discount factors: $\gamma_1$ for discounting across temporally-extended actions (based on prior cumulative primitive steps) and $\gamma_2$ for discounting within a single temporally-extended action.

#### 3.2. Dynamics Functions for Temporally-Extended Actions

- **Temporally-Extended Dynamics Function (F):** The planner requires access to a function $F$ that maps a state $s_k$, action $a_k$, and duration $\delta t_k$ to the next state $s_{k+1}$ and aggregated reward.
- **Iterative Primitive Dynamics ($F_{IP}$):** If the exact primitive dynamics function $f$ is known, $F$ can be implemented by iteratively calling $f$ for $e_k$ times. This `Iterative Primitive Dynamics Function (Algorithm A1)` provides an accurate $F$ but is computationally slow, as simulation time scales with the action duration.
- **Approximating F with Neural Networks ($\hat{F}_{TE}$):** To achieve constant-time evaluation, the core approach is to learn an approximation $\hat{F}_{TE}$ using neural networks. This model takes the current state, primitive action, and action duration as inputs and predicts the next state and aggregated reward. This learning is performed within the MBRL framework.

#### 3.3. Selection of $\delta t_{min}$ and $\delta t_{max}$

- **$\delta t_{min}$:** This is naturally set to the environment's base timescale, $\delta_{env}t$.
- **$\delta t_{max}$ Selection via Multi-Armed Bandit (MAB):** The upper bound for action duration, $\delta t_{max}$, is crucial for performance and not directly provided by the environment. The paper frames its selection as a non-stationary MAB problem.
 - **Candidates:** A set of exponentially-spaced candidate values for $\delta t_{max}$ is defined.
 - **Arms:** Each $\delta t_{max}$ candidate represents an arm of the bandit.
 - **Pulling an Arm:** Choosing an arm means using its corresponding $\delta t_{max}$ value for planning during one episode. The aggregate reward obtained in that episode serves as the arm's reward.
 - **Non-stationarity:** The reward distribution of an arm changes as the agent's learned dynamics model improves over training episodes.
 - **UCB with EMA:** To handle non-stationarity, the Upper Confidence Bound (UCB) heuristic (Auer et al., 2002) is used, coupled with an Exponential Moving Average (EMA) of rewards ($\hat{R}_{i,T}$) for each arm. This weights recent performance more heavily.
 - **Separate Dynamics Models:** Ablation studies confirmed that learning a *separate* dynamics model ($\hat{F}_{TE}$) for each $\delta t_{max}$ candidate (resulting in a "rested bandit" setting) performs better than a single shared model.

#### 3.4. Planning and MBRL Integration

- **Planning with F (Algorithm 1):** A generic shooting-based planner (e.g., CEM) is used. It samples action sequences (where each action includes its duration), simulates trajectories using $F$ (or $\hat{F}_{TE}$), computes aggregate rewards, and updates its action distribution.
- **MBRL Framework (Algorithm 2):**
 - **Data Collection:** The agent interacts with the environment, collecting (state, action, next\_state, reward) tuples where "action" now includes the optimized duration.
 - **Model Training:** An ensemble of neural networks learns $\hat{F}_{TE}$ to predict next states and rewards from temporally-extended actions.
 - **Agent Variants:**
 - `A_STD` (Standard Agent): Uses primitive actions and learns $\hat{f}$. Equivalent to TS$\infty$ from PETS (Chua et al., 2018).
 - `A_TE(F)` (Fixed Duration): Uses a fixed, manually chosen $\delta t_{max}$ for all episodes.
 - `A_TE(D)` (Dynamic Duration): Uses the MAB framework to dynamically select $\delta t_{max}$ at the start of each episode, maintaining separate datasets and $\hat{F}_{TE}$ models for each $\delta t_{max}$ candidate.

#### 3.5. Comparative Analysis (Discussion)

The paper analyzes the benefits of `A_TE` compared to `A_STD` by considering a fixed maximal primitive horizon (H). `A_TE` offers:
1. **Reduced Trajectory Search Space:** For a given H, the number of possible trajectories `A_TE` considers is significantly smaller (e.g., $2^{H/m}$ vs. $2^H$ for binary actions, where $m$ relates to $\delta t_{max}$).
2. **Fewer Optimization Variables:** `A_TE` optimizes $(H/m)(|A|+1)$ variables, compared to $H|A|$ for `A_STD`, further streamlining planning.
3. **Faster Simulation:** With $\hat{F}_{TE}$, temporally-extended actions are evaluated in constant time, leading to faster decision-making for `A_TE` since $D_{TE} < D_{STD}$.
4. **Scalable Planning Horizon:** `A_TE` can scale its effective primitive planning horizon without increasing the number of decision variables by manipulating $m$.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation covers both planning with exact dynamics and MBRL with learned dynamics across various environments.

#### 4.1. Planning with Exact Dynamics

- **Mountain Car:** The temporally-extended action approach (`A_TE`) demonstrated superior performance, solving the sparse-reward environment with a significantly smaller planning horizon ($D_{TE} \ge 4$) compared to the standard primitive action approach (`A_STD`, $D_{STD} \ge 60$).
- **Multi-hill Mountain Car (IPC 2023):** `A_TE` successfully solved all five instances of this more challenging environment, while `A_STD` only managed to solve the easiest first instance (Table 1). Although `A_TE` took fewer decision steps, the total computation time per episode was comparable to `A_STD` due to using the iterative primitive dynamics function ($F_{IP}$) for simulation.
- **Dubins Car (U-shaped map):** This environment, characterized by sparse rewards and initial obstacles, presented a challenge where `A_STD` struggled significantly. `A_TE` with $D_{TE}=75$ and $\delta t_{max}=20$ was able to find solutions. When the action space was augmented with 100 dummy variables, `A_STD` failed due to excessive memory requirements (e.g., needing 4GB vs. 103MB for `A_TE`). This indicated that `A_TE` can transform computationally infeasible search problems into feasible ones.
- **Impact of Two Discount Factors ($\gamma_1$, $\gamma_2$):** Experiments on the Dubins Car cave-mini map explored the effects of the two discount factors in the $J_3$ formulation (Equation 2).
 - Fixing $\gamma_1$ and decreasing $\gamma_2$ resulted in an increase in the number of decision steps, implying a preference for shorter action durations.
 - Fixing $\gamma_2$ and decreasing $\gamma_1$ led to a decrease in the total number of primitive actions taken, indicating a preference for longer action durations to mitigate overall discounting.

#### 4.2. MBRL with Learned Dynamics

Experiments were conducted on Cartpole (Gymnasium) and several MuJoCo environments (Ant, Half Cheetah, Hopper, Reacher, Pusher, Walker), with frame-skip set to 1 for all MuJoCo tasks.

- **Overall Performance and Efficiency:** In general, `A_TE` variants (`A_TE(F)` and `A_TE(D)`) demonstrated better performance than `A_STD` while being significantly faster in terms of computation time (Table A3). The speedup for `A_TE` was attributed to fewer decision points, a reduced search space, and consequently, smaller datasets for model training, leading to faster model training.
- **Cartpole:** With the default sparse reward, `A_STD` exhibited unstable learning and often crashed. Both `A_TE` variants (with $D_{TE}=5$) performed well with both sparse and a more informative (dense) reward function, which stabilized `A_STD`'s learning.
- **Locomotion Tasks (Ant, Half Cheetah, Hopper, Walker):** For these tasks, the reward functions were modified to be bounded between 0 and 1 for the MAB framework.
 - **Half Cheetah:** `A_TE` achieved similar performance to `A_STD` but was approximately 8 times faster.
 - **Ant, Hopper, Walker:** `A_TE` significantly outperformed `A_STD`. `A_TE(F)` (with a manually selected $\delta t_{max}$) often yielded the best performance, while `A_TE(D)` (dynamic selection) explored various candidate $\delta t_{max}$ values, converged more slowly, but eventually matched the performance of `A_TE(F)`.
- **Manipulation Tasks (Pusher, Reacher):** `A_TE` showed performance comparable to `A_STD`. In these environments, the number of decision points for `A_TE` was also similar to `A_STD`, suggesting that `A_TE` identified that a small decision timescale was more effective, indicating its adaptability.
- **Comparison to Action Repeats (Flexibility):** Analysis of the distribution of action durations chosen by `A_TE(F)` at the end of training (Figure 4) revealed its ability to adapt. While in Ant and Half Cheetah, `A_TE` often chose actions with similar durations (akin to fixed frame-skips), in Hopper and Walker, the durations varied significantly within an episode, demonstrating that the agent benefited from this temporal flexibility.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies

- **Single Model vs. Separate Models for MAB:** Preliminary experiments (Figure A4) showed that `A_TE(D)` performed better when learning a separate dynamics model for each $\delta t_{max}$ candidate (rested bandit setting) rather than using a single shared model across all arms (restless bandit setting).
- **$\hat{F}_{TE}$ vs. $\hat{F}_{IP}$ Prediction Quality:** Comparing the prediction error (MSE) on random action sequences (Table A7), $\hat{F}_{IP}$ (learned one-step model iterated) had lower error for one-step sequences. However, for temporally-extended sequences, $\hat{F}_{TE}$ (learned direct temporally-extended model) clearly performed better. This is attributed to compounding errors when iterating a one-step model for longer durations. This supports the choice of $\hat{F}_{TE}$ for both computational efficiency and prediction accuracy in the context of temporally-extended actions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework for integrating temporally-extended actions into planning and Model-Based Reinforcement Learning, offering several contributions and potential impacts:

1. **Enhanced Computational Efficiency:** By allowing planners to optimize action durations, the method significantly reduces the number of decision steps and the overall search space. This translates to faster planning, reduced training times for dynamics models in MBRL, and lower computational resource requirements, making complex problems more tractable.
2. **Improved Performance in Challenging Environments:** The approach demonstrates effectiveness in environments with sparse rewards, long horizons, and complex dynamics, such as Mountain Car variants and Dubins Car. It enables solutions to problems that were previously difficult or computationally infeasible for standard primitive-action planners, particularly when action spaces are large or memory constraints are significant.
3. **Mitigation of Compounding Errors:** In MBRL, the learned models are prone to compounding errors when rolled out over many primitive steps. Temporally-extended actions effectively reduce the number of times the model must predict, thereby decreasing the accumulation of these errors and leading to more stable and robust learning.
4. **Principled Temporal Abstraction:** Unlike heuristic frame-skipping or model-free approaches to learning action durations, this work offers a model-based planning framework where action duration is an explicit optimization variable. This provides a more principled way for agents to adapt their decision timescale to the demands of the task and environment.
5. **Automated Hyperparameter Tuning:** The integration of a Multi-Armed Bandit framework for dynamically selecting the range of action durations ($\delta t_{max}$) removes the need for manual tuning of this critical hyperparameter. While it may require more initial exploration, it eventually converges to performance comparable to carefully chosen fixed values, increasing the robustness and applicability of the method.
6. **Flexibility and Adaptability:** The analysis of action duration distributions demonstrates that the agent can adapt its decision timescale within an episode, choosing varied durations when beneficial (e.g., in Hopper and Walker) rather than adhering to a fixed repeat schedule. This flexibility is a key advantage over simple action repeats.

**Potential Limitations and Future Directions:**

The authors acknowledge several limitations. Firstly, the use of temporally-extended actions does not guarantee improvement in all situations; the reduced flexibility in trajectories compared to primitive actions may sometimes be suboptimal. Secondly, the current MAB formulation assumes a fixed temporally-extended planning horizon ($D_{TE}$). Future work could explore dynamic selection of both $\delta t_{max}$ and $D_{TE}$ simultaneously to further enhance adaptability.

Overall, this research provides a significant step towards more efficient and effective planning and MBRL in domains that are fundamentally continuous, with potential applications in robotics, autonomous systems, and other control problems.
