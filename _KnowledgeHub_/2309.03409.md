# Large Language Models as Optimizers

> [!summary] Summary
> OPRO treats a large language model as a black-box optimizer. A meta-prompt holds a natural-language task description plus past solutions with their scores. The LLM proposes new candidates, which are scored and appended, and the loop repeats. On prompt optimization, discovered instructions beat human-written ones by up to 8% on GSM8K and up to 50% on Big-Bench Hard.

## Problem

- Derivative-based optimizers cannot be applied to many real-world problems that lack gradients, and derivative-free methods need custom algorithms for each task's decision space and performance landscape.
- Prompt performance is highly sensitive to wording, and the discrete prompt space is huge, which makes tuning hard when only API access to the LLM is available.
- Earlier LLM-based prompt generators edit one prompt from feedback or force semantic equivalence, so they do not exploit the full trajectory of past prompts and scores.

## Method

- **OPRO**'s ==meta-prompt== combines a natural-language problem description, meta-instructions, task exemplars, and an ==optimization trajectory== of previous solutions with scores sorted in ascending order, so the LLM can spot patterns in high-scoring ones.
- Each step samples several candidate solutions, up to 8, to stabilize optimization, and the optimizer's ==sampling temperature== (default 1.0) controls the ==exploration-exploitation trade-off==.
- The ==scorer LLM== evaluates candidate instructions on a small training subset (3.5% of GSM8K, 20% of BBH). Scored candidates are added to the meta-prompt, and the loop stops at convergence or a step limit.

## Results

- On GSM8K with a PaLM 2-L scorer, the best OPRO instruction reaches **80.2%** zero-shot, versus 71.8% for 'Let's think step by step' and 34.0% for an empty prompt.
- On 23 BBH tasks, OPRO instructions beat 'Let's think step by step' by over **5%** on **19** tasks with a PaLM 2-L scorer and on **15** tasks with text-bison, with gains up to **50%**.
- On TSP with 10 nodes, all optimizer LLMs reach the optimum, and gpt-4 gets there about **4x** faster. At 50 nodes, gpt-4 has an **11.0%** optimality gap versus 133% for gpt-3.5-turbo and 219.8% for text-bison.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Feeding the LLM a scored history of its own attempts lets it perform gradient-free search, without a formal update rule, so changing the task only requires rewriting the problem description.
> - Optimizer temperature matters: values of 0.0 to 0.5 stall on the same instruction, while 1.5 to 2.0 ignore the trajectory. Sampling several candidates per step also stabilizes the search.
> - LLM optimizers are limited by context length and bumpy landscapes, and they degrade on larger TSP instances. Prompts also overfit, so use a training set of at least tens of examples or early stopping.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of optimization is fundamental to nearly all areas of science, engineering, and business. Traditional optimization techniques often rely on derivative information (e.g., gradient descent) or require extensive problem-specific customization, particularly for derivative-free or combinatorial optimization problems. This paper positions itself at the intersection of large language models and optimization, proposing a new paradigm where LLMs, inherently designed for natural language understanding and generation, are repurposed as general-purpose optimizers.

In the broader context of LLM research, there has been a growing interest in enhancing their reasoning and problem-solving capabilities beyond mere text generation. This includes:
- **Prompt Engineering and Optimization:** The sensitivity of LLMs to prompt phrasing has led to the emergence of "prompt engineering." Initial efforts involved manual crafting of prompts. More recently, methods for *automatic* prompt optimization have emerged, including continuous prompt tuning (optimizing embedding vectors), gradient-guided discrete search, reinforcement learning, and edit-based approaches. This paper's OPRO (Optimization by PROmpting) directly contributes to the latter, proposing a novel iterative, black-box approach for discrete prompt optimization. It differentiates itself from prior LLM-based prompt optimization works like APE (Zhou et al., 2022b) and APO (Pryzant et al., 2023) by leveraging the *entire optimization trajectory* of past solutions and their scores within the meta-prompt, rather than just editing or generating variants of a single instruction.
- **LLMs as Agents/Tools:** There is a burgeoning area of research exploring LLMs as general agents or "tool makers" that can interact with external environments or utilize external tools (e.g., calculators, search engines) to solve problems. While OPRO doesn't explicitly involve external tools for general optimization, its application to prompt optimization where an external "scorer LLM" acts as an objective function evaluator implicitly connects to this trend.
- **LLMs in Evolutionary Algorithms:** Concurrent work explores using LLMs as mutation and crossover operators in evolutionary algorithms (e.g., Promptbreeder, EvoPrompt). OPRO shares some conceptual similarities with these approaches in its iterative search but fundamentally differs in its "gradient-free" and trajectory-based prompting mechanism, allowing the LLM to infer promising directions rather than being explicitly instructed to perform mutation/crossover.

By demonstrating LLMs' capacity for general optimization, the paper contributes to the understanding of their emergent abilities and expands their applicability beyond traditional natural language tasks. It suggests that LLMs possess an inherent capacity for "reasoning about optimization" by identifying patterns in performance data and inferring improvements, even for tasks without explicit natural language solutions.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core objective of this research is to **propose and evaluate Optimization by PROmpting (OPRO)**, a novel approach that leverages Large Language Models (LLMs) to serve as general-purpose optimizers. The primary motivation stems from the inherent challenges of traditional optimization methods in real-world scenarios:
- **Absence of Gradients:** Many real-world problems lack analytical gradients or are expensive to compute, making derivative-based optimization techniques inapplicable.
- **Task-Specific Customization:** Derivative-free optimization often requires algorithms to be painstakingly customized for individual tasks, which is time-consuming and resource-intensive, especially for discrete or complex search spaces.
- **Leveraging LLM Capabilities:** Modern LLMs exhibit remarkable natural language understanding and generation capabilities. The authors hypothesize that this ability can be harnessed to describe optimization tasks in natural language, allowing the LLM to iteratively generate improved solutions without explicit programming or formal problem definitions. This adaptability via natural language is a key motivator.

Specifically, the paper aims to:
- **Demonstrate LLM Feasibility as Optimizers:** Show that LLMs can effectively perform black-box optimization on classic problems like linear regression (continuous) and the Traveling Salesman Problem (discrete), identifying promising directions and finding good-quality solutions.
- **Solve Prompt Optimization as a Key Application:** The main application focus is on finding optimal prompts (instructions) that maximize the task accuracy of a target LLM. This addresses a significant practical challenge in deploying LLMs, where prompt engineering is crucial but often manual and tedious, especially given the large and discrete prompt space.
- **Evaluate OPRO's Effectiveness and Robustness:** Conduct comprehensive evaluations across different LLMs (as optimizers and scorers) and benchmarks (GSM8K, Big-Bench Hard) to demonstrate the performance gains over human-designed prompts and analyze key design choices through ablation studies.

In essence, the paper seeks to establish LLMs as versatile, adaptable optimizers that can "reason" about improvement in a natural language described search space, particularly for black-box optimization problems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, **Optimization by PROmpting (OPRO)**, leverages the LLM's in-context learning capabilities in an iterative optimization loop.

#### 3.1. Framework Overview

OPRO operates as follows:
1. **Initialization:** The optimization begins with an initial set of candidate solutions (e.g., randomly sampled values for `(w, b)` in linear regression, or an empty string/human-designed prompt in prompt optimization).
2. **Iterative Optimization Loop:** In each step:
 - **Meta-Prompt Construction:** A "meta-prompt" is constructed and fed to the LLM (acting as the optimizer). This meta-prompt is crucial and contains three key components:
 - **Optimization Problem Description:** A natural language description of the optimization task, including the objective function and any constraints. For prompt optimization, this includes "meta-instructions" (e.g., "generate a new instruction that achieves a higher accuracy") and desired output format.
 - **Optimization Trajectory:** Previously generated solutions along with their evaluated scores (e.g., `(w, b)` pairs with their loss, or prompts with their training accuracy). Critically, these are sorted in ascending order of score (best solutions last) to leverage recency bias in LLM in-context learning.
 - **(For Prompt Optimization) Task Exemplars:** A few input-output examples from the task's training set are included to provide context and demonstrate the desired task for the generated instructions.
 - **Solution Generation:** The optimizer LLM, given the meta-prompt, generates a batch of new candidate solutions. To enhance stability and exploration, OPRO prompts the LLM to generate multiple solutions (e.g., 8) in each step. A tunable sampling temperature (default 1.0) is used to balance exploration (higher temperature) and exploitation (lower temperature).
 - **Solution Evaluation:** Each newly generated solution is evaluated by an external "objective function evaluator" (e.g., a function calculating MSE for linear regression, or a "scorer LLM" calculating accuracy for prompt optimization).
 - **Trajectory Update:** The new solutions and their scores are added to the optimization trajectory, which is then re-sorted and used for the next iteration.
3. **Termination:** The process continues for a fixed number of steps or until the LLM fails to propose better solutions. The best-performing solution found throughout the trajectory is the final output.

#### 3.2. Key Design Choices

- **Natural Language as Interface:** OPRO fundamentally relies on LLMs' ability to understand and reason about optimization described purely in natural language, eliminating the need for formal specifications or gradient calculations.
- **Exploiting In-Context Learning for Trajectory:** By providing a sorted list of past solutions and their scores, the LLM is implicitly guided to recognize patterns in good solutions and propose modifications that are likely to improve the score. This mimics a "descent direction" or "improvement heuristic" without explicit gradient computation.
- **Batch Generation for Stability:** Generating multiple solutions per step (e.g., 8) helps mitigate the inherent variance and sensitivity of LLM outputs, allowing the optimizer to explore several possibilities and quickly identify promising avenues.
- **Temperature Tuning:** The sampling temperature of the optimizer LLM is a critical hyperparameter to balance between exploiting existing good solutions (low temperature) and exploring new regions of the search space (high temperature).

#### 3.3. Differentiation from Prior Work

OPRO's key differentiators, particularly in prompt optimization, are:
- **Trajectory-based Optimization:** Unlike APE (Zhou et al., 2022b) which generates semantic variants of individual top prompts, or APO (Pryzant et al., 2023) which relies on natural language feedback to *edit* one prompt, OPRO incorporates the *full history* of solution-score pairs. This allows the LLM to learn from a broader "optimization trajectory."
- **Direct Generation vs. Editing:** OPRO's optimizer LLM directly generates new prompts, rather than requiring specific edit operations, offering greater flexibility in the search space.
- **Exemplar Integration:** For prompt optimization, OPRO explicitly includes task exemplars within the meta-prompt, providing the optimizer LLM with a concrete understanding of the downstream task it is optimizing for. This is a crucial difference from some evolutionary prompting methods like EvoPrompt, which initially struggled without such exemplars.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

OPRO's effectiveness is demonstrated across both mathematical optimization and, more significantly, prompt optimization tasks.

#### 4.1. Mathematical Optimization Case Studies

- **Linear Regression:** OPRO successfully optimized `(w, b)` parameters in a synthetic linear regression setting. `gpt-4` consistently outperformed `text-bison` and `gpt-3.5-turbo` in convergence speed and finding optima with fewer explored points, demonstrating its superior "reasoning" about the descent direction. The difficulty increased as true parameters moved further from the starting region.
- **Traveling Salesman Problem (TSP):** For small-scale TSP problems (`n=10`), all LLMs found optimal solutions. `gpt-4` was significantly faster (4x) than others. As `n` increased (`n=15, 20`), `gpt-4` maintained comparable quality to hand-designed heuristics (Nearest Neighbor, Farthest Insertion), while `text-bison` and `gpt-3.5-turbo` struggled, getting stuck at local optima.
- **Limitations:** Despite successes, OPRO on mathematical optimization faced limitations: context window length for large problems, difficulty with "bumpy" loss landscapes (e.g., Rosenbrock function), and occasional "hallucinations" or non-adherence to instructions (e.g., generating already seen solutions). The authors clarify OPRO is not intended to outperform specialized solvers but to showcase LLMs' inherent optimization capability.

#### 4.2. Prompt Optimization Performance

This is the main application, showing significant performance gains:
- **GSM8K:** On grade school math word problems, OPRO-optimized prompts (e.g., "Take a deep breath and work on this problem step-by-step.") achieved up to **80.2% accuracy** with PaLM 2-L as scorer, outperforming human-designed prompts like "Let's think step by step." (71.8%) by up to **8% absolute**. Optimization curves showed consistent upward trends and reduced variance over steps.
- **Big-Bench Hard (BBH):** OPRO-optimized prompts generally outperformed "Let's think step by step." on **19/23 tasks (PaLM 2-L scorer)** and **15/23 tasks (text-bison scorer)** by large margins, sometimes over **50% relative accuracy gain** compared to the empty string baseline. This demonstrates OPRO's broad applicability across diverse reasoning tasks.
- **Qualitative Analysis of Prompts:** Optimized prompts showed diverse styles depending on the optimizer LLM (e.g., concise for PaLM 2-L-IT, long and detailed for GPT models) but consistently led to high performance. The observation that semantically similar instructions can yield drastically different accuracies underscores the challenge of prompt engineering and OPRO's value.

#### 4.3. Transferability of Found Instructions

Prompts optimized on GSM8K also demonstrated strong transferability to other mathematical reasoning datasets, MultiArith and AQuA, outperforming baseline prompts with different scorer LLMs. For instance, an OPRO-optimized prompt on GSM8K achieved 95.3% on MultiArith with PaLM 2-L, significantly higher than "Let's think step by step." (85.7%).

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies

- **Meta-prompt Design:**
 - **Instruction Order:** Sorting past solutions from lowest to highest score (ascending order) in the meta-prompt yielded better and faster convergence, consistent with LLMs' recency bias.
 - **Instruction Scores:** Including numerical accuracy scores (even rounded to integers) was crucial. Without scores, or with coarse bucketing, performance suffered, indicating scores help the LLM understand solution quality differences.
 - **Exemplars:** Including a few (e.g., 3) task exemplars was critical for performance, as they provided essential context about the task. More exemplars (10) did not necessarily improve performance and could distract the LLM.
- **Number of Generated Instructions per Step:** Generating 8 instructions per step (the default) achieved the best performance over a fixed budget, balancing exploration (more new ideas) and exploitation (more steps to learn from trajectory).
- **Starting Point:** While OPRO can improve from weaker initial prompts, starting with a stronger initial prompt generally led to faster convergence and sometimes better final performance, highlighting the benefit of a good initial search point.
- **Diversity (Temperature):** A temperature of 1.0 for the optimizer LLM was optimal, striking a balance between exploration (higher diversity) and exploitation (leveraging past good solutions). Too low a temperature led to stagnation, while too high led to erratic search.
- **Comparison with One-step Generation:** OPRO's iterative, trajectory-leveraging approach significantly outperformed a baseline that generated all instructions in a single step, validating the importance of the iterative optimization loop.

#### 4.5. Overfitting Analysis

The study found that while some overfitting occurs (training accuracy often 5-20% higher than test accuracy), the validation accuracy curves generally tracked the training curves. This suggests that solutions with higher training accuracy tend to correspond to higher validation/test accuracy, making direct optimization on a small training set a viable strategy. Using a larger training set or early stopping could further mitigate overfitting.

#### 4.6. Comparison with EvoPrompt

OPRO consistently outperformed EvoPrompt (both GA and DE versions), especially when exemplars were critical for task understanding (e.g., GSM8K). EvoPrompt struggled without task-specific exemplars, and even with them, its optimization curve was less stable than OPRO's. This highlights OPRO's advantage in providing richer contextual information (exemplars and a broader optimization trajectory) to the optimizer LLM.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Large Language Models as Optimizers" paper introduces a highly significant concept and a practical framework (OPRO) that has substantial implications for both AI research and its applications:

**Significance:**
- **New Paradigm for Optimization:** OPRO demonstrates a novel, "natural language-driven" approach to optimization. It shows that LLMs can go beyond mere language tasks and effectively act as black-box optimizers, inferring improvement directions from historical data without explicit gradients or complex problem-specific algorithms. This is a fundamental shift in how optimization problems can be approached.
- **Democratization of Optimization:** By allowing optimization tasks to be described in natural language, OPRO makes sophisticated optimization more accessible to non-experts. This reduces the barrier to entry for solving complex problems that might otherwise require deep mathematical or algorithmic expertise.
- **Breakthrough in Prompt Engineering:** The most immediate and impactful application demonstrated is prompt optimization. OPRO automates and significantly improves prompt engineering, a critical and often tedious aspect of deploying LLMs. Achieving up to 8% higher accuracy on GSM8K and 50% on BBH tasks compared to human-designed prompts is a remarkable achievement. This could lead to a new standard for optimizing LLM performance.
- **Exploration of LLM Capabilities:** This work further illuminates the emergent reasoning and pattern recognition capabilities of large language models. The ability to identify common characteristics of high-performing solutions from a trajectory and generate new, improved ones suggests a deeper understanding of problem spaces than previously attributed.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Enhanced LLM Performance:** The direct and immediate impact is on improving the performance of LLMs across various tasks by generating superior prompts. This can lead to more robust and accurate AI systems in diverse applications, from question answering to code generation and beyond.
- **Reduced Human Effort:** Automating prompt engineering significantly reduces the manual effort required by human developers, allowing them to focus on higher-level problem formulation and deployment.
- **Broader Optimization Applications:** While current demonstrations are on small-scale mathematical problems and prompt optimization, the underlying principle of OPRO could be extended to other derivative-free, black-box optimization problems in fields like materials science, drug discovery, robotics, or even hyperparameter tuning for other machine learning models.
- **Foundation for Future AI Systems:** OPRO contributes to the vision of more autonomous AI systems that can self-improve and adapt to new tasks with minimal human intervention. Future work might explore how LLMs can not only optimize but also *reason about* and *diagnose* optimization failures more effectively.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
The paper also acknowledges important limitations, which serve as clear directions for future research:
- **Context Window Length:** This remains a constraint for large-scale optimization problems (e.g., high-dimensional linear regression or very large TSP instances), as the entire optimization trajectory needs to fit into the meta-prompt.
- **Bumpy Loss Landscapes:** LLMs can get stuck in local optima, particularly on complex, non-convex landscapes, much like traditional optimizers.
- **Utilizing Error Cases:** The current OPRO implementation for prompt optimization primarily relies on aggregated accuracy scores. A key future direction is to incorporate richer, instance-level feedback about errors to allow the LLM to infer *why* certain solutions fail and how to rectify them more efficiently, potentially reducing the need for large training sets.
- **Robustness to Initialization:** While OPRO generally improves performance, starting from very weak initial solutions might still lead to slower convergence or suboptimal results.
- **Hallucinations and Instruction Following:** Occasional failure of the LLM to strictly follow meta-instructions (e.g., generating duplicate solutions) remains a challenge.

In conclusion, "Large Language Models as Optimizers" introduces a powerful and versatile framework that expands the utility of LLMs, particularly in the critical domain of prompt optimization. It lays the groundwork for more adaptable and autonomous AI systems, opening exciting avenues for future research in AI-driven optimization.
