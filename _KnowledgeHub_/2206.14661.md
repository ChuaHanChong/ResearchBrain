# Online vs. Offline Adaptive Domain Randomization Benchmark

> [!summary] Summary
> An open benchmark comparing four Adaptive Domain Randomization methods - SimOpt, BayRn (online, iterative on-policy target data) and DROID, DROPO (offline, fixed datasets) - on sim-to-sim transfer across Hopper, HalfCheetah, Walker2D and Humanoid under vanilla, noisy and unmodeled-dynamics conditions. Evaluation targets transfer return and real-data efficiency rather than posterior accuracy, revealing distinct failure modes per setting.

## Problem

- Existing ADR methods each report gains over their own baselines but are almost never compared against each other under matched tasks, assumptions and data budgets.
- Prior simulator-calibration benchmarks tested only point-estimate system identification under noiseless, perfectly modeled conditions, ignoring distribution inference and data-efficiency requirements.
- No study quantifies how transfer performance degrades with limited target-domain data, which is expensive and safety-constrained to collect on real robotic hardware.

## Method

- Unified benchmark protocol caps online methods at 5 iterations, one 200-transition trajectory each, and feeds offline methods the identical cumulative SimOpt-collected data for matched information.
- Four ADR methods reimplemented under shared conditions: SimOpt with REPS plus trust region, BayRn with Bayesian Optimization on real returns, DROID with CMA-ES open-loop replay, DROPO with maximum-likelihood inference and state resets.
- Four MuJoCo gym tasks instantiated in vanilla, noisy-observation and unmodeled-parameter variants (parameters underestimated 20%), with SAC policies, a SimOpt-1 single-iteration variant, and UDR random-search baseline over 3 seeds.

## Results

- DROPO solved all four tasks from a single 200-transition trajectory even under observation noise, and gave the highest average return under unmodeled phenomena, at the cost of heavy regularization tuning and slow Monte-Carlo inference.
- SimOpt reached task-solving transfer within 5 trajectories (about 10 seconds of data) in most settings, but degraded on noisy Humanoid after iteration 1 and failed on unmodeled Humanoid due to ill-performing intermediate policies.
- BayRn failed on parameter spaces of dimension 8 or higher within the 5-iteration budget (no Humanoid result reported), yet beat all methods on Hopper with unmodeled dynamics by optimizing returns directly.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Online adaptation is bottlenecked by its own intermediate policy: a bad policy collects uninformative or unsafe data and can derail the entire iterative identification loop.
> - Open-loop replay of recorded actions in simulation yields uninformative discrepancy metrics once trajectories diverge; periodic simulator state resets during replay largely remove this failure mode.
> - CMA-ES-based inference can collapse to near-zero variance, silently degrading domain randomization into point-estimate system identification and discarding robustness benefits.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of deep Reinforcement Learning (RL) has demonstrated potential for various robotic applications by enabling tasks to be solved through trial-and-error. However, training RL agents through real-world interaction typically requires extensive data and poses safety concerns. Physics simulators offer a safer and more unconstrained environment for learning, leading to successful applications in areas such as robotic manipulation, drone flight, and quadruped locomotion. A significant challenge in this approach is the "reality gap," the discrepancy between simulated and real-world performance, often caused by mismatched dynamics models, unmodeled phenomena, and environmental changes.

Domain Randomization (DR) is a technique employed to bridge this reality gap. It involves training robots across diverse simulated environments by randomizing physics parameters according to a probability distribution. While effective, standard DR often requires manual engineering of these distributions, which can be a laborious process. Adaptive Domain Randomization (ADR) methods address this by automatically inferring optimal source domain distributions. These methods typically involve tuning simulator parameters with posterior distributions derived from real-world data, which are then used for domain randomization during training.

Despite the proliferation of ADR methods in recent years, a comprehensive comparative analysis of their performance, particularly concerning sim-to-real transfer and data efficiency, has been largely absent in the literature. Existing ADR studies often present their methods as superior to specific baselines but rarely benchmark against other diverse ADR approaches. This work positions itself to fill this gap by providing an open benchmark for both online and offline ADR methods, aiming to clarify their respective strengths, limitations, and suitability for different robotic tasks and data collection scenarios. The paper’s contribution lies in categorizing and systematically comparing these methods, thereby offering insights for future research and application in robot learning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to conduct an open and systematic benchmark of several existing Adaptive Domain Randomization (ADR) methods. The study focuses on evaluating these methods based on their target domain transfer performance and data efficiency, specifically distinguishing between online and offline approaches.

The motivation for this detailed investigation stems from several factors within the sim-to-real transfer paradigm:

- **Bridging the Reality Gap:** The inherent challenge of transferring policies learned in simulation to real-world hardware, known as the reality gap, remains a significant impediment to the widespread adoption of RL in robotics. ADR methods offer a promising avenue to address this by automatically adjusting simulator parameters to better represent the real environment.
- **Limitations of Manual Domain Randomization:** Manually engineering static domain randomization distributions is a resource-intensive and often suboptimal process. The complexity of tasks and the subtle nature of reality gap factors make manual tuning difficult to generalize and scale. ADR methods aim to automate this process, making it more efficient and robust.
- **Absence of Comparative Benchmarking:** Despite the emergence of numerous ADR techniques—each proposing novel approaches to infer source domain distributions—the literature lacks a thorough, head-to-head comparison of their real-world transfer capabilities and data requirements. Various methods operate under different assumptions, target different tasks, and use distinct evaluation metrics, making it challenging for researchers to ascertain which method is most appropriate for a given problem. This benchmark aims to provide clarity in this fragmented landscape.
- **Understanding Online vs. Offline Trade-offs:** ADR methods can broadly be categorized into online and offline approaches based on their data collection strategies. Online methods iteratively collect data from the real world using the currently learned policy, potentially leading to high long-term performance but entailing risks and costs associated with real-hardware interaction and intermediate policy rollouts. Offline methods rely on pre-collected, fixed datasets, offering safety and parallelization but potentially limiting adaptability with restricted data. A key motivation is to elucidate the practical trade-offs between these two paradigms, informing decisions on data collection strategies in safety-critical or resource-constrained robotic scenarios.
- **Informing Future Research:** By systematically analyzing the performance and data efficiency of representative ADR algorithms under various challenging conditions (noisy observations, unmodeled phenomena), the authors aim to highlight current limitations and suggest directions for further methodological development in the field. The benchmark is intended to serve as a foundation for a common evaluation framework.

The specific contributions outlined by the authors reflect these motivations:
1. To characterize the distinct assumptions and limitations of online versus offline ADR methods.
2. To provide a comprehensive performance analysis of four representative ADR methods (SimOpt, BayRn, DROID, DROPO) in terms of target domain policy performance and data efficiency.
3. To examine how different data collection strategies influence the performance of offline methods.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The study establishes a formal problem setup where the source domain environment is modeled as a discrete-time Markov Decision Process (MDP) parameterized by dynamics parameters, ξ, which follow a parametric distribution p_φ(ξ). The objective of an RL agent is to learn a robust policy that maximizes expected cumulative rewards over this distribution. Adaptive Domain Randomization (ADR) methods are then employed to infer the parameters φ of this source domain distribution using real-world state-transitions D collected from a target domain environment M_real.

The methodology distinctly categorizes ADR methods into online and offline approaches:
- **Online ADR:** These methods, exemplified by SimOpt and BayRn, involve a bi-level optimization where RL policy training in simulation is interleaved with iterative data collection from the real world using the currently trained policy. This process adapts the source domain distribution until convergence. They assume real hardware availability throughout the training, which can be resource-intensive and require expert supervision.
- **Offline ADR:** Methods like DROID and DROPO utilize a pre-collected, fixed dataset D from the target domain to infer an optimal source domain distribution. A single policy is then trained using this distribution for direct transfer to the real world. This approach avoids iterative real-world interactions during training, making it suitable for safety-critical tasks or when expert demonstrations are available.

The benchmark includes four specific ADR methods and a baseline:
- **Uniform Domain Randomization (UDR):** This serves as a baseline, where policies are trained on randomly sampled uniform bounds for dynamics parameters. The performance reflects a random search over 10 evaluations.
- **Bayesian Domain Randomization (BayRn) [Online]:** This method optimizes the parameters φ of a uniform source distribution using Bayesian Optimization, with the target domain return as the optimization metric. It is initialized with results from UDR. BayRn does not explicitly use sensor measurements for inference but requires real-world reward computation.
- **SimOpt [Online]:** SimOpt optimizes p_φ(ξ) by minimizing a discrepancy metric between real and simulated trajectories, employing a trust region to stabilize the process. Data is collected iteratively on the real setup with the current policy, then replayed in simulation with closed-loop actions. A variant, **SimOpt-1**, is introduced to investigate performance when all parameter updates occur in a single iteration using the initial policy.
- **DROID [Offline]:** This method leverages expert human demonstrations (offline data) to adapt source domain distributions. It replays real-world actions in simulation to regress dynamics parameters via CMA-ES, minimizing the L2-norm between observations. The original algorithm was adapted for torque-control tasks and does not assume optimal offline data.
- **DROPO [Offline]:** DROPO optimizes p_φ(ξ) using a maximum-likelihood approach based on offline data. CMA-ES is used to regress both means and variances, replaying offline data in simulation. It assumes access to full state configurations from real-world sensor measurements to arbitrarily reset the simulated environment.

The methods are evaluated across four OpenAI Gym environments: Hopper, HalfCheetah, Walker2D, and Humanoid, which progressively increase in difficulty. Each environment is tested under three conditions to simulate different aspects of the reality gap:
- **Vanilla:** A basic condition with only dynamics parameter value mismatches.
- **Noisy:** Injects noise during target domain data collection.
- **Unmodeled:** A subset of dynamics parameters are excluded from optimization and underestimated, simulating unmodeled phenomena.

The benchmark protocol outlines systematic comparison:
- **Data Collection Limits:** Online methods are limited to a maximum of 5 iterations, with a single trajectory (200 transitions) collected per iteration, totaling 1000 transitions (approximately 10 seconds of locomotion data).
- **Offline Data Sharing:** Offline methods are fed the *cumulative* trajectories collected by SimOpt to ensure a consistent type and amount of data for comparison.
- **Parameter Normalization and Priors:** Dynamics parameters are normalized to. All methods start with a conservative prior source distribution (N(2,1)), except BayRn, which uses uniform distributions initialized from UDR.
- **Policy Training:** Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) from `stable-baselines3` is used, with 3-layer MLP neural networks. Policies are trained on 12 parallel environments for up to 5M timesteps, with task-specific reward thresholds for early stopping.
- **Evaluation Metric:** Performance is reported as the average policy return over 3 random seeds (for both inference and policy training), normalized by the training reward threshold.

Additionally, the study includes an investigation into the impact of different data collection strategies on offline ADR performance, comparing data from SimOpt, randomly collected data, and data collected with a policy trained on the initial prior source distribution.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides a comparative analysis of Adaptive Domain Randomization (ADR) methods across various tasks and conditions, highlighting the performance trade-offs between online and offline approaches.

#### 4.1. Vanilla Parameter Estimation

In the basic setting where domains only differ in dynamics parameter values, all methods were observed to successfully transfer for the low-dimensional Hopper task. However, for more complex environments:
- **SimOpt** consistently improved results iteratively, solving tasks within 5 iterations (equivalent to 5 or fewer collected target trajectories). The trust region mechanism in SimOpt was suggested to prevent diverging trajectories during optimization.
- **DROPO** demonstrated strong performance, solving all tasks and matching SimOpt's long-term results, even with a single target trajectory. The ability to reset the simulator state along trajectories was found to be crucial for maintaining informative distance measures and preventing simulation divergence.
- **DROID** generally yielded less stable results, particularly in tasks like Walker2D. Its open-loop replaying of target trajectories in simulation was identified as a source of issues, as diverging simulated trajectories could lead to uninformative discrepancy metrics.
- **SimOpt-1**, which collects all data using an initial, potentially poorly performing policy, failed in the Walker2D task, indicating that the quality of the data collection policy significantly influences performance.
- **BayRn** struggled with higher-dimensional tasks (e.g., beyond 8 dynamics parameters) given the 5-iteration limit, attributed to the scaling limitations of Gaussian Processes in Bayesian Optimization. Results for Humanoid were not reported due to this limitation.

#### 4.2. Observation Noise

When target domain observations included injected noise, the general trends from the vanilla setting largely persisted:
- All methods continued to succeed on the Hopper task.
- **DROID** and **SimOpt-1** still exhibited failures in tasks like Walker2D due to sim trajectory divergence.
- **BayRn** continued to underperform on higher-dimensional dynamics parameter spaces.
- **SimOpt** showed generally lower performances for harder tasks, such as Humanoid, where intermediate policies occasionally failed to transfer effectively under noisy conditions, hindering the iterative process.
- **DROPO** maintained its ability to transfer across all tasks with a single trajectory of noisy data, showing minor improvements with additional data.

#### 4.3. Unmodeled Phenomena

Under conditions where a subset of dynamics parameters were unmodeled and underestimated, the findings included:
- **Offline ADR methods (DROID and DROPO)** behaved similarly to the vanilla setting.
- **SimOpt** and **SimOpt-1** struggled with the Humanoid environment, with intermediate ill-performing policies potentially preventing successful parameter estimation. However, SimOpt achieved stable transfers for the other tasks within 5 trajectories.
- **BayRn** achieved superior performance in the Hopper task compared to other methods, suggesting a benefit from directly optimizing for target domain returns rather than explicit parameter estimation.
- **DROPO** emerged as the overall best-performing method in this setting. However, this came with the requirement for extensive hyperparameter tuning (specifically the regularization value ε) and higher inference times due to Monte-Carlo sampling for likelihood estimation.
- **DROID**, while offering faster objective evaluations and minimal tuning, continued to suffer from divergent trajectories during parameter search on more complex tasks. It also consistently converged to nearly zero-variance distributions, effectively reducing domain randomization to system identification, which limits its ability to generalize across varying dynamics.

#### 4.4. Off-policy Data Collection

The study also investigated how different data collection strategies impact offline ADR performance, comparing random data, data from a policy trained on a prior distribution, and data collected by SimOpt.
- For the Hopper environment, the data collection strategy did not significantly affect performance once sufficient transitions were provided.
- For the Walker2D task, **DROID** was found to be more sensitive to the quality of collected data compared to **DROPO**. This sensitivity was again linked to DROID’s open-loop replaying, where poor data quality could lead to uninformative trajectory discrepancy measures if simulated trajectories diverged significantly. The authors noted that tasks beyond locomotion might impose additional requirements on data collection, such as explicit object interaction for manipulation tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work offers a significant contribution to the field of sim-to-real transfer in robotics by providing a comparative benchmark of Adaptive Domain Randomization (ADR) methods. The detailed analysis sheds light on the performance, data efficiency, and inherent assumptions of both online and offline ADR approaches, addressing a notable gap in existing literature.

The primary impact of this research is the **provision of empirical insights** that can guide researchers and practitioners in selecting appropriate ADR algorithms for their specific robotic applications. The findings clarify the trade-offs involved, such as the data efficiency and safety benefits of offline methods versus the potential for iterative adaptation in online approaches. For instance, the consistent performance of DROPO across various challenging conditions, despite its computational cost, positions it as a robust option when full state information is available and offline data can be processed. Conversely, the identified limitations of DROID regarding open-loop replaying and convergence to point estimates underscore areas for methodological refinement in offline techniques.

The study's emphasis on different reality gap scenarios (vanilla, noisy, unmodeled phenomena) provides a more **realistic assessment** of method robustness. The observation that online methods like SimOpt can be hampered by the quality of intermediate policies, particularly under noise or unmodeled conditions, highlights the need for more robust policy evaluation or safety mechanisms in iterative real-world deployments. This directly impacts the practicality and safety considerations for deploying online ADR in sensitive robotic systems.

Furthermore, by explicitly investigating how different **data collection strategies** affect offline ADR, the paper contributes to understanding the importance of data quality in these methods. The heightened sensitivity of DROID to data quality compared to DROPO suggests that future offline methods could benefit from incorporating mechanisms that mitigate the effects of divergent simulated trajectories or improve the informativeness of trajectory discrepancy metrics, perhaps through advanced state estimation or intermediate resets.

Overall, this benchmark serves as a **foundational reference** for the ADR community. By identifying the strengths and weaknesses of current algorithms, it not only aids in informed decision-making for current applications but also establishes a basis for future research directions. This includes developing more scalable online methods (addressing BayRn's limitations), more robust offline methods that handle noisy and incomplete data more effectively, and computationally more efficient maximum-likelihood approaches. The encouragement for extending this benchmark to other ADR methods and settings fosters a more standardized and comparative research environment, which is crucial for accelerating progress in robust sim-to-real transfer for robotic learning.
