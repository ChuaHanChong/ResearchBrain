# Real-to-Sim Robot Policy Evaluation with Gaussian Splatting Simulation of Soft-Body Interactions

> [!summary] Summary
> A simulation framework for robot policy evaluation combines 3D Gaussian Splatting for photorealistic rendering with physics-informed soft-body digital twins to accurately model deformable object interactions. The approach achieved a strong correlation (Pearson r > 0.9) between simulated and real-world policy performance across various manipulation tasks and policies, addressing the sim-to-real gap.

## Problem

- Evaluating visuomotor robot policies in the real world is costly, time-consuming, and difficult to reproduce, especially for tasks involving deformable objects.
- The "sim-to-real gap" limits the use of simulation for reliable policy evaluation, as policies successful in simulation often fail in physical environments.
- Existing simulators struggle to accurately represent both the photorealistic appearance (e.g., color for robot cameras) and complex dynamics of deformable objects.

## Method

![[2511.04665-method.png]]
*Overview of the real-to-sim framework, which integrates 3D Gaussian Splatting for photorealistic rendering and PhysTwin for accurate soft-body dynamics to evaluate robotic policies.*

- A unified simulation framework was developed, integrating 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) for high-fidelity rendering with physics-informed soft-body digital twins (PhysTwin) for dynamic object modeling.
- High-fidelity digital twins of the robot workspace and objects were created using 3DGS reconstructions from video, and physical parameters for deformable objects were optimized from interaction videos.
- The system implemented positional and color alignment for visual consistency with robot cameras and a custom NVIDIA Warp-based physics engine for accurate soft-body deformation and collision handling.

## Results

- The simulator demonstrated strong correlation between simulated and real-world policy success rates across all evaluated tasks (plush toy packing, rope routing, T-block pushing) and state-of-the-art policies, with Pearson correlation coefficients consistently exceeding 0.9.
- Compared to the NVIDIA IsaacLab baseline, the proposed method yielded substantially higher sim-to-real correlation (e.g., r=0.915 vs. r=0.649 for T-block pushing), particularly for tasks involving deformable objects.
- Ablation studies confirmed that independently removing either color alignment or physics optimization significantly reduced the sim-to-real correlation, validating the importance of both components for reliable policy evaluation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Both high-fidelity appearance (e.g., through color alignment to robot cameras) and accurate dynamics modeling (e.g., optimized material properties) are critical for achieving trustworthy sim-to-real correlation in robot policy evaluation.
> - Different robot policies exhibit varying sensitivities to visual and dynamic discrepancies, underscoring that a comprehensive approach to bridging the sim-to-real gap is essential for robust evaluation.
> - The framework can effectively monitor policy learning dynamics and aid in selecting optimal training checkpoints, demonstrating its utility beyond just final performance evaluation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation is experiencing rapid advancements, particularly with the development of generalist policies and foundation models. However, the evaluation of these policies primarily relies on real-world trials, which are characterized by high costs, significant time investment, and difficulties in reproducibility, especially for tasks involving deformable objects. This reliance has become a notable bottleneck for the scalable development and benchmarking of new robotic systems.

Simulation offers a potential alternative by providing a scalable and systematic environment for data generation, training, and testing. Despite its advantages in these areas, simulation has been less widely adopted for policy evaluation due to a phenomenon known as the "sim-to-real gap." This gap refers to the discrepancy between simulated and real-world performance, where policies successful in simulation often fail to achieve comparable success in physical environments. Bridging this gap is crucial for enabling simulators to serve as reliable proxies for real-world testing, thereby accelerating development cycles.

Existing approaches to robot policy evaluation include real-world benchmarks that offer high fidelity but are limited in flexibility and scalability, and simulation benchmarks that provide flexibility but often compromise on realism in dynamics and rendering. Prior efforts to narrow the sim-to-real gap have explored various techniques:
- **Appearance-focused methods:** Some approaches, like SIMPLER and RobotArena, use green-screen compositing for scene rendering. Ctrl-World relies on 2D video world models. While these improve visual fidelity, they may not capture complex 3D scene geometry or support arbitrary viewpoints effectively.
- **Rigid-body simulation:** Works like Real-is-sim have focused on creating dynamic digital twins primarily for rigid-body interactions, which does not address the challenges posed by deformable objects.
- **Physical Digital Twins:** The reconstruction and simulation of real-world objects, or "digital twins," have been a focus. Many frameworks rely on pre-specified physical parameters, which struggle with the complexity of real-world materials and behaviors, especially for deformable objects. Learning-based approaches exist but can sacrifice physical consistency. Physics-based methods that optimize parameters from video observations offer a more promising direction. PhysTwin, for instance, reconstructs deformable objects as dense spring-mass systems from interaction videos, achieving both realism and efficiency.
- **Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) in Robotics:** 3D Gaussian Splatting has recently emerged as a powerful technique for photorealistic, real-time scene reconstruction and rendering from arbitrary viewpoints. Its application in robotics has shown promise for improving sim-to-real transfer, augmenting training datasets, and enabling real-to-sim evaluation, predominantly for rigid-body scenarios or focusing solely on visual transfer.

This work contributes to the landscape by integrating high-fidelity 3D Gaussian Splatting for photorealistic rendering with physics-informed soft-body digital twins (building upon PhysTwin) for accurate dynamics. This combination aims to comprehensively address both the visual and dynamics aspects of the sim-to-real gap, specifically for robotic manipulation tasks involving deformable objects, a domain where existing simulators often fall short in reliability for policy evaluation. By doing so, the authors propose a framework intended to provide a more trustworthy and scalable simulation environment for assessing robot policies trained on real-world data.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to develop a simulation framework that can reliably predict the real-world performance of visuomotor robot policies, thereby allowing simulation to serve as a trustworthy proxy for real-world testing. This overarching goal is motivated by several critical challenges in current robotics research and development:

1. **Cost and Time of Real-World Evaluation:** Direct evaluation of robotic manipulation policies in the real world is inherently expensive, time-consuming, and difficult to standardize or reproduce. This is particularly true for tasks involving deformable objects, where interactions are complex and success criteria can be nuanced.
2. **Bottleneck for Advanced Policies:** As the robotics community increasingly focuses on developing foundation models and generalist policies, which require rapid iteration and large-scale benchmarking, the limitations of real-world evaluation become a significant bottleneck, hindering progress and efficient development.
3. **Limitations of Existing Simulators (Sim-to-Real Gap):** While simulation offers scalability, existing simulators often exhibit a substantial "sim-to-real gap." This gap manifests in two primary forms:
 - **Appearance Mismatch:** Policies, especially those relying on visual feedback from wrist-mounted cameras, require rendered scenes to closely match real-world observations. Traditional rendering techniques or simple compositing often fail to achieve the necessary photorealistic fidelity, leading to discrepancies in policy perception.
 - **Dynamics Discrepancy:** Simulators frequently struggle to accurately capture the physical complexity of real-world interactions, particularly for deformable objects with many degrees of freedom. Reliance on low-dimensional parameter tuning is often insufficient to model these behaviors faithfully.
4. **Need for High-Fidelity Reconstruction:** To confidently evaluate policies in simulation, the simulator must faithfully reproduce both the visual characteristics and the physical dynamics of the real world. This necessitates sophisticated methods for reconstructing digital twins of robots, objects, and environments.

To address these motivations, the authors propose a framework with the following key objectives:
- **Develop a unified simulator:** Integrate high-fidelity rendering (using 3D Gaussian Splatting) with physics-informed dynamics modeling (using soft-body digital twins derived from interaction videos) into a single evaluation platform.
- **Validate sim-to-real correlation:** Empirically demonstrate that simulated policy rollouts exhibit a strong correlation with real-world execution performance across representative deformable and rigid-body manipulation tasks and various state-of-the-art policies.
- **Identify critical design factors:** Analyze the impact of specific design choices, such as rendering fidelity (e.g., color alignment) and dynamics accuracy (e.g., physics optimization), on the reliability of simulation-based policy evaluation.
- **Enable scalable and reproducible evaluation:** Provide a framework that allows for efficient, reproducible, and scalable assessment of robot policies, freeing developers from the constraints of solely real-world testing.

Ultimately, the motivation is to answer the central question: "how can we design simulators that are sufficiently realistic to evaluate robot policies with confidence?" by investigating whether a framework combining physics-informed reconstruction and high-quality rendering can reliably predict real-world policy performance.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology for real-to-sim policy evaluation involves constructing high-fidelity digital twins of manipulation environments and objects, integrating them into a simulation platform, and evaluating policies trained on real-world data. The approach focuses on bridging both the visual and dynamics gaps between simulation and reality.

#### 3.1. A. Problem Definition and Components

The authors define policy evaluation as determining if a simulator can reliably predict the real-world performance of visuomotor policies. This requires two core components:
1. **Dynamics Model:** $s_{t+1} = f(s_t, a_t)$, which predicts future environment states given current states and robot actions.
2. **Appearance Model:** $o_t = g(s_t)$, which renders observations (e.g., RGB images) in the modality required by the policy.
The fidelity of the simulation is thus assessed based on the accuracy of its dynamics and the realism of its rendered observations.

#### 3.2. B. Preliminary: PhysTwin

The framework adopts **PhysTwin** for reconstructing and simulating deformable and rigid objects.
- **Representation:** Objects are modeled as dense spring-mass systems, where mass nodes are connected by springs within a distance threshold $d$. Node positions evolve based on Newtonian dynamics.
- **System Identification:** To capture diverse material properties, PhysTwin optimizes physical parameters (spring threshold $d$ and per-spring stiffness coefficients $Y$) from a single video of human-object interaction. Human hand keypoints are tracked and used as kinematic control points, and parameters are tuned to minimize the discrepancy between tracked and simulated object motions. For rigid bodies, $Y$ is set to a large value. This real-to-sim process is applied to objects interacting with the robot (plush toy, rope, T-block).

#### 3.3. C. Real-to-Sim Gaussian Splatting Simulation

The core simulation construction integrates 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) for rendering with PhysTwin for dynamics.

1. **GS Construction:**
 - **Scene Capture:** An iPhone app (Scaniverse) is used to generate 3DGS reconstructions from video recordings. The static robot workspace (robot, table, background) is scanned first, followed by individual experimental objects.
 - **Segmentation:** The resulting 3DGS reconstructions are segmented into robot, objects, and background using the SuperSplat visualizer. This reconstruction step is performed once per task.

2. **Positional Alignment:**
 - **Reference Frames:** All reconstructed components (static background, robot, PhysTwin objects, other static objects) are aligned to the robot base frame and canonical object frames.
 - **Object Alignment:** PhysTwin objects and static meshes are aligned to their corresponding PhysTwin particle sets and 3D models via a 6-Degrees-of-Freedom (6-DoF) transformation.
 - **Robot Alignment:** The transformation between the reconstructed robot 3DGS model and ground truth robot points (generated from its URDF model) is automatically computed using Iterative Closest Point (ICP) combined with RANSAC. This transformation is also applied to the background GS, as it is captured in the same frame as the robot GS.
 - **Link Segmentation:** To enable simulation of continuous robot motion, each Gaussian kernel is associated with its corresponding robot link (including gripper links) by finding its nearest neighbor in the sampled robot point cloud and inheriting that point's link index.

3. **Color Alignment:**
 - **Problem:** GS reconstructions inherit the color characteristics of the iPhone camera, which often differ from the robot's cameras (e.g., Intel RealSense, known for color shifts). This mismatch can impact policies.
 - **Solution:** A color transformation is optimized to align GS colors to the robot camera domain.
 - GS images are rendered from the viewpoints of the robot's fixed cameras using original GS colors.
 - Real images are captured from the same viewpoints, creating paired data.
 - A polynomial transformation (degree $d=2$ empirically found optimal) is solved via Iteratively Reweighted Least Squares (IRLS) to minimize pixel-wise color discrepancy between GS renderings and real camera captures.

4. **Physics and Deformation:**
 - **Physics Engine:** A custom physics engine built on NVIDIA Warp is used, extending the PhysTwin spring-mass simulator.
 - **Collision Handling:** The engine supports collisions between robot end-effectors and objects in the environment.
 - **Grasping Model:** Instead of fixing object nodes, grasping soft-body digital twins is modeled through frictional interactions between gripper fingers and the object. Gripper closing halts automatically when a specified total collision-force threshold is met, aiming for realistic and stable grasps.
 - **Gaussian Kernel Update:** At each simulation step, updated robot and environment states from the physics engine are propagated to the Gaussian kernels:
 - **Rigid bodies:** Kernel positions and orientations are updated using corresponding rigid-body transformations.
 - **Deformable objects:** Linear Blend Skinning (LBS) is applied to transform each kernel based on the underlying soft-body deformation computed by PhysTwin.
 - **Performance:** The simulator runs at 5-30 FPS on a single GPU, achieving several times faster evaluation speeds than real-world execution due to the elimination of real-world environment resets and leveraging multi-GPU parallelization.

#### 3.4. D. Policy Evaluation

- **Training:** Policies are trained exclusively on real-world demonstrations collected via human teleoperation using GELLO.
- **Simulation Environment:** The entire system is exposed through a Gym-style interface for easy integration.
- **Evaluation Protocol:** To ensure consistency and reduce variance, a fixed set of initial object configurations is used for evaluation in both simulation and the real world. A real-time visualization tool aids human operators in accurately replicating these simulated initial states in the real world.
- **Performance Metrics:** Policy performance ($u$) is measured by binary task success rates. Success is human-evaluated in the real world and automatically computed from privileged simulation states in the simulator.
- **Policies Evaluated:** Four state-of-the-art imitation learning algorithms are tested: ACT, Diffusion Policy (DP), SmolVLA, and Pi-0, including checkpoints from different training stages.
- **Hardware:** A UFactory xArm 7 robot with two Intel RealSense RGB-D cameras (wrist-mounted D405, fixed D455) is used.
- **Correlation Metrics:** Alignment between simulation and real-world performance is quantified using the Mean Maximum Rank Variation (MMRV) and the Pearson correlation coefficient ($r$), along with Clopper–Pearson confidence intervals and Bayesian posterior violin plots to account for uncertainty.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research investigated the correlation between simulated and real-world policy performance using the proposed framework, conducted ablation studies on key components, and provided analysis of policy learning dynamics.

#### 4.1. A. Sim-and-Real Correlation

- **Strong Correlation:** The simulation success rates correlated strongly with real-world success rates across all evaluated policies (ACT, DP, SmolVLA, Pi-0) and tasks (plush toy packing, rope routing, T-block pushing). Pearson correlation coefficients ($r$) consistently exceeded 0.9 for all tasks (Toy packing: $r=0.944$, Rope routing: $r=0.901$, T-block pushing: $r=0.915$). This indicates that policies performing better in the real world also showed improved performance in the proposed simulator.
- **Superiority over Baseline:** When compared to the NVIDIA IsaacLab baseline, the proposed simulator yielded substantially stronger sim-to-real correlation. For instance, on the T-block pushing task, their method achieved $r=0.915$ compared to IsaacLab's $r=0.649$. For rope routing, their method achieved $r=0.901$ while IsaacLab yielded $r=0.237$. IsaacLab's limitations in stably simulating deformable objects (like the plush toy) led to its exclusion from quantitative comparison on the toy packing task. The Mean Maximum Rank Variation (MMRV) values for their method were also consistently lower than the baseline, indicating more accurate ranking of policy performance.

#### 4.2. B. Policy Performance Analysis

- **Consistent Trends:** The research observed that simulated success rates generally followed the same progression as real-world success rates across various training iterations for individual policy-task pairs. For example, both simulated and real success rates for toy packing with Diffusion Policy peaked at iteration 5,000 and declined at 7,000.
- **Practical Utility:** These consistent trends suggest that the simulator can serve as a tool for monitoring policy learning dynamics, selecting optimal checkpoints for real-world deployment, and setting approximate expectations for real-world performance.
- **Remaining Discrepancies:** While trends were consistent, some absolute success rates between simulation and reality did not perfectly overlap (e.g., toy packing-SmolVLA, rope routing-ACT). These discrepancies are attributed to residual gaps in visual appearance and dynamics, as well as statistical variance from the limited number of evaluation episodes (16–27 per checkpoint).

#### 4.3. C. Ablation Study

Ablation studies were conducted to assess the importance of rendering and dynamics fidelity:

- **Impact of Color Alignment (Ours w/o color alignment):** Skipping the color alignment step, which resulted in color mismatches between GS renderings and robot camera observations, led to a decrease in correlation. For toy packing, $r$ dropped from 0.944 to 0.805, and for T-block pushing, it fell from 0.915 to 0.529. Visually, rendered images showed noticeable color shifts.
- **Impact of Physics Optimization (Ours w/o physics optimization):** Using a global stiffness value instead of fully optimized per-spring stiffness (for deformable objects) or altering friction coefficients (for rigid objects like the T-block) resulted in inaccurate object dynamics. This also led to a significant drop in correlation. For toy packing, $r$ decreased from 0.944 to 0.694, and for rope routing, from 0.901 to 0.832. Qualitatively, this led to failures such as toy limbs not fitting or the rope slipping.
- **Conclusion on Fidelity:** Both dynamics and appearance fidelity were found to be critical for the trustworthiness of simulation-based policy evaluation. Policies exhibited varying sensitivities to each type of gap, but both contributed significantly to deviations between simulated and real policy rollouts. The full method consistently achieved the highest correlation values and lowest MMRV.

#### 4.4. D. Additional Results (from Appendix)

- **Scaling Up Evaluation:** Increasing the number of simulated evaluation episodes (to 200 randomized initial states per policy) narrowed the Clopper–Pearson confidence intervals, confirming that correlation estimates stabilize with more trials. The strong correlation ($r > 0.897$) persisted, demonstrating robustness under larger-scale evaluation.
- **Replaying Real Rollouts:** Replaying real-world policy rollouts in the simulator using identical control commands showed high agreement between replayed and real outcomes. Confusion matrices for per-episode success/failure indicated strong diagonal dominance (e.g., for toy packing: 106 true positives, 132 true negatives, 37 false positives, 25 false negatives).
 - Some discrepancies were noted: the simulator tended to slightly overestimate success in toy packing (more false positives), possibly due to simplified contact/friction models. For T-block pushing, some real successes were not reproduced in simulation (more false negatives), potentially due to subtle mismatches in friction or initial states. Overall, the ability to reproduce real rollouts open-loop confirmed the simulator's fidelity in dynamics.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presents a significant contribution to the field of robotics by proposing a framework for robot policy evaluation that addresses the long-standing sim-to-real gap, particularly for tasks involving deformable objects. The key significance and potential impact of this work include:

1. **Enabling Scalable and Reproducible Policy Evaluation:** The framework offers a means to evaluate robot manipulation policies that is scalable, reproducible, and less resource-intensive compared to relying solely on real-world trials. This is crucial for the rapid iteration and benchmarking required by modern robotics research, especially with the emergence of foundation models and generalist robot policies.
2. **Accelerating Robotics Development:** By providing a simulation environment that can reliably predict real-world policy performance (as evidenced by strong correlation coefficients), the framework can accelerate the development cycle of new robotic manipulation policies. Researchers and developers can gain confidence in their policies' real-world applicability earlier in the development process, reducing the need for costly and time-consuming physical experiments.
3. **Advancing Deformable Object Manipulation:** The integration of physics-informed soft-body digital twins is a notable advancement for tasks involving deformable objects, a domain where traditional simulators often lack fidelity. This allows for more realistic simulation of complex interactions, such as grasping and manipulating plush toys or routing ropes, which are challenging to model accurately.
4. **Guiding Simulator Design:** The detailed ablation studies provide empirical evidence highlighting the critical importance of both high-fidelity rendering (specifically, color alignment to match robot camera perception) and accurate dynamics modeling (physics optimization of material properties). These findings offer concrete guidance for the design and construction of future evaluation simulators, emphasizing that both visual and physical realism are indispensable for achieving trustworthy sim-to-real correlation.
5. **Foundation for Future Research:** The framework, packaged with a Gym-style interface and planned open-sourcing, can serve as a foundation for further research. It facilitates systematic studies into policy learning dynamics, enables more robust comparisons between different algorithms, and supports generalization to a wider array of manipulation tasks and environments.
6. **Reduced Barrier to Entry:** By offering a more accessible and efficient evaluation platform, the work can potentially lower the barrier to entry for researchers and practitioners in robotics, allowing them to experiment with and validate complex manipulation policies without extensive real-world hardware setups.

In conclusion, this research provides a validated approach to construct high-fidelity simulation environments that bridge the appearance and dynamics gaps between simulation and reality. Its demonstrated ability to predict real-world policy performance with high confidence positions it as a valuable tool for the advancement of robotic manipulation, particularly in tasks involving deformable objects.
