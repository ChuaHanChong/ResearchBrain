# MVID: Feed-Forward Multi-View Intrinsic Image Decomposition

> [!summary] Summary
> MVID is a feed-forward framework that decomposes sparse multi-view RGB inputs into a view-consistent albedo plus per-view diffuse shading and a non-diffuse residual under the residual image formation model. A multi-view transformer builds shared scene tokens, and learned per-factor read-write queries route evidence into dense heads, raising Hypersim albedo PSNR to 24.72 and beating all baselines on cross-view consistency.

## Problem

- Single-image residual-model methods decompose each frame without scene-level context, so albedo drifts in color across views and shadows leak between factors.
- Multi-view inverse-rendering methods estimate BRDF, lighting and geometry, but uncertain parameter estimates block self-supervised RGB reconstruction and force reliance on synthetic training data.
- Generative diffusion intrinsic priors hallucinate sharp but unfaithful detail in weakly textured or shadowed regions, and stay unstable when applied independently across viewpoints.

## Method

- A transformer ==multi-view aggregator== tokenizes each view and alternates frame-wise and ==cross-view attention==, producing a hierarchy of shared cross-view tokens instead of any explicit 3D representation.
- A ==multi-layer factor query adapter== uses eight learned queries per factor to read multi-view evidence and write it back into dense tokens via ==gated cross-attention==, cutting cross-factor leakage.
- Training mixes synthetic intrinsic supervision with ==image-formation reconstruction== on unlabeled real sequences, plus warped ==cross-view albedo consistency==, a ==residual regularizer== against degenerate factorizations, and auxiliary depth and normal supervision.

## Results

- On Hypersim MVID reaches **24.72** albedo PSNR and **20.11** shading PSNR, above Colorful Diffuse at **18.29** and RGB-X at **20.80**, running **0.19** seconds per frame.
- Cross-view albedo consistency hits **26.68** PSNR on Hypersim and **25.14** on real scenes, versus Colorful Diffuse at **25.22** and **24.15** and IDArb at **18.17**.
- Ablations cut albedo PSNR to **22.85** without the factor query adapter and **22.80** without the image-formation constraint; real-world consistency falls to **22.70** without real training data.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Multi-view aggregation resolves factor-assignment ambiguity no single-view prior can: stable albedo needs evidence that a surface looks identical from several viewpoints.
> - Image-domain factorization stays self-supervisable on unlabeled real captures, whereas physically parameterized BRDF outputs cannot reliably close the RGB reconstruction loop.
> - Sharpness and faithfulness trade off: generative priors win perceptual metrics while inventing detail, so multi-view methods can look over-smoothed in under-constrained regions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

Intrinsic image decomposition (IID) is a long-standing computer vision problem that aims to disentangle an image into its underlying scene properties, primarily stable surface appearance (albedo or reflectance) and illumination-dependent effects (shading). Traditionally, IID has been formulated as separating an image ($I$) into albedo ($A$) and shading ($S$) components, often assuming a Lambertian reflectance model and grayscale illumination ($I = A \odot S_{\text{gray}}$). However, real-world images present complexities such as colored illumination, inter-reflections, specularity, and cast shadows, which are not adequately explained by this simplistic model.

Recent advancements in single-image IID have introduced the residual image formation model ($I = A \odot S + R$), where $A$ represents stable albedo, $S$ captures RGB diffuse illumination and shadows, and $R$ accounts for remaining non-diffuse or view-dependent appearance (e.g., specularities). This formulation addresses non-Lambertian effects without requiring explicit estimation of BRDFs, lighting, visibility, or geometry. Despite these improvements, applying single-image methods independently to sequences of images often leads to inconsistencies across views, such as albedo color drift, shadow leakage into albedo, and unstable factor assignments. This occurs because single-view methods lack the scene-level context necessary to distinguish between stable material properties and view-dependent phenomena.

Concurrently, multi-view inverse rendering methods have emerged, aiming to recover more comprehensive physical scene parameters like geometry, materials (e.g., SVBRDF), and lighting from multiple input views. While these methods benefit from multi-view constraints, they typically rely on synthetic data for supervision due to the high uncertainty in estimating physical parameters from real-world observations. They also often involve per-scene optimization, which can be computationally intensive and limit their applicability to real-time or large-scale scenarios. Some feed-forward multi-view methods exist (e.g., IDArb, MAIR/MAIR++), but they often target parameter-space outputs suitable for physically based rendering, and their self-supervised RGB reconstruction can be hindered by uncertain geometry and lighting estimates.

MVID positions itself at the intersection of these two areas. It addresses the limitations of single-view IID by incorporating multi-view context to enhance consistency, and it differentiates itself from multi-view inverse rendering by focusing on image-domain factors ($A$, $S$, $R$) under the residual image formation model rather than full physical scene parameter recovery. This choice allows MVID to leverage self-supervised RGB reconstruction on unlabeled real-world multi-view sequences, making it more adaptable to "in-the-wild" data. By employing a feed-forward architecture, MVID also avoids the computational overhead of per-scene optimization inherent in many multi-view inverse rendering techniques, offering a scalable solution for consistent intrinsic decomposition across multiple views. The work also builds upon recent advancements in multi-view scene representation using transformer architectures to aggregate cross-view information without explicit 3D models.

### 2. Key objectives and motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a feed-forward framework capable of performing multi-view intrinsic image decomposition with high accuracy and cross-view consistency. The authors are motivated by several challenges and limitations observed in existing intrinsic image decomposition methods:

1. **Ambiguity in Single-View Decomposition:** Real-world images are complex, with surface color, illumination, visibility, cast shadows, and non-diffuse effects all contributing to the observed pixel values. This inherent ambiguity makes it difficult for single-image methods to reliably separate stable material properties (albedo) from transient illumination and view-dependent effects (shading and residual).
2. **Inconsistency across Views with Single-View Methods:** When single-view intrinsic decomposition methods are applied independently to consecutive frames or multiple views of the same scene, they often produce inconsistent results. This manifests as "albedo drift" (where the color of a material appears to change across views), and "factor leakage" (where properties that should belong to shading or residual components are incorrectly assigned to albedo, or vice-versa). This inconsistency limits their utility in applications requiring stable scene understanding or coherent editing across viewpoints.
3. **Limitations of Multi-View Inverse Rendering:** While multi-view observations offer valuable constraints, existing multi-view inverse rendering methods typically focus on recovering physical scene parameters (e.g., BRDFs, detailed lighting, geometry). This often requires extensive supervision from synthetic data, and their ability to generalize to real-world captures with unknown and complex physical properties is limited due to the high uncertainty in parameter estimation. Furthermore, many such methods rely on per-scene optimization, making them unsuitable for real-time or large-scale applications.
4. **Need for Robust Real-World Supervision:** Training intrinsic decomposition models effectively requires large datasets. While synthetic datasets offer ground truth for intrinsic factors, models trained solely on synthetic data may not generalize well to the complexities of real-world captures. There is a need for methods that can leverage readily available unlabeled real-world multi-view sequences for training.

To address these motivations, MVID aims to:
- Introduce a feed-forward framework that processes multiple views simultaneously to generate consistent intrinsic factor predictions.
- Adopt the residual image formation model ($I = A \odot S + R$) to robustly explain complex real-world appearance, beyond simple diffuse-only models.
- Leverage a scene-level multi-view representation to provide context, allowing the model to distinguish view-stable material properties from view-dependent effects more reliably.
- Develop a mechanism (multi-layer factor query adapter) to selectively extract information for different intrinsic factors from the shared representation, thereby reducing cross-factor leakage.
- Incorporate a training strategy that combines supervision from synthetic intrinsic datasets with self-supervised RGB reconstruction on unlabeled real-world multi-view sequences.
- Produce view-stable factors that enable practical image-space applications, specifically demonstrating multi-view consistent illumination editing and specularity removal.

### 3. Methodology and approach

MVID is a feed-forward framework designed to decompose sparse overlapping views of a static scene into per-view albedo ($\hat{A}_i$), diffuse shading ($\hat{S}_i$), and a non-diffuse residual ($\hat{R}_i$) using the residual image formation model: $I_i \approx \hat{A}_i \odot \hat{S}_i + \hat{R}_i$. The pipeline comprises three main modules: a multi-view aggregator, a multi-layer factor query adapter, and per-factor dense prediction heads.

#### 3.1. Shared Multi-View Representation

The initial step involves creating a scene-level context representation from the input views. MVID utilizes a transformer-based multi-view aggregator, which processes each input image by tokenizing it into patch embeddings. This aggregator employs alternating frame-wise and cross-view attention mechanisms to fuse information across all overlapping views. The output is a hierarchical representation of dense token features, $\{T_l\}$, where $l$ denotes the aggregation layer. This representation implicitly captures scene-level geometry and appearance cues without explicitly constructing a 3D model. The design ensures that all subsequent per-view predictions are derived from this shared representation, promoting implicit consistency across views.

#### 3.2. Multi-Layer Factor Query Adapter

To prevent factor leakage and ensure that each intrinsic component (albedo, shading, residual) draws relevant, specific cues from the shared multi-view representation, MVID introduces a multi-layer factor query adapter. This adapter functions as a read–write mechanism with learned per-factor probes (queries).
- **Read Operation:** For each factor $k \in \{A, S, R\}$, a set of learned queries, $Q_k \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times C}$ (where $M=8$ is the number of queries and $C$ is the channel dimension), performs cross-attention with the flattened multi-view tokens, $\bar{T}_l \in \mathbb{R}^{(V P) \times C}$ (where $V$ is views, $P$ is patches). This step aggregates factor-specific evidence from the entire multi-view context.
- **Write Operation:** The resulting factor summaries, $Z_l^k$, then write back into the dense patch tokens $T_l$. This is achieved through another cross-attention mechanism where the patch tokens $T_l$ act as queries, and $Z_l^k$ provide the keys and values. A learnable scalar gate $\alpha_l^k$ controls the strength of this factor-specific modulation. This process generates factor-conditioned token hierarchies, $\{\tilde{T}_l^k\}$, tailored for each intrinsic component. The adapter layers are strategically placed at feature scales consumed by the dense prediction heads.

#### 3.3. Per-Factor Dense Prediction

Following the adaptation by the factor query adapter, dedicated DPT-style dense heads decode each factor-conditioned token hierarchy into high-resolution predictions:
- $\hat{A}_i = \text{H}_A(\{\tilde{T}_l^A\})$ for albedo.
- $\hat{S}_i = \text{H}_S(\{\tilde{T}_l^S\})$ for diffuse shading.
- $\hat{R}_i = \text{H}_R(\{\tilde{T}_l^R\})$ for non-diffuse residual.
The albedo head incorporates an additional local refinement mechanism: it fuses albedo tokens with multi-scale CNN features through gated aggregation. This step enhances high-frequency texture details while maintaining cross-view consistency, which is primarily handled by the multi-view tokens.
Optionally, MVID can also predict auxiliary geometry outputs (depth and surface normals) from the shared representation. These auxiliary signals serve as structural regularization during training when ground truth is available.

#### 3.4. Training Objective

MVID is trained using a comprehensive objective function that combines supervision from synthetic data with intrinsic ground truth and self-supervision on unlabeled real-world RGB sequences. The total loss $\mathcal{L}$ consists of several terms:
- **Factor-level supervision ($\mathcal{L}_{\text{intr}}$):** An L1 loss applied directly to predicted albedo, shading, and residual against their ground truth values on synthetic datasets.
- **Image formation constraint ($\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}}$):** An L1 loss comparing the reconstructed RGB image ($\hat{A}_i \odot \hat{S}_i + \hat{R}_i$) with the input RGB image ($I_i$). This is the primary supervision signal for unlabeled real-world data.
- **Cross-view consistency ($\mathcal{L}_{\text{cv}}$):** An L1 loss enforcing consistency of predicted albedo across overlapping views by warping one view's albedo to another. This term is applied only when reliable geometry (depth and camera poses) is available.
- **Adapter and auxiliary regularization ($\mathcal{L}_{\text{ada}}$ and $\mathcal{L}_{\text{aux}}$):** $\mathcal{L}_{\text{ada}}$ includes a diversity term for factor queries and L2/floor terms for the learnable gates, encouraging distinct factor specialization. $\mathcal{L}_{\text{aux}}$ supervises predicted depth and normals against ground truth when available, providing structural cues.
- **Residual regularizer ($\mathcal{L}_R$):** Penalizes large, low-frequency residuals to prevent the residual component from absorbing diffuse appearance, ensuring it captures primarily localized non-diffuse effects.

This multi-faceted training objective enables MVID to learn robust and consistent intrinsic decompositions across diverse datasets, including "in-the-wild" scenarios where intrinsic annotations are absent.

### 4. Main findings and results

The experiments conducted by the authors demonstrate MVID's performance across various metrics and applications, particularly highlighting its strengths in per-frame decomposition quality, cross-view consistency, and practical utility.

#### 4.1. Per-Frame Decomposition Quality

On the Hypersim test split (synthetic indoor scenes with ground truth), MVID achieved the best results for albedo, shading, residual decomposition, and RGB reconstruction metrics (PSNR and LPIPS). It outperformed several single-view intrinsic methods (MGNet, Colorful Diffuse, RGB-X, DNF-Intrinsic) and multi-view inverse-rendering baselines (IDArb, MAIR, MAIR++). Specifically, MVID recorded 24.72 PSNR for albedo, 20.11 PSNR for shading, 25.80 PSNR for residual, and 25.58 PSNR for reconstruction. Even MVID-Single (MVID applied to each view independently) showed improvements over many baselines, indicating the robustness of the core architecture, with the full multi-view MVID further enhancing these results through scene-level context. Qualitatively, MVID produced cleaner albedo with reduced shadow leakage and more structured diffuse shading compared to baselines. The auxiliary geometry predictions (depth and normals) also remained competitive, with MVID ranking favorably among comparable methods.

#### 4.2. Cross-View Consistency

MVID demonstrated superior cross-view albedo consistency compared to all baselines on both synthetic (Hypersim) and real-world multi-view sequences (Mip-NeRF 360, Deep Blending, Tanks & Temples). Measured by A-CV PSNR, MAE, and LPIPS, MVID achieved the highest consistency scores (e.g., 26.68 A-CV PSNR on Hypersim and 25.14 A-CV PSNR on real-world data). Qualitative analyses showed that MVID effectively mitigated albedo drift and minimized warp errors across views, where single-view methods often exhibited significant inconsistencies, shadow leakage, and color shifts. In real-world video sequences, MVID maintained stable albedo across varying viewpoints, while single-view methods produced view-dependent albedo and diffusion-prior methods sometimes generated plausible but incorrect details.

#### 4.3. Sharpness vs. Faithfulness

The study highlighted a trade-off between perceived sharpness and faithfulness to underlying scene materials, particularly in ambiguous or weakly textured regions. Some single-view generative diffusion methods, while achieving competitive LPIPS scores (indicating perceptual quality), sometimes produced visually sharp details that deviated from ground truth. MVID, by contrast, leveraged multi-view evidence to recover more faithful and cross-view stable intrinsics, even if this resulted in slightly smoother output in under-constrained regions.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies

- **Multi-view evidence:** The comparison between MVID and MVID-Single confirmed that multi-view aggregation significantly improves albedo, shading, and cross-view consistency. Performance monotonically increased with more input views, saturating around the default 5-view setting.
- **Factor query adapter:** Removing the multi-layer factor query adapter degraded both per-factor accuracy and cross-view consistency, underscoring its role in promoting factor-specific information extraction and reducing leakage. The adapter queries exhibited emergent specialization, with albedo queries focusing on material/color and shading/residual queries on shadows/specularities.
- **Cross-view consistency loss ($\mathcal{L}_{\text{cv}}$):** This explicit loss term was crucial for achieving strong cross-view albedo consistency, demonstrating its complementary role to the implicit coupling from the shared representation.
- **Image formation constraint ($\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}}$):** Removing this constraint led to the largest drop in per-factor accuracy and reduced cross-view consistency, affirming its fundamental role as a reconstruction anchor, especially for unlabeled real-world data.
- **Real-world RGB training:** Training with unlabeled real-world data significantly improved generalization to "in-the-wild" scenarios, leading to cleaner albedo with less shadow leakage.
- **Auxiliary geometry supervision:** While primarily auxiliary, supervision for depth and normals moderately improved diffuse shading by providing structural regularization.
- **Local refinement for albedo:** The gated CNN branch in the albedo head effectively recovered high-frequency texture details, improving albedo PSNR without compromising cross-view consistency.
- **Residual regularizer ($\mathcal{L}_R$):** This regularizer was found to be necessary to prevent the residual from absorbing diffuse appearance, ensuring it remained small and localized to non-diffuse effects.

#### 4.5. Downstream Applications

MVID's stable and consistent intrinsic factors were shown to support practical image-space applications:
- **Multi-view consistent illumination editing:** By modifying the predicted diffuse shading and residual components while keeping albedo fixed, users could adjust illumination appearance, color temperature, and shading direction. The multi-view consistency of MVID's factors ensured that these edits propagated coherently across different viewpoints without artifacts.
- **Specularity removal:** The diffuse image, obtained by $\hat{A} \odot \hat{S}$, effectively provided a specularity-free version of the input, demonstrating MVID's ability to suppress highlights while preserving underlying surface texture consistently across views.

### 5. Significance and potential impact

MVID represents a notable advancement in the field of intrinsic image decomposition, particularly for multi-view scenarios. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Enhanced Consistency in Intrinsic Decomposition:** By leveraging multi-view context, MVID effectively addresses the long-standing challenge of cross-view inconsistency and albedo drift in intrinsic image decomposition. This consistency is critical for applications requiring stable scene understanding across different viewpoints or over time in video sequences.
2. **Scalable and Efficient Feed-Forward Approach:** Unlike many multi-view inverse rendering methods that rely on per-scene optimization, MVID's feed-forward architecture makes it significantly faster and more scalable. This efficiency enables its use in real-time applications, large-scale data processing, or scenarios where rapid decomposition is required, which was previously a bottleneck.
3. **Robustness to Real-World Unlabeled Data:** The novel training strategy, combining intrinsic supervision from synthetic data with self-supervised RGB reconstruction on unlabeled real-world multi-view sequences, allows MVID to generalize more effectively to "in-the-wild" captures. This broadens the applicability of intrinsic decomposition to diverse real-world environments without the need for costly manual annotations.
4. **Practical Applications in Image Editing and Graphics:** The resulting view-consistent albedo, shading, and residual factors directly enable practical image-space applications. Multi-view consistent illumination editing and specularity removal are significant for creative content generation, virtual production, photography post-processing, and augmented reality, where stable visual manipulation across different views is paramount.
5. **Architectural Innovations:** The introduction of the multi-layer factor query adapter is an architectural contribution that effectively routes factor-specific information from a shared multi-view representation. This mechanism contributes to better disentanglement of intrinsic factors by reducing cross-factor leakage, thereby improving the quality and interpretability of the decomposition.
6. **Foundation for Future Research:** MVID lays a foundation for future research in several directions. It encourages further exploration into dynamic scenes, more sophisticated physical factorization of the residual component (e.g., separating specularity, transparency), and tighter integration with explicit 3D representations for joint view synthesis and relighting. Overcoming current limitations like distortion of thin structures and generalization to highly challenging materials (dark, transparent, highly reflective) could further expand its impact.

In summary, MVID pushes the boundaries of intrinsic image decomposition by providing a fast, consistent, and practical solution that effectively bridges the gap between single-view realism and multi-view stability, opening new possibilities for scene understanding and image manipulation.
