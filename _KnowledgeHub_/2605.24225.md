# ECo-MoE: Embodiment-Conditioned Mixture of Experts Increases the Evolvability of Robots

> [!summary] Summary
> Northwestern University researchers introduced ECo-MoE, a control framework that co-optimizes robot morphology and control by using a mixture of experts whose activation is conditioned on the robot's latent design. This approach led to increased robot evolvability in challenging simulated environments like upright locomotion and potholes, and facilitated directing evolution towards specific morphological characteristics.

## Problem

- Co-optimization of high-resolution robot morphology and adaptable controllers presents significant computational challenges.
- Existing methods either incur high computational costs by training individual policies for each robot or lead to conservative, generalized designs when using monolithic universal controllers.
- Simpler morphological representations used in some evolutionary robotics studies limit their relevance to complex physical robots.

## Method

- An Embodiment-Conditioned Mixture of Experts (**ECo-MoE**) framework is introduced, where multiple control experts are dynamically weighted by a gating network conditioned on the robot's latent genotype.
- The system co-optimizes the latent design genome (which decodes to high-definition morphology) and the ECo-MoE controller using PPO for behavior and CMA-ES for design fitness.
- "Evo by Demo" mechanisms, including predesigned latent priors and the injection of pretrained experts, are used to guide evolution towards specific morphological traits.

## Results

- ECo-MoE demonstrated increased robot evolvability in challenging environments like upright locomotion and potholes, achieving higher peak fitness compared to a monolithic universal controller baseline.
- The framework exhibited parameter efficiency, achieving performance gains with a comparable or slightly smaller total policy size and fewer parameters per individual expert.
- "Evo by Demo" settings, particularly combining predesigned latent priors and frozen pretrained experts (CoSteering), effectively guided evolution towards specific predesigned morphological traits and achieved significantly higher fitness.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Modularity in the control architecture, conditioned on robot embodiment, can increase robot evolvability without necessarily broadening the morphological search space, primarily through more effective control of encountered designs.
> - A routing diversity regularization term is essential to prevent expert collapse and ensure diverse utilization of the specialized control modules within the Mixture of Experts.
> - Injecting prior knowledge through pretrained experts or predesigned latent space initializations (Evo by Demo) provides an effective method to steer freeform morphological evolution toward desired structures.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics and artificial intelligence is actively exploring methods to automatically design and control robots. A central challenge in this domain involves the co-optimization of robot morphology (body plan) and its corresponding controller (behavioral policy). Traditional approaches to this problem face computational complexities, particularly when dealing with high-resolution, complex morphologies and the need for adaptable controllers.

Previous research has explored several avenues:
- **Simplified Morphology:** To make evolutionary processes tractable, some studies have reduced morphological resolution, limiting robots to 2D tilings or rigid stick figures (e.g., Bhatia et al., 2021; Strgar et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2019; Gupta et al., 2021; Yu et al., 2026). While these simplify computation, they reduce relevance to complex physical robots.
- **High-Resolution Morphology without Learning:** Earlier work utilized higher-resolution voxels (e.g., 10³ or 27³ voxels by Cheney et al., 2013, and Kriegman et al., 2021b, respectively) but removed the learning component, relying solely on evolution to position open-loop actuators. This limits complex, adaptive behaviors.
- **Joint Co-optimization via Gradient Information:** Methods like those by Pathak et al. (2019) and Matthews et al. (2023) efficiently co-optimize morphology and control within a single agent using differentiable simulation. However, these methods have been limited to relatively simple design spaces and may struggle with sparse or deceptive search landscapes.
- **Universal (Morphology-Agnostic) Controllers:** Recent work by Li et al. (2025) advanced the field by evolving freeform endoskeletal robots with high-definition morphology (64³ voxels) using a variational autoencoder (VAE) to compress the design space. This approach utilized a "universal" controller, shared across all designs in a population, rather than training a bespoke policy for each individual. While more efficient than individual policies, this monolithic universal controller can lead to conservative designs and behaviors due to its need to generalize across all morphologies. Other related work employed hypernetworks to generate controller weights (Xiong et al., 2023) or shared monolithic controllers across joints or robot types (Huang et al., 2020; Schaff & Walter, 2022; Wang et al., 2023; Strgar & Kriegman, 2026; Bohlinger et al., 2024), often with predefined or restricted morphologies.

This paper positions its Embodiment-Conditioned Mixture of Experts (ECo-MoE) approach as an intermediate solution between training individual policies for every robot and using a monolithic universal controller. It aims to overcome the inefficiencies of the former and the conservatism of the latter by introducing modularity into the control policy, making it adaptable to diverse and evolving morphologies. The work builds directly upon the high-definition endoskeletal robot evolution framework established by Li et al. (2025).

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the evolvability of simulated robots by introducing a modular control architecture that is conditioned on the robot's embodiment. Specifically, the authors aim to co-optimize a distribution of latent design vectors (genotypes) with a mixture of control experts (neural modules), where the activation of these experts is gated by the latent coordinates of each decoded robot design (phenotype).

The motivations for this approach stem from observed limitations in existing robot co-design paradigms:
- **Inefficiency of Individual Policies:** Training a unique control policy for every robot in an evolutionary population is computationally expensive and does not scale well to large populations or complex designs.
- **Conservatism of Monolithic Universal Controllers:** While a single, morphology-agnostic controller (as in Li et al., 2025) offers computational efficiency by being shared, it often results in generalized, sometimes overly conservative, structures and behaviors. This single policy must cater to all possible morphologies, which can restrict the emergence of specialized or highly effective designs. Knowledge about different kinematic structures becomes entangled within a single large network, making adaptation difficult.
- **Need for Modularity:** Drawing inspiration from biological systems where modular genes and neural circuits govern morphology and behavior, the authors posit that modularity in robot control can confer several advantages. A modular system is hypothesized to be more controllable, reconfigurable, robust, adaptive, scalable, and interpretable. This modularity allows specific parts of the controller to be updated or replaced to suit new morphologies without disrupting the "hard-earned knowledge" contained within other expert modules.
- **Preservation and Reusability of Knowledge:** The ECo-MoE framework is motivated by the desire to preserve ancestral control knowledge in a unified, yet modular, system. This allows for the reuse and combination of learned sensorimotor circuits, which can be activated or deactivated based on the robot's body plan.
- **Guiding Evolution:** A key motivation is to introduce a mechanism for steering evolution. By allowing pretrained expert policies to be directly incorporated into the mixture, the system can guide freeform evolution toward specific, desired morphological traits or canonical structures. This concept, termed "evo by demo," provides a method to inject prior knowledge into the evolutionary process.

Based on these motivations, the research seeks to answer three specific questions:
1. Does an ECo-MoE controller influence the evolvability of robots, and if so, how?
2. What design choices affect the performance of ECo-MoE?
3. To what extent can pretrained experts and predesigned initializations direct evolution toward specific morphological characteristics?

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research adopts a co-optimization framework for robot morphology and control, building upon a latent design genome and a simulated environment. The core innovation is the introduction of an Embodiment-Conditioned Mixture of Experts (ECo-MoE) as the controller.

#### 3.1. Joint Co-optimization of Morphology and Control

- **Latent Design Genome:** The system utilizes a 512-dimensional latent genotype vector `z` sampled from a distribution `N(μ, Σ)`. This genotype is decoded by a variational autoencoder (VAE) into a high-definition morphology `m`, represented as a 64x64x64 voxel grid. Each voxel specifies material type (soft tissue or rigid bone) and a bone ID. Joints are automatically formed between opposing bones in the endoskeleton.
- **Simulation and Optimization:** Robots operate in a simulated terrestrial environment. Two objectives are concurrently optimized:
 - **Behavior Reward (`R(τ)`):** A dense reward signal used to train the controller. Policy updates (`U_R`) occur using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO).
 - **Design Fitness (`F(τ)`):** A sparse fitness score used to update the latent distribution. Evolutionary updates (`U_F`) occur using the Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy (CMA-ES).
- **Timescales:** Evolution operates on a slower timescale (generations `g`), while learning occurs on a faster timescale (epochs `e`), with `n` policy updates for each latent update.

#### 3.2. Embodiment-Conditioned Mixture of Experts (ECo-MoE)

- **Controller Architecture:** The ECo-MoE controller `π_θ,ϕ(a | s, z)` comprises `K` expert networks and a gating network. In the experiments, `K=4` experts were used.
- **Expert Networks:** Each expert `π_k_θ(a | s)` is a downsized graph transformer, responsible for processing sensory data (`s`) and suggesting actions (`a`).
- **Gating Network:** A linear layer `w_ϕ(z) = softmax(Wz + b)` takes the robot's latent genotype `z` as input. It outputs a set of weights (`w_k_ϕ(z)`) indicating the relative contribution of each expert to the final action.
- **Policy Output:** The final action `a` is a weighted sum of the actions suggested by each expert: `Σ_k=1^K w_k_ϕ(z) π_k_θ(a | s)`.
- **Routing Diversity Regularization:** To prevent "expert collapse" (where only one or few experts are consistently used), a regularization term `L_div` is added to the loss. This term encourages the gate to produce distinct routing weights for morphologies whose genotypes are far apart in the latent space, promoting diverse expert utilization.

#### 3.3. Evo by Demo: Guiding Evolution

The framework allows for directing evolution towards specific morphological traits using predesigned demos and pretrained experts.
- **Pretrained Expert Injection (PretrainOnly):** One of the mixture's experts (`π_0`) is replaced with a pretrained expert (`π_P_θ`) specifically trained for a predesigned demo. The parameters of this injected expert are then frozen. The other experts and the gating network remain trainable.
- **Compatibility Score:** The gate weight assigned to the pretrained expert, `w_P_ϕ(z)`, serves as a learned compatibility score `c_hat_g(z)`, indicating how much the universal controller relies on the pretrained expert for a given genotype `z`.
- **Augmented Fitness:** This compatibility score is used to augment the design fitness: `F~(z, τ) = F(τ) * (c_hat_g(z))^α`, where `α=2`. This mechanism encourages evolution towards designs that are compatible with the pretrained expert.
- **Predesigned Latent Prior (PredesignOnly):** The mean (`μ_0`) and standard deviation (`Σ_0`) of the initial design distribution `N(μ, Σ)` can be set to `μ_P` and `Σ_P`, derived from an encoded predesigned demo. This provides a structured starting point in the latent space. The predesigned demo is "revoxelized" multiple times with random bone reindexing and then encoded to generate a collection of latent genotype vectors, whose mean and standard deviation define the prior.
- **CoSteering:** This approach combines both a frozen pretrained expert and a predesigned latent prior to simultaneously guide evolution through both policy and initial design space.

#### 3.4. Task Environments

Experiments were conducted in three simulated environments:
- **Flat Ground:** Rewards net displacement on a flat plane.
- **Upright Locomotion:** Rewards net displacement and penalizes loss of clearance (proportion of body voxels below a height threshold).
- **Potholes:** Rewards net displacement on terrain with regularly-spaced depressions.

#### 3.5. Morphological Metrics

To quantify the steerability of evolution and characteristics of evolved designs, two metrics were introduced:
- **Effective-limb-count (`N_eff`):** Measures how evenly mass is distributed across the robot's skeletal branches, normalized by total non-root mass. A value of 1 implies a single dominant branch, while a higher value indicates more balanced branching.
- **Mass-bias-vector-magnitude (`||v||_2`):** Quantifies the asymmetry of the robot's mass distribution relative to its root segment (the bone closest to the overall center of mass).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments aimed to evaluate the impact of ECo-MoE on evolvability, analyze its design choices, and assess its ability to steer evolution.

#### 4.1. Performance Across Task Environments

- **Evolvability Gains:** ECo-MoE demonstrated a significant increase in evolvability compared to the monolithic universal controller baseline (Li et al., 2025) in more challenging tasks:
 - **Upright Locomotion:** ECo-MoE achieved significantly higher peak fitness (Figure 2E). This environment promotes diverse designs, and the gains suggest that embodiment-conditioned specialization becomes beneficial when morphological diversity leads to heterogeneous control demands.
 - **Potholes:** ECo-MoE also increased evolvability in this complex terrain (Figure 2F), indicating advantages as environmental complexity rises.
- **Flat Ground:** On Flat Ground, where simple "snake" morphologies converge due to the task's low complexity, ECo-MoE did not show a significant difference from the baseline (Figure 2D). This suggests that for simple, convergent evolutionary paths, specialized control is less critical.
- **Parameter Efficiency:** ECo-MoE achieved these performance improvements with a comparable (slightly smaller) total policy size and substantially fewer parameters per individual expert compared to the baseline, indicating higher parameter efficiency (Table 1).
- **Exploitation over Exploration:** Analysis of latent space exploration via PCA (Figure 4) showed that ECo-MoE and the baseline explored broadly similar areas and path lengths. This implies that ECo-MoE's increased evolvability stems from better *exploitation* (more effective control) of encountered designs, potentially buffering deleterious mutations and making more paths through design space tractable.

#### 4.2. Analysis and Ablations

- **Expert Specialization:** ECo-MoE dynamically adapted expert allocation. Over evolutionary time, multiple experts remained active across the population, with individual experts becoming increasingly specialized for specific morphological structures (Figure 5).
- **Routing Diversity Regularization:** This regularization was found to be critical. Without it, the routing weights quickly collapsed to a single expert, demonstrating the necessity of this mechanism to maintain diverse expert utilization (Figure 11).
- **Number of Experts:** ECo-MoE proved robust to the number of experts (tested with 2, 4, and 8 experts), with all tested configurations outperforming the single-expert baseline (Figure 10). The size of individual experts was adjusted to maintain comparable total parameter budgets (Table 2). With two experts, routing was less sensitive to morphological differences. With eight experts, only a subset was consistently utilized. Four experts offered a balance of task performance, specialization, and parameter usage.

#### 4.3. Evo by Demo

- **Fitness Gains:** Evo-by-demo settings, particularly **PredesignOnly** (predesigned latent initialization) and **CoSteering** (latent prior + frozen pretrained expert), achieved significantly higher fitness than the basic ECo-MoE and baseline, especially when starting with a "good" demo (Figure 8G, 12C, 13C). PredesignOnly sometimes achieved the highest fitness (e.g., for Demo 1, Figure 8G).
- **Morphological Steering:**
 - **CoSteering** was generally the most effective method for guiding evolution toward designs resembling the predesigned demo, particularly for Demo 1 and Demo 2. It resulted in evolved morphologies with skeletal topologies and bone geometries closest to the demo (Figure 8B, C, D, E, F; Figure 12A, B).
 - For Demo 3, **PredesignOnly** achieved the closest morphological alignment with the demo (Figure 13A, B).
- **Improved Controllability of Evolved Designs:** Robots evolved under ECo-MoE exhibited more balanced anatomies, characterized by higher effective-limb-counts and lower mass-bias-vector-magnitudes (Figure 8E, F). This suggests that ECo-MoE not only improves control of existing designs but also facilitates the evolution of designs that are inherently more controllable.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents the Embodiment-Conditioned Mixture of Experts (ECo-MoE) as a framework for jointly co-optimizing robot morphology and control, offering a scalable alternative to existing methods. The work contributes to the understanding of how modularity in control systems can influence evolutionary outcomes in robotic design.

The findings indicate several areas of significance:
- **Increased Evolvability:** The demonstration that ECo-MoE significantly increases the evolvability of robots, especially in complex behavioral tasks and challenging environments, suggests a pathway to developing more capable and diverse robotic agents. This is achieved without necessarily increasing the search breadth of morphological space, but rather by enabling more effective control (exploitation) of the encountered designs.
- **Enhanced Efficiency:** The parameter efficiency of ECo-MoE, achieving superior performance with comparable or fewer control parameters than monolithic alternatives, is relevant for practical applications where computational resources may be constrained.
- **Bio-Inspired Modularity:** The ECo-MoE framework provides a high-level abstraction of modular cross-embodiment control systems observed in biology, where shared modular genes and neural circuits govern diverse animal forms and behaviors. This alignment with biological principles could lead to new theories about how organisms evolve and learn.
- **Steerable Evolution ("Evo by Demo"):** The introduction of "evo by demo" offers a novel mechanism to guide freeform evolution toward desired morphological traits by leveraging pretrained experts and predesigned latent priors. This capability could be instrumental in designing robots for specific functions or applications, moving beyond purely open-ended, undirected evolution.
- **Evolution of More Controllable Designs:** The observation that ECo-MoE leads to the evolution of robots with more balanced and controllable anatomies (as measured by effective-limb-count and mass-bias-vector-magnitude) suggests that the modular control paradigm facilitates the emergence of inherently more functional and robust physical forms.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Robotics:** This work could have practical implications for automated robot design and manufacturing, potentially leading to the development of novel, highly adaptable, and efficient robotic forms, particularly in the domain of soft robotics.
- **Artificial Life:** By providing a model for the evolution and learning of complex embodied agents, it contributes to the field of artificial life, offering insights into the emergence of intelligence and adaptable systems.
- **Ethical Considerations:** As with any research advancing generally intelligent agents or robotic lifeforms, the authors acknowledge the broader societal benefits and potential dangers, which are recognized in relevant literature. The focus on soft-bodied and controllable forms in this research might mitigate some risks associated with rigid, less controllable robots.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
The authors identify several areas for future work:
- **VAE Latent Manifold Limitations:** The adopted VAE could not adequately reconstruct out-of-distribution structures like bipedal or hexapodal forms. Future research could address this by retraining the VAE with more diverse datasets or dynamically adapting the VAE during evolution.
- **Simplified Predesign:** The arbitrary labeling of bones in the VAE's skeletal graph introduced unnecessary complexity for "evo by demo." Explicitly encoding joints could simplify the predesign process.
- **Scope of Experiments:** The current work is restricted to locomotion in simulated terrestrial environments. Expanding to more diverse and challenging environments (e.g., swimming, object manipulation) and deploying designs in physical machines represents an important next step.
- **Hierarchical Control:** Future work could explore hierarchical routing, where lower-level experts manage embodiment-dependent motor primitives, and higher-level experts specialize in task objectives and sequencing for multiple heterogeneous behaviors.
