# CLAP: Cross-Embodiment Video World Models are Zero-Shot Physical Simulators

> [!summary] Summary
> CLAP trains action-conditioned video world models across human and robot embodiments by harmonizing heterogeneous action spaces into end-effector poses, templated language actions, and learned latent actions, then applying a curriculum that pretrains on unlabeled video with latent actions before regrounding in absolute end-effector actions. It matches state-of-the-art single-embodiment models on DROID, enables zero-shot cross-policy planning, and supports few-shot adaptation to bimanual and humanoid robots.

## Problem

- Prior action-conditioned video world models are trained on a single robot embodiment, foreclosing the internet-scale heterogeneous video corpus that carries generalizable physical signal.
- Robot platforms differ in degrees of freedom and action parameterization, while human video has no action labels at all, blocking a fixed-dimensional conditioning input.
- Existing latent-action models need downstream alignment layers to embodiment-specific commands, and those adapters degrade prediction fidelity at real-world deployment.

## Method

- **CLAP-EE** maps joint positions through ==forward kinematics== to a 7-DoF absolute end-effector space, per-robot normalized to [-1,1]; **CLAP-LANG** projects actions to templated relative text primitives.
- **CLAP**'s ==latent action model== is a spatiotemporal-transformer ==VAE== producing 32-dimensional continuous actions from frame pairs, trained with ==KL-regularized reconstruction== and randomized frame gap augmentation.
- **CLAP-CURR** pretrains the ==U-Net video diffusion== backbone on latent actions, then swaps in an end-effector action head and jointly finetunes, with history conditioning and multi-view latent concatenation.

## Results

- On DROID, CLAP-CURR reaches **19.138 PSNR** and **0.204 LPIPS**, matching single-embodiment Ctrl-World (**18.928 PSNR**, **0.205 LPIPS**) despite fewer DROID samples and identical parameter count.
- Absolute end-effector conditioning beats relative by roughly **14.6%** LPIPS on DROID (**0.215** vs **0.257**), while language conditioning reverses this, favoring relative actions (**0.260** vs **0.276**).
- Cross-policy planning lifts success rates over base policies: measuring tape **80%** versus **75%/40%**, lobster **95%** versus **20%/90%**; bimanual YAM carrot-apple **95%** versus **90%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Universal physics is a strong enough shared prior that cross-embodiment training costs nothing in-domain, inverting the assumption that specialization always wins on a target platform.
> - Optimal action-space parameterization is conditioning-dependent: continuous heads favor absolute actions for error robustness, discrete tokenizers favor relative actions for resolution.
> - Human video alone cannot substitute for robot data; a human-only baseline trails by at least 61% LPIPS, so robot data remains essential for transferring priors.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of action-conditioned video models and embodied artificial intelligence has seen advancements, particularly in generating physically consistent video content and using these models as simulators for robotics. However, a prevailing limitation in this domain is that state-of-the-art action-conditioned video models are typically restricted to a single robot embodiment. This restriction impedes their ability to leverage the extensive and diverse corpus of heterogeneous video data available online, which contains signals for learning generalizable physics. Prior works in video generation for robotics often employ models as data generators, policy backbones, or learned simulators for policy evaluation and improvement, yet they largely remain constrained to agent-specific architectures and single robot platforms.

In parallel, the challenge of achieving generalist robot policies has led to explorations in cross-embodiment robot learning. Approaches have included padding state and action spaces, adopting shared end-effector or language representations, learning embodiment-specific action heads, or utilizing latent actions. While latent action models (LAMs) offer a paradigm for unifying heterogeneous action spaces and learning from unlabeled videos, they frequently require subsequent alignment to specific robot commands for real-world deployment. Existing unified action representations, such as end-effector actions, demand dense action-labeled video data, which limits data scaling to the vast amount of unlabeled internet videos.

This work addresses these fundamental limitations by proposing a framework, CLAP, that enables cross-embodiment action-conditioned video generation. It seeks to unify diverse robot morphologies and human agents within a single model, thereby allowing the learning of generalizable physical priors from internet-scale, heterogeneous video data. The authors draw an analogy to the breakthroughs in Large Language Models (LLMs), which achieved advanced capabilities through web-scale training on diverse text datasets, suggesting a similar potential for robotics by scaling video data across embodiments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central question driving this research is whether generative video models can acquire generalizable physical priors through cross-embodiment data scaling, or if the inherent heterogeneity of different robot morphologies necessitates agent-specific architectures. The authors posit that universal physical laws govern spatiotemporal dynamics irrespective of the acting agent's embodiment. For example, the physical principles governing how an object moves or interacts with its environment remain consistent whether the actor is a robotic arm or a human hand. This insight motivates the hypothesis that restricting video model training to a single embodiment is suboptimal for learning comprehensive physical understanding.

Based on this foundational premise, the key objectives of the CLAP framework are:

1. **Develop a framework for cross-embodiment action-conditioned video generation:** This involves creating a system capable of training on diverse, internet-scale videos, encompassing both human and robotic agents with varying morphologies.
2. **Harmonize disparate action spaces:** Address the challenge that action representations vary significantly across different robot platforms and are often absent in human videos. This objective includes reconciling variations in robotic degrees of freedom, kinematic structures, and the presence or absence of action labels.
3. **Learn foundational physical priors from diverse data:** Leverage the vast body of heterogeneous video data to teach the model fundamental physical laws that are critical for accurate, fine-grained dynamics prediction across different agents and tasks.
4. **Enable zero-shot generalization to real-world tasks:** Demonstrate the model's ability to transfer learned physical priors directly to real-world robot manipulation tasks without requiring additional domain-specific data or adaptation.
5. **Facilitate data-efficient adaptation to target embodiments:** Establish a novel paradigm where cross-embodiment models can be efficiently adapted with minimal data to specific robot platforms, including both previously encountered and entirely novel morphologies, thereby enhancing the training of high-fidelity single-embodiment video world models.

The motivation behind integrating different action representations (end-effector poses, natural language, and learned latent actions) stems from their individual limitations. Latent actions facilitate learning from unlabeled videos but require downstream alignment for real-world deployment. End-effector actions enable direct real-world deployment but necessitate action-labeled data. Language actions offer versatility but can be constrained by precision limits and tokenizer resolution. The research aims to overcome these individual drawbacks by combining their complementary strengths through a structured learning approach.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

CLAP introduces a framework for training cross-embodiment video world models by addressing the heterogeneity of robot and human video data, particularly the absence of action labels in internet-scale videos. The methodology revolves around three core contributions: harmonizing action spaces, implementing a curriculum-based learning recipe, and establishing a paradigm for data-efficient adaptation.

### 4. Harmonizing Heterogeneous Action Spaces

To enable a single video model to generalize across diverse embodiments, CLAP unifies action representations in three forms:

- **End-effector Actions (CLAP-EE):** Instead of joint-space control, which ties models to specific Degrees of Freedom (DoF), CLAP-EE uses 7-DoF operational space (3 translational, 3 Euler angles for orientation, 1 continuous gripper state). This representation is compatible with datasets like Open X-Embodiment (OXE). To handle varying operational space volumes, CLAP-EE uses *absolute* end-effector actions and normalizes them into finite bounds [−1, 1] for each robot, accounting for morphological differences. A central limitation is the requirement for action-labeled video data.

- **Language Actions (CLAP-LANG):** This approach expresses control primitives as natural text. Numerical end-effector actions are projected into language space using concise templated motion primitives (e.g., “x=, y=...”). This addresses the limitations of high-precision numerical formats being truncated by CLIP tokenizers and the poor alignment of existing tokenizers to numerical values. CLAP-LANG transforms *absolute* end-effector actions into *relative* actions to narrow the action space, improving information density for templated language actions. The drawback is a reduction in spatial resolution compared to end-effector-conditioned models due to the discrete nature of language tokenization.

- **Latent Actions (CLAP-LAM):** To leverage the large volume of unlabeled web-scale video data, especially human videos, CLAP uses latent actions as proxy representations. These describe the transformation between pairs of video frames using low-dimensional action tokens. A variational autoencoder (VAE) is employed, taking two video frames (f_t, f_t+∆t) as input and outputting a d-dimensional continuous latent action (d=32). The encoder (a spatiotemporal transformer) extracts features and maps them to the latent action, which the decoder uses with f_t to reconstruct f_t+∆t. The model is trained with a reconstruction loss and KL divergence regularization. Dynamically varying ∆t serves as data augmentation. While effective for data scaling with unlabeled data, CLAP-LAM requires downstream alignment to a target embodiment's action space for real-world deployment, which can degrade prediction fidelity.

- **Curriculum-based Latent-to-EE Actions (CLAP-CURR):** This method combines the strengths of latent and end-effector actions. It involves a two-stage curriculum:
 1. **Pretraining with Latent Actions:** The video model first learns foundational physical priors from unlabeled video data using latent actions.
 2. **Refinement with End-effector Actions:** The action head is then swapped to accept 7-dimensional end-effector actions, retaining the pretrained weights in the video model backbone. The new action head and the video model are jointly trained on action-labeled video data. This strategy aims to achieve unrestricted cross-embodiment scaling without requiring downstream adaptation, thereby avoiding fidelity degradation introduced by alignment layers.

### 5. Training the Cross-Embodiment Video World Model

- **Model Architecture:** CLAP utilizes the video diffusion paradigm, specifically the SVD (Stable Video Diffusion) spatio-temporal U-Net, for action-conditioned video generation. Video tokenization is performed using continuous VAEs, followed by the U-Net for spatiotemporal modeling.
- **Loss Function:** The video model V_θ is supervised using a standard diffusion loss function, which involves denoising partially noised video inputs.
- **History Conditioning and Multi-view Video Prediction:** To provide rich temporal context, the video model is conditioned on a history of past observations and robot poses across a horizon H using causal cross-attention. For geometrical consistency and robustness, multi-view conditioning is incorporated by concatenating all views vertically in the latent space, which is then passed to the model.

### 6. Data-Efficient Adaptation to Target Morphologies

CLAP models, trained on diverse robot video data, learn generalizable priors. These models can be further refined through finetuning for target embodiments:

- **Pretrained Embodiments:** For robots seen during initial training, the cross-embodiment models can be adapted via data-efficient finetuning on the robot’s native end-effector action space. This process requires no architectural modifications, as the action space already matches. This approach is posited to yield superior single-embodiment world models compared to training from scratch.
- **Novel Embodiments:** For new robot morphologies with different action spaces (e.g., bimanual robots, humanoids), finetuning involves substituting the model’s action head with a new one compatible with the target embodiment, while retaining the rest of the pretrained cross-embodiment video model components to preserve spatiotemporal priors.

The models were trained on Open X-Embodiment and EgoDex datasets for 100K steps using 8 H100 or H200 GPUs, with an effective batch size of 64.

### 7. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of CLAP focused on four primary research questions, using perceptual metrics such as SSIM, PSNR, LPIPS, FVD, and FID. Baselines included Ctrl-World for the DROID environment and a newly developed Bridge-Base for the Bridge environment.

### 8. Q1: Do cross-embodiment video models surpass their single-embodiment counterparts?

- **Action-space Harmonization Performance:**
 - **CLAP-EE** exhibited superior prediction fidelity compared to **CLAP-LANG**, particularly for fine-grained dynamics. This was attributed to the continuous precision of end-effector actions versus the limitations of discrete tokenization in language actions.
 - **CLAP-LAM** (latent actions) matched or surpassed **CLAP-EE** in future-prediction accuracy, especially across a diverse mix of robot platforms (OXE-Mix), demonstrating its effectiveness in leveraging unlabeled data.
 - **CLAP-CURR** combined the strengths of latent and end-effector actions, achieving high-fidelity dynamics prediction with fine-grained control and zero-shot real-world deployment capability. On the challenging DROID environment, CLAP-CURR closely followed ground-truth trajectories, outperforming CLAP-LAM and CLAP-EE.
- **Impact of Human Videos:** The DreamDojo-Human baseline, trained exclusively on human videos, showed significantly lower prediction accuracies (e.g., CLAP models improved its LPIPS score by at least 61% on DROID). This indicated that while human videos contribute to learning foundational physical priors, robot-specific data is crucial for effective transfer of these priors to robot morphologies.
- **Comparison to Single-Embodiment Baselines:** CLAP’s cross-embodiment models (e.g., CLAP-LAM, CLAP-EE) matched the performance of state-of-the-art single-embodiment baselines like Ctrl-World in the challenging DROID environment. This was notable because CLAP models had the same parameters and architecture but effectively utilized fewer DROID samples, suggesting that they learn generalizable physical priors from diverse data. In the simpler Bridge environment, CLAP achieved high-accuracy predictions, approaching but not precisely matching the Bridge-Base (a new SOTA).

### 9. Q2: Do relative-action spaces outperform absolute-action spaces in cross-embodiment video modeling?

- **End-effector-action Conditioning:** *Absolute* action spaces demonstrated superior prediction fidelity compared to *relative* action spaces (e.g., ~14.6% improvement in LPIPS on DROID). Absolute actions, when used with the proposed normalization strategies, provided stronger robustness to compounding errors over extended horizons, which relative actions are susceptible to.
- **Language-action Conditioning:** Conversely, *relative* action spaces achieved superior fidelity in dynamics prediction for language-action-conditioned models. This was attributed to the limited resolution of discrete language tokenization spaces, where the compact bounds of relative actions utilized the tokenization space more effectively.

### 10. Q3: Do cross-embodiment video models achieve zero-shot real-world generalization?

CLAP’s models were tested for zero-shot generalization to real-world robot manipulation tasks through inference-time cross-policy planning. CLAP was deployed as a world modeling backbone (using CLAP-LAM with a lightweight adapter) to score action proposals from state-of-the-art policies (π0.5 and MolmoAct-2).
- **Single-arm Manipulation (DROID):** CLAP matched or improved the base success rates of π0.5 and MolmoAct-2 across various pick-and-place and towel-folding tasks. CLAP compensated for the brittle semantic reasoning of individual policies by combining their strengths, leading to increased robustness via sequential, receding-horizon planning.
- **Bimanual Manipulation (Bimanual YAM):** Even when stress-tested on bimanual tasks, where CLAP was originally trained on single-arm data, it achieved higher success rates than the base MolmoAct-2 bimanual-YAM policy. CLAP demonstrated its ability to generate accurate future predictions that distinguished promising action proposals.

### 11. Q4: Does CLAP facilitate data-efficient adaptation to target embodiments?

- **Pretrained Embodiments (DROID and Bridge):** Finetuned CLAP cross-embodiment models consistently surpassed state-of-the-art single-embodiment baselines across nearly all perceptual metrics. This indicated successful transfer of generalizable physical priors and established a new paradigm: finetuning cross-embodiment models is more effective for high-fidelity single-embodiment video models than training from scratch.
- **Novel Embodiments (Bimanual YAM and G1 Humanoid):** Few-shot adaptation of CLAP-CURR to a 14-DoF bimanual YAM robot and a 26-DoF G1 humanoid robot resulted in high-fidelity future prediction. The bimanual YAM model accurately predicted motions of each arm, and the G1 humanoid model predicted both arm and hand movements. This demonstrated CLAP’s effectiveness as a foundational backbone for adaptation to novel, higher-dimensional robot morphologies.

### 12. Inference and Timing Results (Appendix C.2)

CLAP requires less than 12 GB of VRAM for inference, making it compatible with consumer-grade hardware. Inference times for a standard configuration (11 frames, 25 denoising steps) averaged 1.49s on an H200 GPU and 3.24s on an A100-PCIe-40GB, indicating that it does not operate in real-time (e.g., 10 Hz).

### 13. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces CLAP, a framework that represents a step towards developing generalist action-conditioned video world models. Its significance lies in several aspects:

- **Paradigm Shift for Robotics World Models:** By demonstrating the efficacy of cross-embodiment learning, CLAP proposes a shift in how action-conditioned video models are trained, analogous to the impact of large-scale data on Large Language Models. This approach facilitates learning generalizable physical priors from diverse, heterogeneous video data, which was previously a challenge for single-embodiment models.
- **Enhanced Dynamics Prediction:** CLAP achieves or surpasses the performance of state-of-the-art single-embodiment video models in challenging environments, indicating improved prediction fidelity despite operating across a wider distribution of embodiments.
- **Zero-Shot Generalization:** The demonstrated ability of CLAP to generalize to real-world robot manipulation tasks without additional data, through inference-time cross-policy planning, is a notable advancement. This capability can improve the robustness and success rates of existing robot policies by enabling intelligent action selection.
- **Data-Efficient Adaptation:** CLAP establishes a novel and efficient method for training high-fidelity single-embodiment video world models. By leveraging cross-embodiment models as foundational backbones, it enables few-shot adaptation to target robot platforms, including novel morphologies, thereby reducing the data and computational resources typically required for training specialized models.
- **Comprehensive Functionality:** The framework offers a comprehensive suite of action-conditioned video world models that span diverse action-conditioning spaces (end-effector, language, latent) and multiple robot morphologies.
- **Cost-Effective Policy Development:** The potential for using CLAP for cost-effective policy finetuning via video model-based reinforcement learning offers an alternative to expensive real-world or simulation-based RL setups.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The paper acknowledges several limitations. Like other video world models, CLAP is susceptible to hallucinations, which needs mitigation for reliable deployment in critical robotics applications. The current training data is primarily single-embodiment robot data and human video data; extending this to bimanual and humanoid robot data could further enhance prediction accuracy and zero-shot generalization. While the benefits of cross-embodiment learning are evident, the exact point at which these benefits plateau is an area for future investigation. The current inference speed, while capable on consumer hardware, is not real-time. Future research could focus on hallucination detection and mitigation (e.g., via uncertainty quantification), expanding data diversity, and developing more efficient model architectures to reduce training and inference costs.

In summary, CLAP represents a significant contribution to robot learning by demonstrating that unifying diverse video data across embodiments can lead to more generalizable and capable physical simulators, potentially transforming the development of robotic intelligence.
