# Q-Learning With World Models

> [!summary] Summary
> QWM (Q-Learning with World Models) uses a learned world model purely for test-time tree search over candidate actions on top of standard off-policy Q-learning, scoring branches by combining Q-value and imagined-rollout value estimates. Policy and critic train only on real transitions, avoiding compounding model bias. On Robomimic and LIBERO, QWM beats strong model-free and model-based baselines in sample efficiency and success rate.

## Problem

- Model-based RL methods that optimize policy or value functions directly on imagined rollouts suffer from compounding model bias, worsening with task horizon and visual complexity.
- World models have mainly succeeded in supervised policy learning, with no established simple way to use them to boost online off-policy Q-learning without retraining on imagined data.
- Existing Q-learning action-selection schemes (single policy sample, or best-of-N by Q-value alone) only score the immediate next action, ignoring longer-horizon future consequences.

## Method

- ==Tree search with a world model==: alternating state/action layers expand N sampled actions per node and K imagined next-states per action via the dynamics model, to a shallow search depth D.
- **Combined node-value aggregation**: each node's value blends a Q-function estimator (low-variance, depth-independent) with a model-rollout reward-plus-discounted-value estimator, weighted equally by default.
- ==Q-guided beam pruning== heuristic: retains only the top-J highest cumulative-discounted-score paths at each depth to keep the exponentially growing tree computationally tractable during search.

## Results

- **QWM** outperforms model-free baselines (RLPD, DSRL, QSM, QAM, FQL, IDQL) and built on EXPO/RLPD backbones shows consistent sample-efficiency gains across Robomimic Lift, Can, Square, and Tool Hang, with the largest gains on Tool Hang.
- Against model-based baselines TD-MPC2 and EfficientZero V2 on sparse-reward Robomimic tasks, QWM achieves consistently strong success while both baselines reach non-zero success only on Lift.
- Ablations show search depth **2** and discount lambda **0.2** work best, applying tree search during both online sampling and evaluation gives the most consistent gains, and pixel-based LIBERO evaluation with a fine-tuned Wan2.2 video world model still improves over EXPO despite using search only for sampling.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Confining the world model to test-time search rather than training-time rollout generation decouples model error from policy/critic learning, sidestepping the bias-compounding failure mode of classic model-based RL.
> - Combining a state-action value estimator (Q-function) with a rollout-based state value estimator exploits complementary error profiles, since Q is depth-independent but blind to imagined futures, and rollout value uses futures but accumulates model error.
> - Search benefits saturate and can even reverse: too many action candidates or too much weight on deep imagined branches amplifies model and value-estimation error rather than improving action selection.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has seen advancements in sample efficiency, particularly with off-policy methods, enabling applications such as fine-tuning Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. As RL is applied to increasingly complex domains like real-world robotics, further improvements in sample efficiency are critical. World models, which predict state changes rather than merely actions, offer a mechanism for enhancing sample efficiency. However, their application has predominantly been confined to supervised policy learning.

Traditional model-based RL (MBRL) methods often train policies or value functions directly on imagined rollouts generated by a learned dynamics model. This approach is susceptible to compounding bias, where inaccuracies in the world model are integrated into the learned policy, an issue that becomes more pronounced with longer task horizons and increased visual complexity, hindering scalability to high-dimensional problems in robotics.

This work addresses the challenge of leveraging world models within online RL without incurring the compounding bias of traditional MBRL. It positions itself as an alternative to methods that use world models as a substitute for real interaction or primarily as a training data generator. Instead, it seeks to integrate world models to sharpen the decision-making of an RL agent that remains grounded in real, online environment interactions. The paper differentiates its approach from prior work on:
- **Reinforcement learning with prior data:** While many methods focus on how offline data is incorporated into the RL objective (e.g., balancing exploration-exploitation, calibrating value estimates, or direct offline RL fine-tuning), this work focuses on leveraging world models to improve online RL decision-making, making it complementary to such data-incorporation strategies.
- **Traditional Model-Based RL:** Unlike methods that generate synthetic transitions for policy optimization or planning with trajectory optimizers, QWM avoids policy or value function training on model-generated data, thereby mitigating bias accumulation.
- **World models for VLAs:** While previous VLA work uses world models for synthetic data generation or on-policy RL on imagined rollouts, QWM applies world models for test-time action selection in Q-learning settings.
- **Test-time scaling and best-of-N inference:** Prior works use sampling and scoring candidates independently without modeling downstream consequences. QWM extends this by evaluating candidates based on predicted downstream value through imagined trajectories.

The paper aims to bridge the gap between the sample-efficiency benefits of world models and the stability of off-policy Q-learning, particularly in complex, high-dimensional robotics scenarios with sparse rewards.

### 2. Key objectives and motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate whether world models can be leveraged directly within standard Q-learning to improve performance and sample efficiency, while ensuring that the learning process remains grounded in real, online environment interactions.

The motivation for this objective stems from several key observations and challenges in the field of reinforcement learning:
- **Demand for higher sample efficiency:** As RL is applied to increasingly complex real-world scenarios, such as robotic manipulation, the cost and time associated with collecting real-world interaction data become substantial. Therefore, methods that improve sample efficiency are crucial.
- **Potential of world models:** World models have demonstrated the ability to predict future states given current states and actions, offering a natural pathway to enhance sample efficiency by allowing agents to "imagine" consequences.
- **Limitations of existing Model-Based RL (MBRL):**
 - **Compounding model bias:** Many MBRL approaches optimize policies or value functions directly within a learned dynamics model. Imperfections in this model can lead to biases that accumulate over imagined trajectories, resulting in suboptimal or unstable policies when deployed in the real environment. This problem escalates with task horizon and the complexity of observations (e.g., visual inputs).
 - **Scalability issues:** Traditional MBRL methods often struggle to scale effectively to large, high-dimensional problems prevalent in real-world robotics, where model accuracy is difficult to maintain over long horizons.
- **Desire for stable online learning:** Standard off-policy Q-learning methods are known for their stability and empirical performance. The motivation is to augment these stable algorithms with predictive capabilities from world models without undermining their grounding in real data.
- **Exploiting Q-function capabilities:** The learned Q-function inherently estimates the discounted return of a policy given a state-action pair. This provides a valuable signal for evaluating candidate actions, which can be further enhanced by looking ahead with a world model.

The authors' core insight is that a learned world model can be used to perform test-time search over actions by imagining future trajectories. This enhanced action selection can directly improve the performance of Q-learning methods, both during online data collection (to gather better experience) and during evaluation (to act more effectively). By training the policy and value function *only* on real environment transitions, the proposed framework (QWM) aims to circumvent the bias accumulation issues inherent in methods that rely heavily on model-generated rollouts for training.

### 3. Methodology and approach

The proposed framework, Q-Learning with World Models (QWM), integrates a learned world model with standard Q-learning through a test-time search mechanism. The core principle is that the policy and value function are trained exclusively on real environment transitions, while the world model is leveraged solely for improving action selection during online rollouts and evaluation.

The methodology is structured around a tree search over actions, which involves three main components:

#### 3.1. Tree Search with the World Model

At each decision step, a search tree is constructed from the current state (root node) up to a specified depth $D$. Nodes in the tree alternate between state layers and action layers.
- **Expansion at State Nodes**: From a state node at depth $d$, $N$ candidate actions are sampled from the current policy $\pi_\theta(\cdot | s_d)$.
- **Expansion at Action Nodes**: For each sampled action $a_n^d$, the world model $M_\psi(s'|s_d, a_n^d)$ is queried $K$ times to predict $K$ future states $s_{d+1}^k$.
This expansion process is recursively applied until a depth $D$ is reached. The short search horizon is designed to mitigate the accumulation of world model prediction errors.

#### 3.2. Aggregating the Value

The value of each node in the tree is estimated by combining a state-action value estimator and a state value estimator to leverage their complementary strengths.
- **State-action value estimator ($V_Q$)**: This estimator directly uses the Q-function $Q_\phi(s_d, a_n^d)$ for each of the $N$ sampled actions at depth $d$. It is low-variance and independent of the search depth or model error, but relies on the accuracy of $Q_\phi$.
- **State value estimator ($V_r$)**: This estimator leverages the world model's predicted per-step reward $r_\psi(s_d, a_n^d)$ and a discounted value of the next state. It recursively aggregates values from deeper imagined rollouts. For sparse reward settings, the reward model $r_\psi$ is typically not learned, simplifying the reward component. At the final depth $D$, the leaf value is computed as an aggregate of $Q$-values for $N_{leaf}$ actions sampled from the policy.
- **Combined node value**: The final value for any node $V(d | s_d)$ is a weighted average of $V_Q(d | s_d)$ and $V_r(d | s_d)$, typically an equal average: $0.5 \cdot (V_Q + V_r)$.
- **Root value ($Q_{ts}$)**: The tree-search value for each initial candidate action at the root is computed similarly, combining its immediate Q-value with the discounted aggregated value from its predicted next states. The action ultimately selected for execution is the one that maximizes this $Q_{ts}$ value.

#### 3.3. Searching with the Q-function (Pruning)

To manage computational complexity arising from the rapid growth of the tree, a heuristic pruning strategy is employed. Instead of expanding all possible paths, only a subset of $J$ (e.g., $J=1$) high-value partial paths are retained for expansion at each depth. Each surviving path $b$ accumulates a discounted Q-score $\Sigma(b, d)$. At depth $d+1$, candidate children paths are scored by adding their prospective discounted Q-value to the parent's accumulated score. The top $J$ paths based on these scores are selected to form the surviving set for the next depth. This greedy approach aims to approximate the highest-value paths of the full tree.

#### 3.4. Practical Implementation

- **World Model Training**: The action-conditioned world model is pretrained on a dataset of environment transitions ($s, a, r, s'$). In sparse reward settings, a separate reward model is typically not learned.
 - **Low-dimensional state observations (Robomimic)**: A deterministic residual dynamics model $M_\psi(s_t, a_t) = s_t + \Delta_\psi(s_t, a_t)$ is implemented as a three-layer MLP, trained with an MSE objective on offline demonstrations.
 - **Pixel observations (LIBERO)**: The Wan2.2-TI2V-5B model is adapted into an action-conditioned video world model. An MLP-based action encoder maps robot actions into tokens, which are concatenated with text conditioning tokens and provided to a diffusion transformer. The action encoder and diffusion transformer are jointly fine-tuned on demonstration video clips and action sequences using the Wan2.2 flow-matching objective, while the VAE and text encoder remain frozen. During inference, the model predicts short-horizon future observations (e.g., next frame).
- **Base RL Algorithms**: QWM is implemented on top of existing off-policy Q-learning algorithms:
 - **EXPO**: Couples a base flow policy with an edit policy to maximize the Q-function, using an on-the-fly policy for action selection.
 - **RLPD**: Combines offline and online replay data with high update-to-data (UTD) ratios, an ensemble of critics, and a Gaussian actor.
 The original policy and critic optimization procedures of these base algorithms are maintained without modification; QWM only enhances their action selection at decision time.

### 4. Main findings and results

The experiments evaluated QWM on challenging robotic manipulation tasks from Robomimic (state-based and visual) and LIBERO (pixel-based), assessing its performance against state-of-the-art model-free and model-based RL methods, its efficacy when integrated with base algorithms, its scalability to visual inputs, and the importance of its individual components.

#### 4.1. Comparison with state-of-the-art model-free and model-based RL methods

- **Model-free Baselines**: QWM demonstrated superior performance across all evaluated tasks compared to strong model-free RL baselines (RLPD, DSRL, QSM, QAM, FQL, IDQL). These baselines typically sample and execute a single action or select the highest Q-value among multiple immediate candidates. QWM's advantage stems from its test-time search, which leverages the world model to predict future outcomes and select actions based on projected long-term returns, providing more signal than immediate Q-values alone.
- **Model-based Baselines**: QWM consistently achieved stronger performance than state-of-the-art model-based RL methods such as TD-MPC2 and EfficientZero V2, particularly across manipulation tasks with sparse rewards. Unlike these methods that integrate model predictions into policy optimization, QWM utilizes the world model exclusively for test-time search, preventing the compounding bias typically observed in MBRL methods that rely on model-generated trajectories for learning. This design enabled QWM to learn effectively in sparse reward settings where other MBRL approaches struggled to achieve non-zero success.

#### 4.2. Performance compared to base methods

- **QWM on EXPO**: When integrated with EXPO, QWM consistently improved learning efficiency across tasks like Tool Hang, Square, and Can. Gains were more significant on harder, longer-horizon tasks such as Tool Hang, indicating that the test-time search effectively enhances the base algorithm's ability to learn.
- **QWM on RLPD**: Similarly, implementing QWM on top of RLPD also led to consistent improvements in sample efficiency, particularly noticeable in tasks like Square, Can, and Lift. This suggests that QWM provides benefits that are transferable across different underlying Q-learning algorithms, although performance may still be influenced by the sample efficiency of the chosen base algorithm.

#### 4.3. Scalability to pixel-based settings

QWM's performance gains extended to high-dimensional visual observations in the LIBERO benchmark. While computational constraints limited the world model's use to sampling during online data collection (not evaluation) in this setting, QWM still showed a clear overall improvement over EXPO. It learned faster and achieved stronger late-stage performance on tasks like 60, 79, and 29, and reached high performance earlier on Task 28. This demonstrates QWM's ability to leverage world models for improved action selection even with complex visual inputs, a domain where learning accurate dynamics models is challenging.

#### 4.4. Importance of QWM components (Ablation Studies)

- **Sampling versus Evaluation**: Applying world-model-guided action selection during both online RL data collection (sampling) and at evaluation time yielded the strongest and most consistent learning efficiency. Sampling-time search improved the quality of collected transitions, benefiting subsequent policy and critic updates. Evaluation-time search directly enhanced action selection at execution. Combining both maximized the advantage by improving both the data and the learned policy's deployment.
- **Tree-search Hyperparameters**:
 - **Search Depth (D)**: A depth of 2 generally provided the strongest performance. Depths that were too shallow failed to capture sufficient future consequences, while excessively high depths exacerbated world-model prediction error accumulation.
 - **Recursive Discount Factor ($\lambda$)**: Moderate values, specifically $\lambda=0.2$ in the reported settings, were optimal. A larger $\lambda$ made value aggregation sensitive to accumulated errors, while a smaller $\lambda$ limited the contribution of deeper search, suggesting a preference for short-term estimates with a moderate influence from future values.
 - **Number of Action Candidates (N)**: Performance improved as N increased from small values, allowing evaluation of a broader action set. However, gains diminished beyond a moderate N, and very large N could amplify errors and computational cost without proportional benefit.
- **Number of Expanded Nodes (J)**: Performance was relatively insensitive to the number of expanded nodes $J$ when using the pruning heuristic. This indicates that retaining even a small number of high-value branches is sufficient to capture useful candidate futures and approximate the full tree search effectively.
- **Search with Q-function vs. State Value Function (Appendix B)**: Additional experiments demonstrated that searching with the Q-function substantially outperformed searching with a state value function $V$. This suggests that the action-conditioned estimator provided by the Q-function is critical for effective test-time search and is stronger than a state-value-only approach.

### 5. Significance and potential impact

The research introduces QWM, a framework that integrates world models with Q-learning through test-time search, demonstrating a method to improve the performance and sample efficiency of online reinforcement learning. The core contribution lies in leveraging the predictive capabilities of world models for enhanced action selection *without* using model-generated data for policy or value function training. This approach effectively mitigates the problem of compounding model bias, which has historically limited the scalability and reliability of traditional model-based RL methods in complex, high-dimensional environments.

The findings indicate that QWM offers consistent performance improvements across diverse and challenging robotic manipulation benchmarks, outperforming both strong model-free and model-based baselines. Its ability to scale to high-dimensional visual observations suggests its applicability to real-world robotic systems, where visual inputs are common. By improving the sample efficiency of Q-learning, QWM has the potential to accelerate the development of autonomous agents, making them more adaptable and capable with less real-world interaction. This is particularly significant for applications like fine-tuning Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, where sample efficiency is a crucial bottleneck.

Furthermore, the detailed ablation studies provide insights into the design principles of effective model-guided search. The observation that test-time search during both online sampling and evaluation yields the strongest results underscores the dual benefit of improving data collection and real-time decision-making. The preference for moderate search depth, specific discount factors, and a manageable number of action candidates offers practical guidance for future research in this area.

Despite its benefits, QWM introduces computational overhead due to the tree search, which might be a constraint in latency-critical applications. Additionally, the framework relies on learning a high-quality world model, which itself is a complex and resource-intensive task. However, by demonstrating a robust method to utilize world models without their inherent biases propagating into policy learning, this work opens avenues for more reliable and sample-efficient RL, contributing significantly to the advancement of robot learning.
