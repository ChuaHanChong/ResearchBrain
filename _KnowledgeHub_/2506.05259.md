# Learning long range dependencies through time reversal symmetry breaking

> [!summary] Summary
> Recurrent Hamiltonian Echo Learning (RHEL) introduces a physics-inspired algorithm for training Hamiltonian-based recurrent neural networks, provably matching Backpropagation Through Time (BPTT) gradients while requiring only three forward passes. The algorithm demonstrated performance comparable to BPTT on diverse long-range sequence modeling tasks, including classification and regression up to sequence lengths of 50,000.

## Problem

- Efficiently computing gradients for training recurrent neural networks on long sequential data is challenging due to high memory costs (BPTT) or scalability issues (RTRL).
- Existing physics-inspired gradient computation methods (e.g., Hamiltonian Echo Backpropagation) were limited to static inputs, small problems, and lacked a generalized framework for sequence modeling.
- There is a need for energy-efficient, hardware-friendly learning algorithms compatible with novel physical computing substrates for self-learning AI systems.

## Method

- Introduced ==Recurrent Hamiltonian Echo== Learning (**RHEL**), an algorithm leveraging time-reversal symmetry in non-dissipative Hamiltonian dynamical systems.
- RHEL computes gradients as finite differences from two subtly "nudged" echo trajectories, effectively requiring only three forward passes irrespective of sequence length.
- Developed Hamiltonian Recurrent Units (HRUs) using symplectic integrators for discrete-time systems and chained RHEL for hierarchical Hamiltonian State Space Models (HSSMs).

## Results

- RHEL demonstrated near-perfect gradient matching with BPTT for both linear and nonlinear Hamiltonian State Space Models on static comparisons (cosine similarity close to **1.0**, norm ratio close to **1.0**).
- RHEL-trained models achieved performance comparable to BPTT on six multivariate time-series classification datasets (sequence lengths up to **18**,**000**) and a long-range regression task (sequence length ~**50**,**000**).
- Identified and mitigated numerical underflow issues in RHEL's finite difference scheme with a gradient rescaling technique, which restored exact gradient matching with BPTT.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Gradient computation for Hamiltonian systems can be achieved through time-reversal symmetry breaking, enabling a "forward-only" approach equivalent to backward-mode automatic differentiation.
> - RHEL provides a theoretical framework that unifies the continuous adjoint state method and Backpropagation Through Time, offering a deeper understanding of gradient propagation in dynamic systems.
> - Physics-grounded learning algorithms, compatible with novel hardware paradigms, can effectively scale to complex, long-range sequence modeling tasks without sacrificing performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper is strategically positioned at the intersection of several rapidly evolving fields: recurrent neural networks, state-space models, physical computing, and alternative gradient estimation techniques.

The **resurgence of Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and State Space Models (SSMs)**, particularly for long-range sequence modeling, forms the immediate backdrop. The authors cite recent influential works like Mamba, highlighting a renewed interest in these architectures as alternatives or complements to the dominant Transformer architecture. This context positions their work as an exploration of fundamental learning mechanisms for this class of models.

A central theme is the **"hardware lottery" debate**, which questions whether the future of AI hardware should continue to solely optimize for the GPU/TPU-Transformer-backpropagation paradigm or explore alternative computational approaches. This paper explicitly embraces an "extreme view" by positing that algorithms and hardware should not be distinguished, suggesting that some SSMs could be *inherently mapped onto dynamical physical systems* to become "self-learning" machines. This places the work squarely within the burgeoning field of **analog AI, physical neural networks, and neuromorphic computing**, aiming to develop energy-efficient, domain-specific hardware for AI.

The core challenge addressed is **temporal credit assignment** – how to efficiently compute gradients for training models that process sequential data over long durations. Traditional methods face significant limitations:
- **Backpropagation Through Time (BPTT):** While effective, it requires storing the entire forward pass history, leading to high memory costs for long sequences.
- **Real-Time Recurrent Learning (RTRL):** A forward-mode automatic differentiation (AD) technique that avoids backward passes but suffers from cubic memory complexity with respect to the number of neurons, making it unscalable for large networks. Approximations for RTRL often introduce bias or variance.
- **Zeroth-order methods:** These methods, which approximate gradients by perturbing parameters, are generally inefficient due to high computational cost or statistical limitations like excessive variance or bias.

This paper takes inspiration from **Hamiltonian Echo Backpropagation (HEB)**, which demonstrated a physics-grounded way to compute gradients in non-dissipative systems. However, HEB was limited in its applicability to standard machine learning tasks, primarily operating on static inputs and not explicitly designed for time-series or hierarchical models. The current work generalizes this concept to **Recurrent Hamiltonian Echo Learning (RHEL)**, aiming to overcome these limitations and provide a scalable "forward-only" proxy for backward-mode AD, which is crucial for physical implementations where a "backward pass" might not be physically realizable.

Furthermore, the work aligns with the broader movement of **Hamiltonian-based machine learning**, which applies principles from Hamiltonian mechanics to design physics-informed neural networks, memory-efficient models, generative models, and optimization algorithms. By focusing on non-dissipative Hamiltonian systems, the authors position their work within a class of models with inherent energy conservation and time-reversal symmetry, which they exploit for gradient computation. This differentiates it from approaches that simulate learning in dissipative physical systems, which often rely on implicit differentiation and can incur high simulation times.

In essence, this research seeks to push the boundaries of energy-efficient, physically embodied AI by developing a robust and scalable learning algorithm compatible with non-conventional, Hamiltonian computing substrates for dynamic, sequential data.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is profoundly ambitious: to **inform the design of scalable, energy-efficient physical systems endowed with self-learning capabilities for sequence modeling.** This overarching goal aims to bridge the gap between abstract AI algorithms and their concrete realization in physical hardware, fostering a new generation of "self-learning machines."

To achieve this, the authors are driven by several specific motivations and objectives:

1. **Develop a dedicated learning algorithm based on physical principles:** The core motivation is to create an algorithm that inherently respects physical principles, specifically for non-dissipative, Hamiltonian dynamical systems, which can naturally embody RNNs. This is crucial for guiding the design and simulation of novel hardware architectures.
2. **Efficient and hardware-friendly gradient computation:** The paper aims to propose an algorithm that can provably compute loss gradients efficiently. Specifically, it must:
 - Require only a few "forward passes" (ideally three, as RHEL does) regardless of model size.
 - Avoid explicit Jacobian computations, which are computationally expensive and memory-intensive (a key limitation of RTRL).
 - Not incur any variance in gradient estimation, a common pitfall for zeroth-order methods.
 - Emulate backward-mode automatic differentiation (like BPTT) but *forward in time*, to be compatible with physical systems that cannot easily reverse their dynamics or perform explicit backward passes.
3. **Overcome limitations of Hamiltonian Echo Backprop (HEB):** While HEB provided foundational inspiration, it had several practical limitations that hindered its broader adoption in ML:
 - It was derived with specialized theoretical physics tools, making it difficult to integrate with standard ML frameworks.
 - Its theory assumed model parameters were dynamical variables, which is inconsistent with standard RNN parametrization.
 - Its scalability to discrete-time SSMs, sequential data with losses at all timesteps, and hierarchical recurrent units was unclear.
 - It was only evaluated on small, static problems (e.g., XOR, MNIST). This paper aims to generalize HEB's principles using standard ML tools to address these shortcomings.
4. **Establish theoretical equivalence with standard ML algorithms:** A critical objective is to formally demonstrate that RHEL is equivalent, in the limit of small perturbations, to established gradient computation methods: the continuous adjoint state method for continuous-time systems and Backpropagation Through Time (BPTT) for discrete-time systems. This provides a strong theoretical foundation and allows for direct comparison.
5. **Demonstrate practical scalability and performance:** The authors aim to empirically prove RHEL's effectiveness by applying it to train Hamiltonian State Space Models (HSSMs) with both linear and nonlinear dynamics. This includes tackling a variety of long-range time-series tasks, with sequence lengths up to approximately 50,000, and showing that RHEL matches the performance of BPTT across these benchmarks.

In summary, the motivation is to develop a robust, scalable, and physically-grounded learning algorithm that can serve as a cornerstone for future energy-efficient, self-learning hardware for complex sequence modeling tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this paper is a rigorous, multi-faceted approach that integrates principles from Hamiltonian mechanics, control theory, and machine learning to develop and validate the Recurrent Hamiltonian Echo Learning (RHEL) algorithm.

1. **Physics-Grounded Model Formulation:**
 - **Continuous-time Hamiltonian Systems:** The foundational model represents neurons as a system of positions ($\phi$) and momenta ($\pi$), collectively denoted as $\Phi$. These systems evolve according to Hamiltonian dynamics (Eq. 1), ensuring energy conservation and, crucially, time-reversal symmetry. The Hamiltonian $H$ can depend on the state $\Phi$, parameters $\theta$, and input $u(t)$.
 - **Learning as Constrained Optimization:** The learning problem is framed as minimizing a loss function $L$ (defined over the entire trajectory) subject to the constraint that the system's evolution adheres to the Hamiltonian dynamics (Eq. 3). This formulation is more general than prior work like HEB, which often focused on learning initial conditions or had losses only at the final timestep.

2. **Recurrent Hamiltonian Echo Learning (RHEL) Algorithm:**
 - **Core Principle (Time Reversal Symmetry Breaking):** Inspired by HEB, RHEL leverages the time-reversal symmetry of Hamiltonian systems. A free forward trajectory is executed. Then, for the "echo" phase, the system's momenta are reversed ($\Phi \rightarrow \Phi^\star$), the inputs are processed backward, and the trajectory is subtly "nudged" by a small perturbation ($\epsilon$) towards the direction of decreasing loss (Eq. 6).
 - **Gradient Estimation:** RHEL computes gradients as finite differences between two such nudged echo trajectories (one with $+\epsilon$ and one with $-\epsilon$) (Eq. 7). This means, in principle, only three forward passes are needed (one free, two nudged echo passes), avoiding explicit Jacobian computations.
 - **Continuous-Time Equivalence:** RHEL is formally shown to be equivalent to the continuous adjoint state method (ASM) in the limit of $\epsilon \to 0$ (Theorem 3.1). This theoretical underpinning connects RHEL to a well-established method for optimal control and sensitivity analysis, validating its gradient estimation. This is numerically demonstrated on a toy model of coupled harmonic oscillators (Fig. 2).

3. **Extension to Discrete Time and Machine Learning Models:**
 - **"Discretize-then-Optimize" Approach:** To make RHEL practical for ML simulations, the authors discretize the Hamiltonian dynamics *before* applying the optimization principles. This ensures better gradient estimation compared to directly discretizing the continuous RHEL theory.
 - **Hamiltonian Recurrent Units (HRUs):** These are defined as discrete-time, symplectic integrators (specifically, the Leapfrog integrator for separable Hamiltonians) of the continuous Hamiltonian flow. The choice of a symplectic integrator is critical as it preserves key properties like time-reversal symmetry and reversibility in discrete time (Lemma A.4, Corollary A.6).
 - **Equivalence with BPTT:** For HRUs, discrete-time RHEL is formally proven to be equivalent to Backpropagation Through Time (BPTT) in the limit of $\epsilon \to 0$ (Theorem 3.2). This is a major result, demonstrating that a physics-inspired, forward-only method can match the exact gradients of standard BPTT.
 - **Hamiltonian State Space Models (HSSMs):** The concept is extended to hierarchical compositions (stacks) of HRUs, forming HSSMs (Eq. 15).
 - **RHEL Chaining for HSSMs:** An algorithm (Alg. 1) is proposed to recursively apply RHEL backward through layers of HSSMs, allowing for gradient computation in deep, hierarchical sequence models (Theorem 3.3). This involves computing not only parameter gradients but also input gradients for subsequent layers.

4. **Empirical Validation:**
 - **Model Instantiations:** Two types of HRUs are implemented:
 - **Linear HRU:** Based on a Hamiltonian that leads to linear dynamics, allowing for parallel scan optimization (Eq. 17).
 - **Nonlinear HRU:** Based on a Hamiltonian inspired by UniCORRN, designed to mitigate vanishing/exploding gradients in nonlinear dynamics (Eq. 18).
 - **Static Gradient Comparison:** A sanity check compares RHEL-computed gradients directly against BPTT-computed gradients on randomly sampled data for both linear and nonlinear HSSMs. Metrics like cosine similarity and norm ratio are used (Fig. 4).
 - **Training Experiments:** RHEL is applied to train HSSMs on diverse time-series tasks:
 - **Classification:** Six multivariate datasets from the UEA archive, with sequence lengths up to ~18,000 (Table 1).
 - **Regression:** The PPG-DaLiA dataset, a challenging long-range benchmark with sequence lengths up to ~50,000 (Table 2).
 - **Numerical Stability (Gradient Rescaling):** A practical solution involving a gradient scaling factor ($\gamma$) is introduced and empirically evaluated to address numerical underflow issues that can arise with very small $\epsilon$ in finite-precision arithmetic (Appendix A.4.4, Fig. 5, 6).

The methodology is comprehensive, moving from continuous theoretical foundations to discrete algorithmic implementations, rigorous mathematical proofs, and extensive empirical validation across varied tasks and model complexities.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper presents compelling theoretical and empirical results that validate Recurrent Hamiltonian Echo Learning (RHEL) as a viable and scalable gradient estimation algorithm for physics-grounded sequence models.

1. **Theoretical Equivalence of RHEL with Standard Gradient Methods:**
 - **Continuous-time Equivalence (Theorem 3.1):** RHEL is formally proven to compute the exact same gradients as the continuous adjoint state method (ASM) in the limit of infinitesimal nudging strength ($\epsilon \to 0$). This establishes RHEL's mathematical correctness from a control theory perspective. The numerical validation on a toy model of coupled harmonic oscillators (Fig. 2) visually confirms this equivalence, showing near-perfect alignment between RHEL and ASM gradient estimates over time.
 - **Discrete-time Equivalence (Theorem 3.2 & 3.3):** Extending the theory to the discrete domain, RHEL is proven to be equivalent to Backpropagation Through Time (BPTT) when applied to Hamiltonian Recurrent Units (HRUs) and hierarchies of HRUs (Hamiltonian State Space Models, HSSMs). This is a crucial finding, as it means RHEL provides a "forward-only" mechanism that yields exact BPTT-equivalent gradients, addressing a major challenge in hardware-friendly learning.

2. **Static Gradient Comparison (Sanity Check):**
 - **Near-Perfect Matching (Fig. 4):** Empirical comparisons of RHEL and BPTT gradients (cosine similarity and norm ratio) on both linear and nonlinear HSSMs demonstrate a near-perfect match for all parameters across all layers. Cosine similarities are consistently close to 1.0, and norm ratios are close to 1.0, empirically validating the theoretical equivalence. This static analysis is critical for building confidence in the algorithm's correctness before full training experiments.

3. **Performance Matching in Training Experiments:**
 - **Classification Tasks (Table 1):** RHEL-trained linear and nonlinear HSSMs consistently match the performance of BPTT-trained models across six multivariate time-series classification datasets from the UEA benchmark. Sequence lengths ranged from ~400 to ~18,000. While RHEL sometimes showed slight average underperformance (e.g., 62.9% vs. 63.4% for linear HSSMs, 57.8% vs. 58.8% for nonlinear HSSMs), this gap was generally small and statistically significant only in limited cases. This demonstrates RHEL's ability to learn complex long-range dependencies effectively.
 - **Regression & Scalability (Table 2):** On the challenging PPG-DaLiA dataset, a long-range regression task with sequence lengths of ~50,000, RHEL again matched BPTT performance for both linear and nonlinear HSSMs. The mean-square error for RHEL was very close to BPTT (e.g., 9.5 vs. 9.1 for linear, 8.4 vs. 7.8 for nonlinear, all $\times 10^{-2}$). This result is particularly significant as it demonstrates RHEL's scalability to very long sequences, a common bottleneck for other forward-only methods.

4. **Numerical Stability and Gradient Rescaling (Appendix A.4.4, Fig. 5 & 6):**
 - The authors identified that RHEL's finite difference gradient estimation can suffer from numerical underflow when the nudging strength $\epsilon$ is very small, especially when perturbations and state values differ significantly in magnitude.
 - They demonstrated that a simple gradient rescaling method, where the loss is multiplied by a factor $\gamma$ during echo dynamics and then divided back, effectively mitigates these underflow issues. For both linear and nonlinear HSSMs, an optimal $\gamma$ value recovered near-perfect gradient matching with BPTT, highlighting that previous slight performance gaps might be attributable to these numerical instabilities rather than fundamental algorithmic limitations.

In summary, the results firmly establish RHEL as a theoretically sound and empirically effective algorithm for training complex, deep sequence models based on Hamiltonian dynamics, capable of matching the performance of standard BPTT while adhering to principles compatible with future physical computing substrates.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work holds significant implications across theoretical machine learning, novel computing paradigms, and the development of energy-efficient AI hardware.

1. **Enabling Scalable Physical Learning Systems:** The most profound impact lies in its contribution to the quest for "self-learning physical systems." By providing a theoretically sound and empirically proven algorithm (RHEL) for training Hamiltonian systems as sequence models, the paper lays crucial groundwork for designing intrinsically intelligent, energy-efficient analog hardware. This could revolutionize AI by moving computation from abstract digital operations to physical dynamics, potentially leading to substantial gains in energy efficiency and speed for specific tasks.

2. **A Viable Alternative to Backpropagation:** RHEL offers a compelling alternative to traditional backpropagation (BPTT) for sequence models. Its "forward-only" nature, requiring just three passes regardless of model size, bypasses the memory-intensive storage of intermediate activations that plagues BPTT for long sequences. Furthermore, it avoids the explicit Jacobian computations of RTRL and the high variance/bias issues of zeroth-order methods, making it highly attractive for scenarios where a backward pass is difficult to implement physically or computationally expensive.

3. **Advancing Temporal Credit Assignment for Novel Hardware:** The ability of RHEL to handle "long-range dependencies" (up to 50,000 sequence length) is a critical achievement. Many alternative learning algorithms struggle with scalability, often confined to small, static problems. RHEL's performance parity with BPTT on challenging benchmarks demonstrates that physics-grounded learning can be competitive for real-world, dynamic tasks, expanding the scope of what novel computing paradigms can achieve.

4. **Bridging Disciplinary Gaps:** The paper elegantly connects the continuous adjoint state method (from optimal control theory and physics) with Backpropagation Through Time (from machine learning) through the lens of Hamiltonian systems and time-reversal symmetry. This theoretical bridge provides a deeper understanding of gradient computation and opens avenues for cross-pollination of ideas between these disciplines.

5. **Guiding Future Hardware Design:** RHEL provides concrete algorithmic requirements that can guide the design of future analog hardware, such as on-chip photonic circuits, superconducting circuits, or spintronic systems. The emphasis on non-dissipative Hamiltonian dynamics dictates specific physical properties needed for the substrate to efficiently implement RHEL, fostering a co-design approach where algorithms and hardware evolve synergistically.

6. **Paving the Way for New Research Directions:** The authors openly discuss the current limitations and future directions, such as extending RHEL to dissipative systems (which would increase model expressivity), making the algorithm fully "black-box" (i.e., not requiring explicit Hamiltonian parameter gradients), enabling online learning, and further improving memory efficiency. This transparency indicates that RHEL is a foundational step, opening up a rich research agenda for the scientific machine learning community.

In conclusion, this work is a significant stride towards realizing energy-efficient, self-learning AI systems by offering a scalable, physics-grounded learning algorithm that can effectively train complex sequence models. It challenges the conventional wisdom of hardware design in AI and provides a robust theoretical and empirical foundation for exploring novel computational substrates.
