# Visual Spatial Reasoning

> [!summary] Summary
> VSR, a dataset for evaluating visual spatial reasoning in natural image-text pairs, reveals a substantial gap between Vision-and-Language Model performance and human capabilities, with state-of-the-art models achieving around 70% accuracy compared to human 95.4%.

## Problem

- Existing generic Vision-and-Language (V&L) benchmarks often conflate various linguistic and visual challenges, making it difficult to isolate and diagnose specific VLM failures in spatial reasoning.
- Prior studies indicate that state-of-the-art VLMs struggle with relational information, especially nuanced spatial relations, performing much worse than humans.
- Synthetic datasets simplify real-world complexity and linguistic diversity, while natural image datasets lack the controlled focus or specific probing capabilities required for fine-grained spatial understanding.

## Method

- Introduces ==Visual Spatial Reasoning (VSR)==, a large-scale diagnostic dataset of ==10,972 natural image-text pairs== derived from MS COCO, specifically designed to evaluate VLMs' spatial reasoning abilities.
- Employs a ==two-phase human annotation process==: contrastive template-based caption generation ensures diverse and challenging spatial descriptions, followed by multi-annotator validation for quality control and consistency.
- The dataset design incorporates linguistic complexities such as ==varying reference frames==, allowing for targeted analysis of how models interpret spatial language in context.

## Results

- State-of-the-art VLMs (e.g., LXMERT **70.1%**, ViLT **69.3%**) achieve significantly lower accuracy on VSR compared to the human ceiling of **95.4%**, highlighting a **>25 percentage point** performance gap.
- Model performance drops considerably in the concept zero-shot setting (best ViLT **63.0%**), indicating poor generalization of spatial reasoning to unseen visual concepts.
- Models show particular weaknesses in understanding orientation-dependent relations (e.g., "facing") and struggle to infer the correct reference frame (intrinsic vs. relative), though learning latent reference frame features can improve performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Current Vision-and-Language Models (VLMs) demonstrate a substantial gap in spatial reasoning capabilities compared to humans, despite advancements in other vision-language tasks.
> - Explicit positional encoding and object-level features are crucial for VLM performance on spatial reasoning tasks, as models lacking these components perform significantly worse.
> - VLMs particularly struggle with spatial relations involving object orientation and generalize poorly on context-dependent relations like proximity when faced with unseen concepts.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of multimodal NLP, particularly vision-and-language (V&L) research, has seen rapid advancements in recent years, leading to substantial performance gains in tasks such as visual question answering (VQA), vision-language reasoning/entailment, and referring expression comprehension. Benchmarks like VQA, NLVR2, and Visual Genome have driven much of this progress. However, the authors argue that these existing benchmarks, while generic, are often insufficient for deeply probing specific linguistic or cognitive capabilities of VLMs. They tend to conflate multiple sources of error, making it difficult to precisely diagnose model failures.

Specifically, previous research (e.g., Kuhnle et al., 2018; Cirik et al., 2018; Akula et al., 2020; Subramanian et al., 2022) has highlighted that spatial reasoning remains particularly challenging for state-of-the-art models, even more so than recognizing individual entities. This difficulty persists because spatial relations are expressed in natural language in diverse and often ambiguous ways, involving complex linguistic phenomena like varying reference frames (intrinsic, relative, absolute).

Another line of work involves synthetic datasets, such as CLEVR and ShapeWorld, which offer controlled environments for testing specific relations. While useful for isolation, these datasets often oversimplify real-world complexity, omitting crucial aspects like object orientation or scene context, and typically cover a limited number of relations. Natural image datasets like MS COCO or Visual Genome contain spatial relations but either lack negative examples, conflate different linguistic challenges, or rely on restrictive rule-based extraction that misses the nuances of human language. More recent benchmarks like VALSE provide zero-shot probing for linguistic phenomena but are limited in scale for comprehensive probing.

The VSR dataset presented in this paper addresses these limitations by providing a controlled, large-scale, natural image-text dataset explicitly designed to test VLMs' spatial reasoning capabilities. By focusing solely on spatial relations, VSR allows for a precise diagnosis of model performance in this crucial cognitive area, separating it from other potential sources of error. It leverages natural images from MS COCO to capture the richness of real-world scenarios and linguistic expressions, while carefully incorporating challenging aspects like reference frames that are often overlooked by synthetic datasets. This positions VSR as a critical diagnostic tool, filling a gap in the existing suite of V&L benchmarks, and enabling more targeted research into improving VLMs' grounded spatial understanding.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce Visual Spatial Reasoning (VSR), a novel dataset specifically designed to probe and evaluate the spatial reasoning capabilities of Vision-and-Language Models (VLMs). The authors are motivated by several key observations and gaps in the current multimodal NLP research landscape:

1. **Fundamental Importance of Spatial Reasoning:** Spatial relations are a basic and fundamental part of human cognition, crucial for organizing mental space, making sense of the physical world, and establishing grounded semantic models. For VLMs, understanding spatial relations is essential for true visual intelligence and scene comprehension.
2. **Identified Weakness in Current VLMs:** Despite rapid advancements in multimodal NLP, existing benchmarks and studies have consistently indicated that current VLMs struggle significantly with relational information, particularly spatial relations, performing much worse than humans and often failing to capture nuanced relational understanding.
3. **Limitations of Existing Benchmarks:**
 - **Conflation of Errors:** Generic VQA or vision-language reasoning datasets (e.g., VQA, NLVR2) often combine multiple linguistic and visual challenges within a single task, making it difficult to isolate and diagnose specific sources of model error, such as failures in spatial reasoning versus other forms of linguistic comprehension or visual recognition.
 - **Synthetic Data Simplification:** While controlled, synthetic datasets (e.g., CLEVR, ShapeWorld) inherently simplify the problem space, limiting the diversity of spatial relations, ignoring real-world complexities like object orientations and scene context, and lacking the richness of natural language use.
 - **Lack of Probing Capabilities:** Many datasets are designed for general task performance rather than fine-grained diagnostic probing of specific cognitive abilities.
4. **Linguistic Complexity of Spatial Relations:** Spatial relations in natural language are highly variable and context-dependent, often relying on implicit factors such as reference frames (e.g., intrinsic, relative). Current benchmarks do not adequately capture or test this linguistic complexity.

By creating VSR, the authors aim to provide a controlled yet naturalistic environment for evaluating VLMs. The dataset allows researchers to perform controlled analysis on specific linguistic and cognitive properties related to space, identify precisely where models fail, and ultimately guide the development of more robust and human-like VLMs capable of sophisticated spatial reasoning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The VSR dataset creation involved a two-phase human annotation process designed to generate high-quality, controlled, natural image-text pairs focusing on spatial relations.

**Phase 1: Contrastive Template-based Caption Generation**
This phase aimed to elicit diverse and challenging spatial descriptions by making annotators focus on differentiating images based on spatial relationships.
1. **Image Pair Sampling:**
 - Images were sampled from MS COCO 2017 (train and validation sets), a widely used dataset of natural images.
 - For each instance, two "concepts" (objects) were randomly selected (e.g., "cat" and "laptop").
 - Images containing these two concepts were retrieved. To avoid ambiguity, images with multiple instances of either concept or instances with very small pixel areas (< 30,000) were filtered out.
 - Annotators were then presented with a pair of such filtered images.
2. **Template-based Caption Generation:**
 - Annotators were given a caption template: "The ENT1 (is) ____ the ENT2." where ENT1 and ENT2 were the two sampled concepts.
 - They had to select a spatial relation from a pre-defined list of 71 options (adapted from Marchi Fagundes et al., 2021) to fill the blank. Examples include "under," "in front of," "facing," "touching." This ensured a controlled vocabulary of spatial relations.
 - The core "contrastive" aspect was that the chosen relation had to make the caption **true for one image** in the pair and **false for the other**. This compelled annotators to identify a salient spatial difference.
 - This process yielded 12,809 raw data points.

**Phase 2: Second-round Human Validation**
This phase ensured the quality and consistency of the generated captions.
1. **Validation Process:** Every generated data point (image-caption pair with its original label) was reviewed by at least three additional human annotators (validators). Validators were asked to independently classify the caption as True or False for the given image, without seeing the original label.
2. **Agreement Threshold:** Instances were included in the final dataset only if at least two out of the three (or more, if additional validators were used) validators agreed with the original label. This resulted in 10,972 final data points.
3. **Design Choice on Reference Frames:** A crucial instruction given to validators was regarding "reference frames." For relations like "left"/"right" or "in front of"/"behind," which can be interpreted differently based on the observer's viewpoint (relative frame) or an object's inherent orientation (intrinsic frame), validators were instructed to assign "True" if the caption was correct under *either* the intrinsic or the relative frame. A "False" label was assigned only if the caption was incorrect under *both* frames. This design acknowledges the complexity of human spatial language and ensures consistency, but also implicitly makes the task more challenging for models that may not robustly handle frame ambiguity.

**Annotator Hiring and Organization:**
- Annotators were recruited via prolific.co, requiring a bachelor's degree, English fluency, and a high approval rate. They were paid £12/hour.
- Tasks were batched to ensure quality control and prevent fatigue, with distinct groups of annotators for generation (24 unique individuals) and validation (45 unique individuals). Annotators came from diverse demographic backgrounds, with a majority being native English speakers from the UK, South Africa, and Ireland.

**Dataset Splits:**
The final 10,972 data points were split in two ways:
1. **Random Split:** A standard 70/10/20 train/dev/test split, containing 7,680, 1,097, and 2,195 examples respectively.
2. **Concept Zero-Shot Split:** A more challenging split where the concepts (objects) appearing in the train set do not appear in the dev or test sets. This tests models' ability to generalize spatial reasoning to unseen concepts compositionally. This reduced the dataset size to 5,560 examples (4,713 train, 231 dev, 616 test).

**Human Ceiling and Agreement:**
A human ceiling was established by having two additional annotators label 500 randomly sampled test examples. They achieved 95.4% accuracy, with a Fleiss' kappa of 0.895, indicating near-perfect inter-annotator agreement on the validated dataset.

**Dataset Analysis:**
The paper also provides analysis of the collected data, including:
- **Relation Distribution:** Showing a long-tailed distribution, with "touching," "behind," "in front of," "on," "under" being most frequent. 66 out of 71 relations were used.
- **Concept Distribution:** Largely following MS COCO's distribution, with "cat," "dog," "person," "dining table" being common.
- **Annotator Disagreement:** Analysis of instances where annotators disagreed revealed that while some were annotation errors, a significant portion (around 70%) were genuinely ambiguous due to differing interpretations or the vagueness of certain relations (e.g., "at the side of," "near"). Part-whole and in/out relations showed the least disagreement.
- **Reference Frame Case Study:** An explicit labeling of left/right relations showed that 84% of true statements were interpreted under a relative frame, consistent with standard English usage. However, the involvement of a "person" in the caption statistically significantly increased the likelihood (from 9% to 16%) of using an intrinsic reference frame, highlighting the subtle cognitive factors influencing spatial language.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The authors conducted experiments on the VSR dataset using four popular Vision-and-Language Models (VLMs): VisualBERT, LXMERT, ViLT, and CLIP.

**Overall Model Performance:**
- **Significant Gap to Human Performance:** The human ceiling for the VSR task is high at 95.4% accuracy. In stark contrast, even the best-performing VLM, LXMERT, achieved only 70.1% accuracy on the random split. ViLT followed closely at 69.3%. VisualBERT performed poorly at 55.2%, only slightly better than chance. The off-the-shelf CLIP model, used with prompt engineering without finetuning, scored around 56.0%. This highlights a substantial gap (over 25 percentage points) between human and model capabilities in spatial reasoning.
- **Impact of Zero-Shot Setting:** On the more challenging concept zero-shot split (where concepts in test set are unseen during training), all models experienced a significant performance drop. ViLT, the best, only reached 63.0%, and LXMERT was at 61.2%. This indicates that current VLMs struggle to disentangle representations of concepts and relations, having poor generalization abilities to unseen visual concepts.
- **Sensitivity to Random Seeds:** The reported performance metrics include standard deviations (e.g., ±0.9 for ViLT), indicating that model performance can fluctuate by a few percentage points across different runs, emphasizing the need for multiple runs for reliable evaluation.

**Analysis of Model Architectures and Capabilities:**
- **Importance of Positional Information:** LXMERT and ViLT consistently outperformed VisualBERT by large margins (over 10%). The authors attribute this to the former two models explicitly encoding positional information. LXMERT incorporates object-level bounding box features and coordinates, while ViLT uses positional embeddings for image patches. VisualBERT, lacking explicit positional encoding, performs much worse, reinforcing previous findings on the criticality of spatial grounding for VLMs.
- **CLIP's Weakness in Spatial Reasoning:** The off-the-shelf CLIP model, despite its strong performance in other zero-shot vision-language tasks, performed only slightly better than random chance (around 55%) on VSR. This echoes previous research that CLIP, while excellent at concept alignment, struggles with fine-grained relational understanding, especially spatial reasoning.

**Performance by Relation Type:**
- **No Correlation with Frequency:** Counter-intuitively, the performance on specific spatial relations showed little correlation with their frequency in the training data. This suggests that low performance on certain relations is not merely due to insufficient training examples but inherent difficulty.
- **Orientation Relations are Particularly Challenging:** Relations requiring an understanding of object orientation (e.g., "facing," "facing away from," "parallel to," "at the back of") were found to be exceptionally hard for VLMs. Models often failed to correctly infer the front or back of objects, even for common items like a hair drier or a person. This indicates a limitation in models' ability to perceive 3D properties or canonical orientations of objects.
- **Proximity Relations in Zero-Shot:** The "Proximity" category (e.g., "close to," "near," "far from") experienced a drastic performance drop in the zero-shot setting, falling to near-chance levels. The authors hypothesize that the notion of proximity is highly dependent on the specific concepts involved and their typical physical contexts (e.g., "near" a train vs. "near" an oven), which models cannot infer for unseen concepts.
- **Other Failure Modes:** Beyond specific relation types, models also struggled with instances requiring complex visual reasoning (e.g., understanding reflections in mirrors, as shown in Fig. 11) or common sense inferences about object typical use or movement.

**Case Study on Reference Frames (Model Perspective):**
- **Difficulty in Reference Frame Prediction:** The authors trained a ViLT model to predict the human-annotated reference frame (intrinsic vs. relative) for left/right relations. The model achieved poor performance (F1-score of 56.9%), indicating that identifying the underlying reference frame is an extremely challenging task for current VLMs, likely due to the need for sophisticated 3D viewpoint simulation.
- **Positive Transfer to VSR:** Crucially, initializing the VSR task model (for left/right relations) with a checkpoint trained on the reference frame prediction task led to a clear positive transfer, improving accuracy from 54.2% to 59.2%. This suggests that while direct reference frame prediction is hard, learning latent features related to reference frame understanding is beneficial for the overall spatial reasoning task.

**Sample Efficiency:**
- VLMs showed reasonable few-shot learning capabilities, with LXMERT performing well even with 100 training examples.
- However, performance plateaus were observed, particularly for the zero-shot split, suggesting that simply increasing data quantity beyond a certain point does not overcome the inherent difficulties in spatial reasoning for current architectures.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Visual Spatial Reasoning (VSR) dataset and its accompanying analysis represent a significant contribution to the field of multimodal AI, with several key implications and potential impacts:

1. **Diagnostic Benchmark for Spatial Reasoning:** VSR fills a critical gap by providing a targeted, controlled, and large-scale diagnostic benchmark for spatial reasoning in natural image-text pairs. Unlike previous datasets, VSR is explicitly designed to isolate and probe this fundamental cognitive ability, allowing researchers to precisely identify strengths and weaknesses of VLMs without conflating other sources of error. This specificity is invaluable for advancing model design.
2. **Highlighting Core VLM Limitations:** The paper rigorously demonstrates a substantial performance gap (over 25%) between state-of-the-art VLMs and human capabilities in spatial reasoning. This underscores that despite impressive progress in other V&L tasks, current models lack a deep, grounded understanding of space, a crucial aspect of embodied intelligence.
3. **Identifying Specific Failure Modes:** The detailed by-relation analysis points to specific categories of spatial relations that are inherently challenging for VLMs, notably:
 - **Orientation Relations:** The poor performance on "facing," "parallel to," etc., highlights a fundamental limitation in VLMs' ability to perceive or infer object orientations and 3D properties from 2D images. This calls for future research into models that can better represent or reason about 3D space.
 - **Context-Dependent Relations (e.g., Proximity):** The significant drop in performance for "Proximity" relations in the zero-shot setting indicates that models struggle to generalize these context-dependent notions to unseen concepts. This suggests a need for VLMs that can learn more abstract, transferable representations of spatial concepts independent of specific objects.
 - **Reference Frames:** The case study on reference frames reveals that VLMs find it challenging to correctly interpret statements requiring intrinsic vs. relative viewpoints, but also that learning these distinctions can improve performance. This paves the way for models that explicitly incorporate mechanisms for handling diverse reference frames.
4. **Guiding Future Model Development:** The findings provide concrete directions for future VLM research:
 - **Positional Encoding:** The superior performance of LXMERT and ViLT reaffirms the critical importance of explicit and robust positional encoding (e.g., bounding box features, patch embeddings) for spatial reasoning tasks.
 - **Beyond Correlation-Based Learning:** The lack of correlation between relation frequency and performance suggests that models might be over-relying on statistical co-occurrences rather than learning genuine relational understanding. Future architectures need to build more robust compositional and relational reasoning capabilities.
 - **Scaling Up vs. Architectural Innovation:** While performance improved with more data, the plateau, especially in zero-shot, indicates that simply scaling current architectures might not resolve all spatial reasoning challenges. New architectural designs or pretraining objectives might be necessary to capture these nuances.
5. **Facilitating Cross-Cultural and Sociolinguistic Research:** The comprehensive annotation process, including the collection of annotator metadata and the analysis of disagreement (including genuinely ambiguous cases), creates a valuable resource for cross-lingual and cross-cultural studies. Researchers can leverage VSR to investigate how "space" is described and perceived across different dialects of English or potentially different populations, bridging computational linguistics with cognitive science and sociolinguistics.
6. **Benchmarking Next-Generation VLMs:** VSR will be a crucial benchmark for evaluating the spatial reasoning capabilities of increasingly large and powerful VLMs like Flamingo, PaLI, and LiT, providing insights into whether scale alone can address these complex challenges. A comparison of dual- and cross-encoder architectures on VSR could also guide future model design.

In conclusion, VSR is not just another dataset; it's a strategically designed probing tool that shines a light on some of the most profound limitations of current VLMs concerning grounded spatial understanding. Its insights will be instrumental in fostering the development of truly intelligent, perceptually grounded AI systems.
