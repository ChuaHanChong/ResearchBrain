# DenoiseRL: Bootstrapping Reasoning Models to Recover from Noisy Prefixes

> [!summary] Summary
> The DenoiseRL framework enhances large language model reasoning by training models to recover from noisy reasoning prefixes generated by weaker models, thus circumventing the need for stronger teacher models or extensive data curation. This approach led to consistent performance gains on mathematical and general reasoning benchmarks, with DenoiseRL-DAPO improving average scores for Qwen3-8B-Base from 42.8% to 44.8%.

## Problem

- Advanced reinforcement learning methods for large language model (LLM) reasoning are often constrained by the availability of stronger teacher models or the high cost of curating difficult datasets.
- Standard RL training for LLMs does not explicitly teach models how to recover from erroneous intermediate reasoning steps, leaving robust self-correction capabilities underdeveloped.
- On-policy RL can face an exploration bottleneck, limiting the diversity of learning signals and potentially hindering further capability improvements once a policy is reasonably performant.

## Method

- **DenoiseRL** introduces a framework where a policy is trained to "denoise" and correctly complete reasoning trajectories starting from incorrect partial solutions (noisy prefixes) sampled from a weak model.
- The approach augments standard RL training by incorporating both "main rollouts" (solving from scratch) and "denoise rollouts" (recovering from noisy prefixes) into a joint objective.
- During denoise rollouts, gradients are only applied to the model's generated continuation, with off-policy noisy prefix tokens masked from the PPO loss to ensure training stability.

## Results

- DenoiseRL consistently improved reasoning performance, with DenoiseRL-DAPO on Qwen3-8B-Base achieving an average score of **44.8%**, up from its **42.8%** baseline across mathematical and general reasoning benchmarks.
- Experiments showed an optimal balance with a prefix ratio of ρ=0.2 and 4 denoise rollouts (K=4K=4K=4) per problem, yielding the highest average gain (+**16.3%** over the base model for Qwen3-4B-Base).
- Ablation studies confirmed that enabling gradient flow through off-policy prefix tokens leads to training instability and performance collapse, while an unfairly large output budget for recovery also reduced performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly training models to recover from mistakes (denoising noisy prefixes) fosters a stronger self-corrective capability, enhancing overall reasoning robustness.
> - Leveraging readily available weak model failures as structured perturbations provides a scalable, low-cost mechanism for generating diverse and informative learning signals, thereby overcoming exploration limitations.
> - Optimal performance hinges on carefully tuning the intensity of noise (prefix ratio) and the proportion of denoise rollouts, as excessive noise or over-emphasis on recovery can hinder overall learning efficiency.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become a prominent paradigm for enhancing the reasoning capabilities of large language models (LLMs) following their initial training. Existing state-of-the-art RL-based methods for improving LLM reasoning, such as those discussed in, typically rely on external supervision or guidance. This supervision often originates from stronger "teacher" models or necessitates the use of extensively curated and difficult datasets. This dependence introduces a structural limitation: the ability to achieve further capability improvements becomes constrained when a sufficiently powerful teacher model is unavailable, or when the cost and effort of data curation become prohibitive. This limitation raises a fundamental challenge regarding the scalable improvement of LLMs for complex reasoning tasks.

Prior research has explored two primary avenues to mitigate this dependence. The first is the "weak-to-strong generalization" (W2SG) paradigm, where weaker models generate supervision signals to improve stronger "student" models. While demonstrating practical effectiveness, the performance ceiling of this approach is inherently limited by the quality of the weak teacher's signal, which can introduce noise into the training process. The second direction focuses on increasing task difficulty through advanced data construction techniques. These include synthesizing harder problems, generating adversarial examples, or creating longer reasoning trajectories. However, these methods typically require complex, engineered pipelines, meticulous filtering and verification procedures, and significant human intervention in data design and curation. This reliance on external resources and manual effort restricts their scalability.

Another line of work involves prefix-conditioned and off-policy exploration in RL. These methods aim to improve exploration efficiency by injecting external prefixes, expert solutions, oracle hints, or successful traces into RL training. For instance, LUFFY mixes off-policy reasoning traces, and PrefixRL conditions on successful off-policy prefixes. These approaches leverage existing correct or useful information to guide the model.

DenoiseRL positions itself within this landscape by seeking to unify aspects of weak-to-strong learning with difficulty-driven data synthesis, while addressing the limitations of relying on external supervision or extensive data curation. It diverges from previous prefix-conditioned methods by utilizing incorrect weak-model prefixes not as demonstrations or privileged hints, but as a source of "misleading reasoning states" from which the policy must actively recover. The work echoes principles from denoising autoencoders and BART-style pretraining, where a model learns to reconstruct a correct output from a corrupted input.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to develop a scalable reinforcement learning framework that can improve the reasoning capabilities of large language models without relying on stronger teacher models or heavily curated, difficult datasets. This objective is driven by several motivations:

1. **Addressing Scalability Limitations in RL for LLMs:** Current advanced RL methods for reasoning often necessitate access to models that are already more capable than the one being trained, acting as supervisors or teachers. When the frontier of model capability is reached, and no "stronger" teacher is readily available, further improvements become challenging. This creates a bottleneck for continuous capability scaling. Similarly, methods relying on manually curated difficult datasets are labor-intensive and expensive, limiting their applicability to large-scale, continuous improvement. DenoiseRL aims to circumvent these dependencies by generating learning signals directly from the failures of weak models, thereby enabling more scalable capability gains.

2. **Explicitly Training for Recovery from Errors:** Real-world reasoning processes frequently involve navigating and correcting incorrect intermediate steps. However, standard RL training paradigms typically focus on generating correct solutions from scratch and do not explicitly train models on how to recover from erroneous reasoning states. This can leave a critical aspect of robust reasoning underdeveloped. DenoiseRL is motivated by the idea that intentionally exposing the model to its own or a weak model's mistakes, and then requiring it to correct those mistakes, can foster a stronger self-corrective capability. By converting incorrect reasoning traces into explicit opportunities for improvement, the framework aims to make "self-correction" a direct training target rather than an emergent behavior.

3. **Generating Diverse and Informative Learning Signals:** Standard on-policy RL can suffer from an "exploration bottleneck" where, once a policy becomes reasonably performant, it predominantly generates correct rollouts or a limited set of failure modes. This scarcity of informative failures can hinder further learning. DenoiseRL is motivated by the potential to generate a richer and more diverse set of learning signals. By repurposing weak models as generators of structured perturbations (noisy prefixes), it automatically increases the difficulty of the training task and exposes the policy to a broader space of "off-policy contexts" or failure modes that would rarely be encountered in standard on-policy rollouts. This is intended to improve exploration efficiency and lead to more robust models.

In essence, the paper seeks to answer how strong models can be obtained without relying on even stronger models as supervisors or on labor-intensive data engineering, by leveraging existing model weaknesses as a source of structured learning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

DenoiseRL introduces a reinforcement learning framework designed to train large language models to recover from incorrect intermediate reasoning states. The core approach involves sampling "noisy reasoning prefixes" from a weak model and injecting these into the policy's rollouts. The policy is then optimized to "denoise" these corrupted prefixes and complete the reasoning trajectory correctly.

#### 3.1. Denoising Reasoning Task

The framework defines denoising reasoning as a task where an incorrect partial solution, treated as structured noise, is prepended to the policy's generation. The policy is then trained to continue reasoning from this corrupted state towards the correct answer. This setup serves a dual purpose: it acts as a weak-to-strong perturbation signal and a low-cost mechanism to increase reasoning difficulty.

- **Noisy Prefix Collection:** Candidate solutions are initially sampled from a designated "weak model." These solutions are then subjected to a verifier, and only those judged to be incorrect are retained. This collection of incorrect solutions forms a fixed pool, denoted as $\mathcal{W}(q)$ for each question $q$, which is generated offline prior to RL training, incurring no additional cost during the training steps. If a weak model fails to produce an incorrect answer for a specific problem after a set number of trials, that problem's "denoise slots" are instead filled with standard main rollouts.

#### 3.2. Reinforcement Learning for Recovering from Noisy Prefixes

The training process in DenoiseRL augments standard RL by incorporating "denoise rollouts" alongside "main rollouts" in each training step.

- **Main Rollouts:** For each sampled question $q \sim \mathcal{D}$, $N$ standard on-policy rollouts are generated by the policy model $\pi_{\theta}(\cdot | q)$. These serve as the baseline for learning to solve problems from scratch.

- **Denoise Rollouts:** For each question $q$, $K$ denoise rollouts are generated. An incorrect solution $w \sim \mathcal{W}(q)$ is drawn from the pre-collected pool. A partial noisy prefix $w_{1:p}$ is extracted from $w$, where $p = \max(1, \lfloor \rho |w| \rfloor)$ based on a fixed "prefix-ratio" hyperparameter $\rho \in (0, 1]$. The policy then generates a continuation $y_{>p}$ conditioned on $q$ and $w_{1:p}$.

- **Output Budget and Folding:** To ensure a fair comparison with main rollouts, both types of rollouts adhere to a maximal response length $R$. For denoise rollouts, the generated continuation $y_{>p}$ is truncated to $L = \min(T_{y_{>p}}, R - p)$ tokens, where $T_{y_{>p}}$ is the number of generated tokens. The final "folded" response $\tilde{y} = [w_{1:p}, y_{p+1:p+L}]$ is then evaluated by the verifier, which assigns a reward $r(\tilde{y}; q)$ based on its correctness. Crucially, during training, only the on-policy continuation part $y_{p+1:p+L}$ is updated, meaning gradients do not flow through the off-policy prefix tokens.

- **Token-level GRPO Objective:** DenoiseRL utilizes a Group-Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) advantage function. This advantage is calculated relative to a shared baseline across all $N+K$ trajectories (main and denoise) for the same problem $q$. The advantage $A_i$ for trajectory $i$ is normalized by the mean ($\mu_q$) and standard deviation ($\sigma_q$) of rewards within the group. A per-token importance ratio $r_{i,t}(\theta)$ is computed, comparing the current policy's probability of a token against the old policy's. This ratio is used in a trajectory-level clipped surrogate objective $\mathcal{L}_{\text{PPO}_i}(\theta)$, which applies a clipping range $[\epsilon_{\text{low}}, 1+\epsilon_{\text{high}}]$ to the importance ratio multiplied by the advantage.

- **Joint Objective Over Two Distributions:** The final training objective $\mathcal{J}(\theta)$ is a weighted combination of objectives for the main rollouts and denoise rollouts:
 $\mathcal{J}(\theta) = \frac{N}{N+K} \mathcal{J}_{\text{main}}(\theta) + \frac{K}{N+K} \mathcal{J}_{\text{denoise}}(\theta)$.
 This formulation encourages the model simultaneously to solve problems from scratch and to recover from corrupted intermediate reasoning states. The practical optimization uses a Monte-Carlo estimator, averaging the PPO loss components over a batch of problems and their respective rollouts. The weights $N/(N+K)$ and $K/(N+K)$ determine the balance between the two learning signals.

#### 3.3. Experimental Settings

- **Weak Model:** Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct was used to collect noisy prefixes by sampling 8 rollouts per question on the MATH-7.5K dataset and filtering for incorrect ones.
- **Policy Models:** Qwen3-4B-Base and Qwen3-8B-Base were chosen as the models to be optimized.
- **RL Configuration:** Training involved $N=12$ standard on-policy rollouts and $K=4$ denoise rollouts per problem, with a fixed prefix ratio $\rho=0.2$. A response length of 4096 tokens was used. Hyperparameters included a learning rate of $10^{-6}$, PPO clipping range $\epsilon_{\text{low}}=\epsilon_{\text{high}}=0.2$, and sampling temperature of 1.0 with top-p=1.0 during training.
- **Evaluation:** Models were evaluated on mathematical reasoning benchmarks (MATH500, AMC23, AIME2024, AIME2025) and a general reasoning benchmark (BBEH). Metrics were AVG@16 for competitive contests (AMC, AIME) and AVG@1 for others.
- **Baselines:** Performance was compared against the base model (before RL) and two on-policy RL baselines: GRPO and DAPO.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of DenoiseRL yielded several findings regarding its effectiveness and the influence of its design parameters.

#### 4.1. Overall Performance Improvements

DenoiseRL consistently improved the reasoning performance of both GRPO and DAPO baselines across different model scales and benchmarks.

- **Qwen3-4B-Base:** DenoiseRL-GRPO increased the average score from 39.6% (GRPO baseline) to 42.0%, showing gains across MATH500, AIME2024, AIME2025, and BBEH. DenoiseRL-DAPO improved the DAPO baseline from 39.8% to 41.5%, achieving the best performance on AMC23 and BBEH. DenoiseRL-GRPO achieved the highest overall average for the 4B model.
- **Qwen3-8B-Base:** DenoiseRL-GRPO improved its baseline from 43.0% to 43.3%. DenoiseRL-DAPO demonstrated stronger gains, increasing DAPO's average from 42.8% to 44.8%, and achieving the best result on every evaluated benchmark for the 8B model.

These results indicate that denoise rollouts provide complementary training signals, particularly for more challenging reasoning tasks, and that DenoiseRL is a generally applicable strategy across model sizes and RL backbones.

#### 4.2. Intensity of Noise

The study investigated the impact of two hyperparameters related to noise intensity: the prefix ratio ($\rho$) and the number of denoise rollouts ($K$).

- **Prefix Ratio ($\rho$):** Experiments on Qwen3-4B-Base with GRPO showed that a mild prefix ratio of $\rho=0.2$ resulted in relatively compact average response lengths (approx. 1.38K tokens). In contrast, larger ratios ($\rho=0.5$ and $\rho=0.8$) led to significantly longer and more unstable generations, averaging 3.87K and 2.26K tokens, respectively, often reaching the 4096-token budget. A qualitative analysis suggested that higher $\rho$ induced "overthinking behavior," characterized by endless self-doubt, verification, rewriting, or restarting the derivation process from incorrect intermediate conclusions, rather than directly reaching a solution.
- **Number of Denoise Rollouts ($K$):** Varying $K$ while fixing $\rho=0.2$ showed an optimal balance. With $K=1$, the corrective signal was too sparse, yielding a +14.9% average gain over the base model. With $K=8$ (allocating half of the sampled trajectories to recovery), the average gain was the weakest (+11.9%), suggesting that over-emphasizing recovery can distract from the primary problem-solving objective. The setting $K=4$ achieved the best trade-off, leading to the highest average gain of +16.3%, with notable improvements on harder benchmarks like AIME24 (+16.5%) and AIME25 (+16.9%). This indicates the need to jointly tune recovery intensity with the standard RL objective.

#### 4.3. Off-policy Prefix Masking

An ablation study examined whether updating the off-policy prefix tokens (i.e., allowing gradients to flow through $w_{1:p}$) was beneficial. The default DenoiseRL setup masks these tokens out of the PPO loss. When the prefix tokens' response mask was set to 1, enabling gradient flow, training became unstable. Validation accuracy initially improved but then sharply degraded and collapsed to 0 across all benchmarks by step 400. Simultaneously, mean response length exhibited erratic behavior, first shrinking and then spiking to saturate the maximum budget. This instability was attributed to a large mismatch between the log-probability distributions of the offline noisy prefix under the current and behavior policies, causing noisy and high-variance gradient updates, consistent with prior observations in RL for language models.

#### 4.4. Fairness of Output Budget

The default DenoiseRL setup enforces a "length-fair" output budget, where the combined length of the noisy prefix and the policy's continuation does not exceed the maximal response length $R$ ($p+L \le R$). An ablation where the budget cap was removed for denoise rollouts (allowing up to $p+R$ total tokens) resulted in a weaker overall performance, reducing the average score by 1.8 percentage points (40.2% vs. 42.0%). This suggests that providing an unfairly long recovery budget encourages verbose but less reliable reasoning, and that enforcing equal footing in terms of generation capacity is beneficial.

#### 4.5. Training Time Efficiency

Comparing DenoiseRL-GRPO ($N=12, K=4, \rho=0.2$) with the GRPO baseline ($N=16$ on-policy rollouts) on Qwen3-4B-Base, DenoiseRL was slightly slower per training step (49.7 s vs. 43.8 s). This modest overhead was linked to DenoiseRL generating approximately 1.27 times more continuation tokens on average due to enhanced reasoning and repair capabilities. Despite this, DenoiseRL remained within a comparable cost regime while delivering higher accuracy.

#### 4.6. Case Study: Recovery Behavior

Case studies, such as the example in Table 4, illustrate that DenoiseRL induces genuine denoising and recovery. When presented with a noisy prefix containing a partially correct derivation but an incorrect final answer due to faulty enumeration, the model's continuation did not follow the erroneous conclusion. Instead, it re-checked the core constraint, recomputed the feasible range, and repaired the final answer. Other cases showed the model rejecting misleading probabilistic framings or inefficient search procedures from the prefix, and instead switching to more effective problem-solving strategies (e.g., using Vieta's formulas instead of explicit root search). This suggests that DenoiseRL teaches the model to use weak-model errors as perturbations to reassess the reasoning state, preserve useful partial reasoning, and correct failure modes.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

DenoiseRL presents a framework that offers several contributions to the advancement of reasoning in large language models:

- **Scalable Capability Improvement:** The primary significance of DenoiseRL lies in its ability to improve LLM reasoning capabilities without relying on external, stronger teacher models or labor-intensive, manually curated datasets. By converting readily available weak-model failures into structured learning signals, it provides a more scalable and resource-efficient pathway for continuous model enhancement. This mechanism reduces the dependence on expensive external resources, potentially democratizing access to advanced training techniques.

- **Enhanced Self-Correction and Robustness:** DenoiseRL explicitly trains models to recover from erroneous intermediate reasoning states. This transforms self-correction from an emergent behavior into a direct optimization target, fostering a critical capability for robust reasoning. Models trained with DenoiseRL demonstrate the ability to identify mistakes within a given prefix, revise their reasoning, and steer towards a correct solution, which is valuable for real-world applications where errors can inevitably occur.

- **Diversified Learning Signal:** By injecting noisy prefixes, DenoiseRL continuously reshapes the training distribution, exposing the policy to a broader and more diverse set of failure modes and off-policy contexts than standard on-policy RL. This can mitigate the exploration bottleneck often encountered in RL, leading to a richer learning signal and potentially more robust and generalizable reasoning abilities.

- **Generalizability Across Models and Tasks:** The consistent performance improvements observed across different model scales (Qwen3-4B-Base, Qwen3-8B-Base) and distinct RL backbones (GRPO, DAPO), as well as on a variety of mathematical and general reasoning benchmarks, suggests the broad applicability and generalizability of the DenoiseRL paradigm.

- **Novel Perspective on RL for LLMs:** This work introduces a new conceptualization of RL for reasoning, framing it as a "denoising problem" where weak-model errors serve as structured corruptions of reasoning trajectories. This perspective extends ideas from denoising autoencoders to the domain of sequential reasoning in LLMs, opening new avenues for research into how model mistakes can be systematically leveraged for learning.

**Potential Impact:**
The approach proposed by DenoiseRL has the potential to influence how LLMs are trained for complex tasks, particularly those requiring multi-step reasoning. By making model failures a direct source of learning, it could reduce the need for constantly seeking stronger supervisory models or dedicating significant resources to data curation. This could accelerate the development of more capable and self-correcting reasoning agents. Furthermore, the insights into "overthinking" behavior under strong noise and the importance of output budget fairness provide valuable guidance for future RL system design in language models. While the method's effectiveness still depends on the quality of weak model errors and requires balancing recovery supervision with efficient reasoning, DenoiseRL offers a promising direction for scalable and robust LLM development.
