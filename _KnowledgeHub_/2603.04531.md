# PTLD: Sim-to-real Privileged Tactile Latent Distillation for Dexterous Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> The PTLD framework enables learning dexterous manipulation policies with tactile feedback by distilling real-world privileged sensor information, bypassing the need for accurate tactile sensor simulation. This approach resulted in an average 182% improvement in total rotation for in-hand rotation tasks and a 57% increase in goals reached for continuous reorientation compared to proprioception-only baselines.

## Problem

- Dexterous manipulation with multi-fingered robot hands is inherently complex, making tasks requiring contact-rich interactions difficult to automate.
- Accurately simulating tactile sensors is challenging, leading to a significant sim-to-real gap that hinders the deployment of simulation-trained tactile policies.
- Existing privileged distillation methods often distill to modalities still facing sim-to-real gaps or are limited by the performance ceiling of initial proprioceptive teacher policies, preventing robust real-world performance.

## Method

- **PTLD** introduces a novel approach that uses an ==instrumented real-world setup== to provide ==privileged sensor== data (e.g., object pose from external cameras) to a simulation-trained policy.
- An ==Asymmetric Actor Critic (AAC)== framework with an ==online latent distillation loss== is employed to efficiently train a privileged sensor policy in simulation, streamlining the process from two stages to one.
- A ==tactile encoder== is then trained via supervised learning using collected real-world data, matching real-world tactile observations and proprioception to the latent representations generated by the privileged sensor policy, using ==DAgger== to mitigate distribution shift.

## Results

- PTLD policies demonstrated superior robustness and performance, achieving an average **182%** improvement in total rotation over proprioception-only baselines on the in-hand rotation task.
- For the continuous in-hand reorientation task, PTLD-trained policies with tactile feedback achieved a **57%** increase in goals reached compared to proprioception-only policies.
- Tactile information incorporated into latent representations reduced 6D object rotation prediction errors by approximately **50%** (from **0.43** to **0.21** Radians for absolute pose), confirming its utility for enhanced state estimation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Leveraging real-world, high-fidelity privileged sensor information for latent distillation effectively bypasses the challenges of simulating complex tactile sensors, providing a robust path for sim-to-real transfer.
> - The Asymmetric Actor Critic (AAC) framework combined with an online latent distillation loss allows for efficient single-stage training of powerful privileged teacher policies in simulation.
> - Tactile sensing provides crucial information for object state estimation, significantly reducing errors in 6D rotation prediction and enabling sophisticated recovery behaviors during manipulation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Dexterous manipulation, particularly for human-like tasks in unstructured environments, represents a fundamental challenge in robotics. Current approaches to learning robot policies primarily fall into two categories: Learning from Demonstrations (LfD) and Sim-to-Real Reinforcement Learning (RL).

LfD methods, while offering a scalable recipe for policy learning, encounter significant obstacles when applied to multi-fingered dexterous hands. Obtaining high-quality demonstrations for intricate tasks via teleoperation or kinesthetic teaching proves challenging, often being prohibitive for complex actions like in-hand object reorientation. Specialized hardware like hand-held grippers has shown promise but remains limited to simpler tasks due to the inherent trade-offs between flexibility and stability.

Sim-to-Real RL has achieved notable successes, particularly in robot locomotion, but often relies on proprioception-only policies. While perceptive policies utilizing vision have been developed, they face challenges related to slow image rendering in simulation and a substantial sim-to-real gap that hinders direct deployment.

This paper specifically focuses on tactile dexterous manipulation, a critical component of human dexterity for dynamic tasks such as in-hand rotation and reorientation. Learning effective tactile policies in simulation, however, presents two core difficulties. First, accurate simulation of tactile sensors is challenging, leading existing works to use simplified contact models that fail to capture the rich force and geometry information provided by real sensors. Furthermore, existing simulation packages are not standardized for all tactile sensors and primarily rely on rigid-body mechanics, exacerbating accuracy issues. Second, the sim-to-real gap for tactile sensing is significant, as real-world contact dynamics, sensor hysteresis, and surface properties are difficult to reproduce, making it hard to deploy simulation-trained tactile policies directly.

The presented work, PTLD, positions itself by addressing these simulation and sim-to-real challenges for tactile sensing. It aims to bypass the need for accurate tactile simulation entirely by anchoring sim-to-real transfer on "privileged sensors" and subsequently distilling the knowledge into a tactile-only policy. This approach contrasts with existing tactile adaptation algorithms, which often refine RL policies using real-world data but are constrained by the performance ceilings of their proprioceptive teacher policies and may rely on simpler techniques like rejection sampling. PTLD seeks to overcome these limitations by leveraging privileged sensor policies to learn more robust and dynamic behaviors.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable the learning of effective tactile-enabled robot policies for dexterous manipulation without relying on accurate or realistic tactile simulation. This overarching goal is driven by several key motivations:

1. **Difficulty of Acquiring High-Quality Demonstrations for Multi-fingered Hands:** Human-like dexterous manipulation tasks are complex and often require intricate coordination of multiple fingers. Existing methods for collecting robot demonstrations, such as teleoperation or kinesthetic teaching, become exceptionally challenging or "prohibitive" for multi-fingered hands performing fine-grained tasks like using tools or in-hand object reorientation. This limitation restricts the scalability and applicability of imitation learning for dexterous skills.

2. **Lack of Realistic and Fast Tactile Simulation:** Reinforcement Learning (RL) in simulation offers a promising avenue for learning complex robot behaviors due to its ability to generate vast amounts of data. However, for tactile sensing, a critical component of dexterous manipulation, the development of fast and realistic simulation environments remains a significant challenge. Current simulation efforts often resort to highly simplified contact models (e.g., single-point or binary contact) that fail to capture the detailed force and geometry information provided by real tactile sensors. Furthermore, there is a lack of standardization across simulation packages for different tactile sensor types, and most simulations rely on rigid-body physics which poorly models real-world deformable contacts.

3. **Large Sim-to-Real Gap for Tactile Sensing:** Even if tactile simulation were improved, deploying policies trained in simulation directly to the real world is complicated by a substantial sim-to-real gap. Real-world contact dynamics, sensor hysteresis, and complex surface properties are exceedingly difficult to model accurately in simulation. This disparity prevents straightforward transfer of simulation-trained tactile policies to physical robots, diminishing their practical utility.

To address these motivations, the authors propose PTLD, a novel approach designed to circumvent the need for high-fidelity tactile simulation. The core idea is to leverage the concept of "privileged latent distillation." Instead of simulating tactile sensors, the method trains an "oracle" policy in simulation using privileged state information (e.g., object pose and shape). This privileged policy is then deployed in a real-world instrumented setup where external sensors (acting as real-world privileged sensors) provide the necessary state information. By collecting paired real-world tactile observations and the latent states from this privileged policy, a separate tactile encoder is trained to map real-world touch into the privileged policy's latent space. This process aims to align the internal representation of a deployable tactile policy with that of a high-performing privileged policy, making the tactile policy robust and effective without ever needing to simulate touch directly.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

PTLD (Privileged Tactile Latent Distillation) is structured as a two-stage approach, primarily relying on simulation for policy training and real-world data collection for tactile encoder distillation, thus entirely bypassing tactile simulation.

#### 3.1. Online Latent Distillation with Asymmetric Actor-Critic (AAC)

The first stage of PTLD focuses on training a high-performance "privileged sensor policy" in simulation. Unlike traditional privileged latent distillation which involves two distinct simulation stages (oracle training followed by student distillation), PTLD utilizes an Asymmetric Actor-Critic (AAC) framework for a simplified, single-stage simulation training.

- **AAC Framework**: In this framework, the critic network is granted access to the full, privileged state (`X_priv`) available in simulation, allowing it to learn an accurate value function. Conversely, the actor network, which determines actions, is provided only with partial observations (`X_sensor`). In PTLD, these `X_sensor` are later conceptualized as privileged sensor observations (e.g., object pose) when deploying in the real world for distillation.
- **Online Latent Distillation**: A key innovation is the integration of an online latent distillation loss within the AAC framework. The actor is parameterized with an observation encoder `E` and a policy `π`. A self-distillation representation loss (`L_latent`) is applied between the latent representations learned by the critic's privileged encoder (`ˆE`) and the actor's student encoder (`E`). This loss, `E(X_sensor) - sg(ˆE(X_priv))`, where `sg` denotes stop gradient, encourages the actor's encoder to implicitly recover the rich object information available to the critic, despite operating on partial observations.
- **Training Objective**: The networks are trained simultaneously using the clip variant of Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), augmented with this online latent distillation loss. The total loss `L` is `L_PPO + c_latent * L_latent`, where `c_latent` is a weighting factor. This approach streamlines the simulation phase into a single training step, improving efficiency and performance compared to traditional two-stage distillation.

#### 3.2. Privileged Sensors for Sim-to-Real Tactile Distillation

The core idea for sim-to-real transfer in PTLD involves bridging the gap using privileged sensors as an interface.

- **Real-World Data Collection**: After training the privileged sensor policy (using AAC with object pose and shape as `X_sensor` in simulation), this policy is deployed in a real-world instrumented cell. This cell is equipped with external tracking systems (e.g., multiple cameras and object markers) to provide "real-world privileged sensor" information, specifically noisy object poses (`T_W_t`). Simultaneously, the robot hand, outfitted with tactile sensors, collects real-world tactile observations.
- **Offline Dataset Creation**: During policy rollouts in the real world, an offline dataset is collected consisting of paired tuples: (real-world tactile observation + proprioception, latent representation from the privileged sensor policy). The latent representation from the privileged sensor policy serves as the ground truth for the tactile encoder training.
- **Tactile Encoder Training**: A separate "tactile encoder" is then trained using this collected dataset via supervised learning (MSE loss). This encoder takes real-world tactile sensor data and proprioception as input and aims to output latents that match those produced by the privileged sensor policy.
- **Addressing Distribution Shift (DAgger)**: To mitigate distribution shift, which can occur when an encoder is trained on a static dataset, DAgger (Dataset Aggregation) is employed. This iterative process refines the tactile encoder by periodically collecting new experience using the intermediately trained tactile encoder, adding it to the dataset, and retraining.
- **Deployment**: At test time, the trained tactile encoder replaces the privileged sensor encoder. The final policy then relies exclusively on real-world tactile signals and proprioception, without requiring any external pose tracking.

#### 3.3. Manipulation System and Tasks

- **Hardware**: The system utilizes an Allegro hand equipped with Xela uSkin tactile sensors (18 pads, 368 3-axis sensors). This is mounted on a Franka Panda robot arm. For real-world privileged sensing, four Realsense D435i/D435 RGBD cameras are calibrated to track an Aruco marker on the object, providing multi-view pose estimates refined by Pose Graph Optimization (PGO). Known object shapes are assumed. ROS2 is used for inter-process communication, enabling a policy deployment rate of approximately 20Hz. Simulation is conducted in IsaacGym.
- **Grasp Generation**: For both tasks, stable initial grasps are generated in simulation, similar to prior work, by randomizing joint configurations and retaining stable holds.
- **In-Hand Rotation**: This task requires rotating an object around its z-axis while maintaining grasp. The tactile encoder for this task uses an MLP followed by a 1D temporal convolutional network, processing a 0.5-second history of concatenated tactile observations and sensor positions.
- **In-Hand Reorientation**: This more complex task involves reaching a target object orientation. The adaptation module (`E`) receives Allegro joint states, previous targets, noisy object pose, and goal orientations (6-D representation) with a 30-step history. The privileged encoder (`ˆE`) additionally receives object velocities and full fingertip states. The tactile encoder for reorientation employs a causal Transformer, which acts as a recursive state estimator to autoregressively predict the next latent state based on embedded tactile observations, proprioception, goal orientations, and previous latent states.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper presents several findings regarding the efficacy of PTLD in learning tactile dexterous manipulation policies.

#### 4.1. Privileged Sensors Improve In-Hand Rotation Performance in Simulation

- **Impact of Object Pose**: The integration of object pose information alongside proprioception significantly improved reward accumulation during the distillation phase in simulation for the in-hand rotation task (Figure 5a).
- **Performance Metrics**: When evaluated under randomized physics properties, policies trained with privileged information (AAC + Pose) demonstrated superior performance across all measured criteria compared to proprioception-only baselines (Table 1). This included higher Rotation Reward (RotR), extended Time to Fall (TTF), and reduced Undesired Rotation Penalty (RotP).
- **AAC vs. RMA**: The Asymmetric Actor Critic (AAC) framework, particularly when augmented with the online latent distillation loss, was found to yield superior final performance and real-world behaviors compared to the RMA (Rapid Motor Adaptation) distillation method (Figure 5b, Table 1). This justified the simplification of the simulation phase to a single AAC training step.

#### 4.2. PTLD Improves Policy In-Hand Rotation Robustness in the Real World

- **Superior Performance**: PTLD significantly outperformed all evaluated baselines in real-world in-hand rotation tasks, exhibiting higher total rotation achieved and longer time to fall (Figure 6). This included proprioception-only policies (RMA Proprioception, AAC Proprioception) and other tactile-related baselines (Proprioceptive Distillation, Tactile Adaptation).
- **Dynamic Contact Capture**: The tactile student policy within PTLD demonstrated the ability to capture dynamic contact sequences that proprioception alone could not, such as subtle force distribution changes during slip or upward push events. This capability allowed for adaptive finger-gaiting and sophisticated slip recovery behaviors inherited from the teacher policy (Figure 6, bottom-left).
- **Object Diversity (Cube Rotation)**: PTLD exhibited robust performance on objects with irregular geometries, such as cubes, where proprioceptive baselines failed quickly. The tactile signals enabled the policy to differentiate between edge and face contact, facilitating active corrections from unstable edge contact to stable face contact (Figure 6, bottom-right; Table 3).
- **Teacher Surpassing**: A notable finding was that the PTLD tactile policy for cube rotation could, in some instances, outperform its AAC privileged-sensor teacher policy. This was attributed to noise and drift in the markerless object pose estimation (FoundationPose) used for the teacher, suggesting that the distilled tactile policy, relying on a more robust sensing modality, could achieve higher real-world performance.

#### 4.3. Tactile Information Enhances Object State Estimation

- **Orientation Reconstruction**: Analysis of the information encoded in tactile latents showed that tactile data significantly reduced prediction error for both absolute and relative object orientations when a decoder was trained to reconstruct pose from student encoder rollouts (Table 2).
- **Ambiguity Resolution**: Proprioception-only encoders struggled particularly with relative pose estimation. This was attributed to the ambiguity of finger-gait patterns during unobserved slippage, which tactile feedback was able to disambiguate through direct contact signals. Absolute pose estimation results were also visualized (Figure 7).

#### 4.4. Tactile Object In-Hand Reorientation

- **Essential for Reorientation**: For the more complex in-hand reorientation task, tactile input was found to be essential. Success rates, measured by goals reached and time to fall, decreased significantly without tactile feedback (Table 4).
- **Transformer Efficacy**: The causal Transformer-based tactile encoder effectively captured the long-range dependencies required for multi-directional reorientation and gait adaptation, outperforming temporal-convolutional encoders which tended to collapse into unidirectional gaiting. This highlights the importance of the appropriate architecture for handling the complexity of tactile information in dynamic tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces PTLD (Privileged Tactile Latent Distillation), a framework that presents a notable shift in the approach to learning tactile dexterous manipulation policies. Its significance and potential impact stem from several key aspects:

1. **Bypassing Tactile Simulation Challenges**: PTLD offers a direct solution to the long-standing problem of the lack of accurate and fast tactile simulation. By anchoring sim-to-real transfer on readily available privileged sensors (like external object pose trackers) for data collection in the real world, and then distilling this knowledge into a tactile-only policy, the method completely sidesteps the need to model complex tactile physics in simulation. This is a critical advancement for fields where high-fidelity simulation of specific sensing modalities is prohibitive.

2. **Enhanced Policy Robustness and Performance**: The method demonstrates substantial performance gains on complex, dynamic manipulation tasks. For in-hand rotation, PTLD achieved a 182% improvement, and for the challenging in-hand reorientation task, it showed a 57% improvement in goals reached. These policies are also shown to be robust to changes in object properties and capable of generalizing to irregular geometries (like cubes), exhibiting sophisticated slip recovery and adaptive finger-gaiting behaviors. This robustness is crucial for deploying robots in varied, unstructured real-world environments.

3. **Efficiency in Training**: The integration of online latent distillation within an Asymmetric Actor-Critic (AAC) framework simplifies the simulation training phase from a laborious two-stage process to a single, more efficient step. This reduction in training complexity can accelerate the development cycle for new manipulation skills.

4. **Information Exploitation**: PTLD effectively leverages the rich information provided by real-world tactile sensors, demonstrating that tactile feedback is superior to proprioception alone for detailed object state estimation, particularly in disambiguating slippage and contact geometry. The ability for the distilled tactile policy to occasionally outperform its privileged-sensor teacher, especially when the teacher relies on noisy real-world privileged sensors, underscores the potential for tactile sensing to provide more robust and reliable state information.

5. **Generalizability of Methodology**: While the current work focuses on tactile sensing, the authors propose that the underlying methodology is general-purpose. This "blueprint" could be extended to learn perceptive policies across other sensing modalities, such as vision, that also suffer from significant sim-to-real gaps or are difficult to simulate accurately. This broad applicability suggests a potential impact on various areas of robot learning.

**Potential Impact**:
The development of highly dexterous and robust tactile manipulation skills is essential for robots to operate effectively in human environments like healthcare, manufacturing, and homes. PTLD contributes a practical framework towards this goal, potentially enabling robots to perform complex tasks that require fine-grained contact understanding. This could lead to advancements in areas such as precision assembly, handling delicate objects, and interacting with diverse object geometries without prior knowledge.

**Limitations (as identified by the authors)**:
Despite its advancements, PTLD has acknowledged limitations:
- **Information Overlap and Asymmetry**: The success of distillation is contingent on the degree of information overlap between the privileged sensor (e.g., object pose) and the deployment sensor (tactile). Significant information asymmetry could hinder effective distillation.
- **Privileged Sensor Noise Floors**: The real-world noise inherent in the privileged sensors used during distillation (e.g., object pose estimation from external cameras) can impose a ceiling on the performance of the deployed tactile policy. High-precision external systems are necessary to mitigate this.
- **Instrumented Setup Requirements**: The method currently necessitates an instrumented real-world setup with external cameras or trackers to provide privileged state information during the distillation phase. This requirement may limit its rapid application in fully unstructured or "in-the-wild" environments where such instrumentation is unavailable.
