# Mastering Diverse, Unknown, and Cluttered Tracks for Robust Vision-Based Drone Racing

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Shanghai Jiao Tong University developed a vision-based autonomous drone racing framework that navigates diverse, unknown, and cluttered tracks. The approach enables agile flight exceeding 5 m/s in real-world cluttered environments and demonstrates robustness to gate position errors over 1 meter through zero-shot sim-to-real transfer.

## Problem

- Existing reinforcement learning (RL) solutions for drone racing struggle with generalization to novel and cluttered environments, often lacking robustness to gate position command noise.
- The exploration-exploitation conflict in high-speed, collision-prone environments hinders the learning of aggressive racing behaviors and creates an imbalance between speed optimization and obstacle avoidance.
- Perceptual ambiguity between gates and obstacles in depth maps makes it difficult for vision systems to acquire discriminative representations without explicit semantic supervision.

## Method

- A two-phase ==reinforcement learning== framework (soft-collision followed by hard-collision) was introduced to facilitate learning aggressive flight behaviors before refining precise obstacle avoidance.
- An adaptive noise-augmented curriculum, combined with an asymmetric actor-critic architecture, was employed to enhance policy robustness to noisy gate commands and improve perception-aware gate recognition.
- A track primitive generator was used to create diverse training tracks, and Local Lipschitz Continuity Constraints (L2C2) were applied to improve action smoothness and temporal stability for better sim-to-real transfer.

## Results

- The two-phase training method achieved higher average speeds across terrain difficulty levels compared to one-phase training, demonstrating improved aggressive racing without sacrificing safety.
- The adaptive noise-augmented curriculum maintained nearly an **80%** success rate under **2.1** m gate position noise, significantly outperforming policies trained with fixed or no noise.
- The trained policy achieved agile flight exceeding 5 m/s in diverse, cluttered real-world environments with zero-shot sim-to-real transfer and demonstrated robustness to gate position errors over 1 meter in physical tests.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A phased training approach, starting with 'soft' collisions, allows the agent to explore aggressive racing trajectories without early termination, effectively balancing speed and safety learning.
> - Dynamically increasing noise in gate position commands during training forces the policy to prioritize robust visual perception over potentially inaccurate external guidance, leading to better generalization.
> - Decoupling actor and critic observations (asymmetric actor-critic) enables the actor to learn from noisy, realistic inputs while the critic maintains a stable value estimate from ground truth, enhancing policy learning and robustness.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Autonomous drone racing represents a challenging benchmark problem in robotics, requiring high-speed, collision-free navigation through gates, often with the goal of surpassing human pilot performance. Historically, methodologies in this field have evolved from traditional perception-planning-control pipelines to end-to-end reinforcement learning (RL) approaches. While conventional methods faced limitations due to cumulative errors and processing latency, RL-based solutions offered reduced inference times and direct optimization of flight performance, potentially exceeding human capabilities.

However, existing RL solutions for drone racing have typically been trained on fixed, obstacle-free tracks. This specialization often results in poor generalization to novel environments and limited safety in scenarios with obstacles. The ability of RL agents to adapt to unknown, cluttered environments, tolerate noise in gate position commands, and maintain aggressive flight while ensuring safety has largely remained an open challenge.

Prior work in obstacle-free drone racing includes optimization-based methods, which use mathematical formulations for minimum-time trajectory planning with discrete waypoints. Examples include trajectory progression parameterization and Model Predictive Contouring Control (MPCC), which are computationally intensive or rely heavily on accurate drone and gate states without visual perception. RL-based methods have also achieved high speeds in obstacle-free settings, with approaches ranging from end-to-end racing networks to teacher-student distillation for vision-based policies, and systems that outperform human pilots. Some work has also focused on enhancing generalization to unseen tracks.

In contrast, autonomous drone racing in cluttered environments is less explored, particularly with RL-based methods. Optimization and planning-based solutions have shown some effectiveness, such as sampling-based methods for time-optimal trajectories and online replanning for dynamic gates, but these often lack generalization or depend on precise state estimates. RL-based approaches in cluttered settings often train on fixed environments, limiting generalization, or lack real-world validation. This paper positions itself by addressing the gap in developing a vision-based policy that generalizes to diverse, unknown, and cluttered real-world environments with zero-shot sim-to-real transfer capabilities.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust, vision-based autonomous drone racing framework capable of operating in diverse, partially unknown, and cluttered environments. This involves training a single policy that can generalize across varied tracks, adapt to noise in gate position commands, and execute aggressive gate traversals while avoiding collisions.

The authors identify several key challenges motivating their approach:

1. **Exploration-exploitation conflict in high-speed, collision-prone environments:** In typical RL setups for racing, early termination due to collisions in cluttered environments limits the agent's ability to explore long trajectories. This hinders the learning of aggressive racing behaviors and creates an imbalance between speed optimization and obstacle avoidance, as limited learning signals are available from short, collision-terminated episodes.
2. **Perceptual ambiguity between gates and obstacles in depth maps:** Both gates and obstacles can present similar depth cues (e.g., thin structures against complex backgrounds). However, they require fundamentally different control responses: aggressive approach for gates versus cautious avoidance for obstacles. This ambiguity makes it difficult for a depth-only vision system to acquire discriminative representations without explicit semantic supervision, thus reducing adaptability to unfamiliar scenarios. The problem is compounded when only coarse gate position information is available.
3. **Need for robustness and generalization to diverse track layouts and obstacle configurations:** Traditional RL solutions often struggle with generalization when faced with novel track layouts or varied obstacle arrangements beyond their training data. A robust policy needs to acquire transferable flight proficiencies that enable effective performance across a wide spectrum of environmental conditions.
4. **Computational constraints for real-world deployment:** Any developed policy must be capable of running on computationally limited onboard hardware, necessitating efficient perception and control mechanisms suitable for real-time operation.

To overcome these challenges, the authors propose a novel RL framework. Their motivation is to enable a quadrotor to master complex racing tasks by balancing speed and safety, enhancing visual perception for ambiguous targets, and ensuring broad generalization, ultimately leading to robust real-world performance.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework integrates several components designed to address the challenges of vision-based drone racing in cluttered environments. The methodology can be broadly categorized into a two-phase learning strategy, a curriculum learning scheme for generalization, and specific policy training techniques, all supported by a high-fidelity simulator.

**A. Two-Phase Learning for Obstacle-Aware Agile Racing:**
To mitigate the exploration-exploitation conflict and facilitate learning aggressive flight behaviors, a two-phase reinforcement learning framework is introduced:

- **Soft-Collision Phase:** In this initial phase, the drone is trained in a simulated environment where rigid body collision effects are "soft." This means collisions with obstacles do not immediately terminate an episode or disrupt flight dynamics significantly. Instead, a mild, small-weighted collision penalty is introduced based on the number of "collision points" (uniformly placed on the drone's body) that penetrate the environment mesh. This approach allows the agent to execute longer trajectories, gather more positive racing samples, and learn aggressive racing behaviors without premature terminations, thus preserving exploration for speed optimization while beginning to balance speed and safety.
- **Hard-Collision Phase:** Following the soft-collision phase, the pre-trained policy is fine-tuned in a physically realistic scenario where collisions result in early termination and a larger penalty. This phase forces the drone to learn safer, more precise obstacle avoidance behaviors, prioritizing exploration for balancing speed maximization with collision avoidance. The weights for smoothness and collision penalties are increased in this phase.

**B. Curriculum Learning for Generalizable Racing:**
To enhance policy robustness and generalization, especially in distinguishing gates from obstacles, the framework incorporates an adaptive noise-augmented curriculum within an asymmetric actor-critic architecture, combined with a track primitive generator.

- **Adaptive Noise-Augmented Curriculum:** This curriculum dynamically adjusts the noise level applied to the next-gate position commands provided to the agent. The noise level (represented by `iμt`) increases if the policy performs well (passing more than three gates) and decreases if performance falls below a threshold (passing fewer than three gates). This adaptive adjustment prevents performance degradation from excessively high noise initially while gradually compelling the actor to rely less on noisy command observations and more on extracting gate-relevant features directly from the depth map.
- **Asymmetric Actor-Critic Architecture:** Integrated with the noise curriculum, this architecture separates the observations used by the actor and critic. The actor receives noisy next-gate position commands, pushing it to develop perception-driven behaviors. In contrast, the critic receives ground-truth commands, providing a stable and accurate signal for value estimation and robust training. This combination facilitates a gradual shift in the policy's reliance from external command guidance to learned visual perception.
- **Track Primitive Generator:** To promote generalization across diverse track configurations, three types of training tracks are dynamically generated: circular (clockwise/counterclockwise), zigzag, and elliptical. These primitives are designed to capture fundamental motion patterns (straight-line flight, left/right turns), enabling the learned policies to acquire transferable proficiencies applicable to a wide variety of unseen tracks.

**C. Policy Training Details:**

- **Observation:** The policy's observation vector `ot` at time `t` includes linear base velocity in the body frame, the third row of the rotation matrix (encoding roll and pitch), relative translations to the next and second subsequent gates (with injected noise), the previous action `at-1` (for smooth control), and a downsampled depth image (`It` to 96x72 pixels).
- **Action:** The policy outputs mass-normalized collective thrust and body rates (CTBR), a common control representation for quadrotors.
- **Reward Function:** The reward function largely follows established methods, incorporating terms for progress towards the target, perception (encouraging the drone to face the target), and penalties for body rates, action rates, and collisions. Weights for these terms are adjusted between the soft- and hard-collision phases.
- **Network Design:** A multi-layer perceptron (MLP) processes state-only and command observations, while a convolutional neural network (CNN) processes depth images. Their concatenated feature vectors are then fed into further MLP layers to output control commands or value estimates.
- **Gate Crossing Detector:** To overcome the unreliability of position-based gate checks due to noise, an offline-trained detector uses network embeddings. It learns to identify gate crossings from a balanced dataset generated in the simulator, and its prediction head remains frozen during policy training.
- **Sim-to-real Transfer Techniques:**
 - **Domain Randomization:** Extensive randomization is applied to drone parameters (air drag coefficients, mass, inertia, angular velocity controller PID gains) and observations (Gaussian noise) to improve robustness and bridge the sim-to-real gap.
 - **Local Lipschitz Continuity Constraint (L2C2):** An additional loss term is introduced to regularize both the actor and critic networks, bounding output variation between consecutive states. This enhances temporal smoothness of actions and value functions, reducing motion oscillations.

**D. Quadrotor Simulator (DiffLab):**
The authors developed DiffLab, a high-fidelity, parallelized drone simulator built on NVIDIA Isaac Lab. It features a more accurate air drag model (including first- and second-order terms), accounts for control delay, and has parameters calibrated using real flight data. DiffLab supports multiple control levels (position/yaw, linear velocity/yaw, CTBR, single-rotor thrusts) operating within a cascade PID loop.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper evaluates the proposed framework through extensive simulation and real-world experiments, focusing on ablation studies, performance in various simulated environments, and robustness tests on a physical quadrotor.

**A. Ablation Studies:**
Ablation studies were conducted using average speed, success rate (proportion of 20 drones completing the track without collision within 8 seconds), and action rate as metrics.

- **Two-Phase vs One-Phase Training:** The two-phase training method (soft-collision followed by hard-collision) achieved higher average speeds across all terrain difficulty levels compared to the one-phase (hard-collision-only) method. Notably, the hard-collision phase of the two-phase method maintained a success rate similar to the one-phase method. This indicates that the two-phase approach effectively leverages initial exploration for aggressive racing while subsequently refining obstacle avoidance, avoiding the conservatism observed in the one-phase policy.
- **Adaptive Noise-Augmented Curriculum:** The adaptive noise curriculum demonstrated significantly enhanced policy robustness under varying levels of gate position noise. Policies trained without noise or with fixed noise (1 m/axis) showed notable performance degradation, with success rates dropping to near 0% at 0.9 m noise for the noise-free policy and 54% at 2.1 m noise for the fixed-noise policy. In contrast, the adaptive curriculum maintained nearly an 80% success rate under the same 2.1 m noise conditions, indicating its effectiveness in promoting perception-aware gate recognition.
- **Locally Lipschitz Continuous Constraint (L2C2):** The application of L2C2 regularization resulted in a 25% reduction in the action rate, leading to smoother actions and reduced motion oscillation in the policy's behavior.

**B. Simulation Experiments:**
Experiments were conducted in both the custom DiffLab simulator and the AirSim simulator, featuring factory and forest environments.

- **Generalization to Unseen Environments and Tracks:** The policy demonstrated effective generalization to unseen, realistic environments and novel track layouts in both DiffLab and AirSim. Multi-perspective visualizations confirmed agile flight, achieving peak speeds of 5 m/s in both forest and factory scenes. This result supports the effectiveness of the track primitive generator in fostering transferable flight proficiencies.
- **Robustness to Gate Position Noise:** In simulation, the policy exhibited high robustness to gate position errors. It achieved a 100% success rate when the single-axis position error was below 0.6 m and maintained over 50% success even with errors up to 1.2 m. Maximum speeds exceeded 5 m/s, with only moderate degradation as noise increased. The study noted that failures under high noise were primarily due to incorrect commands causing the drone to deviate from the track, rather than collisions, indicating strong underlying obstacle avoidance capabilities.

**C. Real-World Experiments:**
The policy was validated on a lightweight quadrotor (0.46 kg) equipped with an Intel RealSense D435i depth camera and a Radxa Zero3W onboard computer. A VICON motion capture system provided accurate state estimates, and a Betaflight controller handled low-level control.

- **Agile Flight in Cluttered Environments:** The quadrotor successfully navigated various track and obstacle layouts in cluttered real-world environments, including zigzag, circular, and U-shaped tracks with randomly placed obstacles. It achieved a maximum speed exceeding 5 m/s. Accurate gate-crossing detection was also demonstrated during flight. These results confirm the framework's robustness and generalization capabilities in physical scenarios.
- **Robustness to Gate Position Errors in Reality:** Real-world tests further validated the policy's robustness to gate position errors. In experiments where gate positions were randomly perturbed or the target command was intentionally set beyond the track's end (while keeping gates within camera view but misaligned with the command), the drone successfully tolerated noise exceeding 1 meter. It demonstrated perception-driven behavior by autonomously adjusting its trajectory to pass through the gates, rather than strictly following noisy or misaligned commands, confirming its gate perception-aware capability.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work presents a learning-based approach for vision-based drone racing that addresses key limitations of prior methods, particularly in generalizing to diverse, unknown, and cluttered environments. The proposed two-phase training strategy, coupled with an adaptive noise-augmented curriculum and an asymmetric actor-critic architecture, successfully develops a robust, gate perception-aware racing capability. The integration of a track primitive generator and Lipschitz constraints further enhances motion stability and cross-environment generalization.

The authors claim that this is the first vision-based autonomous drone racing system capable of operating in diverse, unknown, and cluttered real-world environments. This represents a notable advancement in autonomous aerial robotics, pushing the boundaries of what is achievable with vision-only perception and end-to-end reinforcement learning.

The potential impact of this research includes:
- **Enhanced Autonomous Navigation:** The framework's ability to navigate high-speed in complex, previously unseen, and cluttered environments with robustness to imprecise commands could be applied to other challenging autonomous navigation tasks beyond racing, such as search and rescue, inspection, or delivery in complex urban or natural settings.
- **Improved Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The detailed simulator (DiffLab) and the comprehensive domain randomization and L2C2 regularization techniques contribute to more effective sim-to-real transfer, which is a critical aspect for deploying learned policies on physical systems.
- **Advancements in Deep Reinforcement Learning for Robotics:** The two-phase training and adaptive noise curriculum represent methodological contributions to RL, specifically addressing the exploration-exploitation trade-off and perceptual ambiguity in high-stakes robotic tasks.

Despite these advancements, the authors acknowledge limitations, including potential for further improvement in flight speed, the impact of scene distribution on drone speed, and the need for further study into the balance between optimality and safety in various scenarios. Future work may also focus on perception-based pose correction to reduce reliance on external localization systems.
