# The Three Dimensions of ROS 2 Middleware

> [!summary] Summary
> A systematic analysis of ROS 2 middleware reveals inherent architectural trade-offs across Space (location transparency), Time (temporal predictability), and State (contextual continuity), identifying eleven specific manifestations where optimizing one dimension often strains the others. The work proposes a research roadmap for developing more robust and scalable robotic communication architectures by addressing these fundamental conflicts.

## Problem

- ROS 2 middleware implementations exhibit structural limitations in dynamic, resource-constrained, and wireless environments, leading to performance degradation and unpredictable behavior.
- Current research on ROS 2 middleware lacks a unifying architectural framework to systematically analyze the complex cross-dimensional interactions among system requirements.
- Mechanisms designed to preserve state continuity introduce significant computational and network overhead, competing directly with time-critical communication.

## Method

- The authors developed a conceptual framework formalizing three fundamental dimensions for distributed robotic systems: ==Space (location transparency)==, ==Time (temporal predictability)==, and ==State (contextual continuity)==, collectively termed the **Space, Time, and State framework**.
- They conducted an architectural analysis of ROS 2 communication, detailing discovery, data exchange, and state management mechanisms for ==DDS== and ==Zenoh==.
- A comprehensive literature review of 94 middleware-focused studies was performed, systematically mapping them onto ==conflict pairs== derived from the Space, Time, and State framework.

## Results

- The paper formalized the '**Space, Time, and State**' framework, providing a diagnostic tool that links inadequate support for each dimension to specific classes of system failure.
- It identified and categorized **eleven** distinct manifestations of structural trade-offs (e.g., serialization overhead, discovery storms, retransmission congestion) through a review of **94** studies, consistently demonstrating a compromise in dimensional guarantees.
- A principled research roadmap was proposed, advocating for Cross-Layer Co-Design, Adaptive Middleware, and Decoupled State Architecture to address the root causes of dimensional conflicts and advance future robotic middleware.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Optimizing one dimension (Space, Time, or State) in ROS 2 middleware inherently strains or compromises the others due to shared resource dependencies, indicating an unavoidable trade-off.
> - Eleven specific manifestations of structural conflicts consistently demonstrate that proposed mitigation strategies improve one dimensional guarantee by partially relinquishing another.
> - Current middleware practices are hindered by QoS semantic loss, inadequate evaluation methodologies, and significant security overhead, which exacerbate dimensional conflicts and complicate effective deployment.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robot Operating System 2 (ROS 2) has become a widely adopted standard for modern robot software development, facilitating the modularization and distribution of robotic components across various devices and network domains. As robotic systems evolve towards multi-robot coordination, edge-cloud integration, and deployment in wireless and resource-constrained environments, the underlying communication middleware becomes increasingly critical.

The existing body of research on ROS 2 primarily concentrates on application-level utilization, framework development, and performance optimization, particularly in areas such as real-time scheduling, executor design, and latency control. A quantitative assessment of ROS 2-specific publications indicates that a comparatively small fraction, approximately 3.5%, explicitly addresses middleware-layer design and analysis. Although interest in ROS 2 middleware has increased, especially concerning Quality of Service (QoS) policies, network behavior, and analytical modeling, these investigations frequently proceed in isolation. They often lack a unifying architectural framework that systematically captures the complex cross-dimensional interactions among the fundamental requirements of distributed robotic systems.

This research aims to address this identified gap by providing a systematic survey of ROS 2 middleware. It introduces a conceptual framework designed to examine the architectural limits of these systems by formalizing three structural dimensions: Space, Time, and State. By synthesizing architectural patterns and pinpointing gaps in current modeling and analysis approaches, this work positions itself to outline a principled research roadmap for the development of robust and scalable robotic middleware architectures, thereby contributing to a more holistic understanding of the field.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the increasing complexity and distributed nature of modern robotic systems. As perception, planning, and control functionalities are deployed across multiple processes, devices, and heterogeneous network domains, the communication infrastructure—specifically the middleware layer—plays a central role in determining system scalability, responsiveness, and robustness. The paper highlights several critical issues that drive its objectives:

- **Structural Limitations in Dynamic Environments:** Current ROS 2 middleware implementations, such as Data Distribution Service (DDS) and Zenoh, exhibit structural limitations when deployed in dynamic, resource-constrained, and wireless environments. These limitations manifest as performance degradation, weakened temporal predictability due to bandwidth fluctuations and latency variability, and disruptions in discovery and synchronization processes caused by dynamic topology changes.
- **Overhead of State Management:** Mechanisms designed to preserve state continuity, such as buffering, replication, and session maintenance, introduce computational and network overhead. This overhead can compete with time-critical communication, potentially leading to congestion or session instability in long-running deployments.
- **Lack of a Unifying Architectural Framework:** Despite growing interest, existing studies on ROS 2 middleware often operate in isolation. They frequently lack a comprehensive architectural framework that can systematically capture and analyze the cross-dimensional interactions among the critical requirements of distributed robotic systems, namely temporal predictability, spatial abstraction, and state continuity.
- **Increasing Constraints on System Performance:** The architectural properties of middleware directly influence system-level behavior under realistic deployment conditions. As robotics advances towards more sophisticated applications like vision-language-action integration, large-scale multi-robot coordination, and persistent autonomous operation, communication and state management are no longer secondary concerns but direct determinants of performance.

Based on these motivations, the paper sets forth a series of key objectives:

- **Structured Examination of ROS 2 Middleware:** To provide a systematic analysis of ROS 2 middleware’s architectural constructs and operational dynamics, including discovery, data exchange, and state management mechanisms.
- **Formalization of Three Fundamental Dimensions:** To define and formalize three essential structural dimensions required by distributed robotic systems: Space (representing location transparency and modular deployment), Time (representing temporal predictability for control loops), and State (representing contextual continuity despite dynamic node participation and intermittent connectivity). The paper aims to articulate their architectural implications for middleware design.
- **Systematic Reinterpretation of Existing Research:** To review existing middleware research through the newly proposed conceptual framework of Space, Time, and State, organizing studies according to the structural trade-offs that arise among these dimensions.
- **Identification of Structural Gaps and Research Roadmap:** To synthesize the identified architectural gaps in current middleware design and propose a principled research roadmap aimed at guiding the development of more robust and scalable robotic middleware architectures.

These objectives aim to provide a foundational understanding of the challenges and inherent trade-offs in ROS 2 middleware, guiding future research and development towards systems that can reliably meet the demands of advanced robotic applications.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The paper employs a systematic methodology, combining architectural analysis with a comprehensive literature review, to establish a conceptual framework for understanding ROS 2 middleware.

**A. Architectural Analysis of ROS 2 Middleware:**
The work begins by structuring the ROS 2 communication architecture into a four-tier hierarchy: application, middleware, operating system (OS), and physical layers. It abstracts the core communication into five structural entities:
- **Endpoint (E):** The fundamental unit of data production or consumption, mapped from RCL publishers/subscriptions/services/actions to middleware constructs (e.g., DDS DataWriter/DataReader, Zenoh publishers/subscribers).
- **Topic (T):** A logical communication channel with a name and data type, mapped to middleware-specific constructs (e.g., DDS topic names, Zenoh key expressions).
- **Message (M):** A concrete data instance conforming to a ROS 2 interface definition, serialized for transmission.
- **Process (P):** An OS-level execution unit corresponding to a middleware participant (e.g., DDS DomainParticipant, Zenoh session), serving as the entry point for node participation.
- **Host (H):** A distinct computing unit providing the physical execution environment for processes, defining spatial boundaries for local or network transport.

The paper then details the operational dynamics of middleware, focusing on how DDS and Zenoh, the primary Tier one implementations, realize these mechanisms:

- **Discovery:**
 - **Participant Discovery:** DDS uses the Real-Time Publish-Subscribe (RTPS) protocol, specifically the Simple Participant Discovery Protocol (SPDP) with multicast for peer-to-peer (P2P) awareness. Zenoh uses mechanisms like multicast scouting and gossip-based discovery, with routers propagating information.
 - **Endpoint Matching:** DDS uses the Simple Endpoint Discovery Protocol (SEDP) to disseminate metadata and establish logical associations based on topic names, types, and QoS compatibility. Zenoh matches published key expressions with subscription interests, with routers mediating visibility.
- **Data Exchange:**
 - **Serialization and Encoding:** ROS 2 uses Common Data Representation (CDR) at the RCL layer. DDS serializes messages into data samples with inline RTPS metadata. Zenoh encapsulates serialized byte payloads with key expressions and protocol metadata.
 - **Transport Mechanisms:** Both DDS and Zenoh support shared memory for intra-host communication. DDS primarily uses UDP-based RTPS (with TCP support). Zenoh uses a pluggable transport layer (TCP, UDP, QUIC) and can operate over direct P2P or router-mediated forwarding.
 - **Reliability and Delivery Control:** DDS ensures reliability via stateful RTPS mechanisms like AckNack exchanges, Heartbeats, and retransmission windows, governed by reliability and history QoS policies. Zenoh offers best-effort and reliable channels with sequence numbers and acknowledgments, and publishers can control congestion with drop/block policies.
- **State Management:**
 - **Liveliness Maintenance:** DDS uses Heartbeat and lease mechanisms at participant and endpoint levels for decentralized monitoring. Zenoh uses session leases, keep-alive messages, and liveliness tokens for session-centric monitoring.
 - **History Restoration:** DDS uses endpoint-local HistoryCache with durability QoS for replaying messages to new DataReaders. Zenoh decouples history via independent storage managers that serve historical state via pull queries.
 - **Resource Control:** DDS enforces constraints via resource limits QoS policies on HistoryCaches. Zenoh manages resources through configurable transmission queues on sessions and routers, distributing control path-based rather than endpoint-based.

**B. Conceptual Framework of Three Dimensions:**
The paper formalizes Space, Time, and State as universal structural requirements, each associated with a "Key Question" and "Primary Objective":
- **Space:** "Where is the entity located?" – Objective: Location Transparency.
- **Time:** "When is the message delivered?" – Objective: Temporal Predictability.
- **State:** "How is state preserved?" – Objective: Contextual Continuity.
It then introduces the concept of "Structural Trade-offs," arguing that these dimensions are coupled and optimizing one often strains the others due to shared resource dependencies (execution capacity, network bandwidth, buffer memory).

**C. Dimensional Conflict Analysis:**
The core of the methodology involves a comprehensive review of 94 middleware-focused studies. These studies are systematically mapped onto three structural conflict pairs derived from the conceptual framework:
- **Space–Time Conflict:** Examines how spatial abstraction (location transparency) imposes costs on temporal predictability. Manifestations analyzed include serialization overhead, payload fragmentation, intra-host redundancy, and inter-host redundancy (multicast).
- **Time–State Conflict:** Explores how state continuity mechanisms consume resources needed for temporal performance. Manifestations analyzed include lock contention, control overhead (e.g., Heartbeats), and retransmission congestion.
- **Space–State Conflict:** Investigates how location transparency impacts state management costs and vice versa. Manifestations analyzed include node anonymity, path-blind forwarding, discovery storms, and intermediary dependency.
For each manifestation, the analysis identifies underlying architectural assumptions, traces proposed mitigation approaches, and highlights recurring structural commonalities, particularly how solutions often entail sacrificing one dimensional guarantee for another.

**D. Research Roadmap:**
Finally, the paper identifies three structural gaps in current middleware practice (QoS semantic loss, evaluation inadequacy, security overhead) and proposes three long-term architectural directions to address the root causes of dimensional conflicts: Cross-Layer Co-Design, Adaptive Middleware, and Decoupled State Architecture. This roadmap aims to guide future research toward negotiating the trade-off surface more effectively.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The core findings of this paper revolve around the articulation and empirical validation of the inherent structural trade-offs within ROS 2 middleware, framed by the proposed three dimensions: Space, Time, and State.

**A. Formalized Three Dimensions:**
The paper successfully formalizes Space as **Location Transparency** (where entities are located, enabling modularity), Time as **Temporal Predictability** (when messages are delivered, ensuring stable control loops), and State as **Contextual Continuity** (how state is preserved, maintaining system consistency). This framework provides a diagnostic tool, linking inadequate support for each dimension to specific classes of system failure (e.g., breakdown of modular deployment, unstable control loops, loss of contextual consistency).

**B. Identification of Structural Trade-offs (Dimensional Conflicts):**
Through a comprehensive review of 94 middleware-focused studies, the paper identifies and analyzes eleven distinct manifestations of three fundamental conflict pairs:

1. **Space–Time Conflict:** This conflict arises because spatial abstraction, while enabling location transparency, often conceals physical network realities, thereby degrading temporal predictability.
 - **Serialization Overhead:** Applications’ indifference to physical co-location renders serialization non-elidable, even for intra-host communication, causing CPU/memory pressure and latency. Mitigations like zero-copy and shared memory intrinsically reintroduce locality awareness, partially relinquishing spatial abstraction.
 - **Payload Fragmentation:** The middleware’s concealment of network Maximum Transmission Unit (MTU) limits causes large messages to fragment. Physical packet loss then amplifies into message-level retransmission, degrading latency. Solutions like MTU-aligned configurations or application-layer fragmentation reintroduce physical-layer awareness.
 - **Intra-Host Redundancy:** Treating each subscriber as an independent logical entity, regardless of co-location, results in redundant message copies for local subscribers, linearly scaling resource consumption. Shared memory transport and node composition reduce this by exploiting physical co-location, again reducing spatial abstraction.
 - **Inter-Host Redundancy (Multicast):** Multicast's assumption of network-level replication efficiency is vulnerable to physical switch processing thresholds, leading to network-wide jitter amplification. Unicast fallback or router-mediated fan-out mitigate this by making the middleware aware of or responsible for fan-out, sacrificing spatial efficiency or relocating the cost.

2. **Time–State Conflict:** This conflict highlights that mechanisms for contextual continuity consume the same computational and communication resources required by temporal performance.
 - **Lock Contention:** Ensuring message ordering and shared state consistency requires synchronization, introducing lock contention, context-switching overhead, and non-deterministic delays. Solutions like lock-free data structures, priority-aware executors, or state decoupling reduce contention by relaxing consistency or offloading state management.
 - **Control Overhead:** Reliable communication generates persistent background control traffic (e.g., Heartbeats, AckNacks), which consumes bandwidth and CPU resources, competing directly with time-critical application data. Tuning Heartbeat periods or using transport-level reliability displaces or reduces this overhead at the cost of immediate liveliness detection or by relying on lower layers.
 - **Retransmission Congestion:** Reliable QoS unconditionally retransmits missing samples upon detection of loss, which can exacerbate congestion by injecting additional traffic into an already saturated channel, leading to positive feedback loops. Mitigations involve delaying state restoration, applying congestion-aware control, or separating history restoration from active data exchange.

3. **Space–State Conflict:** This conflict shows how spatial abstraction and scalability demands interact with the cost and feasibility of state management.
 - **Node Anonymity:** Location transparency's data-centric anonymity prevents middleware from tracking persistent physical node identities. This leads to issues like "ghost nodes" and state ambiguity for reconnecting entities. Solutions require reintroducing identity enforcement or managing lifecycles via external orchestrators.
 - **Path-Blind Forwarding:** The middleware’s lack of physical path state (buffer occupancy, link capacity) causes it to continue injecting data into saturated buffers, leading to message backlogs and bursts upon recovery. Congestion-aware control or adaptive injection rates introduce physical path awareness.
 - **Discovery Storm:** The full-mesh visibility model (e.g., DDS default) generates 𝑂 (𝑁2) discovery traffic and memory consumption, leading to network saturation in large-scale deployments. Domain segmentation or centralized discovery servers reduce this by constraining mutual visibility, but this comes at the cost of complete transparency or introduces single points of failure.
 - **Intermediary Dependency:** Centralized discovery (e.g., Discovery Servers, Zenoh routers), while solving 𝑂 (𝑁2) scalability, reintroduces a single point of failure. Replicating intermediaries increases complexity and cost, or reverting to P2P fallback reintroduces scalability issues.

**C. Overarching Conclusion on Trade-offs:**
A central finding is the consistent observation that every proposed mitigation strategy across all eleven manifestations improves one dimensional guarantee (Space, Time, or State) by partially relinquishing another. There is no "silver bullet" solution that simultaneously satisfies all three dimensions under resource-constrained conditions. These trade-offs are inherent, stemming from the shared use of bounded resources (execution capacity, network bandwidth, buffer memory).

**D. Identified Structural Gaps:**
The paper also identifies three structural gaps in current middleware practice that hinder effective deployment:
- **QoS Semantic Loss:** Middleware-level QoS policies often fail to propagate effectively to lower layers (OS kernel scheduling, network priorities), leading to priority inversions and unpredictable latency.
- **Evaluation Inadequacy:** Current evaluation methodologies (isolated benchmarks, disconnected simulators) fail to capture emergent degradation under realistic, integrated, and dynamic deployment conditions.
- **Security Overhead:** Enabling security mechanisms (encryption, authentication) introduces significant latency and throughput degradation, competing for the same resources as time-critical data, and compounding timing non-determinism.

**E. Proposed Research Roadmap:**
To address the root causes of these conflicts and gaps, the paper outlines a research roadmap:
- **Cross-Layer Co-Design:** Enabling bidirectional information flow between middleware and application (upward state propagation) and between middleware and OS/physical layer (indirect state estimation) to overcome unidirectional limitations.
- **Adaptive Middleware:** Developing middleware that continuously estimates underlying link capacity and dynamically adjusts transmission behavior or failover mechanisms to sustain temporal predictability without rigid cross-layer dependencies.
- **Decoupled State Architecture:** Designing adaptive hybrid state architectures that leverage centralized efficiency for baseline operation and dynamically fall back to decentralized mechanisms during failures or extreme scaling events, balancing fault tolerance and scalability.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research offers a significant contribution to the field of robotic systems by providing a foundational and systematic understanding of ROS 2 middleware. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Unified Conceptual Framework:** The introduction of the "Space, Time, and State" framework provides a novel and comprehensive lens through which to analyze and design distributed robotic middleware. This framework moves beyond isolated performance metrics, enabling a more holistic understanding of the architectural trade-offs inherent in these complex systems. Its universality allows it to be applied to various middleware implementations beyond DDS and Zenoh, fostering cross-platform insights.
- **Informed Design and Deployment Decisions:** By explicitly identifying and detailing eleven manifestations of structural conflicts, the paper equips middleware designers and robotic system architects with critical knowledge. It clarifies that achieving desired properties (e.g., location transparency, temporal predictability, contextual continuity) often involves an unavoidable compromise in another dimension. This understanding enables more deliberate, rather than implicit, design choices, leading to more robust and predictable deployments under realistic, constrained conditions.
- **Principled Research Roadmap:** The proposed research roadmap outlines concrete, long-term directions for future investigations. These directions focus on addressing the root causes of dimensional conflicts and structural gaps, rather than merely mitigating symptoms. This systematic guidance can stimulate focused research in areas like cross-layer co-design, adaptive middleware, and decoupled state architectures, which are crucial for advancing the capabilities of ROS 2 and similar distributed systems.
- **Addressing Challenges of Advanced Robotics:** The analysis directly addresses the escalating communication demands posed by emerging trends in robotics, such as Physical AI, vision-language-action integration, embodied agents, and large-scale multi-robot coordination. As these systems operate in increasingly dynamic and open-world environments, the ability to manage communication reliability, latency, and state consistency becomes paramount. This work provides a basis for developing middleware that can meet these challenges.
- **Improving System Reliability and Scalability:** By highlighting the mechanisms that lead to performance degradation (e.g., discovery storms, retransmission congestion, serialization overhead) and proposing pathways for improvement, the research can lead to the development of more reliable and scalable robotic platforms. This will facilitate the deployment of advanced robotic applications in diverse and challenging settings, from industrial automation to autonomous exploration and smart cities.
- **Enhancing the ROS 2 Ecosystem:** As ROS 2 continues to evolve as a de facto standard, this research contributes to its long-term viability by pinpointing fundamental architectural limitations and offering pathways for enhancement. Addressing these limitations will ensure that ROS 2 remains a robust and high-performing framework capable of supporting the next generation of robotic innovation.

In summary, this paper's significance lies in its ability to systematically unpack the complexities of ROS 2 middleware, transforming an often-fragmented area of research into a structured domain where inherent trade-offs are understood and strategically navigated. This analytical foundation and forward-looking roadmap are poised to influence both academic research and practical engineering in the development of future distributed robotic systems.
