# PersistGS: Differentiable Physics for Object Permanence in 4D Gaussian Splatting

> [!summary] Summary
> PersistGS restores object permanence in dynamic 3D Gaussian Splatting when a moving object is fully occluded from all training cameras. It splits the scene into static background Gaussians plus per-object Gaussians and a collision mesh, estimates friction and initial velocity through a differentiable rigid body simulator supervised by a centroid silhouette loss, and drives the object's Gaussians along the predicted SE(3) trajectory, beating constant-velocity extrapolation by 2.46 dB PSNR.

## Problem

- Dynamic 3DGS methods rely wholly on photometric supervision, so fully occluded object Gaussians get no gradients and are pruned, drift, or collapse entirely.
- Generative and diffusion priors for incomplete observations hallucinate plausible appearance but give no guarantee of physical correctness about where an object actually is.
- Kinematic extrapolation cannot represent contact events like bounces, friction deceleration, or direction changes that occur while the object is unobserved.

## Method

- ==Scene decomposition== into frozen static background Gaussians and per-object Gaussians plus a collision mesh from MV-SAM3D, composited in one rasterization pass with depth ordering.
- ==Differentiable rigid body simulation== in NVIDIA Newton estimates friction and initial velocity, using a smooth harmonic friction model and bypassed material averaging to keep contact gradients non-zero.
- A ==centroid silhouette loss== matching rendered alpha-map centroid to mask centroid, plus a three-phase ==observability-aware curriculum== separating pre-contact velocity from post-contact friction estimation.

## Results

- Mean **17.15 dB** PSNR on withheld evaluation cameras during occlusion: **2.46 dB** above constant velocity, **1.41 dB** above linear interpolation, within **0.19 dB** of the ground-truth trajectory bound.
- Centroid silhouette loss cuts mean trajectory RMSE **40%** versus photometric supervision (**0.586** vs **0.984**) and produces roughly **100x** larger friction gradients on ball_fall.
- Friction recovered within **10%** and velocity within **0.5 m/s**; the decomposed architecture beats a joint 4DGS baseline by **6.9 dB** under only **5** training cameras.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Split supervision by what each source knows: vision supplies appearance from observed frames, an analytical simulator supplies position across unobserved intervals.
> - Supervise geometry with a signal that isolates position from appearance; centroid displacement gives a global basin of attraction where pixel losses only fire at silhouette boundaries.
> - Physics priors pay off mainly when the hidden interval contains contact events, yet those are precisely the cases where parameters are hardest to identify from visible frames.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is positioned within the field of 4D (spatiotemporal) scene reconstruction, specifically building upon recent advancements in 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS). 3DGS has emerged as a representation for photorealistic scene reconstruction from multi-view images. Extensions to dynamic scenes, termed Dynamic 3D Gaussian Splatting, aim to reconstruct time-varying environments from synchronized multi-camera video. These methods typically learn per-Gaussian deformation fields, sparse control-point trajectories, or per-object SE(3) poses to represent motion.

A recognized limitation in dynamic scene reconstruction is the challenge of incomplete observations. When a moving object becomes fully occluded from all available training cameras, the photometric supervision for the Gaussians representing that object vanishes. This absence of gradient signal causes the object's Gaussians to degrade, be pruned, or collapse, leading to a loss of the object's representation during the occlusion period. Upon re-emergence, the system effectively has to reconstruct the object anew, failing to maintain object permanence—the principle that objects continue to exist even when unobserved.

Previous approaches to address incomplete observations in neural reconstruction have largely relied on learned generative priors, such as diffusion models. These models aim to hallucinate visually plausible content for unobserved regions or complete multi-object 4D reconstructions through occlusion. While these methods can produce visually coherent results, they do not inherently guarantee physical correctness regarding the object's position and trajectory during the unobserved interval. Their focus is on appearance plausibility rather than dynamic consistency.

This research distinguishes itself by proposing an alternative: leveraging physics as a prior. While differentiable simulation has been integrated with neural rendering for tasks like system identification, dynamics generation, and material property estimation (e.g., in NeRF or continuum Gaussian frameworks for deformable objects), fewer methods have applied it to rigid body dynamics specifically for addressing temporal observation gaps in 4D reconstruction. Existing rigid body dynamics approaches often focus on forward dynamics, material estimation, or state tracking from robot data. Furthermore, while object permanence has been explored in object tracking (e.g., maintaining 2D bounding boxes), this work extends the concept to 4D reconstruction, aiming to maintain a complete 3D Gaussian representation throughout occlusion.

PersistGS contributes to the landscape by integrating differentiable rigid body simulation directly into the 3DGS framework to predict and maintain the *position* of occluded rigid objects. This approach prioritizes physical correctness and dynamic consistency, offering a causal method to fill temporal observation gaps, contrasting with generative priors that focus on appearance. It represents an advancement in physics-informed neural reconstruction for rigid objects, addressing a fundamental challenge in dynamic scene understanding and reconstruction.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to restore object permanence in 4D Gaussian Splatting when a moving rigid object undergoes full temporal occlusion from all training cameras. This restoration aims to ensure that the object's 3D representation persists with correct geometry and appearance throughout the unobserved period, eventually re-emerging seamlessly.

The motivation behind this objective stems from several limitations observed in current dynamic 3DGS methodologies:
1. **Vulnerability to Occlusion:** Existing dynamic 3DGS methods, which rely on photometric supervision, are inherently limited when observations are temporally incomplete. When an object passes behind an occluder and becomes completely unobserved by the training cameras, its corresponding Gaussians lose their supervisory signal. This leads to the degradation or elimination of these Gaussians, causing the object's 4D reconstruction to fail during the occlusion and requiring its re-creation upon re-emergence. This directly violates the principle of object permanence.
2. **Limitations of Generative Priors:** While contemporary research has explored generative priors (e.g., diffusion models) to address incomplete observations by hallucinating plausible content for unobserved regions, these methods primarily focus on visual plausibility. They are designed to infer "what the object might look like" from unobserved angles or during unobserved intervals, rather than accurately determining "where the object is" based on its physical dynamics. Consequently, such generative approaches do not guarantee physical correctness, which is crucial for applications requiring accurate spatiotemporal object localization.
3. **Necessity of Dynamical Reasoning:** For a dynamic object moving behind an occluder, its trajectory is governed by physical laws and interactions with its environment (e.g., gravity, friction, collisions). Kinematic extrapolation methods, such as constant velocity models, cannot accurately predict trajectories that involve complex contact events like bounces, friction-induced deceleration, or changes in direction. Physics-based simulation, conversely, can model these interactions and provide a dynamically consistent prediction of the object's trajectory.
4. **Complementary Roles of Vision and Physics:** The authors postulate a natural decomposition where vision excels at providing appearance information from observed frames, while physics is adept at predicting position during unobserved frames. By integrating these two modalities, the aim is to achieve a more faithful and robust 4D reconstruction. This integration allows for the estimation of physical parameters from observed object motion and then leverages these parameters to drive a simulation that accurately predicts the object's SE(3) pose (translation and rotation) throughout the occlusion period.

In summary, the core motivation is to overcome the fragility of dynamic 3DGS to temporal occlusions by providing a physically principled prior that maintains accurate object localization, rather than merely visually plausible appearance, throughout unobserved intervals.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

PersistGS operates through a three-stage pipeline: Scene Decomposition, Physics Parameter Estimation, and Physics-Guided Reconstruction.

#### 3.1. Scene Decomposition

The approach utilizes a decomposed representation of the scene, separating it into static background Gaussians and distinct per-object Gaussian models for dynamic elements. This architecture was chosen after empirical evaluation indicated it outperformed a joint 4D Gaussian Splatting approach by approximately +6.9 dB PSNR in sparse-view settings (5 training cameras), primarily by avoiding overfitting associated with learning complex deformation fields from limited data.
- **Object Gaussians:** For each dynamic object, its Gaussian representation and a corresponding collision mesh are reconstructed at a designated reference frame (five frames prior to the first occlusion) when all objects are visible. This is achieved using MV-SAM3D. The collision mesh is subsequently used by the physics simulator.
- **Background Gaussians:** The static environment is reconstructed using standard 3DGS. An inverse-mask weighted loss is applied to exclude the dynamic object's regions during background training.
- **Sparse-View Regularization:** Given that training cameras are clustered to facilitate consistent occlusion patterns, several regularization techniques are applied during the decomposition stage. These include Mip-Splatting's 3D smoothing filter, DropGaussian dropout (with a probability `p=0.5`), and depth supervision derived from Depth Anything V2.

#### 3.2. Physics Parameter Estimation

This stage estimates the physical parameters of the dynamic object from its visible trajectory using a differentiable simulation and rendering loop.
- **Parameterization:** The system optimizes four free parameters: the friction coefficient (μ, represented in log₁₀ space) and the initial velocity vector (v₀ = (vₓ, vᵧ, v₂)). Other contact parameters, such as mass, contact stiffness (kₑ), friction stiffness (k_f), and damping (k_d), are fixed. This choice is based on an analysis of parameter identifiability; kₑ and mass are degenerate as they appear as a ratio in contact force equations, while k_f and k_d are poorly conditioned for joint estimation with μ from trajectory data alone. Thus, the focus is on parameters with direct and independent observable influence.
- **Simulator:** NVIDIA Newton, a GPU-accelerated rigid body simulator, is employed. It models the object's interactions with scene contact surfaces using a semi-implicit solver and penalty-based contact, with 8 substeps per frame. The simulator outputs per-frame SE(3) poses (translation and quaternion). Crucially, Warp's tape-based reverse-mode automatic differentiation is used to propagate gradients through the entire simulated trajectory, including complex contact events.
- **Simulator Modifications:** To improve gradient flow through contact dynamics, two modifications were implemented:
 1. The default friction model, which uses a hard `min()` clamp leading to zero-gradient regions when friction saturates, was replaced with a smooth harmonic combination. This ensures non-zero gradients across all friction regimes.
 2. Newton's default material averaging for stiffness parameters was bypassed. This routing modification approximately improved the gradient magnitude for contact parameters by 50x.
- **Centroid Silhouette Loss:** To disentangle positional supervision from appearance quality, a centroid silhouette loss (L_sil) is introduced. Standard pixel-wise photometric losses can conflate errors in object position with errors arising from the fixed (pre-occlusion) appearance of the object Gaussians when rendered from novel viewpoints. L_sil involves transforming the object Gaussians to the simulated SE(3) pose, rendering an alpha map, and comparing the rendered centroid (weighted average of pixel positions by alpha values) against the ground-truth mask centroid using an L2 norm. This loss provides a global basin of attraction and produces significantly larger friction gradients (approximately 100x) compared to photometric loss, as centroid displacement is a first-order function of position.
- **Observability-Aware Curriculum:** The estimation process is structured into three phases to exploit the varying identifiability of parameters:
 1. **Phase 1 (60 iterations):** Focuses on estimating initial velocity (v₀) using pre-contact frames where friction has no influence on trajectory. Friction is frozen, and velocity is warm-started from finite differences.
 2. **Phase 2 (60 iterations):** Focuses on estimating friction (μ) using a window of post-contact frames. Velocity is frozen.
 3. **Phase 3 (80 iterations):** Jointly refines both friction and velocity with a reduced learning rate (0.3x).
 Five random initializations are run per scene, with the best-performing result (lowest loss) selected.

#### 3.3. Physics-Guided Reconstruction

Once the physical parameters are estimated, they drive a full forward simulation to generate a dynamically consistent SE(3) trajectory spanning the entire occlusion period.
- **Gaussian Application:** The static background Gaussians remain fixed. The object Gaussians are transformed frame-by-frame according to the predicted SE(3) trajectory. This involves updating positions (μ' = R_t μ_can + t_t) and quaternions (q' = q_R_t ⊗ q_can), while scales remain unchanged as the motion is rigid. For spherical harmonics, an inverse object rotation is applied to the viewing direction before evaluation to achieve the correct appearance for the rotated object.
- **Visibility-Dependent Optimization:**
 - **Visible Frames:** During frames where the object is observable, per-frame residual translations are optimized to minimize photometric error against training views. This corrects for small positional discrepancies that may arise from imperfect physics parameter estimation.
 - **Occluded Frames:** During the occlusion period, the object's Gaussians are positioned purely based on the physics-predicted trajectory. Residual optimization is disabled, and no gradients propagate to the object representation, ensuring physical consistency without external photometric supervision.
 - **Re-emergence:** Upon the object's re-emergence, residual optimization and photometric supervision resume, facilitating a smooth transition back to vision-guided reconstruction.
- **Rendering:** All Gaussians (background and object) are composited in a single differentiable rasterization pass.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of PersistGS was conducted on three synthetic scenes designed to feature rigid balls interacting with a static environment, including ground planes and occluding walls, where contact events occur specifically during occlusion. These scenes were "ball fall" (long occlusion, ground bounce), "ball bounce" (hidden second bounce), and "ball roll" (friction-based deceleration). Each scene used 5 training cameras and 2 evaluation cameras (overhead and side views, withheld from training) at 512x512 resolution, 60 fps.

#### 4.1. Parameter Estimation

- **Accuracy:** The proposed method accurately recovered physical parameters. Friction coefficients were estimated within a 10% error margin across all scenes. Initial velocity components (vₓ, vᵧ, v₂) were estimated with an error of less than 0.5 m/s.
- **Trajectory Error:** The 3D trajectory Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) during occlusion periods ranged from 0.288 on "ball roll" to 1.147 on "ball fall," indicating a reasonable level of accuracy for the physics-predicted paths.

#### 4.2. Reconstruction Quality During Occlusion

- **Overall Performance:** PersistGS achieved a mean PSNR of 17.15 dB on evaluation cameras during occlusion, demonstrating improved reconstruction fidelity. This significantly outperformed the constant velocity baseline by +2.46 dB PSNR and the linear interpolation baseline by +1.41 dB PSNR. The method approached the ground-truth trajectory upper bound with a small gap of 0.19 dB (17.34 dB for GT). LPIPS metrics also showed consistent improvements across scenes, with PersistGS achieving a mean LPIPS of 0.314 compared to 0.381 for linear interpolation and 0.491 for constant velocity.
- **Scene-Specific Advantages:**
 - On "ball fall," characterized by a long occlusion (248 frames) and a ground bounce during the unobserved interval, PersistGS outperformed linear interpolation by +3.78 dB, highlighting its ability to model nonlinear contact trajectories.
 - For "ball bounce," where a second bounce was hidden behind an occluder, PersistGS yielded a +3.09 dB advantage over the constant velocity baseline.
 - On "ball roll," which featured a shorter (45 frames) and nearly linear occlusion path with friction, linear interpolation performed comparably to PersistGS (+0.17 dB gap). However, constant velocity exhibited a substantial deficit of -2.66 dB due to its inability to account for friction.
- **Trajectory RMSE during Occlusion:** The quantitative analysis of trajectory RMSE further supported these findings. Constant velocity demonstrated significant divergence on "ball fall" (8.936 RMSE) and "ball bounce" (5.716 RMSE), confirming its inadequacy in modeling contact interactions. PersistGS maintained significantly lower trajectory errors (mean 0.600 RMSE).

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies

- **Centroid vs. Photometric Loss:** The centroid silhouette loss, introduced for physics parameter estimation, demonstrated superior performance compared to a masked pixel-wise Mean Squared Error (MSE) photometric loss. It achieved 40% lower mean trajectory RMSE (0.586 vs. 0.984) and +0.41 dB higher PSNR. This advantage was most pronounced on "ball fall" (3.4x lower RMSE), attributed to the centroid loss generating approximately 100x larger friction gradients compared to photometric loss.
- **Noise Tolerance:** The method exhibited graceful degradation in reconstruction quality when i.i.d. noise was added to the ground-truth trajectory, with approximately 1 dB PSNR drop for every σ=0.25 increment of noise. Even with substantial noise (σ=2.0), the PSNR still exceeded the "no physics" baseline, indicating robustness to estimation inaccuracies. The estimated trajectory RMSE values (0.29–1.15) fell within a range where high-quality reconstruction was maintained.
- **Occlusion Duration:** The advantage of the physics-based approach over constant velocity increased with longer occlusion durations, confirming that kinematic extrapolation accumulates errors over time, while physics respects underlying contact constraints.
- **Visible Frame Count:** Complex trajectories (e.g., "ball fall") showed improvement in PSNR with more visible frames used for estimation, while simpler dynamics ("ball bounce") maintained stable performance with fewer observations.
- **Sparse-View Regularization:** The regularization techniques (Mip-Splatting, DropGaussian, depth supervision) were critical. Without them, reconstruction quality on "ball bounce" and "ball roll" degraded by +3.0 dB and +4.2 dB PSNR, respectively, on evaluation cameras.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The PersistGS method addresses a fundamental challenge in dynamic 4D scene reconstruction: maintaining object permanence when objects are temporarily occluded. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Principled Approach to Occlusion:** The work establishes differentiable rigid body simulation as a principled, physically informed alternative to purely generative priors for filling temporal observation gaps in 4D Gaussian Splatting. By focusing on dynamic consistency rather than just visual plausibility, it offers a more robust and accurate method for predicting object locations during unobserved intervals.
- **Enhanced Reconstruction Fidelity:** The empirical results demonstrate that incorporating physics priors significantly improves the quality of 4D reconstructions during occlusion periods. PersistGS consistently outperforms kinematic baselines, especially in scenarios involving complex contact events (e.g., bounces, friction), where traditional extrapolation methods fail. The ability to approach ground-truth quality within a small margin (0.19 dB PSNR) highlights its effectiveness.
- **Causality and Predictive Power:** Unlike non-causal methods such as linear interpolation (which require future information), PersistGS provides a causal prediction of an object's trajectory through occlusion. This characteristic is essential for real-world applications that necessitate real-time reasoning about unobserved objects, such as robotic planning, autonomous navigation, and the maintenance of digital twins, where agents must act without foreknowledge of future states.
- **Methodological Contributions:**
 - The introduction of a centroid silhouette loss provides a robust and efficient mechanism for supervising physics parameter estimation, decoupling positional gradients from appearance noise and yielding significantly lower trajectory errors compared to photometric losses.
 - The observability-aware curriculum for parameter estimation effectively leverages the varying identifiability of physical parameters across different stages of object motion (pre-contact vs. post-contact).
 - The modifications to the differentiable physics simulator (NVIDIA Newton) to ensure effective gradient flow through contact dynamics are a notable technical advancement, enabling the practical application of physics-based optimization.
- **Architectural Validation:** The research reinforces the utility of decomposed, object-centric architectures for dynamic 3DGS, particularly in sparse-view settings. This design choice, demonstrated to mitigate overfitting, aligns well with physics-based trajectory supervision, which benefits from explicit object-level representations.
- **Future Directions:** The study suggests several avenues for future work, including extending the centroid silhouette loss to asymmetric objects by incorporating silhouette covariance for joint translation and rotation constraint. Further research could involve scaling the approach to multi-object scenes with inter-object contact and integrating automatic object decomposition methods to remove the requirement for known object segmentations. The concept of iterative refinement of physics parameters using post-occlusion observations also shows promise for further accuracy improvements.

In conclusion, PersistGS represents a substantial step towards more physically accurate and robust 4D scene reconstruction, particularly in challenging scenarios involving occluded dynamic objects. By providing a principled and causal mechanism for maintaining object permanence, it contributes to the development of more intelligent systems capable of understanding and interacting with dynamic environments.
