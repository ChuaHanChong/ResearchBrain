# Learning Social Navigation from Positive and Negative Demonstrations and Rule-Based Specifications

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers developed PioneeR, a framework for mobile robot social navigation that integrates density-based reward learning from positive and negative demonstrations with rule-based safety and goal-reaching specifications. This approach yielded higher success rates, up to 99.6% in challenging elevator co-boarding simulations, and demonstrated effective real-world deployment on a mobile robot in human-dense environments.

## Problem

- Mobile robots struggle with balancing adaptability to diverse human behaviors and ensuring strict safety in shared, dynamic environments.
- Classical navigation methods often rely on handcrafted rules, limiting their generalization in complex social contexts.
- Purely learning-based approaches, such as reinforcement or imitation learning, frequently lack explicit safety guarantees or require extensive training resources.

## Method

- PioneeR, a teacher-student framework, learns a composite reward function combining density-based rewards from positive and negative demonstrations with rule-based obstacle avoidance and goal-seeking terms.
- A teacher policy, utilizing short-horizon lookahead control and an adaptive weighting scheme for its reward components, provides robust supervision.
- An uncertainty-aware ==Mixture Density Network (MDN)== is distilled as a compact student policy via dataset aggregation (DAgger) for real-time operation and risk assessment.

## Results

- PioneeR achieved high success rates (**99.4%** HR-RL, **99.6%** HL-RR, outperforming CVaR-BF/CrowdNav++ by up to **26.6%**), and its MDN student policy achieved even higher rates (**98.0%**/**100.0%** vs an MLP), with uncertainty estimates correlating with proximity to humans in risky frames.
- Ablation studies confirmed the critical role of the learned density reward for resolving congested interactions and the complementary benefits of rule-based priors for safety and efficiency.
- Real-world experiments validated the framework's practical deployability, demonstrating successful single-policy navigation in diverse elevator co-boarding scenarios with human participants.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating density-based rewards derived from both positive and negative demonstrations effectively encodes nuanced human navigation preferences, including both desired and undesired behaviors.
> - Combining data-driven adaptability from learned rewards with explicit rule-based safety and goal constraints provides a robust balance for navigation in dynamic, human-shared environments.
> - Uncertainty estimates from the student MDN policy can serve as valuable indicators of navigation risk, potentially enabling more conservative or risk-aware fallback strategies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Mobile robot navigation in environments shared with humans presents challenges related to safety, adaptability, and real-time operation. Current research approaches can be broadly categorized into classical methods, learning-based methods, and hybrid frameworks.

Classical approaches, such as window-based search, potential fields, velocity–obstacle formulations, and control barrier functions, often provide interpretability and explicit safety guarantees. However, these methods typically rely on meticulously specified objectives and handcrafted rules, which can limit their generalization capabilities in complex, socially dynamic contexts characterized by diverse human behaviors and interactions.

Learning-based methods, including reinforcement learning and imitation learning, aim to capture human interaction patterns directly from data. Reinforcement learning can produce socially compliant behaviors but often requires extensive reward shaping and substantial training budgets. Imitation learning is generally more data-efficient but can be susceptible to distributional shifts and may lack explicit mechanisms for ensuring safety.

Hybrid frameworks integrate learning components with rule-based or optimization modules to combine adaptability with structural safety. While these approaches address some limitations of purely classical or learning-based methods, they can still depend on handcrafted logic and are sometimes evaluated in simplified environments.

This paper positions itself within the hybrid paradigm, addressing the limitations of existing methods by proposing a framework that integrates data-driven rewards with rule-based objectives. The work focuses on developing navigation policies that achieve a balance between adaptability to diverse human behaviors—a strength of learning-based approaches—and compliance with safety constraints—a strength of classical approaches. Specifically, it leverages both positive and negative demonstrations to learn a nuanced social reward, which is then augmented with explicit safety and goal-reaching rules. This integration seeks to provide a more robust and generalizable solution for navigation in safety-critical, human-shared environments than approaches relying solely on one paradigm.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a mobile robot navigation framework that effectively balances adaptability to diverse human behaviors with compliance to safety constraints in dynamic human environments. The authors hypothesize that integrating data-driven rewards with rule-based objectives can achieve a more effective balance of these two requirements.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges inherent in socially aware robot navigation:
- **Uncertainty in Human Intent and Variability in Motion Patterns:** Humans exhibit diverse and unpredictable behaviors, making it difficult for robots to anticipate and react appropriately using static rules.
- **Dense Interactions and Bottlenecks:** Crowded environments, such as elevator co-boarding scenarios, involve close-proximity interactions and potential bottlenecks that demand sophisticated negotiation strategies.
- **Compliance with Social Conventions and Right-of-Way:** Robots need to navigate not only physically safely but also in a socially acceptable manner, respecting unspoken rules and conventions.
- **Strict Real-Time Requirements:** Navigation policies must operate efficiently on embedded platforms to enable real-world deployment.
- **Limitations of Existing Methods:** As noted, classical methods struggle with generalization in dynamic social contexts, while learning-based methods often lack explicit safety guarantees or demand extensive resources.

To address these motivations, the paper outlines three main contributions:
1. **A novel reward formulation:** This formulation integrates density-based learning from both positive and negative demonstrations with rule-based specifications for obstacle avoidance and goal reaching. The goal is to encode human-informed navigation preferences while enforcing explicit safety.
2. **A teacher-student policy framework:** A teacher policy, built on the combined reward formulation, provides adaptive and safe supervision by evaluating short-horizon rollouts. This is coupled with an uncertainty-aware distillation process that yields a compact student policy suitable for real-time operation.
3. **Evaluation in challenging scenarios:** The effectiveness of the proposed approach is assessed in elevator co-boarding scenarios, both in simulation and through real-world trials. This evaluation aims to confirm the benefits of combining data-driven adaptability with rule-based safety in environments characterized by dense human interactions.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework, named PioneeR (Positive and negative demonstration density–driven rewards with Rule-based specifications), is designed for mobile robot navigation in dynamic human environments. It employs a teacher-student paradigm, where a sophisticated teacher policy generates supervision for a compact student policy.

**A. State and Observation Formulation**
The robot's state is defined as `x = (p_x, p_y, θ)`, representing its 2D position and heading angle, following a unicycle model. The control input `u = [v, ω]^T` consists of translational and rotational velocities.
Observations `o` are derived from LiDAR measurements and geometric descriptors. A LiDAR scan is aggregated into `K` features by taking minimum ranges within groups of beams. Additionally, `b` nearest obstacle descriptors (distance and angle) and the relative goal angle are included. This downsampling produces a compact `R^29` observation vector.

**B. Teacher Policy via Reward Design**
The teacher policy evaluates short-horizon rollouts of candidate velocity commands and selects the action leading to the highest return. This return is calculated using a composite reward function that integrates learned and rule-based components.

1. **Density Reward Learning from Positive and Negative Demonstrations:**
 - A reward function `R(x)` over state-action pairs `x = (s, a)` is learned to align reward values with the empirical occupancy of demonstrated behavior. This is achieved by maximizing `⟨ˆμ, R⟩`, where `ˆμ` is the empirical state-action density from demonstrations.
 - The reward `R` is represented in a Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS) using a positive semidefinite kernel `k`, inducing points `U`, and coefficients `α`.
 - The optimization problem for `α` includes quadratic regularization terms (weighted by `λ` and `β`) for smoothness and numerical stability, leading to an analytic solution for `α`.
 - To incorporate demonstrations of varying quality, each sample is augmented with a fidelity score `γ ∈ [−1, 1]`. Positive demonstrations are assigned `γ = +1` and negative demonstrations `γ = −1`.
 - A smooth leveraged kernel `k_SL` is employed, which modulates cross-sample similarity using the fidelity scores: `k_SL((x, γ), (x', γ')) = cos(π/2 * (γ - γ')) * k_PSD(x, x')`, where `k_PSD` is a squared exponential (SE) kernel. This kernel construction allows the reward estimator to be explicitly sensitive to the distinction between positive and negative samples, shaping the density-based reward map to reflect both desired and undesired behaviors.

2. **Sampling-Based Lookahead Control:**
 - At each control cycle, candidate actions `u` are generated and evaluated through short-horizon forward simulations (e.g., `T = 3` s, `∆t = 0.3` s). An exponential moving average is applied to successive velocity commands for stability.
 - The total score for a rollout `ξ` is a weighted sum of three components:
 `R_PioneeR(ξ) = α_den. R_den.(ξ) + α_goal R_goal(ξ) + α_obs. R_obs.(ξ)`
 - **Learned Reward (R_den.):** This term averages the density-based reward `r_den.(o_ℓ)` (from Sec. IV-A.1) over the rollout's observations, capturing physical occupancy and socially compliant preferences learned from demonstrations.
 - **Goal Reward (R_goal):** This term encourages progress towards the target, defined as `1 - tanh(d_goal / d_total)`, where `d_goal` is the distance from the rollout's terminal pose to the goal, and `d_total` is the initial start-goal distance.
 - **Obstacle Reward (R_obs.):** This term promotes clearance from obstacles, calculated as `tanh(1/L * Σ d_ℓ)`, where `d_ℓ` is the minimum clearance at each step `ℓ` of the rollout, derived from LiDAR observations.
 - **Adaptive Weighting:** The weights `α_den.`, `α_goal`, and `α_obs.` are dynamically adjusted based on the remaining goal distance `r = clip(d_goal / d_total, 0, 1)`. Specifically, `α_den. = 1 + cos(π(1 - r))`, `α_goal = 2(1 - r)`, and `α_obs. = 1`. This scheme prioritizes the density reward initially, shifts towards goal-directed behavior as the robot approaches the target, and maintains constant emphasis on obstacle avoidance.

**C. Uncertainty-Aware Distillation**
The teacher policy, requiring forward simulation, is not suitable for real-time deployment. Therefore, it is distilled into a compact student policy `π_ϕ(u | o)` using dataset aggregation (DAgger).
- The student policy is parameterized as a Mixture Density Network (MDN), which models the conditional distribution over velocity commands as a Gaussian mixture. The MDN predicts mixture weights, means, and diagonal covariances, trained to maximize the likelihood of the teacher's actions.
- An additional property of the MDN is its ability to provide uncertainty estimates (aleatoric and epistemic) through the law of total variance. These estimates serve as indicators of navigation risk.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of PioneeR was evaluated through a series of experiments in synthetic, dynamic simulation, and real-world environments.

**A. Synthetic Study in a Static Environment**
A static planar environment was used to illustrate how positive and negative demonstrations shape the learned reward maps and influence navigation.
- **Positive-only demonstrations:** Training with only positive samples highlighted both feasible corridors around a central human, with a bias towards the more frequently demonstrated path.
- **Positive and Negative demonstrations:** Incorporating negative demonstrations (e.g., human collisions) reduced reward values in unsafe regions near the human, resulting in preferred trajectories that maintained greater clearance.
- **Combined with Rule-Based Specifications:** Further augmenting the learned reward with rule-based obstacle avoidance and goal-seeking terms yielded trajectories that maintained clearance around the human while efficiently achieving the navigation objective. These results indicate that the density-based reward effectively captures navigation preferences, while rule-based terms enforce explicit safety.

**B. Dynamic Environments in Elevator Co-Boarding Simulation**
PioneeR was evaluated in a realistic elevator co-boarding simulation where humans were modeled using a social force model. Two scenarios were considered: HR-RL (Human-Right, Robot-Left) and HL-RR (Human-Left, Robot-Right). Evaluation metrics included success rate (SR), total time (TT), and path length (PL).

1. **Navigation Performance:**
 - PioneeR achieved high success rates (99.4% in HR-RL, 99.6% in HL-RR) while maintaining low total time and path length.
 - Compared to baseline methods, CVaR-BF and CrowdNav++, PioneeR demonstrated higher success rates. For example, in HR-RL, PioneeR achieved 99.4% SR compared to CVaR-BF's 72.8% and CrowdNav++'s 78.6%. Similar improvements were observed in HL-RR. This suggests that the integration of demonstration-driven rewards with rule-based safety specifications contributes to reliable and efficient navigation.

2. **Ablation Study of Reward Terms:**
 - Removing the density-based reward (`R_den.`) resulted in the largest degradation in success rate (e.g., 83.0% in HR-RL vs. 99.4% with all terms), indicating its importance for resolving congested interactions.
 - Excluding the obstacle prior (`R_obs.`) led to reduced total time but decreased success rates.
 - Excluding the goal prior (`R_goal`) increased total time due to less directed motion.
 - These ablation results confirm the complementary roles of both learned rewards and rule-based priors in balancing adaptability, efficiency, and safety.

3. **Uncertainty-Aware Distillation:**
 - The teacher policy was distilled into a Mixture Density Network (MDN) and compared to a standard Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP).
 - The PioneeR-MDN student policy achieved higher success rates (98.0% in HR-RL, 100.0% in HL-RR) compared to PioneeR-MLP (95.8% in HR-RL, 98.8% in HL-RR), while also yielding shorter travel times and path lengths.
 - Analysis of predictive uncertainty revealed that both epistemic and aleatoric uncertainty values were consistently higher in "risky" frames (human-robot distance < 0.8 m) compared to "safe" frames. For instance, in HR-RL, epistemic uncertainty was 0.382 in risky frames versus 0.290 in safe frames. These findings suggest that the MDN provides informative signals regarding potential hazards, which could be utilized for risk-aware fallback strategies.

**C. Real-World Demonstrations**
Real-world experiments were conducted with human participants in elevator co-boarding scenarios using a four-wheel mobile robot.
- The robot successfully adapted to varied human motions in a single-policy deployment across diverse co-boarding situations.
- The robot also navigated encounters involving multiple humans effectively.
- These demonstrations indicate the practical deployability of the framework for safe, efficient, and socially compliant navigation in human-robot interaction settings beyond simulation.

**D. Limitations**
The authors acknowledge two main limitations:
- The human motion simulation relies on a social-force model, which may not fully capture the variability of real pedestrian behaviors.
- While the reward learning formulation supports a continuous fidelity variable `γ`, the practical implementation used binary labels (+1/-1), potentially limiting the capture of nuanced demonstration quality.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of mobile robot navigation by offering a framework that systematically integrates data-driven learning with explicit safety specifications. The core significance lies in its capacity to address the dual requirements of adaptability and safety, which are often at odds in dynamic human environments.

The development of a density-based reward function derived from both positive and negative demonstrations represents an advance in learning social navigation preferences. By explicitly modeling undesired behaviors, the framework can more robustly guide robots away from unsafe or socially unacceptable actions. This learned reward, when combined with rule-based objectives for obstacle avoidance and goal reaching, creates a comprehensive navigation policy that exhibits higher reliability and efficiency compared to methods relying solely on either data or rules. The adaptive weighting scheme for these reward components further refines the robot's behavior, enabling context-aware transitions between safety, adaptability, and efficiency throughout a task.

The teacher-student distillation approach is significant for practical deployment. By distilling the robust but computationally intensive teacher policy into a compact, real-time-capable student policy (MDN), the framework facilitates operation on embedded platforms. Furthermore, the ability of the MDN student policy to provide uncertainty estimates offers a mechanism for risk awareness during navigation. This predictive uncertainty can serve as a valuable signal for implementing conservative fallback strategies in potentially hazardous situations, thereby enhancing the overall safety of autonomous navigation in uncertain human-robot interaction scenarios.

The consistent improvements in success rates and efficiency observed in challenging elevator co-boarding simulations, coupled with successful real-world demonstrations, indicate the practical utility and robustness of the PioneeR framework. This work has the potential to impact the deployment of autonomous mobile robots in a variety of public and shared spaces, where reliable, safe, and socially compliant navigation is crucial. The methodology provides a pathway for developing robot behaviors that are not only effective but also acceptable to humans, fostering greater trust and acceptance of robotic agents in everyday environments.
