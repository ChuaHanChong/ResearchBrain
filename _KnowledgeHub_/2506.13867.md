# ATK: Automatic Task-driven Keypoint Selection for Robust Policy Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> The ATK framework from the University of Washington presents an automatic task-driven method for selecting a minimal set of 2D keypoints to learn robust visuomotor policies. This approach yields higher success rates, averaging around 64% in sim-to-real tasks, and improved generalization compared to baseline methods, particularly under visual disturbances and for challenging object types.

## Problem

- Visuomotor policies exhibit brittleness and lack generalization to real-world complexities due to the inherent "perceptual gap" between training and deployment environments.
- Existing visual representations (e.g., raw pixels, 6D pose estimation) struggle with diverse visual variations, unseen objects, or require extensive task-specific engineering.
- Manually identifying and selecting optimal task-relevant visual features for robotic policy learning is inefficient, non-scalable, and prone to human bias.

## Method

- **ATK** utilizes robust ==2D keypoints== as the visual state representation, leveraging pre-trained ==correspondence functions== for consistent tracking across frames and environments.
- It employs a distillation-based process with a ==sparse masking model== to automatically select a minimal set of task-relevant keypoints from a pool of candidates.
- Keypoint selection and the downstream policy are jointly learned through an optimization objective that encourages both predictive accuracy for actions and sparsity in the selected keypoints.

## Results

- ATK policies achieved an average success rate of approximately **64%** in sim-to-real transfer tasks under various visual disturbances, substantially outperforming RGB, Depth, and PointCloud baselines (e.g., **2-4%**).
- Policies learned with ATK demonstrated high robustness in real-world imitation learning for complex tasks (e.g., deformable object manipulation), maintaining performance even under significant visual variations like random object poses or distractors.
- Qualitative analyses show that ATK consistently selects interpretable, semantically meaningful, and transferable keypoints that align with task execution requirements, surpassing heuristic and GPT-based keypoint selection methods.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A minimal, automatically selected set of task-driven 2D keypoints can effectively encode sufficient information for robust policy learning, outperforming methods that use full visual inputs or all keypoints.
> - Integrating keypoint selection directly into the policy learning process through distillation is crucial for identifying keypoints that are both reliably trackable and highly predictive of optimal actions.
> - Focusing on task-relevant visual cues via selected keypoints significantly enhances the robustness and generalization capabilities of visuomotor policies against diverse visual disturbances and challenging object properties.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of visuomotor policy learning has made significant strides, enabling robots to perform complex tasks by learning directly from visual inputs. However, a pervasive challenge remains: the brittleness and lack of generalization of these policies when faced with real-world complexities. Policies often suffer from "perceptual challenges," where minor visual discrepancies between training and evaluation environments (e.g., changes in lighting, background, object pose, or the presence of distractors) severely degrade performance.

Existing approaches to visuomotor control generally fall into a few categories:
- **Raw Sensor-based Policies (e.g., RGB, Depth, Point Clouds):** These methods often leverage powerful pre-trained visual representations (e.g., DINOv2, R3M, MAE) obtained through self-supervised learning on large datasets. While improving sample efficiency and initial robustness, these representations can still entangle task-irrelevant features, leading to brittleness under distribution shifts. Policies directly operating on raw pixels struggle with visual variations not seen during training.
- **State Estimation-based Policies (e.g., 6D Pose Estimation):** These policies rely on accurate estimations of object poses or other explicit state variables. While robust if estimations are perfect, they require task-specific tracking modules, are difficult to scale to novel objects or deformable ones, and can fail with challenging appearances (e.g., transparent, reflective objects).
- **Domain Randomization (DR):** A common sim-to-real technique that attempts to bridge the perceptual gap by randomizing visual parameters in simulation during training. While effective to some extent, it requires careful tuning of randomization parameters and may not cover all real-world variations, especially for fine-grained tasks.
- **Latent Representation Learning and Image Translation:** Methods that try to learn invariant representations or translate images between domains to close the sim-to-real gap. These still face challenges in handling complex tasks or require specific assumptions.

This paper positions **2D keypoints** as a flexible and robust visual state representation that offers a compelling alternative to existing methods. Keypoints, extensively used in computer vision for tracking semantically meaningful points, have demonstrated robustness to occlusions, lighting changes, and scale variations, particularly with recent advances in web-scale pre-trained trackers (e.g., TAPIR, Cotracker, diffusion features). Unlike 6D pose, keypoints don't rely on rigid structures, making them suitable for articulated and deformable objects, and they generalize well to challenging appearances like transparent objects.

The novelty of ATK lies not just in using keypoints, but in addressing the critical question: *What is the minimal set of task-relevant keypoints that can serve as an effective state representation for decision making?* Simply using all keypoints is inefficient and prone to noise, while arbitrary selection risks missing crucial information. ATK proposes a principled, task-driven method for *automatically selecting* these keypoints, moving beyond heuristic or manual selection common in prior keypoint-based control works. By integrating keypoint selection directly into the policy learning process via distillation, ATK aims to extract the minimal, most predictive visual information for a given task, thereby improving robustness and transferability in both sim-to-real and real-world imitation learning settings. This addresses a significant gap in robust visual representation learning for robot manipulation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching motivation behind ATK is to overcome the **brittleness and lack of generalization** that currently plague visuomotor policies in robotics. Despite advancements in deep learning and visual representation learning, policies often struggle to transfer from simulation to reality (sim-to-real) or generalize across varying conditions in real-world imitation learning. This brittleness arises from the inherent "perceptual gap" and sensitivity to visual disturbances.

The key objectives of this research are:

1. **Develop a general-purpose yet tailorable visual representation:** The authors aim to find a representation that is robust to environmental variations (e.g., distractors, lighting, background changes) and transferable across different scenarios, while still being flexible enough to encode task-specific information. They propose 2D keypoints as this representation due to their inherent robustness and ability to track semantically meaningful features.
2. **Automatically select task-relevant keypoints:** Recognizing that the optimal set of keypoints varies by task, the core objective is to design a method, ATK, that automatically identifies a *minimal set* of keypoints crucial for successful task execution. This selection should be "task-driven," meaning the chosen keypoints are highly predictive of optimal actions for that specific task.
3. **Jointly learn keypoint selection and policy:** To avoid the "chicken-and-egg" problem (needing a good representation to learn a policy, but needing an optimal policy to identify the right representation), the method aims to jointly optimize for both the minimal keypoint set and the policy conditioned on these keypoints. This is achieved through a distillation-based process leveraging expert data.
4. **Demonstrate robust transfer and generalization:** The ultimate objective is to validate that policies learned with ATK's selected keypoints can effectively transfer from simulation to reality and generalize robustly in imitation learning settings, maintaining high performance even under significant visual variations and perceptual challenges (e.g., transparent objects, fine-grained tasks, deformable objects).

In essence, the motivation stems from the need for more reliable and deployable robotic systems that can operate effectively in unstructured, visually diverse real-world environments without requiring extensive re-training or explicit state estimation scaffolding for every new task or variation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

ATK (Automatic Task-driven Keypoint Selection) proposes a novel methodology for robust policy learning by jointly selecting a minimal set of task-relevant 2D keypoints and training a policy conditioned on these selected keypoints. The approach is designed for both sim-to-real transfer and real-world imitation learning.

#### 3.1. Keypoints as Policy Representations

The core idea is to use 2D keypoints – salient (x,y) pixel locations in an image – as the visual state representation. Keypoints are advantageous because they are spatially consistent, robustly trackable across frames (even with occlusion, lighting changes, and scale variations, thanks to large-scale pre-trained visual trackers like Cotracker and diffusion-feature models), and more flexible than 6D pose for articulated or deformable objects. A set of N keypoints, $\{k_t^i\}_{i=1}^N$, provides a compact scene representation. The paper utilizes a correspondence function, $h_C$, to track these keypoints over time, maintaining semantic consistency.

#### 3.2. Automatic Task-Driven Keypoint Selection: Training Process

The crucial aspect is selecting the *minimal set* of *task-relevant* keypoints (K, where K << N). This selection is guided by two criteria:
1. **Realizability of the optimal policy:** The selected keypoints must contain enough information to learn a near-optimal policy.
2. **Trackability:** The chosen keypoints must be reliably and consistently trackable by $h_C$.

The ATK algorithm addresses the challenge of joint selection and policy learning through a distillation-based process:

1. **Canonical Image and Candidate Keypoints:** A single "canonical template image," $I_{can}$, is chosen to capture the complete task context. On this image, $C$ candidate keypoints, $\{k_j^0\}_{j=1}^C$, are randomly sampled.
2. **Keypoint Propagation and Data Annotation:** Using the correspondence function $h_C$, these candidate keypoints are aligned and propagated across all trajectories and timesteps within an expert dataset $D = \{(o_i, a_i)\}_{i=1}^M$. This creates a keypoint-annotated dataset $D_k = \{(o_{i,t}, K_{i,t}, a_i)\}$, where $K_{i,t}$ is the set of all tracked candidate keypoint positions for observation $o_{i,t}$.
3. **Joint Masking Model and Policy Training:** A sparse masking model $M_\phi$ and a downstream keypoint-based policy $\pi_k^\theta$ are jointly learned via supervised learning.
 - **Masking Model ($M_\phi$):** The $C$ candidate keypoints are fed into $M_\phi$. This model outputs $C$ independent Bernoulli probabilities, indicating the likelihood of retaining each corresponding keypoint.
 - **Binary Mask Sampling:** A binary mask $m \in \{0, 1\}^C$ is sampled, which zeroes out (deselects) keypoints that are not chosen, yielding a reduced set of selected keypoints, $\tilde{K}$.
 - **Policy ($ \pi_k^\theta $):** The selected keypoints $\tilde{K}$ are then passed to $\pi_k^\theta$, which produces the action distribution.
 - **Optimization Objective:** The training minimizes a loss function that combines the negative log-likelihood of the expert actions given the masked keypoints and an $L_1$ sparsity penalty on the mask:
 $ \min_{\phi, \theta} -\mathbb{E}_{(k,a) \sim D_k} \left[ \log \pi_k^\theta(a_t^* | M_\phi(\{k_t^i\}_{i=1}^N)) \right] + \alpha \|M_\phi(\{k_t^i\}_{i=1}^N)\|_1 $.
 This objective ensures that the selected keypoints are both predictive of optimal actions and minimal in number. Keypoints difficult to track reliably by $h_C$ will also be filtered out because their unreliable representations won't consistently predict optimal actions.
 - **Gumbel-softmax Relaxation:** Since mask sampling is discrete and non-differentiable, the Gumbel-softmax relaxation is employed to enable gradient-based optimization of the masking model.

#### 3.3. Inference with Task-Driven Keypoints

Once trained, the masking model $M_\phi$ and policy $\pi_k^\theta$ are used for inference:

1. **Keypoint Transfer:** The initial set of keypoints selected during training is transferred to the new test scene. This involves using the correspondence function $h_C$ to find the highest-score matches between the training canonical keypoints and the initial image of the test scenario.
2. **Tracking:** These transferred keypoints are then tracked robustly over time in the test environment using $h_C$ (e.g., Cotracker).
3. **Policy Execution:** The tracked keypoints are directly fed as input to the deployed policy $\pi_\theta$, without needing additional masking at test time (as the selection has already been finalized during training).

The choice of $h_C$ is critical; for initial keypoint transfer, methods like diffusion-feature are used for their visual robustness, while Cotracker handles subsequent temporal tracking.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

ATK was evaluated on a variety of robotic manipulation tasks, covering both sim-to-real transfer and robust imitation learning, under diverse visual disturbances.

#### 4.1. Sim-to-Real Transfer of Keypoint-based Policies

- **Tasks:** Sushi pick-and-place, glass pot tip lifting (challenging due to reflectivity and small target), clock manipulation (two subtasks: button press, hand turning, involving articulated objects).
- **Performance:** ATK policies demonstrated significantly higher success rates in the real world compared to baselines using alternative input modalities (RGB, Depth, PointCloud).
- **Robustness:** ATK showed strong resilience to various environmental variations introduced during evaluation, including random object poses (RP), background texture shuffling (RB), random distractor objects (RO), and altered lighting (Light). While extreme disturbances could reduce performance, ATK consistently outperformed baselines.
- **Specific Strengths:** The performance gap was particularly notable in tasks involving transparent objects (e.g., glass pot) and fine-grained manipulation (e.g., clock tasks), where traditional vision systems struggle. For instance, ATK achieved average success rates around 64% across tasks and disturbances, vastly outperforming RGB (4%), Depth (4%), and Pointcloud (2%) policies.

#### 4.2. Robust Imitation Learning with Keypoint-based Policies

- **Tasks:** Grape-oven, blanket hanging (deformable), towel folding (deformable, few distinct markers), pan filling (multi-object, multi-step). All tasks were in a multifunctional kitchen environment.
- **Performance:** ATK-learned policies were highly resilient to visual disturbances (RP, RB, RO, Light) even though training occurred under a single emission function (one visual setting).
- **Comparison to Keypoint Selection Baselines:** The specific task-driven selection by ATK was crucial. It outperformed:
 - **FullSet:** Using all sampled keypoints, which suffered from redundancy and variations.
 - **RandomSelect:** Randomly selecting the same number of keypoints as ATK, often missing critical task-relevant information.
 - **GPTSelect:** Using GPT-4 to select keypoints based on an image and task description. GPTSelect often suffered from spatial or language mismatches, leading to erroneous selections and poor performance (e.g., 0% for Pan Filling and Grape Oven).
- **Success Rates:** ATK maintained high success rates (e.g., 85% for Blanket Hanging under RP, RB, RO) compared to RGB, Depth, PointCloud baselines (which often failed completely, achieving 0% in many disturbed conditions) and other keypoint selection methods.

#### 4.3. Qualitative Visualization of Keypoints Learned by ATK

- **Interpretability and Task-Relevance:** Visualizations showed that ATK consistently selected interpretable keypoints that directly corresponded to semantically meaningful and task-relevant parts of the scene (e.g., edges of a towel for folding, specific parts of a clock for turning, key points on a grape and inside an oven for the grape-oven task).
- **Transferability:** The chosen keypoints transferred accurately from simulation to the real world and from training to testing, demonstrating robustness to distractors, lighting, and background changes. In multifunctional environments (like the kitchen or clock), the keypoints were specialized to the specific utility of each task.

#### 4.4. Additional Evaluations

- **Category-Level Generalization:** The correspondence matching algorithm achieved high confidence scores (0.76–0.82) and low mean distance errors (2.79–6.75 pixels) for object category transfer (e.g., different types of sushi), suggesting reliable sim-to-real keypoint transfer.
- **High-precision Task (Shoe Lacing):** ATK successfully tackled a fine-grained insertion task with 1.7mm tolerance, demonstrating its applicability to high-dexterity scenarios under varied backgrounds and distractors.

In summary, ATK consistently demonstrated superior robustness and generalization across a diverse set of robotic manipulation tasks and challenging real-world visual conditions, largely attributed to its ability to automatically select minimal, task-relevant, and trackable keypoint representations.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The ATK framework represents a significant step forward in addressing the critical challenges of robustness and generalization in visuomotor policy learning for robotics. Its contributions and potential impacts are multi-faceted.

#### 5.1. Scientific Significance

- **Principled Task-Driven Representation Learning:** ATK provides a novel and principled methodology for automatically learning minimal, task-relevant visual representations directly from expert data. This moves beyond heuristic approaches to keypoint selection and implicitly filters out task-irrelevant visual features, leading to more focused and robust policies.
- **Bridging Perceptual Gaps:** By leveraging the inherent robustness of 2D keypoints (powered by advanced visual trackers) and focusing on task-relevant information, ATK effectively mitigates the perceptual gap between simulation and reality, and between varied real-world training and testing environments. This is a fundamental problem in robot learning.
- **Generalizability Across Object Properties:** The method's success with transparent, reflective, deformable, and fine-grained objects highlights its applicability to a broader range of manipulation challenges where traditional pose-estimation or raw-pixel methods often fail.
- **Interpretability:** The chosen keypoints are often semantically meaningful, offering a level of interpretability into *what* visual features the policy relies on, which can be valuable for debugging and understanding robot behavior.

#### 5.2. Practical Impact and Future Applications

- **More Robust Robot Deployment:** ATK's ability to learn policies that generalize well across visual disturbances means robots can be deployed in more varied and unstructured environments without constant retraining or brittle failures, reducing operational costs and increasing reliability.
- **Reduced Data Requirements:** By extracting compact, task-relevant representations, ATK potentially improves sample efficiency for policy learning, which is crucial for real-world robotics where data collection can be expensive and time-consuming.
- **Enhanced Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The framework offers a powerful tool for transferring policies learned in simulation to the real world, accelerating the development and deployment cycle for complex robotic tasks.
- **New Directions for Imitation Learning:** For real-world imitation learning, ATK provides a path to training policies that are inherently more robust to environmental changes, making human demonstrations more valuable and broadly applicable.
- **Foundation for Multi-Task Learning:** The work lays a foundation for future multi-task learning by proposing a mechanism for task-specific representation selection. A task-conditional module, as suggested in limitations, could further unify this approach, allowing a single system to tackle diverse skills.
