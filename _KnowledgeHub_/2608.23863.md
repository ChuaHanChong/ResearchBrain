# DreamLedger: Where to Refuse World-Model Imagination Using Execution-Settled Credit

> [!summary] Summary
> DreamLedger registers each world-model prediction a planner consumes as a claim, settles it against execution outcomes, and accumulates agreement counts in a persistent condition, region and horizon history. A calibrator fuses that history with a stop-gradient introspection head into credit, gating reliance before action. It cuts burn rate 4.8 percentage points versus random refusal and replays 1,062 hardware prediction uses.

## Problem

- Planners consume world-model predictions without any persistent record of where, under which conditions, and over which horizons those predictions previously agreed with reality.
- Instantaneous signals like ensemble disagreement, self-report confidence and learned verifiers assess only the current prediction, and internal confidence correlates weakly with task risk.
- Conventional robot logs and decision-provenance tools never identify which decisions depended on imagined futures, the credit assigned then, or how predictions later settled.

## Method

- **Settlement engine**: typed, pre-frozen predicates settle each consumed claim against arriving observations, with ==three-stage attribution== (confidence gating, residual decomposition, discard) separating model error from localization drift.
- **Contextual credit**: a ==Mondrian ladder== coarsens condition-region-horizon cells until 25 settlements support them, feeding agreement rate, support and Wilson interval width into a ==prequential logistic calibrator==.
- **Stop-gradient introspection head** on the host latent, supervised by settled on-policy outcomes, plus a ==threshold gate== whose ==fallback ladder== shortens the horizon or triggers observation.

## Results

- Ten-seed manipulation replication at matched refusal volume: burn rate **4.8** percentage points below random refusal (95% CI **0.8-8.7**), **0.276** fewer probes per episode, pooled success plus **3.8** points.
- Quadrotor gating cut burned predictions **62%**, **56%** and **44%** across model doses without lowering reach; in 2D navigation ablating or resetting history raised burn rate **0.022-0.027** at matched refusal.
- Randomized audits: denied candidates fail at **0.31-0.34** versus **0.17-0.21** for allowed; Franka replays **1,062/1,062** uses, credit falling to **0.8154** triggers refusal below frozen threshold **0.816**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Reliability is contextual and earned: agreement varies with condition, region and horizon, so one global confidence score discards the discrimination a valid partition supplies.
> - Finer partitions improve discrimination but degrade thin-bin calibration; plain empirical counting wins on dense histories, and fusion pays off only below roughly 1,000 settlements.
> - Lower burn exposure does not automatically become task value: shielded streams hide drift, and gains hold only below the measured break-even failure cost.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

DreamLedger gives a robot a memory of whether its past world-model predictions came true, and uses that memory to decide when to stop trusting new ones. In one hardware episode, a Franka arm is pushing a cylinder. Before each push it predicts where the cylinder will end up, and that predicted endpoint is checked against the endpoint later seen by its wrist camera.

The robot's credit in this prediction starts at 1.00, supported by 52 warmup agreements. Then comes one more agreement and 12 consecutive failures. Together they produce 53/65 = 0.8154, just below the frozen threshold of 0.816. The candidate prediction is therefore denied before action 14, and a probe is invoked instead. The final probe also fails, and the episode ends without reaching the goal. The episode demonstrates the gate transition. It does not show task success.

One hardware episode. The first prediction matches, the next 12 fail at 5.45–6.27 cm, and the final credit is 53/65 = 0.8154.

### 2. A prediction becomes a record

The idea is simple: record what the robot expected, see what happened, and let that history affect the next decision. DreamLedger records a consumed prediction and checks it against an observable execution outcome. It then uses settled outcomes from similar conditions, locations, and prediction horizons to inform later reliance.

The loop works in four steps:

- **Making a claim.** A claim freezes a prediction together with the rule by which it will be checked.
- **Settling it.** Settlement means checking the consumed prediction against the corresponding observed execution outcome. A failed consumed prediction is a burn.
- **Handling unclear outcomes.** Unobserved outcomes can remain pending. Outcomes that cannot be attributed to model error, rather than sensing or localization error, are discarded rather than used to update credit.
- **Consulting the record.** Before using another prediction, the planner queries a credit estimate informed by settled history and model-side signals. Sufficient credit permits reliance. Insufficient credit can shorten the imagined rollout or trigger observation.

Throughout this process, the world model itself is left unchanged. Each decision's prediction, decision-time credit, and later outcome can be replayed.

### 3. Does history pick better refusals?

A skeptic might suspect that such a system is merely more conservative. The real test is whether history changes *which* predictions are refused. Absolute burn counts can mislead here, because simply consuming fewer predictions can mechanically reduce the total number of failures. The comparisons therefore use **burn rate**: failed predictions divided by consumed predictions. It measures failure exposure among predictions actually used, not task success.

The first comparison is a ten-seed 2D-navigation battery with matched refusal rates. Each arm's realized refusal rate is matched to the ledger's, and a denied use invokes the same observation ladder. Under these conditions, every ablation that removes history features or persistence has a higher burn rate. For example, episode-local books score +0.027 in burn rate and +0.07 in reach relative to the ledger. This is a trade-off: these arms reach more often while failing on a larger fraction of the predictions they consume.

Change in burn rate and change in reach, arm minus ledger, with pooled bootstrap 95% CIs. The random-refusal burn-rate per-seed interval includes zero.

The second comparison is an independent ten-seed manipulation replication at matched refusal volume. Here, random refusal has a burn rate 4.8 percentage points higher than DreamLedger's (95% CI: 0.8–8.7). Random refusal also uses more probes. The independent task-success difference, however, is unresolved.

One qualification sits beside these results. In a quadrotor comparison with sparse histories, global risk–coverage ranking did not reliably favor the ledger over the book-blind verifier. The original ordering reversed in a fresh replication, and the pooled interval left the difference unresolved.

### 4. What is established

DreamLedger connects past execution outcomes to the next reliance decision. The evidence supports two things: changed refusal placement in the dense-history navigation battery, and an online, credit-driven gate transition on hardware. It is a deployment-time, empirical record of claim reliability under declared settlement rules. It is not model repair, formal safety certification, or a universal task-success guarantee, and task value still depends on the fallback and the cost of unfinished work.
