# CRL-VLA: Continual Vision-Language-Action Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the University of Southampton and Westlake University developed CRL-VLA, a framework for continual post-training of Vision-Language-Action models in robotics that prevents catastrophic forgetting while acquiring new skills. The method, based on controlling goal-conditioned advantage magnitude, demonstrated improved anti-forgetting, achieving a positive Backward Transfer of 0.17 in multi-task scenarios on VLA benchmarks.

## Problem

- Enabling embodied agents to continually learn new robotic manipulation skills without forgetting previously acquired abilities.
- Addressing the stability-plasticity dilemma in Continual Reinforcement Learning for large-scale, multimodal Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models.
- Limitations of current CRL methods (e.g., experience replay, second-order regularization, simple KL regularization, backbone freezing) for complex, long-horizon VLA tasks.

## Method

- A continual learning framework, **CRL-VLA**, for VLA models grounded in a theoretical understanding of ==goal-conditioned advantage magnitude==.
- A novel ==dual-critic architecture== featuring a frozen ==Goal-Conditioned Value (GCV) critic== for stability and a trainable ==Monte Carlo (MC) critic== for plasticity.
- ==Asymmetric regulation strategy== through ==GCV consistency==, MC critic learning, and policy divergence constraints (==PPO== + ==KL regularization==) for balanced stability and plasticity.

## Results

- CRL-VLA consistently outperformed baselines in continual VLA benchmarks, demonstrating superior balance of stability and plasticity.
- The CRL-VLA (V) variant achieved a positive Backward Transfer of **0.17** in multi-task learning, suggesting learning new tasks improved performance on prior ones, alongside a Final Average Return of **0.74**.
- Ablation studies validated that the dual-critic Goal-Conditioned Value Formulation and specific regularization terms are essential for CRL-VLA's effectiveness in anti-forgetting and forward adaptation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Forgetting in continual VLA learning is primarily caused by the goal-conditioned advantage magnitude.
> - Stability (retaining old knowledge) and plasticity (acquiring new skills) can be controlled independently by managing critic approximation error on old tasks and leveraging the natural boundedness of Monte Carlo returns for new tasks.
> - A Goal-Conditioned Value Formulation (GCVF) within the critic architecture is crucial for robust language-following and accurate value estimation in VLA.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research directly addresses a critical challenge in the field of artificial intelligence: enabling embodied agents to learn continuously and adapt to new tasks in dynamic, open-world environments without forgetting previously acquired skills. This concept, known as **lifelong learning** or **continual learning (CL)**, is paramount for the practical deployment of intelligent systems, especially in robotics.

The paper focuses on **Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models**, which have emerged as a prominent paradigm for unified robotic manipulation policies. VLA models combine visual perception, language understanding, and action generation, allowing robots to interpret high-level natural language instructions and execute complex physical tasks. Recent advancements in VLA models often leverage **Reinforcement Learning (RL) post-training** to fine-tune pre-trained models, enhancing their adaptability and goal-directed behavior beyond what purely supervised learning can provide.

Within this landscape, the specific problem tackled by CRL-VLA is **Continual Reinforcement Learning (CRL)** for VLA models. The core difficulty in CRL lies in the "stability-plasticity dilemma": how to ensure the agent retains (stability) old skills and knowledge while simultaneously acquiring (plasticity) new ones without "catastrophic forgetting."

The authors highlight several limitations of existing CRL methods when applied to modern VLA architectures:
- **Experience Replay (ER)** methods often suffer from off-policy errors as replayed transitions become outdated, leading to destabilization.
- **Second-order regularization** techniques are computationally prohibitive for the large scale and high dimensionality of multimodal Transformer-based VLA models.
- **KL regularization** typically imposes transition-level constraints, which are insufficient to preserve long-term task performance, focusing only on short-horizon behavioral similarity.
- **Backbone-freezing strategies**, while lightweight, assume that pre-trained multimodal representations remain aligned with language goals, which often breaks down under significant task shifts, leading to value learning drift and degraded adaptation.

The paper positions its contribution by stating that CRL for VLA models remains "underexplored," particularly in complex robotic manipulation scenarios involving multi-modal observations and long-horizon dynamics. While existing works in continual learning for VLA (e.g., VLM2VLA, Stellar-VLA, DMPEL, ChatVLA) exist, they do not explicitly model the stability-plasticity trade-off through a unified theoretical framework. CRL-VLA aims to fill this gap by providing a principled framework grounded in theoretical bounds, offering a novel perspective on how forgetting occurs and how it can be mitigated. This work is at the intersection of large-scale AI models, robotics, and the fundamental challenges of lifelong learning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to develop a robust and theoretically grounded framework for **continual post-training of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models**, enabling them to learn new robotic manipulation skills sequentially without experiencing catastrophic forgetting of previously acquired abilities.

The key motivations driving this research stem from the limitations of current VLA model deployment and continual learning paradigms:

1. **Enabling Lifelong Learning for Embodied Agents:** In open-world environments, embodied agents, such as robots, must continuously acquire new skills and adapt to novel situations. Static, pre-trained models are insufficient for such dynamic scenarios. Continual learning is essential for their practical application and long-term autonomy.

2. **Addressing the Stability-Plasticity Dilemma in CRL:** The fundamental challenge in continual learning is balancing the need to retain old knowledge (stability) with the ability to acquire new knowledge (plasticity). Existing Continual Reinforcement Learning (CRL) methods often struggle with this trade-off, leading to either poor adaptation to new tasks or severe forgetting of old ones. For VLA models, this dilemma is particularly acute due given their complex, multimodal nature and the high stakes of robotic manipulation.

3. **Overcoming Limitations of Current CRL Approaches for VLA Models:** As detailed in the "Broader Research Landscape" section, existing CRL techniques (e.g., experience replay, second-order regularization, simple KL regularization, backbone freezing) are often inefficient, computationally prohibitive, or ineffective when applied to large VLA architectures in long-horizon robotic tasks. There is a clear need for a method that can scale to these models and handle the nuances of multimodal, language-conditioned learning.

4. **Identifying the Root Cause of Forgetting in VLA:** The authors explicitly motivate their work by stating their aim to show that "forgetting in continual VLA learning is fundamentally driven by the goal-conditioned advantage magnitude." This insight is crucial as it reframes the stability-plasticity dilemma into an "asymmetric regulation problem," suggesting a targeted approach to control forgetting.

Specifically, the paper's objectives are to:
- Derive a unified performance bound that connects the stability-plasticity trade-off to goal-conditioned advantage magnitude and policy divergence.
- Propose CRL-VLA, a continual learning framework that leverages this theoretical insight through an asymmetric regulation strategy.
- Develop a novel dual-critic architecture with a Goal-Conditioned Value Formulation (GCVF) to practically implement the asymmetric regulation.
- Empirically demonstrate that CRL-VLA effectively harmonizes stability and plasticity, outperforming baselines in both anti-forgetting and forward adaptation on VLA benchmarks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

CRL-VLA proposes a novel framework for continual post-training of VLA models, built upon a theoretical understanding of the stability-plasticity dilemma. Its methodology can be broken down into theoretical characterization, architectural design, and specific regularization techniques.

#### 3.1. Theoretical Characterization of Stability-Plasticity Dilemma

The core theoretical insight of CRL-VLA is that catastrophic forgetting in continual VLA learning is fundamentally driven by the **goal-conditioned advantage magnitude**. The authors formalize this with:

- **Definition 4.1: Advantage Magnitude (M_g)**: This metric quantifies the maximum absolute advantage of an anchored policy (typically the previous policy, π_old) over the state-action pairs visited by a new policy (π_κ) for a given goal (g). A small M_g indicates close alignment between the policies.
- **Stability Metric (M_old)**: The expected advantage magnitude of the *old* policy evaluated on the state distribution of *new* tasks, reflecting performance retention.
- **Plasticity Metric (M_new)**: The expected advantage magnitude achievable on *new* tasks under the *new* policy, reflecting improvement potential.
- **Theorem 4.1 (Unified Stability-Plasticity Bounds)**: This crucial theorem establishes that both performance degradation on old tasks and performance improvement on new tasks are bounded by a coupling mechanism of advantage magnitude (M_old or M_new) and policy divergence (D_old or D_new, measured by KL divergence). Specifically, `J(π_new) - J(π_old) ≤ (scaling factor) * M * D`. This reframes the problem as an asymmetric regulation challenge: suppressing M_old (and D_old) for stability while allowing controlled growth in M_new (and D_new) for plasticity.

The paper further introduces **Corollaries 4.1 and 4.2** to demonstrate that M_old and M_new can be controlled in a decoupled manner:
- **Corollary 4.1 (Controllability of Stability)**: M_old is bounded by the critic's approximation error (ε_V) on the old task data. Minimizing this error directly controls M_old.
- **Corollary 4.2 (Natural Boundedness of Plasticity)**: M_new is naturally bounded by the environment's return range [G_min, G_max] when using Monte Carlo (MC) estimation for new tasks. This implies that new task learning, when using MC returns, inherently limits the advantage magnitude, preventing excessive destabilization.

This theoretical decoupling suggests that independent mechanisms can be used to manage stability and plasticity.

#### 3.2. Dual Goal-Conditioned Critic Architecture

To operationalize these insights, CRL-VLA proposes a **dual-critic architecture** with a novel **Goal-Conditioned Value Formulation (GCVF)**.
- **Goal-Conditioned Value Formulation**: The value function (V(s,g) or Q(s,a,g)) is directly conditioned on language goal (g) by concatenating language embeddings with global state representations before an MLP. This enhances language-following capacity and resolves issues with poor language-goal following in standard VLA value heads.
- **Infeasibility of Direct Advantage Optimization**: The authors argue against direct advantage estimation for VLA due to the high computational cost of multi-step rollouts and bootstrapping for large VLA models. Instead, they favor indirect control via value functions and Monte Carlo (MC) estimation, which is more efficient for scaling.
- **Dual-Critic Components**:
 1. **Frozen Goal-Conditioned Value (GCV) Critic (θ_GCV)**: This critic is initialized from the converged MC critic of the *previous* task and then **frozen**. It serves as an anchor, providing a stable reference value (V_old(s,g) or Q_old(s,a,g)) to minimize approximation error (ε_V) on old tasks, thereby controlling M_old and ensuring stability (as per Corollary 4.1).
 2. **Trainable MC Critic (θ_MC)**: This critic is continually **trained** on *new* task trajectories using Monte Carlo (MC) returns. It drives adaptation and plasticity, exploiting the natural boundedness of M_new by the environment's return range (as per Corollary 4.2).
- **Shared Backbone (ϕ)**: Both critics share a common backbone (ϕ), which acts as a unified feature extractor. This allows for efficient knowledge transfer while the decoupled heads enable targeted optimization.

#### 3.3. Regularization and Training Recipe

The theoretical insights are translated into a practical training recipe with three key regularization mechanisms:

1. **Goal-Conditioned Value (GCV) Consistency (L_V_GCV or L_Q_GCV)**: This loss penalizes deviations of the shared backbone's predictions (via the frozen GCV critic) from the reference old values (V_old or Q_old) on old task data (B_old). This directly minimizes ε_V, controlling M_old and enforcing stability.
 - `L_V_GCV(ϕ) = β_V * E_(s,g)~B_old [∥V_ϕ,θ_GCV(s,g) - V_old(s,g)∥²]`

2. **MC Critic Learning (L_V_MC or L_Q_MC)**: The trainable MC critic (θ_MC) is optimized to fit the Monte Carlo returns (G_g_new_t) from new task trajectories (B_new). This ensures accurate value estimation for new tasks, allowing M_new to emerge naturally within its bounded range, facilitating plasticity.
 - `L_V_MC(θ_MC, ϕ) = E_(s,g)~B_new [∥V_ϕ,θ_MC(s,g) - G_g_new_t∥²]`

3. **Policy Divergence Constraint (L_PPO + L_KL)**:
 - **PPO Loss (L_PPO)**: The Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) objective is used to maximize new-task return while its trust-region constraint (clipped surrogate objective and KL early stopping) inherently limits policy divergence on new tasks (D_new).
 - **KL Regularization (L_KL)**: An additional KL divergence loss is applied to samples from the old replay buffer (B_old) to explicitly constrain the new policy's divergence from the *old* policy (D_old), further preserving stability.
 - `L_KL(ϕ, ω) = E_(s,g)~B_old [D_KL(π_old(·|s,g)∥π(·|s,g))]`

#### 3.4. Total Training Loss

These components are integrated into a unified Lagrangian relaxation of the continual learning objective:
`L_total = L_PPO + α L_KL + β_V L_V_GCV + η L_V_MC`
Here, α, β_V, and η are hyperparameters acting as Lagrange multipliers, balancing the stability-plasticity trade-off.

The pipeline (Algorithm 1) involves training with standard RL on the first task, then sequentially adapting to new tasks. At each task transition, the converged MC critic becomes the frozen GCV critic, and a new MC critic is initialized. The VLA backbone model used is OpenVLA-OFT, with LoRA adapters for efficient parameter-efficient fine-tuning.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were conducted on subsets of the LIBERO benchmark (Liu et al., 2023), specifically designed for VLA model continual learning tasks, covering various task scales and complexities. The evaluation used standard CRL metrics: Final Average Return (FAR), Backward Transfer (BWT), Forgetting (F), and Forward Transfer (FT).

#### 4.1. Baselines and CRL-VLA Variants

- **Baselines**: Sequential Learning (SL), Multi-Task Learning (MTL), Experience Replay (ER), Learning Without Forgetting (LWF).
- **CRL-VLA Variants**: CRL-VLA (V) (using V-based goal-conditioned value) and CRL-VLA (Q) (using Q-based goal-conditioned value).

#### 4.2. Single-Task Learning Scenario (Table 1)

In scenarios involving learning a single long-horizon task, the results were:
- **CRL-VLA (Q)** demonstrated the best overall performance, achieving the highest FAR (0.98), indicating superior adaptability.
- It also showed the lowest forgetting, with a BWT of -0.02 (meaning minimal performance degradation on prior tasks, close to retaining 98% of performance) and the lowest F metric (0.03). This confirms strong anti-forgetting capabilities.
- Forward Transfer (FT) was similar across most methods (around -0.06), except for SL, which performed poorly.
- This suggests that CRL-VLA (Q) effectively balances stability and plasticity for sequential single-task learning.

#### 4.3. Multi-Task Learning Scenario (Table 2 & Figure 2)

For scenarios involving learning multiple long-horizon tasks, the findings were:
- **CRL-VLA (V)** emerged as the top performer, achieving the highest FAR (0.74), indicating improved overall task success.
- It also showed the highest BWT (0.17), which is a **positive** value, suggesting that learning new tasks *improved* performance on previously learned tasks, demonstrating beneficial cross-task knowledge sharing and strong resistance to forgetting.
- Forward Transfer (FT) was highest for SL and MTL (0.25), while other methods, including CRL-VLA, showed 0.0, implying that CRL-VLA primarily focuses on robust knowledge retention and successful learning of new tasks rather than direct positive transfer from previous skills for new task initialization.
- **Observation on V vs. Q variants**: The experiments revealed that CRL-VLA (Q) performs better in single-task learning, while CRL-VLA (V) excels in multi-task continuous learning. The authors attribute this to the nature of constraints: Q-based consistency implicitly enforces outdated action semantics, which can conflict with diverse actions in multi-task settings. In contrast, V-based consistency operates at the state-goal level, marginalizing actions, thus offering robustness to increased action diversity while preserving long-horizon value semantics.

#### 4.4. Ablation Study (Figures 3 & 4)

The ablation studies elucidated the importance of CRL-VLA's core components:
- **GCV and Policy KL Loss Weights (Fig. 3)**:
 - **L_Q_GCV and L_V_GCV**: Moderate loss weights (around 0.01 for Q-GCV and 0.001 for V-GCV) were crucial for optimal FAR, BWT, and forgetting. Excessively small weights failed to suppress M_old, while excessively large weights over-constrained updates and reduced plasticity.
 - **Policy KL Term (α)**: Moderate KL weights (around 0.01) yielded the best performance. Too strong a KL term restricted policy updates, limiting plasticity, while too weak a term led to policy drift and increased M_old. This confirms the delicate balance required for policy divergence constraints.
- **MC Critic Loss Weights (Fig. 4, left and middle)**:
 - Optimal weights for L_V_MC and L_Q_MC were around 1. This indicates that accurate critic estimation is vital. Small weights led to increased judgment errors and worse forgetting, while excessively large weights resulted in conservative optimization and reduced final task performance. This directly supports the theoretical claim that critic quality (bounding M_g) is key to sustained learning.
- **GCV Ablation (Fig. 4, right)**:
 - Directly conditioning the critic on the language goal (enabling GCV) significantly improved success rates (FAR), resistance to forgetting (BWT), and overall plasticity compared to a setup without GCV. This validates the importance of the Goal-Conditioned Value Formulation.

In summary, CRL-VLA consistently outperforms baselines, demonstrating a superior ability to balance stability and plasticity in continual VLA learning tasks. The theoretical framework and architectural design are empirically validated, with the dual-critic GCVF and carefully tuned regularization proving essential for its success.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The CRL-VLA framework represents a significant advancement in the field of continual reinforcement learning for embodied AI, particularly for Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models in robotic manipulation.

#### 5.1. Scientific Significance

- **Principled Theoretical Foundation**: The work's primary contribution is its rigorous theoretical grounding. By identifying the **goal-conditioned advantage magnitude** as the key driver of the stability-plasticity trade-off and deriving a unified performance bound (Theorem 4.1), the authors provide a novel perspective on catastrophic forgetting. This reframing of the problem as an asymmetric regulation challenge offers a clear conceptual pathway for developing effective solutions.
- **Decoupled Control**: The theoretical derivation that stability and plasticity metrics (M_old and M_new) can be controlled independently through critic approximation error and natural boundedness of MC returns is a fundamental insight. This allows for targeted intervention strategies rather than global, often conflicting, constraints.
- **Novel Architectural Design**: The dual-critic architecture with Goal-Conditioned Value Formulation (GCVF) is an elegant and practical manifestation of the theoretical insights. It enables the simultaneous anchoring of old knowledge and adaptive learning of new skills through specialized critics, all while leveraging a shared VLA backbone.

#### 5.2. Practical Impact and Potential Applications

- **Lifelong Robotic Learning**: CRL-VLA offers a concrete pathway towards deploying VLA models in real-world, lifelong robotic scenarios. Robots equipped with this framework could continually learn new manipulation skills from natural language instructions in dynamic environments without requiring full retraining or experiencing catastrophic forgetting. This is critical for applications in industrial automation, domestic assistance, and exploration where adaptability is paramount.
- **Enhanced Adaptability and Generalization**: By effectively balancing stability and plasticity, CRL-VLA ensures that VLA models remain highly adaptable to new tasks while preserving a broad repertoire of previously learned skills. This improves the overall robustness and generalization capabilities of VLA-powered agents.
- **Efficient Post-training**: The method's design, leveraging a shared backbone with LoRA adapters and avoiding computationally prohibitive second-order regularization, makes continual post-training more efficient for large VLA models.
- **Reduced Development Costs**: For developers of robotic systems, CRL-VLA can reduce the need for constant redesign or retraining of VLA policies, making it easier to update and expand a robot's skill set over its operational lifetime.

#### 5.3. Future Work

The authors themselves suggest important avenues for future research:
- **More Diverse and Unstructured Task Streams**: Extending CRL-VLA to handle an even wider variety of tasks, including those with higher complexity, less structure, or fewer demonstrations.
- **Partial Observability**: Adapting the framework to environments where the agent has incomplete information about the state, a common challenge in real-world robotics.
- **Non-stationary Language Goals**: Addressing scenarios where the interpretation or context of language instructions might change over time, requiring even more sophisticated forms of adaptation.

In conclusion, CRL-VLA makes a significant contribution by providing a theoretically sound and empirically validated framework for continual learning in VLA models. Its ability to mitigate catastrophic forgetting while fostering adaptation is a crucial step towards creating truly intelligent and autonomous embodied agents capable of lifelong learning in complex, open-ended environments.
