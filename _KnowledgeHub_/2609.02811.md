# Do Better Imagined Rollouts Mean Better Robot Control? A Controlled Study of World-Model Evaluation Under Feedback

> [!summary] Summary
> A controlled differential-drive path-tracking study asking whether open-loop rollout accuracy predicts closed-loop control quality. Six state estimators (dead reckoning, EKF, and GRU/SSM residual observers anchored to each) are compared across 24 sensing conditions under trajectory replay, 20-step measurement-free rollout, and closed-loop tracking. Replay error tracks closed-loop cross-track RMSE far better than rollout error (Spearman 0.923 vs 0.774), and rollout horizon interacts strongly with measurement-update interval.

## Problem

- Predictive world models are judged by open-loop fidelity metrics like reconstruction or multi-step rollout error, which ignore that deployed robots re-sense and re-plan continuously.
- Prior model-based control work shows accuracy does not imply policy gain, but leaves unquantified how sensing and correction schedules govern that mismatch at the estimation stage.
- Large visual world models confound representation error, learned dynamics, action conditioning, and replanning, so the rollout-versus-feedback question cannot be isolated in them.

## Method

- Controlled unicycle testbed: ==pure-pursuit tracking== of a lemniscate with biased odometry and intermittent ==landmark range-bearing sensing==, degraded along noise, landmark count, gyro bias, and outage axes.
- Six predictive state models, including recurrent residual observers (==GRU== and ==selective diagonal SSM==) that add a learned additive pose correction on top of a ==dead-reckoning== or ==EKF== analytic anchor.
- Three matched protocols (==replay==, ==measurement-free rollout==, ==closed-loop tracking==) plus a two-dimensional sweep over rollout horizon H and measurement-update interval k, scored by ==rank correlation== and ==selection regret==.

## Results

- Replay position RMSE reached Spearman **0.923** with closed-loop cross-track RMSE and mis-selected the best estimator in **5/24** conditions, versus **0.774** and **18/24** for the 20-step measurement-free rollout.
- The horizon-interval grid showed interaction: at H=80 with per-step corrections correlation stayed **0.90**, but with no corrections it collapsed to **0.32** with **24/24** failures and **0.76 m** mean regret.
- Training on longer sensing blackouts cut GRU-EKF cross-track RMSE from **1.72 m** to **1.06 m** and SSM-EKF from **1.94 m** to **1.42 m** under combined degradation, yet worsened SSM-DR (**5.29** to **5.41 m**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - An evaluation protocol is underspecified by horizon alone: report the measurement-update schedule too, since long horizons stay informative when corrections remain frequent.
> - Metric quality should be judged by rank agreement and selection regret against closed-loop outcomes, not just average prediction error, which can hide ordering flips.
> - A strong analytic prior dominates learned residuals; training on longer observation gaps yields condition- and architecture-dependent gains, not uniform robustness.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Predictive models, frequently referred to as world models, have become integral components in contemporary robotic systems. These models are employed for various tasks including state estimation, planning, control action evaluation, and, in some advanced systems, direct generation of control commands from learned dynamics or visual representations. The increasing reliance on these models necessitates robust and relevant evaluation methodologies to ensure their efficacy in practical robotic deployments.

Conventional evaluation of predictive models often relies on offline metrics such as open-loop prediction accuracy, reconstruction error, perceptual similarity, or error accumulated over a multi-step rollout. These metrics quantify how closely a predicted trajectory aligns with the actual system dynamics. However, robotic systems typically operate in a closed-loop manner: a controller issues an action, the robot executes it, new sensor measurements become available, the state estimate is updated, and a new control command is computed. This iterative feedback mechanism means that prediction errors interact with subsequent measurements and control decisions, influencing the overall system behavior.

Prior research in model-based control and reinforcement learning has identified a distinction between model accuracy and control performance, demonstrating that a model accurately reproducing system trajectories does not always optimize feedback controller performance. This study extends this line of inquiry by focusing on the state-estimation stage of the control loop. Rather than modifying the dynamics model or retraining the controller, this work maintains a fixed controller and varies the estimator providing state feedback.

The present study addresses a gap in understanding how offline prediction metrics, particularly multi-step rollouts, relate to closed-loop control performance when models are continually updated with sensor data. Many robotic systems incorporate measurements and recompute control inputs at a frequency significantly higher than the typical length of an open-loop evaluation rollout. The research posits that a model accumulating substantial error during an extended open-loop prediction might still perform effectively when regular sensor updates are available. Conversely, a model with low open-loop rollout error might generate state errors that prove detrimental within a feedback control context.

To isolate these intricate interactions, the authors conduct a controlled study in a simplified, low-dimensional mobile-robot setting. This approach avoids the complexities inherent in large visual world models, such as representation errors, learned visual dynamics, and high-dimensional observations, allowing for a focused investigation into the interplay of prediction, measurement correction, and feedback in a robotic system.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The increasing integration of predictive models into robotic systems necessitates a thorough understanding of their evaluation criteria. The motivation for this research stems from the observation that while predictive models are frequently evaluated using offline metrics like open-loop prediction accuracy or multi-step rollouts, these evaluations may not accurately reflect the model's performance when integrated into a closed-loop robotic system. In closed-loop operation, robots continuously act, receive new sensor measurements, update their state estimates, and recompute control inputs, a process fundamentally different from static, measurement-free offline predictions. This disparity can lead to discrepancies between a model's perceived offline quality and its actual performance in a feedback control context.

The primary objectives of this study are:

1. **Quantify the Correlation between Offline Prediction Metrics and Closed-Loop Control Performance:** To compare the effectiveness of conventional offline state-prediction accuracy (based on trajectory replay with continuous measurement updates) and measurement-free multi-step rollout error in predicting closed-loop path-tracking performance. This objective aims to determine whether offline metrics, particularly those designed to evaluate multi-step propagation, are reliable indicators of real-world control efficacy.
2. **Investigate the Influence of Prediction Horizon and Measurement-Update Frequency:** To systematically analyze how variations in the prediction horizon and the frequency of measurement updates during offline rollout evaluations affect the correlation between these metrics and closed-loop control performance. This involves examining their individual and interactive effects to identify optimal evaluation conditions that reflect deployment scenarios.
3. **Assess the Impact of Training Data Distribution on Robustness:** To determine whether exposing recurrent estimators to longer periods of sensing outages during training translates into improved closed-loop robustness, particularly under degraded sensing conditions. This objective explores the relationship between training data characteristics and the generalization capabilities of learned predictive models.
4. **Formulate Recommendations for World-Model Evaluation:** To derive practical principles and guidelines for evaluating predictive models intended for use within robotic feedback loops, emphasizing the importance of aligning evaluation protocols with the operational information patterns of the target robotic application.

In essence, the research seeks to move beyond purely open-loop prediction accuracy as the sole determinant of a world model's utility, advocating for evaluation methodologies that explicitly account for the feedback mechanisms and sensing schedules inherent in robotic control systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The study employs a controlled experimental testbed centered on a planar mobile robot to systematically investigate the relationship between offline prediction accuracy and closed-loop control performance.

**Controlled Experimental Testbed:**
The platform is a simulated differential-drive robot operating in a planar environment. Its state is defined by its 2D position `(x, y)` and heading `(θ)`. The robot follows a lemniscate reference trajectory using a pure-pursuit path-tracking controller with a look-ahead distance of 0.8 meters and a sampling interval of 0.1 seconds. Critically, the controller computes actions based on the *estimated* pose provided by the active observer, not the ground-truth pose. This coupling ensures that estimation errors directly influence control commands, altering the robot's subsequent trajectory and encountered sensor measurements.

**Sensing Modalities and Degradation:**
The robot receives two types of sensor data:
- **Wheel Odometry:** Provides incremental motion measurements, corrupted by multiplicative wheel slip and additive gyroscope bias.
- **Landmark Range-Bearing Measurements:** Provides intermittent absolute corrections from known landmarks.
Sensing conditions are systematically varied across 24 configurations by adjusting:
- Landmark availability (number of visible landmarks).
- Range measurement noise.
- Gyroscope bias magnitude.
- Scheduled complete sensing outages (periods with no landmark observations).

**Predictive State Models (Estimators):**
Six state estimators, acting as compact predictive models of robot motion, are evaluated:
1. **Dead Reckoning (DR):** Propagates robot pose using odometry alone, without measurement corrections.
2. **Extended Kalman Filter (EKF):** Uses a unicycle motion model and analytic Jacobians for landmark range-bearing observations. It propagates state and covariance between measurements and performs standard measurement updates when observations are available.
3. **Recurrent Residual Observers:** These models learn an additive residual correction on top of an analytic reference estimate. Two recurrent architectures are used:
 - **Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU):** GRU-DR (anchored to DR) and GRU-EKF (anchored to EKF).
 - **Selective Diagonal State-Space Model (SSM):** SSM-DR (anchored to DR) and SSM-EKF (anchored to EKF).
 These recurrent models are trained via backpropagation through time on 200 domain-randomized expert trajectories, encompassing various sensing qualities and motion uncertainties.

**Training with Extended Sensing Outages:**
To investigate robustness to prolonged sensor loss, additional versions of SSM-EKF, GRU-EKF, and SSM-DR (denoted with `†`) are trained. These models use 300 trajectories, with half containing extended sensing outages (32-58 steps) in contrast to the standard training's 6-27 step outages. This procedure isolates the effect of missing measurement updates by masking landmark channels while preserving vehicle dynamics and odometry.

**Evaluation Protocols:**
Each estimator is assessed using three distinct protocols, varying in measurement availability and influence on the robot's trajectory:
1. **Trajectory Replay with Measurement Updates:**
 - All observers process the *same, fixed, recorded* input sequence (odometry, landmark measurements, and availability masks).
 - The robot's trajectory remains constant, ensuring that observer differences do not alter future inputs.
 - Metrics: Position RMSE and Heading RMSE against ground truth. This protocol represents a conventional offline evaluation of state-estimation accuracy.
2. **Measurement-Free Rollout:**
 - The observer's internal state is saved at regular checkpoints during a replay of the recorded trajectory.
 - From each checkpoint, a copy of the observer is propagated for a fixed horizon (`H = 20` steps) using only odometry; landmark measurements are *withheld*.
 - Metric: Position RMSE at the final rollout horizon, aggregated across checkpoints. This tests the observer's ability to propagate its state without external correction over a multi-step horizon.
3. **Closed-Loop Path Tracking:**
 - The observer operates *online* within the simulator.
 - Its pose estimate directly drives the pure-pursuit controller, meaning estimation errors influence control commands and subsequently the robot's physical trajectory.
 - Landmark measurements are incorporated according to the evaluated sensing condition.
 - Metrics: Cross-track RMSE (primary), control effort, recovery time, and divergence fraction. This protocol simulates actual robotic deployment conditions.

**Analysis and Comparison:**
For each of the 24 sensing conditions, the offline metrics generate a ranking of the six primary observers. These rankings are then compared with the ranking derived from closed-loop cross-track RMSE using:
- Spearman rank correlation (`ρ`) across all observer-condition pairs.
- The frequency with which an offline criterion selects an observer different from the closed-loop optimum (Top-1 failures).
- The resulting selection regret (difference in closed-loop performance from the true optimum).
Ablation studies systematically vary the rollout horizon `H` and the measurement-update interval `k` (number of prediction steps between landmark corrections) during rollouts to understand their individual and combined effects. Additional experiments investigate the impact of training-set size and outage distribution.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several key findings concerning the relationship between predictive model evaluation metrics and closed-loop robot control performance.

#### 4.1. Influence of Analytic Estimator on Nominal Closed-Loop Stability

Under nominal sensing conditions, the quality of the underlying analytic estimator strongly influenced closed-loop robustness. GRU-EKF achieved the lowest replay position error and the lowest closed-loop cross-track error. EKF-anchored recurrent estimators (GRU-EKF, SSM-EKF) consistently demonstrated greater stability and lower divergence rates compared to their dead-reckoning (DR)-anchored counterparts. This suggests that learning a residual correction on top of a well-conditioned analytic estimate, like the EKF, is more reliable than applying the same strategy to a weaker reference such as dead reckoning. Across various sensing degradation sweeps, the EKF and EKF-anchored models remained the most stable group, though the standard EKF sometimes outperformed learned EKF residual observers during long complete sensing outages where recurrent corrections lacked fresh exteroceptive data.

#### 4.2. Discrepancy Between Measurement-Free Rollout Error and Closed-Loop Performance

Despite measuring multi-step state propagation, the 20-step measurement-free rollout was found to be a less reliable predictor of closed-loop performance than conventional replay.
- **Correlation:** Replay position RMSE showed a Spearman rank correlation of `ρ = 0.923` with closed-loop cross-track RMSE. In contrast, the 20-step measurement-free rollout had a lower correlation of `ρ = 0.774`.
- **Estimator Selection:** Replay position RMSE selected an estimator different from the closed-loop optimum in 5 out of 24 sensing conditions. The 20-step measurement-free rollout, however, selected a different optimum in 18 out of 24 conditions.
- **Regret:** The maximum selection regret (performance difference from the best closed-loop observer) was 0.0347 meters for replay position RMSE, significantly lower than 0.1208 meters for the measurement-free rollout.
These results indicate that while measurement-free rollouts provide information about open-loop state propagation, this information is not sufficient to consistently predict or preserve the closed-loop estimator ranking.

#### 4.3. Interaction of Rollout Horizon and Measurement-Update Frequency

The study demonstrated that the utility of an offline rollout metric depends significantly on both the prediction horizon and the measurement-update schedule during the rollout.
- **Fixed Horizon (H=20), Varying Update Interval (k):** As the measurement-update interval `k` increased (i.e., less frequent measurement corrections during the rollout), agreement with closed-loop performance systematically decreased. Spearman rank correlation dropped from `ρ = 0.916` when measurements were incorporated at every step (`k=1`) to `ρ = 0.774` when no measurements were provided (`k=∞`). The number of top-1 selection failures increased as corrections became less frequent.
- **Grid Search (Horizon H vs. Update Interval k):** Long prediction horizons (e.g., `H=80`) maintained relatively high rank correlation (`ρ = 0.90`) with closed-loop performance when frequent measurement corrections (`k=1`) were retained. However, combining a long prediction horizon (`H=80`) with no measurement correction (`k=∞`) resulted in the largest degradation, with rank correlation falling to `ρ = 0.32`, selection failures in all 24 conditions, and mean selection regret of 0.76 meters.
This finding clarifies that increasing prediction horizon alone does not necessarily reduce the informativeness of an offline evaluation; rather, the interaction with measurement-update frequency is crucial.

#### 4.4. Condition-Dependent Improvements from Training with Longer Sensing Outages

Exposing recurrent estimators to longer periods without landmark measurements during training produced mixed results.
- **Combined Degradation:** Under a combined sensing-degradation condition, extended-blackout training improved both EKF-anchored recurrent estimators. GRU-EKF cross-track RMSE decreased from 1.72 m to 1.06 m, and SSM-EKF decreased from 1.936 m to 1.419 m.
- **Isolated Outages:** However, the same training modification did not provide uniform gains across isolated blackout conditions. SSM-EKF†, the extended-blackout trained version, exhibited *higher* tracking error than the standard SSM-EKF at every tested blackout duration under isolated sensing outages. The DR-anchored SSM-DR also did not show improvement.
- **Training Data Distribution:** A matched-data experiment revealed that simply increasing training set size or matching the training blackout distribution to evaluation conditions did not consistently guarantee improved closed-loop behavior. The benefits of extended-outage training appeared to be dependent on the estimator architecture, the quality of the underlying analytic estimate, and the specific sensing regime.

#### 4.5. Additional Findings (Appendices)

- **Policy-Coupled Rollout:** An offline rollout protocol where the predicted state directly influenced subsequent control inputs (policy-coupled rollout) did not improve agreement with closed-loop performance and sometimes worsened it, further emphasizing the challenge of accurately replicating feedback effects offline without measurement updates.
- **Estimation-Error Structure:** Injecting synthetic estimation errors with identical position RMSE but varying temporal and directional patterns showed that the structure of estimation error significantly affects closed-loop control performance. Persistent displacement errors were more detrimental than high-frequency oscillatory errors, and heading biases, even with zero position error, caused substantial tracking errors.

Statistically, paired bootstrap and sign-flip permutation tests confirmed the significance of observed differences in selection regret between replay position RMSE and the measurement-free rollout, and between frequent vs. absent measurement updates during rollouts.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research provides insights into the evaluation of predictive models in robotics, particularly those operating within feedback control loops. The findings highlight limitations of conventional offline metrics and offer a framework for designing more relevant evaluation protocols.

**Implications for World-Model Evaluation:**
The study demonstrates that a crucial distinction exists between an offline evaluation's information pattern and the information available to a deployed robotic system. An evaluation protocol's relevance to closed-loop performance is not solely determined by the prediction horizon.
1. **Context-Specific Evaluation Protocols:** The results emphasize that predictive model evaluation should reproduce the sensing schedule and feedback structure of the intended closed-loop system. Measurement-free rollouts, while informative about open-loop propagation, can provide a misleading ranking of models when the deployed robot receives regular measurement corrections. Conversely, for applications requiring extended autonomous prediction (e.g., long-horizon planning), long measurement-free rollouts might be appropriate. This calls for tailoring evaluation protocols to match the specific operational conditions, including the timing of new measurements, state updates, and control recomputations.
2. **Beyond Prediction Accuracy:** Relying solely on scalar state-prediction error (e.g., RMSE) may be insufficient. The research shows that different error structures, even with the same RMSE, can have vastly different impacts on a feedback controller. Consequently, evaluation should include metrics that directly compare model rankings at the prediction and control levels, such as estimator-selection accuracy, policy-ranking agreement, or closed-loop regret. These metrics provide a more comprehensive view of a model's utility in a feedback context.
3. **Disentangling State Propagation and Measurement Correction:** Predictive models in robotics often integrate multiple sensor modalities. The study suggests that evaluation should characterize both how a model propagates the system state between measurements and how its state estimate evolves when new measurements become available. A model adept at long-term open-loop propagation might perform poorly with measurement corrections that introduce unfavorable errors for control, and vice versa.

**Practical Impact for Robotic Systems Development:**
The findings provide actionable guidance for researchers and engineers developing robotic systems that utilize predictive models.
- **Informed Model Selection:** Developers can make more informed decisions when selecting or developing predictive models by evaluating them under conditions that closely mimic deployment. This could reduce the risk of deploying models that perform well offline but poorly in practice.
- **Refined Training Strategies:** The observation that extended-outage training does not uniformly improve robustness, and depends on estimator architecture and sensing conditions, suggests that training curricula for learned estimators should be carefully designed and validated against representative deployment scenarios, rather than relying on generalized assumptions about blackout exposure.
- **Benchmarking Standards:** The study advocates for richer benchmarking standards that articulate not only prediction horizons but also measurement-update schedules, feedback mechanisms, and the impact on control performance. This could lead to more robust and generalizable robotic systems.

While conducted in a controlled, low-dimensional setting, the principles uncovered are relevant to the evaluation of more complex visual world models and general robotic AI. The research lays a foundation for future work to extend these controlled comparisons to higher-dimensional visual models, different robot dynamics, and physical closed-loop experiments, thereby strengthening the empirical basis for robust world-model evaluation in robotics.
