# SUSD: Structured Unsupervised Skill Discovery through State Factorization

> [!summary] Summary
> SUSD, a framework from Sharif University of Technology, introduces structured unsupervised skill discovery by integrating state factorization and curiosity-driven weighting into a distance-maximizing approach. This method learns comprehensive, disentangled skill sets in complex environments, outperforming prior baselines in multi-object tasks and achieving better state coverage.

## Problem

- Existing Mutual Information (MI)-based Unsupervised Skill Discovery (USD) methods learn simple, static skills, struggling with dynamic and complex behaviors.
- Distance-Maximizing Skill Discovery (DSD) methods, while improving dynamics, often exhibit a bias towards easily controllable factors, leading to incomplete skill repertoires in multi-object environments.
- Previous USD approaches lack explicit mechanisms to ensure learned skills comprehensively cover all controllable factors or entities within complex, compositional environments.

## Method

- **SUSD** implements Factorized Distance-Maximizing ==Skill Discovery== (DSD) by decomposing the state and skill latent spaces into N independent components, with a factorized mapping function and a factor-wise objective.
- It incorporates a curiosity-based factor weighting mechanism using a conditional density model to dynamically identify and prioritize harder-to-control or less explored environmental factors for skill learning.
- The framework optimizes a skill policy via Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) using an intrinsic reward, which is a curiosity-weighted sum of factor-wise skill rewards, alongside dual gradient updates for mapping functions and a Lagrange multiplier.

## Results

- SUSD consistently outperformed all tested baselines on downstream tasks in factorized environments like Multi-Particle and Kitchen, with "accidental task completions" showcasing its intrinsically useful skills.
- The framework achieved substantially better state coverage, particularly "Worst Agent State Coverage" and "Average Agent State Coverage," ensuring balanced exploration across all factors, including harder-to-control ones.
- SUSD learned a richer latent skill embedding, demonstrated by significantly lower factor-wise Mean Squared Error (MSE) when decoding observations from the latent skill space, confirming comprehensive and disentangled skill representations.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Leveraging the inherent compositional structure of environments through state factorization provides a powerful inductive bias for learning disentangled and comprehensive skill sets.
> - Dynamically weighting the learning process based on factor-wise curiosity is crucial for overcoming the bias towards easily controllable elements and encouraging balanced exploration across all environmental components.
> - Integrating state factorization into a DSD framework allows for the discovery of both dynamic and comprehensive skills, addressing limitations of both MI-based and prior DSD methods.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has demonstrated capabilities in solving complex tasks, particularly when supported by dense, well-shaped reward functions. However, a significant limitation of standard RL is the requirement for manual design and tuning of these reward functions, which poses challenges in sparse-reward, long-horizon, and complex real-world scenarios.

Unsupervised Skill Discovery (USD) is an approach within RL that seeks to address this limitation by enabling agents to autonomously learn diverse and useful behaviors without relying on extrinsic rewards. The pre-trained skills can then serve as a foundation for efficiently learning various downstream tasks, potentially through Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning (HRL), without requiring extensive task-specific supervision.

Current USD methods can be broadly categorized into two main branches:
1. **Mutual Information (MI)-based methods:** These approaches aim to maximize the mutual information between latent skill variables and states (I(S; Z)). While effective in some contexts, these methods often exhibit invariance properties that lead to the discovery of simple, static skills, limiting their capacity to capture dynamic, task-relevant behaviors. Examples include DIAYN and DUSDi.
2. **Distance-Maximizing Skill Discovery (DSD) methods:** This branch focuses on promoting dynamic skills by maximizing state changes along skill-specific directions, often by leveraging state-space distances (e.g., Wasserstein distance). While DSD methods aim to overcome the invariance limitations of MI-based approaches, they frequently exhibit a bias towards easily controllable factors within an environment. This bias can result in learned skill latent spaces that underrepresent harder-to-control or less immediately responsive elements, leading to less comprehensive skill sets, especially in complex environments with multiple controllable entities. Examples include LSD, CSD, and METRA.

The presented work, SUSD, builds upon the DSD framework. It addresses the identified limitations of existing DSD methods in complex, multi-object environments by explicitly incorporating the compositional structure of environments. While state factorization has been previously explored in MI-based skill discovery (e.g., DUSDi), SUSD integrates this inductive bias into DSD methods, proposing a mechanism to ensure skill diversity across all controllable components and dynamically adjust learning emphasis based on the agent's progress across these factors. This positions SUSD as an advancement aimed at improving the diversity, comprehensiveness, and disentanglement of learned skills in structured domains.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the identified shortcomings of existing Unsupervised Skill Discovery (USD) methods, particularly in environments characterized by compositional structure and multiple controllable entities.

1. **Addressing Limitations of MI-based USD:** Mutual Information (MI)-based USD methods, while foundational, often learn skills that are simple and static. This is attributed to their inherent invariance properties, which can prevent the discovery of more dynamic and complex behaviors relevant for interacting with an environment.

2. **Overcoming Bias in Distance-Maximizing Skill Discovery (DSD) methods:** DSD methods were developed to encourage more dynamic behaviors by maximizing state-space distances. However, a key limitation persists: these methods tend to prioritize and emphasize easily controllable factors or entities within the environment. Consequently, the learned skill sets often lack comprehensiveness, failing to adequately explore or represent behaviors related to harder-to-control or less immediately responsive components. This becomes particularly pronounced in complex environments containing several independent or semi-independent controllable elements (e.g., multiple objects or agents).

3. **Leveraging Environmental Compositional Structure:** Many real-world and simulated environments inherently possess a compositional structure, where the overall state can be factorized into independent or semi-independent components (e.g., distinct objects, agents, or environmental features). Existing DSD methods, by treating the state space holistically, overlook this inductive bias. The researchers hypothesize that explicitly leveraging this factorization can significantly enhance the skill discovery process.

Based on these motivations, the key objectives of the SUSD framework are:

1. **To introduce a novel factorized embedding approach for DSD:** This involves decomposing the environment's state space into distinct factors and associating dedicated subsets of skill variables with each factor. The aim is to enable the agent to learn localized skills within each subspace, leading to more fine-grained, disentangled, and composable skill representations.

2. **To develop an adaptive, curiosity-driven factor weighting mechanism:** This mechanism is designed to dynamically adjust the learning emphasis across different state factors. By tracking the agent's progress and identifying underexplored or harder-to-control components, it aims to adaptively steer the agent's focus, thereby promoting a more balanced and comprehensive acquisition of skills across all controllable elements.

3. **To discover richer, more diverse, and composable skills:** The ultimate goal is for SUSD to learn a repertoire of skills that not only cover the entire state space but are also disentangled and reusable, facilitating the efficient training of compositional downstream tasks, particularly within a Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning (HRL) framework.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

SUSD, or Structured Unsupervised Skill Discovery, integrates state factorization and a curiosity-driven weighting mechanism into the Distance-Maximizing Skill Discovery (DSD) framework. The approach operates within reward-free Factored Markov Decision Processes (FMDPs), where the state space $S$ is represented as a Cartesian product of $N$ subspaces, $S = S_1 \times \cdots \times S_N$, with each $S_i$ corresponding to a distinct factor. Similarly, the skill space $Z$ is factored as $Z = Z_1 \times \cdots \times Z_N$.

#### 3.1. Factorized DSD

The core of SUSD's methodology lies in its factorized design:

- **Observation Space Factorization:** The environment's observation space is explicitly decomposed into $N$ distinct factors $\{s_i\}_{i=1}^N$, each representing a meaningful component or controllable entity within the environment (e.g., agent position, ammo state, target state in 2D-Gunner). This leverages the compositional structure of the environment as an inductive bias.
- **Skill Latent Space Factorization:** Correspondingly, each skill vector $z$ is composed of $N$ latent skill factors $\{z_i\}$, each of dimension $D$. This design allows for the allocation of specific skill variables to influence particular state factors, promoting localized and disentangled skill learning.
- **Factorized Mapping Function:** The state-to-skill latent space mapping function $\phi(\cdot)$ is implemented as $N$ separate neural networks, $\phi_i(\cdot)$. Each network $\phi_i$ takes a specific state factor $s_i$ as input and outputs its corresponding latent embedding $\phi_i(s_i)$.
- **Reformulated Objective:** The original DSD optimization problem (Eq. 2), which aims to maximize state change along a skill direction, is reformulated to a factorized version (Eq. 3). This new objective sums the contributions from each factor, where each factor's change is independently driven by its corresponding skill factor:
 $\sup_{\pi,\{\phi_i\}^N_{i=1}} \mathbb{E}_{p(\tau,z)} \sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} (\phi_i(s^i_{t+1}) - \phi_i(s^i_t))^\top z_i$
 subject to $\sum_{i=1}^N \|\phi_i(s'^i) - \phi_i(s^i)\|^2 \le 1, \forall (s,s') \in \mathcal{S}_{adj}$
 This allows for independent learning of skill embeddings for each factor while collectively contributing to the overall skill policy.

#### 3.2. Curiosity-Based Factor Weighting

To address the DSD bias towards easily controllable factors, SUSD introduces a fine-grained, factor-wise curiosity mechanism:

- **Controllability-Aware Distance:** Inspired by prior work, SUSD uses the negative log-likelihood of a state transition, $-\log q_\theta(s'|s)$, as a measure of controllability. Rare or difficult-to-achieve transitions receive higher values, indicating greater "curiosity."
- **Factor-wise Curiosity Signals:** Instead of a single curiosity score per transition, SUSD calculates factor-wise curiosity signals. A density model $q_\theta(s'|s)$ is trained as a multivariate Gaussian distribution $N(\mu_\theta(s), \Sigma_\theta(s))$. By partitioning $\mu_\theta(s)$ and diagonal $\Sigma_\theta(s)$ according to the predefined state factorization, marginal means and variances for each state factor $s_i$ are extracted. These are then used to compute the curiosity weight for each factor as the square root of its negative log-likelihood, $\sqrt{-\log q_\theta(s^i_{t+1}|s_t)}$ (Eq. 5).
- **Integration into Objective:** A lemma (Lemma 4.1) allows the distance term to be incorporated directly into the DSD objective as a coefficient. Consequently, the factor-wise curiosity signals, interpreted as valid distance metrics, are used to weight each factor's contribution to the overall intrinsic reward. The final optimization problem (Eq. 6) integrates these weighted terms:
 $\sup_{\pi,\{\phi_i\}^N_{i=1}} \mathbb{E}_{p(\tau,z)} \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \sum_{i=1}^N \sqrt{-\log q_\theta(s^i_{t+1}|s_t)} (\phi_i(s^i_{t+1}) - \phi_i(s^i_t))^\top z_i$
 subject to $\sum_{i=1}^N \|\phi_i(s'^i) - \phi_i(s^i)\|^2 \le 1, \forall (s,s') \in \mathcal{S}_{adj}$

#### 3.3. SUSD Training

SUSD is trained using dual gradient descent:

- **Intrinsic Reward:** The skill policy $\pi(a|s,z)$ receives an intrinsic reward $R$ (Eq. 10), which is the sum of the curiosity-weighted factor-wise skill rewards:
 $R = \sum_{i=1}^N \sqrt{-\log q_\theta(s^i_{t+1}|s_t)} r^{SUSD}_i$ where $r^{SUSD}_i = (\phi_i(s^i_{t+1}) - \phi_i(s^i_t))^\top z_i$.
- **Policy Optimization:** The skill policy is updated using Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) with $R$ as the intrinsic reward.
- **Network Updates:** The factorized mapping functions $\phi_i(s_i)$ and the Lagrange multiplier $\lambda$ (which enforces the Lipschitz constraint) are updated via gradient ascent using their respective objectives (Eqs. 8 and 9). The conditional density model $q_\theta(s'|s)$ is periodically refitted using collected trajectory samples.
- **Discrete Skills:** The methodology also supports discrete skill spaces by modifying the intrinsic reward calculation to ensure zero-mean sums over each skill factor, preventing skill learning collapse.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of SUSD involved a series of experiments across various environments and comparisons against five state-of-the-art unsupervised skill discovery baselines: LSD, CSD, METRA (DSD-based), DIAYN (MI-based), and DUSDi (factorized MI-based).

#### 4.1. Experimental Setup

- **Environments:** Evaluations were conducted in both factorized environments (Multi-Particle, Kitchen, 2D-Gunner) and unfactorized environments (Ant, HalfCheetah). The majority of the focus was on complex, factorized environments.
- **Factorization Strategy:** For factorized environments, a simple strategy was employed where each entity was treated as a distinct factor. In some cases, the agent's state was incorporated into entity factors to better capture causal influence.
- **Downstream Tasks:** Performance was assessed on various downstream tasks specific to each environment (e.g., Unlimited/Limited Ammo in 2D-Gunner, Put Butter in Pot in Kitchen, Sequential interaction in Multi-Particle, Zero-shot Goal Reaching in Ant/HalfCheetah).
- **Skill Dimensions:** For unfactorized environments, a latent skill dimension of D=2 was used. For factorized environments, SUSD used D=2 for each factor, resulting in a total skill dimension proportional to the number of factors (e.g., 20 for Multi-Particle with 10 agent-station factors).

#### 4.2. Evaluating Factorized Environments (Q1)

SUSD generally demonstrated superior performance compared to all baseline methods across multiple downstream tasks in the Multi-Particle and Kitchen environments.

- **Downstream Task Performance (Figure 3, Figure 12):** SUSD consistently achieved higher average returns on tasks in both Multi-Particle (e.g., Sequential interaction, Food-poison) and Kitchen (e.g., Put Butter in Pot, Put Meatball in Pot, Put Pot on Stove, Put Pot on Target). The performance gap was particularly pronounced in the more complex Kitchen environment, which has a high-dimensional observation space.
- **Ablation Study (Figure 8):** An ablation study confirmed that both the factorized embedding and the curiosity-based factor weighting mechanism contribute positively to SUSD's performance. Removing either component resulted in a noticeable performance decrease.
- **Factorization Strategy Impact (Figure 9):** In the Multi-Particle environment, grouping each agent with its corresponding station as a single factor (reflecting prior knowledge of agent-station interaction) yielded better performance than treating each agent and station as independent factors.
- **Over/Under-factorization Effects (Figure 13):** Experiments in 2D-Gunner showed that performance was highest when the true factorization structure was used. Both over-factorization (splitting a true factor) and under-factorization (merging true factors) led to a degradation in performance.

#### 4.3. Unfactorized Environments (Q2)

To assess its general applicability, SUSD was evaluated in unfactorized environments.

- **Zero-shot Goal Reaching (Figure 6):** SUSD achieved competitive results with other DSD baselines (LSD, CSD, METRA) in the Ant and HalfCheetah environments on zero-shot goal-reaching tasks. This indicates that SUSD's structured approach does not hinder its performance in simpler, unfactorized settings and can generalize.

#### 4.4. Richness of Factorized Latent Skills (Q3)

Two experiments were conducted to evaluate the quality and richness of SUSD's learned factorized latent skill embeddings.

- **State Coverage Across Factors (Figure 4, Figure 11):** SUSD achieved substantially better state coverage than baselines in both the Multi-Particle and 2D-Gunner environments. Specifically, it showed higher "Worst Agent State Coverage" (minimum coverage across factors) and "Average Agent State Coverage," as well as "Bin Coverage." This suggests that SUSD promotes balanced exploration and skill acquisition across all factors, including those that might be harder to control or less frequently explored by other methods.
- **Factor Decoding (Table 1):** SUSD produced lower factor-wise Mean Squared Error (MSE) when reconstructing observations from its latent skill embeddings in Multi-Particle, Kitchen, and 2D-Gunner environments compared to baselines. This finding indicates that SUSD learns a more comprehensive latent skill embedding that effectively captures compositional information about all individual factors necessary for reconstruction.
- **Impact of Increased Skill Dimensionality on Baselines (Figure 10):** Increasing the total skill dimensionality for baseline methods (METRA, CSD) from 2 to 20 in the Multi-Particle environment did not yield performance comparable to SUSD. This supports the argument that the factorized approach, combined with curiosity weighting, provides more significant benefits than merely expanding the latent skill space.

#### 4.5. Effectiveness of Learned Skills During Skill Learning

An experiment in the Kitchen environment assessed the inherent utility of the learned skills during the skill discovery phase itself, without explicit downstream task training.

- **Accidental Task Completions (Table 2):** SUSD's learned skill policies led to significantly more accidental completions of downstream tasks (e.g., Put Butter in Pot, Put Meatball in Pot) compared to baselines. For several tasks, SUSD achieved an average task reward, while most baselines obtained zero. This suggests that SUSD's skills are more diverse and intrinsically useful, capable of completing task objectives even when executed randomly, indicating a richer repertoire of behaviors.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The SUSD framework presents several contributions to the field of Unsupervised Skill Discovery (USD) and Reinforcement Learning, particularly for complex, compositional environments:

1. **Enhanced Skill Diversity and Comprehensiveness:** SUSD effectively addresses a limitation of existing DSD methods by promoting the discovery of skills that collectively cover all controllable factors in an environment. This is achieved through its factorized embedding architecture, which explicitly assigns skill variables to distinct state components. The empirical results on state coverage and accidental task completions suggest that SUSD learns a richer and more balanced repertoire of behaviors, including those pertaining to less immediately controllable elements.

2. **Disentangled and Composable Skill Representations:** By factorizing the state and skill spaces, SUSD naturally yields disentangled skill representations. This allows for fine-grained control over individual entities or aspects of the environment, which is valuable for constructing complex behaviors. Such disentangled and composable skills are a critical prerequisite for efficient Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning (HRL) in solving multi-stage or compositional downstream tasks.

3. **Adaptive Learning Focus:** The integration of a curiosity-based factor weighting mechanism is a notable advancement. This component dynamically steers the agent's exploration towards underexplored or harder-to-control factors, ensuring a more uniform and effective skill acquisition process across all environmental components. This mechanism contributes to the comprehensive nature of the learned skills.

4. **Improved Performance in Complex Environments:** The experimental results demonstrate that SUSD significantly outperforms existing DSD and MI-based USD methods in factorized environments like Multi-Particle and Kitchen, particularly in tasks requiring interaction with multiple objects. This indicates SUSD's capability to handle the complexities introduced by high-dimensional observation spaces and multiple controllable entities.

5. **Applicability and Robustness:** SUSD's competitive performance in unfactorized environments (Ant, HalfCheetah) suggests that its structured approach does not impede its effectiveness in simpler settings, indicating a degree of generality. The ablation studies further confirm the importance of both the factorized embedding and the curiosity mechanism for the observed performance gains.

The potential impact of SUSD lies in advancing the capabilities of autonomous agents to learn general-purpose skills in realistic, structured environments without extensive human supervision. These learned skills can serve as a robust foundation for more efficient and scalable training of downstream tasks, reducing the burden of reward engineering. This work contributes to making agents more adaptable and capable of operating in diverse and dynamic environments.
