# Learning with Rare Success but Rich Feedback via Reflection-Enhanced Self-Distillation

> [!summary] Summary
> Reflection-Enhanced Self-Distillation (RESD) is a framework that enables Large Language Models to learn from rare successes by actively interpreting and structuring failure feedback into natural language reflections and a persistent playbook. RESD consistently achieves superior performance across various complex tasks, particularly in environments with initially low success rates, and improves interaction efficiency compared to existing self-distillation and reinforcement learning methods.

## Problem

- Large Language Models (LLMs) struggle with continuous improvement in post-training phases when relying on sparse reward signals from complex, multi-step tasks, as successful trajectories are infrequent.
- Existing on-policy self-distillation methods treat environmental feedback as a passive conditioning signal, heavily relying on successful demonstrations and performing poorly in "rare-success regimes."
- Raw failure signals are often diagnostic rather than instructional, indicating that a trajectory failed but not why or how to correct specific decisions, making it difficult for self-teachers to construct effective token-level supervision.

## Method

- RESD augments self-distillation by integrating active feedback interpretation and experience accumulation, modifying the self-distillation loop to enrich the teacher's context with structured information derived from failed rollouts.
- The student model, in a diagnostic mode, generates natural language "reflections" of failures to explain causes and suggest corrections, and these insights are curated into a persistent "playbook" of reusable lessons.
- The self-teacher model is prompted with an enriched context, including the current playbook, reflection (if from a failure), previous trials, raw environment feedback, and any cached successful solutions, allowing it to provide a more corrective token-level distribution.

## Results

- RESD achieved the best performance across four diverse evaluation tasks, including MANUFACTORIA-HAS where it boosted Per-Task Accuracy (m@4/b@4) from 0.57/2.27 (SDPO+ss) to 35.80/65.91.
- The reflection- and playbook-augmented teacher provided a more corrective signal, indicated by a higher average self-distillation loss concentrated on reasoning decision tokens, suggesting active correction of the student's strategy.
- RESD demonstrated enhanced interaction efficiency, achieving faster early-stage improvement across tasks with only a single rollout per prompt, compared to GRPO which required eight rollouts per prompt.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Transforming passive failure feedback into active, interpreted, and structured knowledge (reflections and playbooks) is crucial for effective LLM self-improvement, especially when successful demonstrations are rare.
> - Organizing recurring failure patterns and their corrections into a persistent memory (playbook) allows for the preservation and reuse of valuable lessons across trajectories, enhancing learning beyond isolated failure events.
> - Augmenting the self-teacher's context with structured failure analysis provides a stronger, more corrective token-level distillation signal, guiding the student's reasoning strategy more effectively than relying solely on raw feedback or successful examples.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a central challenge in the post-training phase of Large Language Models (LLMs): enabling continuous improvement through environmental interactions. Traditionally, Reinforcement Learning (RL) algorithms, such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) or Generalized Reinforcement Learning Policy Optimization (GRPO), have been employed for aligning models with desired outcomes. However, these methods often encounter a bottleneck in complex, multi-step tasks due to their reliance on sparse reward signals. In scenarios where successful trajectories are infrequent, standard RL algorithms can struggle to bootstrap effective policies because they lack dense supervision across the vast exploration space.

To mitigate the sparse reward problem, On-Policy Distillation (OPD) has emerged as an alternative. OPD utilizes a more capable "teacher" model to generate dense, token-level target probabilities for each token in a generated trajectory, thereby translating sparse trajectory-level outcomes into granular learning signals. A limitation of standard OPD is the requirement for a separate expert model, which incurs computational costs and risks distribution mismatch between the teacher and the student.

Self-distillation variants, such as Self-Distillation Policy Optimization (SDPO) and On-Policy Self-Distillation (OPSD), address these issues by instantiating the teacher from the model itself, often using an earlier version or an Exponential Moving Average (EMA) of the student's weights. By conditioning this self-teacher on environmental feedback, these methods eliminate the need for an external oracle and align the supervisory signal with the model’s own generation distribution.

This paper identifies a limitation within existing self-distillation methods: they predominantly treat environmental feedback as a passive conditioning signal. This approach leads to heavy reliance on successful demonstrations, causing performance degradation in "rare-success regimes" where positive examples are scarce. The current work positions itself by proposing a framework, Reflection-Enhanced Self-Distillation (RESD), to bridge this gap. RESD aims to transform raw failure feedback into an active, interpretable, and reusable source of corrective supervision, thereby improving the efficacy of self-distillation, particularly in environments with limited successful interactions.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the continuous self-improvement capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs) from environmental interactions, specifically addressing the challenges posed by sparse-reward regimes where successful demonstrations are rare.

The motivation for this work stems from an observed limitation in existing on-policy self-distillation methods. While self-distillation has proven effective in mitigating issues like reward sparsity and distribution mismatch inherent in traditional Reinforcement Learning (RL) and external-teacher distillation, its success is largely contingent on the quality of the self-teacher's supervision. Current approaches, such as SDPO, typically treat environmental feedback as a static, passive conditioning variable. This passive treatment overlooks the potential for deeper interpretation and structured representation of the feedback itself.

The authors motivate this limitation through an ablation study, demonstrating that when SDPO operates with a batch size of N=1 (meaning a single rollout per prompt, thus eliminating peer successful demonstrations), its performance degrades substantially. This degradation indicates that the self-teacher struggles to effectively translate raw failure feedback alone into actionable, corrective token-level distributions. In rare-success environments, where most rollouts are failures and provide no positive counterfactual demonstrations, the teacher is left to construct supervision from unstructured failure signals, leading to minimal performance improvements. The paper argues that raw failure signals are often diagnostic rather than instructional; they indicate *that* a trajectory failed, but not *why* or *how* to correct specific decisions.

Therefore, the key motivation is to transform this passive feedback exposure into an active feedback understanding process. The objectives are to:
1. **Retrospectively interpret failed trajectories:** Enable the model to connect the final failure feedback to the specific intermediate reasoning steps that caused the error.
2. **Preserve useful lessons across trajectories:** Organize recurring failure patterns and their corrections into a persistent memory that can be reused in future training steps, rather than treating each failure as an isolated event.

By achieving these objectives, the research aims to make failure feedback a robust and actionable source of corrective supervision, allowing self-distillation to drive meaningful improvement even in the absence of successful demonstrations.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, Reflection-Enhanced Self-Distillation (RESD), extends existing self-distillation frameworks by integrating active feedback interpretation and experience accumulation. RESD modifies the self-distillation loop by enriching the teacher's context with structured information derived from failed rollouts.

**Core Framework (Algorithm 1 and Figure 2):**
RESD operates in an online streaming training protocol, where the model makes a single pass over the training data, and each training example is processed at most once. The training loop consists of several phases:

1. **Rollout & Reward:**
 - The student policy, $\pi_\theta$, generates a rollout (a trajectory of tokens) for a given prompt $x$.
 - The environment provides feedback $c(x, y)$ and a reward $R(x, y)$ indicating the success or failure of the rollout.

2. **Context Update:** This is a crucial phase where RESD processes the feedback to enrich the teacher's knowledge:
 - **Concise Operation:** Before processing the current rollout, the playbook $P$ undergoes a `CONCISE` operation. This prunes stale or harmful entries based on their accumulated usage tags (helpful/harmful counts) and recency of tagging. This ensures the playbook remains compact and relevant.
 - **Retrospective Reflection on Failures:** If the current rollout $(x, y)$ failed (i.e., $R(x, y) < \text{threshold}$):
 - The student model, operating in a diagnostic mode, generates a natural language `reflection (r)` of the failure. This reflection aims to explain the likely cause of the failure and suggest a correction.
 - The reflector also provides entry-level tags ($\ell_{t,j} \in \{\text{helpful, harmful, neutral}\}$) for existing playbook entries, indicating their utility in the current attempt.
 - **Playbook Curation from Past Experiences:** Following reflection, the `CURATE` operation is invoked. It prompts the student model with the current playbook and the generated reflection `r` to identify and generate new, non-redundant natural-language entries that capture reusable lessons from the failure. These new entries are added to the playbook $P$. Each playbook entry $p_j$ maintains counts of helpful ($h_j$) and harmful ($d_j$) tags.
 - **Solution Buffer:** If the rollout was successful, the generated solution $y$ for prompt $x$ is cached in a solution buffer $B(x)$ for potential future replay and conditioning. In this case, no reflection is generated, and $r$ is set to empty.

3. **Enriched Teacher Prompt Construction:**
 - The self-teacher model is prompted with an "enriched context." This context includes the current playbook $P$, the reflection $r$ (if available from a failed rollout), the previous trial $y$, the raw environment feedback $c$, and any cached successful solution $B(x)$ for the given prompt.

4. **Policy Update:**
 - The teacher model's weights, $\theta_{\text{old}}$, are typically an Exponential Moving Average (EMA) of the student's current weights $\theta$, and are updated (`sync teacher weights`) after the policy update.
 - The teacher computes a feedback-informed distribution $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot | x, y_{<t}, c, r, P, B(x))$.
 - The student policy $\pi_\theta$ is then optimized to match this privileged distribution at every decoding step. The optimization objective is a self-distillation loss, $L_{\text{SD}}(\theta)$, which minimizes the expected f-divergence across all tokens.
 - **f-Divergence Choice:** The paper discusses three types of f-divergence: Forward KL (mean-seeking), Reverse KL (mode-seeking, penalizing improbable tokens, used to suppress logical hallucinations), and Jensen-Shannon Divergence (JSD, smoothed and symmetric, for stability when distributions are disjoint). Reverse KL is used for some tasks.
 - **Success-Rate Rebalancing:** To prevent the gradient signal from being dominated by majority outcomes in imbalanced batches (very low or very high success rates), a per-sample weighting mechanism $w_i$ is applied. Successful samples receive higher weights when success is rare, and failed samples receive higher weights when success is common.

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Datasets:** Evaluated on four tasks: MANUFACTORIA-HAS, BOUNCINGSIM-EASY/MEDIUM (from RL-Grok benchmark, characterized by near-zero initial success rates), and FINER (financial entity tagging, higher initial success rate).
- **Models:** Qwen3-4B-Thinking-2507 and Qwen3-30B-A3B-Thinking-2507.
- **Training Protocol:** Online streaming with K=4 inner-loop update iterations per batch. RESD experiments use N=1 rollout per prompt.
- **Metrics:** Per-Task Accuracy (fraction of problems passing all test cases) and Per-Test-Case Accuracy (average fraction of test cases passed). Both mean@4 and best@4 are reported. Checkpoint selection uses a rank-based rule over four validation metrics.
- **Baselines:** Base model (pre-trained), SDPO, SDPO+ss (SDPO variant distilling on self-successful samples). GRPO is used for interaction efficiency comparison.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical evaluations demonstrate that RESD enhances learning capabilities, particularly in challenging rare-success regimes, and improves interaction efficiency compared to several baselines.

1. **Superior Performance Across Tasks:**
 - Table 2 presents the main results, showing RESD achieving the best performance across all four evaluation tasks (MANUFACTORIA-HAS, BOUNCINGSIM-EASY, BOUNCINGSIM-MEDIUM, FINER) and all reported metrics (mean@4 and best@4 for Per-Task Accuracy and Per-Test-Case Accuracy).
 - The most substantial gains are observed in tasks with initially rare success rates, such as MANUFACTORIA-HAS. Here, RESD increased Per-Task Accuracy (m@4/b@4) from 0.57/2.27 (SDPO+ss) to 35.80/65.91, and Per-Test-Case Accuracy from 37.97/71.93 to 76.95/96.53.
 - RESD consistently outperforms SDPO and SDPO+ss on BOUNCINGSIM tasks, indicating the value of failure-derived reflection and playbook memory beyond domain-specific languages.
 - Even on FINER, a task with a higher initial success rate, RESD obtained the strongest results, suggesting that structured feedback is beneficial regardless of initial success prevalence.

2. **More Corrective Teacher Signal:**
 - Analysis of token-level distillation loss (Figure 5) indicates that RESD often results in a higher average self-distillation loss compared to baselines. This suggests that the reflection- and playbook-augmented teacher provides a distribution that deviates more significantly from the student's current behavior, implying a stronger corrective signal.
 - RESD’s loss mass is more concentrated on reasoning decision tokens (e.g., "create," "solution," "The goal"). This indicates that the enhanced teacher is actively correcting the student’s reasoning strategy rather than merely superficial aspects of generation.

3. **Enhanced Interaction Efficiency Compared to GRPO:**
 - A comparison with GRPO (Figure 6) highlights RESD's interaction efficiency. Despite using only a single rollout per prompt (compared to GRPO's eight rollouts per prompt), RESD demonstrates faster early-stage improvement across the evaluated tasks.
 - This suggests RESD's suitability for sample-efficient bootstrapping when interaction budgets are limited. The paper notes that RESD's validation curves can exhibit non-monotonic behavior and occasional dips, particularly in later stages for some tasks, suggesting that a hybrid approach combining RESD for early-stage learning with stabler reward-based optimization like GRPO for later stages could be beneficial.

4. **Component Ablation Study:**
 - Table 3 presents an ablation study on the components of RESD's enriched teacher context (reflection, persistent playbook, solution buffer). The full RESD framework consistently achieves the strongest performance.
 - Removing reflection caused the most significant performance degradation on MANUFACTORIA-HAS, suggesting its critical role in providing immediate, local corrective guidance.
 - Removing the playbook or the solution buffer also led to performance reductions, indicating that the playbook's preservation of reusable lessons and the solution buffer's caching of successful exemplars contribute to overall learning and prevent regressions.

5. **Qualitative Case Study on Failure Interpretation:**
 - A detailed case study on a MANUFACTORIA-HAS task (Table 4) illustrates how RESD progressively diagnoses and corrects errors. The model improved from syntax errors to incorrect transition handling, and finally to a subtle edge case involving empty-tape behavior.
 - This study demonstrates how the playbook accumulates situation-specific rules, recording different corrections for distinct failure modes (e.g., skipping non-pattern symbols vs. rejecting on empty tape). This supports the argument that organizing feedback into persistent, scoped knowledge is more effective than passive appending.

6. **Instruction Following Preservation and Training Latency:**
 - Evalutation on the IFEval benchmark (Appendix E) indicates that RESD successfully preserves the model's general instruction-following capabilities, with performance remaining stable or slightly improving after training on specific tasks.
 - Latency analysis (Appendix F) shows that while RESD introduces a moderate computational overhead due to reflection and curation compared to standard SDPO, it remains substantially faster than GRPO, which requires a larger number of rollouts per step.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Reflection-Enhanced Self-Distillation (RESD) framework presents several significant contributions to the field of Large Language Model (LLM) post-training and continuous improvement, with potential impacts across various applications.

1. **Enabling Learning in Rare-Success Regimes:** A primary significance of RESD is its ability to facilitate LLM learning in environments characterized by sparse rewards and infrequent successful demonstrations. Traditional RL and existing self-distillation methods often struggle in such scenarios due to the lack of sufficient positive signals. By actively interpreting and structuring failure feedback, RESD transforms typically uninformative failures into a rich source of corrective supervision. This capability is critical for deploying LLMs in real-world settings where tasks may be novel, complex, or inherently difficult to succeed at initially.

2. **Enhanced Interaction Efficiency:** RESD achieves faster early-stage improvement with a substantially lower interaction budget compared to methods like GRPO. By utilizing only a single rollout per prompt, RESD significantly reduces the computational and environmental interaction costs. This makes it more practical for real-world applications where obtaining multiple rollouts or extensive environmental interactions can be costly or time-consuming. The improved sample efficiency allows models to bootstrap learning more rapidly from limited experiences.

3. **Paradigm Shift in Feedback Utilization:** The work shifts the paradigm from treating environmental feedback as a passive conditioning signal to an active, interpreted, and organized source of knowledge. The mechanisms of retrospective reflection (diagnosing local errors) and persistent playbook curation (preserving reusable lessons) provide a structured way for LLMs to learn from their mistakes and accumulate actionable knowledge across training steps. This structured feedback provides dense, token-level supervision even in the absence of successful trajectories, addressing a fundamental limitation of prior methods.

4. **Plug-and-Play Feedback Enhancement Module:** The authors position the reflection and playbook mechanisms as a plug-and-play module that can augment other self-distillation objectives. This suggests that RESD’s innovations in feedback processing are generalizable and can potentially enhance the performance and stability of a broader range of self-improvement algorithms, rather than being restricted to a specific self-distillation formulation. This modularity could foster further research into hybrid approaches.

5. **Complementary to Reward-Based Optimization:** The findings suggest that RESD is particularly effective for bootstrapping learning in early, rare-success stages. It complements reward-based optimization methods like GRPO, which may become more stable once sufficient successful rollouts are available. This insight could lead to the development of sophisticated multi-stage training strategies where RESD is used for initial rapid learning from failures, followed by reward-based methods for fine-tuning and stable convergence.

6. **Improved Model Interpretability and Debuggability:** The process of generating natural language reflections and curating a playbook provides a degree of interpretability into the model's learning process. By explicitly articulating reasons for failure and corrective actions, the system offers insights that could aid human developers in understanding model behavior and debugging complex reasoning tasks.

In conclusion, RESD contributes significantly by enabling LLMs to learn robustly and efficiently from failures, especially in challenging low-success scenarios. Its focus on actively structuring and reusing feedback opens new avenues for scalable and continuous self-improvement in LLMs, with broad implications for autonomous agents and complex problem-solving.
