# RSIBench-Data: Benchmarking Data-Centric Research for Recursive Self-Improvement

> [!summary] Summary
> RSIBench-Data is a controlled benchmark that tests whether LLM agents can act as data-centric post-training researchers: diagnose a capability gap, synthesize training data, and revise from checkpoint feedback while the base model, LoRA SFT backend, serving path, sandboxed evaluator, and budget stay fixed. Across four frontier agents and six benchmarks, agents beat their first valid attempt in 58.33% of settings but regress after peaking in 78.26% of continued searches.

## Problem

- Prior automated post-training benchmarks let agents change optimization, serving, evaluation, and system code, so research skill cannot be separated from infrastructure engineering.
- Data-curation benchmarks only score selection from a fixed corpus or a produced dataset, never the iterative hypothesis-to-revision loop that recursive self-improvement needs.
- Closed-loop predecessors give student-model feedback but do not enforce train/eval data isolation or service-isolated training, serving, and scoring, so results stay unauditable.

## Method

- **Fixed-boundary protocol**: agents submit message-format supervision plus a whitelisted config, trained as ==LoRA SFT== on one base model through shared ==Tinker== services, never training code.
- **Closed-loop data-synthesis research task**: each round the agent forms a capability hypothesis, picks sources, filtering, verification, curricula and mixtures, then revises from permitted ==selection feedback==.
- ==Harbor-orchestrated E2B sandbox== evaluation with official verifier and scoring, separating selection feedback from a final official rerun of the agent-chosen checkpoint.

## Results

- A later candidate beats the first valid one in **14 of 24** settings, yet **18 of 23** continued searches end below their peak and **5** only recover it.
- No agent dominates: best scores reach **46.00%** on SWE-bench Verified, **22.00%** Multilingual, **9.00%** Pro, **20.22%** Terminal-Bench 2.0, with spreads of **8.00-20.00** points.
- Raising Claude Code Sonnet-5 reasoning effort from high to max lifts the official SWE-bench Verified score **35%** to **52%**, using **149** training records instead of **50**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Fixing the surrounding stack turns performance differences into attributable research decisions, a template for isolating any single capability inside a multi-component pipeline.
> - Discovery and reliability are separate skills: agents find good strategies but cannot reliably tell whether new feedback should change them, so keep historical-best checkpoints.
> - Cost and score decouple. Spending after a peak often buys nothing, making explicit stopping rules as valuable as better data-synthesis ideas.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated within the emerging field of recursive self-improvement (RSI) for artificial intelligence systems, particularly focusing on large language model (LLM) agents. RSI posits that an AI system should be able to iteratively identify its own failures and translate that evidence into effective model improvements. Recent advancements have seen LLM agents being evaluated on increasingly complex, long-horizon tasks that involve interacting with software repositories, terminals, and external tools. These environments provide rich observational data regarding agent failures, including intermediate plans, tool calls, and recovery attempts. However, the authors note that this observed behavior constitutes "evidence about model behavior rather than training experience that can directly improve it."

Previous research has addressed various aspects of this evidence-to-training pipeline. For instance, benchmarks like SWE-Gym and R2E-Gym provide executable environments for training software engineering agents, while DataComp and DataComp-LM investigate data selection within controlled learning stacks. Other works, such as PostTrainBench and Agent2 RL-Bench, aim to evaluate broader automated post-training systems.

The authors identify a critical gap in the existing landscape: these benchmarks often "entangle research decisions with optimization, serving, evaluation, and system implementation." This entanglement makes it difficult to isolate and rigorously evaluate an LLM agent's pure "data-centric research capability"—its ability to diagnose capability gaps, design training data strategies, and revise them based on feedback in a closed loop. While DataEnvGym and Curation-Bench approach closed-loop settings, RSIBench-Data extends this by isolating a single researcher agent responsible for the entire data-synthesis hypothesis and revision process, with a fixed outer training and evaluation loop.

In contrast to systems that automate experience acquisition for training specific agent methods, RSIBench-Data focuses on comparing how different frontier LLM agents perform the *role* of a data-centric researcher to improve a *separate target model* under controlled experimental services. Furthermore, while broader automated post-training benchmarks measure end-to-end system improvement, their extensive scope can obscure the specific contribution of data-centric research decisions. RSIBench-Data positions itself in a "middle ground," providing a controlled environment to specifically assess whether an LLM agent can function as an effective data-centric researcher, distinct from the underlying training and evaluation infrastructure. This focus aims to provide a more precise and auditable testbed for a core component of recursive self-improvement.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to evaluate the capability of LLM agents to perform data-centric research, a crucial component for achieving recursive self-improvement. The motivation for this work stems from the observation that while AI systems need to learn from their failures, the process of converting "evidence about model failures into better models" is complex and involves several distinct steps. A central part of this process is "data-centric post-training research," which encompasses:
1. **Diagnosing a capability gap:** Identifying specific areas where the model is performing suboptimally.
2. **Designing and validating a training-data strategy:** Creating or curating data that specifically targets the identified weaknesses.
3. **Learning from checkpoint feedback:** Interpreting evaluation results from updated models to refine the data strategy.

The authors articulate a core research question: "Can LLM agents automate this data-centric improvement loop?" They are motivated by the current limitations of existing automated post-training benchmarks, which tend to conflate an agent's research decisions with other operational aspects like optimization, model serving, evaluation protocols, and system implementation. This entanglement makes it challenging to isolate and measure an agent's genuine ability to conduct research—to form hypotheses, generate verifiable training data, and adapt strategies based on empirical feedback.

To address this, the specific objectives of introducing RSIBench-Data are:
- **To provide a controlled benchmark:** This benchmark is designed to isolate the LLM agent's role as a data-centric researcher.
- **To fix the surrounding post-training stack:** This involves standardizing the base model, the bounded training interface, shared Tinker-backed services for training and serving, Harbor-orchestrated E2B sandboxes for official evaluation, and fixed resource budgets. By fixing these external components, the benchmark ensures that variations in performance are attributable primarily to the agent's research capabilities, rather than differences in infrastructure or learning algorithms.
- **To evaluate an iterative training-data research process:** Unlike benchmarks that focus on one-off dataset generation, RSIBench-Data assesses a closed-loop, iterative process where agents:
 - Formulate hypotheses about what the target model needs to learn.
 - Develop and validate specific training-data strategies.
 - Train a new model checkpoint using shared LoRA SFT.
 - Utilize controlled evaluation feedback to inform and revise their subsequent training-data strategies.
- **To create a measurable and auditable testbed:** The standardized infrastructure and protocol generate a structured record of each attempt, linking training-data strategies to checkpoint performance and evaluation behavior. This auditable trail allows for detailed analysis of why a run improved or regressed, facilitating the attribution of performance differences to the agent's data-centric research policy.

Ultimately, the motivation is to facilitate the development of more capable and reliable AI agents that can genuinely self-improve by systematically addressing their own performance limitations through data-centric research.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

RSIBench-Data implements a methodology centered on isolating and evaluating the data-centric research capabilities of LLM agents within a controlled, iterative framework. The core approach involves fixing the surrounding post-training stack while allowing the agent to iteratively develop and refine training-data strategies.

**Problem Definition and Attribution:**
The benchmark evaluates data-centric research through the performance of the final selected checkpoint and the experimental process leading to its creation. A "frontier researcher agent" iteratively improves a "separate target model" by proposing training data ($D_t$) and a whitelisted training configuration ($c_t$). Each resulting model ($M_t$) is evaluated, providing "selection feedback" ($h_t$). After a set number of attempts ($T$), the agent selects a final checkpoint ($M_{t^\star}$) for "official evaluation" ($s_{off}(\pi)$), which measures improvement over the base model ($\Delta_{off}(\pi)$). Crucially, agents only use selection feedback for iteration and checkpoint selection, not official outcomes, and modify training experience only through a bounded interface.

**Fixed Infrastructure:**
A fundamental aspect of the methodology is maintaining a fixed infrastructure for training, serving, and evaluation. This ensures that performance variations are attributed to the agent's research policy rather than external factors.
- **Training Backend:** All agents utilize the same Tinker-backed LoRA SFT backend for fine-tuning a fixed base model.
- **Serving Path:** Checkpoints are served through a uniform sampler interface.
- **Evaluation Environment:** Official evaluations are conducted within Harbor-orchestrated E2B sandboxes, providing a consistent environment, runner, verifier, and scoring rule.
- **Resource Budget:** A common, fixed budget for time and cost is allocated across all agents, promoting fair comparison of efficiency.

**Agent Inputs and Data-Use Constraints:**
Each agent receives comprehensive information including benchmark descriptions, success criteria, public seed repositories or examples, tool/environment schemas, base-model diagnostics, and its resource budget. A key constraint is that evaluation-only tasks, protected benchmark materials, or their labels/trajectories are explicitly forbidden from being used as supervision data. This ensures that agents focus on diagnosing failures and generating novel training data, rather than directly exploiting test data.

**Closed-Loop Data-Synthesis Research Process:**
The central iterative process for the researcher agent involves several stages within each round:
1. **Hypothesis Formulation:** The agent diagnoses model failures, forms a hypothesis about the necessary capability improvements, and decides what the target model needs to learn.
2. **Training Data Synthesis:** The agent constructs training experience. This can involve choosing data sources, designing tasks and environments, representing trajectories (successful, failed, recovery), applying filtering rules, using verification methods, designing difficulty curricula, and mixing different data types.
3. **Training and Checkpoint Creation:** The agent submits the synthesized message-format supervision and a whitelisted training configuration. The system validates these artifacts and invokes the shared LoRA SFT backend to train a new checkpoint from the base model.
4. **Evaluation and Feedback:** The newly trained checkpoint is evaluated by the fixed Harbor/E2B service. The agent receives "permitted selection feedback," including scores, task trajectories, verifier outcomes, execution diagnostics, token usage, and costs.
5. **Strategy Revision:** Based on the feedback, the agent decides whether to continue iterating and how to revise its data-synthesis strategy for the next round (e.g., replacing data sources, adjusting task coverage, changing representations, refining filtering, or rebalancing curricula). This iterative process continues until the budget is exhausted or the agent decides to stop.

**Experimental Setup:**
The evaluation was conducted as a 4x6 matrix, involving four frontier LLM researcher agents and six distinct benchmarks, with the target model, training interface, evaluation services, and budget fixed.
- **Researcher Agents:** Claude Code (Opus-4.8, Sonnet-5) and Codex (gpt-5.6-sol, gpt-5.6-terra) were evaluated. Claude Code agents ran at "high" reasoning effort, while Codex agents ran at "max." Claude Opus 4.8 was fixed as the external rollout model for all agents to generate reasoning traces.
- **Shared Training Pipeline:** The target model was Qwen/Qwen3.5-35B-A3B-Base, fine-tuned using LoRA adaptation via the Tinker SFT backend. Each run had a nominal 16-hour wall-clock budget and a $500 Tinker cost budget.
- **Benchmarks:** Six benchmarks were chosen to span diverse training-experience regimes:
 - **SWE-bench Variants:** Verified, Multilingual, and Pro (requiring repository-grounded supervision for code modification).
 - **Terminal-Bench 2.0:** Emphasizing long-horizon tool use and verification.
 - **GPQA Diamond and AIME 2026:** Focused on verifiable reasoning and difficulty control.
 - All agents shared the same evaluation subsets, runners (Mini-SWE-Agent for SWE-bench variants, Terminus-2 for others), verifiers, and official hyperparameters.
- **Metrics:** The primary outcome was the selected checkpoint's official benchmark-native score. Secondary analyses tracked improvement over the base model, within-run selection trajectories, first and best candidates, training/sampling cost, and wall-clock time.

This comprehensive methodology ensures that RSIBench-Data rigorously tests the data-centric research capabilities of LLM agents, providing a standardized and auditable framework for understanding their strengths and weaknesses in guiding model improvement.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation using RSIBench-Data revealed a "discovery–reliability gap" in current LLM agents' data-centric research capabilities, indicating that while agents can make useful discoveries, they struggle with consistent and monotonic improvement from feedback.

**Performance Analysis:**
- **Initial Discoveries:** Agents demonstrated core data-centric research capabilities by improving upon their first valid attempt in 14 out of 24 settings (58.33%). This indicates that iterative refinement from feedback can lead to stronger candidates.
- **Task-Dependent Performance:** Gains were not uniform across tasks or agents. Performance remained weak on several executable tasks, with the best agents achieving only 9.00% on SWE-bench Pro, 22.00% on SWE-bench Multilingual, and 20.22% on Terminal-Bench 2.0, suggesting shared limitations.
- **Inconsistent Dominance:** No single researcher agent consistently outperformed others across the entire suite. For instance, Claude Code Opus-4.8 led on SWE-bench Verified, Claude Code Sonnet-5 on SWE-bench Multilingual, and Codex gpt-5.6-sol on GPQA Diamond, AIME 2026, and Terminal-Bench 2.0. The three SWE-style tasks were won by three different agents, demonstrating that task-family similarity does not guarantee a stable ranking.
- **Potential for Harm:** Some trained checkpoints either failed to improve or actively degraded performance compared to the base model, particularly on GPQA Diamond and SWE-bench Multilingual, highlighting that data-centric strategies can be counterproductive if not well-executed.
- **Significant Performance Spreads:** The difference between the best and worst agents within a benchmark was substantial, ranging from 8.00 percentage points (SWE-bench Pro) to 20.00 percentage points (AIME 2026), indicating a strong interaction between researcher-agent policy and benchmark structure.

**Iterative Evolution and Candidate Selection:**
- **Non-Monotonic Improvement Trajectories:** A significant finding was the lack of consistent improvement. Among the 23 settings where agents continued searching after reaching their best observed selection score, 18 (78.26%) concluded with a final attempted candidate that scored lower than their historical peak, while the remaining 5 only recovered the peak. This indicates that agents frequently fail to translate additional feedback into further gains, making early or intermediate candidates often the strongest.
- **Role of Historical-Best Selection:** Despite non-monotonic progress, agents' ability to select their historical-best checkpoint mitigated the impact of later regressions on the final submitted model.
- **Varying Iteration Value and Speed:** The utility of iteration was benchmark-dependent. AIME 2026 saw a median gain of 25.42 percentage points for three of four agents, often involving late, discrete jumps in performance after multiple weak attempts. GPQA Diamond, starting from a stronger baseline, showed smaller median gains.
- **Task Families Not Indicative of Search Dynamics:** Within broad task families, such as SWE-style benchmarks, the evolution trajectories and agent rankings varied significantly, underscoring that the agent-benchmark interaction is a more relevant unit of analysis than the task category alone.

**Budget Utilization and Search Efficiency:**
- **Disparate Costs for Similar Outcomes:** High official scores were not uniformly efficient. Agents showed wide variations in resource consumption: median 4.5 valid candidates (range 2–15), $62.51 median Tinker cost (range $4.80–$363.77), and 6.55 hours median wall-clock time (range 1.14–14.91 hours). Some high-scoring runs were comparatively inexpensive, while others were costly for modest gains.
- **Costly Post-Peak Regression:** Trajectories demonstrated that continued spending after an agent reached its peak performance often did not lead to further improvements, highlighting inefficient search behavior. For example, Terminal-Bench runs spent $23.79–$96.74 after their peak without refreshing the frontier.
- **Checkpoint Selection as an Efficiency Factor:** The selection of the best historical checkpoint was critical for translating search expenditure into the strongest discovered model. Although selected checkpoints generally aligned with the best development-stage scores, there was an average 3.89 percentage point difference between selection and official evaluation scores, indicating that development metrics are not perfect proxies for final performance.

**Mechanism Analysis (Trajectory Patterns):**
Analysis of stronger runs identified four recurring patterns in effective data-centric research:
- **Accurate Hypotheses:** Successful agents effectively diagnosed the target capability gap.
- **Validation-Grounded Supervision:** Using executable or decidable checks (e.g., answer-agreement filtering, environment-successful trajectories) for data trustworthiness.
- **Behavior-Aligned Data:** Matching the granularity and source of supervision to the target evaluation behavior (e.g., using real submission traces for SWE-bench Pro rather than synthetic finish actions).
- **Preservation of Strong Checkpoints:** Agents benefited from explicit stop or rollback policies to protect earlier discoveries from later, less effective revisions.
- **Reasoning Effort Trade-off:** A diagnostic experiment showed that higher reasoning effort could lead to earlier discovery of stronger candidates and more resource-intensive data programs, suggesting a trade-off between search depth and breadth.

**Early RSI Experiment:**
An exploratory same-family RSI experiment with a Claude Code agent (kimi-k2.6) improving a moonshotai/Kimi-K2.6 model on SWE-bench Pro showed strategy evolution over seven attempts, improving candidate scores from 8% to 21%. However, even the best candidate remained below the 33% score of an unadapted reference model, suggesting that the current researcher agent's RSI capability was insufficient to improve an already capable instruction model.

In summary, the findings indicate that LLM agents possess foundational data-centric research abilities, capable of iterative refinement and discovery. However, their current performance is characterized by significant task dependency, non-monotonic progress, and inefficiencies in budget utilization, revealing a critical need for more reliable and consistent feedback-driven learning strategies.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

RSIBench-Data represents a significant contribution to the field of AI research, particularly in the domain of recursive self-improvement, by addressing a crucial methodological gap. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Isolation of Data-Centric Research Capability:** The most immediate impact of RSIBench-Data is its controlled design, which successfully isolates the LLM agent's data-centric research capability from confounding factors such as optimization algorithms, serving infrastructure, and evaluation protocols. This disentanglement provides a clearer understanding of agents' strengths and weaknesses in this specific, critical aspect of self-improvement.
- **Establishment of a Measurable and Auditable Testbed:** By fixing the post-training stack and capturing a detailed, round-by-round record of agent actions, hypotheses, and outcomes, RSIBench-Data creates an "auditable testbed." This enables researchers to systematically trace the development of training-data strategies, attribute performance changes, and diagnose the mechanisms underlying success or failure. This level of auditability is essential for scientific progress in agent development.
- **Identification of the "Discovery–Reliability Gap":** The research reveals a fundamental "discovery–reliability gap" in current frontier LLM agents. While agents can iterate and make genuine discoveries (improving on first attempts in a majority of settings), their progress is often non-monotonic. The finding that a substantial majority of searches regress after reaching their peak highlights a significant challenge: agents currently struggle to consistently translate feedback into *sustained* improvements. This insight is critical for guiding future research efforts.
- **Guidance for Future Agent Development:** The detailed analysis of trajectories and mechanisms provides actionable insights for designing more effective researcher agents. Future work can focus on:
 - **Improved Diagnosis:** Developing agents that can more accurately pinpoint capability gaps and formulate better hypotheses.
 - **Enhanced Feedback Integration:** Creating strategies that enable agents to reliably integrate feedback to make monotonic improvements, avoiding costly post-peak regressions.
 - **Efficient Search and Stopping Policies:** Implementing more sophisticated budget allocation, rollback, and early stopping mechanisms to maximize efficiency and ensure the preservation of strong candidates.
 - **Behavior-Aligned Data Generation:** Emphasizing the creation of training data that closely aligns with the actual target behavior and evaluation protocols.
 - **Understanding Reasoning Effort:** Investigating the optimal balance between reasoning depth and search breadth in data-centric research.
- **Advancement of Recursive Self-Improvement:** By providing a structured framework for evaluating and improving the data-centric research component, RSIBench-Data directly contributes to the long-term goal of building truly recursive self-improving AI systems. Reliable data-centric research is a prerequisite for any AI to autonomously learn from its environment and continuously enhance its own capabilities.
- **Open-Source Contribution:** The open-sourcing of the benchmark code (github.com/evolvent-ai/RSIBench-Data) fosters community engagement, allows for reproducibility, and invites collaborative expansion and further investigation of agentic research capabilities.

**Limitations Acknowledged by the Authors:**
The authors acknowledge certain limitations in the current work, which also point to avenues for future research:
- **Adaptive Generalization:** The current evaluation uses fixed subsets for official evaluation, meaning it measures performance in fresh environments but does not yet establish adaptive generalization to statistically held-out or new tasks.
- **Bundled Researcher Identity:** The comparison of "researcher agents" bundles the underlying LLM, agent scaffold, and configured reasoning effort. This means the study compares complete systems rather than isolating the causal effect of any single component, making it challenging to attribute performance solely to the core LLM or agent architecture.

Despite these limitations, RSIBench-Data provides a foundational and timely benchmark for dissecting a critical capability required for advanced AI systems. Its methodology and findings lay the groundwork for developing more dependable, self-improving AI agents that can autonomously discover and implement effective learning strategies.
