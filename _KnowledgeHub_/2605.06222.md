# When to Trust Imagination: Adaptive Action Execution for World Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> A new framework, FFDC-WAM, enables World Action Models to adaptively execute actions by continuously verifying their predicted future observations against real-world sensory input. This approach improves robotic task success rates on challenging manipulation tasks while reducing computational overhead for simpler, predictable actions, mimicking human-like adaptive planning.

## Problem

- World Action Models (WAMs) traditionally execute fixed-size action chunks, leading to computational inefficiencies in predictable scenarios and accumulating errors in uncertain environments.
- This fixed-chunk approach does not dynamically account for the varying reliability of WAM predictions across different task phases.
- Prior adaptive execution methods for other policies lack an explicit mechanism to compare the policy's imagined future observations with real-world sensor data.

## Method

![[2605.06222-method.png]]
*The architecture of the FFDC-WAM. (a) The FFDC module processes predicted actions, predicted visuals, and real observations to produce a confidence score. (b) The causal attention mechanism and the use of a KV cache for computational efficiency.*

- A framework, **FFDC-WAM**, is proposed, combining a **WAM** backbone for ==macro planning== with a lightweight, high-frequency ==verifier== for online decision-making.
- The ==Future Forward Dynamics Causal Attention== (**FFDC**) verifier assesses the trustworthiness of the **WAM**'s predicted action segment by comparing predicted future visual tokens with real-world observations.
- The system adaptively adjusts the effective action chunk size, continuing execution when **FFDC** confidence is high and triggering replanning when significant deviations are detected.

## Results

- In simulated RoboTwin environments, FFDC-WAM improved success rates on 'hard' tasks (e.g., from **54.20%** to **76.40%** on Rand.hard) while reducing WAM inference calls by **69.10%** on average.
- Real-world experiments with an Astribot S1 robot showed FFDC-WAM increased average success rates from **45%** (baseline) to **80%** on pick-and-place tasks.
- Ablation studies confirmed that predicted visual tokens were the most critical input for the FFDC verifier, as their removal led to the largest drop in success rate (from **76.4%** to **71.6%**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Human-like adaptive execution, involving continuous verification of internal predictions against external reality, is crucial for robust physical interaction.
> - A WAM's unique ability to predict future visual observations can be explicitly leveraged to dynamically assess the trustworthiness of its imagined future during physical execution.
> - Adaptive action chunking, based on this future-reality verification, achieves a better trade-off between computational efficiency and robustness in robotic control.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research addresses a limitation in the execution paradigm of World Action Models (WAMs) within the field of robotic manipulation. WAMs represent a recent advancement, extending traditional Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies by jointly predicting future visual observations and future actions. This joint prediction capability allows WAMs to acquire rich spatio-temporal priors and physical dynamics knowledge through large-scale video-action pretraining, leading to improved generalization in novel environments, unseen tasks, and new motion patterns. Unlike VLAs, which primarily focus on generating actions from current observations and instructions, WAMs offer an internal expectation of how the world should evolve under planned actions.

Despite their ability to imagine future world states, current WAMs typically employ a fixed action chunk size for execution. This means that after each model inference, the robot executes a predetermined number of actions without continuously verifying if the imagined future remains consistent with the actual physical rollout. This fixed-size execution presents two main challenges:
1. **Inefficiency:** In predictable or simple task phases, repeatedly invoking the WAM after a short, fixed chunk size leads to redundant computation.
2. **Unreliability:** In complex, deformable, contact-rich, or stochastic interaction phases, the WAM's predicted future can rapidly become inaccurate. Blindly executing a long, fixed action chunk in such scenarios can accumulate errors and lead to task failure.

Existing adaptive execution methods for diffusion policies or VLA models attempt to mitigate these issues by adjusting action chunk length based on signals like action uncertainty, entropy, or policy-side confidence. However, these methods do not fully exploit the distinguishing characteristic of WAMs: the explicit prediction of future visual observations. Consequently, they lack a direct mechanism to compare the model's internal future visual expectation with the real-world sensory feedback during execution.

This work contributes to the landscape by introducing a mechanism that leverages the WAM's future visual prediction capability for real-time "future–reality verification." It aims to bridge the gap between WAMs' generative capacity and their practical execution, allowing for dynamic adjustment of the action execution horizon based on the perceived consistency between imagination and reality. This approach moves beyond policy-centric uncertainty estimation to a world-model-centric self-verification process, thereby enhancing both the efficiency and robustness of WAM-based robotic control.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from an observation regarding human physical interaction: humans do not commit to a fixed future plan but rather continuously predict world evolution under their actions and compare these predictions with observed reality. When predictions align with observations, humans can act smoothly over longer horizons; when deviations occur, they adapt their actions, slow down, or replan. The paper uses the example of missing a stair step to illustrate how a mismatch between expected and actual sensory feedback triggers immediate re-evaluation.

This human-like adaptive behavior highlights a fundamental limitation in the current deployment of World Action Models (WAMs). While WAMs are capable of jointly predicting future visual observations and actions, their typical execution paradigm involves a fixed action chunk size. This fixed execution scheme ignores the inherent variability in the reliability of WAM predictions across different tasks and phases of a single task.

The key objectives of this work are to:
1. **Formulate Adaptive WAM Execution as a Future–Reality Verification Problem:** Instead of simply choosing an optimal fixed chunk size, the core objective is to determine *when the WAM's imagined future can still be trusted* during physical execution. This reframes the problem of adaptive execution into a verification task where the robot continuously checks the consistency between its internal predictions and actual observations.
2. **Develop a Lightweight Verifier:** Design a module, termed Future Forward Dynamics Causal Attention (FFDC), that can efficiently estimate the reliability of a WAM-predicted action segment. This verifier needs to jointly reason over predicted future actions, predicted visual dynamics, real observations, and language instructions.
3. **Enable Adaptive Action Chunk Sizes:** Achieve an emergent behavior where the robot executes longer action chunks when the WAM-predicted future remains reliable and replans earlier when reality deviates from imagination. This aims to combine the efficiency benefits of long-horizon execution with the responsiveness required in contact-rich or difficult task phases.
4. **Improve Robustness and Efficiency:** The ultimate goal is to enhance overall task success rates by reducing execution failures in challenging scenarios, while simultaneously improving computational efficiency by minimizing unnecessary WAM inference calls during predictable phases.

By addressing these objectives, the research seeks to unlock the full potential of WAMs, transitioning them from open-loop, fixed-horizon planners to adaptive, feedback-aware controllers that can dynamically adjust their planning horizons based on real-time prediction-observation consistency.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework, FFDC-WAM, integrates low-frequency macro planning from a World Action Model (WAM) with high-frequency, lightweight verification using a novel module called Future Forward Dynamics Causal Attention (FFDC).

#### 3.1. World Action Model (WAM) Backbone

The framework builds upon Motus, a WAM that jointly predicts future actions ($\hat{A}_{t+1:t+H}$) and corresponding latent future visual tokens ($\hat{O}_{t+1:t+H}$). This prediction is conditioned on the current observation ($o_t$) and a language instruction ($\ell$). The WAM is trained using rectified flow-matching losses for both action prediction ($L_{act}$) and video prediction ($L_{vid}$).

#### 3.2. Adaptive Action Execution with FFDC

During inference, after the WAM predicts an action chunk and its associated future visual tokens, a verifier module $\mu_\phi$ is introduced to determine the trustworthiness of the remaining rollout.
- **Verification Input:** At a verification step $t$, the verifier takes the latest real observation $o_t$, the WAM-predicted future actions ($\hat{A}_{t:t+k}$), the WAM-predicted future latent visual tokens ($\hat{O}_{t:t+k}$), and the language instruction $\ell$ as input.
- **Confidence Score:** The verifier outputs a confidence score $e_t \in$, indicating the estimated reliability of the remaining action segment.
- **Execution Decision:** If $e_t \ge \tau$ (a predefined threshold, e.g., 0.5), the robot continues executing the current action chunk. If $e_t < \tau$, the robot stops the current rollout and triggers a replanning phase by querying the WAM again.

#### 3.3. Future Forward Dynamics Causal Attention (FFDC) Verifier Architecture

The FFDC is designed as a lightweight, N-layer Transformer. Its primary function is to model the temporal interactions between various input modalities to assess prediction-observation consistency.
- **Input Sequence to FFDC:** The input sequence $X_t$ comprises:
 - Language instruction tokens (L).
 - WAM-predicted historical visual tokens ($\hat{O}_t^p = [\hat{o}_{t-k+r}, \dots, \hat{o}_t]$).
 - Current real observation tokens ($O_t$).
 - WAM-predicted future visual tokens ($\hat{O}_t^f = [\hat{o}_{t+r}, \dots, \hat{o}_{t+k}]$).
 - WAM-predicted future action segment ($\hat{A}_t = [\hat{a}_t, \dots, \hat{a}_{t+k}]$).
 - A learnable [CLS] token for global aggregation.
 (Note: $r$ is the action-to-video frequency ratio.)
- **Efficiency Mechanism (KV Cache):** To maintain computational efficiency, the WAM-predicted tokens (L, $\hat{O}_t^p$, $\hat{O}_t^f$, $\hat{A}_t$) are generated once after the initial WAM inference and stored in a Key-Value (KV) cache. During subsequent verification steps, FFDC *only re-encodes the latest real observation $O_t$* and performs attention against these cached tokens. This avoids the computational cost of rerunning the full WAM for each high-frequency verification step.
- **Structured Causal Attention:** The Transformer layers in FFDC incorporate a structured Boolean visibility matrix (mask). This mask enforces causal interactions:
 - Each future visual token $\hat{O}_t^{f(j)}$ can attend to L, $O_t$, $\hat{O}_t^p$, *and* preceding future visual tokens ($\hat{O}_t^{f(\le j)}$), *and* corresponding preceding future action tokens ($\hat{A}_t^{(\le t+jr)}$).
 - A similar causal structure applies to future action tokens.
 - To further reduce computation, a local window attention mechanism is applied over temporally ordered future tokens, ensuring interaction only with nearby aligned action/visual tokens, maintaining temporal causality, and preventing information leakage.
- **Output Head:** The output of the [CLS] token, which aggregates the execution state, is passed through an MLP head $g_\psi$ and a sigmoid function $\sigma$ to produce the confidence score $e_t$.

#### 3.4. Training Strategy and Dataset Construction

- **WAM Training (Mixture-of-Horizon):** To improve long-horizon trajectory coverage and reduce bias towards early episode prefixes, the WAM is trained using a mixture-of-horizon sampling strategy. For an episode of length $T$, a conditioning timestep $s$ is uniformly sampled, and action and video sequences are constructed from $s$ up to a horizon $H$, with out-of-range positions padded.
- **FFDC Verifier Training:** The FFDC verifier is trained as a binary classifier using a constructed dataset $D_{ver} = \{(X, y)\}$, where $y \in \{0, 1\}$ indicates executability (1 for valid, 0 for failure-inducing).
 - **Positive Samples ($y=1$):** Collected from successful demonstration data and a limited number of successful robot rollouts.
 - **Negative Samples ($y=0$):** Obtained from a small number of failed rollouts and from *synthetically corrupted* action segments derived from valid demonstrations.
 - **Data Augmentation (Corruption Methods):** The synthetic corruptions include temporal swap (swapping action pairs), gripper flip (negating gripper dimensions), late-stage Gaussian noise (perturbing the second half of the sequence), and tail scaling (shrinking a suffix).
 - **Loss Function:** Binary cross-entropy ($L_{ver}$).

This methodology enables FFDC-WAM to achieve adaptive execution by continuously verifying the imagined future against reality in an efficient manner, leading to dynamic adjustment of action chunk sizes and improved control performance.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper evaluates FFDC-WAM across various tasks in the RoboTwin simulator and on real-world robotic manipulation tasks, demonstrating its effectiveness in achieving a balance between robustness and efficiency.

#### 4.1. Evaluation in Simulation Environment (RoboTwin Benchmark)

Experiments were conducted on 50 manipulation tasks in RoboTwin under both "clean" and "random" settings, with 100 executions per task. Tasks were categorized into "hard" (success rate < 65% for Base-Motus) and "easy." Metrics included success rate (SR), average task completion time (T), and average WAM inference calls.

- **Overall Performance:** FFDC-WAM achieved the highest average success rate (88.20% on random tasks, 88.90% on clean tasks) while significantly reducing WAM forward passes (69.10% reduction compared to Base-Motus in random settings) and execution time (34.02% reduction in random settings). This indicates a strong robustness–efficiency trade-off.
- **Performance on Hard Tasks:** FFDC-WAM showed substantial robustness improvements. On randomized hard tasks, it increased the average SR from 54.20% (Base-Motus) to 76.40%. On clean hard tasks, SR improved from 57.80% to 76.00%. In these scenarios, FFDC-WAM performed WAM inferences more frequently, demonstrating its adaptive nature to task difficulty.
- **Performance on Easy Tasks:** For predictable easy tasks, FFDC-WAM maintained comparable SR while significantly reducing completion time (e.g., from 23.5s to 15.7s on random easy tasks and from 20.4s to 12.9s on clean easy tasks). The number of model inference calls was closer to baselines with large, fixed chunk sizes (e.g., LC-64), indicating efficient long-horizon execution when predictions were reliable.
- **Qualitative Examples (Fig. 3):**
 - For the "move can pot" task (simple), FFDC-WAM completed the task with a single WAM inference due to consistently high FFDC confidence scores. In contrast, Base-Motus required three inferences.
 - For the "hanging mug" task (challenging), FFDC-WAM adaptively executed long chunks during the predictable transport phase and then triggered frequent replanning in the precision-critical hanging stage when FFDC confidence dropped. Blindly executing a long chunk without FFDC led to accumulated errors and failure.

#### 4.2. Real-World Experiments

The method was tested on an Astribot S1 robot for two pick-and-place tasks.

- **Success Rate Improvement:** FFDC-WAM improved the average success rate from 45% (LC-16 baseline) to 80% across both tasks.
- **Adaptive Behavior:** The qualitative examples (Fig. 4) illustrated FFDC-WAM's ability to detect execution drift and trigger replanning online. In both banana and carrot pick-and-place tasks, FFDC-WAM successfully completed the tasks by alternating between execution and replanning, whereas LC-16 failed due to open-loop execution and error accumulation.
- **Computational Cost in Real World:** While FFDC-WAM had slightly higher average execution time (28.1s vs. 25.6s) and model calls (16 vs. 14) than LC-16 in the real world, this was attributed to necessary online corrections for dealing with perception noise, actuation error, and contact uncertainty. The increase in computational effort was justified by the substantial gain in robustness.

#### 4.3. Ablation Study

An ablation study on the hard-task subset of RoboTwin evaluated the contribution of FFDC's four main input components: language instruction, predicted visual tokens, current real observation, and predicted actions.

- **Importance of Predicted Visuals:** Removing predicted visual tokens caused the largest drop in average success rate (from 76.4% to 71.6%), indicating that the imagined future observations are the most informative signal for assessing rollout trustworthiness.
- **Importance of Real Observation:** Removing the real observation also led to a significant drop in SR (to 72.4%), confirming the necessity of comparing predicted dynamics against the actual current state.
- **Contribution of Actions and Language:** Removing predicted action input reduced SR to 73.4%, and removing language conditioning reduced it to 74.8%. This demonstrates that both the planned control sequence and task semantics provide complementary information for accurate reliability assessment.
- **Full Model Superiority:** The full FFDC-WAM model consistently achieved the best overall performance, with the highest average SR and lowest average completion time, across various hard tasks.

In summary, the results consistently indicate that FFDC-WAM effectively leverages the unique capabilities of WAMs to perform adaptive execution. It significantly improves both efficiency in predictable scenarios and robustness in challenging ones, demonstrating its practical utility in robotic manipulation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of robotic manipulation, particularly concerning the practical deployment of World Action Models (WAMs). Its primary significance lies in redefining WAM execution from a static, fixed-horizon problem to a dynamic, reliability-aware control strategy.

#### 5.1. Paradigm Shift in WAM Execution

The core innovation is the formulation of adaptive WAM execution as a "future–reality verification problem." By directly leveraging WAMs' ability to predict future visual observations, the system gains a novel self-verification capability. This moves beyond simply generating actions or estimating policy uncertainty, enabling the robot to continually ask: "Can I still trust my imagined future?" This mechanism allows the robot to dynamically adjust its planning horizon.

#### 5.2. Enhanced Robustness and Efficiency

The proposed FFDC-WAM framework offers a balanced trade-off between robustness and computational efficiency, a long-standing challenge in robotics.
- **Efficiency:** In predictable or easy task phases, the system prolongs execution of a single WAM inference output, reducing redundant computation and execution time. This is observed in the significant reduction of WAM forward passes and overall task completion time in simulation.
- **Robustness:** In difficult, contact-rich, or uncertain scenarios, FFDC's ability to detect deviations between the imagined future and real observations triggers timely replanning. This mechanism prevents error accumulation from blind open-loop execution, leading to substantial improvements in success rates, particularly for challenging tasks in both simulation and real-world environments.

#### 5.3. Adaptive and Responsive Control

The research demonstrates that the effective action chunk size is no longer a fixed hyperparameter but an emergent consequence of prediction-observation consistency. The robot's behavior becomes more analogous to human cognition, where planning horizons are fluid and adapt to environmental uncertainty and feedback. This adaptability is critical for complex real-world tasks where dynamics are often unpredictable.

#### 5.4. Broader Implications for Autonomous Systems

The concept of online self-verification, where an autonomous agent compares its internal world model predictions with real-world sensory input, has broader implications beyond robotic manipulation. It suggests a pathway for developing more intelligent and resilient autonomous systems that can operate effectively in uncertain and dynamic environments. The lightweight nature of the FFDC verifier, utilizing a KV cache for pre-computed WAM outputs, makes this real-time verification computationally feasible.

#### 5.5. Potential Future Impact

The work lays a foundation for future research in:
- **Richer Failure Mode Learning:** Expanding FFDC training data to include a more diverse range of real-world execution deviations and failure modes could further enhance its robustness.
- **Adaptive Thresholding:** Investigating dynamic or learned thresholds for the FFDC confidence score, rather than a fixed value, might optimize the robustness-efficiency trade-off for different tasks or task phases.
- **More Sophisticated Verification Models:** Exploring larger or more expressive FFDC architectures, potentially through meta-learning or reinforcement learning, could yield even more nuanced and reliable trust assessments.
- **Integration with Other Adaptive Strategies:** Combining future–reality verification with other adaptive methods (e.g., action uncertainty, novelty detection) could lead to hybrid systems with enhanced capabilities.

In conclusion, this research significantly advances the utility of World Action Models by providing a principled method for adaptive action execution. By empowering robots to verify their own imagined futures against reality, it contributes to the development of more robust, efficient, and intelligent robotic systems capable of operating in complex, unstructured environments.
