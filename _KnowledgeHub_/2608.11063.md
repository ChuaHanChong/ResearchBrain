# Deployment Is Not Destiny: Robot Recomposition in the Field with Unseen Software, Hardware, and Compute Payloads

> [!summary] Summary
> A runtime recomposition framework letting robots integrate previously unseen (nondum visum) software, hardware, and compute payloads as plug-and-play components. Containerized software plus keyword-indexed databases of behaviors and PDDL fragments, a Component Manager watching udev events and peer signaling, and behavior-tree task plans composed from whatever is currently available. Field demos reconfigured robots in minutes without developer intervention.

## Problem

- Robots ship as tightly coupled monolithic systems, so adapting them after deployment to new missions or environments demands expert effort and long downtime.
- Existing modular and middleware approaches assume a closed, a priori known configuration space, so genuinely unknown modules cannot be integrated without developer work.
- Prior reconfiguration methods need reboots or relaunches, and distributed or cloud robotics frameworks only schedule capabilities already installed and known to every peer.

## Method

- **Composable component abstractions** unify three payload types: ==USB device enumeration== for hardware, ==peer-to-peer plus RPC daemon== over Ethernet or WiFi for compute, ==containerized processes== for software.
- Software payloads ship a compiled ==container image== plus a ==keyword-indexed relational database== holding behavior and interface libraries per CPU architecture and ==PDDL== actions, domain, and problem fragments.
- A **Component Manager** reacts to ==USB block-device udev events==, maps new device nodes into containers, and exchanges databases with discovered peers, feeding a system-level ==behavior tree== that regenerates ==PDDL==-derived task-level trees.

## Results

- Radioactive source localization with a Spot robot ran three stages, RGB-D person following, thermal-camera following after a blackout, then Compton camera plus pan-tilt laser pointing, recomposing in **1m33s**, **2m16s**, and **3m19s**.
- Thermal-guided person search shared one GPU external compute payload across a GPU-less Panther, wired via Ethernet, and a **2GB** Raspberry Pi Turtlebot, wirelessly, with recomposition times of **5m39s** and **7m22s**.
- The two demonstrations jointly validated all **four** claimed attributes, incremental integration, distributed sharing, host-agnostic reuse, and multi-payload concert, against hours of expert effort for traditional manual integration.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Shipping each capability as a self-describing bundle, container image plus planning and interface database, is what turns an unknown module into an immediately plannable one.
> - Separating a persistent system-level behavior tree from regenerated task-level trees lets task plans track a changing component set without any reboot.
> - The framework schedules and routes payloads but does not choose them; mounts lack proprioception and the classical PDDL planner yields non-reactive plans.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The majority of robotic systems are currently designed and deployed as static entities, preconfigured for specific tasks and environments. This monolithic design approach, while yielding reliable and high-performing systems, results from the dense integration of complex software, electrical, and mechanical subsystems. Consequently, robots often exhibit rigidity, becoming ineffective when mission requirements evolve or new technologies emerge. Reconfiguration typically necessitates significant expert intervention.

The broader research landscape has explored several paradigms to address this rigidity. One approach involves developing general-purpose robots, initialized with a wide array of capabilities to handle diverse tasks without modification. However, these systems can be overbuilt, increasing cost and complexity, and may still struggle to adapt to truly unanticipated demands.

Modularity offers an alternative, enabling robots to be reconfigured by adding or removing hardware and software modules. While prior research in modular robotics has investigated extending functionality and deploying new payloads in the field, these systems commonly encounter two primary limitations:
1. **Closed Configuration Space:** They often require *a priori* knowledge of modules, restricting adaptation to a predefined set of configurations.
2. **Integration Overhead:** Integrating new capabilities frequently demands a system reboot or direct developer intervention, hindering rapid deployment and responsiveness to unforeseen tasks.

Related work threads, as summarized in Table I of the paper, include:
- **Composition Middleware (e.g., ROS):** These frameworks standardize communication but typically require developer action and process relaunch for new components [1, 11–14]. Prior work by the authors improved software composition but lacked automatic runtime adaptation.
- **Software Reconfiguration:** While decades of research exist, practical application often limits dynamic reconfiguration to parameter-level adjustments, with runtime integration of independent components remaining an open challenge.
- **Adaptive Resource Management:** Frameworks like can dynamically allocate components and share physical resources but require components to be known in advance and fault-tolerant behaviors to be manually programmed.
- **Distributed and Cloud/Fog Robotics:** These approaches offload computation to peers or cloud infrastructure [18–20]. However, they generally allocate known tasks over known networks and resources, with developers specifying offloading decisions. They schedule known capabilities rather than composing newly discovered ones.
- **Hardware Modular Robotics:** This area often focuses on standardized physical interfaces for reconfiguration among a fixed kit of modules [8, 21–27], again implying a closed configuration space.
- **Manufacturing Settings:** While emphasizing runtime flexibility with skill-based frameworks and automatic hardware detection, these typically rely on predefined skill catalogs, static knowledge bases, or require custom-engineered self-describing components.

This current work aims to transcend these limitations by presenting a framework that enables open-ended runtime modularity for "nondum visum" payloads—resources previously unknown to the robot—without requiring prior knowledge, reboots, or manual developer intervention. The paper positions itself as the first to achieve full support across the criteria of open configuration space, no reboot, no manual intervention, self-contained payloads, automatic sharing, automatic composition, and support for hardware, software, and compute components.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from the inherent rigidity of most deployed robotic systems. Robots are typically built as monolithic structures, preconfigured for a narrow range of tasks and operational environments. This design philosophy, driven by the complexity of integrating diverse subsystems (software, electrical, mechanical), leads to systems that are difficult and time-consuming to adapt once in the field. Consequently, robots may rapidly become obsolete or ineffective when mission requirements change, new technologies become available, or unexpected environmental conditions arise. Reconfiguring such systems traditionally demands specialized expertise, prolonged development cycles, and significant downtime.

To address these challenges, the paper outlines several key objectives:
- **Enable Rapid In-Field Adaptation:** The primary objective is to allow robots to quickly and seamlessly integrate previously unseen ("nondum visum") modular software, hardware, and compute payloads during runtime. This capability aims to eliminate the need for *a priori* knowledge of the modules or expert intervention.
- **Facilitate Plug-and-Play Functionality for Non-Experts:** The framework is designed to empower non-expert users to augment a robot's capabilities in the field through a true plug-and-play process. This minimizes the technical barriers to extending robot functionality.
- **Ensure Immediate Availability and Distribution of New Resources:** New resources, once integrated, should not only be immediately available to the host robot but also shared automatically with distributed peer systems. This capability is particularly important for compute-constrained robots, allowing them to leverage powerful new remote capabilities.
- **Reduce Reconfiguration Time:** A critical objective is to drastically reduce the time required for re-tasking or adapting a robot. The aim is to achieve reconfiguration in minutes, contrasting sharply with the hours of expert effort often required for traditional, manual integration processes.
- **Demonstrate Efficacy in Dynamic Scenarios:** The research seeks to validate the proposed framework through demonstrations in real-world-like application domains that demand timely and flexible adaptation, specifically citing disaster response scenarios. This includes tasks such as radioactive source localization and thermal-guided person search in challenging environments.

Ultimately, the motivation is to advance robotics towards systems that can dynamically evolve alongside changing tasks, technologies, and environments, thereby overcoming the limitations imposed by static, preconfigured designs.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology is built upon the principle of compositionality, treating every nondum visum payload as a self-contained component capable of dynamic integration into the robot’s decision-making and execution pipeline. The framework is structured in three interdependent parts: Component Design and Abstractions, Component Discovery and Management During Runtime, and Component Utilization.

#### 3.1. A. Component Design and Abstractions

The framework unifies and extends existing interfaces for software, hardware, and compute to create similar composable properties:
- **Hardware Abstraction:** Leveraging existing physical connectors and signal protocols (e.g., USB), the system integrates hardware devices. Operating system device management stacks enable enumeration and access. Physical mounting is facilitated by a rail-based mechanism for larger components and a 3D-printed vise for cameras, allowing for rapid physical recomposition.
- **Distributed Compute Abstraction:** Standardized network protocols (Ethernet, WiFi, IP) are used for communication. Peers discover each other via UDP multicasting and transmit data over TCP. Each compute component runs a peer-to-peer (P2P) and remote procedure call (RPC) daemon to manage these functions.
- **Software Abstraction:** This is the most structured abstraction, extending the authors' prior work which uses Docker-containerized processes, behavior trees for control, and ROS for interprocess communication. To support dynamic runtime changes, sharing, and automatic composition, software components must provide:
 - **Compiled Images:**-compliant container images.
 - **Relational Database:** A keyword-indexed database containing low-level compiled behavior and interface libraries (for various CPU architectures) and high-level PDDL actions and domain/problem fragments that describe the payload’s behaviors. This enables reliable execution across hosts and seamless sharing of planning definitions.

#### 3.2. B. Component Discovery and Management During Runtime

A dedicated **Component Manager** orchestrates the discovery and management of nondum visum payloads. It runs on all participating robots and external compute components, managing component lifetimes and reacting to local system updates:
- **USB Block Device Updates:** `udev` events detect the insertion or removal of USB block devices. New devices are scanned for valid software components (identified by a compiled image and relational database). The host verifies CPU architecture compatibility and resource availability (disk space, GPU memory) before loading images and copying database files. A Docker container is then instantiated, configured, and launched using. Upon removal, associated containers are stopped, images removed, and databases pruned.
- **New Device Nodes:** Non-block USB devices (sensors, actuators) are automatically mapped into the containers of concurrently introduced software components, facilitating plug-and-play integration of physical hardware with its drivers.
- **Peer Signaling:** A P2P and RPC daemon monitors for new networked peers via UDP multicasting. Upon discovery, component databases are exchanged, allowing peers to leverage each other's capabilities. Liveness checks ensure pruning upon disconnection. Newly introduced component databases are automatically shared with known peers, and alerts are issued upon local component removal.

#### 3.3. C. Component Utilization

The control and information flow among components are coordinated by a **system-level behavior tree**, initially composed of pre-runtime software components. This tree handles component monitoring, world model updates for planning, task-level planning requests, and human-robot interaction.
- **Human-Robot Interaction:** A speaker and microphone hardware component combined with software for local speech-to-text and text-to-speech enables natural language goal specification.
- **Task Planning:** PDDL is used for plan generation. A world model aggregates actions, predicates, and objects from all available component databases. Natural language requests are translated into PDDL goals using a local large language model (LLM) software component. Mappings between PDDL actions and component behaviors allow the generated PDDL plan to be translated into a task-level behavior tree (similar to), which is then dynamically inserted and executed as part of the system-level behavior tree.
- **Dynamic Recomposition:** By continuously monitoring updates from the Component Manager and re-querying all stored component databases, the system can dynamically generate and execute plans that utilize the current set of available components, including those shared from distributed peers.

#### 3.4. D. The Result: Plug-and-Play System Recomposition

This integrated approach allows end-users to simply plug in new payloads, which are automatically discovered, installed, and shared, becoming immediately useful. Users can then issue high-level goals that the robot translates into executable task plans utilizing the newly available capabilities.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research validates its framework through two demonstrations across three distinct host robots and a diverse set of nondum visum payloads, showcasing rapid payload utilization and reuse. The demonstrations collectively validate four key attributes: Incremental Payload Integration (A1), Distributed Capability Sharing (A2), Host-Agnostic Integration (A3), and The Concert of Independently Designed Payloads (A4).

#### 4.1. Demonstration 1: Radioactive Source Localization (Fig. 4)

- **Scenario:** Mimics a nuclear reactor incident requiring person tracking and radioactive source localization. A Boston Dynamics Spot robot (host 1, with GPU) is deployed with a human teammate.
- **Stage I (Person-following):** Spot, initially with basic components, is augmented by plugging in an RGB-D camera (hardware 1) with drivers (software 1) and a YOLO image detection model/tracking controller (software 5). The robot follows a "follow me" command. Recomposition time: 2m 16s.
- **Stage II (Dark environment adaptation):** As lights fail, the RGB-D camera is replaced with a thermal camera (hardware 2) and monocular depth-estimation model (software 2). The robot continues person-following using thermal sensing. Recomposition time: 1m 33s.
- **Stage III (Source localization):** Thermal payloads are removed, and a Compton radiation camera (hardware 3) with drivers (software 3), and a pan-tilt unit with laser (hardware 4) with drivers (software 4) are plugged in. The robot then localizes and indicates a hidden radiation source upon command. Recomposition time: 3m 19s.
- **Findings:** This demonstration effectively validated A1 (incremental integration) through successive modifications based on changing task and environmental requirements. It also implicitly supported A3 and A4 by demonstrating the use of various generic sensors and software.

#### 4.2. Demonstration 2: Thermal-Guided Person Search (Fig. 5)

- **Scenario:** A mock disaster search where a large Panther robot searches outdoors, then a smaller Turtlebot enters a confined building. Both robots are computationally limited and share a GPU-equipped external compute payload.
- **Stage I (Outdoor search):** A Husarion Panther robot (host 2, no GPU) is connected via Ethernet to an external compute payload (compute 1, with GPU). This external payload hosts speech models (software 6), an LLM (software 7), and the YOLO/tracking controller (software 5). A thermal camera (hardware 2) with drivers (software 2) is plugged directly into the Panther. The Panther performs a "search for people" task, utilizing its local thermal camera and the remote GPU-intensive software on the external compute payload. Recomposition time: 7m 22s.
- **Stage II (Indoor search via smaller robot):** The Panther is too large for an opening. The thermal camera and drivers are moved from the Panther to a Robotis Turtlebot (host 3, no GPU). The Turtlebot connects wirelessly to the *same* external compute payload. The Turtlebot then executes the "search for people" task inside the building, again using its local thermal camera and the shared remote GPU-intensive software. Recomposition time: 5m 39s.
- **Findings:** This demonstration validated A2 (distributed capability sharing) by showcasing both the Panther and Turtlebot leveraging a shared external compute unit. It strongly supported A3 (host-agnostic integration) by reusing the same thermal camera and software payloads across different robot platforms and environments. A4 (concert of independently designed payloads) was also demonstrated through the cooperation of thermal sensing, image detection, LLM, and speech components.

#### 4.3. Overall Outcomes (Fig. 6)

- **Rapid Adaptation and Reuse:** The demonstrations successfully showed rapid payload utilization and significant reuse of hardware, software, and compute payloads across different host robots and scenarios, despite variations in morphology, computational resources, and tasks.
- **Reduced Downtime:** Recomposition times, including physical interfacing and automatic resource loading, ranged from 1m 33s to 7m 22s. This represents a substantial reduction compared to the hours often required for traditional integration, even for experts.
- **Enhanced Accessibility:** The framework enables non-roboticist end-users to perform complex reconfiguration tasks that typically demand domain expertise, thereby lowering the barrier to deploying and adapting robotic systems.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant advancement in robotic system design by challenging the conventional paradigm of monolithic, static robot deployments. Its core contribution of enabling runtime recomposition with "nondum visum" payloads offers several important implications and potential impacts:

- **Increased Operational Flexibility and Responsiveness:** The ability for robots to rapidly integrate new capabilities (within minutes) in the field provides unprecedented flexibility. This is particularly critical in dynamic and unpredictable environments, such as disaster response, search and rescue, or military operations, where mission requirements and environmental conditions can change suddenly and demand immediate adaptation.
- **Reduced Reliance on Expert Intervention:** By facilitating a true plug-and-play process for non-expert users, the framework democratizes access to advanced robotic capabilities. This could reduce the operational costs and logistical challenges associated with deploying and maintaining robots, as specialized roboticists are no longer strictly required for basic reconfiguration tasks.
- **Enhanced Resource Utilization and Sharing:** The capacity to automatically share newly introduced capabilities, including compute resources, among distributed peer robots is impactful. It allows compute-constrained systems to leverage more powerful remote processing, optimizing resource allocation and extending the operational reach of less capable robots. This could lead to more efficient and collaborative multi-robot deployments.
- **Promotion of Payload Reusability and Cost Efficiency:** The demonstration of reusing identical software, hardware, and compute payloads across different robot platforms and tasks indicates a potential for significant cost savings and increased efficiency in robot development and deployment. Instead of purpose-building every component for every robot, a library of reusable modules can be developed and deployed as needed.
- **Foundation for Future Adaptive Robotics:** This work represents a crucial step towards the realization of truly adaptive and evolutionary robotic systems. By enabling robots to continuously evolve their capabilities alongside evolving tasks, technologies, and environments, it lays a foundation for more autonomous and intelligent robots capable of operating in highly complex and uncertain contexts.
- **Challenges Current Integration Paradigms:** The framework directly addresses the limitations of tightly coupled, pre-engineered robotic systems, promoting a modular, component-based approach that can fundamentally alter how robots are designed, developed, and deployed.

While the current work focuses on scheduling, information flow, and utilization of dynamically introduced payloads, with human responsibility for payload selection and physical mounting, it nonetheless establishes a robust framework for greatly enhancing the adaptability and utility of robotic systems in the field.
