# Model-Based Diffusion for Trajectory Optimization

> [!summary] Summary
> Model-Based Diffusion (MBD) introduces a framework for trajectory optimization that guides diffusion processes with known system dynamics, removing the need for large demonstration datasets. The method successfully generates complex, dynamically feasible trajectories for contact-rich robotics tasks like humanoid locomotion and outperforms state-of-the-art black-box optimization methods in high-dimensional settings.

## Problem

- Traditional trajectory optimization methods struggle with non-linear dynamics, non-convex landscapes, and high-dimensional state spaces.
- Existing diffusion-based robotics methods are model-free, necessitating large, high-quality demonstration datasets and limiting generalization.
- Gradient-based and sampling-based optimization techniques face limitations in differentiability, local optima, or computational expense in high dimensions.

## Method

- **MBD** integrates known ==system dynamics== into a ==diffusion process== to guide trajectory generation without large datasets.
- It approximates the ==score function== directly using the system dynamics model, employing ==Bayes' rule== and ==Monte Carlo integration==.
- The framework can seamlessly incorporate various qualities of demonstration data to enhance performance when available.

## Results

- Successfully generated complex, contact-rich motions for humanoid standing/running and quadruped locomotion.
- Outperformed state-of-the-art zeroth-order optimization methods (CMA-ES, TuRBO, LA-MCTS) on black-box benchmarks, particularly in high dimensions.
- Achieved superior performance (e.g., **59% better** than PPO) and sample efficiency compared to various existing trajectory optimization and sampling methods in challenging robotics tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - System dynamics can directly approximate the score function in a diffusion model, enabling model-based trajectory generation without large datasets.
> - The framework combines the generative power of diffusion models with the efficiency of model-based approaches for complex optimization.
> - MBD can effectively leverage diverse forms of demonstration data, including partial or dynamically infeasible trajectories, and incorporates constraints directly.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Introduction

Trajectory optimization (TO) is a fundamental problem in robotics, where the goal is to find optimal sequences of states and controls that accomplish specific tasks while satisfying system dynamics and constraints. Traditional approaches to trajectory optimization often struggle with non-linear dynamics, non-convex optimization landscapes, and high-dimensional state spaces common in robotics applications.

Recent advances in generative AI, particularly diffusion models, have shown promise in generating complex and realistic trajectories. However, most existing diffusion-based methods for robotics are model-free, requiring large datasets of high-quality demonstration data and limiting their generalization to new scenarios.

The paper "Model-Based Diffusion for Trajectory Optimization" introduces a novel approach called Model-Based Diffusion (MBD) that leverages known system dynamics to guide the diffusion process, eliminating the need for large demonstration datasets while maintaining the powerful generative capabilities of diffusion models.

### 2. Background and Motivation

Trajectory optimization is typically approached through gradient-based optimization methods, sampling-based techniques, or learning-based approaches. Each has its limitations:

1. **Gradient-based methods** require differentiable dynamics and can get trapped in local optima
2. **Sampling-based methods** like Cross-Entropy Method (CEM) or Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy (CMA-ES) can be computationally expensive in high-dimensional spaces
3. **Learning-based approaches** often require extensive demonstration data and struggle with generalization

Diffusion models have recently emerged as powerful generative models in computer vision and have been applied to robotics tasks. These models work by gradually adding noise to data in a forward process and then learning to reverse this process to generate new samples. However, existing diffusion-based methods for robotics are predominantly model-free, requiring large datasets of demonstration data.

The key motivation behind MBD is to combine the strengths of model-based approaches (leveraging known dynamics) with the generative power of diffusion models, thereby addressing the limitations of both paradigms.

### 3. Model-Based Diffusion Framework

The Model-Based Diffusion (MBD) framework consists of two primary processes:

1. **Forward Process**: Progressively adds noise to the initial trajectory, creating increasingly noisy versions
2. **Backward Process**: Iteratively refines noisy trajectories toward dynamically feasible and optimal solutions

Unlike conventional diffusion models that learn the backward process from data, MBD directly computes the score function (gradient of log probability density) using the known system dynamics model, eliminating the need for demonstration data.

The key insight of MBD is using the system's dynamics model to approximate the score function, which guides the trajectory refinement during the backward process. This is done by connecting the current noisy state back to the original target distribution via Bayes' rule and then using Monte Carlo integration.

### 4. Mathematical Formulation

The MBD framework can be formalized as follows:

1. **Target Distribution**: The goal is to sample from a target distribution that assigns high probability to optimal trajectories:

 $$p_0(Y) \propto \exp(-J(Y))$$

 where $J(Y)$ is the cost function for trajectory $Y$.

2. **Forward Process**: A sequence of increasingly noisy distributions is created according to:

 $$Y^{(i)} = \sqrt{\alpha_i}Y^{(i-1)} + \sqrt{1-\alpha_i}w$$

 where $\alpha_i$ controls the noise schedule and $w$ is standard Gaussian noise.

3. **Score Function Approximation**: The score function $\nabla_Y \log p_i(Y)$ is approximated using the system dynamics model through Bayes' rule and Monte Carlo integration:

 $$\nabla_Y \log p_i(Y) \approx \frac{\mathbb{E}_{Y_0|Y}[\nabla_Y \log p(Y|Y_0)]}{\mathbb{E}_{Y_0|Y}}$$

4. **Backward Process**: The noisy trajectory is refined using Monte Carlo score ascent:

 $$Y^{(i-1)} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_i}}(Y^{(i)} + (1-\alpha_i)\nabla_{Y^{(i)}}\log p_i(Y^{(i)}))$$

This mathematical formulation allows MBD to generate trajectories without requiring demonstration data, while still leveraging the powerful generative capabilities of diffusion models.

### 5. Integration with Demonstration Data

While MBD can function without demonstration data, it can also seamlessly incorporate available data of varying quality to improve performance. This flexibility is a significant advantage over purely model-free or model-based approaches.

MBD can utilize different types of demonstration data:

1. **Complete and dynamically feasible demonstrations**: High-quality demonstrations can be used to guide the diffusion process toward known good solutions
2. **Partial-state demonstrations**: MBD can work with incomplete state information, as demonstrated in humanoid running tasks using only lower-body motion data
3. **Dynamically infeasible trajectories**: Even trajectories that violate system dynamics (e.g., from simplified planners) can guide the diffusion process toward promising regions

The paper demonstrates how MBD can effectively use lower-body-state-only human motion data to generate full humanoid running trajectories, and how infeasible paths from a rapidly exploring random tree (RRT) planner can guide long-horizon navigation tasks.

### 6. Experimental Results

The authors evaluate MBD on several challenging robotics tasks and high-dimensional black-box optimization problems:

1. **Contact-rich robotics tasks**: Humanoid standing up and running, quadruped running
2. **Long-horizon navigation**: Car maneuvering through complex environments
3. **Standard black-box optimization benchmarks**: Ackley and Rastrigin functions in various dimensions

For the black-box optimization benchmarks, MBD outperforms state-of-the-art zeroth-order optimization methods like CMA-ES, TuRBO, and LA-MCTS, especially in high-dimensional spaces.

### 7. Comparison with Existing Methods

MBD demonstrates significant advantages over existing approaches:

1. **Compared to model-free diffusion methods**:
 - Does not require large demonstration datasets
 - Better generalization to new scenarios
 - Can incorporate dynamics constraints directly

2. **Compared to traditional trajectory optimization**:
 - More robust to non-smooth dynamics and non-convex cost landscapes
 - Explores multiple solution modes simultaneously
 - Achieves 59% better performance than PPO in various tasks

3. **Compared to sampling-based methods**:
 - More sample-efficient in high-dimensional spaces
 - Better at handling complex constraints

The paper shows that MBD significantly outperforms state-of-the-art methods like PPO, CMA-ES, CEM, and MPPI in challenging robotics tasks, often achieving better results within tens of seconds of computation.

### 8. Integration of Constraints and Imperfect Data

One key feature of MBD is its ability to handle constraints and leverage imperfect data:

1. **Constraint handling**: MBD incorporates constraints (e.g., collision avoidance, joint limits) into the target distribution and uses constrained sampling during the backward diffusion process.

2. **Working with imperfect data**: As shown in Figure 6, MBD can effectively use data points even when they lie in infeasible regions, guiding the diffusion process toward feasible solutions.

### 9. Limitations and Future Work

Despite its promising results, MBD has several limitations that open avenues for future research:

1. **Computational efficiency**: The Monte Carlo score approximation requires multiple forward model rollouts, which can be computationally expensive for complex systems.

2. **Approximate dynamics models**: While the paper assumes access to accurate dynamics models, real-world robots often have approximate or uncertain dynamics models. Future work could explore making MBD robust to model errors.

3. **Hyperparameter sensitivity**: The diffusion schedule and Monte Carlo sampling parameters require careful tuning, which could be automated in future work.

4. **Multi-modal solutions**: While MBD can theoretically capture multiple solution modes, the current implementation may not fully exploit this capability. Future work could enhance MBD's ability to generate diverse solution sets.

5. **Real-world deployment**: The paper primarily demonstrates results in simulation. Applying MBD to real robotic systems with sensor noise and actuation delays presents additional challenges.

### 10. Conclusion

Model-Based Diffusion (MBD) represents a significant advancement in trajectory optimization by combining the strengths of model-based approaches with the generative power of diffusion models. By using the system's dynamics to approximate the score function, MBD eliminates the need for large demonstration datasets while maintaining the ability to generate complex, dynamically feasible trajectories.

The key contributions of MBD include:

1. A novel framework that integrates system dynamics into the diffusion process
2. The ability to work without demonstration data while still being able to leverage available data of varying quality
3. Superior performance compared to existing trajectory optimization methods on challenging robotics tasks
4. Effective handling of constraints and imperfect data

MBD opens up new possibilities for robotics applications by simplifying the process of generating complex motions without extensive data collection, potentially enabling the development of more capable and adaptable robots across various domains.
