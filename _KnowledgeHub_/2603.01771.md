# Hyperparameter Trajectory Inference with Conditional Lagrangian Optimal Transport

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of Cambridge developed Hyperparameter Trajectory Inference (HTI), a method leveraging Conditional Lagrangian Optimal Transport (CLOT) to create surrogate models that dynamically adapt neural network behavior at inference time. This approach, which learns continuous output distribution dynamics from sparse observations, reduced the computational adaptation time for reinforcement learning policies from 38 to 11 GPU hours in cancer therapy simulations and achieved superior performance in various tasks for unseen hyperparameter settings.

## Problem

- Neural network hyperparameters are typically fixed at design, yet optimal settings can change post-deployment due to evolving user preferences or environmental conditions.
- Retraining neural networks for dynamic hyperparameter adjustments is often computationally expensive and impractical for real-time, inference-time adaptation.
- Existing trajectory inference methods often do not adequately account for conditioning variables influencing dynamics, and prior neural optimal transport approaches use fixed cost functions.

## Method

- Introduces Hyperparameter Trajectory Inference (**HTI**), which learns a neural network's conditional output distribution changes with hyperparameters by constructing a surrogate model.
- Proposes a Conditional Lagrangian ==Optimal Transport== (CLOT) method that jointly learns a conditional Lagrangian function (kinetic and potential energy terms) and associated optimal transport maps and geodesics.
- Incorporates inductive biases for dense data traversal (via a potential energy term) and least-action principles (via a learned kinetic energy term with an expressive neural metric), all conditioned on input data using FiLM layers.

## Results

- Achieved the lowest Negative Log-Likelihood and Circle Distance in synthetic conditional trajectory inference tasks, accurately reconstructing complex conditional probability paths.
- Reduced computational adaptation time for reinforcement learning policies from **38** to **11** GPU hours in cancer therapy simulations, yielding the highest average reward at held-out hyperparameter values.
- Demonstrated superior performance with the lowest Mean Squared Error in quantile regression and lowest Wasserstein Distance in generative modeling for unseen hyperparameter settings across diverse applications.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The learned potential energy term guides inferred paths to traverse dense regions of the data space, ensuring path feasibility and meaningfulness.
> - An expressive neural parametrization for the kinetic energy term's metric (learning both rotation and eigenvalues) effectively captures complex, non-Euclidean hyperparameter-induced dynamics.
> - A joint min-max optimization procedure allows for the efficient approximation of continuous neural network output distributions across a hyperparameter spectrum from sparsely observed ground-truth distributions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Neural network (NN) behavior is largely determined by hyperparameters, which are typically fixed at design time. However, in post-deployment scenarios, user preferences or environmental conditions can evolve, rendering initial hyperparameter settings suboptimal. This often necessitates computationally expensive retraining of the NN, which can be infeasible. This paper introduces Hyperparameter Trajectory Inference (HTI) as a method to address this challenge by learning a surrogate model capable of approximating NN outputs across a spectrum of hyperparameter settings at inference time.

The work draws inspiration from Trajectory Inference (TI), a field primarily focused on recovering continuous temporal dynamics from sparse observations, particularly in domains such as single-cell genomics. However, existing TI approaches often do not adequately account for conditioning variables that influence these dynamics. This paper extends TI to the Conditional Trajectory Inference (CTI) setting, where a conditioning variable explicitly impacts the dynamics of interest.

The proposed methodology is grounded in Optimal Transport (OT) theory, specifically Conditional Lagrangian Optimal Transport (CLOT). Neural networks have been widely adopted to address the computational challenges of high-dimensional OT by parameterizing Kantorovich potentials and transport maps. While some works have explored neural Conditional Optimal Transport (COT), they typically utilize fixed cost functions. This research advances this area by learning a *conditional Lagrangian cost function* that can embed specific inductive biases.

Furthermore, this work relates to conditional generative modeling, such as diffusion and flow matching models, which transport mass between distributions. However, these models primarily focus on high-fidelity sample generation from target distributions and are not inherently designed to ensure the feasibility or optimality of intermediate paths, a critical aspect addressed by the Lagrangian framework in this paper.

Finally, while Bayesian Optimization (BO) also involves building surrogate models over hyperparameters, it typically focuses on scalar objective functions (e.g., validation accuracy). This work differentiates itself by learning a surrogate for the NN's entire conditional output distribution, allowing for more flexible, post-hoc objective evaluations. The introduction of HTI, therefore, contributes a novel approach to the challenges of adaptability and personalization in deployed neural networks, bridging gaps in TI, neural OT, and generative modeling for this specific application.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to introduce and develop a solution for Hyperparameter Trajectory Inference (HTI). HTI is defined as the task of learning, from observed data, how a neural network's conditional output distribution changes with its hyperparameters, and subsequently constructing a surrogate model that can approximate the NN's behavior at unobserved hyperparameter settings.

The motivation for HTI stems from several practical challenges in the deployment and adaptation of neural networks:
- **Evolving User Preferences and Conditions:** In real-world applications, the optimal settings for subjective hyperparameters (e.g., reward weights in reinforcement learning, quantile targets in regression) can change post-deployment due to evolving user preferences, patient-specific factors, or dynamic environmental conditions.
- **Prohibitive Retraining Costs:** When initial hyperparameter settings become suboptimal, the traditional approach is to retrain the neural network with new parameters. This process is often computationally intensive, time-consuming, and thus infeasible for dynamic, real-time adaptation or personalized adjustments in deployment scenarios.
- **Need for Inference-Time Adaptation:** There is a need for mechanisms that allow for rapid, approximate adjustment of NN behavior at inference time without requiring full retraining. This capability would enable personalized treatment strategies, dynamic uncertainty quantification, or on-the-fly trade-offs in NN characteristics.

The paper elaborates on several specific use cases to underscore this motivation:
- **Reinforcement Learning (RL) Policy Adaptation:** In RL, hyperparameters (e.g., reward weights) dictate fundamental policy behaviors. For instance, in cancer treatment, a reward weight (λ_nk) might balance tumor reduction against immune system damage. Optimal balance can vary significantly per patient based on comorbidities. HTI aims to create a surrogate policy (p(a|s, λ)) that permits personalized treatment strategies by adjusting λ at inference time, avoiding the lengthy process of training a new policy for each patient.
- **Dynamic Quantile Regression:** Quantile regression models provide prediction intervals. However, examining arbitrary quantiles for tailored uncertainty bounds can be computationally demanding if separate models must be trained or fine-tuned for each quantile. HTI could learn the dynamics across a quantile range, providing a surrogate that predicts intermediate quantiles efficiently.
- **General Hyperparameter Interpolation:** The methodology is also motivated by the need to interpolate across other continuous hyperparameters like dropout rates in generative models, allowing dynamic adjustment of properties like output fidelity or diversity.

A core challenge highlighted is that hyperparameter-induced dynamics (λ -> pθλ(y|x)) are typically non-linear due to the complex optimization landscapes of deep learning models. This complexity means that straightforward interpolation schemes are unlikely to produce feasible or meaningful surrogate models. Therefore, the objectives include developing an approach capable of capturing these complex, non-Euclidean dynamics from sparse ground-truth distributions, while ensuring the inferred paths remain meaningful by incorporating appropriate inductive biases.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology for Hyperparameter Trajectory Inference (HTI) is based on Conditional Lagrangian Optimal Transport (CLOT). The approach involves jointly learning the Lagrangian function governing hyperparameter-induced dynamics, along with the associated optimal transport maps and geodesics between observed marginal distributions. These learned components then form the basis of the surrogate model.

The core of the method lies in designing a conditional Lagrangian, $L(q_t, \dot{q}_t|x)$, which consists of kinetic and potential energy terms:
$L(q_t, \dot{q}_t|x) = K(q_t, \dot{q}_t|x) - U(q_t|x) = \frac{1}{2} \dot{q}_t^T G(q_t|x) \dot{q}_t - U(q_t|x)$.
The action $S(q|x)$ of a curve $q$ is defined as the integral of this Lagrangian. The Lagrangian cost function $c(y_0, y_1|x)$ is then the least-action (geodesic) path between $y_0$ and $y_1$.

The methodology incorporates two inductive biases into the learned Lagrangian:
1. **Dense Traversal (Manifold Hypothesis):** This bias encourages inferred paths to traverse regions of the data space where observed data samples are dense. This is encoded through the **potential energy term, $\hat{U}(q|x)$**.
 - $\hat{U}(q|x) = \alpha \log(\hat{p}(q|x) + \epsilon)$, where $\alpha > 0$ controls the bias strength and $\epsilon > 0$ ensures numerical stability.
 - $\hat{p}(q|x)$ is a Nadaraya-Watson kernel density estimator that estimates the conditional data density at point $q$ given condition $x$, using the observed samples $D_{obs} = \{(y_i, x_i, t_i)\}$. A higher density $\hat{p}(q|x)$ leads to a higher $\hat{U}(q|x)$, which reduces the overall action $S(q|x)$ (due to the negative sign in the Lagrangian) for paths through dense regions, thus favoring them. This potential energy term is fixed after its initial estimation.

2. **Least-Action Principles:** This bias assumes that dynamics between observed distributions are efficient in the underlying data manifold. This is embedded by learning the **kinetic energy term, $K(q_t, \dot{q}_t|x)$**, through its metric $G(q_t|x)$.
 - Neural networks are used to parametrize the metric $G_{\theta_G}(q|x)$, the Kantorovich potentials $g_{\theta_{g,k}}(y|x)$ (for intervals between observed times $t_k$ and $t_{k+1}$), the conditional optimal transport maps $T_{\theta_{T,k}}(y_k|x)$, and the parameters of spline-based geodesic estimation $S_{\theta_S}(y_k, y_{k+1}, x)$.
 - **Joint Learning Procedure:** A min-max optimization scheme (Algorithm 1) is employed:
 - The outer loop minimizes an objective $L_{metric}(\theta_G)$ by updating $\theta_G$. This objective sums the estimated CLOT costs between adjacent observed marginals, thus shaping the metric to reflect the most "efficient" (least-action) dynamics.
 - The inner loop maximizes the semi-dual OT objective $L^{(k)}_{dual}(\theta_{g,k})$ for each interval between observed distributions $k$ and $k+1$, by updating $\theta_{g,k}$. This ensures accurate estimation of the CLOT cost for the current metric.
 - Within the inner loop, neural approximators $T_{\theta_{T,k}}$ and $S_{\theta_S}$ are simultaneously trained. $T_{\theta_{T,k}}$ is trained to regress to the optimal transport map obtained via a limited L-BFGS optimization (warm-started by $T_{\theta_{T,k}}$). $S_{\theta_S}$ is trained to minimize the action $S(q_\phi|x)$ for paths connecting points specified by the transport maps.
 - **Conditional Dependence:** All neural networks (for $G$, $g$, $T$, and $S$) are conditioned on $x$ using FiLM layers, allowing the dynamics to be condition-specific.

- **Metric Parametrization:** A critical component is the parametrization of $G_{\theta_G}(q|x)$, which must be symmetric, positive-definite, and avoid degenerate solutions (where $G \to 0$ leads to near-zero cost regardless of movement).
 - The paper proposes parametrizing $G_{\theta_G}$ via its eigendecomposition: $G_{\theta_G} = R_{\theta_G} E_{\theta_G} R_{\theta_G}^T$.
 - $R_{\theta_G}$ is a D_y-dimensional rotation matrix formed by multiplying Givens rotation matrices whose angles are parametrized by a neural network.
 - $E_{\theta_G}$ is a D_y-dimensional diagonal matrix, also parametrized by a neural network. To avoid degeneracy, its entries (eigenvalues) are enforced to be positive and sum to a non-zero "eigenvalue budget." This provides expressivity while maintaining sufficient volume. This is an extension of previous 2D-only methods.

- **Sampling from the Surrogate Model (Algorithm 2):**
 - To generate a sample $\hat{y}$ for a target hyperparameter $t^*$ and condition $x$, the method identifies the closest preceding observed time $t_k$.
 - A sample $y_k$ is drawn from the ground-truth distribution $p_{t_k}(\cdot|x)$.
 - The learned CLOT map $T_{\theta_{T,k}}(y_k|x)$ predicts the end-point $\hat{y}_{k+1}$ of the geodesic path.
 - The spline-based geodesic path $q_\phi$ between $y_k$ and $\hat{y}_{k+1}$ is constructed using $S_{\theta_S}$.
 - The final sample $\hat{y}_{t^*}$ is obtained by evaluating $q_\phi$ at the normalized position corresponding to $t^*$. This avoids optimization at inference time.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical evaluation of the proposed Conditional Lagrangian Optimal Transport (CLOT) method for Hyperparameter Trajectory Inference (HTI) is presented across several synthetic and real-world applications.

- **Illustrative CTI (Semicircles Experiment):**
 - The method was evaluated on a synthetic 2D dataset where conditional distributions evolve along semicircular paths. Four ablations of the method were compared: $K_I$ (Euclidean), $K_I - \hat{U}$ (Euclidean + Potential), $K_\theta$ (Learned Kinetic), and $K_\theta - \hat{U}$ (Full method).
 - The full method ($K_\theta - \hat{U}$) demonstrated the most accurate reconstruction of the true conditional probability paths, correctly capturing condition-dependent divergence and semicircular geometry (Figure 1).
 - Ablation studies showed that the potential energy term ($\hat{U}$) biased paths towards dense regions, while the learned kinetic term ($K_\theta$) improved the capture of underlying curvature.
 - Quantitatively, the full method achieved the lowest Negative Log-Likelihood (NLL) and Circle Distance (CD) at withheld intermediate times (Table 1), indicating superior path feasibility.

- **HTI for Reward-Weighting in Reinforcement Learning (RL):**
 - **Cancer Therapy:** In a simulated cancer environment (DTR-Bench Cancer), HTI was applied to learn policy dynamics induced by `λ_nk`, a hyperparameter balancing tumor reduction and NK cell preservation.
 - Policies were observed at `λ_nk ∈ {0, 5, 10}`. The full method ($K_\theta - \hat{U}$) yielded the highest average reward at held-out `λ_nk` values (Table 2), outperforming direct regression, Conditional Flow Matching (CFM), Metric Flow Matching (MFM), and Neural Lagrangian Optimal Transport (NLOT).
 - The surrogate policy's behavior regarding NK cell preservation closely mirrored that of the ground-truth policies (Figure 2).
 - Computational efficiency was significant: surrogate training took ~15 minutes, compared to 3.5 hours for each new PPO policy, reducing total GPU hours for policy adaptation from 38 to 11.
 - **Reacher Environment:** In the OpenAI Gym Reacher task, HTI was used to interpolate policies based on `λ_c`, a hyperparameter weighting control effort.
 - Policies were observed at `λ_c ∈ {1, 5}`. The full method ($K_\theta - \hat{U}$) again demonstrated the highest average reward at unseen `λ_c` values (Table 3).
 - **Non-Linear Reward Scalarization (Cancer_nl):** A more challenging variant of the Cancer environment with a non-linear (hinge) NK cell penalty was tested.
 - The full method ($K_\theta - \hat{U}$) maintained its superior performance, achieving the highest average reward (Table 4), indicating robustness to more complex hyperparameter dynamics.

- **HTI for Quantile Regression (Time-Series Forecasting):**
 - Applied to a higher-dimensional task using the ETTm2 dataset for 3-step time-series forecasting, with `τ` (quantile target) as the hyperparameter.
 - Forecasts were observed from models trained at `τ = 0.01` and `τ = 0.99`.
 - The full method ($K_\theta - \hat{U}$) achieved the lowest Mean Squared Error (MSE) for surrogate forecasts at withheld quantiles (Table 5).
 - Qualitative evaluation showed that the full method's central 80% prediction intervals closely matched the width and shape of the true intervals (Figure 3).

- **HTI for Dropout in Generative Modelling:**
 - Investigated dropout rate `p` as a general hyperparameter for diffusion models trained on the 2D two moons dataset.
 - Models were observed at `p ∈ {0, 0.5, 0.99}`.
 - Methods incorporating the density bias ($\hat{U}$), specifically $K_I - \hat{U}$, achieved the lowest Wasserstein Distance (WD) between surrogate and ground-truth distributions at held-out dropout settings (Table 6).

- **Metric Learning Ablation:**
 - A comparison of the proposed neural metric parametrization ($R_{\theta_G} E_{\theta_G} R^T_{\theta_G}$ with learned eigenvalues) against a variant with fixed eigenvalues (as in Pooladian et al., 2024) in 2D experiments.
 - The proposed parametrization generally led to better performance (lower NLL, higher rewards) in most tasks (Table 7), suggesting benefits from learning the eigenvalues of the metric. It also inherently extends to higher dimensions, as demonstrated in the quantile regression task.

- **Sparsity Investigation (Appendix E):**
 - An analysis of performance with varying numbers of anchor distributions showed that the performance gap between the proposed method and baselines widened as data sparsity increased. The proposed method exhibited less degradation in performance under sparser conditions, highlighting the utility of its inductive biases.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces Hyperparameter Trajectory Inference (HTI) as a novel and relevant problem in machine learning, addressing the practical challenge of dynamically adapting neural network behavior in deployment without the need for computationally expensive retraining. The development of a Conditional Trajectory Inference (CTI) method grounded in Conditional Lagrangian Optimal Transport (CLOT) represents a significant methodological contribution.

The key significance of this work lies in:
- **Enabling Inference-Time Adaptability:** By learning the continuous dynamics of a neural network's conditional output distribution across hyperparameters, the proposed surrogate model allows for rapid, real-time adjustments to NN behavior. This can lead to more flexible and responsive intelligent systems, particularly valuable in dynamic environments where preferences or conditions frequently change.
- **Computational Efficiency:** The demonstrated efficiency gains, such as reducing adaptation time for RL policies from 38 GPU hours to 11 GPU hours, indicate a substantial practical impact. This makes personalization and dynamic tuning of NNs feasible in scenarios where frequent retraining is otherwise prohibitive.
- **Robust Inductive Biases:** The incorporation of both dense traversal (via a learned potential energy term) and least-action principles (via a learned kinetic energy term with an expressive metric) significantly improves the feasibility and meaningfulness of inferred conditional probability paths, particularly when learning from sparsely observed ground-truth distributions. This helps ensure that the surrogate model generates consistent and effective outputs even for unseen hyperparameter settings.
- **Generalizability Across Domains:** The empirical results demonstrate the method's utility across diverse applications, including reinforcement learning (reward weighting in cancer therapy and continuous control), time-series forecasting (quantile regression), and generative modeling (dropout settings). This suggests the broad applicability of HTI for various types of neural network behavioral adjustments.
- **Methodological Advancements in Optimal Transport:** The proposed neural metric parametrization, which learns both rotation matrices and eigenvalues, represents an advancement over prior work by providing greater expressivity and extending gracefully to higher-dimensional spaces. This enhances the ability of optimal transport methods to capture complex, non-Euclidean data geometries.

The potential impact of this work extends to various fields:
- **Personalized AI:** In domains like healthcare, personalized treatment regimens could be dynamically adjusted based on patient-specific data or evolving clinical conditions, as demonstrated in the cancer therapy example.
- **Adaptive Control Systems:** RL agents could adapt their reward trade-offs on the fly in robotics or autonomous systems to prioritize different objectives based on immediate context.
- **Enhanced Uncertainty Quantification:** Flexible generation of prediction intervals in forecasting and other tasks can provide more nuanced insights and support better decision-making.
- **Flexible Generative Models:** Users could dynamically control attributes like diversity or fidelity in generative models without retraining.
- **Advanced Hyperparameter Optimization:** HTI could serve as a powerful component in Bayesian Optimization frameworks, allowing objective functions to be defined and evaluated post-hoc using the learned output distribution surrogate, enabling the exploration of complex Pareto frontiers.

While the current method focuses on a single continuous hyperparameter, its demonstrated capabilities lay a foundation for future research into multi-hyperparameter settings and more chaotic underlying dynamics, indicating a pathway toward more adaptive and efficient neural network deployment strategies.
