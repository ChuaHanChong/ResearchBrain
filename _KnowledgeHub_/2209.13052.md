# Training Efficient Controllers via Analytic Policy Gradient

> [!summary] Summary
> This research introduces a method for training control policies using Analytic Policy Gradients (APG) that achieves trajectory tracking performance similar to Model Predictive Control (MPC) with significantly faster inference times. The approach stabilizes APG training through a concurrent receding-horizon strategy and curriculum learning, outperforming Reinforcement Learning (RL) in accuracy, efficiency, and sample complexity across various robotic tasks.

## Problem

- Model Predictive Control (MPC) provides high accuracy but is computationally intensive, limiting its real-time deployment on resource-constrained robotic platforms.
- Reinforcement Learning (RL) methods offer fast runtime but often lack the precision of MPC for trajectory tracking and demand extensive training data.
- Analytic Policy Gradient (APG) approaches suffer from training instabilities, such as vanishing/exploding gradients and local minima, which hinder their practical application in complex robotic systems.

## Method

- A framework for offline control policy training is developed using Analytic Policy Gradients (APG) within a differentiable simulator, minimizing a fixed-horizon trajectory tracking cost function.
- A concurrent receding-horizon training strategy is introduced, where the policy network outputs all actions for the entire prediction horizon simultaneously, reducing gradient chain length and enhancing training stability.
- A curriculum learning scheme is implemented to stabilize the training process, gradually increasing the allowable divergence from reference trajectories and target speeds to promote robust learning.

## Results

- APG achieved tracking errors comparable to MPC (e.g., 0.05m for quadrotor vs. MPC's 0.03m) while reducing runtime by more than an order of magnitude (e.g., 0.17ms for quadrotor vs. MPC's 5.2ms).
- APG consistently outperformed both model-free (PPO) and model-based (PETS) RL algorithms in trajectory tracking accuracy and demonstrated significantly lower inference times than model-based RL.
- For vision-based control, APG achieved competitive performance (0.15 m/s cart velocity for CartPole) using only 1K samples, demonstrating superior sample efficiency compared to PPO (230K samples).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The concurrent receding-horizon training strategy provides superior stability and performance compared to traditional autoregressive APG approaches, especially for longer prediction horizons.
> - Curriculum learning is crucial for stabilizing APG training, preventing convergence to local minima and enabling the controller to learn robustly across varying initial conditions and task complexities.
> - APG exhibits orders of magnitude higher sample efficiency than both model-free and model-based RL, making it highly effective for learning directly from high-dimensional inputs and for rapid adaptation to new system dynamics.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic control design constantly seeks to balance accuracy, robustness, and computational efficiency. This paper addresses a critical trade-off at the intersection of classical control theory and modern machine learning approaches.

Traditionally, control strategies like PID controllers are simple but often lack the adaptability or precision for complex, dynamic tasks. **Model Predictive Control (MPC)**, a form of online optimization, offers high accuracy and can handle complex dynamics and constraints by repeatedly solving an optimization problem at runtime. However, MPC's primary drawback, especially for systems with limited onboard computation like aerial vehicles, is its high computational demand, requiring significant research into approximation algorithms or specialized hardware to achieve real-time performance.

In parallel, **learning-based control**, particularly **Reinforcement Learning (RL)**, has emerged as a powerful paradigm. Model-free RL shifts the computational burden to an offline training phase, allowing for fast inference at runtime. However, model-free RL treats system dynamics as a black box, requiring vast amounts of training data and often struggling to match the precision of MPC in trajectory tracking tasks. Model-based RL methods, while using a model, often still involve online optimization or complex model learning, inheriting some of MPC's computational intensity or RL's data hunger.

The recent advent of **differentiable simulators** represents a significant shift in this landscape. These simulators allow for the analytical calculation of gradients through the entire simulation pipeline, enabling direct training of policies using gradient descent – a technique referred to as **Analytic Policy Gradient (APG)**. APG offers the promise of combining the best aspects of both worlds: the data efficiency of model-based approaches (by leveraging explicit system dynamics) and the fast execution time of learned policies (by pre-training offline). Previous work has shown APG to be orders of magnitude more data-efficient than RL.

Despite its potential, APG faces significant challenges, notably **training instabilities** such as vanishing or exploding gradients due to Backpropagation-Through-Time (BPTT), and a propensity to get trapped in local minima. Most existing APG applications have been limited to simpler environments (e.g., MuJoCo) or specific domains (e.g., fluid simulations), and systematic comparisons against established, high-performance robotic control methods like MPC have been rare.

This paper directly addresses these limitations by developing methods to stabilize APG training and rigorously evaluating its performance against a broad spectrum of established control techniques. By demonstrating APG's efficacy on complex robotic systems (quadrotors, fixed-wing drones) and challenging tasks (trajectory tracking, vision-based control), the authors aim to push APG from theoretical promise to practical applicability in robotics, effectively bridging the gap between highly accurate but computationally intensive online optimization and fast but often less accurate offline learning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core motivation of this research stems from the critical need for highly accurate yet computationally efficient controllers, particularly for resource-constrained robotic systems such as unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs). The authors identify a significant gap between existing control paradigms:

1. **High-Performance but Computationally Demanding Control:** Model Predictive Control (MPC) offers excellent trajectory tracking performance by solving an optimization problem online. However, its real-time computational demands make it impractical for many embedded robotic platforms, where processing power and energy are limited.
2. **Efficient but Less Accurate Learning-Based Control:** Reinforcement Learning (RL) methods, especially model-free variants, can achieve fast execution at runtime after an offline training phase. However, they often fall short of MPC's accuracy in precise trajectory tracking tasks and require extensive data collection, as they treat system dynamics as a black box.

Given these limitations, the **key objectives** of this paper are:

- **Develop an Accurate and Efficient Controller:** The primary objective is to create a control method that can achieve tracking performance comparable to state-of-the-art MPC while requiring significantly less computation at execution time, thus being suitable for platforms with limited onboard compute.
- **Leverage Analytic Policy Gradient (APG):** To achieve this, the authors aim to exploit the capabilities of differentiable simulators and the Analytic Policy Gradient (APG) framework. APG promises to combine the data efficiency of model-based methods with the fast inference of learned policies.
- **Address APG Training Instabilities:** A crucial objective is to overcome the known challenges associated with APG, specifically the training instabilities caused by vanishing/exploding gradients during backpropagation-through-time (BPTT) and the tendency to get stuck in local minima. This involves proposing novel training strategies.
- **Systematic Comparison:** To rigorously validate their approach, the authors aim for a comprehensive comparison of their APG method against:
 - **Classical Optimal Control:** Model Predictive Control (MPC).
 - **Learning-Based Control:** Both model-free Reinforcement Learning (PPO) and model-based Reinforcement Learning (PETS).
 - This comparison is designed to establish APG's standing relative to the broader control landscape.
- **Demonstrate Versatility and Practical Advantages:** The research seeks to showcase APG's strengths beyond just basic tracking, including:
 - **Sample Efficiency:** Particularly important for adaptation scenarios and real-world learning.
 - **Flexibility with High-Dimensional Inputs:** Demonstrating the ability to handle inputs like images, which are challenging for traditional control methods.
 - **Robustness Across Diverse Systems:** Evaluating APG on a variety of dynamic systems (CartPole, quadrotor, fixed-wing drone) to confirm its generalizability.

Ultimately, the motivation is to unlock the full potential of APG as a practical, high-performance control method for robotics by making it more stable and broadly applicable.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the proposed methodology lies in leveraging Analytic Policy Gradients (APG) for offline control policy training, augmented with novel strategies to enhance training stability and performance.

**A. Problem Formulation and APG Core**
The authors frame the control problem as a fixed-horizon trajectory tracking task, where the goal is to minimize a cost function $J^*(\pi)$ rather than maximize a reward. This cost function is defined as the Mean Squared Error (MSE) between the robot's states and a reference trajectory $\nu_t$, combined with a control cost (Lp-norm of actions). The policy $\pi$ maps current state $s_t$ and reference $\nu$ to an action $a_t$. Crucially, APG leverages differentiable dynamics models $f$ and a differentiable policy $\pi$ (typically a neural network) to directly minimize this cost function via gradient descent, enabling end-to-end differentiable training.

**B. Receding-Horizon Training**
Similar to MPC, the APG controller is trained in a receding-horizon fashion. At each training step, the policy predicts actions for a fixed horizon $T$ steps into the future, and the loss is computed based on the divergence of the resulting states from the reference trajectory over this horizon. The reference states within the horizon are provided as input to the policy network.

**C. Backpropagation Over a Horizon: Concurrent vs. Autoregressive (Key Contribution)**
The paper proposes and systematically compares two strategies for backpropagating gradients over the prediction horizon:

1. **Concurrent Training (Proposed):** At time $t$, the policy network $\pi$ outputs all $T$ actions simultaneously (i.e., $a_t = [\pi(s_0, \nu_{0:T})]_t$). Backpropagation-Through-Time (BPTT) then passes multiple times through the dynamics model $f$ to calculate the loss, but *only once* through the policy network $\pi$. This approach is hypothesized to alleviate gradient issues by simplifying the path through the policy.
2. **Autoregressive Training (Traditional):** At each time step within the horizon, the policy predicts a single action $a_t = \pi(s_t, \nu_{t:t+T})$. This action is then applied, and the resulting state $s_{t+1}$ is fed back into the policy network to predict the next action. This means BPTT passes through both the policy network and the dynamics model $T$ times, creating a longer chain of gradients known to be prone to vanishing or exploding gradients. The authors also tested an autoregressive model with an LSTM for memory.

The paper argues that their concurrent approach leads to improved training stability compared to the traditional autoregressive method.

**D. Curriculum Learning for Stability (Key Contribution)**
To further address training instabilities and the risk of local minima, the authors introduce a curriculum learning strategy:

- **Divergence Threshold ($\tau_{div}$):** During data collection for training, if the robot's state diverges too much from the reference trajectory (i.e., $|| \hat{s}_{t+1} - \nu_{t+1} || > \tau_{div}$), the agent is immediately reset to the reference state $\nu_{t+1}$.
- **Gradual Increase of Threshold:** The $\tau_{div}$ is initially set low and gradually increased over time. This forces the controller to first learn to track accurately from nearby states and then progressively handle larger initial errors or disturbances, leading to more stable and robust learning.
- **Speed Curriculum (for quadrotor):** For the quadrotor, an additional curriculum is applied where training starts on slower reference trajectories, and the speed is gradually increased as the controller masters the task.

**E. Experimental Setup and Evaluation**
The methodology includes a comprehensive evaluation across diverse robotic tasks and against strong baselines:

- **Dynamic Systems:**
 - **CartPole Balancing:** A classic low-dimensional benchmark for control.
 - **Quadrotor Trajectory Tracking:** A complex 3D task using a first-principles model from the Flightmare simulator.
 - **Fixed-Wing Aircraft Target Reaching:** A challenging 3D control task with less maneuverable dynamics based on a first-order model.
- **Baselines:**
 - **Model Predictive Control (MPC):** Implemented using Casadi for non-linear optimization, representing state-of-the-art classical control.
 - **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO):** A widely used model-free RL algorithm (from stable-baselines).
 - **Probabilistic Ensembles with Trajectory Sampling (PETS):** A prominent model-based RL algorithm (from mbrl toolbox).
- **Evaluation Metrics:**
 - **Tracking Error:** Primary measure of control accuracy (e.g., cart velocity for CartPole, average position error for aerial vehicles).
 - **Runtime (Inference Time):** Crucial for evaluating computational efficiency on deployment.
 - **Tracking Success:** Ratio of successfully tracked trajectories.
 - **Sample Efficiency:** Especially for adaptation tasks, measuring how many samples are needed to learn or adapt to new dynamics.
 - **Vision-Based Control:** Testing the flexibility to handle high-dimensional image inputs.
- **Implementation:** All experiments are conducted using PyTorch, leveraging its autograd capabilities for differentiable programming.

**F. Adaptation Strategy**
For adaptation tasks, a neural network $\Delta_\phi$ is trained as a residual on top of the known dynamics model $f$. This residual network learns the difference between the source dynamics $f$ and the target dynamics $f^*$, based on a small dataset of state-action-state triples from the target domain. The pre-trained APG controller is then fine-tuned alongside this learned residual.

This comprehensive methodology, combining novel APG training strategies with rigorous comparative evaluation across diverse tasks, aims to thoroughly assess the practical viability and advantages of APG for robotic control.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper presents compelling results that validate the efficacy of the proposed Analytic Policy Gradient (APG) method, especially when combined with curriculum learning and a concurrent receding-horizon training strategy.

**A. Performance Comparison (Table I)**
The most significant finding is APG's ability to bridge the performance gap between highly accurate MPC and computationally efficient RL:

- **APG vs. MPC:** APG achieves tracking performance "similar" to MPC across all three tasks (CartPole, Quadrotor, Fixed-wing). For instance, on the quadrotor, APG's tracking error is 0.05 ± 0.01m, very close to MPC's 0.03 ± 0.01m. Crucially, APG reduces runtime by "more than an order of magnitude" compared to MPC. For the quadrotor, APG runs in 0.17ms versus MPC's 5.2ms, making it far more suitable for resource-constrained systems.
- **APG vs. Reinforcement Learning (PPO, PETS):** APG consistently outperforms both model-free (PPO) and model-based (PETS) RL algorithms in terms of tracking error. For the quadrotor, APG's error (0.05m) is significantly lower than PPO's (0.36m). PETS, a model-based RL method, struggled severely, being "unstable" for the full quadrotor task and still showing higher error (0.24m) than APG for the fixed-wing drone. Like MPC, PETS also suffered from high computation times (e.g., 4919ms for quadrotor).
- **Overall Runtime and Accuracy:** The results clearly show that APG uniquely combines very low tracking error with very low inference runtime, distinguishing it as a superior learning-based approach for trajectory tracking.

**B. Validation of Receding-Horizon Training and Curriculum Learning (Table II & Figure 5)**

- **Impact of Horizon Length (T):** Experiments with varying horizon lengths (T) for the quadrotor task confirmed the importance of this parameter.
 - Too short a horizon (e.g., T=1 or T=3) results in poor performance, as the controller cannot plan far enough ahead.
 - An optimal horizon length (T=10 for the concurrent approach) yielded the best tracking error (0.05 ± 0.01m).
 - Too long a horizon (e.g., T=15 for concurrent) led to performance degradation, attributed to the increasing length of gradient chains and associated numerical instabilities.
- **Concurrent vs. Autoregressive:** The proposed **concurrent** training strategy significantly outperformed the **autoregressive** approach, especially for longer horizons. For T=10, concurrent achieved 0.05m error compared to autoregressive's 0.12m. The autoregressive approach with an LSTM (recurrent) also performed worse or failed for short horizons. This validates the proposed concurrent training as a key to stability.
- **Effectiveness of Curriculum Learning (Figure 5):** Training with the proposed curriculum learning strategy led to a "significant reduction of the tracking error" and a "stable learning process." In contrast, training without curriculum learning resulted in higher tracking errors, jumps in performance, and a tendency to get stuck in local minima, demonstrating its crucial role in stabilizing APG.

**C. Vision-Based Control (Table III)**
APG demonstrated its flexibility and sample efficiency for high-dimensional inputs:

- For the CartPole task, using only images as input, APG achieved a cart velocity of 0.15 ± 0.11 m/s, outperforming PPO (0.28 ± 0.23 m/s).
- Crucially, APG accomplished this with "a fraction of the sample budget" (1K samples) compared to PPO (230K samples), highlighting its superior sample efficiency when dealing with high-dimensional observations.
- Classical online optimization (MPC) was "unstable" or produced "poor solutions" when trying to perform control directly from image space, underscoring APG's advantage in this domain.

**D. Sample Efficiency in Adaptation Tasks (Table IV & Figure 6)**
APG showed remarkable sample efficiency in adapting to changes in system dynamics, which is critical for real-world deployment:

- When adapting to unknown velocity drag on quadrotor and fixed-wing models, APG (by training a residual dynamics model and fine-tuning the controller) required "orders of magnitudes fewer samples" than RL methods.
- For the quadrotor, APG recovered its initial performance with just 1K few-shot samples, while PPO still showed significantly higher error even with 150K samples.
- Similarly for the fixed-wing, APG adapted with 2K samples, whereas PPO required up to 150K samples to achieve comparable performance.
- This demonstrates that APG's model-aware nature allows for highly efficient adaptation to new environments, a key advantage over black-box RL.

In summary, the main findings highlight APG as a robust, accurate, and computationally efficient control method that surpasses current RL techniques and offers a compelling alternative to MPC, particularly for scenarios demanding fast inference or vision-based control, and efficient adaptation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work presented in "Training Efficient Controllers via Analytic Policy Gradient" holds significant implications for the field of robotic control, particularly for autonomous systems operating under computational constraints.

**A. Enabling High-Performance Control for Resource-Constrained Robots:**
The most immediate impact is the provision of a control method that combines the high accuracy of Model Predictive Control (MPC) with the low inference time of offline-trained policies. This "best of both worlds" solution is revolutionary for platforms like micro aerial vehicles (drones) and other embedded robotic systems where processing power, battery life, and payload capacity are severely limited. Previously, designers had to compromise between robust, accurate control (MPC) and fast, deployable solutions (RL). APG, as demonstrated, offers a pathway to achieve both, enabling more agile, precise, and autonomous operations in real-world scenarios.

**B. Advancing Analytic Policy Gradient (APG) towards Practicality:**
The paper makes crucial methodological contributions by addressing long-standing challenges in APG training. The introduction of the **concurrent receding-horizon training strategy** and the **curriculum learning scheme** directly tackles issues like vanishing/exploding gradients and susceptibility to local minima, which have historically limited APG's application to complex robotic systems. By stabilizing the training process, the authors render APG a much more robust and viable candidate for real-world control tasks. This work serves as a significant step in maturing APG from a promising theoretical concept to a practical tool for robotics engineers.

**C. Superiority over Existing Learning-Based Methods:**
The rigorous comparison unequivocally establishes APG's advantage over both model-free (PPO) and model-based (PETS) reinforcement learning algorithms in trajectory tracking performance, runtime efficiency, and critically, sample efficiency. This suggests that for tasks where a differentiable system model is available (even an approximate one), APG is a superior choice, offering a clear path to higher performance with less data and faster deployment than traditional RL.

**D. Opening Doors for Vision-Based and Complex Perception-Action Control:**
The successful demonstration of vision-based control for the CartPole task, coupled with APG's high sample efficiency, highlights its potential for more sophisticated perception-to-action control in robotics. Traditional control methods often struggle with high-dimensional sensor inputs like images, requiring extensive state estimation pipelines. APG's ability to directly learn from images with few samples paves the way for end-to-end learning for tasks where explicit state information is difficult or impossible to obtain, such as navigating cluttered environments or interacting with deformable objects.

**E. Facilitating Rapid Adaptation and Real-World Deployment:**
The demonstrated sample efficiency in adaptation scenarios (e.g., learning about unknown drag forces) is profoundly significant for real-robot deployment. Collecting vast amounts of data on physical robots is expensive, time-consuming, and potentially dangerous. APG's capacity to adapt to new dynamics with very few real-world samples means that a policy trained largely in simulation can be quickly and safely fine-tuned on a physical robot. This significantly reduces the sim-to-real gap and accelerates the development and deployment cycles for autonomous systems.

**F. Influencing Future Research Directions:**
The paper concludes by pointing to future research, acknowledging limitations in horizon length and the need for validation on real robots. However, by providing strong evidence for APG's capabilities in simulation with highly non-realistic references and learned dynamics, it motivates increased attention to gradient-based policies within the control community. This work could inspire further research into:
- Developing APG for longer-horizon planning and more complex sequential decision-making tasks.
- Integrating APG with robust estimation techniques for scenarios with unknown or partially known dynamics.
- Translating these simulation successes to real-world hardware, including addressing real-world disturbances and model inaccuracies.

In essence, this research provides compelling evidence that APG, when meticulously engineered, can be a transformative force in robotic control, offering an optimal trade-off between performance, efficiency, and flexibility that addresses many of the current limitations in autonomous robotics.
