# CANVAS: Commonsense-Aware Navigation System for Intuitive Human-Robot Interaction

> [!summary] Summary
> CANVAS enables robots to interpret abstract and noisy human navigation instructions by integrating multimodal inputs with a Vision-Language Model trained on commonsense knowledge. The system demonstrated superior performance in complex and unseen simulated environments, achieving up to a 67% success rate with misleading instructions and showing robust Sim2Real transfer with a 69% total success rate in real-world office navigation.

## Problem

- Robots struggle to interpret abstract, imprecise, or noisy human instructions due to a lack of commonsense understanding and reliance on explicit, detailed commands.
- Existing navigation systems, whether rule-based or deep learning-based, often fail in complex, unvisited, or ambiguous scenarios where human intent is implicit.
- Quantifying and defining explicit reward functions for subjective, commonsense-aware navigation behaviors is challenging, limiting the application of traditional reinforcement learning.

## Method

- Developed **CANVAS**, a ==Vision-Language Model (VLM)== based framework that processes robot's front-view images, a composite canvas map (initial sketch + hindsight trajectory), and language instructions.
- Trained the system using imitation learning on COMMAND, a comprehensive, human-annotated dataset specifically designed to include both precise and deliberately misleading instructions.
- Employed K-means clustering to discretize continuous waypoints into a classification problem for the VLM, simplifying the action space and enabling multimodal action distribution modeling.

## Results

- CANVAS significantly outperformed the rule-based ROS NavStack in complex simulated environments (e.g., orchard: **67%** SR vs. **0%** SR for NavStack with misleading instructions) and demonstrated strong generalization to unseen environments.
- Achieved consistently lower Instruction Violation Rates (IVR) compared to NavStack, demonstrating its capacity to learn and adhere to commonsense constraints (e.g., lane adherence, crosswalk usage) even with misleading inputs.
- Exhibited robust Sim2Real transfer capabilities, achieving a **69%** total success rate in real-world office navigation despite being trained exclusively on simulated data, and maintained real-time feasibility with 400ms inference latency for CANVAS-S.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating multimodal inputs (visual sketches, hindsight trajectory, and natural language) with a VLM allows robots to infer human intent and adhere to commonsense even with imperfect instructions.
> - Imitation learning with a diverse dataset, including deliberately misleading instructions and a focus on human demonstrations, is effective for training robots to handle real-world ambiguities and integrate commonsense reasoning.
> - Leveraging pre-trained VLM weights provides a robust foundation for learning generalizable driving dynamics, crucial for strong generalization to unseen environments and effective Sim2Real transfer.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robot navigation research has evolved from purely rule-based systems to more flexible neural network approaches. Historically, frameworks like ROS NavStack relied on predefined rules for navigation, which provided determinism but limited adaptability to complex or unforeseen situations. The advent of deep learning has introduced more adaptable methods, including visual navigation models (e.g., NoMaD, ViNT, GNM) that utilize images as goal representations. However, these often depend on topological graphs and first-person visual observations, making them sensitive to environmental changes and unable to navigate unvisited locations effectively.

Another significant area is Vision-Language Navigation (VLN), which integrates visual information with natural language instructions. While this enhances human-robot interaction by allowing verbal commands, the inherent ambiguity of natural language can limit precise control over detailed navigation routes. More recent developments, such as LIM2N, combine language and sketch trajectories to offer a richer, multimodal interface. However, these systems typically demand highly accurate and detailed instructions, making them vulnerable to small errors or missing information and thus less usable for non-expert users in real-world applications.

CANVAS positions itself within this landscape by addressing a specific gap: the interpretation of abstract, imprecise, or noisy human instructions by leveraging commonsense knowledge. Existing systems often struggle with the implicit assumptions humans make when giving directions. CANVAS aims to bridge this gap by integrating multimodal inputs (visual sketches and linguistic commands) with a vision-language model (VLM) that has acquired a versatile understanding of navigation dynamics through imitation learning. Unlike previous methods that require explicit, detailed instructions, CANVAS is designed to infer human intent and adhere to commonsense constraints, even when the input is imperfect. This approach aims to create a more intuitive and robust human-robot interaction paradigm for navigation, moving beyond literal interpretation to a shared understanding. The work contributes a novel framework and a comprehensive dataset designed to foster commonsense-aware navigation, thereby advancing the field's ability to handle the nuances of human guidance.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the observation that real-life robot navigation extends beyond mere destination arrival. It often involves optimizing movements to align with scenario-specific goals, which humans intuitively communicate using abstract and sometimes imprecise guidance. For example, an agricultural robot may need to maximize field coverage, or a delivery robot must adhere to road lanes and use crosswalks—goals not explicitly stated in a simple destination command.

The key objectives are detailed as follows:

- **Interpreting Abstract Human Instructions:** A central objective is to enable robots to interpret and execute high-level, abstract human guidance, such as verbal commands or rough sketches. Humans naturally assume a shared commonsense understanding, allowing them to provide instructions that lack explicit detail or may contain noise. Robots, without this commonsense, struggle to convert such vague guidance into actionable navigation plans. The research aims to equip robots with the ability to infer human intent from these inputs.
- **Integrating Commonsense Knowledge:** To bridge the gap between abstract instructions and concrete robot actions, the work seeks to integrate commonsense knowledge into robot navigation. Commonsense, in this context, refers to the general understanding humans use to make decisions, encompassing aspects like human desires, basic physics, and causality. Robots need to leverage this knowledge to flexibly adjust their paths, ensuring their navigation decisions align with human expectations and environmental constraints.
- **Addressing Challenges in Reward Definition:** In the domain of commonsense-aware navigation, quantifying successful navigation behaviors with explicit reward functions is challenging due to the subjective and implicit nature of commonsense. Therefore, a key objective is to employ imitation learning. This approach allows the robot to learn from human demonstrations, thereby comprehending the user's underlying intent, even when instructions are noisy or imprecise, without the need for manually engineered reward functions.
- **Developing a Comprehensive Training Dataset:** To facilitate the training of commonsense-aware navigation systems via imitation learning, a significant objective is the creation of a comprehensive and diverse dataset. This dataset would feature human-annotated navigation results in varied simulated environments, including both precise and deliberately misleading instructions, to robustly train models to handle real-world ambiguities.
- **Demonstrating Robustness and Generalization:** The research aims to demonstrate that a system trained with these principles can outperform existing rule-based systems, especially when faced with noisy or challenging conditions. Furthermore, it seeks to prove the system's ability to generalize its learned commonsense understanding to unseen environments and transfer effectively from simulated training to real-world deployment.

In summary, the motivation is to advance human-robot interaction by allowing robots to "understand" and act upon intuitive, human-like instructions, moving beyond rigid rule-following or strict reliance on perfectly precise input, ultimately making robots more adaptable and useful in complex real-world scenarios.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The CANVAS (Commonsense-Aware NaVigAtion System) framework is designed to enable robots to interpret abstract human instructions using multimodal inputs and commonsense reasoning. Its methodology encompasses a novel architecture, a data collection strategy, and a training paradigm.

#### 3.1. CANVAS Architecture

CANVAS employs a Vision-Language Model (VLM) to process various inputs and generate navigation actions.
- **Inputs:**
 - **Front View Image (Xf(t)):** Captured by the robot's camera at timestep *t*, providing immediate visual context.
 - **Canvas Map Image (Xc(t)):** This is a composite image that integrates the initial sketch instruction (S) provided by a human with the robot's hindsight trajectory (H(t)) up to timestep *t-1*. This allows the robot to contextualize its current position relative to the desired path and past movements.
 - **Language Instruction (L):** Textual commands or descriptions outlining overall goals and requirements.
- **Processing Pipeline:**
 - **Vision Encoder (gφ):** A vision encoder processes both the front view image (Xf(t)) and the canvas map image (Xc(t)) to extract visual features (Zf and Zc, respectively).
 - **Projector (pφ):** These visual features are then projected into the word embedding space by a projector, yielding a sequence of visual tokens (τv).
 - **Language Tokenization:** The language instruction (L) is processed to obtain a sequence of language tokens (τl).
 - **Large Language Model (fφ):** Both the visual tokens (τv) and language tokens (τl) are fed into a large language model. This model is responsible for integrating the multimodal information and leveraging embedded commonsense knowledge to predict the next navigation step.
- **Action Generation:** The VLM outputs a sequence of discrete waypoint tokens, specifically [w0, w1, w2, w3]. To manage the complexity of continuous action spaces, continuous waypoints are discretized into 128 distinct waypoint tokens using K-means clustering. This approach simplifies the action space into a classification problem, facilitating the modeling of multimodal action distributions.
- **Robot Control:** During inference, these discrete waypoint tokens are converted back into continuous waypoints. These continuous waypoints are then fed into a Proportional-Derivative (PD) controller, which translates them into linear and angular velocities (v, ω) that command the robot's actuators.
- **Hindsight Trajectory Update:** After each action, the robot's new position (p(t)) is appended to its hindsight trajectory, updating H(t+1). This updated trajectory is incorporated into the canvas map image (Xc(t+1)) for the next timestep, providing continuous feedback on the robot's progress relative to the initial sketch.

#### 3.2. COMMAND Dataset

CANVAS is trained using COMMAND (COMMonsense-Aware Navigation Dataset), a comprehensive dataset specifically designed for commonsense-aware robot navigation.
- **Environments:** The dataset features three simulated environments with distinct characteristics: an office (coffee delivery), a street (package delivery), and an orchard (agricultural spraying). These environments were designed using Unreal Engine and simulated in NVIDIA Isaac Sim.
- **Data Collection:**
 1. **Environment Setup:** Simulated environments are created, and occupancy maps are extracted.
 2. **Sketch Instruction Labeling:** Human annotators draw sketch trajectories on these canvas maps. To enhance robustness, two conditions are used:
 - **Precise:** Annotators trace efficient routes, closely following guidelines.
 - **Misleading:** Annotators deliberately introduce noise, such as drawing paths through walls or objects, to simulate real-world imperfections in human instructions.
 3. **Teleoperation:** Human experts teleoperate a virtual robot in NVIDIA Isaac Sim, following both the sketch and language instructions. During teleoperation, front-view images, canvas map images, and the human-demonstrated trajectory are recorded. The Frechet distance (FD) is used to quantify the discrepancy between the initial sketch and the actual human-demonstrated trajectory, indicating the level of noise.
- **Scale and Content:** COMMAND comprises 3,343 fully human-annotated navigation results. It includes 48 hours of driving data, covering a distance of 219 kilometers, thereby offering significant diversity and scope for imitation learning. Each datapoint contains a canvas map, sketch and language instructions, commonsense constraints (derived from the environment and language instructions), and human teleoperation records.

#### 3.3. Training Process

- **Imitation Learning:** CANVAS is trained using imitation learning, where the model learns to mimic expert human demonstrations. This approach is chosen because defining explicit reward functions for commonsense-aware navigation is challenging.
- **Loss Function:** Training involves minimizing the negative log-likelihood loss. The navigation task is reframed as a classification problem, where the model predicts the sequence of next waypoint tokens based on the current front view, canvas map, and language instruction. This discrete action space helps manage multimodal distributions and enhances stability and accuracy in complex navigation scenarios.
- **Model Variants:** Two main variations of CANVAS are evaluated:
 - **CANVAS-S:** Utilizes a modified Idefics2 8B architecture with a SigLIP-L vision encoder and Qwen2-0.5B text encoder, resulting in a smaller 0.7B parameter model for real-world deployment.
 - **CANVAS-L:** Retains the original Idefics2 8B architecture and its pre-trained weights.
- **Optimization:** Both models are trained using LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) with specific parameters (r=256, a=512, dropout rate=0.1), the AdamW optimizer with distinct learning rates for different components, a batch size of 32, and 5 training epochs.

This methodology combines a data-driven approach with a multimodal VLM to enable robots to understand and act on abstract human commands, integrating commonsense into navigation behaviors.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted aimed to evaluate CANVAS's ability to perform commonsense-aware navigation in simulated and real-world environments, handle noisy instructions, and assess the impact of VLM pre-training. Performance was primarily measured using Success Rate (SR), Collision Rate (CR), Trajectory Deviation Distance (TDD), and Instruction Violation Rate (IVR).

#### 4.1. Performance in Simulated Environments

- **Seen Environments:**
 - **Overall Performance:** In easier navigation scenarios such as the office and street (road) environments with precise instructions, CANVAS achieved success rates and collision rates comparable to the rule-based ROS NavStack. This indicated its ability to learn fundamental navigation behaviors from human demonstrations.
 - **Challenging Scenarios and Noisy Instructions:** In more complex environments like street (sidewalk) and especially the orchard, CANVAS significantly outperformed NavStack. With misleading instructions, NavStack often recorded a 0% success rate, indicating its inability to handle such ambiguities. For instance, in the orchard environment, NavStack had a 0% total success rate, whereas CANVAS-S achieved 67% and CANVAS-L achieved 64%.
 - **Commonsense Adherence:** Table IV demonstrated that CANVAS consistently achieved lower Instruction Violation Rates (IVR) compared to NavStack, even when presented with misleading instructions. This highlighted CANVAS's capacity to learn and adhere to commonsense constraints (e.g., lane adherence, crosswalk usage) from human demonstrations, which rule-based systems struggled with. For example, in the street (sidewalk) environment, NavStack had an IVR of 100% with misleading instructions, while CANVAS-S and CANVAS-L achieved 26% and 13% respectively.
- **Unseen Environments (Gallery):**
 - CANVAS demonstrated strong generalization capabilities in the unseen gallery environment. With precise instructions, CANVAS-L achieved a 100% SR. Even with misleading instructions, where NavStack completely failed (0% SR), CANVAS-L achieved a total SR of 67%, showing its robustness in novel settings.

#### 4.2. Performance in the Real-World Environment

- CANVAS was tested in a physical office environment that replicated one of the simulated training environments. Despite being trained exclusively on simulated data, CANVAS exhibited strong Sim2Real transfer capabilities.
- In the real-world office environment, CANVAS-S achieved a total success rate of 69%, with 77% SR for precise instructions and 60% SR for misleading instructions. CANVAS-L achieved a total success rate of 63%. This demonstrated the potential for deploying such learning-based systems in real-world applications without extensive real-world data collection.

#### 4.3. Ablation Study: Impact of VLM Pre-training

- An ablation study investigated the importance of leveraging pre-trained weights from the Vision-Language Model.
- The results, particularly in unseen simulated environments (Gallery) and real-world settings (Real - Office), indicated that pre-trained VLM weights were crucial for CANVAS's performance and generalization. For instance, in the unseen gallery environment, CANVAS-L (with pre-training) achieved a total SR of 67%, while a version without pre-training achieved 50%. This suggests that the knowledge embedded in pre-trained VLMs provides a robust foundation for learning generalizable driving dynamics.

#### 4.4. Additional Case Study: NavStack Failure Analysis

- Qualitative analysis of NavStack's failure cases revealed that 56% of failures were due to stumbling over small obstacles like rocks in the orchard environment. NavStack, being rule-based with limited perception, struggled to distinguish hazardous rocks from traversable terrain. Its LiDAR-based obstacle detection was prone to misclassification and sensitivity issues with small threshold adjustments.
- In contrast, CANVAS utilized visual inputs from its camera to detect unexpected obstacles and assessed their navigation risk based on learned experiences from human demonstrations, allowing it to navigate effectively in the challenging orchard environment where NavStack failed.

#### 4.5. Real-Time Navigation Feasibility

- Deployment on an NVIDIA AGX Orin, the robot's onboard computing unit, showed that CANVAS-S had an average inference latency of 400ms, and CANVAS-L had 800ms. These timings indicate that CANVAS is feasible for real-time navigation tasks without introducing substantial delays, supporting its practical application.

In summary, CANVAS consistently outperformed a strong rule-based baseline, demonstrated robustness to noisy instructions, exhibited effective generalization to unseen environments, and showed promising Sim2Real transfer capabilities, largely attributed to its imitation learning approach and the integration of commonsense via VLMs.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of CANVAS represents a contribution to the field of robotic navigation and human-robot interaction, particularly in addressing the complexities of real-world scenarios.

One of the primary significances is its **enhancement of intuitive human-robot interaction**. By enabling robots to interpret abstract, noisy, or vague multimodal instructions (sketches and natural language), CANVAS reduces the cognitive load on human users. Instead of requiring precise, detailed, or code-like commands, humans can communicate with robots more naturally, relying on their shared commonsense understanding. This paves the way for more accessible and user-friendly robotic systems for a broader range of individuals, including non-experts.

The framework's ability to **integrate commonsense reasoning into navigation** is a substantial impact. Current robotic systems often struggle with implicit human intent and contextual nuances. CANVAS demonstrates a method for robots to learn and leverage commonsense constraints and expectations from human demonstrations. This enables robots to make more human-aligned decisions, such as adhering to traffic rules or avoiding minor obstacles like rocks that rule-based systems might misinterpret, thereby increasing their reliability and safety in complex environments.

**Robustness to imperfect instructions** is another key significance. The COMMAND dataset, with its deliberate inclusion of "misleading" sketch instructions, trains CANVAS to be resilient to noise and imprecision. This robustness is critical for real-world deployment, where human input is rarely flawless. The superior performance of CANVAS over traditional rule-based systems in such scenarios highlights its practical applicability.

Furthermore, the demonstrated **strong generalization capabilities to unseen environments and effective Sim2Real transfer** indicate the scalability and practical potential of the learning approach. Training solely in simulation and achieving reliable performance in the real world is a significant step towards reducing the cost and time associated with real-world data collection, accelerating the development and deployment of intelligent robots.

Finally, the **open-sourcing of the CANVAS framework and the COMMAND dataset** offers a valuable resource to the research community. This contribution can accelerate further research in imitation learning, commonsense reasoning, and multimodal human-robot interaction within navigation contexts. Researchers can build upon this foundation to develop more sophisticated, intelligent, and adaptable robotic systems for diverse applications, including agriculture, logistics, and service robotics. The work moves robot navigation closer to a state where robots can truly "understand" and anticipate human needs, rather than merely executing literal commands.
