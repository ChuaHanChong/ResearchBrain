# A note on the motion representation and configuration update in time stepping schemes for the constrained rigid body

> [!summary] Summary
> This research systematically analyzes how the choice of configuration space impacts constraint preservation in numerical simulations of constrained rigid bodies. It demonstrates that modeling rigid body motion using the special Euclidean group (SE(3)) leads to machine-precision satisfaction of kinematic constraints for lower kinematic pairs, in contrast to the traditional direct product Lie group (SO(3) " R^3) which exhibits integration-scheme-dependent constraint violations.

## Problem

- Numerical integration of constrained multibody systems frequently results in violations of geometric constraints, necessitating various ad-hoc stabilization techniques.
- The common practice of representing a rigid body's configuration space as SO(3) ", R^3 geometrically misaligns with the inherent screw motions of rigid bodies, which are properly described by SE(3).
- Empirical observations suggested differing constraint satisfaction based on configuration space choice, but lacked a systematic theoretical explanation for a single constrained rigid body.

## Method

- The paper develops a theoretical framework that establishes conditions for exact satisfaction of kinematic constraints during numerical configuration updates, based on the ==Lie group structure== of the configuration space.
- It contrasts the behavior of two Lie groups for configuration space (==SO(3) ", R^3== and ==SE(3)==), explicitly deriving their ==Lie algebras==, ==exponential maps==, and ==kinematic reconstruction equations==.
- Numerical examples using a ==4th order Runge-Kutta method== on a single rigid body with various common kinematic joints validate the theoretical predictions regarding constraint preservation.

## Results

- For joints defining subgroups of SE(3) (e.g., spherical, revolute, cylindrical), the SE(3) formulation maintains constraint satisfaction to machine precision (errors around **10^-14**), while SO(3) ", R^3 shows errors scaling with integration accuracy (e.g., **10^-10** for RK4 with ",t = **10^-4**).
- Planar joints demonstrated exact constraint satisfaction for both SE(3) and SO(3) ", R^3 formulations due to their motion manifold being geometrically equivalent to a subgroup in both Lie groups.
- For higher kinematic pairs that do not define Lie subgroups (e.g., hook or pin-in-slot joints), neither formulation guarantees exact constraint satisfaction for all components, though SE(3) sometimes performs better for position constraints.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Kinematic constraints are exactly preserved in numerical integration steps if they restrict the body's motion to a subgroup of the chosen configuration space Lie group.
> - The Special Euclidean Group SE(3) naturally encompasses the kinematics of most lower kinematic pairs (e.g., spherical, revolute, prismatic joints) as its subgroups, ensuring geometric consistency.
> - The traditional SO(3) ", R^3 formulation generally fails to represent the kinematics of common lower pairs as its subgroups, leading to inherent and accumulating constraint violations during numerical integration.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a fundamental issue in the numerical simulation of multibody systems (MBS), particularly those involving holonomically constrained rigid bodies. Traditionally, MBS dynamics frequently employ an "absolute coordinate formulation," which models systems as differential-algebraic equations (DAEs). These DAEs, often of index 3, are typically reduced to an index 1 DAE and solved using numerical integration schemes designed for ordinary differential equations (ODEs). A known challenge with this approach is the numerical violation of geometric constraints, necessitating the development of various constraint stabilization methods (e.g., Baumgarte stabilization, projection methods).

A historical practice in MBS dynamics is the representation of a rigid body's configuration space (c-space) as the direct product Lie group SO(3) × R$^3$. This representation is characterized by the decoupled parameterization of rotations and translations. However, rigid body motions inherently constitute screw motions, which are properly described by the semidirect product Lie group SE(3) (the special Euclidean group). The latter ensures frame invariance of the motion equations, a property not guaranteed by SO(3) × R$^3$.

Recent developments in Lie group integration schemes for MBS have brought increased attention to the importance of the geometric model of rigid body motions. Observations have indicated that constraint satisfaction can vary significantly depending on whether SO(3) × R$^3$ or SE(3) is used as the c-space. This paper aims to provide a systematic, geometrically-grounded analysis of this phenomenon, specifically focusing on the impact of c-space choice on constraint preservation during numerical time integration of the index 1 DAE formulation. While previous work explored this for general MBS via simulations, this paper focuses on a single constrained rigid body to derive a concrete theoretical statement.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to systematically investigate how the choice of the configuration space (c-space) Lie group impacts the preservation of the constraint manifold during the numerical integration of a constrained rigid body's dynamics. Specifically, the paper seeks to answer the question: "How does the choice of c-space Lie group affect the preservation of the constraint manifold h$^{-1}$(0) when numerically integrating the rigid body model (4a,4b)?"

The motivation for this investigation stems from several observations and recognized issues in multibody dynamics:
- **Constraint Violation in Numerical Integration:** Standard numerical integration schemes for DAEs (transformed from index 3 to index 1) frequently lead to violations of geometric constraints. Existing solutions typically aim to minimize or correct these violations rather than prevent them inherently.
- **Geometric Inconsistency of c-space Choice:** The prevalent use of SO(3) × R$^3$ as the c-space in MBS dynamics, implying decoupled rotations and translations, contradicts the geometric reality that rigid body motions are screw motions, correctly described by SE(3). SE(3) is also the only c-space that ensures frame invariance for motion equations.
- **Observed Differences in Constraint Satisfaction:** Empirical evidence from numerical simulations has suggested that the choice between SO(3) × R$^3$ and SE(3) significantly affects how well constraints are satisfied. This observation lacked a comprehensive theoretical explanation.
- **Relevance for Multibody Systems:** While the direct application is to a single constrained rigid body, the insights gained are intended to inform and potentially improve the modeling and simulation of general MBS, especially those involving common kinematic joints (lower pairs). The paper explicitly notes that lower kinematic pairs inherently constrain motion to specific subgroups of SE(3).
- **Advancing Lie Group Integration:** The findings are also relevant to Lie group integration methods, which implicitly or explicitly rely on the underlying Lie group structure of the c-space.

By providing a geometric perspective, the paper aims to establish a theoretical basis for understanding and mitigating constraint violations by selecting an appropriate c-space Lie group, thereby contributing to more accurate and robust numerical simulations of constrained rigid body systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this paper combines theoretical geometric analysis with numerical examples to demonstrate the effect of c-space choice on constraint satisfaction.

#### 3.1. Mathematical Modeling of Constrained Rigid Body Dynamics

The paper begins by formulating the dynamics of a holonomically constrained rigid body.
- **Free Rigid Body Dynamics:** The motion is represented as a curve C(t) in a 6-dimensional Lie group G (the c-space). Velocity is defined as a left-invariant vector field V = C$^{-1}\dot{C} \in \mathfrak{g}$, where $\mathfrak{g}$ is the Lie algebra. The dynamics are governed by Euler-Poincaré equations, relating the momentum $\Pi$ and velocity V.
- **Constrained Dynamics:** When subjected to $m$ geometric constraints h(C) = 0, the equations of motion (EOM) become an index 3 DAE system comprising Euler-Poincaré equations with Lagrange multipliers, kinematic reconstruction equations ($\dot{C} = CV$), and the holonomic constraints h(C) = 0.
- **Index 1 DAE and ODE Transformation:** Following standard MBS practice, this system is transformed into an index 1 DAE by adding acceleration constraints. This results in a system of the form:
 $\begin{pmatrix} M & J^T \\ J & 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \dot{V} \\ \lambda \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \text{ad}_V^T \Pi + W \\ \eta \end{pmatrix}$
 Solving this for $\dot{V}$ yields an ODE for the velocity $\dot{V} = F(X, V)$, which is then coupled with the kinematic reconstruction equation $\dot{X} = \text{dexp}_{-X}^{-1}(V)$ to form an ODE system on the vector space R$^6$ × R$^6$. This system (4a, 4b) is the target for numerical integration.

#### 3.2. Configuration Space Choices

Two Lie groups are explicitly considered as c-spaces:
- **Direct Product Lie Group SO(3) × R$^3$:**
 - Historically used in MBS, implies decoupled rotations and translations.
 - Group multiplication: $(R_1, r_1) \cdot (R_2, r_2) = (R_1 R_2, r_1 + r_2)$.
 - Lie algebra $\mathfrak{so}(3) \times \text{R}^3$, with elements $X = (\xi, r)$, and Lie bracket $[X_1, X_2] = (\xi_1 \times \xi_2, 0)$.
 - Exponential map: $\exp(\xi, r) = (\exp \hat{\xi}, r)$.
 - Velocity $V_m = (\omega_b, \dot{r})$ (mixed velocity, body-fixed angular, spatial translational).
 - Results in decoupled Newton-Euler equations (12) when RFR is at COM.
- **Special Euclidean Group SE(3):**
 - The geometrically proper c-space for rigid body motions (screw motions).
 - Group multiplication: $(R_2, r_2) \cdot (R_1, r_1) = (R_2 R_1, r_2 + R_2 r_1)$.
 - Lie algebra $\mathfrak{se}(3) = \mathfrak{so}(3) \rtimes \text{R}^3$, with elements $X = (\xi, \eta)$, and Lie bracket $[X_1, X_2] = (\xi_1 \times \xi_2, \xi_1 \times \eta_2 - \xi_2 \times \eta_1)$.
 - Exponential map: $\exp(\xi, \eta) = (\exp \hat{\xi}, \text{dexp}_{\xi} \eta)$.
 - Velocity $V_b = (\omega_b, v_b)$ (body-fixed velocity screw, with $v_b = R^T \dot{r}$).
 - Results in coupled body-fixed Newton-Euler equations (17).
For both groups, the explicit forms of the $\text{dexp}$ and $\text{dexp}^{-1}$ mappings are provided, which are crucial for the kinematic reconstruction equation (4b).

#### 3.3. Theoretical Analysis of Constraint Satisfaction

The core of the theoretical argument is presented in Theorem 1 and Corollary 1.
- **Assumptions for Theorem 1:**
 1. The configuration update increment $\Phi^{(i)}$ (from $X^{(i-1)}$ to $X^{(i)}$) is determined as a linear combination of nested Lie brackets of velocity samples $V^{(j)}$ (Assumption 1). This is a general property of many numerical integration schemes when the $\text{dexp}^{-1}$ mapping is expanded in a series.
 2. The velocity $V^{(j)}$ satisfies the kinematic constraints at all intermediate time steps (Assumption 2).
- **Theorem 1:** States that kinematic motion constraints are satisfied by a configuration update step (as defined by Assumption 1 and 2) if the constraints restrict the body's motion to a subgroup of its c-space Lie group. The proof highlights that if the velocities belong to a Lie subalgebra corresponding to a subgroup, then the Lie brackets formed by these velocities also remain within that subalgebra, ensuring the configuration increment belongs to the subgroup.
- **Subgroup Analysis and Corollary 1:** The paper analyzes the subgroups of SE(3) and SO(3) × R$^3$.
 - SE(3) subgroups (Table 1) correspond directly to lower kinematic pairs (e.g., prismatic, revolute, spherical, cylindrical, planar joints) even when the RFR is not at the center of rotation/translation.
 - SO(3) × R$^3$ subgroups (Table 2) represent decoupled rotations about the RFR origin and translations. They generally do *not* capture the kinematics of lower pairs unless the RFR origin coincides with the joint's center of rotation/translation.
 - Corollary 1 concludes that SE(3) updates satisfy constraints imposed by lower pair joints (and general SE(3) subgroups). SO(3) × R$^3$ updates only satisfy constraints that are, as manifolds, identical to subgroups of SO(3) × R$^3$ (e.g., planar motions, Schöenflies motions, pure translations).

#### 3.4. Numerical Examples

To validate the theoretical findings, several numerical examples are presented using a single constrained rigid body (a rectangular aluminum solid) connected to an inertial frame by various joints.
- **Setup:** A 4th order Runge-Kutta (RK4) method is used with different step sizes ($\Delta t = 10^{-2}, 10^{-3}, 10^{-4}$s) to integrate the index 1 DAE (system 18).
- **Joint Types:**
 - **Spherical Joint (Heavy Top):** Constrains motion to SO(3) $\subset$ SE(3). Predicted: SE(3) exact, SO(3) × R$^3$ error. (Confirmed in Fig. 2)
 - **Revolute Joint:** Constrains motion to SO(2) $\subset$ SE(3). Predicted: SE(3) exact, SO(3) × R$^3$ error. (Confirmed in Fig. 3)
 - **Cylindrical Joint:** Constrains motion to SO(2) × R $\subset$ SE(3). Predicted: SE(3) exact, SO(3) × R$^3$ error. (Confirmed, similar to revolute)
 - **Planar Joint:** Constrains motion to SE(2) $\subset$ SE(3). Predicted: Both SE(3) and SO(3) × R$^3$ exact due to manifold equivalence. (Confirmed)
 - **Hook Joint:** Does not define a subgroup of SE(3) or SO(3) × R$^3$. Predicted: Neither exact for rotation constraints. SE(3) might satisfy position constraints due to "completion group" being SO(3). (Confirmed in Fig. 5, 6)
 - **Pin-in-Slot Joint:** Does not define a subgroup. Predicted: Neither exact. (Confirmed in Fig. 7)
- **Error Measurement:** Position errors ($\epsilon = \|h\|$) are plotted against time for different step sizes.

The numerical results consistently support the theoretical conclusions, demonstrating that for joints defining SE(3) subgroups, the SE(3) formulation maintains constraint satisfaction to machine precision, whereas the SO(3) × R$^3$ formulation exhibits errors scaling with the integration scheme's accuracy and step size.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper establishes a theoretical framework and provides numerical evidence to clarify the impact of c-space choice on constraint satisfaction in numerical time integration of constrained rigid body dynamics.

1. **Theoretical Condition for Exact Constraint Satisfaction:** The central theoretical finding is Theorem 1, which states that kinematic motion constraints imposed on a rigid body are *exactly satisfied* by a numerical configuration update step if these constraints restrict the body's motion to a *subgroup* of its chosen c-space Lie group. This satisfaction is independent of the order or accuracy of the integration scheme. The key mechanism identified is that if velocities belong to a Lie subalgebra corresponding to a subgroup, then increments formed by Lie brackets of these velocities will also remain within that subalgebra, thereby ensuring the updated configuration belongs to the subgroup.

2. **SE(3) as the Appropriate c-space:** Based on the subgroup analysis and Corollary 1, the paper concludes that SE(3) is the appropriate c-space for modeling constrained rigid bodies, particularly when the constraints represent common lower kinematic pairs. The reason is that the kinematics of lower kinematic pairs (such as revolute, prismatic, spherical, cylindrical, and planar joints) naturally define subgroups of SE(3).
 - For example, a spherical joint restricts motion to the SO(3) subgroup of SE(3), and a revolute joint restricts motion to an SO(2) subgroup of SE(3).

3. **Limitations of SO(3) × R$^3$:** The traditional SO(3) × R$^3$ c-space, which treats rotations and translations as decoupled, is generally insufficient for exact constraint satisfaction for most lower kinematic pairs. Its subgroups primarily represent rotations about the body-fixed reference frame (RFR) origin and decoupled translations. Consequently, if a joint's constraint defines an SE(3) subgroup that does not coincide with an SO(3) × R$^3$ subgroup (e.g., a revolute joint where the axis does not pass through the RFR origin), the SO(3) × R$^3$ formulation will exhibit constraint violations proportional to the numerical integration scheme's accuracy and step size.

4. **Specific Cases of Equivalence:** The paper identifies specific scenarios where the SO(3) × R$^3$ formulation yields equally accurate constraint satisfaction as SE(3). These occur when the constrained motion's manifold is geometrically identical to a subgroup of SO(3) × R$^3$. Examples include pure translational motions, planar motions (SE(2) $\subset$ SE(3) is manifold-identical to SO$_0$(2) $\times$ R$^2$ $\subset$ SO(3) $\times$ R$^3$), and Schönflies motions (SO(2) $\rtimes$ R$^3$ $\subset$ SE(3) is manifold-identical to SO$_0$(2) $\times$ R$^3$ $\subset$ SO(3) $\times$ R$^3$).

5. **Numerical Validation:** The numerical simulations for various joint types consistently support these theoretical findings:
 - For spherical, revolute, and cylindrical joints, the SE(3) formulation maintains constraint satisfaction to machine precision, while the SO(3) × R$^3$ formulation shows errors of the order of the integration scheme's accuracy (e.g., $10^{-10}$ for RK4 with $\Delta t = 10^{-4}$ vs. $10^{-14}$ for SE(3)).
 - For the planar joint, both formulations achieve exact constraint satisfaction, as predicted.
 - For higher kinematic pairs (e.g., hook joint, pin-in-slot joint) that do not define subgroups of either SE(3) or SO(3) × R$^3$, neither formulation guarantees exact constraint satisfaction for all constraint components. However, for the hook joint, the SE(3) formulation still managed to satisfy position constraints to machine precision, a behavior explained by the "completion group" of the constrained velocities being SO(3), which implies points on the body move on spheres. This was not the case for SO(3) × R$^3$ position constraints.

6. **Practical Implication:** The practical implication of using SE(3) as c-space is the necessary adoption of screw coordinates X = ($\xi$, $\eta$) and corresponding coefficient matrices in the kinematic equations, which represents a shift from current common MBS modeling practices.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research provides a significant contribution to the field of multibody dynamics and numerical simulation by offering a rigorous geometric explanation for previously observed numerical phenomena. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Enhanced Understanding of Constraint Preservation:** The paper provides a clear theoretical condition (Theorem 1) under which geometric constraints are *exactly satisfied* during numerical integration, rather than merely being stabilized or corrected. This shifts the focus from ad-hoc error reduction to fundamental geometric consistency.

2. **Validation for SE(3) as the Proper c-space:** The work strongly reinforces the argument for using SE(3) as the default geometric model for rigid bodies in MBS, especially when constrained by lower kinematic pairs. It demonstrates that SE(3) inherently aligns with the physical reality of these constraints, leading to superior numerical performance in terms of constraint satisfaction. This finding supports the growing interest in Lie group integrators and geometrically exact formulations.

3. **Improved Numerical Accuracy and Robustness:** By showing that SE(3) can lead to machine-precision constraint satisfaction for a wide range of common joints, the research suggests a path towards more accurate and robust MBS simulations. If the underlying geometric model is correct, the need for external constraint stabilization methods, which can introduce artificial forces or reduce overall simulation efficiency, may be reduced or eliminated for certain classes of problems.

4. **Guidance for MBS Modeling Practices:** The paper highlights a fundamental discrepancy between historical MBS modeling practices (using SO(3) × R$^3$) and the geometric reality of rigid body motion. It implies that a re-evaluation of how rigid body configurations and motions are parameterized in MBS frameworks is warranted, advocating for the use of screw coordinates. This could influence the development of future MBS software and algorithms.

5. **Foundation for Future Research:** While the current study focuses on a single rigid body constrained to an inertial frame, it explicitly states this as a foundational step. The results lay the groundwork for future research into extending these findings to general multibody systems, where interconnected bodies impose complex constraints. Understanding the single-body case is crucial before addressing the complexities of multi-body interactions.

6. **Relevance to Lie Group Integration Schemes:** The findings also bolster the theoretical underpinnings for Lie group integration methods, indicating that their design principles align with ensuring geometric properties, including constraint satisfaction, when the correct c-space is chosen.

In summary, this paper contributes to a more principled approach to numerical modeling in multibody dynamics, moving beyond empirical observations to a geometrically informed understanding that can lead to more accurate, stable, and theoretically consistent simulations.
