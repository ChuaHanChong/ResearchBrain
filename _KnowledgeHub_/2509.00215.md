# First Order Model-Based RL through Decoupled Backpropagation

> [!summary] Summary
> DMO introduces a first-order gradient reinforcement learning method that decouples trajectory generation using a high-fidelity simulator from gradient computation via a learned differentiable model, effectively mitigating compounding prediction errors. This approach achieves over tenfold higher sample efficiency and up to a 20% reduction in wall-clock training time compared to model-free baselines, demonstrating robust sim-to-real transfer on a quadrupedal robot.

## Problem

- Model-free deep reinforcement learning algorithms exhibit considerable sample inefficiency, requiring vast numbers of environment interactions.
- Developing fully differentiable simulators for First-order Gradient RL (FoG-RL) is challenging, especially for complex contact dynamics and multi-physics interactions.
- Traditional First-order Gradient Model-Based RL (FoG-MBRL) methods suffer from compounding prediction errors as learned dynamics models generate entire trajectories, leading to inaccurate policy gradients.

## Method

- **DMO** (==Decoupled forward-backward Model-based policy Optimization==) uses a high-fidelity simulator for generating accurate trajectories (forward pass).
- A learned, ==differentiable dynamics model== is employed to approximate partial derivatives for efficient gradient computation (backward pass).
- Policy gradients are calculated using the learned model's derivatives, evaluated at the true states and actions obtained from the high-fidelity simulator.

## Results

- DMO achieves over **10x** higher sample efficiency than model-free baselines like PPO, converging with fewer than **4 million** samples.
- The method demonstrates up to a **20%** reduction in wall-clock training time across various environments compared to PPO and SAC.
- Policies trained with DMO successfully transferred to a physical Unitree Go2 quadruped robot for both quadrupedal walking and bipedal locomotion tasks, highlighting robust sim-to-real transfer.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decoupling trajectory generation from gradient computation using different models significantly mitigates compounding prediction errors, improving learning stability and performance.
> - Leveraging the realism of high-fidelity simulators for forward passes and the differentiability of learned models for backward passes combines the strengths of both approaches.
> - This method provides a practical way to achieve analytical policy gradients without the need for a fully differentiable high-fidelity simulator, making FoG-RL more accessible.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has demonstrated capabilities in robotics for tasks such as agile locomotion and dexterous manipulation. However, many successful deep RL applications, particularly those utilizing model-free algorithms like DDPG, SAC, or PPO, exhibit considerable sample inefficiency. This inefficiency often necessitates extensive simulation throughput, frequently leveraging GPU-accelerated environments for parallel rollouts.

A developing area of interest involves differentiable simulators, which permit the direct computation of policy gradients through the physics engine. These First-order Gradient RL (FoG-RL) methods generally lead to faster learning and improved numerical stability due to more informative updates. Nevertheless, developing fully differentiable simulators for complex environments is challenging due to the piecewise differentiability of contact dynamics and the significant effort required for gradient implementation.

Model-Based RL (MBRL) offers an alternative by learning a differentiable model of the environment dynamics from data. This learned model enables fast, GPU-based rollouts and first-order optimization. Recent FoG-MBRL methods have shown superior sample efficiency compared to model-free approaches. However, a significant limitation of these methods is the accumulation of prediction errors during training rollouts generated solely by the learned dynamics model, which can degrade policy performance and efficiency. Furthermore, these methods often do not fully leverage the realism and physical principles embedded in high-quality robot simulators.

Prior research, such as PILCO and SVG(∞), explored computing policy gradients using derivatives of learned models along *real* trajectories rather than *predicted* ones, thereby avoiding prediction error accumulation. This concept of decoupling trajectory generation from gradient computation, despite its theoretical benefits, has largely been under-explored in subsequent research.

This work introduces an approach that re-examines and integrates this decoupling principle into modern FoG-MBRL. It aims to combine the benefits of high-fidelity simulators for accurate trajectory generation with the analytical gradient advantages of learned differentiable models, addressing the limitations of both direct differentiable simulation and traditional learned-model-based trajectory unrolling.

### 2. Key objectives and motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an efficient and stable first-order gradient reinforcement learning method, termed Decoupled forward-backward Model-based policy Optimization (DMO), by mitigating the compounding prediction errors typically observed in Model-Based RL.

The motivations driving this research are detailed as follows:

- **Addressing Sample Inefficiency in Deep RL:** Model-free RL algorithms often require vast numbers of samples, which translates to extensive training times, even with highly parallelized simulation environments. The researchers aim to improve the sample efficiency of RL to make it more feasible for complex robotics applications, particularly as the cost of interaction with more advanced simulators or real robots increases.
- **Overcoming Limitations of Differentiable Simulators:** While direct gradient computation through differentiable simulators offers performance benefits, their development is complex, time-consuming, and prone to errors, especially in environments with intricate contact dynamics or multi-physics interactions. The goal is to leverage the advantages of gradient information without requiring the explicit implementation of gradients for the entire high-fidelity simulator.
- **Mitigating Compounding Prediction Errors in FoG-MBRL:** Traditional First-order Gradient Model-Based RL methods generate entire trajectories using a learned dynamics model. Errors in this learned model accumulate over the rollout horizon, leading to inaccurate policy gradients and degraded learning performance. A key motivation is to devise a mechanism that can compute policy gradients analytically while circumventing these compounding errors.
- **Revisiting Decoupled Gradient Evaluation:** The concept of using real environment interactions for forward passes and a learned model for backward gradient computation was previously explored but not widely adopted or systematically evaluated within modern FoG-MBRL frameworks. This work is motivated to revisit this decoupling paradigm and empirically demonstrate its benefits in enhancing learning efficiency and stability.
- **Enhancing Generality and Practicality for Robotics:** The research aims to develop a method that is broadly applicable to existing FoG-based RL algorithms and can facilitate robust sim-to-real transfer. This includes demonstrating its effectiveness across a range of control benchmarks, including challenging tasks like quadrupedal and bipedal locomotion on a physical robot.
- **Improving Wall-Clock Training Time:** Beyond sample efficiency, the researchers also seek to improve the overall wall-clock time required for training, a practical metric that often poses a challenge for model-based methods due to the overhead of model learning and gradient computation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of DMO lies in **decoupling trajectory generation from gradient computation**. Instead of relying solely on a learned model for both forward simulation and backward gradient calculation, DMO uses a high-fidelity simulator for generating trajectories (forward pass) and a learned differentiable dynamics model for approximating gradients (backward pass).

**A. Model Learning:**
A differentiable dynamics model, $\hat{f}_\phi$, is learned concurrently with policy and critic training. This model is parameterized as a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) and is trained using samples collected from the high-fidelity simulator, stored in a replay buffer. The model learns to predict the next state given a current state and action, modeling the transition as a Gaussian distribution $p_\phi(s_{t+1} | s_t, a_t) = N(\mu_\phi(s_t, a_t), \Sigma_\phi(s_t, a_t))$. It is optimized via a maximum likelihood objective $L_{\hat{f}}(\phi) = E_{(s,a,s') \sim B}[p_\phi(s' | s, a)]$.

**B. Critic Learning (for DMO-SHAC and DMO-SAPO):**
For tasks requiring longer horizons or sparse rewards, DMO incorporates a learned value function approximator, $V^{\pi_\theta}_\psi$. This critic is trained using *real samples* from the high-fidelity simulator, which is a departure from traditional MBRL where the critic might be trained on samples generated by the learned model. The critic estimates the value of the current policy $\pi_\theta$ to provide a bootstrap for future returns, effectively shortening the rollout horizon for policy optimization.
- **DMO-SHAC** learns $V^{\pi_\theta}_\psi(s_i) \approx E_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta, f} [\sum_{t=i}^\infty \gamma^{t-i} r(s_t, a_t)]$.
- **DMO-SAPO** incorporates maximum entropy regularization, learning $V^{\pi_\theta}_\psi(s_i) \approx E_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta, f} [\sum_{t=i}^\infty \gamma^{t-i} (r(s_t, a_t) + \alpha H_\pi[a_t|s_t])]$.
The critic is optimized with SGD using a loss function based on the squared difference between the predicted value and a target value derived from actual rewards and bootstrapped future values.

**C. Actor Learning:**
The actor $\pi_\theta$ is also parameterized as an MLP, typically outputting a Gaussian distribution (stochastic policy for exploration). The actor is optimized using gradient descent on a batch of rollouts.
- **Forward Pass:** During the forward pass of the optimization, the *high-fidelity simulator* $f$ is used to compute the true next state $s_{t+1} = f(s_t, a_t)$. This is the key difference from previous FoG-MBRL methods that would use $\hat{f}_\phi$ for forward predictions.
- **Backward Pass:** During the backward pass, the *learned dynamics model* $\hat{f}_\phi$ is used to approximate the partial derivatives of the true dynamics, i.e., $\frac{\partial \hat{f}_\phi(s,a)}{\partial s}$ and $\frac{\partial \hat{f}_\phi(s,a)}{\partial a}$. Crucially, these derivatives are evaluated at the *true states* $(s_t, a_t)$ obtained from the simulator, not at states predicted by the learned model. This ensures that the gradient computation benefits from the learned model's differentiability while being anchored to accurate state transitions.
- **Policy Optimization Objectives:**
 - **DMO-BPTT:** Optimizes $\sum_{h=1}^{H-1} r(s_h, a_h)$, directly propagating returns through the dynamics model without a value function, suitable for dense rewards or short horizons.
 - **DMO-SHAC:** Optimizes $\sum_{h=1}^{H-1} \gamma^h r(s_h, a_h) + \gamma^H V^{\pi_\theta}_\psi(s_H)$.
 - **DMO-SAPO:** Optimizes $\sum_{h=1}^{H-1} \gamma^h (r(s_h, a_h) + \alpha H_\pi[a_t|s_t]) + \gamma^H V^{\pi_\theta}_\psi(s_H)$, incorporating entropy regularization.
- **Implementation:** The reparameterization trick is used for sampling from stochastic policies to allow gradient flow. PyTorch's automatic differentiation framework is leveraged, with a custom `GradientSwapingFunction` to efficiently manage the decoupled gradient computation without manual graph manipulation.

**D. Experimental Setup:**
- **Simulation Environments:** Experiments are primarily conducted on six DFlex differentiable simulator environments (Ant, Hopper, Cheetah, Humanoid, SNUHumanoid, AllegroHand). These environments offer GPU-accelerated simulation and reward functions compatible with FoG methods.
- **Real Robot Experiments:** DMO policies are deployed on a Unitree Go2 quadruped robot for velocity-commanded walking and bipedal locomotion. For these tasks, the non-differentiable IsaacGym simulator is used, with domain randomization incorporated to facilitate sim-to-real transfer.
- **Baselines:** Performance is compared against PPO and SAC (model-free RL) and MAAC (a modernized FoG-MBRL method).

### 4. Main findings and results

The research presents several findings regarding the performance and characteristics of DMO:

- **Sample and Time Efficiency:**
 - DMO demonstrates significant sample efficiency, achieving asymptotic convergence with fewer than 4 million samples, which is over ten times less than PPO.
 - Even when PPO and SAC are trained with substantially more samples (160M for PPO, 40M for SAC), DMO, utilizing only 4M samples, maintains superior sample efficiency and often surpasses the asymptotic performance of these model-free methods.
 - DMO also exhibits improved wall-clock training time, achieving up to a 20% reduction across environments compared to PPO and SAC. This is notable as prior MBRL methods often incurred computational overhead that offset their sample efficiency gains.
 - On DFlex environments, DMO achieves performance competitive with SHAC/SAPO, which utilize true simulator gradients, despite DMO relying on learned approximations.

- **Go2 Quadrupedal Locomotion:**
 - For the Go2 walking task, a densely informative reward function allowed effective training with DMO-BPTT, negating the need for a value function.
 - A surprising finding was that sample efficiency improved as the batch size for policy updates decreased, peaking at a batch size of 1. This suggests DMO can enable stable "no-batch learning," a typically challenging setting for RL algorithms.

- **Real Robot Deployment:**
 - Policies trained with DMO were successfully transferred and deployed on the physical Unitree Go2 quadruped robot for both quadrupedal walking and challenging bipedal locomotion tasks. This demonstrates the robustness and practical transferability of DMO-optimized policies. DMO-SAPO was particularly effective for the exploration-heavy bipedal task due to its enhancements.

- **Ablation Study on Decoupling Effect:**
 - An ablation comparing DMO to a variant where the learned model was also used for forward trajectory unrolling ("Model-Based Forward") demonstrated the substantial benefit of decoupling. DMO achieved nearly double the asymptotic performance on average, highlighting the critical role of using the high-fidelity simulator for accurate trajectory generation.

- **Ablative Analysis of Gradients:**
 - Comparing the cosine similarity of policy gradients: gradients computed by DMO showed higher cosine similarity with gradients derived from the fully differentiable simulator (DFlex) than gradients computed using trajectories predicted solely by the learned model. This empirical evidence indicates that decoupled gradients lead to more precise policy updates by grounding the gradient calculation in accurate state transitions.

### 5. Significance and potential impact

DMO represents a contribution to the field of reinforcement learning for robotics, addressing several key challenges and offering practical benefits.

- **Enhanced Learning Efficiency:** By combining the strengths of high-fidelity simulators for forward dynamics with the analytical gradient capabilities of learned models, DMO significantly improves sample efficiency, reducing the number of environment interactions required for convergence by an order of magnitude compared to strong model-free baselines like PPO. This directly translates to reduced training costs and faster development cycles for complex robotic behaviors.
- **Improved Wall-Clock Training Time:** Beyond sample efficiency, DMO also demonstrates superior wall-clock training time, overcoming a common limitation of many model-based RL approaches that can incur significant computational overhead. This makes DMO a more practical solution for real-world applications where rapid iteration is crucial.
- **Mitigation of Compounding Prediction Errors:** The decoupled approach effectively circumvents the issue of compounding prediction errors that plague traditional FoG-MBRL methods. By leveraging a high-fidelity simulator for trajectory generation, DMO ensures that policy updates are based on accurate state sequences, leading to more stable and optimal learning.
- **Generality and Adaptability:** DMO's framework is shown to be applicable across different FoG-based RL algorithms (DMO-BPTT, DMO-SHAC, DMO-SAPO), indicating its potential for broader integration into existing and future gradient-based methods.
- **Robust Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The successful deployment of DMO-trained policies on a physical quadruped robot for both walking and bipedal tasks underscores the robustness and transferability of the learned behaviors. This is a critical step towards deploying advanced RL policies in real-world robotic systems.
- **Implications for Resource-Constrained Learning:** The demonstrated ability of DMO to achieve high performance with fewer samples and faster training times is particularly impactful for scenarios with high simulator evaluation costs, such as future foundation world models or direct learning on real robots. The finding of stable "no-batch learning" for certain tasks could further extend its applicability to highly resource-constrained settings.

**Potential Impact:** DMO has the potential to accelerate the development and deployment of complex robotic skills by making deep RL more accessible and efficient. Its ability to provide robust policies transferable to real hardware could foster new applications in areas like agile locomotion, manipulation, and human-robot interaction where high-fidelity simulation is available but fully differentiable simulators are not practical.

**Limitations and Future Directions:** The paper acknowledges two primary limitations. First, DMO requires differentiable reward functions, often necessitating redesign for compatibility with FoG methods. Second, the current implementation uses a simplistic MLP world model, limiting its applicability to complex sensor inputs like images or point clouds. Future work could focus on integrating more sophisticated world models capable of handling high-dimensional observations, further expanding DMO's capabilities for complex real-world tasks.
