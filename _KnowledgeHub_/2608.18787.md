# Dream2Reward: Transition-Alignment Reward Models from Positive Demonstrations for Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Dream2Reward learns a language-conditioned successful latent transition field from positive-only demonstrations via flow matching, then scores executed transitions by signed cosine (direction) and symmetric log-ratio (magnitude) agreement against the predicted successful displacement. This causal, dense reward beats progress-based baselines on failure separation and real-robot offline/online policy learning.

## Problem

- Progress-based rewards order observations along successful trajectories but stay high or ambiguous once execution departs from those trajectories, e.g. dropped or misplaced objects.
- State similarity or progress cannot reveal whether the transition producing an observation followed correct task dynamics, missing wrong-direction, stalled, or overshooting motion.
- Prior demonstration-based reward methods mainly exploit trajectory ordering or need relabeling, rewinding, failure annotations, or synthetic negatives rather than local transition structure.

## Method

- **Successful latent transition field**: a conditional ==flow-matching== model predicts the successful endpoint latent from visual history and language goal, trained only on positive demonstrations across multiple horizons.
- ==Transition-alignment reward==: combines signed cosine similarity (angular alignment) and a symmetric log-ratio magnitude score between predicted and observed latent displacements, multiplied with a magnitude-shortfall penalty and clamped.
- ==Causal reward normalization== pipeline: frozen reward stream is standardized with fixed pre-training statistics, then used directly for online ==SAC== or as offline ==IQL==-style critic supervision via ==Q-Guided Flow==.

## Results

- On a 400-trajectory real-robot scoring set, Dream2Reward reaches **0.95** failure-detection AUC and **+0.376** success-failure gap, versus 0.93/+0.253 for ROBOMETER and 0.41/-0.107 for Robo-Dopamine.
- Online SAC on LIBERO tasks 28 and 33 reaches near-full success with Dream2Reward while Robo-Dopamine solves neither; late-stage reward-hacking gap is **0.001** versus **0.222** on task 33.
- Real-robot offline RL with Q-Guided Flow achieves **34.5%** pooled success (69/200) versus **28%** ROBOMETER-QGF, **22.5%** Robo-Dopamine-QGF, and **17%** behavior-cloning baseline across four contact-rich tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Comparing realized transitions against a predicted successful displacement, not just scoring endpoint state or progress, resolves the ambiguity of visually plausible but dynamically wrong motion.
> - Multiplicative (not additive) combination of direction and magnitude scores enforces conjunction, preventing a reward from being gamed by matching only one factor.
> - Dense causal rewards built without failure labels or synthetic negatives can still separate success from failure and reduce reward hacking in both online and offline robot policy learning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The application of reinforcement learning (RL) to robotic manipulation frequently encounters a significant challenge in reward design, particularly in complex environments characterized by contact-rich interactions and partial observability. In such scenarios, sparse task success feedback provides insufficient guidance for policy learning. The broader research landscape has increasingly focused on developing general-purpose robotic reward models that can learn from diverse data, including large cross-embodiment robot datasets and vision-language policies.

Prior approaches to reward learning from demonstrations have explored methods such as inverse reinforcement learning, preference-based RL, and dynamical-system-based learning that model demonstrated behavior as a motion field. More recently, large-scale multimodal models have emerged, leveraging visual and linguistic information from robot trajectories. These include methods that estimate task progress (e.g., RoboReward, ReWiND, Robo-Dopamine, ROBOMETER), use visual similarity, goal-conditioned representations, or pre-trained vision-language models (e.g., LIV, VLM-based zero-shot rewards). Some works have also utilized generative models like video prediction or diffusion models to derive rewards based on likelihood, conditional uncertainty, or alignment with generated futures (e.g., VIPER, Diffusion Reward, GenReward, DIFO).

However, existing progress-based reward models can exhibit ambiguity. When robot execution deviates from successful trajectories, a scalar progress value may remain high even if the underlying transition is incorrect (e.g., stalling, overshooting, or moving in the wrong direction). These methods often rely on various forms of additional supervision such as failure annotations, progress labels, synthetic negatives, or preference comparisons, which can be resource-intensive to obtain. Generative model-based rewards often evaluate the generator's output or predicted endpoint, rather than directly assessing the correctness of the *motion* actually executed by the robot.

Dream2Reward positions itself in this landscape by addressing the limitations of existing methods. It shifts the focus from state-level progress or endpoint prediction to *transition-level alignment*. By learning a successful latent *transition field* from *positive-only* demonstrations, it aims to provide dense, causal rewards that specifically penalize wrong-direction, overshooting, or stagnant motion. This approach directly exploits the local transition structure inherent in successful demonstrations as a reference, which the authors identify as having received less direct attention for scoring realized behavior. The work contributes to the development of robust and generalizable reward models that can simplify the application of RL to complex robot manipulation tasks by providing more informative feedback.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a novel reward model, named Dream2Reward, that can generate dense, informative, and failure-sensitive reward signals for robotic manipulation tasks. This model is designed to overcome several limitations observed in current reward learning paradigms, particularly in the context of robot learning from demonstrations.

The key motivations for this work are:
- **Addressing the Reward Design Bottleneck in RL:** For robot manipulation, especially in scenarios involving physical contact or partial observability, designing effective reward functions remains a significant challenge. Sparse success signals are insufficient for efficient learning, and handcrafted rewards require extensive engineering and lack generalizability across tasks or environments.
- **Improving upon Progress-Based Rewards:** Current general-purpose robotic reward models often estimate task progress. While progress provides a temporal ordering, it becomes ambiguous when execution deviates from nominal successful trajectories. An observation might appear to show "progress" (e.g., visually similar to a late-stage successful state) even if the *transition* that led to it was incorrect (e.g., pushing an object in the wrong direction, stalling near a goal, or overshooting). This ambiguity can lead to policies learning suboptimal or incorrect behaviors that accumulate high rewards without achieving true task success.
- **Leveraging Local Transition Structure from Positive Demonstrations:** Successful demonstrations inherently contain information about the *local change* (direction and magnitude of motion) that characterizes successful execution. Existing methods primarily exploit the *temporal ordering* of observations or rely on auxiliary supervision like relabeling or synthetic negatives. The authors argue that the local transition structure itself, which specifies finite-horizon task-relevant displacements, is a valuable, underutilized resource for directly scoring realized behavior.
- **Developing a Causal and Failure-Sensitive Reward:** A reward model for policy learning needs to provide feedback on the motion that the robot *actually executes*, ideally in a causal manner (based on information available *before* the outcome of the transition is fully observed). Existing generative models often evaluate prediction likelihood or alignment with *predicted futures*, which may not directly translate to evaluating the *correctness of the observed action*. The motivation is to create a reward that can penalize deviations (wrong direction, overshooting, stalling) at the transition level, without requiring explicit failure annotations or negative examples during training.
- **Minimizing Supervision Requirements:** Many advanced reward learning methods require costly supervision such as failure trajectories, progress labels, or human preferences. A core motivation for Dream2Reward is to learn effectively from *positive demonstrations alone*, which are becoming increasingly abundant.

Based on these motivations, the specific objectives articulated by the authors include:
1. **Formulating Reward Learning as Latent Transition Field Estimation:** To learn the task-conditioned latent change expected under successful execution using only positive demonstrations.
2. **Introducing a Causal Transition-Alignment Reward:** To compare observed and predicted successful transitions based on both directional and magnitude agreement, providing dense feedback for various deviations without requiring failure annotations or synthetic negatives.
3. **Evaluating Mechanism Properties and Downstream Utility:** To analyze the alignment and stability properties of the proposed reward and to demonstrate its effectiveness in both online and offline policy learning scenarios, including real-robot manipulation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Dream2Reward operates by learning a language-conditioned successful latent transition field exclusively from positive demonstrations. During inference, it compares an observed candidate transition's latent displacement to a predicted successful latent displacement, evaluating agreement in both direction and magnitude.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The work frames goal-conditioned manipulation as a Partially Observed Markov Decision Process (POMDP). The reward function, denoted as `r(o_t-h-H+1:t-h, o_t, g)`, is history-dependent, taking as input a visual context of length `H` (frames `o_t-h-H+1:t-h`), the visual observation at the end of the transition `o_t`, and the language-specified goal `g`. Rewards are computed for `h`-step transitions.

#### 3.2. Latent Representation

Each visual observation `o_t` is first encoded into a latent representation `z_t = E_ψ(o_t)` using a frozen visual encoder `E_ψ`. The conditioning context `c_t = C(z_t-h-H+1:t-h, g)` is then constructed from the language goal `g` and the `H` latent frames preceding the transition `(z_t-h-H+1:t-h)`. Both the visual encoder and a language encoder, as well as the context construction module `C`, are frozen during reward model training.

#### 3.3. Learning the Successful Latent Transition Field

Dream2Reward's core is a model that predicts the successful latent endpoint state `bz_t+`. The successful displacement `c∆z_t+ = bz_t+ - z_t-h` is then derived by referencing this predicted endpoint to the encoded start latent `z_t-h`.

- **Conditional Flow-Matching Model:** Instead of directly regressing the displacement, the model predicts the successful endpoint latent using a conditional flow-matching model. This model learns a velocity field `v_θ` that transports Gaussian noise to the successful endpoint latent. The use of flow matching, specifically the constant endpoint-directed velocity target of rectified flow, is adopted.
- **Multi-Horizon Training:** The model is trained over multiple prediction horizons `κ ∈ K`. For a given task context `c_t,κ = C(E_ψ(o_t-κ-H+1:t-κ), g, κ)`, the target successful endpoint latent is `z_t+ = E_ψ(o_t)`. This exposes the model to successful motion at various temporal scales.
- **Training Objective:** The training objective is an L2 loss (`L_FM`, Eq. 4) that minimizes the difference between the learned velocity field `v_θ(x_τ, τ | c_t,κ)` and the target velocity field `z_t+ - ϵ`, where `x_τ = (1-τ)ϵ + τz_t+` is an interpolation between Gaussian noise `ϵ` and the target latent `z_t+`, and `τ` is the flow-matching time.
- **Inference:** At deployment, a single fixed horizon `h` is used. The predicted successful displacement `c∆z_t+` is obtained by integrating the learned velocity field `v_θ` from Gaussian noise `x_0` to produce `x_1`, and then subtracting the start latent: `c∆z_t+ = x_1 - z_t-h`. Deterministic 10-step ODE integration and a fixed global initial-noise tensor ensure repeatable predictions.

#### 3.4. Transition-Alignment Reward

Once `c∆z_t+` (the predicted successful displacement) is obtained, the reward model compares it with the `observed candidate displacement`, `∆z_cand_t = z_t - z_t-h`, where `z_t` is the encoded observation at the end of the candidate transition. This comparison involves two main components:

- **Angular Alignment (`r_ang_t`):** Measured by the signed cosine similarity (Eq. 7) between `∆z_cand_t` and `c∆z_t+`. This component assesses whether the observed motion is in the same direction as the predicted successful motion, penalizing opposing or orthogonal movements.
- **Magnitude Agreement (`r_mag_t`):** Measured by a symmetric log-ratio score (Eq. 8), `exp(-|log ρ_t|/τ_m)`, where `ρ_t` is the ratio of the norms `∥c∆z_t+∥_2 / ∥∆z_cand_t∥_2`. This component penalizes both insufficient (stalled) and excessive (overshooting) motion proportionally.
- **Per-Step Raw Reward (`r_raw_t`):** The angular and magnitude components are combined multiplicatively (`s_t = r_ang_t * r_mag_t`) to ensure both aspects are met for a high score. An explicit magnitude-shortfall penalty (`λ_pen(1 - r_mag_t)`) is then subtracted, and the result is clamped to `[-1, 1]` (Eq. 9, 10). The multiplication ensures that low scores in one dimension are not compensated by high scores in the other.
- **Causality:** The reward is causal because the predicted successful displacement `c∆z_t+` is formed using only information available at the transition's start (`t-h`), while the observed displacement `∆z_cand_t` uses the endpoint `o_t`.

#### 3.5. Reward Normalization for Policy Learning

For downstream policy learning (online RL or offline critic training), the raw reward stream `q_t` (which is `r_raw_t` for Dream2Reward) is standardized `r_t = (q_t - μ) / σ`. The mean `μ` and standard deviation `σ` are estimated once from initial data and then held fixed throughout optimization, preserving the relative signal.

#### 3.6. Implementation Details

- **Encoders:** Visual encoder is RAEv2 with a DINOv3 ViT-L/16 backbone. Language encoder is Qwen3-0.6B.
- **Temporal Settings:** History `H=4` frames. Training horizons `K = {1, 2, 4, 8, 16}`. Deployment inference horizon `h=2` (0.5s transition).
- **Datasets:** Trained on positive manipulation demonstrations from EgoDex and Open X-Embodiment, sampled at 4 fps.
- **Hyperparameters:** `ϵ_num = 10^-6`, `τ_m = 1.0`, `λ_pen = 0.3`.

#### 3.7. Theoretical Properties

The paper presents theoretical propositions:
- **Alignment Maximization (Proposition 1):** The reward function is maximized (equal to 1) if and only if the observed and predicted displacements are positively collinear and have equal magnitudes. Angular and magnitude factors contribute multiplicatively to penalties.
- **Resolution of State-Progress Ambiguity (Proposition 2):** Transition alignment can distinguish between successful and failed transitions that lead to states with identical scalar progress values, by leveraging directional information.
- **Stability under Field Perturbation (Proposition 3 & Corollary 1):** The reward is stable under bounded perturbations of the predicted transition field, meaning small changes in the prediction lead to proportionally small changes in the reward, away from near-zero transition norms.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments systematically evaluate Dream2Reward across mechanism diagnostics, reward-stream quality, and downstream policy learning performance in both simulation and real-robot settings.

#### 4.1. Latent Transition Alignment Diagnostics

- **Failure Sensitivity:** Across four real-robot manipulation tasks, successful transitions consistently received higher raw reward (`r_raw_t`) and angular scores (`r_ang_t`), and exhibited lower absolute log magnitude mismatch (`|log ρ_t|`), compared to transitions associated with wrong-language instructions or failed episodes. This indicates that Dream2Reward differentiates correct local motion from incorrect or irrelevant motion.

#### 4.2. Failure-Sensitive Reward Validation

- **Success-Failure Separation:** On a 400-trajectory real-robot scoring set, Dream2Reward achieved a failure-detection ROC-AUC of 0.95 and a success-failure reward gap of +0.376. These metrics were higher than ROBOMETER (AUC 0.93, gap +0.253) and significantly higher than Robo-Dopamine (AUC 0.41, gap -0.107). This suggests Dream2Reward provides a more reliable ordering and margin between successful and failed episodes.
- **Temporal Resolution:** Per-step reward traces showed that Dream2Reward's reward decreased during specific failure events (e.g., stalled grasp, object knocked over, plate-edge interference) even when visual progress might still appear high. Conversely, it showed an increase during recovery motions, demonstrating sensitivity to local success or failure rather than just end-of-episode outcomes.

#### 4.3. Ablation Study

- **Component Contribution:** Ablating either the angular or magnitude component of the reward function (angle-only or magnitude-only) resulted in a reduced failure-detection AUC (0.92 for both, compared to 0.95 for the full model), indicating that both components contribute to the overall performance.
- **Language Conditioning:** Removing language conditioning significantly reduced the AUC to 0.80, highlighting the importance of task-specific context.
- **Generative Predictor Role:** A control experiment using denoising energy from the generative predictor instead of transition alignment yielded a substantially lower AUC (0.458), demonstrating that the improved performance stems from the transition alignment mechanism, not solely the underlying generative model.

#### 4.4. Online Reinforcement Learning on LIBERO

- **Downstream Performance:** In online SAC training on two LIBERO manipulation tasks (close top drawer, close microwave), Dream2Reward-guided policies achieved near-full success rates. In contrast, Robo-Dopamine-guided policies did not solve these tasks.
- **Reduced Reward Hacking:** Analysis of model reward versus true success rates across training checkpoints indicated that Dream2Reward's model reward closely correlated with true task success, exhibiting a near-zero "late-stage hacking gap" (0.001) on Task 33. Robo-Dopamine, however, showed a notable hacking gap (0.222), with high model rewards not translating to actual task completion.

#### 4.5. Per-Step Reward Quality for Online RL

- **Dense and Informative Feedback:** On the Robo Arena dataset, Dream2Reward demonstrated a low locally-flat step fraction (0.024) and high signal coverage (0.942), indicating dense and broadly distributed reward variation across trajectories.
- **Negative Feedback for Low Quality:** Dream2Reward assigned below-neutral rewards to 74.5% of steps in low-quality episodes, significantly higher than ROBOMETER (41.3%) and Robo-Dopamine (6.1%), showing effective negative feedback.
- **Behavior-Sensitive, Time-Independent Signal:** Dream2Reward exhibited the lowest reward-time mutual information (0.011), suggesting its reward signal is less correlated with elapsed time or linear progress and more responsive to executed behavior. This was further confirmed by suppressing nonterminal outputs, which dramatically increased the flat-step fraction.

#### 4.6. Real-Robot Offline RL with Q-Guided Flow

- **Improved Offline Policy Learning:** When used as a zero-shot transition labeler for real-robot offline RL with Q-Guided Flow (QGF) across four contact-rich manipulation tasks, Dream2Reward-QGF achieved the highest success rates on all tasks. Its pooled success rate was 34.5%, outperforming ROBOMETER-QGF (28%), Robo-Dopamine-QGF (22.5%), and the behavior cloning (BC) baseline (17%).

#### 4.7. Transition-Level Credit Assignment in Offline RL

- **Effective Critic Labels:** On the Robo Arena dataset, Dream2Reward provided non-trivial critic labels (effective-credit step fraction) on 80.1% of steps, compared to 70.3% for ROBOMETER and 40.4% for Robo-Dopamine.
- **Terminal Failure Differentiation:** It showed the highest failure-drop minimum-AUC (0.661) and end-segment success-failure gap (0.098), and the most negative mean reward for failed end segments (-0.091). These properties are beneficial for training critics in offline RL by providing clear distinctions between successful and failing behaviors, especially towards the end of episodes.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

Dream2Reward represents a notable advancement in the field of reward learning for robotic manipulation, primarily by re-framing the problem from state-level progress to transition-level correctness. Its significance and potential impact stem from several key aspects:

- **Novelty in Reward Formulation:** The core innovation lies in learning a task-conditioned successful *latent transition field* from positive demonstrations and then evaluating observed transitions by their *directional and magnitude alignment* with this predicted successful change. This distinguishes it from methods that focus on scalar state-progress, likelihood of generated futures, or model fit, offering a more direct assessment of local motion correctness.
- **Reduced Supervision Requirements:** A substantial impact is its ability to learn dense, informative rewards solely from *positive demonstrations*. This bypasses the often costly and complex requirements for failure annotations, explicit progress labels, or synthetic negative examples, thereby simplifying the data collection process and potentially enabling broader application of RL in real-world settings where obtaining diverse failure data is challenging.
- **Enhanced Reward Signal Quality:** The findings indicate that Dream2Reward produces dense, causal, and failure-sensitive reward signals. Its ability to provide distinct feedback for wrong-direction, overshooting, or stagnant motion, even when resulting states might appear to show progress, is crucial. This granularity helps agents learn more precise and robust behaviors.
- **Mitigation of Reward Hacking:** The empirical results demonstrate that policies guided by Dream2Reward exhibit reduced reward hacking, a common issue where agents exploit imperfections in the reward function to achieve high scores without accomplishing the true task. The transition-level comparison enforces adherence to correct local dynamics, making it harder for agents to "cheat" the reward.
- **Improved Downstream Policy Learning:** Dream2Reward consistently facilitated stronger policy learning performance in both online simulation (LIBERO tasks) and real-robot offline settings (Q-Guided Flow on four manipulation tasks). This suggests that the quality of its reward signal directly translates to more effective and efficient training of robotic policies.
- **Generalizability Across Tasks and Domains:** The model's reliance on learned latent representations and language conditioning suggests a degree of generalizability across various manipulation tasks and even different robot embodiments, as demonstrated by its performance on diverse tasks and different evaluation protocols.
- **Robustness in Mixed-Quality Data:** Its effectiveness in offline RL, where it labels mixed-quality datasets (human demonstrations and policy-collected rollouts containing failures), highlights its utility in leveraging readily available data to improve policies without needing curated, perfect trajectories.

In summary, Dream2Reward offers a practical and effective solution to the reward design bottleneck in robotic RL. By providing a fine-grained, causal, and accurate reward signal from more easily acquirable data (positive demonstrations), it has the potential to accelerate the development of robust and generalizable robotic manipulation skills, making advanced RL techniques more accessible and applicable to complex real-world challenges.
