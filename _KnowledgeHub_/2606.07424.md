# Rapid co-design of Buoyancy-assisted robots for Challenging Locomotion using Gaussian Evolutionary Specialists

> [!summary] Summary
> A framework named Gaussian Evolutionary Specialists (GES) was developed to accelerate robot co-design by addressing computational bottlenecks and policy collapse issues in reinforcement learning-based approaches. This method enabled the discovery of robot morphologies achieving up to 3x greater obstacle clearance on hardware and reduced co-design time by 37%.

## Problem

- The high computational cost of robot co-design, particularly when using reinforcement learning (RL) to train optimal controllers for each morphology candidate.
- Behavioral diversity collapse in design-conditioned universal policies, where monolithic networks converge to a single, suboptimal locomotion strategy across varied robot morphologies.
- Representation collapse in Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures, where experts fail to specialize due to a lack of discriminative routing signals during end-to-end training.

## Method

- Introduced Gaussian Evolutionary Specialists (GES), a two-stage framework that explicitly decouples design-space partitioning from policy learning.
- Stage 1 iteratively evolves K specialists, each associated with a Gaussian territory, where specialists are trained on designs within their territory and competitively assigned based on performance.
- Stage 2 integrates a GES density router into a Bayesian Optimization co-design loop, allowing rapid, zero-shot evaluation of new morphology candidates by selecting the most suitable pre-trained specialist.

## Results

- GES-optimized designs achieved up to **25.5%** higher mean performance in simulation for obstacle traversal, clearing obstacles 16 cm higher than baseline MLP designs.
- The framework reduced total co-design wall-clock time by **37%** for the 3D design space and demonstrated a **3x** improvement in obstacle clearance (24 cm vs 8 cm) on physical BALLU hardware.
- Empirical analysis confirmed GES successfully resolved behavioral diversity and representation collapse, with specialists learning distinct locomotion strategies and creating performance-grounded partitions in the design space.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly decoupling design-space partitioning from policy learning is essential to overcome behavioral diversity collapse in universal policies and representation collapse in Mixture-of-Experts.
> - A competitive assignment mechanism, where specialists "win" designs based on superior performance, effectively drives specialization and shapes distinct, performance-grounded territories in the design space.
> - Iterative refinement of Gaussian territories, combined with optimized initialization, ensures robust space coverage and efficient learning of diverse locomotion strategies by specialized policies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in this paper contributes to the field of robot co-design, which involves the simultaneous optimization of a robot's physical morphology and its control policy to achieve a specified task performance. This approach is motivated by the understanding that neither morphology nor control can be optimally designed in isolation.

Existing methodologies for robot co-design include:
- **Differentiable frameworks:** These methods co-optimize morphology and control using analytical gradients, but require fully differentiable simulators, which may not always be available or accurate for complex physical systems.
- **Meta-learning:** This approach enables faster adaptation of controllers to new designs, yet typically still necessitates per-candidate fine-tuning during the design evaluation phase, retaining a computational bottleneck.
- **Graph-based methods:** These are effective for exploring combinatorial design spaces but may not scale efficiently to continuous parameter spaces.
- **Evolutionary methods:** While capable of exploring diverse designs, they often require numerous evaluations to converge to an optimal solution.
- **Generative approaches:** Recent advancements involve using models like diffusion models to synthesize morphologies, expanding the design search beyond predefined parameters.

A common challenge across these methods is the computational cost associated with evaluating each candidate morphology, particularly when each evaluation requires training a new controller. This cost becomes substantial when reinforcement learning (RL) is employed for controller optimization, as RL policies often demand millions of environment interactions.

To address this, researchers have explored **universal policies** that are conditioned on morphology parameters and can be pre-trained over a broad design space. The intention is to enable zero-shot evaluation or minimal fine-tuning for new designs, thereby decoupling controller learning from design search and making large-scale co-design more feasible. However, the paper identifies two significant limitations in this approach:
1. **Behavioral Diversity Collapse:** Design-conditioned universal policies, particularly monolithic MLPs, tend to converge to a single locomotion strategy. This occurs because conflicting gradients from diverse morphologies can cancel each other out during backpropagation, leading to a suboptimal compromise that cannot effectively distinguish performance differences across various designs. This makes the policy an unreliable evaluator for co-design.
2. **Representation Collapse in Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) Architectures:** While MoE architectures are a natural extension to capture multi-modal behaviors by assigning specialists to different design space regions, end-to-end training of MoEs can fail. If the router (which assigns designs to specialists) is initialized randomly, experts receive similar design distributions early on, leading them to converge to similar behaviors. Once homogenized, the router lacks sufficient signal to drive specialization.

This work focuses on the Buoyancy-Assisted Light Legged Unit (BALLU) platform, which presents unique co-design challenges due to its aerodynamic sensitivity, non-linear spring-servo actuation, and inherent inaccuracies in aerodynamic modeling within simulation. The paper aims to develop a framework that mitigates the computational cost of co-design and overcomes the identified collapse problems in universal policy training, thereby fitting into the broader research landscape as an advancement in efficient and robust robot co-design using RL.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a framework that facilitates the rapid and efficient co-design of high-performance legged robots for challenging locomotion tasks, specifically targeting the limitations of existing reinforcement learning (RL)-based co-design approaches.

The motivation for this work is multi-faceted:

- **Addressing the Computational Bottleneck in Bi-level Co-design:** Robot co-design is inherently a bi-level optimization problem where an outer loop evaluates morphology candidates, and an inner loop trains an optimal controller for each candidate. When RL is used for the inner loop, this process is computationally expensive, requiring millions of environment interactions per design evaluation. This cost makes extensive design exploration impractical and serves as a significant bottleneck for achieving optimized robot systems. The research is motivated by the need to amortize this cost and enable faster design iteration.

- **Overcoming Behavioral Diversity Collapse in Universal Policies:** A promising approach to reduce the inner-loop cost is to pre-train a single universal policy capable of controlling various morphologies. However, the authors identify a fundamental limitation they term "behavioral diversity collapse." This occurs when a design-conditioned universal policy, such as a monolithic Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP), is trained across a diverse design space. Conflicting behavioral gradients from different morphologies can lead to the policy converging to a single, generalized locomotion strategy that performs sub-optimally across many designs. This undermines the policy's ability to serve as a reliable evaluator for distinguishing performance variations between candidate designs in the outer optimization loop. The paper is motivated to resolve this issue to enable effective co-design with universal controllers.

- **Mitigating Representation Collapse in Mixture-of-Experts (MoE):** As a potential remedy for behavioral diversity collapse, Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures, which employ multiple specialist networks, could be considered. However, the authors observe that standard end-to-end MoE training suffers from "representation collapse." In this scenario, if the router responsible for assigning designs to specialists is randomly initialized, all experts may receive similar design distributions during early training phases. This leads to experts converging to similar behaviors, thus losing their specialized capabilities. Once the experts become homogeneous, the router lacks the necessary signal to differentiate and route designs effectively, rendering the MoE ineffective. This motivates the development of a framework that can foster genuine specialization among experts.

- **Addressing Challenges of the BALLU Platform:** The research uses the Buoyancy-Assisted Light Legged Unit (BALLU) robot as a testbed. BALLU's unique characteristics—lightweight construction, helium balloons introducing severe aerodynamic nonlinearities (buoyancy and drag), and difficulties in analytical modeling of these forces—make both control design and multi-parameter morphology optimization particularly challenging. These dynamics make model-free RL a practical approach for controller design, but also exacerbate the co-design bottleneck. The motivation is to demonstrate a co-design solution that is effective even on platforms with complex and difficult-to-model dynamics.

By addressing these motivations, the research aims to propose Gaussian Evolutionary Specialists (GES), a two-stage framework that explicitly decouples design-space partitioning from policy learning. GES is designed to capture diverse behaviors, assign specialist policies to evolving Gaussian regions, and iteratively refine them, ultimately substituting costly per-candidate RL retraining with direct evaluation via specialized policies.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on Gaussian Evolutionary Specialists (GES), a two-stage framework designed to address the challenges of computational cost and policy collapse in robot co-design.

**A. Problem Formulation**

1. **BALLU Platform:** The target platform is the Buoyancy-Assisted Light Legged Unit (BALLU), a bipedal robot that utilizes helium-filled balloons to counteract most of its weight. It consists of a pelvis, two upper and two lower limbs, connected by spring-loaded knee joints. Actuation involves a single servo per leg, providing two actuated degrees of freedom. The robot's lightweight design and fall-safe operation are noted, but its aerodynamic nonlinearities (buoyancy and drag forces varying with balloon inflation and environmental conditions) pose significant control challenges, making model-free RL a suitable control approach.

2. **Design Space (D):** The robot morphology is parameterized by four continuous variables:
 - Femur length ($l_f$)
 - Tibia length ($l_t$)
 - Gravity Compensation Ratio (GCR): Ratio of buoyancy force to robot weight. Higher GCR means less effective weight.
 - Spring Coefficient (SPCF): Stiffness of the knee joint, influencing energy storage during dynamic maneuvers.
 The experiments are conducted on two design parameter spaces: a 2D space ($D_2 = \{(GCR, SPCF)\}$) and a 3D space ($D_3 = \{(GCR, SPCF, l)\}$, where $l_f = l_t = l$).

3. **Bi-level Co-design Objective:** The goal is to maximize task performance $J(\theta_c, \theta_m)$ by jointly optimizing morphology ($\theta_m$) and control policy ($\theta_c$). This intractable joint optimization is decomposed into a bi-level problem:
 $\max_{\theta_m} J(\theta_c^*, \theta_m)$, where $\theta_c^* = \arg\max_{\theta_c} J(\theta_c, \theta_m)$.
 The inner loop finds the optimal controller for a given morphology, and the outer loop searches the morphology space.

4. **MDP Formulation:** Locomotion control is modeled as a Markov Decision Process (MDP).
 - **State ($s_t$):** Proprioceptive information including base linear/angular velocity, joint positions/velocities, and the last action. An augmented state $\tilde{s}_t = [s_t, \theta_m]$ includes the design vector.
 - **Action ($a_t$):** 2D vector specifying target positions for left and right knee joints, tracked by a PD controller.
 - **Policy ($\pi_{\theta_c}(a_t | \tilde{s}_t)$):** Parameterized as a Gaussian MLP and optimized using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) to maximize expected discounted return.
 - **Reward Functions:**
 - **Obstacle Traversal:** Combines navigation (distance to goal), forward velocity, and a jumping term (encouraging toe clearance).
 - **Ramp Locomotion:** Similar to obstacle traversal but without the jumping term.

**B. Gaussian Evolutionary Specialists (GES) Algorithm**

GES is a two-stage framework to approximate the optimal performance across the design space without requiring per-design RL policy training. The core idea is to decouple design-space partitioning from expert specialization.

**Stage 1: Iterative Territory Evolution**
This stage involves K specialists, each associated with a Gaussian territory $G(\mu_k, \Sigma_k)$ (mean $\mu_k$, diagonal covariance $\Sigma_k$) over the normalized design space $X =^d$. The process repeats until the territories collectively cover 95% of the design space:

1. **Initialization:**
 - **Farthest Point Sampling (FPS):** K initial Gaussian centers are selected from a uniform sample set in $X$ by iteratively choosing the point farthest from existing centers, ensuring dispersion.
 - **Lloyd Relaxation:** Centers are refined through T iterations of Lloyd's algorithm, reassigning each center to the centroid of its Voronoi region, leading to well-separated and approximately space-filling centers.
 - Initial diagonal covariance $\Sigma_k$ is set based on an initial coverage of 12%.

2. **Train:**
 - Each specialist $\pi_k$ samples $n_{train}$ designs from its current Gaussian territory $G(\mu_k, \Sigma_k)$.
 - $\pi_k$ then trains for $n_{ppo}$ PPO steps, resuming from its previous checkpoint to retain learned behaviors.

3. **Probe:**
 - $F$ candidate designs are sampled near the 2-standard-deviation borders of all Gaussian territories.
 - Each candidate is evaluated by *all* K specialists.
 - The candidate is assigned to the specialist $k^*$ that achieves the highest task performance score $J$ on that design. This competitive assignment mechanism drives specialists to expand into regions where they perform best.

4. **Refit:**
 - Each specialist's Gaussian territory ($G(\mu_k, \Sigma_k)$) is re-fit to the set of designs it has "won" during the probing phase. The mean ($\mu_k$) and diagonal covariance ($\Sigma_k$) are updated accordingly. The diagonal covariance constraint ensures axis-aligned territories.

5. **MC Coverage Check:** Monte-Carlo (MC) sampling is used to estimate the coverage of the design space. A design $\theta$ is considered "covered" if its maximum un-normalized multivariate density contribution from any specialist $k$ (i.e., $\max_k f_k(\theta)$) exceeds a threshold $\tau = e^{-2} \approx 0.135$. The iteration stops when the estimated MC coverage reaches 95%.

**Stage 2: GES Density Router for BO-based Co-Design**

After Stage 1 (iterative territory evolution) is complete, the trained K specialists serve as zero-shot evaluators within a Bayesian Optimization (BO) co-design loop.
- For a new candidate design $\hat{\theta}_m$ proposed by the BO acquisition function, a geometric router selects the specialist $k^*$ whose Gaussian likelihood is highest (i.e., $k^* = \arg\max_k N(\hat{\theta}_m | \mu_k, \Sigma_k)$).
- The policy of this selected specialist ($\pi_{k^*}$) is evaluated to obtain the performance score $\hat{J}$.
- This score is returned to the BO surrogate model, which updates its belief over the design space, and the BO loop continues until convergence. This process replaces expensive per-candidate RL training with a fast evaluation from a pre-trained specialist.

**C. Experimental Setup**

- **Simulation Environment:** Isaac Lab 2.0 with Isaac Sim 4.5.
- **Physics/Control Rates:** Physics runs at 200 Hz; control actions applied at 20 Hz.
- **Tasks:** Obstacle traversal and ramp climbing, both employing curriculum learning (increasing obstacle height or ramp slope per level).
- **Optimization:** Bayesian Optimization uses Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE).
- **Control Policy:** PPO algorithm optimizes an MLP with layers for both actor and critic.
- **Coverage Calculation:** $N_{mc} = 10^4$ samples for Monte Carlo coverage.

**D. Baselines**

GES performance is compared against two baselines, both morphology-conditioned and trained end-to-end via design randomization:
- **e2e MLP:** A monolithic Multi-Layer Perceptron.
- **e2e MoE:** A soft-gated Mixture-of-Experts with the same MLP architecture per expert.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides a detailed evaluation of GES against baseline methods across various tasks and design space dimensions, including simulation and hardware validation.

**A. Overall Performance (Simulation)**

GES demonstrated consistent performance improvements over both baseline approaches (e2e MLP and e2e MoE) in both obstacle traversal and ramp locomotion tasks, across both 2D and 3D design spaces.

1. **Obstacle Traversal:**
 - **2D Design Space:** GES identified designs capable of clearing a 54 cm obstacle, which is 16 cm higher than the e2e MLP (38 cm) and 2 cm higher than the e2e MoE (52 cm). Over 1000 unseen test designs, GES achieved a mean performance improvement of 25.5% over e2e MLP and 8.62% over e2e MoE. In head-to-head comparisons, GES outperformed e2e MLP in 94% of designs and e2e MoE in 72%.
 - **3D Design Space:** GES found designs capable of clearing 54 cm obstacles, compared to 41 cm for e2e MLP, representing a peak gain of 31.7%. The mean improvement over e2e MLP across 1000 test designs was 18.8%. GES won 68% of head-to-head comparisons against e2e MLP and 76% against e2e MoE. The e2e MoE baseline showed a approximately 20% degradation in the 3D case compared to 2D, attributed to representation collapse with increasing design space dimensionality.
 - **Design Analysis (Obstacle Traversal):** Optimal designs generally featured higher Gravity Compensation Ratio (GCR) and higher Spring Coefficient (SPCF), facilitating flight and energy storage for jumping. The optimal leg length in the 3D space was found to be 45.58 cm, close to the baseline design.

2. **Ramp Locomotion:**
 - **2D Design Space:** GES identified designs capable of climbing slopes up to 36.25°, surpassing both baselines (32.61°) by 11.2% at peak performance. Mean performance across 1000 test designs improved by approximately 13% over both baselines. GES won 81% of head-to-head comparisons against e2e MLP and 78% against e2e MoE.
 - **3D Design Space:** GES achieved a peak gain of approximately 8% and a mean improvement of approximately 5% over e2e MLP. GES won 75% of head-to-head comparisons against e2e MLP and 86% against e2e MoE.
 - **Design Analysis (Ramp Locomotion):** The optimal leg length for ramp locomotion (31.45 cm) was shorter than for obstacle traversal, suggesting a benefit for higher-frequency limb motion. Bimodal performance was observed regarding buoyancy (high and low GCRs yielding good results with distinct motions), and higher spring stiffness generally improved performance. The contact-rich nature of this task presented higher control complexity, resulting in smaller performance gains for GES compared to obstacle traversal.

**B. Convergence Analysis**

1. **MoE Representation Collapse:** Empirical results confirmed that the end-to-end MoE router distributed designs almost equally among all experts, indicating a failure to specialize and confirming the representation collapse problem. In contrast, GES assigned each design to a single dominant specialist based on its Gaussian territory, creating distinct, performance-grounded partitions within the design space.

2. **Behavioral Diversity:** The evolution of Gaussian territories in the 3D design space showed specialists converging to distinct regions that collectively tiled the space. Analysis of foot trajectories demonstrated that different GES specialists controlling the same robot morphology produced qualitatively distinct strategies for clearing an obstacle, indicating successful resolution of behavioral diversity collapse.

**C. Other Analysis**

1. **Number of Specialists (K):** In the 2D design space, GES performance peaked at K=3 specialists, then degraded for larger K. This suggests a trade-off where increasing K initially helps specialize but beyond a point, may lead to under-trained specialists if the overall design space coverage target (95%) is met too quickly, or if individual territory coverage becomes too sparse.

2. **Initialization Strategy:** FPS+Lloyd relaxation for initial Gaussian center placement demonstrated superior performance (highest mean and peak obstacle clearance) and faster convergence (approximately 2x faster than random initialization) compared to grid-based, random, or FPS-only initialization strategies. This validates its use for establishing initial, well-dispersed territories.

3. **Computational Cost:** GES reduced the total co-design wall-clock time from approximately 100 hours to 63 hours (a 37% reduction) for the 3D design space on a single GPU. The framework's inherent parallelizability (training and probing specialists independently) further allows for significant speed-up, reducing Stage 1 training from 48 hours to approximately 8 hours with K GPUs.

4. **Performance Sensitivity to Design:** Performance sensitivity maps illustrated that the relationship between design parameters (GCR, SPCF) and task performance (obstacle traversal, ramp walking) is often non-linear, bimodal, and non-monotonic. For obstacle traversal, simply maximizing GCR and SPCF did not yield optimal results, validating the optimizer's convergence to interior points in the design space.

**D. Hardware Analysis**

- A GES-optimized BALLU design (GCR = 0.86, SPCF = 0.008) was validated on physical hardware using teleoperation to isolate morphology contributions. This robot successfully cleared a 24 cm obstacle, representing a 3x improvement over the baseline BALLU design (8 cm clearance).
- A sim-to-real gap was observed; the baseline design cleared 14.2 cm in simulation but only 8 cm on hardware. Hardware limitations prevented achieving the optimal GCR of 0.88 identified in simulation, limiting the maximum GCR to 0.86. Despite these gaps, the morphology improvements transferred effectively to the physical platform.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework, Gaussian Evolutionary Specialists (GES), that offers advancements in the domain of robot co-design, particularly for systems employing reinforcement learning (RL) for control.

The significance of this work can be understood through its contributions:

- **Resolution of Critical Failure Modes:** GES directly addresses two identified fundamental limitations in applying universal policies for robot co-design:
 - **Behavioral diversity collapse:** Where monolithic design-conditioned policies converge to a single, suboptimal locomotion strategy across diverse morphologies.
 - **Representation collapse:** In end-to-end Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures, where experts fail to specialize due to a lack of discriminative routing signals.
 By resolving these issues, GES provides a more robust and effective method for controller generalization across varying robot morphologies, which is a prerequisite for successful co-design.

- **Accelerated Robot Co-Design:** A primary impact of GES is the significant reduction in the computational cost and wall-clock time required for co-design. By replacing costly per-candidate RL retraining with zero-shot evaluation from pre-trained specialists, GES curtails design optimization time by 37% (from ~100 hours to ~63 hours for a 3D design space). The inherent parallelizability of the specialist training further enables substantial speedups. This acceleration makes it more feasible to explore complex design spaces and optimize robot morphology and control for challenging tasks.

- **Discovery of High-Performance Robot Designs:** The framework demonstrably leads to the discovery of morphologies that achieve substantially higher performance. For example, in simulation, GES-optimized designs showed up to 25.5% higher mean performance in obstacle traversal compared to naive universal policies. More critically, these improvements translated to hardware, with a GES-optimized BALLU clearing a 24 cm obstacle, a 3x improvement over the baseline design. This indicates the practical utility of the framework for enhancing robot capabilities.

- **Enhanced Understanding of Morphology-Control Interactions:** The iterative territory evolution process within GES and the resulting performance sensitivity maps provide insights into the complex, often non-linear and non-monotonic, relationships between morphology parameters and task performance. This deeper understanding can guide future robot design principles.

- **Generalizability Potential:** While validated on the buoyancy-assisted BALLU platform, the authors state that the GES framework is platform-agnostic. This suggests a broader potential impact, implying that the methodology could be applied to co-design other types of legged robots, such as quadrupeds and humanoids, which also face challenges in optimizing morphology and control for dynamic locomotion.

- **Advancement in RL for Robotics:** The work contributes to the broader field of reinforcement learning in robotics by proposing a novel training paradigm that enables the effective training of specialized policies within a universal control framework, overcoming known issues with multi-task and multi-embodiment learning.

The future impact of this work may include the development of adaptive GES variants that can dynamically adjust the number of specialists, further reducing hyperparameter sensitivity, and its application to a wider range of continuous and discrete design spaces and robot platforms.
