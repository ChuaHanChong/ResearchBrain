# Generative Uncertainty in Diffusion Models

> [!summary] Summary
> A Bayesian framework is introduced to quantify "generative uncertainty" in diffusion and flow-matching models, leveraging last-layer Laplace approximation and a semantic likelihood in CLIP feature space. This method effectively identifies and filters out low-quality synthetic samples, achieving improved FID and Precision compared to prior uncertainty-based techniques and complementing other quality metrics.

## Problem

- State-of-the-art generative models frequently produce low-quality samples with artifacts or semantic inconsistencies, which hinders their practical deployment.
- There is a lack of principled, computationally tractable methods to quantify uncertainty specifically for the *quality* of generated samples in large diffusion models.
- Uncertainty calculated directly in high-dimensional pixel space often does not align with human perception of image quality due to sensitivity to perceptually irrelevant variations.

## Method

- A ==Bayesian framework== is proposed to quantify ==generative uncertainty== as the variability of the posterior predictive distribution over generated samples given a fixed random noise input.
- The framework employs a computationally tractable ==last-layer Laplace approximation== to estimate the posterior distribution over model parameters for large diffusion (and flow-matching) models.
- A ==semantic likelihood== is introduced, which leverages pre-trained feature extractors (like ==CLIP==) to measure uncertainty in a perceptually meaningful latent space rather than raw pixel space, improving alignment with human perception of quality.

## Results

- Successfully filtered out "hallucinated" (poor-quality) generations from a 2D toy diffusion model, confirming the correlation between high uncertainty and undesirable samples.
- Outperformed existing uncertainty-based filtering methods (**BayesDiff**, Aleatoric Uncertainty) in improving ImageNet quality metrics (FID and Precision) for **UViT** and **ADM** models, and was competitive with non-uncertainty-based methods (Realism/Rarity Scores).
- Demonstrated applicability to latent flow matching models and showed that computational overhead can be reduced significantly (e.g., **10x**) with minimal performance degradation by optimizing Monte Carlo samples and sampling steps for uncertainty estimation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Generative uncertainty, derived from model parameter variability, reliably correlates with visual quality and can distinguish high-quality from low-quality (e.g., "hallucinated") samples.
> - Applying Bayesian uncertainty quantification specifically to the last layer of large generative models, combined with a semantic likelihood, provides a practical and effective strategy for assessing sample quality.
> - Generative uncertainty offers complementary information to existing non-uncertainty-based quality metrics like realism or rarity scores, allowing for improved performance when combined.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Generative modeling, particularly with the advent of diffusion models, has undergone a revolution, enabling the creation of highly realistic and diverse synthetic data across various modalities, especially images. Models like Stable Diffusion and Midjourney have captivated public attention with their ability to synthesize high-quality images from text prompts. However, despite these impressive advancements, a persistent challenge remains: even state-of-the-art generative models can produce "low-quality" samples, characterized by artifacts, incoherence, or a failure to align with conditioning information. Manually inspecting and filtering these undesirable samples is time-consuming and impractical for large-scale deployment.

This paper addresses this critical gap by introducing a principled framework for "generative uncertainty" in diffusion models. It positions itself at the intersection of two major fields: **deep generative modeling** and **Bayesian deep learning/uncertainty quantification (UQ)**.

Historically, UQ has been extensively applied in **predictive models** (e.g., classification, regression) using Bayesian Neural Networks (BNNs), Monte Carlo Dropout, Deep Ensembles, or Laplace approximation. The goal in predictive UQ is to quantify the model's confidence in its *predictions*, often to detect out-of-distribution inputs or unreliable outputs. While successful in predictive tasks, applying Bayesian UQ directly to generative models for *sample quality assessment* has been less common and faces unique challenges due to the high-dimensional output space and the complexity of generative processes.

Prior attempts to quantify uncertainty in diffusion models for quality assessment include:
- **BayesDiff [Kou et al., 2024]:** This work also utilizes the Laplace approximation but calculates uncertainty by tracking epistemic uncertainty throughout the *entire diffusion sampling process*. The current paper explicitly contrasts its approach, which applies Bayesian inference to *model parameters* and uses the *standard sampling process*, arguing for greater generality and empirical effectiveness.
- **Aleatoric Uncertainty (AU) [De Vita and Belagiannis, 2025]:** This method focuses on aleatoric uncertainty by measuring the sensitivity of intermediate diffusion scores to perturbations, primarily in pixel space.
- Other UQ efforts like DECU [Berry et al., 2024] and Chan et al. focus on epistemic uncertainty for *conditional* diffusion models or inverse problems, but often require modifying or retraining model components and don't provide per-sample uncertainty for unconditional generation. Conformal prediction methods [Angelopoulos et al., 2022] are also more geared towards inverse problems or coverage guarantees rather than identifying individual poor samples in unconditional generation.

Beyond uncertainty, **non-uncertainty-based metrics** like realism scores [Kynkäänniemi et al., 2019] and rarity scores [Han et al., 2023] exist for filtering generated images. These methods typically assess sample quality based on their distance to a reference data manifold in a learned feature space. This paper explicitly compares its generative uncertainty framework to these methods, highlighting the complementary nature of its approach, which captures uncertainty stemming from model parameters (epistemic uncertainty) rather than just sample-data manifold proximity.

In summary, this work contributes significantly by:
1. **Formulating "generative uncertainty"** as a principled Bayesian concept for assessing sample quality in generative models, analogous to predictive uncertainty.
2. **Developing a practical and scalable framework** to estimate this uncertainty for large, modern diffusion (and flow-matching) models, addressing the computational intractability inherent in applying full Bayesian inference.
3. **Introducing the "semantic likelihood"** to overcome the limitations of pixel-space uncertainty in high-dimensional image generation, ensuring perceptual relevance.
4. **Demonstrating empirical superiority** over existing uncertainty-based filtering methods and offering competitive/complementary performance with non-uncertainty-based metrics.
5. **Operating post-hoc**, meaning it can be applied to any pre-trained diffusion or flow matching model without requiring costly retraining or significant modifications to the sampling process. This makes it highly versatile and practical for existing deployments.

This research pushes the boundaries of reliable AI, enabling more robust deployment of generative models by providing an automated mechanism to self-assess and filter low-quality outputs.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to **propose a Bayesian framework for estimating "generative uncertainty" of synthetic samples produced by modern generative models, particularly diffusion models.** The ultimate goal is to enable automated detection and filtering of low-quality generated samples without requiring human inspection.

The motivations driving this objective stem from critical challenges in the current state of generative AI:

1. **Persistent Issue of Low-Quality Samples:** Despite the remarkable progress and state-of-the-art performance of diffusion models in generating high-quality samples on average, individual generations can still be flawed. These flaws include artifacts, distortions, or semantic inconsistencies with the given conditioning information (e.g., a prompt).
2. **Deployment Challenges:** The presence of these low-quality samples poses a significant hurdle for the practical deployment of generative models. In real-world applications, users expect consistent, high-quality outputs. Requiring manual inspection and repeated generation attempts to find an acceptable sample leads to a poor user experience and inefficiency.
3. **Lack of Principled Uncertainty Quantification in Generative Models:** While Bayesian inference has been a cornerstone for principled uncertainty quantification in predictive models (allowing models to signal when their predictions are unreliable), its application to generative models for assessing sample quality has been surprisingly limited. The authors explicitly pose the question: "How can Bayesian principles help us detect poor generations?"
4. **Computational Intractability for Large Models:** Applying traditional Bayesian methods (e.g., deep ensembles, full variational inference) to the immense parameter spaces of modern diffusion models (hundreds of millions to billions of parameters) is computationally prohibitive, making such approaches impractical.
5. **Challenges with High-Dimensional Output Spaces:** For modalities like natural images, which exist in extremely high-dimensional pixel space, directly computing uncertainty based on pixel-level differences often does not correlate well with human perception of quality. Small pixel variations can lead to large L2-norm differences, even if perceptually the images are very similar. This necessitates a more semantically aware approach to uncertainty.

To address these motivations, the authors set out to:
- Formalize the concept of "generative uncertainty" as the variability of the posterior predictive distribution over generated samples.
- Develop a computationally tractable method to estimate this uncertainty for large diffusion models, specifically using the last-layer Laplace approximation.
- Introduce a "semantic likelihood" that leverages pre-trained feature extractors (like CLIP) to measure variability in a perceptually meaningful latent space, rather than raw pixel space.
- Empirically demonstrate that this proposed generative uncertainty effectively identifies poor-quality samples and outperforms existing uncertainty-based methods.
- Explore strategies to minimize the computational overhead of their Bayesian framework during sampling, making it practical for real-world scenarios.
- Show the broader applicability of their framework beyond diffusion models to other generative architectures like flow matching models.

By tackling these intertwined objectives, the paper aims to provide a vital tool for improving the reliability and usability of cutting-edge generative AI.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the proposed methodology revolves around extending the principles of Bayesian uncertainty quantification from predictive models to generative models, specifically diffusion models. The authors formalize the concept of "generative uncertainty" and then tackle the practical challenges of applying it to large-scale, high-dimensional generative models.

#### 3.1. Generative Uncertainty Formalization

The authors define **generative uncertainty** for a synthetic sample as the variability of the posterior predictive distribution over the generated data given a fixed random noise input `z` and the training data `D`:
$u(z):= V(p(x|z, D)) = E_{p(\theta|D)}[p(x|g_\theta(z))]$
where $g_\theta$ is the generative model with parameters $\theta$, and $p(\theta|D)$ is the posterior distribution over the model parameters given the training data. Analogous to predictive uncertainty in classification (how uncertain is the prediction for a given input), generative uncertainty aims to quantify how uncertain the *generated output* is for a given random seed `z`. High uncertainty should ideally correlate with low-quality or unreliable generations.

#### 3.2. Making Bayesian Inference Practical for Large Models: Last-Layer Laplace Approximation

Applying full Bayesian inference to modern diffusion models, which can have hundreds of millions to billions of parameters, is computationally intractable. To address this, the authors employ the **Laplace approximation** to approximate the posterior distribution over model parameters, $q(\theta|D)$. This approximation models the posterior as a Gaussian distribution centered at the maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimate (which, for neural networks, is typically the trained parameters $\hat{\theta}$) with a covariance matrix derived from the inverse Hessian of the loss function.
$q(\theta|D) = N(\theta|\hat{\theta}, \Sigma), \Sigma = [\nabla^2_\theta L(\theta; D)]^{-1}_{\hat{\theta}}$

To further reduce computational overhead, they adopt the **last-layer Laplace approximation**. This means that only the parameters of the final layer of the denoising network $f_\theta$ (which constitutes the core of $g_\theta$) are given a Bayesian treatment; the vast majority of parameters in earlier layers remain fixed at their point estimates. This significantly reduces the dimensionality of the parameter space over which the Hessian needs to be computed, making it feasible to apply post-hoc to any pre-trained diffusion model.

The authors acknowledge a theoretical subtlety: the diffusion training loss (Eq. 1) is not a direct log-likelihood in the strictest Bayesian sense without specific reweighting. However, they empirically demonstrate that applying the Laplace approximation directly to the standard diffusion loss still yields meaningful uncertainty estimates.

Crucially, their approach differs from prior work like BayesDiff [Kou et al., 2024]. While BayesDiff also uses Laplace approximation, it modifies the diffusion sampling process by resampling weights at *each diffusion step*. In contrast, this paper's method maintains the standard diffusion sampling process $g_{\hat{\theta}}(z)$ for the primary sample, and only uses the sampled parameters $\theta_m \sim q(\theta|D)$ to generate *additional* Monte Carlo samples of the *output* (i.e., $\hat{x}_m = g_{\theta_m}(z)$) to compute the variability for the uncertainty estimate. This distinction contributes to both simplicity and empirical performance.

#### 3.3. Addressing High-Dimensional Sample Spaces: Semantic Likelihood

A major challenge with high-dimensional data like natural images is that pixel-level differences (e.g., L2 norm) often do not align with human perception of image quality or semantic similarity. Computing uncertainty directly in pixel space can lead to estimates that are overly sensitive to minor, perceptually irrelevant variations (e.g., background noise).

To overcome this, the authors introduce a **semantic likelihood**. Instead of placing a Gaussian likelihood over the raw pixel values $x$, they propose to map the generated samples $x$ into a lower-dimensional, perceptually meaningful "semantic" latent space $S$ using a pre-trained feature extractor $c_\phi: X \to S$. Popular choices for $c_\phi$ include Inception-Net or, as used in their experiments, a CLIP encoder [Radford et al., 2021]. The likelihood is then defined in this semantic space:
$p(x|g_\theta(z); \phi) = N(e(x) | c_\phi(g_\theta(z)), \sigma^2I)$
where $e(x)$ is the semantic feature vector of $x$.

The posterior predictive distribution for uncertainty estimation is then approximated by a single Gaussian via moment matching in this semantic space (Eq. 8):
$p(x|z, D) \approx N(e(x) | \bar{e}, \text{Diag}[\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^M e_m^2 - \bar{e}^2] + \sigma^2I)$
where $\bar{e} = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^M e_m$, and $e_m = c_\phi(g_{\theta_m}(z))$ for $\theta_m \sim q(\theta|D)$. The generative uncertainty $u(z)$ is finally computed as the entropy of this approximated semantic posterior predictive distribution.

#### 3.4. Algorithm Summary (Algorithm 1)

1. Generate the primary sample $\hat{x}_0$ using the original pre-trained model $g_{\hat{\theta}}(z)$.
2. Extract its semantic features $e_0 = c_\phi(\hat{x}_0)$.
3. For $M$ Monte Carlo samples:
 a. Draw model parameters $\theta_m$ from the approximate Laplace posterior $q(\theta|D)$.
 b. Generate an additional sample $\hat{x}_m = g_{\theta_m}(z)$ (using the *same* initial noise $z$).
 c. Extract its semantic features $e_m = c_\phi(\hat{x}_m)$.
4. Compute the approximate semantic posterior predictive distribution $p(x|z, D)$ using $\{e_m\}_{m=0}^M$ (including $e_0$).
5. Calculate the entropy of this distribution as the generative uncertainty $u(z)$.

This approach provides a flexible and efficient way to quantify the variability in the semantic representation of samples generated from different parameterizations of the same model, effectively signaling when the model "disagrees" about the output for a given input noise, thus indicating low quality.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments thoroughly demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed generative uncertainty framework across synthetic and natural image datasets, comparing it against established baselines and ablations.

#### 4.1. Toy Demonstration (2D Gaussian Dataset)

- **Setup:** A small diffusion model was trained on a 2D dataset with 25 distinct Gaussian modes, a common setup to illustrate "hallucinations" (samples between modes).
- **Result:** Applying generative uncertainty to filter out the 50% most uncertain samples successfully removed all "hallucinated" (poor-quality) generations that appeared between the true data modes (Figure 2).
- **Significance:** This initial experiment qualitatively validates the core hypothesis: high generative uncertainty correlates with undesirable, out-of-distribution generations, even without sophisticated approximations like Laplace or semantic likelihood.

#### 4.2. Detecting Low-Quality Generations on Natural Images (ImageNet)

- **Setup:** Experiments were conducted on ImageNet using large UViT and ADM diffusion models. 12,000 samples were generated, and uncertainty estimates were computed for each. Subsets of `n` samples (from 6K to 11K) with the lowest uncertainty were selected and evaluated using FID, Precision, and Recall.
- **Baselines:** Compared against a random selection baseline, existing uncertainty-based methods (BayesDiff, Aleatoric Uncertainty (AU)), and non-uncertainty-based methods (Realism Score, Rarity Score).
- **Key Quantitative Results (Figures 3 & 7):**
 - **Superiority over Existing UQ Methods:** The proposed generative uncertainty significantly outperformed BayesDiff and AU in terms of image quality metrics (FID and Precision). For instance, on UViT, filtering to 10K images yielded FID 7.89, compared to 9.45 (Random), 9.16 (BayesDiff), and 9.20 (AU). This indicates that the proposed method is more effective at identifying and filtering out perceptually poor samples.
 - **Precision-Recall Trade-off:** While improving quality (Precision), filtering resulted in a slight drop in diversity (Recall). This is attributed to the filtering process potentially skewing the class distribution (Figure 16). This trade-off is common in quality-enhancing methods (e.g., classifier-free guidance).
- **Key Qualitative Results (Figures 4, 8, 9, 10):**
 - **Strong Correlation with Visual Quality (Figure 4):** Samples with high generative uncertainty exhibited numerous artifacts and were visually incoherent, often difficult to discern. Conversely, samples with low uncertainty were consistently high-quality and appeared as "canonical" examples of their conditioned class.
 - **Importance of Semantic Likelihood (Figure 10):** An ablation study showed that computing uncertainty directly in pixel space resulted in estimates sensitive to background clutter, similar to BayesDiff, and yielded worse FID/Precision. This strongly supports the necessity of the proposed semantic likelihood using CLIP features for perceptually meaningful uncertainty.
 - **Contrast with BayesDiff (Figures 8 & 9):** BayesDiff's uncertainty was shown to be less informative of visual quality, often being overly sensitive to background complexity rather than core semantic content.

#### 4.3. Comparison with Realism and Rarity Scores

- **Performance:** Generative uncertainty performed on par with (and sometimes slightly better than) realism and rarity scores in terms of FID (Figures 13 & 14).
- **Complementary Information:** Generative uncertainty exhibited a weaker correlation with realism and rarity scores (Spearman correlation < 0.4), unlike the strong negative correlation between realism and rarity themselves (> -0.8) (Figure 15). This suggests that generative uncertainty captures different aspects of sample quality.
- **Ensemble Benefits:** Combining generative uncertainty with either realism or rarity scores yielded the best FID performance (e.g., 7.60 for Ours + Realism on UViT, compared to 8.26 for Realism + Rarity) (Table 1). This confirms the complementary nature of the proposed metric.

#### 4.4. Improving Sampling Efficiency

- **Overhead:** The method typically incurred an overhead of M*T additional network function evaluations (NFEs), where M is Monte Carlo samples and T is diffusion steps. For M=5, T=50, this means 250 additional NFEs.
- **Optimization:** Reducing the number of Monte Carlo samples `M` (e.g., to M=1) and using fewer sampling steps `T` for the uncertainty-estimation samples (while keeping original `T` for the main sample) significantly reduced the overhead. With M=1 and T=25, the method still achieved competitive FID (10.36 on ADM, outperforming baselines) with only 25 additional NFEs, a 10x reduction (Figure 6).

#### 4.5. Applicability to Flow Matching Models

- **Demonstration:** The framework was successfully applied to a latent flow matching model with a DiT backbone.
- **Result:** Similar to diffusion models, generative uncertainty effectively identified and filtered out low-quality samples, achieving FID of 10.48 (vs. 11.80 random) and precision of 64.71 (vs. 61.04 random) on a filtered set of 10K images (Figure 17).
- **Significance:** This demonstrates the broader applicability of the generative uncertainty framework beyond just diffusion models, suggesting it's a general concept for assessing sample quality in continuous normalizing flow-based generative models.

In summary, the results overwhelmingly support the paper's claims: generative uncertainty, especially when estimated with last-layer Laplace and semantic likelihood, is a powerful and practical tool for identifying and filtering poor-quality samples from modern generative models.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces a significant advancement in the reliability and practical utility of state-of-the-art generative models. Its significance and potential impact can be multifaceted:

1. **Enabling Reliable Deployment of Generative Models:** The primary impact is providing a principled, automated mechanism to detect and filter low-quality generations. This is crucial for deploying generative AI systems in sensitive applications where erroneous or artifact-ridden outputs are unacceptable (e.g., medical imaging, design, content creation). By ensuring a higher average quality of samples, it reduces the need for costly and time-consuming manual human inspection, leading to better user experiences and more efficient workflows.

2. **Bridging Bayesian Inference and Generative AI:** The paper successfully translates established Bayesian principles of uncertainty quantification from predictive modeling to the complex domain of deep generative models. This is a foundational contribution that could inspire further research at this intersection. It demonstrates how parameter uncertainty (epistemic uncertainty) can be effectively leveraged to assess output quality in a generative context.

3. **Practical Scalability and Post-Hoc Applicability:** The judicious use of the last-layer Laplace approximation makes the method computationally tractable for extremely large generative models. Its post-hoc nature, allowing application to any pre-trained diffusion or flow matching model without modification or re-training, is a major advantage. This makes it immediately usable for a vast ecosystem of existing generative models.

4. **Semantic Awareness:** The introduction of "semantic likelihood" is a key innovation. By shifting uncertainty computation from raw pixel space to a perceptually meaningful latent space (e.g., CLIP embeddings), the method ensures that the uncertainty estimates align well with human judgment of image quality. This addresses a critical limitation of previous pixel-space based uncertainty measures.

5. **Competitive and Complementary Performance:** The method's empirical performance, outperforming prior uncertainty-based filtering techniques and being competitive with established non-uncertainty-based metrics (realism, rarity scores), solidifies its utility. Furthermore, the demonstration that generative uncertainty captures distinct aspects of sample quality (evidenced by low correlation with realism/rarity) and can synergistically combine with these metrics for even better results opens new avenues for multi-faceted quality assessment.

6. **Efficiency Optimizations:** The analysis of sampling overhead and proposed solutions (reducing Monte Carlo samples M and sampling steps T for uncertainty computation) make the framework practical for real-time or high-throughput scenarios, addressing a key concern for industrial adoption.

7. **Generalizability:** The successful application to flow matching models beyond diffusion models indicates that the underlying principles of generative uncertainty and the proposed methodology are broadly applicable to a class of continuous normalizing flow-based generative models.

**Potential Future Impact and Research Directions:**

- **Beyond Natural Images:** The current reliance on CLIP limits applicability to other modalities (molecules, text, audio) where diffusion models are used. Future work could explore modality-agnostic semantic feature extraction or alternative likelihoods, potentially by leveraging insights from self-supervised learning on latent spaces within diffusion models themselves.
- **Deeper Bayesian Treatment:** Exploring more sophisticated approximate Bayesian inference techniques beyond diagonal last-layer Laplace (e.g., full Laplace, SWAG, deeper layer approximations, or optimizing hyperparameters) could potentially yield even more informative uncertainty estimates.
- **Guiding Generation:** Uncertainty could be used not just for filtering but also for *guiding* the generation process itself (e.g., by steering away from high-uncertainty regions in the latent space during sampling) or for active learning to improve model training.
- **Detecting Memorization:** Generative uncertainty might also be instrumental in detecting memorized training samples, a critical concern for privacy and intellectual property in synthetic data generation.
- **Theoretical Connections:** Further exploration of the theoretical connection between uncertainty-based filtering and other quality-enhancing techniques like classifier-free guidance, given their similar precision-recall trade-offs, could lead to a deeper understanding of generative model behavior.

In conclusion, "Generative Uncertainty in Diffusion Models" provides a robust, practical, and theoretically grounded solution to a pressing problem in generative AI. By enabling automated quality assessment and filtering, it paves the way for more reliable, trustworthy, and widely deployable generative AI systems, impacting various fields from creative industries to scientific research.
