# A4A: Cross-Embodiment Transfer of Action-Oriented 4D Affordances from Human Demonstrations

> [!summary] Summary
> A4A pretrains vision-language-action policies on action-oriented 4D affordances. These are language-conditioned future 3D trajectories of points on the manipulated object, tool, or contacted part, taken from over 80K human interaction clips. Each policy's state and action interfaces are swapped for point inputs and point-motion targets during pretraining, then restored for robot finetuning. Gains hold across five VLA families, RLBench, and real-world tasks.

## Problem

- Existing affordance representations such as 2D masks, 3D regions, contact points, and actionability scores only say where to interact, not how the interaction geometry should move over time.
- Prior human-video transfer methods rely on high-level abstractions like subtasks, key states, or constraints, or need robot data to bridge the embodiment gap between human and robot motion.
- 2D point tracks and flow lack metric 3D structure and suffer viewpoint ambiguity, while generic 4D scene-motion pretraining supervises unrelated scene regions instead of the task-relevant interaction.

## Method

- ==Action-oriented 4D affordance==: a language-conditioned trajectory field over 3D query points, motivated by the short-horizon SE(3) correspondence between end-effector displacement and the motion of attached interaction points.
- 4D affordance dataset pipeline: over 80K clips from HOI4D, EPIC-Kitchens, and about 30K in-house RGB-D demos, processed with GroundingDINO, SAM 2, CoTracker3, and refined depth into identity-preserving 3D point trajectories.
- ==Prediction-space substitution==: pretrain with a point projector and 4D trajectory head under the base VLA's native objective, then swap in a state projector and action head while keeping the shared latent weights.

## Results

- On LIBERO-Object, **A4A** raises average success for all five VLAs: Octo from **28.2%** to **57.2%**, OpenVLA and π0 by **10.0** points, OpenVLA-OFT by **0.6**, and π0.5 by **2.0**.
- On four RLBench tasks the RGB-only policy climbs from **39.0%** to **61.0%** average success, beating ManiGaussian at **51.0%** and ARM4R at **41.0%**, which both use explicit 3D or 4D observations.
- The same ARM4R architecture pretrained on A4A data instead of scene-wide tracks rises from **41.0%** to **60.0%**, and real-world Piper tasks improve mean success from **56.7%** to **72.5%** across four VLAs.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - What transfers from human video is the geometric effect of manipulation, meaning how task-relevant points move, not human arm motion and not static contact regions alone.
> - Restricting pretraining supervision to interaction-relevant points beats generic scene-wide motion under a matched architecture, so choosing which geometry to supervise matters, not just data scale.
> - Reusing the base policy's own objective and latent module for auxiliary pretraining avoids objective mismatch, making one prior pluggable across autoregressive, regression, diffusion, and flow-matching policies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

Pouring rice takes more than knowing where to grasp. The rice container moves through a task-specific path, including a change in orientation. A robot must reproduce that physical change without copying the person’s arm movements. A4A teaches a robot policy to predict how task-relevant 3D points on an object, tool, or contacted part will move, then adapts the policy to the robot’s own controls. The prior for those point motions comes from human demonstrations. In real-world tests, adding this human-derived pretraining improved the average success rate across four policies and six physical tasks from 56.7% to 72.5%.

The authors’ physical “Pour rice” demo, described as a Piper-arm skill. It shows the robot task, not the human pretraining demonstration.

### 2. From “where” to “how”

A static affordance can say *where* to touch, but not how the interaction should unfold. A drawer handle’s location does not specify how far to pull it. Local points on the handle moving outward convey more of the operation.

A4A captures this with an **action-oriented 4D affordance**: a language-conditioned prediction of future positions for 3D points sampled from the interaction-relevant geometry. The initial points locate the relevant geometry, and their trajectories encode its task-conditioned motion.

Robot demonstrations are costly, while human videos are plentiful. A4A uses the human videos to supply an interaction prior, then still adapts on robot demonstrations. The human corpus contains more than 80K interaction clips, and about 30K of them are in-house RGB-D demonstrations.

The link between human and robot motion is geometric. The correspondence is exact for points rigidly attached to a robot’s gripper, tool, or grasped object. For broader interaction geometry, it is only a short-horizon local approximation. This premise does not establish that human and robot actions are interchangeable.

In Stage I, vision-language input and query-point coordinates pass through a point projector and shared latent representation to a future 4D trajectory head. Stage II restores robot state and action interfaces around the retained shared representation. The example instruction is “Pick up the cup,” and short-horizon SE(3) motion is depicted as the geometric link.

Training has two stages. Pretraining replaces robot-state input with interaction-point coordinates and replaces robot-action targets with predicted point trajectories. Adaptation restores the policy’s native state and action interfaces while retaining and fine-tuning its learned representation. This is A4A’s proposed transfer mechanism, not direct imitation of human motor commands.

### 3. Interaction-centric versus generic tracks

The closest prior work, **ARM4R**, pretrains on generic scene-wide 3D point tracks from human video and transfers the learned representation to robot control ([[2502.13142|Niu et al., *Pre-training Auto-regressive Robotic Models with 4D Representations*]]). A4A instead predicts points on the manipulated object, tool, or contacted part.

The paper tested this distinction in RLBench. Both variants use the ARM4R architecture, robot demonstrations, fine-tuning recipe, and evaluation protocol. The comparison is ARM4R’s released generic-track initialization versus pretraining from scratch on A4A’s corpus. Mean task success rose from 41% to 60%.

Successful rate (%) for ARM4R (Original Pretrain, gray) and ARM4R (4D-Affordance Pretrain, green). Results were 68/84 on Meat off grill, 48/64 on Sweep to Dustpan, 28/60 on Turn tap, and 20/32 on Slide Block to Target. No error bars are shown.

This matched comparison supports the claim that A4A’s pretraining data and supervision provide a stronger prior. It does **not** isolate point selection as the sole cause. The pretraining data and supervision differ together, and the A4A variant is trained from scratch rather than from ARM4R’s released initialization.

### 4. What transfers

In the physical evaluation, four policies were tested on six tasks on an AgileX Piper arm, with ten rollouts per task. The overall mean across policies increased from 56.7% to 72.5%. This is consistent with transfer beyond simulation.

Adaptation remains part of the method: the physical evaluation used 100 teleoperated demonstrations per skill. The short-horizon correspondence also has limits. The authors identify large non-rigid deformation, severe slippage, repeated contact switching, and complex bimanual work as limitations. For example, multi-turn cap opening with repeated release and regrasp is not modeled.
