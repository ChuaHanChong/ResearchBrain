# Self-Evolving Search Index

> [!summary] Summary
> SELF-INDEX lets a retrieval index evolve without humans. An LLM Optimizer diagnoses retrieval shortfalls from co-retrieval patterns, selectively rewrites only the affected documents' index keys, and accepts revisions only if they pass faithfulness, specificity, and separation checks, while a Query Simulator proactively generates novel queries to drive evolution. It raises BRIGHT nDCG@10 by 39-57% relative across three retrievers and improves search agents and agent-memory retrieval.

## Problem

- Fixed index-optimization strategies such as pseudo-query expansion, summaries, or scenario profiles perform inconsistently across corpora and retrievers, sometimes degrading retrieval, e.g., Doc2Query hurting code corpora.
- Adapting an index to its environment stays human-driven: people must diagnose which keys cause failures and redesign strategies or collect new relevance annotations to retrain learned optimizers.
- Revised strategies are reapplied to every document, reprocessing the whole index at high compute cost even when only a small subset of keys causes retrieval shortfalls.

## Method

- **Self-Diagnosis** builds ==co-retrieval profiles==, recording which other documents' keys are retrieved alongside each key, and an LLM uses them as ==pseudo-relevance feedback== to identify under-distinguishing keys, without relevance labels.
- **Self-Revision** rewrites each flagged document's key set jointly (max 10 keys, original text fixed); **Self-Validation** keeps only keys passing ==Faithfulness== (LLM judge), ==Specificity== (key retrieves its source), and ==Separation== (lower competitor similarity).
- **Query Simulator** performs **Self-Exploration**: it abstracts a sampled document's underlying need, writes lay-language queries without seeing the document, and filters them by ==Answerability== and ==Jaccard Dissimilarity== before feeding the Optimizer.

## Results

- On BRIGHT, overall nDCG@10 rises from **14.5** to **20.4** (BM25), **13.9** to **21.8** (BGE), and **18.8** to **26.1** (Qwen3-Emb-8B), versus best baselines of **15.8**, **15.4**, and **21.2**.
- On BrowseComp-Plus, GPT-OSS-120B+BM25 accuracy rises from **31.08** to **58.92** with **21%** fewer search calls; LongMemEval-V2 memory accuracy improves **9.2-13.9%** across three memory systems.
- Removing Self-Validation drops average nDCG@10 below the base index in every corpus type (natural language **26.6** to **16.1**); removing co-retrieval profiles costs up to **6.9** points.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Retrieval outcomes alone, via co-retrieval patterns, provide enough label-free feedback to repair document representations, reviving pseudo-relevance feedback as an index-side rather than query-side signal.
> - Unvalidated LLM self-edits are harmful: accepting every proposed revision makes the index worse than doing nothing, so cheap retrieval-grounded acceptance tests are what make self-evolution safe.
> - Selective, feedback-targeted revision beats uniform corpus-wide enrichment on both quality and cost, and index quality matters more as corpora grow, where index-free direct corpus interaction degrades sharply.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

“Self-Evolving Search Index” addresses a problem at the intersection of information retrieval, large language model (LLM) agents, and autonomous system improvement. Modern retrieval systems generally represent each document with one or more index keys, such as the original text, summaries, generated queries, scenarios, propositions, or other semantic descriptions. A retriever then matches user queries against these keys to rank documents.

Recent research has shown that enriching document representations can improve retrieval, particularly for queries requiring reasoning rather than simple lexical matching. Approaches such as Doc2Query generate likely queries for documents, while methods such as SPIKE and EnrichIndex create scenario-based or multi-view representations. Other methods, including RL-Index and AutoIndex, learn or optimize representation strategies from relevance data. However, these methods typically rely on a fixed representation strategy, manually designed prompts, annotated data, or broad reprocessing of the corpus.

The paper identifies two limitations in this research direction. First, an optimization strategy that works for one corpus or retriever may perform poorly for another. Natural-language documents, code, mathematical text, and database schemas expose different retrieval challenges, and sparse and dense retrievers respond differently to the same representation. Second, existing systems generally do not autonomously diagnose retrieval failures and revise only the representations responsible for those failures.

The proposed framework also relates to recent work on self-evolving reasoning models and agents. Those systems use model-generated supervision, interaction, or self-play to improve performance without extensive human annotation. This paper transfers the self-evolution concept from models and agents to the search index itself. It further connects index optimization with agent memory, where past interactions are stored and later retrieved to support future tasks.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

The central objective is to develop an index that can adapt to its retrieval environment without requiring humans to redesign the representation strategy. The authors argue that index quality depends not only on the information contained in a document but also on how that information is expressed in searchable keys.

The work has several specific goals:

1. **Automate retrieval-failure diagnosis.** The system should identify when current index keys fail to expose relevant information or fail to distinguish a document from competing documents.

2. **Revise only affected index entries.** Instead of regenerating representations for every document whenever the optimization strategy changes, the system should update only document key sets associated with diagnosed shortfalls.

3. **Avoid dependence on a predefined representation format.** The system should determine whether a document needs a summary, a question-like representation, a use-case description, a technical reformulation, or another form of key.

4. **Validate revisions before incorporating them.** LLM-generated keys may contain unsupported claims, generic content, or wording that increases confusion with other documents. The framework therefore needs safeguards against harmful revisions.

5. **Expand beyond observed queries.** If the index only responds to queries already submitted by users, its evolution will remain narrow. The authors therefore introduce a query simulator to generate plausible, previously uncovered information needs.

6. **Improve downstream agent behavior.** The authors investigate whether better retrieval improves not only ranking metrics but also the accuracy, search efficiency, and memory use of LLM agents.

The motivation is therefore both methodological and practical. The proposed approach aims to reduce human labor, avoid repeated full-corpus processing, and support retrieval systems that operate across changing corpora, retrievers, and task demands.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Index Representation and Retrieval

For each document \(d\), the index contains a set of keys \(K(d)\). The original document text is always retained as a fixed key. Additional keys are generated and revised over time. For a query \(q\), the document score is the maximum relevance between the query and any key belonging to that document:

\[
s(q,d)=\max_{k\in K(d)} \mathrm{rel}(q,k).
\]

The relevance function depends on the retriever, such as BM25 or embedding cosine similarity. Using maximum-score aggregation avoids the need to tune weights between original text and generated representations.

#### 3.2. Optimizer

The Optimizer operates in three stages.

##### 3.2.1. Self-Diagnosis

For a batch of queries, the system retrieves the top 30 keys from the current index. It records which keys from different documents are retrieved together and how frequently they co-occur. These co-retrieval profiles provide information about competing representations.

An LLM then examines each retrieved document, its current keys, the queries that retrieved it, relevance scores, and co-retrieval information. It determines whether the current representations have a shortfall, identifies the responsible key or key set, and describes what should change. The Optimizer does not require relevance labels or human annotations.

##### 3.2.2. Self-Revision

Documents with diagnosed shortfalls become revision targets. The Optimizer revises the entire key set for a target document rather than editing individual keys independently. This helps avoid redundancy among keys that represent the same document.

The LLM receives the source document, current keys, diagnosis, and competing keys. It may retain useful keys, remove or rewrite problematic keys, and create new keys that reflect how potential users might search for the document. Each document can have at most ten keys, including the fixed original-text key.

##### 3.2.3. Self-Validation

Proposed generated keys are accepted only if they satisfy three criteria:

- **Faithfulness:** The key expresses an information need that the source document actually addresses. An LLM judge assigns a score from 0 to 3, and a score of at least 2 is required.
- **Specificity:** The key should describe information specific to the source document rather than generic content shared by many documents. The key is tested as a query against the corpus and must retrieve its source document within a specified rank threshold.
- **Separation:** The proposed key should be less similar to observed competing keys than the current key set is. This is intended to reduce ambiguity between related documents.

Keys that fail any criterion are removed. A proposed revision is incorporated only if at least one newly proposed key passes all checks.

#### 3.3. Query Simulator

The Query Simulator enables proactive rather than purely reactive evolution. It samples documents from the corpus and first asks an LLM to describe the underlying problem or information need in general language, without using specialized terms from the document. A second LLM call converts that abstraction into user-like questions without seeing the original document.

Generated queries are retained if the sampled document can answer them according to an answerability judge. The system also applies a Jaccard-similarity filter with a threshold of 0.8 to reduce lexical overlap with previously used queries and with other newly generated queries. This encourages exploration of different phrasings and information needs.

In the main experiments, the Optimizer and Query Simulator use Qwen3.6-35B-A3B. Each run uses batches of 128 optimization queries for 20 iterations, totaling 2,560 queries. Evaluation queries are withheld during index evolution.

#### 3.4. Experimental Evaluation

Retrieval is evaluated on BRIGHT, covering natural language, code, and mathematics, and on Spider 2.0, FIBEN, and BEAVER for table retrieval. The primary metric is nDCG@10. Comparisons include Doc2Query, SPIKE, RL-Index, and EnrichIndex where applicable. Retrievers include BM25, BGE-Large, and Qwen3-Embedding-8B.

Downstream evaluation uses BrowseComp-Plus, which measures search-agent answer accuracy, evidence recall, number of search calls, and calibration error. Agent memory is evaluated using LongMemEval-V2 Small with three memory designs and fixed stored memory contents.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Retrieval Performance

SELF-INDEX produces consistent gains across the tested corpora and retrievers. On BRIGHT, the overall nDCG@10 scores are:

- BM25: **14.5** for the base index and **20.4** with SELF-INDEX, a relative gain of **40.4%**.
- BGE-Large: **13.9** to **21.8**, a relative gain of **57.0%**.
- Qwen3-Embedding-8B: **18.8** to **26.1**, a relative gain of **38.8%**.

The method also achieves the highest average score for each corpus type under each retriever. Competing approaches are less stable. For example, Doc2Query improves some table results but can reduce performance on code corpora, while SPIKE and RL-Index provide smaller or inconsistent gains.

On table retrieval, SELF-INDEX reaches average scores of **49.5** with BM25, **53.1** with BGE-Large, and **57.0** with Qwen3-Embedding-8B. These correspond to improvements of 49.1%, 17.9%, and 16.2% over the respective base indexes.

#### 4.2. Search-Agent Performance

On BrowseComp-Plus, SELF-INDEX improves answer accuracy and evidence recall across all evaluated agent backbones and retrievers. For example, with GPT-5.4-nano and BM25, answer accuracy rises from **36.51%** to **64.94%**, while average search calls fall from **19.23** to **16.00**. With Kimi-K2.5 and BM25, accuracy increases from **50.96%** to **71.93%**, and search calls decrease from **33.98** to **29.00**.

The framework generally reduces calibration error and search frequency relative to the base index. The authors estimate that these reductions translate into lower online LLM costs. In the corpus-scale experiment, SELF-INDEX maintains stable accuracy as the corpus expands from 100,000 to 400,000 documents, whereas SPIKE’s accuracy declines and its online cost increases.

#### 4.3. Agent Memory

Applying SELF-INDEX to memory keys improves answer accuracy while leaving the stored memory contents unchanged. Overall accuracy increases from:

- **0.415 to 0.472** for Query-to-Slice memory, a 13.9% improvement.
- **0.448 to 0.503** for Query-to-Slice plus Notes, a 12.4% improvement.
- **0.532 to 0.581** for AgentRunbook-R, a 9.2% improvement.

Benefits are strongest for static, dynamic, and workflow questions. The gotchas category shows little or no improvement, which the authors attribute to its greater dependence on how useful information is constructed and stored rather than merely retrieved.

#### 4.4. Ablation Findings

Removing co-retrieval profiles substantially reduces performance, indicating that diagnosis benefits from information about competing keys. Removing Self-Validation causes the largest degradation: performance falls below the base index across all BRIGHT corpus types. Removing any one of faithfulness, specificity, or separation also lowers scores.

Removing the query simulator’s dissimilarity filter reduces retrieval performance, supporting the value of exploring non-redundant information needs. Larger LLM backbones generally improve index evolution, although parameter count alone does not determine performance. The 35B model used in the main experiments outperforms both smaller and larger tested alternatives on the reported average.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The paper’s main significance is its treatment of the index as an adaptive component rather than a static preprocessing artifact. SELF-INDEX provides a mechanism for combining retrieval feedback, LLM-based representation generation, selective updates, and validation in a repeated loop.

If the reported results generalize, the approach could reduce the human effort required to adapt retrieval systems to new domains, retrievers, and agent workflows. Its selective revision strategy may also reduce offline computation relative to methods that generate representations for every document. The reported cost analysis shows that SELF-INDEX can exceed SPIKE’s retrieval performance while using lower cumulative LLM cost in several evaluated settings.

The framework is also potentially useful for search agents and long-term memory systems, where retrieval quality affects both final answer quality and the number of online search operations. However, its effectiveness depends on the quality, cost, and reliability of the LLM used for diagnosis, generation, and judging. Synthetic query generation may miss important real-world demands, and the validation criteria rely partly on the same retrieval model being optimized. The experiments are broad but remain benchmark-based, and further work would be needed to assess long-term stability, index drift, multilingual settings, privacy-sensitive corpora, and operational costs at much larger scale.
