# Self-Supervised Learning with Gaussian Processes

> [!summary] Summary
> This work introduces Gaussian Process Self-Supervised Learning (GPSSL), a framework that integrates Gaussian Processes and generalized Bayesian inference to learn data representations without relying on explicit data augmentation. It explicitly quantifies uncertainty in these representations, leading to improved performance in downstream classification tasks, particularly for tabular and spatial transcriptomics data.

## Problem

- Most Self-Supervised Learning (SSL) methods heavily depend on data augmentation for positive pair generation, limiting their applicability to diverse data types like tabular data, time series, or text where augmentations are difficult to define.
- Existing SSL approaches produce deterministic point estimates for representations, lacking uncertainty quantification, which leads to overconfidence and hinders their utility in risk-sensitive applications.
- Existing probabilistic SSL variants that add uncertainty quantification (e.g., BYOV, VI-SimSiam) still depend on robust data augmentation models, leaving them unsuitable for non-image data types.

## Method

- **GPSSL** employs ==Gaussian Processes (GPs)== with a ==generalized Bayesian inference framework== to learn a distribution over ==representation functions== rather than deterministic mappings.
- The ==GP prior's kernel function== intrinsically encodes data similarity, eliminating the need for explicit ==data augmentation== to create positive pairs.
- A ==VICReg-inspired loss function== (variance and covariance terms) is minimized using ==generalized variational inference==, augmented with ==inducing points== for scalability to larger datasets.

## Results

- GPSSL successfully provides meaningful uncertainty quantification, with standard deviations in learned representations varying inversely with training data density on synthetic tasks.
- Across UCI tabular datasets and real-world spatial transcriptomics data, GPSSL-full consistently achieved the best performance in downstream classification based on accuracy, ROC AUC, and Area Under Risk-Coverage (AURC) metrics.
- In spatial transcriptomics applications, GPSSL effectively integrated multi-modal features, produced interpretable cell-type predictions with informative uncertainty maps, and demonstrated the lowest mean squared error for predicted probabilities.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The Gaussian Process prior, through its kernel, implicitly acts as an invariance loss, enforcing smoothness and similarity in representations based on input similarity, thereby removing the need for augmentation-based invariance terms.
> - GPSSL establishes a theoretical connection to classical Kernel PCA, demonstrating that for a 1D representation, its maximum a posteriori estimate is equivalent to the first principal component of Kernel PCA.
> - Propagating the full uncertainty from the learned representation distribution (GPSSL-full) consistently yields superior performance in downstream tasks compared to using only deterministic mean representations (GPSSL-mean).

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated at the forefront of **Self-Supervised Learning (SSL)**, a rapidly evolving field in machine learning. SSL aims to learn robust data representations by leveraging the inherent structure of unlabeled data, without requiring explicit human-provided labels. These learned representations have proven highly effective for various downstream tasks, including clustering, classification, and object detection.

The broader SSL landscape is currently dominated by two main paradigms:
- **Contrastive Learning (e.g., SimCLR, MoCo):** These methods explicitly encourage representations of "positive pairs" (similar objects) to be close in the embedding space while pushing "negative pairs" (dissimilar objects) apart. Positive pairs are typically generated through data augmentation techniques (e.g., cropping, rotation, blurring for images).
- **Non-Contrastive Learning (e.g., BYOL, SimSiam, VICReg):** These methods simplify the process by only requiring positive pairs, avoiding the need for carefully constructed negative pairs. They prevent "representational collapse" (where all inputs map to the same representation) by introducing mechanisms like normalization, stop-gradients, or diversity-promoting loss terms. VICReg, a specific non-contrastive method, is particularly relevant here as GPSSL builds upon its loss function structure.

However, the authors identify two critical limitations of most current SSL approaches:
1. **Reliance on Data Augmentation for Positive Pairs:** While effective for image data where augmentations are well-understood, generating meaningful positive pairs is challenging for many other data types, such as tabular data, time series, text, or graph-structured data. This restricts the applicability of current SSL methods.
2. **Lack of Uncertainty Quantification:** Most SSL methods learn a deterministic mapping from observations to representations. This means they do not provide estimates of uncertainty associated with the learned representations, which can lead to overconfidence in predictions, especially for out-of-sample data, and limits their utility in risk-sensitive applications.

Existing probabilistic SSL variants (e.g., BYOV, VI-SimSiam, SimVAE) have attempted to address the uncertainty gap by using Bayesian Neural Networks or variational inference. However, these still largely depend on the availability of robust data augmentation models, making them more suitable for high-dimensional data like images.

GPSSL directly addresses these limitations. By leveraging **Gaussian Processes (GPs)** and **kernel methods**, it offers a novel approach that:
- **Eliminates the need for explicit positive pair generation:** The inherent covariance function of GPs naturally encodes similarity based on a defined kernel, providing a mechanism for smoothness without augmentation. This expands SSL's applicability to diverse data types where kernel functions can be more readily defined than augmentations.
- **Provides explicit uncertainty quantification:** As a probabilistic model, GPSSL yields a distribution over representations, allowing for the quantification and propagation of uncertainty to downstream tasks.

Furthermore, the paper establishes important theoretical connections. It demonstrates how GPSSL relates to **Kernel PCA**, a classical dimensionality reduction technique, and draws parallels between non-contrastive SSL methods like VICReg and kernel-based approaches. This linkage contributes to a deeper theoretical understanding of SSL, complementing existing work on spectral interpretations of these methods. In essence, GPSSL broadens the scope of SSL, grounds it more firmly in probabilistic modeling, and offers a mechanism for more robust and transparent representation learning, particularly for structured and tabular data.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations behind the development of Gaussian Process Self-Supervised Learning (GPSSL) are clearly articulated by the authors, stemming from observed gaps in current Self-Supervised Learning (SSL) methodologies:

1. **Overcome the Dependency on Explicit Positive Pair Generation:** A central motivation is to address the challenge that most existing SSL methods, both contrastive and non-contrastive, necessitate the generation of "positive pairs" through data augmentation. While this paradigm has seen immense success in image processing, defining meaningful and effective augmentations for other data types—such as tabular data, time series, text, or structured graphs—remains a significant hurdle. The authors seek a method that can inherently enforce similarity among related data points without relying on such explicit augmentation strategies.

2. **Incorporate Robust Uncertainty Quantification:** Traditional SSL methods typically produce deterministic point estimates for representations, offering no insight into the confidence or variability associated with these embeddings. This lack of uncertainty quantification is a major limitation, particularly when representations are used in downstream tasks where reliable predictions and risk assessment are critical. The authors are motivated to develop an SSL approach that provides a full distribution over representations, thereby allowing uncertainty to be captured and propagated through to subsequent analyses.

3. **Enhance Out-of-Sample Prediction Performance and Error Control:** Building on the previous point, the absence of uncertainty quantification can lead to poor performance and overconfidence when making predictions on data far from the training distribution. By explicitly modeling uncertainty, the authors aim to improve the robustness and reliability of representations for out-of-sample predictions and enable better error control in downstream applications.

4. **Establish Theoretical Connections to Foundational Methods:** The work is also motivated by a desire to bridge the gap between modern neural network-based SSL methods and more classical machine learning techniques. By demonstrating connections to kernel methods like Kernel PCA and to popular SSL frameworks like VICReg, the authors aim to provide deeper theoretical insights into how SSL works and to position GPSSL within a broader spectrum of representation learning techniques.

In summary, the core motivation is to create a more versatile, robust, and transparent SSL framework—one that is not constrained by the need for data augmentation, quantifies uncertainty, and offers improved performance in diverse application settings, especially for data types where current SSL struggles.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

GPSSL proposes a novel approach to self-supervised learning by integrating Gaussian Processes (GPs) with a generalized Bayesian inference framework, building upon principles from non-contrastive SSL methods.

#### 3.1. The GPSSL Model Formulation

The core idea is to learn a mapping `f_z: X → R^J` from an observation space `X` to a low-dimensional representation space `R^J`. Instead of learning a deterministic mapping, GPSSL learns a *distribution* over such functions.

1. **Gaussian Process Prior:** A Gaussian Process prior `f_z(·) ∼ GP(m(·), k(·, ·))` is placed on the representation function `f_z`. The mean function `m(x)` is typically set to zero. The crucial component is the **covariance function (kernel) k(·, ·)**. This kernel, defined on the input space `X`, intrinsically encodes assumptions about pairwise similarities between observations. By doing so, it naturally enforces a "smoothness" or "invariance" inductive bias on the representations: if two observations are similar in the input space (as defined by the kernel), their representations will also be similar. This mechanism *replaces* the need for explicitly generating "positive pairs" through data augmentation, a key differentiator from most existing SSL methods.

2. **Generalized Bayesian Posterior:** Since SSL operates on unlabeled data, a traditional likelihood model `p(Y|Z, X)` is not available. GPSSL adopts **generalized Bayesian inference** (Bissiri et al. 2016, Knoblauch et al. 2019), where the likelihood is replaced by an arbitrary loss function `ℓ(Z)`. The generalized posterior is then defined as `˜p(f_z | X) ∝ p(f_z) exp{−ℓ(Z := f_z(X))}`.

3. **VICReg-Inspired Loss Function:** The loss function `ℓ(Z)` is inspired by VICReg, a prominent non-contrastive SSL method. It comprises two main terms:
 - **Variance Loss (ℓ_var(Z)):** Encourages diversity among representations by penalizing low standard deviations for each dimension of the representation. This prevents representational collapse.
 - **Covariance Loss (ℓ_cov(Z)):** Decorrelates the dimensions of the representation, ensuring that different dimensions capture independent aspects of the data, further preventing information collapse due to correlated variables.
 - **Exclusion of Invariance Loss:** Notably, GPSSL *excludes* VICReg's explicit invariance loss (which encourages positive pairs to have similar representations). This is because the GP prior, through its kernel function, intrinsically fulfills this role by enforcing smoothness based on input similarity.

#### 3.2. Approximating the Generalized Bayesian Posterior

To make inference tractable, the authors use **generalized variational inference** (Knoblauch et al., 2019). This involves finding a distribution `q*(f_z)` from a chosen variational family `Q` that minimizes the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence to the generalized posterior, modified to include the expected loss term:
`q*(f_z) = argmin_{q∈Q} { E_{q(f_z)}[ℓ(Z)] + KL(q || p) }`
To handle scalability, particularly for large datasets, the model is augmented with **inducing points**, a common technique in scalable GP methods (Hensman et al., 2015). This allows approximating the posterior over latent variables `U_z` corresponding to these inducing points, and then computing expectations of the loss function using Monte Carlo sampling.

#### 3.3. Relationship to Other Models

1. **GP Prior as an Invariance Loss:** The paper formally explains how the GP prior implicitly acts as an invariance loss. For a specially constructed kernel that defines positive pairs (Equation 5), the log-joint of `Z` under the GP prior effectively becomes a loss function that encourages similarity between positive pairs, similar to existing SSL methods. This is why the explicit invariance term from VICReg can be omitted.

2. **Connection to Kernel PCA:** A significant theoretical contribution is demonstrating a connection between GPSSL and Kernel PCA. For a one-dimensional representation (`J=1`) and a specific variance loss modification, the maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimate of the GPSSL generalized posterior is shown to be equivalent to the first principal component obtained by Kernel PCA. For `J > 1`, the covariance loss in GPSSL serves a similar, albeit softer, role to the orthogonality constraint in Kernel PCA, encouraging decorrelation among representation dimensions. This establishes a direct link between probabilistic SSL and classical kernel-based dimensionality reduction.

#### 3.4. Using Representations in Downstream Tasks

GPSSL offers two ways to utilize the learned representations for downstream tasks:
1. **GPSSL-mean:** Use the mean of the learned representation distribution as a deterministic embedding, similar to outputs from traditional SSL methods.
2. **GPSSL-full:** Propagate the full uncertainty from the representation distribution. This is done by sampling multiple representations from `˜p(Z|X)` and then marginalizing over these samples when computing `p(Y|X)` for a downstream task. This approach leverages the key strength of GPSSL in uncertainty quantification.

The chosen kernel for experiments is the **squared exponential kernel**, with lengthscales determined by nearest neighbors, demonstrating a flexible and data-driven approach to defining similarity.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrate GPSSL's effectiveness across synthetic, tabular, and real-world spatial transcriptomics datasets, consistently highlighting its advantages in uncertainty quantification and performance.

#### 4.1. Qualitative Evaluation on Synthetic Data (Quadrant-Weighted Concentric Circles)

- **Representation Learning:** GPSSL successfully learned representations that captured the underlying circular structure of the data, even with uneven sampling density.
- **Uncertainty Quantification:** Crucially, the standard deviation of the learned representations varied meaningfully across the space. It was lowest in areas with high training data density (e.g., top-right quadrant) and increased as data density decreased or moved into regions with no training data. This validates GPSSL's ability to provide interpretable uncertainty estimates.
- **Comparison:** kPCA yielded mean representations similar to GPSSL but lacked any associated uncertainty. VICReg's representations were smoother but sometimes noisier, and also provided no uncertainty.

#### 4.2. Quantitative Evaluation on Downstream Classification (Concentric Circles)

- **Mean Predictions:** GPSSL-full's mean predictions for a downstream Bayesian logistic regression classifier were comparable to GPSSL-mean and kPCA.
- **Meaningful Uncertainty:** When considering the full distribution, GPSSL-full demonstrated significantly more meaningful uncertainty in classification predictions. Unlike GPSSL-mean and kPCA, which showed overconfidence far from training data, GPSSL-full's uncertainty increased appropriately in sparsely sampled regions and at the edges of the data distribution.
- **Risk-Coverage Curves:** GPSSL-full achieved the highest overall accuracy (lowest risk at 100% coverage) and the lowest Area Under the Risk-Coverage (AURC) curve, indicating superior utility in selective classification tasks where decisions are made with controlled risk.

#### 4.3. Quantitative Evaluation on UCI Tabular Datasets

- **Datasets:** Experiments were conducted on Breast Cancer, Dermatology, Ecoli, and Mice Protein datasets.
- **Performance Metrics:** Accuracy, ROC AUC, and AURC were used to compare GPSSL-mean, GPSSL-full, original data, VICReg, and kPCA.
- **Superiority of GPSSL-full:** GPSSL-full consistently yielded the best performance in most cases across all three metrics. Where it wasn't the absolute best, it was highly competitive.
- **Value of Uncertainty:** In all assessments, GPSSL-full significantly outperformed GPSSL-mean, providing strong empirical evidence that incorporating the full uncertainty from the representation learning process is beneficial for downstream tasks.

#### 4.4. Real Data Application: Spatial Transcriptomics (ST)

1. **Semi-Synthetic PBMC Data:**
 - **High Performance:** GPSSL-full and GPSSL-mean consistently ranked as the best-performing methods in terms of accuracy, ROC AUC, AURC, and critically, the mean squared error of predicted probabilities (pMSE) against true probabilities.
 - **Best Probability Modeling:** GPSSL-full showed the lowest pMSE, indicating its superior ability to model the true underlying cell-type probabilities, which is vital for compositional analysis.
 - **Robustness:** GPSSL demonstrated good risk control in the risk-coverage curve.

2. **Breast Cancer Cell-Type Composition Analysis:**
 - **Multi-Modal Integration:** GPSSL successfully integrated features from principal components of gene expression, 2D spatial coordinates, and RGB values from histology images to learn 5-dimensional representations.
 - **Interpretable Representations and Uncertainty:** The learned representations reflected data structure, and the standard deviation maps showed higher uncertainty at the edges of the data space or where cell types blended, providing valuable context for downstream analysis.
 - **Effective Cell Type Inference:** The complete GPSSL pipeline (embedding + classification) produced smooth and plausible cell-type predictions across the tissue. The uncertainty maps for dominant cell types were informative, increasing in transitional regions or sparsely sampled areas. The ability to report probabilities for different cell types offered a richer understanding of tissue composition.

Overall, the findings strongly support GPSSL as a robust and powerful SSL method, particularly for non-image data. Its ability to quantify and propagate uncertainty provides a distinct advantage, leading to more reliable predictions and better decision-making in downstream applications.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work on "Self-Supervised Learning with Gaussian Processes" presents several significant contributions and holds substantial potential for impact across various domains of machine learning and its applications.

1. **Broadened Applicability of Self-Supervised Learning (SSL):** The most immediate and impactful contribution is GPSSL's ability to perform SSL without relying on explicit data augmentation techniques to generate "positive pairs." This addresses a critical limitation of current SSL methods, which are predominantly designed for image data where augmentations are well-understood. By replacing augmentation with the inherent similarity encoded by a Gaussian Process kernel, GPSSL opens the door for effective SSL on a much wider array of data types, including:
 - **Tabular Data:** A ubiquitous data type in business, healthcare, and science, where defining augmentations is notoriously difficult.
 - **Time Series Data:** Where temporal correlations can be captured by appropriate kernels.
 - **Structured Data (Text, Graphs):** Where kernels can be defined based on linguistic, semantic, or graph-theoretic similarities.
 This significantly expands the utility and reach of SSL paradigms into previously underserved areas.

2. **Introduction of Robust Uncertainty Quantification in SSL:** GPSSL provides a principled framework for quantifying uncertainty in learned representations. Most existing SSL methods yield deterministic embeddings, which lack transparency regarding their confidence. By leveraging generalized Bayesian inference and Gaussian Processes, GPSSL generates a full distribution over representations. This uncertainty can then be propagated to downstream tasks, leading to:
 - **More Reliable Predictions:** Downstream models can account for the inherent uncertainty in the learned features, leading to more calibrated and robust predictions, especially for out-of-sample or novel data points.
 - **Enhanced Risk Assessment:** In critical applications (e.g., medical diagnostics, financial modeling), understanding the confidence of a prediction is as important as the prediction itself. GPSSL enables better risk control by indicating when a model is less certain.
 - **Improved Decision-Making:** Practitioners can make more informed decisions by weighing predictions against their associated uncertainties, potentially deferring ambiguous cases for human review.

3. **Strong Theoretical Grounding and Connections:** The paper strengthens the theoretical understanding of SSL by drawing explicit connections between modern non-contrastive SSL (specifically VICReg) and classical kernel methods like Kernel PCA. This theoretical linkage helps to demystify some aspects of how SSL methods learn effective representations and provides a bridge between different subfields of machine learning. Such connections can inspire new hybrid architectures or lead to more principled design choices in future SSL algorithms.

4. **Demonstrated Efficacy in Real-World Scientific Applications:** The successful application of GPSSL to spatial transcriptomics (ST) data is particularly impactful. ST is a cutting-edge biological technique generating complex, multi-modal data. The ability of GPSSL to integrate gene expression, spatial coordinates, and histology image features, along with providing cell-type composition probabilities and their uncertainties, is highly valuable for biological discovery. It can help researchers:
 - **Better Characterize Tissue Microenvironments:** By providing fine-grained, uncertain-aware cell-type maps.
 - **Uncover Heterogeneous Regions:** Pinpointing areas where cell type assignments are ambiguous, potentially indicating transitional states or mixed populations requiring further investigation.
 - **Improve Downstream Biomedical Analysis:** By providing more robust features for identifying disease biomarkers, predicting treatment responses, or understanding disease progression.

5. **Future Research Directions:** The work also implicitly opens up avenues for future research, such as exploring different kernel functions tailored to specific data types, investigating scalable GP approximations for even larger datasets, and integrating GPSSL into more complex deep learning architectures as a probabilistic embedding layer.

In essence, GPSSL represents a significant step forward in making self-supervised learning more versatile, transparent, and trustworthy. By addressing fundamental limitations of existing methods, it paves the way for broader adoption of SSL across diverse data landscapes and enhances the interpretability and reliability of learned representations in high-stakes applications.
