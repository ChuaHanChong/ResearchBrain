# LocoWM: High-Precision Locomotion through World-Model-Guided Residual Adaptation

> [!summary] Summary
> LocoWM is a preactive residual adaptation framework for high-precision locomotion: a frozen base locomotion policy proposes an action, an action-conditioned world model forecasts a short sequence of future task substates (tilt, acceleration, angular velocity) in one forward pass, and a residual adapter adds corrections for anticipated deviations. On a Go2-W wheeled-legged robot carrying unsecured payloads, it beats end-to-end and reactive residual baselines and transfers zero-shot to hardware.

## Problem

- Joint end-to-end optimization of tracking and precision lets dense tracking rewards dominate sparse precision signals, yielding competent locomotion but insufficiently optimized task-specific precision objectives.
- Observation-conditioned residual controllers such as SEEC and SteadyTray correct only after deviations become observable in current or historical measurements, so they remain inherently reactive.
- MPC-style model-based residuals anticipate deviations using analytical dynamics, but are brittle under model mismatch, unmodeled contact or payload dynamics, and require costly online optimization.

## Method

- ==Task-substate interface==: one physically meaningful low-dimensional quantity (platform tilt, linear acceleration, angular velocity) simultaneously defines the precision reward, the world-model prediction target, and the residual adapter's input.
- ==Action-conditioned world model== maps proprioceptive history plus the proposed base action to a roughly 100 ms future substate chunk in a single forward pass, trained with MSE on base-policy rollouts.
- Two-stage training: Stage 1 learns a ==PPO== base policy on tracking reward alongside the world model; Stage 2 freezes both and trains a ==zero-initialized additive residual adapter== on tracking plus precision rewards.

## Results

- Terrain leveling on Go2-W: slope success reaches **91.1%** versus **61.5%** for current-state reconstruction and **45.3%** for the base policy; bridge roll RMS drops to **0.92 deg** from **11.24 deg**.
- On bumps, peak vertical acceleration falls to **3.97 m/s^2** versus **10.17** for reconstruction, with **87.5%** versus **67.7%** success; overall gains reach **29.6 points** and **91.8%** roll-RMS reduction.
- Push recovery keeps the widest 50%-success envelope across directions; bimanual G1 tray transport reaches **94.1/82.8/92.6%** success, beating the SteadyTray residual by **1.9-3.1 points**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Conditioning residual corrections on action-conditioned forecasts turns reactive feedback into preactive correction; the gain over current-state reconstruction isolates the value of predicting the future, not denoising the present.
> - Predicting a low-dimensional, physically meaningful task quantity instead of full state or latent codes keeps world models interpretable, cheap, and directly usable without planning or rollouts.
> - Decoupling skill acquisition from precision adaptation via frozen modules stops dense objectives from swamping sparse ones; zero-initialized residuals preserve the prior while corrections emerge gradually.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A wheeled-legged robot carries an unsecured cup while moving over uneven ground. If one side of the path is raised, its back-mounted platform can tilt. The controller must keep moving and also keep the payload from slipping or toppling. LocoWM does not wait for the platform to tilt. It predicts how the proposed movement may affect the platform and adjusts before acting.

In simulation on slope terrain, the payload stayed on the platform for the full trial in **91.1%** of trials with LocoWM. A current-state reconstruction baseline, Recon, reached **61.5%**.

Author demo labeled one-sided bridge · cup.

### 2. Forecast first, correct second

A single policy can learn to move without giving the precision objective enough weight. A reactive correction arrives only after a deviation is observable.

LocoWM forecasts the *task substate*, which is the physical quantity that matters for the task. Here it captures platform/back tilt, linear acceleration, and angular velocity. The desired platform orientation follows effective gravity. It stays level during steady movement and tilts to counter inertial loading during acceleration.

Each control step runs in sequence:

1. A base policy proposes a command-following action.
1. A world model takes proprioceptive history and that proposed action and predicts a short sequence of future task substates.
1. An adapter uses the forecast, history, and base action to produce an additive correction.

The forecast describes what is expected if the **base action** continues. It is not a rollout under the corrected action.

Training has two stages. First, command-following locomotion and the task-substate predictor are learned from shared rollouts, with separate objectives. Then both are frozen, and the adapter trains on tracking plus precision rewards. The adapter's output is zero-initialized, so it begins near the base policy's action.

At deployment, the policy reads proprioception only, without external motion capture or a platform-mounted force sensor.

Stage 1 trains the base policy with the tracking objective and trains the world model on the same trajectories with a separate prediction loss. Stage 2 freezes both while the residual adapter learns precision corrections. At inference, the frozen modules run in sequence and the residual is added to the base action.

### 3. What the comparisons establish

The key comparison is Recon versus LocoWM on the Go2-W robot. Recon adds reconstructed current task state to a reactive residual controller. LocoWM substitutes future task-substate prediction. The slope success result is consistent with the authors' interpretation, but this comparison does not prove that prediction is the only cause of the difference.

The robot was tested on four terrain families: slope, bumps, one-sided bridge, and rough terrain. Across these, the paper reports the most precise leveling and highest retention for LocoWM on each terrain. Precision and retention can improve alongside larger tracking error. LocoWM is the lowest-tracking-error residual adapter, though not necessarily lower-error than the base policy.

In a simulated push test, the robot drives forward at 0.5 m/s while the direction and magnitude of a push to its torso are varied. Direction-averaged retention falls as force increases for every method, and LocoWM is highest over the tested force range. All methods tolerate larger lateral than forward/backward pushes. LocoWM's 50% success envelope is outermost in nearly every direction.

(a) Success rate against force (140–220 N); all methods decline with force, and LocoWM (solid orange) remains highest. (b) 50% success contours by direction; LocoWM's is outermost in nearly every direction. (c) LocoWM alone; higher-success regions extend farther laterally than forward/backward.

### 4. Closing

Forecasting a task-relevant consequence gives the adapter a chance to correct before that consequence is observed. LocoWM was deployed zero-shot on the Go2-W for terrain leveling, acceleration compensation, and push recovery. In the main real-world backward and leftward push sequences, the unsecured bottle tilts transiently but remains on the platform. These results support the approach in the tested setup. They do not establish that the gain transfers to other robots and tasks.
