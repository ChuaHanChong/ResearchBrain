# Adaptive Wiping: Adaptive contact-rich manipulation through few-shot imitation learning with Force-Torque feedback and pre-trained object representations

> [!summary] Summary
> A robotic framework enables adaptive contact-rich wiping of deformable objects by integrating few-shot imitation learning with real-time Force-Torque feedback and pre-trained object representations. The system achieved 100% contact and applied 96% of the human-demonstrated reference force across unseen surface heights and varied sponge properties, demonstrating robust adaptation.

## Problem

- Robots struggle with versatile, contact-rich manipulation tasks involving deformable objects due to their variability and complexity.
- Current imitation learning methods require extensive demonstrations and lack dynamic adaptability to unseen environmental changes like surface height or object properties.
- Existing closed-loop force control strategies often rely on predefined target parameters, which are impractical for objects with unknown or variable deformation characteristics.

## Method

- A ==two-step learning framework== pre-trains an object encoder on unlabeled simulated data and then trains motion and ==force feedback== components using ==few-shot== real-world demonstrations.
- A pre-trained ==Variational Autoencoder (VAE)== learns ==latent representations== of deformable object properties from exploratory ==force-torque interactions==.
- A ==closed-loop control system== dynamically adjusts the robot's vertical motion in real-time by integrating force-torque feedback with the learned object representations.

## Results

- The method achieved **100%** contact and applied an average of **96%** of the reference force across **40** scenarios involving unseen table heights and sponge properties.
- It outperformed an open-loop imitation learning baseline (**4%** reference force, poor contact) and admittance control (**42%** reference force, constant force application).
- Successfully generalized to wall-wiping tasks, maintaining 100% contact and applying 104% of reference force, indicating gravity-independent force adaptation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Pre-training a VAE on exploratory force-torque data effectively extracts a low-dimensional latent representation of deformable object properties, enabling generalization to unseen objects.
> - Integrating real-time force-torque feedback into a closed-loop control framework allows robots to adapt their vertical motion to dynamic environmental changes with minimal demonstrations.
> - A two-step learning approach, separating object property representation from task learning, enhances data efficiency and adaptability for contact-rich tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robotic manipulation, particularly in contact-rich tasks involving deformable objects, presents significant challenges. Traditional pre-programming methods are often impractical due to the variability and complexity of real-world environments. This has led to increased interest in learning-based approaches, prominently imitation learning (IL), which allows robots to acquire skills from human demonstrations.

The broader research landscape for robot learning includes:
- **Imitation Learning (IL):** IL offers advantages in sample efficiency compared to reinforcement learning (RL) by directly acquiring desired behaviors from demonstrations, circumventing the need for carefully designed reward functions. However, IL faces challenges such as the requirement for extensive demonstration data and the sim-to-real gap, where policies learned in simulation struggle to transfer to physical robots.
- **Contact-Rich Tasks:** These tasks necessitate precise force control and adaptability to environmental changes, which is particularly complex when dealing with deformable objects. Examples include assembly, grasping, and wiping.
- **Representation Learning:** To enhance adaptability, several studies have integrated representation learning, which extracts object properties or features prior to or during demonstrations. This can involve visual, tactile, or haptic time-series information to capture physical characteristics.
- **Sim2Real Transfer:** Bridging the gap between simulation and real-world environments is crucial for efficient data collection and policy learning. Techniques like domain randomization (e.g., randomizing lighting, textures, or physical properties) are employed to improve model robustness and generalization.
- **Closed-Loop Force Control:** For contact-rich tasks, closed-loop control strategies are often necessary to adapt to dynamic environmental interactions. Methods like impedance control and admittance control (AC) are established for this purpose. However, these methods typically require prior knowledge of target forces or positions, which may not be available or constant when dealing with deformable objects and varying surfaces.

This work specifically builds upon previous research, such as Aoyama et al., which demonstrated the successful transfer of haptic representations from simulation to reality for force control using few-shot imitation learning in deformable object manipulation. A key limitation of Aoyama et al.'s approach was its open-loop control strategy for wiping motions, which prevented adaptation to environmental changes like varying surface heights. The current paper addresses this gap by integrating real-time force-torque (FT) feedback to enable closed-loop control, thereby allowing robots to dynamically adjust to unseen changes in both wiping surface heights and the physical properties of deformable objects with limited demonstrations. The presented method is positioned as an advancement over existing open-loop IL methods and an alternative to traditional closed-loop control techniques that may not be suitable for scenarios with unknown target parameters for deformable objects.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robotic system capable of performing versatile and adaptive manipulation in contact-rich tasks with deformable objects, specifically focusing on wiping, using a limited number of human demonstrations. This overarching goal is driven by several key motivations and specific challenges:

- **Addressing the limitations of existing imitation learning (IL) methods:** While IL offers benefits in enabling robots to learn tasks from demonstrations, it often requires extensive data. Furthermore, many IL approaches struggle to adapt to discrepancies between training and deployment environments or variations in task parameters. The motivation is to overcome these limitations by enabling adaptation with *few-shot* demonstrations.
- **Enabling adaptability in contact-rich tasks:** Robotic manipulation involving physical interaction with the environment and deformable objects, such as wiping with a sponge, presents a particular challenge. These tasks demand precise force control and the ability to adjust to dynamic conditions. The paper aims to allow robots to adapt to two specific types of environmental changes:
 - **Variations in wiping surface height:** Robots need to maintain consistent contact and apply appropriate force even when the surface being wiped is at an unseen height or has an uneven profile (e.g., sloped).
 - **Changes in the physical properties of manipulated objects:** The deformability, stiffness, and friction of the wiping tool (e.g., a sponge) can vary significantly. A robot should be able to adjust its force application based on these properties, even for previously *unseen* sponges.
- **Overcoming the limitations of previous work in deformable object manipulation:** Specifically, the paper aims to improve upon methods like Aoyama et al., which successfully learned force-based motions for deformable objects but employed an open-loop control for wiping. This open-loop nature prevented dynamic adaptation to changing environmental conditions. The motivation is to introduce a *closed-loop* control strategy that leverages real-time feedback for online adaptation.
- **Developing an alternative to traditional closed-loop control methods:** While impedance and admittance control (AC) are established for force control, they often require pre-defined target forces or positions. For deformable objects whose properties can vary, and for tasks where the exact contact surface height is unknown, these methods become less suitable. The motivation is to propose an approach that can infer appropriate forces and adapt to positions without such prior explicit information.
- **Enhancing robot autonomy and reducing human intervention:** By enabling robots to adapt to unseen scenarios and object properties with minimal demonstrations, the research aims to make robots more versatile and robust in real-world applications, allowing humans to focus on more complex or creative tasks.

In essence, the central objective is to answer the question: "Could robots learn a versatile manipulation policy via few-shot imitation learning capable of adapting to environmental changes: the height of manipulating surface and the physical properties of manipulated objects?" This involves integrating advanced learning techniques (pre-trained object representations, few-shot IL) with real-time sensory feedback (Force-Torque) to achieve adaptive, closed-loop manipulation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, termed "Adaptive Wiping," is a two-step learning framework (pre-training and training) followed by deployment, designed to enable a robot to perform adaptive contact-rich manipulation.

#### 3.1. A. Pre-training Step (Simulator)

The initial phase involves pre-training a sponge properties encoder, $\phi_{sponge}$, using unlabeled simulated data.
- **Objective:** To capture a wide range of sponges' physical properties (stiffness, friction, etc.) within a 5-dimensional latent space, $Z_{sponge}$.
- **Data Collection:** Unlabeled simulated data, $D_{sim}$, consisting of Force-Torque (FT) trajectories ($\tau_{exp} \in \mathbb{R}^{400 \times 6}$), is collected by having the robot perform two pre-defined exploratory actions: pressing vertically and moving laterally. These actions are designed to evoke diverse FT responses indicative of the sponge's characteristics.
- **Sim2Real Bridge:** The simulation data incorporates dynamics domain randomization (randomizing sliding friction, stiffness, and damping parameters) to narrow the gap between simulated and real-world dynamics.
- **Model Architecture:** A Variational Autoencoder (VAE) architecture is employed, comprising an encoder ($\phi_{sponge}$) and a decoder ($\theta_{sponge}$).
 - **Encoder ($\phi_{sponge}$):** Consists of two fully connected layers that process the flattened 6-dimensional FT time-series data into the latent space $Z_{sponge}$.
 - **Decoder ($\theta_{sponge}$):** Reconstructs the FT trajectory from $Z_{sponge}$, also using two fully connected layers with ReLU activation and a dropout rate of 0.1.
- **Training:** The VAE is trained using a self-supervised loss function ($L_{ssl}$), which combines the Mean Squared Error (MSE) between the original and reconstructed FT trajectories and a Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence term to ensure the latent space distribution is Gaussian. The Adam optimizer is used for 200 epochs.

#### 3.2. B. Training Step (Real Robot)

Following pre-training, the framework proceeds to train two components using real-world data: a motion trajectory decoder and an FT feedback loop.
- **Data Collection:**
 - **Unlabeled Real-World Data ($D_{real}$):** Collected using the same pre-defined exploratory actions as in the simulation, but with a real robot using a "normal" ready-made sponge.
 - **Few-shot Human Demonstration Data ($D_{demo}$):** Eight human demonstrations of a wiping task are collected kinesthetically. The demonstrator wipes an inclined table, applying maximum force. This data includes end-effector position ($x_{demo} \in \mathbb{R}^{25 \times 2}$ for horizontal (x,y) motion), vertical displacement ($\Delta h_{demo} \in \mathbb{R}^{25}$), and FT trajectories ($\tau_{demo} \in \mathbb{R}^{6 \times 25}$).

- **1) Motion Trajectory Decoder ($\theta_{traj}$):**
 - **Objective:** To generate the horizontal (x, y) wiping motion based on the manipulated sponge's properties.
 - **Method:** Learning from Demonstration (LfD).
 - **Input:** The FT trajectory ($\tau_{exp}$) from $D_{real}$ is fed into the *pre-trained* $\phi_{sponge}$ (whose weights are frozen), yielding $Z_{sponge}$.
 - **Model Architecture:** The motion trajectory decoder ($\theta_{traj}$) consists of one fully connected layer with a dropout rate of 0.1.
 - **Output:** The decoder outputs the predicted horizontal wiping motion ($\hat{x}_{demo}$).
 - **Training:** Trained using an MSE loss function ($L_{motion}$) between $\hat{x}_{demo}$ and the demonstrated $x_{demo}$. The Adam optimizer is used for 10,000 epochs.

- **2) FT Feedback Loop ($\phi_{ft} - \theta_{height}$):**
 - **Objective:** To provide a closed-loop control input for the end-effector's vertical position based on real-time contact state and sponge properties, enabling adaptive force application.
 - **Input:** The FT history from demonstrations ($D_{demo_{ft}} = \{\tau_{demo_{t-4}}..., \tau_{demo_{t}}\}$) is processed by the FT encoder ($\phi_{ft}$).
 - **Model Architecture:**
 - **FT Encoder ($\phi_{ft}$):** Uses two layers of a Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN) with 25 hidden channels and a dropout rate of 0.1. TCNs are chosen for their efficiency in sequence modeling. It encodes the FT history into a 6-dimensional latent space ($Z_{ft}$).
 - **Vertical Position Decoder ($\theta_{height}$):** Takes the concatenated latent spaces ($Z_{sponge}$ from the pre-trained $\phi_{sponge}$ and $Z_{ft}$ from $\phi_{ft}$) as input. It consists of two fully connected layers (first with 128 hidden dimensions, ReLU, dropout; second as the final output layer).
 - **Output:** The decoder outputs the predicted vertical displacement for the next time step ($\Delta\hat{h}_{demo_{t+1}}$).
 - **Training:** Trained using an MSE loss function ($L_{height}$) between $\Delta\hat{h}_{demo_{t+1}}$ and the ground truth $\Delta h_{demo_{t+1}}$. The Adam optimizer is used for 2000 epochs.

#### 3.3. C. Deployment

During deployment, the robot executes a wiping motion by combining offline horizontal motion with online vertical adaptation.
1. **Sponge Characterization:** The robot first performs exploratory actions with the specific sponge to be used in the task, collecting unlabeled data ($D_{task}$). This data is fed through the pre-trained $\phi_{sponge}$ to obtain $Z_{sponge}$.
2. **Horizontal Motion Generation:** The motion trajectory decoder ($\theta_{traj}$) uses $Z_{sponge}$ to generate the horizontal (x, y) wiping path ($\hat{x}_{task}$), which is then replayed offline.
3. **Vertical Adaptation:** The FT feedback loop actively infers the next vertical position ($\Delta\hat{h}_{task_{t+1}}$) in real-time. This inference is based on the current and previous FT history ($D_{task_{ft}}$) from the FT sensor, combined with $Z_{sponge}$. This continuous feedback allows the robot to adapt its vertical motion to maintain appropriate contact force, compensating for variations in surface height or sponge properties.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates the proposed Adaptive Wiping method through real-world experiments, comparing its performance against a baseline (Aoyama et al.) and Admittance Control (AC) across various challenging conditions. The evaluation focused on contact percentage, average applied vertical force, its standard deviation, and the ratio of applied force to reference force from human demonstrations.

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Wiping Task:** A UR5e robot with an FT sensor and a sponge end-effector.
- **Variations:** 3 wiping table heights (low, high, sloped), 1 vertical wall, and 10 sponges (1 "normal" for training, 9 custom-made with varying stiffness and friction for testing unseen properties). This resulted in 40 scenarios.
- **Baselines:**
 - **Baseline:** An open-loop imitation learning method without real-time FT feedback.
 - **Admittance Control (AC):** A non-learning-based closed-loop force control method. For comparison, a target force was defined as the force applied when the sponge was pressed by 1 cm, and fixed impedance parameters were used.

#### 4.1. A. Verification of Adaptation to Changes in Height

The study assessed the robot's ability to maintain consistent force across different table heights (low, high, sloped) compared to the inclined table used for demonstrations.
- **Baseline:** Achieved a contact percentage ranging from 0% to 44% of the time. The average applied force was only 4% of the desired reference force. In some cases, particularly with low and sloped tables, the average force became positive, indicating a lack of contact and gravitational force dominance. This demonstrated the baseline's inability to adapt to height variations.
- **Admittance Control (AC):** Maintained 100% contact across all 30 cases (10 sponges x 3 heights). However, it applied an average of only 42% of the reference force, indicating it maintained a pre-defined target force but did not adapt to optimal wiping force specific to the sponge properties or demonstration intent.
- **Proposed Method (Ours):** Achieved 100% contact in all 30 cases. Crucially, it successfully maintained an appropriate average force on the sponge, averaging 96% of the reference force. The applied force did not vary significantly with table height changes, and its standard deviation was only about 5% greater than that of human demonstrations, indicating effective adaptation.

#### 4.2. B. Verification of Adaptation to Changes in Sponge Properties

The ability to adapt to unseen sponge properties (varying stiffness and friction) was evaluated.
- **Baseline:** Demonstrated poor performance, failing to maintain contact in several cases, particularly with unseen sponges on the low table (0% contact ratio for all 9 unseen sponges). The average force was less than 25% of the expected force for specific sponges, and overall averaged 4% of the reference force, indicating no adaptation.
- **Admittance Control (AC):** Maintained 100% contact across all 30 cases. However, it merely maintained the predefined target force, without adjusting for the physical properties of the different sponges. This resulted in applied forces ranging from 23% to 63% of the expected force for specific sponges, averaging 42% of the reference force.
- **Proposed Method (Ours):** Achieved 100% contact in all 30 cases. It successfully adjusted the applied force according to the type of sponge, achieving over 63% and an average of 96% of the reference force. This demonstrated the method's ability to adapt to unseen sponge properties.

#### 4.3. C. Wall Wiping (Gravity-Neutral Setting)

To further validate gravity-independent adaptability, the method was tested on a wall-wiping task using the same model trained on table wiping data. Adjustments were made for the end-effector's frame rotation and base-link coordinate output.
- **Proposed Method (Ours):** Maintained 100% contact with the wall across all 10 sponges. The applied forces were comparable to the expected reference forces, averaging 104%. This indicates the robot's capacity to adapt to wall wiping with unseen sponges, showcasing gravity-independent force adjustment.

#### 4.4. D. Ablation Studies (Appendix)

Ablation studies were conducted to examine the impact of hyper-parameters on performance:
- **Number of layers in FT feedback loop:** A 2-layer model (proposed) performed optimally (97% average force ratio). A 1-layer model lacked capacity (159-182% too high force), while a 5-layer model was too complex and overfitted, failing to generalize (152-170% not ideal).
- **Window size of TCN:** A window size of 5 (proposed) was found appropriate (97% average force ratio). A window size of 1 struggled with adaptability (190% too high force), while a window size of 10 yielded slightly less optimal performance (114%).
- **Number of demonstrations:** 8 demonstrations (proposed) were found sufficient (97% average force ratio). 4 demonstrations led to excessive force (114% too high), while 12 demonstrations yielded similar performance (104%), suggesting diminishing returns beyond 8.

In summary, the proposed method significantly outperformed both the open-loop baseline and admittance control in terms of maintaining contact and accurately applying reference forces across varied surface heights and unseen deformable object properties.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework that addresses a critical challenge in robotics: enabling adaptive, contact-rich manipulation of deformable objects with minimal human demonstrations. The work's significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Enhanced Adaptability in Contact-Rich Tasks:** The core contribution is the integration of real-time Force-Torque (FT) feedback with pre-trained object representations within a closed-loop control strategy. This allows robots to dynamically adjust their wiping motions to previously unseen variations in both wiping surface height and the physical properties of deformable objects (sponges). This capability is crucial for tasks requiring dexterous interaction with varied environments.
- **Reduced Data Requirements:** By leveraging few-shot imitation learning and pre-trained object representations, the method significantly reduces the need for extensive human demonstrations. This lowers the cost and effort associated with robot training and deployment, making robot learning more practical for real-world applications.
- **Improved Performance Over Current Methods:** The experimental results demonstrate a substantial improvement over an open-loop imitation learning baseline, which largely failed to adapt, and admittance control, which maintained contact but did not adapt force application to object properties. The proposed method achieved a 96% accuracy in applying reference forces and maintained contact across diverse scenarios, showcasing its robustness and versatility.
- **Gravity-Independent Force Adjustment:** The successful application of the method to a wall-wiping task, without retraining on wall-specific data, highlights its ability to adjust applied forces independently of gravitational effects. This expands the range of environments and tasks where such adaptive manipulation can be applied.
- **Advancement in Sim2Real Transfer:** The use of dynamics domain randomization during pre-training in simulation, combined with freezing the pre-trained encoder weights for real-world training, effectively bridges the sim-to-real gap, enabling the transfer of learned representations.

**Potential Impact:**
The proposed adaptive wiping framework has the potential to impact various domains:
- **Domestic and Service Robotics:** Robots could perform household chores like cleaning surfaces, windows, or floors more effectively and autonomously, adapting to furniture variations and different cleaning tools.
- **Industrial Applications:** In manufacturing or logistics, robots could handle delicate or deformable materials, perform polishing, deburring, or assembly tasks that require adaptive force control and interaction with varied object properties.
- **Healthcare Robotics:** Potential applications in tasks requiring gentle contact and adaptive force, such as cleaning surfaces in hospitals or assisting with patient care activities involving soft materials.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
Despite its advancements, the method has acknowledged limitations:
- **Force Consistency:** While accurate on average, the standard deviations in applied force were approximately 5% greater than human demonstrations and about three times greater than admittance control. This suggests room for improvement in achieving higher consistency, which might be critical for tasks requiring very high precision.
- **Object Type Specificity:** The current approach is specifically designed for deformable and elastic tools, where maximizing applied force generally translates to maximizing cleaning efficiency. It struggles with rigid objects, where applying excessive force could trigger safety alarms. This limits its applicability to a broader range of objects.

Future work aims to address these limitations by:
- **Expanding Object Applicability:** Research will focus on extending the method to handle non-deformable objects, potentially by incorporating more sophisticated pre-training on large datasets and leveraging multimodal inputs (visual, linguistic prompts) to allow robots to infer appropriate actions for various object types.
- **Improving Force Consistency:** Further refinement of the control policy could aim to reduce the variability in applied forces, enhancing precision for tasks demanding greater consistency.

By expanding its applicability and improving its consistency, this framework could contribute to developing more versatile and robust robotic systems capable of operating adaptively in complex and unstructured real-world environments.
