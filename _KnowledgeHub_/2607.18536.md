# MAGE: Human-Like Macro Placement via Agentic Multimodal Reasoning

> [!summary] Summary
> MAGE is a multi-agent multimodal framework that produces human-like macro placements in VLSI physical design by integrating expert floorplanning principles and visual feedback. It yields post-route timing improvements of 11.1%–19.3% in WNS and 70.0%–74.0% in TNS over commercial placers, along with superior 'human-likeness' scores, while generalizing to unseen designs.

## Problem

- Automated macro placement tools fail to consistently reproduce the intricate, higher-level reasoning employed by expert physical design engineers.
- Existing methods often produce floorplans with suboptimal structural organization, fragmented whitespace, and poor macro alignment.
- The discrepancy between automated outputs and expert-desired placements necessitates significant manual refinement in industrial design flows.

## Method

- Introduces **MAGE**, a ==multi-agent== multimodal framework that decomposes the complex macro placement task into a six-phase pipeline handled by ten specialized sub-agents.
- Implements a two-stage process: initial synthesis prioritizes structural properties, followed by a ==tournament-style evolutionary refinement== (==Go-With-The-Winners==) for PPA optimization while preserving structural integrity.
- Integrates ==multimodal reasoning== by combining natural-language directives from a knowledge corpus with mandatory visual checks and feedback from rendered artifacts.

## Results

- MAGE achieved geometric-mean improvements of **11.1%–19.3%** in Worst Negative Slack (WNS) and **70.0%–74.0%** in Total Negative Slack (TNS) over commercial macro placers.
- The framework outperformed a human expert baseline on NG45 designs, improving WNS by **18.3%** and TNS by **72.5%**, while also achieving **6.0%** higher overall human-likeness (S_HM).
- Ablation studies confirmed that removing the knowledge corpus degraded WNS by **35.2%** and TNS by **84.4%**, and disabling visual feedback degraded WNS by **40.5%** and TNS by **133.0%**, underscoring their importance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Adherence to human-like floorplanning principles, quantified by metrics like notch and alignment scores, positively correlates with improved post-route timing.
> - Multimodal reasoning, including mandatory visual inspection and feedback, is critical for identifying and correcting macro organization issues that numerical checks often miss.
> - Explicitly encoding expert knowledge via natural-language prompts and a knowledge corpus, rather than purely data-driven learning, can effectively guide complex physical design tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Macro placement is a foundational and complex problem in Very Large Scale Integration (VLSI) physical design, involving the strategic positioning and orientation of large pre-designed blocks (macros) within a chip floorplan. The quality of macro placement critically influences downstream performance, power, and area (PPA) metrics, as well as the overall convergence of the design process.

Historically, macro placement methods have evolved across several categories:
- **Classical Floorplanning**: Early approaches relied on algorithms like simulated annealing and compact geometric representations (e.g., sequence-pair, B*-trees) to optimize for area and connectivity.
- **Analytical and Mixed-Size Placement**: These methods formulate placement as a continuous optimization problem, integrating macro placement within standard-cell placement frameworks (e.g., ePlace-MS, RePlAce, MAPLE, NTUplace4h).
- **Hierarchy-Aware Macro Placement**: This category leverages design hierarchy and architectural structure to guide placement, aiming to improve routability and reflect microarchitectural intent (e.g., RTL-MP, Hier-RTLMP).
- **Learning-Based Approaches**: More recent research has explored machine learning techniques, including genetic algorithms and deep reinforcement learning, for chip floorplanning.

Despite these advancements, existing automated macro placement tools often fall short in consistently reproducing the intricate, higher-level reasoning employed by expert physical design (PD) engineers. These experts consider not only quantifiable physical cost metrics but also abstract principles such as logical hierarchy, dataflow, symmetry, pin accessibility, and the creation of structured, interpretable floorplans that are amenable to subsequent optimization stages. Consequently, automatically generated placements frequently necessitate substantial manual refinement, a time-consuming and expertise-intensive process in industrial flows.

This work, MAGE (Macro Placement Agentic Engine), addresses this gap by aiming to embed expert floorplanning principles directly into the automated macro placement process. It distinguishes itself from prior work by:
- **Multimodal Reasoning**: Unlike purely optimization-based or learning-based methods that often rely on numerical metrics, MAGE integrates structured floorplanning rules, visual checks, and natural-language directives to guide placement decisions.
- **Agentic Framework**: It adopts a multi-agent architecture, decomposing the complex macro placement task into a series of phases handled by specialized agents, which allows for a more structured and human-like design process.
- **Focus on Human-Likeness**: MAGE explicitly targets the generation of "human-like" macro placements, characterized by spatial regularity and functional coherence, rather than solely optimizing for conventional PPA metrics. This contrasts with efforts like VeoPlace, which uses vision-language models (VLMs) to guide existing low-level placers primarily for HPWL improvement, or other LLM-based EDA efforts that focus on code generation or design assistance. MAGE applies multimodal foundation models directly to the physical-design task, emphasizing structural organization.
- **Knowledge-Driven, Not Data-Driven Learning**: Instead of learning placement heuristics from large datasets of labeled placement examples, MAGE encodes expert knowledge through explicit natural-language prompts, a knowledge corpus, and validation criteria.

By incorporating qualitative, human-centric design principles into an automated, multimodal framework, MAGE seeks to bridge the gap between automated tool outputs and the refined, structured floorplans produced by experienced PD engineers, thereby reducing the need for manual intervention and improving overall design quality.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind MAGE is to overcome the limitations of current automated macro placement tools, which, despite achieving competitive legality and PPA metrics, frequently fail to generate floorplans that align with the higher-level organizational principles applied by expert physical design engineers. This deficiency necessitates significant manual refinement in industrial design flows, leading to increased design cycle times and costs.

The work identifies several specific aspects where current tools fall short:
- **Lack of Expert Reasoning Replication**: Automated placers do not consistently capture the nuanced reasoning about logical hierarchy, dataflow, symmetry, pin accessibility, and aesthetic spatial patterns that characterize expert-designed floorplans.
- **Suboptimal Structural Organization**: Automated placements often result in fragmented whitespace, poor macro alignment, and undesirable geometric artifacts, which, while technically legal, complicate downstream routing, power delivery, and clock distribution.
- **Need for Manual Refinement**: The discrepancy between automated and expert-desired placements leads to extensive manual post-processing by experienced engineers, introducing human variability and increasing design iterations.

To address these challenges, MAGE sets forth the following key objectives:

1. **Develop a Multi-Agent Framework for Human-Like Macro Placement**: To create MAGE, a multi-agent system comprising ten specialized agents organized into a six-phase pipeline. This framework aims to decompose the complex macro placement problem into manageable sub-tasks, facilitating structured design decisions and the incorporation of expert knowledge. This objective is geared towards being the first agentic macro placement framework explicitly designed to generate human-like solutions.

2. **Implement a Two-Stage Synthesis and Evolutionary Refinement Process**: To structure the placement process into an initial stage that prioritizes structural properties (e.g., macro grouping, spatial organization, alignment) and a second stage that applies an evolutionary (tournament-style) optimization (Go-With-The-Winners) to improve implementation metrics (wirelength, timing) while preserving the established structural integrity. This allows for exploration of multiple candidate placements.

3. **Formalize Principles and Metrics for Human-Likeness**: To codify abstract floorplanning principles that define human-like macro placement (e.g., boundary placement, macro stacking, pin-aware orientation, IO keepouts, pocket minimization, whitespace quality). Furthermore, to introduce four quantifiable metrics (notch score, whitespace score, pocket score, and alignment score) that capture these structural properties, which are not directly measured by conventional PPA metrics.

4. **Evaluate on Diverse Floorplan Types**: To rigorously assess MAGE's performance on a broad range of designs, including standard benchmarks from NanGate45 and GlobalFoundries 12nm enablements, industrial netlists, rectilinear floorplan variants, and previously unseen designs. This objective aims to demonstrate the framework's robustness and generalization capabilities.

5. **Achieve Improved PPA Over Baselines**: To demonstrate that MAGE can produce macro placements that yield superior post-route PPA, particularly in terms of Worst Negative Slack (WNS) and Total Negative Slack (TNS), compared to existing commercial macro placers, academic tools (Hier-RTLMP), and even human expert baselines, while maintaining competitive wirelength and power characteristics.

By achieving these objectives, MAGE seeks to provide an automated solution that not only meets quantitative PPA targets but also inherently incorporates the qualitative, structural elegance characteristic of expert human floorplans, thereby streamlining the physical design process and enhancing chip quality.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

MAGE operates as a multimodal, multi-agent framework designed to refine macro placements by integrating structured floorplanning rules, visual checks, and iterative refinement. The core methodology is based on a "decompose–place–validate–refine" workflow, executed through a pipeline of ten specialized sub-agents.

**A. Framework Overview and Six-Phase Pipeline:**
MAGE's workflow is organized into six distinct phases, employing eight sub-agents for the core pipeline and two additional sub-agents for tournament-style optimization:

1. **Phase 1: Data Parsing & Knowledge Extraction**:
 - **Agent**: Data Parser.
 - **Function**: Extracts geometric, hierarchical, connectivity, IO, and pin information from the input design (netlist, floorplan, IO data). For rectilinear floorplans, it identifies core regions, boundary corners, and dead zones. It also queries a knowledge corpus (K) to retrieve prior placement knowledge and identify known floorplan issues.

2. **Phase 2: Hierarchy-Aware Grouping**:
 - **Agent**: Grouping Sub-agent.
 - **Function**: Organizes macros into hierarchy-aware groups (G) using hierarchical prefixes. It computes inter-group connectivity (full logical connectivity, including standard cells) and identifies families of structurally similar groups (Q) to facilitate shared reference placements. IO pin connections to macro groups are tracked.

3. **Phase 3: Group Placement**:
 - **Agent**: Group Placement Sub-agent.
 - **Function**: Iteratively assigns bounding boxes (B_g) to each macro group within the core. The objective is to minimize weighted flyline length (based on inter-group connectivity) while preserving IO access and ensuring the group bounding boxes tile the core region without gaps or overlaps. This phase includes a mandatory visualization gate for visual assessment of flyline diagrams and iterative refinement.

4. **Phase 4: Feasibility Checking**:
 - **Agent**: Feasibility Checker.
 - **Function**: Verifies if each macro group can be physically realized within its assigned bounding box B_g. Checks include macro area, candidate packing patterns, and routing-channel spacing. Groups exceeding a size threshold (τ_max) are recursively partitioned into sub-groups. If checks fail, it reduces channel spacing, changes packing patterns, or requests a revised bounding box from Phase 3.

5. **Phase 5: Macro Placement**:
 - **Agent**: Reference Placement Sub-agent, Transform-and-Merge Sub-agent, Macro Validation Sub-agent.
 - **Function**: For each similar-group family (Q_j), a reference group is selected, and a detailed macro placement (P_ref) is generated, guided by the human-likeness principles. This process uses a boundary-first strategy, considering core and group edges, pin-aware orientation, and arrangement patterns (stacks, rows, L-shapes, etc.).
 - **Transform-and-Merge**: P_ref is then geometrically transformed (translation, reflection, rotation) and applied to all other groups in Q_j to create a chip-level placement (P).
 - **Validation**: P undergoes checks for legality (containment, non-overlap), pin access, IO keepouts, and whitespace quality. Failures trigger feedback loops to either reference placement (for macro arrangement issues) or group placement (for bounding box issues).

6. **Phase 6: Independent Validation**:
 - **Agent**: Independent Validation Auditor.
 - **Function**: Performs a stateless, second-pass audit of the post-merge macro placement (P). This agent operates independently of the prior phases' reasoning history, reviewing rendered artifacts and validation reports to catch residual issues (e.g., blocked pins, insufficient IO keepout). It generates a visual feedback report with specific rearrangement suggestions. A regression guard tracks quality across iterations.

**B. Human-Likeness Principles and Metrics:**
MAGE's core approach is built upon six codified floorplanning principles that characterize expert macro placements:
1. **Boundary Placement**: Macros positioned along core boundaries.
2. **Macro Stacking**: Macros organized into regular vertical or horizontal stacks.
3. **Pin-Aware Orientation**: Macro orientations chosen to preserve signal-pin access.
4. **IO Keepouts**: Sufficient clearance from IO interfaces.
5. **Pocket Minimization**: Reduction of unusable free-space regions.
6. **Whitespace Quality**: Preservation of compact, contiguous, and usable whitespace.

To quantify these principles, MAGE introduces four metrics, each normalized to (1 being most human-like):
1. **Notch Score (S_notch)**: Penalizes concave steps in the collective macro silhouette caused by misalignment.
2. **Whitespace Score (S_ws)**: Measures the shape quality of free-space regions using area-weighted rectangularity.
3. **Pocket Score (S_pocket)**: Penalizes small, low-utility residual free-space subregions.
4. **Alignment Score (S_align)**: Evaluates boundary placement, inter-macro regularity, corner occupation, and stacking correctness.
These are combined into an **Overall Human-Likeness Score (S_HM)** with equal weighting.

**C. Tournament-Style Parallel Optimization (Go-With-The-Winners - GWTW):**
For enhanced optimization, MAGE employs a GWTW-inspired tournament mode:
- **Variant Pool**: Multiple placement variants (N_v) are initialized with distinct seeds, contexts, and improvement suggestions.
- **Parallel Execution**: Each variant runs the full MAGE pipeline (Phases 1-6) in parallel.
- **Evaluation**: Resulting macro placements are evaluated using Cadence Innovus Early Global Routing (eGR) to obtain routed wirelength (rWL, denoted μ_v) as the primary objective.
- **Selection**: Top N_s variants (survivors) are selected based on their rWL.
- **Analysis and Reflection (Sub-agents 9 & 10)**: An analysis agent compares survivor placements to a reference, attributing HPWL changes to specific placement discrepancies. A reflection agent evaluates past suggestions and generates revised guidance for the next round.
- **Replication**: Higher-ranked survivors are replicated more often to populate the next round's variant pool, inheriting parent context, analysis, and reflection feedback. This iterative process continues for R rounds.

**D. Multimodal Reasoning and Visual Feedback:**
MAGE's methodology emphasizes visual inspection as a mandatory component:
- **Mandatory Visualization Gates**: In Phases 3 and 5, sub-agents are required to generate visualizations (e.g., group flyline diagrams, macro pin edges, whitespace diagnostics), inspect them, extract "visual-only facts" not captured by numerical data, and produce structured reports. Visual failures trigger feedback loops.
- **Auxiliary Vision Pipeline**: This pipeline converts selected visual artifacts into structured natural-language feedback (observations, actionable suggestions) that are written into the shared context. This allows visual insights to propagate through the workflow, guiding downstream agents and subsequent iterations without constantly re-processing raw images.

**E. Implementation Details:**
MAGE is implemented in Python, leveraging Anthropic Claude Opus 4.6 as the Vision-Language Model (VLM) backbone for all agents and the auxiliary vision pipeline. The evaluation flow uses Cadence Genus 21.1 for synthesis and Innovus 21.1 for place-and-route. Crucially, input design data is anonymized and scaled (names replaced with generic identifiers, dimensions scaled) before being provided to the VLM to prevent exposure to proprietary or design-specific semantic information.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of MAGE was conducted on nine benchmark designs across NanGate45 (NG45) and GlobalFoundries 12nm (GF12) technologies, including standard benchmarks, industrial designs, and two unseen GF12 designs (VTA, GeneSys01). MAGE's performance was compared against a human expert, two releases of an unnamed commercial macro placer (Comm-1, Comm-2), and OpenROAD's Hier-RTLMP.

**A. Evaluation of PPA (Performance, Power, Area)**

- **Overall Timing Improvement**: MAGE achieved geometric-mean improvements of **11.1%–19.3% in WNS** (Worst Negative Slack) and **70.0%–74.0% in TNS** (Total Negative Slack) over commercial macro placers.
- **Against Human Expert (NG45)**: MAGE improved WNS by **18.3%** and TNS by **72.5%** over the human expert baseline, while maintaining comparable routed wirelength (rWL) and power.
- **Against Hier-RTLMP (NG45)**: MAGE showed significant gains, improving WNS by **47.0%** and TNS by **80.4%**, alongside reductions in rWL by **9.8%** and power by **1.1%**. This indicated MAGE improved all reported PPA metrics over Hier-RTLMP.
- **Trade-offs with Commercial Placers**: Compared to Comm-1 and Comm-2, MAGE generally traded increased rWL (around 29.7% higher) and power (around 3.8%–4.5% higher) for substantial improvements in timing (WNS and TNS). The paper attributes this timing advantage to MAGE's adherence to human-like placement rules, leading to lower clock insertion delay and skew, and reduced congestion around critical logic. For instance, on Ariane-NG45, MAGE lowered worst clock insertion delay from 0.43 to 0.20 ns and skew from 0.44 to 0.34 ns. On BP-NG45, MAGE kept critical logic at a lower standard-cell utilization (45.8% vs. 49.5%), avoiding numerous violating paths.

**B. Evaluation of Human-Likeness Metrics**

- **Overall Human-Likeness Score (S_HM)**: MAGE outperformed all baselines, achieving an improvement of **6%–48%** in the overall S_HM.
- **Against Human Expert (NG45)**: MAGE improved S_HM by **6.0%** over the human expert, with the largest gain in notch score (+14.0%), suggesting MAGE's uniform-width stacking strategy yields better aligned macro silhouettes.
- **Correlation with Timing**: A positive correlation was observed between higher human-likeness scores (S_HM) and better post-route timing outcomes (Pearson r = +0.38, p = 0.053; Spearman ρ = +0.42, p = 0.030).

**C. Ablation Studies (Effect of Knowledge Corpus and Visual Feedback)**

- **Effect of Knowledge Corpus (K)**: Removing the knowledge corpus (MAGE\K) resulted in an average degradation of WNS by **35.2%** and TNS by **84.4%**, with minimal impact on rWL, power, or S_HM. This suggests the knowledge corpus provides design-specific cues that primarily benefit timing, rather than structural properties directly enforced by the principles.
- **Effect of Visual Feedback**: Disabling all visual feedback (MAGE\V) led to significant degradation in WNS by **40.5%** and TNS by **133.0%**, and a drop in S_HM by **4.2%**. rWL and power were largely unaffected. This highlights that visual inspection is critical for preventing fragmented macro groups, ensuring better boundary utilization, and maintaining overall human-like macro organization, which numerical checks alone often miss.

**D. Case Studies**

- **Anonymized Designs**: Anonymizing design information (removing semantic names) significantly worsened timing (e.g., TNS from -32.7 ns to -69.9 ns on Ariane), indicating that descriptive identifiers allow the VLM to recognize regular arrays and functional groupings, leading to better group placement.
- **Unseen Designs (Zero-Shot Generalization)**: MAGE demonstrated effective zero-shot generalization on two unseen GF12 designs (VTA, GeneSys01), improving WNS by **6%–27%** and TNS by **27%–40%** over Comm-1, and achieving higher human-likeness scores, at the cost of 14%–16% higher rWL.
- **Rectilinear Floorplans**: MAGE generalized well to dense rectilinear floorplans (>70% utilization), improving WNS on all four variants tested, with substantial timing gains on some (e.g., BP-HV WNS improved from -205 ps to -123 ps).
- **Utilization Sweep**: At increased utilization levels (68%, 75%, 80%) on GF12 designs, MAGE consistently achieved the best WNS and TNS, especially at high utilization (e.g., on BP at 80% utilization, WNS improved from -0.33 to -0.07, TNS from -2850.0 to -42.9). MAGE maintained structured placements, including boundary-snapped stacks and organized routing channels, under tighter area constraints, whereas commercial placers showed more fragmentation.
- **Tournament Evolution**: The tournament-style optimization consistently reduced routed wirelength across iterations (average 9% rWL improvement over iteration 1 mean), confirming the effectiveness of the analysis and reflection agents in guiding iterative improvement. Most gains occurred within the first 2-3 iterations.

**E. LLM Resource Usage**:
A full MAGE tournament run (6 variants, 6 rounds) cost an average of $310 and took an average of 10.0 hours wall-clock time. Runtime was dominated by downstream Place & Route evaluation rather than LLM inference.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The MAGE framework represents a notable advancement in automated physical design, particularly in the domain of macro placement. Its significance and potential impact stem from several key aspects:

1. **Bridging the Gap Between Automated and Expert Design**: MAGE directly addresses the long-standing challenge of automated tools failing to replicate the nuanced, higher-level reasoning of expert physical design engineers. By encoding expert floorplanning principles through natural language directives, visual checks, and refinement criteria, MAGE moves beyond purely numerical optimization to produce macro placements that are "human-like" in their structural organization and interpretability. This capability has the potential to significantly reduce, or even eliminate, the labor-intensive and time-consuming manual refinement steps currently required in industrial design flows, leading to faster design cycles and reduced engineering effort.

2. **Improved Design Quality, Especially Timing**: The experimental results demonstrate that MAGE can yield superior post-route timing (WNS and TNS) compared to both academic and commercial baselines, and even surpass human expert performance in these metrics. This improvement, achieved while maintaining comparable wirelength and power, suggests that human-like macro organization is not merely an aesthetic preference but a functional prior that positively impacts downstream routing and timing closure. For complex, high-performance designs, this timing advantage can be critical for design convergence.

3. **Demonstrated Generalizability and Robustness**: MAGE's performance across diverse benchmarks, including unseen designs, anonymized netlists, rectilinear floorplans, and varying utilization levels, highlights its robustness and generalizability. The ability to generalize zero-shot to new designs without specific retraining or prompt development is a substantial advantage over many learning-based approaches that can be sensitive to design distribution. This indicates a broader applicability of the framework in industrial settings.

4. **Novel Application of Multimodal Reasoning in EDA**: MAGE's pioneering use of multimodal reasoning (combining text-based instructions with visual feedback and analysis) for a core physical design task like macro placement showcases a promising paradigm for future EDA tools. Unlike previous applications of Large Language Models (LLMs) in EDA, which often focused on code generation or design assistance, MAGE directly applies foundation models to make physical design decisions. This approach allows for a more transparent and editable representation of expert knowledge, moving beyond black-box optimization.

5. **Foundation for Future EDA Automation**: The agentic, phase-based workflow of MAGE provides a modular and extensible framework. The success in macro placement suggests that similar multimodal reasoning methodologies could be applied to adjacent or more complex floorplanning tasks, such as pin access planning, power-grid planning, handling soft macros, standard-cell-aware floorplanning, and even multi-die (3DIC) floorplanning. This work opens avenues for more sophisticated, AI-driven automation across the entire IC physical design spectrum.

In conclusion, MAGE represents a significant step towards intelligent automation in physical design, demonstrating that integrating human-like reasoning and visual understanding into EDA tools can lead to functionally superior and more efficiently produced chip designs.
