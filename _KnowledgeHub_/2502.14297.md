# Evaluating Sakana's AI Scientist: Bold Claims, Mixed Results, and a Promising Future?

> [!summary] Summary
> This paper is an independent evaluation of Sakana's AI Scientist, an LLM system that claims to automate the whole research lifecycle. The authors ran it on a recommender-systems task and found weak novelty checks, frequent experiment failures and flawed manuscripts. They also found it cheap and fast, at $6-15 and about 3.5 human hours per paper, and they propose community measures such as benchmarks and attribution markup.

## Problem

- Sakana's AI Scientist claimed to automate idea generation, experiments, writing and review end to end, but no thorough independent evaluation based on direct experimentation existed to check these bold claims.
- Reviews and hype around the system came largely from people who had not run it, relying on Sakana's own information, while users reported setup and technical difficulties.
- No standards, benchmarks or attribution frameworks existed for judging AI-driven research agents or tracking AI contributions, leaving publishers, venues and universities without policies for autonomous research output.

## Method

- ==Black-box case study==: a Bachelor's student installed the **AI Scientist** with GPT-4o and ran it on a user-built ==FunkSVD template== for Green Recommender Systems on MovieLens-100k, with two seed ideas.
- Stage-by-stage audit of ==idea novelty checking== through Semantic Scholar, ==Aider-based code edits== per iteration, generated manuscripts, and the ==reviewer agent== on AI-written and ten human-written OpenReview papers.
- Proposes community remedies: benchmarking suites for research agents, ==Research Attribution Markup Language (RAML)==, ==Research Process Markup Language (RPML)==, research logs, and competitions with blind review at venues like SIGIR.

## Results

- The novelty check labelled all **12** ideas as novel, including known techniques like micro-batching SGD; **5 of 12** experiments (**42%**) failed from coding errors, and each Aider iteration added only about **8%** characters.
- The **7** manuscripts had a median of **5** references, only **5 of 34** from 2020 or later; **4 of 7** had structural flaws and **4 of 7** had hallucinated or incorrect numbers.
- Its reviewer rejected **9 of 10** human-written papers, including **4** accepted ones; papers cost about **$6** each (**$42** for 7) with about **3.5** human hours, versus **10-40** hours for humans.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Keyword-based literature search cannot judge novelty; autonomous research needs real synthesis of prior work, otherwise well-known ideas get labelled novel and results mislead.
> - Systems that cannot critically check their own results, such as a missing baseline rerun or a fixed hyperparameter, need human oversight; they act as assistants, not independent scientists.
> - Fluent, well-structured output hides shallow substance, and superficial review can miss it; cheap, fast generation raises risks of paper floods and integrity problems, so attribution and logging standards matter.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research paper is situated at the forefront of a rapidly evolving and critical area: the application of artificial intelligence to automate scientific discovery, a concept the authors term **Artificial Research Intelligence (ARI)**. ARI is envisioned as a significant milestone towards Artificial General Intelligence (AGI) and a prerequisite for achieving Super Intelligence, representing AI capable of conducting research indistinguishable from human efforts.

The landscape is currently characterized by a surge of interest and investment in AI for science. Companies like Sakana.ai, a Tokyo-based startup, have made audacious claims about their "AI Scientist"—a system purportedly capable of automating the entire research lifecycle, from idea generation and experimentation to manuscript writing and peer review. Google's announcement of the "AI Co-Scientist" and specialized workshops such as "Towards Agentic AI for Science" at ICLR 2025 further underscore the growing momentum in this field.

Prior research has already explored AI's role in various facets of scientific work, including literature retrieval (e.g., Semantic Scholar, Elicit), idea generation (with some LLMs even showing potential to surpass human creativity), and automated experimental design (e.g., IBM Watson Discovery). Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated the ability to generate research ideas comparable to human scientists. However, until this paper, a comprehensive, independent, and systematic evaluation of a system claiming *end-to-end automation* of the entire research lifecycle, like Sakana's AI Scientist, had been lacking. Sakana's tool garnered significant attention and citations, yet most positive reviews were based solely on the company's own information, with many users reporting difficulties or technical issues.

This paper addresses this crucial gap, providing an objective assessment that moves beyond promotional material and anecdotal user experiences. It specifically targets the **Information Retrieval (IR) community**, highlighting a paradigm shift: from LLMs merely *assisting* IR research to potentially *autonomously conducting* it. This shift raises profound questions about the future role of human researchers, the nature of scientific discovery, and the reliability of AI-generated knowledge, building upon previous discussions within the IR community regarding LLMs' transformative impact on traditional IR tasks (e.g., SIGIR perspective paper by Zhai). By focusing on Sakana's AI Scientist due to its ambitious claims and open-source nature, the authors aim to provide a detailed, critical analysis of its current capabilities and limitations, thereby contributing to an urgent and necessary discussion on shaping the trajectory of AI-driven science.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to conduct a **thorough, independent, and systematic assessment of Sakana.ai’s "AI Scientist"**. This evaluation aims to scrutinize the system's capabilities, identify its current limitations, and gauge its trajectory towards achieving Artificial Research Intelligence (ARI).

The motivation behind this study is multi-faceted:

- **Addressing a Knowledge Gap:** Sakana.ai made bold claims about the AI Scientist's ability to automate the entire research lifecycle, attracting significant academic and public attention. However, there was a noticeable absence of any comprehensive, independent evaluation of the system, with many positive commentaries relying solely on Sakana's self-reported information. The authors were motivated to fill this void with empirical evidence.
- **Contextualizing "Artificial Research Intelligence" (ARI):** The paper introduces and explores the concept of ARI—AI capable of autonomously conducting research indistinguishable from human research. Evaluating the AI Scientist serves as a practical case study to understand how close current AI systems are to this ambitious goal and what technical and conceptual hurdles remain.
- **Informing the Academic Community:** The authors aim to provide the Information Retrieval (IR) and broader scientific communities with a realistic understanding of AI-driven research automation. This includes demonstrating the system's ability to mimic academic work while also exposing its critical flaws, particularly given the rapid pace of AI advancement.
- **Stimulating Critical Discussion and Policy Development:** The emergence of tools like the AI Scientist necessitates urgent discussions within academia on how AI should be integrated into scientific workflows, how AI-generated research should be attributed, and how academic integrity can be maintained. The study intends to catalyze these discussions and propose concrete steps, such as updated guidelines for authors, benchmarking standards, and new attribution frameworks.
- **Preparing for the Future of Research:** By evaluating the current state of autonomous research agents, the authors seek to prepare researchers, educators, and institutions for the inevitable future where AI will play an increasingly central role in scientific discovery. This includes understanding potential benefits (speed, cost-efficiency) and risks (mass generation of low-quality papers, academic misconduct).

In essence, the authors are motivated to move beyond the hype surrounding the AI Scientist to provide a grounded, critical perspective that informs responsible development and governance of AI in scientific research.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors adopted a hands-on, experimental approach to evaluate Sakana’s AI Scientist. Their methodology involved setting up the open-source system, configuring it for specific research tasks, and meticulously analyzing its performance across the claimed stages of the research lifecycle.

1. **System Setup and Environment:**
 - The AI Scientist was installed and prototyped on a consumer laptop and subsequently for main experiments on a university computing cluster. This dual environment allowed for initial testing and robust execution.
 - To ensure reproducibility, the experimental codebase, including a Singularity container definition file, was made publicly available on GitHub.
 - The core functionality of the AI Scientist relies on a foundation Large Language Model (LLM); for this study, OpenAI’s `gpt-4o-2024-05-13` was selected, as it was supported at the time of experiments.

2. **Defining the Research Task and Inputs:**
 - **Research Domain:** Green Recommender Systems, a topic aiming to reduce the carbon footprint of recommendation algorithms, was chosen.
 - **Research Goal:** The AI Scientist was prompted with the task: "Your goal is to find novel ways to optimize energy-efficiency" for a given Python-based experimental pipeline.
 - **Experimental Pipeline:** The authors implemented a minimal Python-based pipeline for the FunkSVD algorithm (a collaborative filtering technique) using the MovieLens-100k dataset. This pipeline defined dataset usage, model training, and result visualization (RMSE metric, line charts). Crucially, the AI Scientist requires this pipeline in a specific format, implying that users cannot simply input arbitrary Python code.
 - **Seed Ideas:** Two user-provided seed ideas were supplied: "Adaptive Learning Rates for SGD" and "E-Fold Cross-Validation." These included numerical scores for interestingness, feasibility, and novelty, though the authors observed these scores had no apparent impact on the AI Scientist's processing.
 - **LaTeX Templates:** The default LaTeX templates provided by Sakana were used for manuscript generation.

3. **Evaluation Stages and Metrics:**

 - **Idea Generation:** The AI Scientist generated ten research ideas. The authors assessed the "novelty" claims made by the AI Scientist (which queries Semantic Scholar API for up to 10 results and iterates to determine novelty) against known research concepts.
 - **Experiment Execution:** The AI Scientist modifies the user-provided experimental pipeline using Aider (an LLM-driven coding assistant). The authors analyzed:
 - The success rate of experiment execution.
 - The extent and nature of code modifications (character count changes per iteration, Table 1).
 - Methodological correctness and logical consistency of the experimental designs and results.
 - **Manuscript Generation:** For the successfully conducted experiments, the AI Scientist generated manuscripts. These were evaluated based on:
 - Length and inclusion of figures/tables.
 - Quality and recency of citations (number, year, relevance).
 - Presence of structural errors (missing sections, placeholders, duplicates).
 - Accuracy and logical soundness of reported numerical results and findings (detection of "hallucinated" results or inconsistencies).
 - **Review Functionality:** The AI Scientist’s reviewer agent was tested in two contexts:
 - Reviewing its *own* AI-generated manuscripts.
 - Reviewing ten *human-written* papers from OpenReview.net (five accepted, five rejected by human reviewers).
 - Evaluation criteria included the structure, depth, identified strengths/weaknesses, and accept/reject recommendations, comparing them against human judgment where possible.
 - **Cost and Effort Analysis:** The authors meticulously tracked the API costs for using the LLM and the human hours required for setup, template implementation, brainstorming, and output review.

4. **Limitations of the Authors' Own Evaluation:** The authors acknowledged that their evaluation had limitations, including the use of a single dataset (MovieLens-100k), one research domain (Green Recommender Systems), fixed seed ideas, and one LaTeX template. They state that these constraints were chosen to ensure a controlled assessment but might affect generalizability and reproducibility. Despite this, they believe their findings remain meaningful.

This comprehensive methodology allowed for a systematic dissection of the AI Scientist's capabilities across the entire research workflow, providing empirical evidence to substantiate their conclusions.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of Sakana’s AI Scientist revealed a system that, while demonstrating remarkable automation capabilities, suffers from significant critical shortcomings across all stages of the research lifecycle.

- **Idea Generation:**
 - The AI Scientist successfully generated ten research ideas (in addition to the two seed ideas provided).
 - However, its novelty assessment mechanism proved highly unreliable. All twelve ideas (including well-established concepts like micro-batching for Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) and adaptive learning rates) were classified as "novel" by the AI Scientist.
 - This misclassification stems from a simplistic reliance on keyword matching via the Semantic Scholar API, rather than a deeper semantic synthesis or understanding of existing research, leading to poor novelty assessments.

- **Experiment Execution:**
 - The system exhibited a lack of robustness: five out of twelve proposed experiments (42%) failed to execute due to unresolvable coding errors, often cycling through flawed code iterations.
 - Code modifications introduced by Aider (the LLM-driven coding assistant) were minimal, averaging an initial 8% character increase, diminishing in subsequent iterations. This suggests limited adaptability and a tendency to rework predefined templates rather than devising novel methodologies.
 - Even for successfully executed experiments, the results were often logically flawed or misleading. For instance, in an e-fold cross-validation experiment, the AI Scientist erroneously kept the `e` parameter fixed, failed to re-run the baseline, and consequently made the e-fold cross-validation appear superior in performance (instead of just energy savings), which is methodologically incorrect.
 - A fundamental limitation observed was the AI Scientist's inability to critically assess its own results, detect methodological flaws, or identify logical inconsistencies.

- **Manuscript Generation:**
 - The AI Scientist produced manuscripts for all seven successfully executed experiments, ranging from six to eight pages.
 - **Poor Substantiation:** The manuscripts had a median of only five citations per paper, most of which were outdated (only 5 out of 34 citations were from 2020 or later). It frequently cited foundational works (like Goodfellow et al.'s Deep Learning textbook) rather than specific, relevant literature, and critically, failed to cite existing papers on "e-fold cross-validation" despite their availability.
 - **Structural Errors:** Frequent structural flaws included missing or misplaced figures (57% of manuscripts), incomplete sections (e.g., "Conclusions Here"), repeated sections, and placeholder text. Related work sections were consistently poor.
 - **Hallucinated Results:** A significant concern was the presence of incorrect or hallucinated numerical results in four out of seven manuscripts (57%), with discrepancies in hyperparameters and performance metrics. One case involved an algorithm optimized for energy efficiency reporting better accuracy while increasing memory usage, a logical contradiction that was not discussed.
 - **Overall Quality:** The authors characterized the quality of generated manuscripts as akin to "an unmotivated undergraduate student rushing to meet a deadline," formally structured but careless in execution.

- **Review Functionality:**
 - The AI Scientist’s reviewer agent generated structured reviews, but it could not interpret figures, tables, or supplementary material.
 - For its own AI-generated manuscripts, it consistently recommended rejection, identifying valid criticisms like the use of a single small dataset (MovieLens 100k). However, it overlooked more critical flaws such as redundant text, severe formatting errors, missing sections, and flawed experimental results.
 - When reviewing ten human-written papers from OpenReview.net (five accepted, five rejected by human reviewers), the AI Scientist rejected 9 out of 10, including four papers that human reviewers had accepted. This indicated a strong conservative bias and a significant misalignment with human judgment.
 - While its reviews were consistently structured and appeared plausible on the surface, they lacked the depth required for rigorous academic evaluation.

- **Costs and Effort:**
 - The monetary cost was remarkably low: the total API cost for generating ten ideas, running experiments for seven ideas, and producing seven manuscripts with reviews amounted to only $42 USD, averaging $6 per manuscript. This largely validated Sakana's claim of producing papers for around $15.
 - Human effort, however, was still substantial: approximately 30 hours for setup, template implementation, brainstorming, and output review, translating to about 3.5 hours per manuscript from an undergraduate student.
 - Despite the human involvement, the AI Scientist operated approximately 3 to 11 times faster than a human researcher at comparable (low) quality levels.

In summary, while the AI Scientist demonstrates a capacity for autonomous execution of various research tasks, its current output is marred by critical flaws in novelty assessment, experimental soundness, methodological rigor, and the reliability of reported results. It serves more as an advanced, yet heavily supervised, research assistant rather than an independent scientific agent.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The findings of this evaluation hold profound significance for the future of scientific research, AI development, and academic integrity. Despite its significant shortcomings, the AI Scientist represents a **"significant leap forward in research automation,"** fundamentally challenging conventional expectations of AI-generated scientific work. The system's ability to produce complete research manuscripts with minimal human intervention, even if of low quality, signals the **acceleration towards Artificial Research Intelligence (ARI)**.

The potential impacts are multi-faceted:

- **Mimicry and Deception:** A key finding is that the AI Scientist's output, despite its flaws, is formally structured and plausible enough that "many reviewers or university instructors conducting only a superficial assessment may struggle to distinguish its output from that of human researchers." This demonstrates AI's progress in mimicking academic writing and structuring scientific arguments, posing immediate threats to academic integrity and the peer-review process.
- **Cost and Speed Efficiency:** The AI Scientist's ability to generate a full research paper for $6–$15 with only 3.5 hours of human involvement is unprecedented. This speed and cost efficiency could dramatically change the economics and pace of scientific discovery, making research generation accessible at scale, albeit with current quality issues.
- **Urgent Need for Community Discussion:** The emergence of such powerful, albeit flawed, tools necessitates an urgent, widespread discussion within the Information Retrieval (IR) and broader scientific communities. This includes shaping the development and governance of ARI to ensure it aligns with scientific rigor and ethical principles.
- **Opportunities for the IR Community:** AI-driven research agents offer new avenues for automating literature retrieval, citation analysis, and experimental design. The IR community is uniquely positioned to lead this transformation by developing robust methodologies for these tasks, ensuring the credibility and impact of AI-generated research.
- **Challenges to Academic Integrity:**
 - **Mass AI-Generated Papers:** The capability to rapidly generate numerous "seemingly novel but low-value papers" could inundate academic venues, overwhelming reviewers and potentially diluting the quality of scientific discourse.
 - **AI-Generated Assignments:** For universities, the AI Scientist poses an immediate threat to academic integrity, as students could generate entire assignments without genuine engagement with the learning process. This demands a rethinking of assessment methodologies, emphasizing personal mastery and iterative work over traditional take-home assignments.
- **Paradox of Productivity vs. Satisfaction:** While AI can enhance scientific productivity, studies suggest it might decrease researcher job satisfaction. This raises questions about the long-term role of human scientists if AI can outperform them in key research tasks.
- **Proposed Solutions and Future Directions:** The authors propose concrete steps for the IR community and academia:
 - **Researcher Engagement:** Researchers should actively familiarize themselves with and contribute to the development of AI-assistive tools.
 - **Updated Guidelines:** Publishers (like ACM) need to update guidelines to address AI's role in conducting research and generating full manuscripts, potentially disallowing entirely AI-generated submissions for now, but revisiting this stance.
 - **Benchmarking ARI:** Structured benchmarks are crucial to evaluate AI agents across all research stages (idea generation, experimentation, peer review, manuscript generation) to determine when "Scientific AGI" is achieved.
 - **Transparency and Reproducibility:** Mandating AI Research Logs, a Research Process Markup Language (RPML), Research Attribution Markup Language (RAML) for content tagging, standardized dataset annotation, and integration with code repositories (e.g., PapersWithCode) are essential for accountability and traceability.
 - **Pilot Projects and Competitions:** Integrating AI-generated paper competitions into major conferences (like SIGIR) could provide unbiased comparisons and foster responsible development.
 - **Strategic Dialogue:** Initiating multi-day workshops or retreats (e.g., Dagstuhl seminars) to codify opinions and catalyze community efforts on AI-driven research.

In conclusion, while Sakana's AI Scientist currently operates at the level of an "unmotivated undergraduate," its existence signals a monumental shift. The technical limitations identified are surmountable, and future iterations will undoubtedly achieve greater sophistication. This paper serves as a critical early warning and a call to action, urging the scientific community to actively engage in shaping the ethical, methodological, and practical integration of AI into the very fabric of scientific discovery before it is simply dictated by technological momentum.
