# Behavior Prompting Policy: Demonstrations as Prompts for Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> A system named Behavior Prompting Policy (BPP) enables robots to learn and execute new manipulation tasks at inference time by conditioning on a single human demonstration. This approach, supported by a novel data collection interface and new evaluation benchmarks, reduced errors on unseen drawing tasks by 33.3-80.7% and improved success rates on complex manipulation sequences.

## Problem

- Traditional robot learning methods require extensive retraining or fine-tuning for each new task, making skill acquisition inefficient.
- Existing task descriptors like language or goal images often provide incomplete spatial and temporal information for complex, low-level manipulation actions.
- A lack of sufficiently diverse benchmarks for evaluating test-time adaptation to truly novel robot behaviors.

## Method

- Introduced ==behavior prompting==, conditioning a robot policy on a complete sensorimotor demonstration of a desired task, implemented as **Behavior Prompting Policy (BPP)**, an ==in-context visuomotor policy== using a ==Transformer-based prompt encoder== and a ==diffusion model== action decoder.
- Created **iPhUMI**, a handheld interface leveraging ==ARKit== for efficient, real-time collection of diverse human demonstrations and wireless transmission as behavior prompts.
- Established new benchmarks (DrawAnything-Sim/Real, LIBERO-Gen Combination/Chain) designed with procedural generation for high task diversity and rigorous evaluation of adaptation to unseen tasks.

## Results

- BPP achieved an **80.7%** error reduction on unseen simulated drawing tasks compared to a Goal-Image baseline and a **33.3%** reduction against a prior behavior prompt model (ICRT).
- BPP improved success rates on unseen manipulation tasks across LIBERO-Gen Combination and Chain benchmarks compared to Language and Goal-Image baselines, showing greater gains with increased temporal task complexity.
- Training on a higher diversity of tasks (fewer demos per task, more task types) significantly enhanced adaptation to unseen tasks compared to training on fewer, more heavily demonstrated tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Behavior prompts facilitate dense sub-goal conditioning, with the prompt encoder's attention mechanism dynamically tracking task progression and identifying corresponding states and actions.
> - High task diversity in the training dataset is critical for enabling robust test-time adaptation to unseen tasks, outperforming simply increasing the number of demonstrations per task.
> - Optimal behavior prompt representation requires multi-modal sensorimotor information (observations and actions) and sufficient observation frequency to maintain fidelity to the demonstration.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics is actively pursuing methods to enable robots to acquire and perform new skills more efficiently. Traditionally, teaching robots new tasks has involved extensive retraining or fine-tuning of policies, which can be resource-intensive and time-consuming. This paper addresses the challenge of enabling robots to adapt to novel tasks at inference time with minimal human input, specifically a single demonstration.

The work builds upon advancements in multi-task learning and few-shot learning for manipulation. Prior research has explored various conditioning mechanisms for robot policies, including language descriptions and goal images. While these methods have shown success in adapting to new environments or object categories (e.g., vision-language-action (VLA) models), their capabilities often exhibit limitations when it comes to generalizing to new low-level actions or highly specific manipulation strategies. Language or goal images can provide incomplete information regarding the precise spatial and temporal execution details required for complex tasks.

This research draws inspiration from the success of in-context learning in large language models, where models adapt to new tasks by processing a few examples provided during inference. In robotics, this concept translates to conditioning robot behavior on demonstrations. Existing in-context learning approaches for manipulation have used diverse prompt representations, such as robot trajectories, human hand trajectories, scene keypoints, or multi-modal prompts combining text and images, often employing attention mechanisms or transformer models to process these inputs.

The paper positions "behavior prompting" as a specific form of in-context, few-shot learning that utilizes a complete sensorimotor task demonstration as the prompt. This distinguishes it from methods relying solely on language or goal images by providing rich temporal and spatial information about "how" to complete a task, not just "what" the task is. It extends prior work in behavior prompting, such as ICRT, by specifically investigating how this paradigm scales with increased task diversity and the types of test-time capabilities it enables, particularly focusing on continuous and chained manipulation tasks rather than a smaller set of discrete motion primitives. The proposed methodology, data collection interface, and evaluation benchmarks aim to provide a more comprehensive framework for studying this specific form of in-context adaptation in robotics.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a paradigm, termed "behavior prompting," that allows robots to perform new and unseen tasks at inference time by conditioning their actions on a single human demonstration. This demonstration, referred to as a "behavior prompt," encapsulates both the intended task and an example of its execution.

The motivation for pursuing this objective stems from several key challenges and observations in contemporary robot learning:
1. **Inefficiency of Traditional Methods:** Conventional methods for teaching robots new skills typically necessitate extensive retraining or fine-tuning of policies whenever a new task is introduced. This process is resource-intensive, time-consuming, and hinders rapid deployment of robotic capabilities. The researchers aim to enable "on-the-fly" skill deployment.
2. **Limitations of Existing Task Descriptors:** While language descriptions and goal images have been explored as ways to specify tasks to robots, these modalities can often provide incomplete information. Language might lack the specificity for intricate spatial-temporal sequences, and a goal image only captures the final state, omitting the crucial steps of "how" to achieve it. Behavior prompts, by providing a full sensorimotor demonstration, are hypothesized to offer a more expressive and complete representation of the desired task and behavior.
3. **Inspiration from In-Context Learning:** The success of in-context learning in large language models, where models adapt to new tasks given a few examples without parameter updates, motivates exploring analogous capabilities in robotics. The goal is to bring a similar level of flexibility and scalability to robot learning.
4. **Addressing Data Diversity Requirements:** The researchers hypothesize that enabling robust test-time adaptation to *unseen* tasks requires training data with high task diversity, rather than just many demonstrations for a limited set of tasks. A specific motivation is to investigate and validate this hypothesis, then develop tools to facilitate such data collection.
5. **Need for Robust Evaluation:** Existing benchmarks for robot manipulation are often insufficient for evaluating test-time adaptation to truly novel tasks, particularly in terms of action adaptation. Many focus on semantic or visual adaptation (e.g., new objects), but not adaptation to entirely new low-level behaviors. This motivates the creation of new benchmarks that feature high task diversity, procedural generation capabilities, and explicit mechanisms for evaluating adaptation to unseen tasks and behaviors.

To achieve the overarching goal, the research focuses on three interlinked objectives:
- **Algorithm Development:** To design an in-context visuomotor architecture, Behavior Prompting Policy (BPP), that can effectively interpret a behavior prompt and the robot's current observation to generate appropriate actions, accounting for both temporal correspondences and spatial differences between the prompt and the current scene.
- **Data Strategy and Interface:** To systematically study the data requirements for behavior prompting, particularly the role of task diversity, and to develop a practical human demonstration interface (iPhUMI) that enables efficient collection of diverse training data and serves as a real-time tool for specifying behavior prompts at test time.
- **Evaluation Framework:** To introduce novel benchmark suites, DrawAnything (simulated and real) and LIBERO-Gen (Combination and Chain), that allow for rigorous, reproducible evaluation of test-time adaptation to unseen drawing and tabletop manipulation tasks, specifically designed to capture challenges of closed-loop visual control, high task diversity, and the ability to specify new tasks during inference.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces the concept of behavior prompting and develops a comprehensive system encompassing an algorithm, data collection methods, and evaluation benchmarks.

**Behavior Prompts:**
A behavior prompt is defined as a single demonstration of a desired task, provided as a sequence of observations, proprioception, and actions, consistent with the robot's sensorimotor space. This demonstration originates either from existing training data or from a human operator during deployment. Unlike language or goal images, behavior prompts are designed to convey both "what" the task is and "how" to execute it, including spatial and temporal details. This approach is deemed useful in multi-task settings where tasks involve following spatial-temporal information (e.g., step-by-step drawing), are more easily described by example than language (e.g., human preferences, complex folds), or require specific manipulation strategies (e.g., a particular grasp).

**Behavior Prompting Policy (BPP) Architecture:**
BPP is an in-context visuomotor policy designed to process a behavior prompt and the current observation to generate closed-loop actions. It consists of two main components:
1. **Prompt Encoder:**
 - **Temporal Downsampling and Chunking:** To manage computational load, observations and proprioception within the behavior prompt are temporally downsampled (typically to 1Hz), while actions are retained at full resolution. The prompt is segmented into chunks, each containing a single timestep of observation (`o`), proprioception (`q`), and the sequence of actions (`a`) leading to the next chunk.
 - **Attention Pooling:** An attention pooling mechanism is applied within each chunk to merge `{o, q, a}` into a single chunk embedding (`p_i`). This pooling associates information from the same timestep and reduces the overall sequence length of the prompt embeddings.
 - **Transformer Decoder:** The prompt encoder is implemented as a transformer decoder. It extracts relevant information from the sequence of prompt chunk embeddings (`P`) by performing cross-attention with tokens representing the current observation (history of observations). Learned positional embeddings are used for both the prompt and current observation tokens.
2. **Action Decoder:**
 - This component is a diffusion model, specifically a CNN U-Net architecture with FiLM conditioning, adapted from prior work.
 - It takes as input the current observation, the relevant prompt information extracted by the prompt encoder, and a diffusion timestep `k`.
 - The model iteratively denoises actions over `K` diffusion steps to generate the robot's action.

**Training:**
BPP is trained using standard behavior cloning practices for action diffusion policies. In each training step, a single demonstration is sampled from the training data to serve as the behavior prompt. Subsequently, a batch of receding horizon observations and future action chunks are loaded from *other* demonstrations of the *same task*. Crucially, these demonstrations may vary in environment configurations, requiring the policy to learn to generalize by discerning temporal similarities and spatial differences between the prompt and the current observation. The training does not require explicit spatial or temporal correspondence between different demonstrations of the same task; the prompt is provided as an additional input, and the model learns to leverage it end-to-end. The granularity of task groupings in the training data defines the scope of prompt influence.

**Inference:**
During inference, a single behavior prompt is selected per rollout. The prompt chunk embeddings are generated and cached once per rollout. The prompt encoder extracts relevant information once per inference step, which is then passed to the action decoder. The action decoder can then perform multiple denoising steps without repeatedly referencing the entire prompt.

**iPhUMI: Handheld Manipulation Interface:**
To facilitate data collection and test-time prompting, the researchers developed iPhUMI, a handheld interface. It is an adaptation of the UMI gripper, replacing the GoPro camera with an iPhone 15 Pro. This provides two benefits:
1. **Instant Localization:** Leverages ARKit for real-time Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM), eliminating the need for tedious environment mapping.
2. **Wireless Prompting:** A custom iPhUMI app enables wireless transmission of human-provided behavior prompts to a workstation, allowing immediate conditioning of the policy.
iPhUMI collects multi-modal data including main camera, ultrawide camera, LiDAR depth, gripper pose, and gripper width. It also supports bimanual configurations and integrates speech-to-text for task narration during data collection. For drawing tasks, a marker with a spring attachment is used to provide compliance.

**Evaluation Benchmarks:**
To assess test-time adaptation to unseen tasks, two new benchmarks were introduced:
1. **DrawAnything-Sim:** A simulated drawing environment.
 - **Training:** 2000 procedurally generated drawing tasks, 5 demonstrations per task, varying board orientations.
 - **Evaluation:** 50 unseen human-collected drawing tasks. The policy must reconstruct the unseen drawing given a single human demonstration, with the rollout occurring at a different board orientation than the prompt. This evaluates continuous, fine-grained action adaptation and spatial understanding.
2. **DrawAnything-Real:** A real-world drawing setup using an ARX robot arm with an iPhone wrist camera.
 - **Training:** 1000 tasks (200 human iPhUMI demos, 800 scripted demos), varying drawing regions and rotations.
 - **Evaluation:** 10 tasks (4 training, 6 unseen) collected by human via iPhUMI. Requires 6DoF action and robustness to visual occlusions.
3. **LIBERO-Gen Combination:** An extension of the LIBERO tabletop manipulation benchmark with procedural generation.
 - **Tasks:** 164 tasks where the robot picks one of two identical bowls and places it at one of nine locations.
 - **Evaluation:** 10 held-out "combinations" of pick-place locations. Individual pick and place locations are seen during training, but never jointly in a single task.
4. **LIBERO-Gen Chain:** Another extension of LIBERO focusing on sequential tasks.
 - **Tasks:** 311 tasks involving sequentially executing two single-step tasks (e.g., opening a drawer then placing an object).
 - **Evaluation:** 10 held-out two-step tasks where individual manipulation skills are seen during training, but not the specific chained sequence.

These benchmarks are designed to offer high task diversity and the ability to procedurally generate demonstration data, enabling scientific study of behavior prompting without industrial-scale data collection.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several key findings regarding the effectiveness, mechanisms, data requirements, and limitations of behavior prompting.

**Effectiveness of Behavior Prompting:**
- **Improved Test-Time Adaptation:** Across all benchmarks, behavior prompting demonstrated an ability to improve test-time adaptation to unseen tasks compared to baseline methods.
- **DrawAnything:** BPP showed substantial error reduction on unseen drawing tasks. In simulation, it achieved an 80.7% error reduction compared to a Goal-Image baseline and a 33.3% error reduction compared to ICRT, a prior behavior prompt model. Qualitatively, BPP was able to reconstruct unseen drawings from single human demonstrations in both simulation and real-world settings, whereas Goal-Image often produced disorganized strokes for unseen drawings. The temporal richness of a behavior prompt was identified as crucial for step-by-step guidance in continuous tasks like drawing.
- **LIBERO-Gen:** For unseen manipulation tasks in both LIBERO-Gen Combination and LIBERO-Gen Chain, BPP exhibited improved success rates over Language and Goal-Image baselines. The benefits were more pronounced when temporal task complexity increased. For example, in an ablation where second-step tasks were removed from LIBERO-Gen Chain training (making adaptation to unseen chained tasks more challenging), BPP achieved a 20.8% gain over Language, compared to a 10.7% gain in the full training setting. Notably, BPP, without foundation pretraining, rivaled the performance of a finetuned π0.5 foundation VLA model on these benchmarks.

**Mechanism of Prompt Utilization (Attention Analysis):**
- **Dense Sub-Goal Conditioning:** Visualizations of the prompt encoder's attention mechanism indicated that BPP utilizes the behavior prompt for dense sub-goal conditioning.
- **DrawAnything:** Attention continuously tracked the portion of the prompt most similar to the current observation. This enabled BPP to identify temporal similarities and extract upcoming states and actions, simplifying action generation by providing step-by-step guidance and accounting for spatial differences (e.g., varying board poses).
- **LIBERO-Gen:** Attention tracked task progression in a more discrete manner, focusing on identifying key events such as transitions between sub-tasks, the next object to interact with, or specific placement locations.

**Optimal Behavior Prompt Representation (Ablations on DrawAnything-Sim):**
- **Multi-modal Sensorimotor Information:** Including both observations and actions in the prompt was found to be necessary. Observations provided grounding for prompt lookup, while actions offered useful temporal transitions between downsampled observations. Proprioception was less impactful when cursor position was visually represented in observations.
- **Observation Frequency:** Aggressive downsampling of prompt observations (below 1Hz) led to performance degradation, suggesting the need for sufficiently high-frequency observational data to maintain fidelity to the demonstration.
- **Attention Pooling:** Using attention pooling to aggregate modalities within each prompt chunk was beneficial, as it helped temporally associate information and reduced the prompt sequence length by merging modalities into single embeddings.

**Training Data Requirements (Ablations on DrawAnything-Sim):**
- **Task Diversity over Quantity per Task:** For a fixed demonstration budget, training on a higher diversity of tasks (fewer demonstrations per task across many different drawing tasks) resulted in significantly better test-time adaptation to unseen tasks, as compared to training on fewer tasks with many demonstrations per task.
- **Increased Task Count:** Increasing the total number of training tasks improved performance on unseen tasks, even with a consistent number of demonstrations (5) per task.
- **Complex Training Tasks:** Training on more complex drawings (e.g., 4 to 6 parts stitched together) yielded better adaptation to complex, unseen drawings. Conversely, training exclusively on low-complexity drawings (1 to 3 parts) resulted in poor generalization to complex unseen tasks. However, training on highly complex drawings required a substantially larger dataset.

**Case Study: Laundry Folding (Low Task Diversity Limitation):**
In a case study with three real-world laundry folding tasks (low task diversity), BPP exhibited weaker task conditioning compared to language conditioning. BPP occasionally completed a different task than instructed, suggesting that in settings with limited task diversity, the rich temporal and spatial information in behavior prompts might introduce unnecessary complexity and variation, potentially leading to overfitting on spurious cues rather than providing clear task disambiguation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of behavior prompting, BPP, iPhUMI, and the new evaluation benchmarks represents a contribution to the field of robot learning, with several potential impacts:

**Flexible and Scalable Robot Skill Acquisition:**
Behavior prompting offers a paradigm for teaching robots new skills that bypasses the need for extensive retraining or fine-tuning. This enhances the flexibility and scalability of robot deployment, enabling users to rapidly introduce novel capabilities "on the fly." This is particularly impactful for applications requiring frequent adaptation to new tasks or user preferences.

**Intuitive Human-Robot Interaction:**
The iPhUMI interface provides a practical and intuitive method for humans to command robots. By allowing users to specify tasks via a single demonstration, it lowers the barrier for non-experts to interact with and program robots. This could lead to more accessible and user-friendly robotic systems in various domains, from industrial settings to personal assistive robotics. The ability for humans to express preferences or demonstrate specific manipulation strategies directly could personalize robot behavior more effectively.

**Enhanced Task Specification:**
Behavior prompts address a fundamental limitation of traditional task descriptors like language commands or goal images, which often fail to convey the intricate spatial and temporal details of "how" a task should be performed. By providing a full sensorimotor example, behavior prompting offers a more complete and unambiguous specification for complex, fine-grained, or temporally extended tasks (e.g., drawing, multi-step manipulation).

**Advancing Research in Generalization:**
The introduction of DrawAnything and LIBERO-Gen benchmarks provides a standardized and procedurally generated framework for rigorously evaluating test-time adaptation to unseen tasks and behaviors. These benchmarks, with their high task diversity and focus on action generalization, can facilitate reproducible scientific study and accelerate progress in areas like closed-loop visual control and learning low-level, continuous actions. They mitigate the need for industrial-scale data collection for initial research.

**Foundation for Future Robot Learning Architectures:**
The findings regarding the importance of task diversity and the mechanisms of prompt utilization can inform the design of future generalist robot policies, potentially leading to "large foundation behavior prompting models." Such models could leverage extensive pretraining and then adapt to new environments or specific user preferences through just a single demonstration, using the rich information within the prompt to understand the target environment and desired manipulation strategy.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
While significant, the research also highlights limitations. Behavior prompting may not always be superior to simpler task descriptors if language or goal images are sufficient for a given task. Furthermore, high task diversity is a crucial prerequisite for effective test-time adaptation, and the current approach may not generalize to entirely novel action primitives. The observed weaker task conditioning in low-diversity settings (e.g., laundry folding) suggests areas for further refinement. Future work could focus on integrating behavior prompts with other task descriptors, extending capabilities to dexterous hands, bridging the sim-to-real gap for prompts, and combining behavior prompting with foundation-level pretraining.
