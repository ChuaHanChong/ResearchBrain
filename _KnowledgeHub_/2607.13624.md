# From Language to Navigation Goals: A Vision-Language Approach for Semantic Navigation of Mobile Robots Using RGB-D Perception

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the University Pablo de Olavide developed a ROS 2 framework that enables mobile robots to interpret natural language navigation commands and autonomously reach semantically specified destinations. The system integrates Vision-Language Models with RGB-D perception to translate linguistic references into executable metric navigation goals, achieving average localization errors around 0.5-0.7 meters in both simulated and real-world environments.

## Problem

- Traditional mobile robot navigation often requires specialized user knowledge to input explicit goal definitions like metric coordinates, waypoints, or predefined semantic maps.
- Bridging the gap between abstract natural language instructions and concrete, executable robot actions remains a challenge in human-robot interaction.
- Many Vision-and-Language Navigation (VLN) approaches rely on task-specific datasets, end-to-end learned policies, or simulation-centric settings, posing integration difficulties with conventional robotic navigation frameworks.

## Method

- A modular ==ROS 2== framework processes natural language instructions, integrating a remote ==Vision-Language Model (VLM)== server for semantic understanding and ==RGB-D perception== for geometric localization.
- The system identifies target objects referenced in natural language using VLM-generated ==bounding boxes==, calculates their 3D positions from depth data, and applies an adjustable ==depth offset== for practical robot positioning.
- Localized 3D target points are transformed into global map coordinates and sent as navigation goals to a standard ROS 2 ==Navigation2 (Nav2)== stack for autonomous path planning and execution.

## Results

- The Semantic Perception Module demonstrated an average localization error of **0.68 meters** in simulation for a bus stop target and **0.51 meters** in real-world validation for microwave and chair targets.
- End-to-end navigation in simulation achieved an average navigation error of **0.70 meters** with an average execution time of **26 seconds** for person and mailbox targets.
- The framework successfully transferred from simulation to a physical Unitree Go2 robot, consistently identifying targets and generating accurate bounding boxes across varied linguistic formulations and viewpoints.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Combining Vision-Language Models with RGB-D geometric projection allows for the direct translation of abstract linguistic concepts into physically meaningful metric navigation goals for mobile robots.
> - A modular ROS 2 architecture facilitates portability and easy integration with existing robotic navigation infrastructures, making language-driven capabilities accessible across diverse platforms.
> - Applying a calculated depth offset to VLM-detected object centroids enables the robot to position itself at a practical distance from the target, rather than its exact geometric center, enhancing navigation utility.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses the challenge of enhancing human-robot interaction (HRI) for mobile robots, particularly for non-expert users. Traditionally, mobile robots have relied on explicit goal definitions, such as metric coordinates, manually selected waypoints, or predefined semantic maps, which necessitate specialized knowledge from users. The growing accessibility of robotic platforms to a wider audience creates a demand for more intuitive communication methods, with natural language emerging as a user-friendly modality.

Recent advancements in Vision-Language Models (VLMs) have significantly improved the ability of systems to interpret natural language in the context of visual information. Models like CLIP and BLIP-2 have demonstrated robust capabilities in associating linguistic concepts with visual elements, enabling semantic understanding and visual grounding. This has led to their adoption in robotics, where they facilitate understanding human intentions and translating them into robot actions. Previous works, such as SayCan, RT-2, PaLM-E, and $\pi^{0.5}$, have explored integrating foundation models for tasks ranging from action planning to unified vision-language-control architectures.

The field of Vision-and-Language Navigation (VLN) specifically focuses on enabling agents to follow natural language instructions using visual observations. While benchmarks like Room-to-Room have spurred extensive research, many VLN approaches often depend on task-specific datasets, end-to-end learned policies, or simulation-centric settings, which can pose integration challenges with conventional robotic navigation frameworks. A critical step in language-driven navigation is the conversion of semantic references into physically meaningful navigation goals. Early efforts focused on mapping instructions to actions and visual goals, while more recent methods, like VLMaps, leverage VLM features to create semantic maps for object-centric navigation. Complementing this, RGB-D sensing is a prevalent perception modality in mobile robotics, providing essential geometric information for localization and navigation. When combined with classical geometric projection techniques, RGB-D data can transform visual detections into metric coordinates suitable for standard navigation systems such as ROS 2 Navigation2 (Nav2).

This research differentiates itself by proposing a lightweight and portable ROS 2 framework designed to directly translate natural language requests into executable navigation goals. Unlike some VLN and embodied AI approaches that learn comprehensive navigation policies, this work integrates VLM-based semantic understanding with RGB-D geometric localization to estimate target positions in metric space. This design prioritizes compatibility with existing ROS 2 and Nav2 infrastructures, facilitating deployment across various robotic platforms equipped with RGB-D cameras without requiring the development of new navigation policies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a language-driven navigation framework that allows mobile robots to interpret natural language commands from non-expert users and autonomously navigate to specified destinations. This objective is motivated by the increasing presence of mobile robots outside specialized industrial or research environments, which necessitates more intuitive human-robot interaction paradigms.

The core motivations and specific objectives of the work are detailed as follows:

- **Enabling Intuitive Human-Robot Interaction:** The researchers aim to overcome the limitations of traditional robot navigation systems, which often require users to input explicit metric coordinates, select predefined waypoints, or possess programming knowledge. By utilizing natural language, the framework seeks to provide a user-friendly communication modality that allows non-expert individuals to command robots using high-level instructions (e.g., "go to the fridge" or "move to the door") rather than technical specifications. This abstraction of low-level robot control is intended to democratize robot usage.

- **Bridging Language Understanding and Robot Action:** A key challenge in language-driven navigation is the transformation of abstract linguistic concepts into concrete, executable navigation actions. The framework is motivated by the need to enable robots to relate natural language references to physical elements in their environment, estimate their positions, and generate actionable navigation goals.

- **Leveraging Vision-Language Models and RGB-D Perception:** The research is motivated by the advancements in Vision-Language Models (VLMs) and RGB-D sensing. VLMs offer the capability to jointly process visual and textual information, associating linguistic concepts with visual cues. By integrating VLMs with RGB-D perception, the system aims to not only identify target objects referenced in natural language but also accurately estimate their three-dimensional positions in the physical environment.

- **Developing a Modular and Portable Robotic Framework:** A specific objective is to implement the framework using modular ROS 2 components. This design choice is motivated by the desire to create a flexible and portable system that can be deployed on diverse robotic platforms equipped with RGB-D cameras. The utilization of the ROS 2 Navigation2 (Nav2) stack for autonomous navigation is intended to ensure compatibility with existing robotic infrastructures.

The main contributions of the paper, reflecting these objectives, are summarized as:
- The development of a language-driven navigation framework that integrates semantic perception and autonomous navigation to convert natural language requests into executable navigation goals.
- The creation of a methodology for grounding VLM detections in metric space through the use of RGB-D data, which facilitates the generation of physically meaningful navigation objectives.
- The implementation and validation of a comprehensive ROS 2-based pipeline capable of interpreting both direct and contextual navigation requests without requiring explicit coordinates or predefined waypoints.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed language-driven navigation framework is structured as a system of modular ROS 2 components designed to process natural language instructions and facilitate autonomous robot navigation. The approach integrates natural language understanding, environment perception using RGB-D data, and a standard navigation stack.

The system architecture comprises four main modules that cooperate to achieve the overall objective:

**A. User Module:**
This module serves as the human-robot interface, enabling users to issue navigation commands via natural language, eliminating the need for metric coordinates or predefined waypoints. It processes user requests such as "go to the refrigerator" or "navigate to the chair." Following processing, the module generates natural-language feedback (e.g., "Okay, I will go to the chair") to confirm the interpreted destination to the user before navigation commences.

**B. Communication Module:**
Operating as the central orchestrator, this module manages the execution logic and information flow across the system. It controls data acquisition, processing, and transmission. Implemented with ROS 2, it leverages topics, services, and actions for inter-module communication, providing an abstraction layer that allows other modules to function independently of specific robot hardware. Upon receiving a command from the User Module, it acquires RGB and depth data from the robot's sensors, forwards the request to the Semantic Perception Module, and delivers the localized target to the Movement Module for execution. It also aggregates feedback from both the VLM and navigation processes.

**C. Semantic Perception Module:**
This module bridges natural language understanding and robot perception. Its function is to communicate with a remote Vision-Language Model (VLM) server, process the user’s navigation request, identify the visual target in the robot's RGB image, and translate this detection into a three-dimensional navigation objective in the global map frame.
1. **VLM Interaction:** The module sends the user's natural language instruction and the RGB image to the VLM server. The VLM processes this multimodal input to identify the referenced object and returns its location as a bounding box defined by top-left (p_tl) and bottom-right (p_br) corner coordinates. Concurrently, the VLM generates a natural language response for the user, confirming its interpretation.
2. **Target Image Point Selection:** A single representative image point for the detected target is derived by calculating the center of the bounding box: p = (p_x, p_y) = (p_tl + p_br) / 2.
3. **Depth Estimation:** To estimate the target's depth, the module queries the depth image D(u, v) using the selected image coordinates (p_x, p_y). To enhance robustness against noise and missing depth values, the depth value (d) is computed as the minimum valid depth within a (2r + 1) × (2r + 1) square neighborhood centered at (p_x, p_y). Invalid measurements (zero or infinite depth) are discarded.
4. **Depth Offset Application:** An adjustable offset ($\delta$) is applied to the estimated depth to position the robot at a suitable distance from the target rather than its precise geometric center. The corrected depth is d' = d - $\delta$. The value of $\delta$ is determined based on the characteristics of the target object (e.g., 0.6 m for a bus stop, 0.3 m for a person/mailbox, 0.5 m for a microwave/chair).
5. **3D Position Calculation:** Using the pinhole camera model, the 3D position (P_c = (X, Y, Z)) of the target in the camera's reference frame is calculated from (p_x, p_y), d', and the camera's intrinsic parameters (c_x, c_y, f_x, f_y).
6. **Transformation to Global Map Frame:** The local 3D point P_c is transformed into the global map reference frame (P_m) using the rigid-body transformation m_T_c obtained from the ROS 2 TF framework. P_m then constitutes the final navigation goal.

**D. Movement Module:**
This module acts as the intermediary between the high-level navigation goals generated by the Semantic Perception Module and the robot's underlying navigation stack. It converts the received navigation objective (P_m) into an executable navigation action and monitors its progress. It sends the goal position to the ROS 2 Navigation Stack (Nav2), which is responsible for path planning, obstacle avoidance (using global and local costmaps generated from onboard sensors like LiDAR and depth cameras), and generating velocity commands (cmd_vel) for the robot's locomotion system. The module continuously receives feedback from Nav2, including task progress, completion status, or failures, but assumes the presence of a functional low-level controller on the robot.

The entire framework leverages ROS 2 for its modularity and inter-component communication, facilitating portability across different robotic platforms equipped with RGB-D sensors by merely configuring the relevant ROS 2 topics and services.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The proposed language-driven navigation framework was evaluated through three distinct experiments, encompassing both simulated and real-world scenarios.

**A. Experiment 1: Semantic Perception Evaluation (Simulation)**
This experiment aimed to assess the accuracy of the Semantic Perception Module in generating navigation goals from natural language instructions. Navigation actions were not executed, isolating the perception pipeline's performance.
- **Setup:** Conducted in Gazebo using a TurtleBot3 Waffle. A single target object, a bus stop, was used. Four trials were performed with varied natural language prompts and initial robot poses, leading to different viewing angles and distances to the target. The depth offset ($\delta$) was set to 0.6 m. The ground-truth target position was (18.0, 0.0) m.
- **Metric:** The localization error ($e_{goal}$) was defined as the Euclidean distance between the ground-truth target position and the estimated goal.
- **Results:** The system demonstrated consistent identification of the bus stop target across linguistic variations and changes in robot viewpoint. An average localization error of 0.68 m was recorded across all trials. This non-zero error was considered expected due to the intentional depth offset applied to position the robot at a practical distance from the target, rather than its exact geometric center. Qualitative observations indicated that the VLM consistently generated bounding boxes around the target despite variations in perspective.

**B. Experiment 2: End-to-End Navigation Evaluation (Simulation)**
This experiment evaluated the complete language-driven navigation framework, including semantic perception, goal generation, and autonomous navigation execution.
- **Setup:** Performed in Gazebo with a TurtleBot3 Waffle. Six experiments were conducted, involving two distinct semantic targets (a person and a mailbox), various natural language formulations (e.g., "Reach the person," "I need to send a letter. Can you give me a way to send it?"), and different initial robot poses. The depth offset ($\delta$) was set to 0.3 m for these targets.
- **Metrics:** The navigation error ($e_{nav}$) was defined as the Euclidean distance between the final robot pose after navigation and the ground-truth target position. Additionally, execution time, traveled distance, and the VLM's natural language response were recorded.
- **Results:** The framework successfully combined semantic perception and autonomous navigation. The robot consistently identified the intended targets, provided appropriate natural language feedback (e.g., "I have located the person"), and reached the desired destinations. An average navigation error of 0.70 m was observed. The average execution time was 26 seconds, with an average traveled distance of 6.22 meters. The non-zero navigation error was again attributed to the applied depth offset, the physical dimensions of the target objects, and the obstacle avoidance behavior of Nav2. Errors for the mailbox target were noted as larger than for the person target, likely due to the mailbox's larger size influencing the offset and target centroid. Qualitative sequences demonstrated successful navigation towards the semantically identified targets.

**C. Experiment 3: Real-World Validation (Physical Robot)**
This experiment aimed to validate the Semantic Perception Module's transferability from simulation to real-world environments.
- **Setup:** Conducted using a Unitree Go2 robot equipped with an Intel RealSense RGB-D camera. Target objects included a microwave and a chair. Both direct (e.g., "Reach the microwave") and contextual (e.g., "I need something to heat up my food") natural language prompts were used, with varied robot viewpoints and scene configurations. The depth offset ($\delta$) was set to 0.5 m. Ground-truth target positions were obtained via direct environmental measurements, and localization errors were computed in the robot's local reference frame.
- **Metric:** The localization error ($e_{goal}$) was computed as the Euclidean distance between the estimated and ground-truth target coordinates in the robot's local frame.
- **Results:** The Semantic Perception Module demonstrated successful generalization to real-world conditions. The system accurately identified the requested objects, yielding an average localization error of 0.51 m. This error value was considered comparable to the applied depth offset, suggesting that a significant portion of the error was attributable to the intentional displacement for practical navigation. Qualitative results showed that the VLM consistently identified targets and generated accurate bounding boxes despite variations in object category, viewpoint, scene layout, and language formulation, confirming the approach's transferability to physical robotic platforms.

In summary, the experiments demonstrated that the framework reliably interprets natural language, semantically grounds objects using RGB-D data, and generates executable navigation goals, enabling autonomous navigation in both simulated and real-world settings.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of robotics by developing a practical and portable framework for language-driven navigation. Its significance lies in several key areas:

- **Enhanced Human-Robot Interaction for Non-Experts:** The primary impact is the provision of an intuitive communication paradigm for mobile robots, enabling non-expert users to command robots using natural language. This moves beyond traditional methods that require explicit coordinates or programming knowledge, thereby increasing the accessibility and usability of robotic platforms for a broader audience. This advancement is crucial for the wider adoption of robots in daily life and various service industries.

- **Bridging High-Level Commands and Low-Level Robot Control:** The framework effectively bridges the gap between abstract natural language instructions and concrete robot navigation actions. By integrating Vision-Language Models with RGB-D perception and a standard navigation stack (Nav2), it offers a method to translate semantic understanding into physically meaningful navigation goals, a fundamental capability for intelligent autonomous systems.

- **Modularity and Portability:** The ROS 2-based, modular architecture is designed for portability, allowing deployment on diverse robotic platforms equipped with common RGB-D sensors. This flexibility is significant as it reduces the development overhead for integrating language-driven capabilities into existing or new robotic systems, fostering wider application and research.

- **Robustness and Real-World Applicability:** The validation across various simulated scenarios and a physical robotic platform demonstrates the framework's robustness against variations in linguistic formulations, robot viewpoints, and environmental conditions. The successful transferability from simulation to real-world environments indicates its practical applicability beyond controlled laboratory settings.

- **Foundation for Future Research:** By releasing the source code as open-source upon acceptance, the researchers aim to promote reproducibility and facilitate further advancements in language-driven robotics. This transparency can accelerate research into more complex multi-step tasks, adaptive goal generation (e.g., dynamic offset estimation), and performance evaluation in more challenging and dynamic environments.

Overall, this work demonstrates the feasibility of combining semantic perception and autonomous navigation through VLMs to establish an intuitive human-robot interaction paradigm. Its impact includes making robots more accessible, adaptable, and capable of understanding human intent expressed through natural language, paving the way for more natural and collaborative human-robot ecosystems.
