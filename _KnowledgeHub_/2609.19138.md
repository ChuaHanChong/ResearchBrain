# In-Context Robot Learning with VLM Agents

> [!summary] Summary
> GPT-Policy is a general-agent framework that lets a frozen off-the-shelf VLM control real robots through in-context learning, with no gradient updates. A context compiler keyframes human or robot demonstration videos, goal images, and interaction history; the VLM emits parameterized Cartesian tool calls; a constrained IK controller verifies, executes, and reports outcomes. With GPT-6 Astra, human videos lift towel and notebook pickup from 0/3 to 2/3.

## Problem

- Robot policies trained on finite demonstration sets cannot cover every deployment situation, and in-context learning from test-time demonstrations or feedback remains largely beyond existing robotic policies.
- Recent in-context robot models such as GEN-1.5, S1, and Zero-WAM require dedicated embodied training, leaving unclear how much adaptation off-the-shelf general-purpose VLMs already support.
- Prior VLM-robotics agents rely on affordance skill selection, code synthesis, text-encoded poses, or discrete semantic actions, without systematically testing which context forms guide continuous Cartesian control.

## Method

- **Context compiler**: a vision model selects demonstration keyframes over overlapping windows, then globally deduplicates to at most 24 keyframes, interleaved with timestamps, camera labels, stage annotations, and optionally time-aligned end-effector actions.
- **Fixed VLM policy with structured tool interface**: the frozen model returns one ==JSON tool call== per decision (move_to, move_eef_chunk, set_gripper, check_path, locate_point, done/give_up) with ==absolute TCP poses== and evidence notes.
- **Constrained Cartesian execution harness**: ==linear-plus-SLERP pose interpolation==, sequentially seeded IK with residual checks (2 mm, about 1 degree), ==Ruckig jerk-limited retiming==, and settling feedback or rejection reasons returned for closed-loop replanning.

## Results

- Human video demos, without robot action labels, raise GPT-6 Astra from **0/3** to **2/3** on towel and notebook pickup, cutting decisions **96.3** to **76.7** and **94.0** to **66.7**.
- Adding aligned action references to robot video lifts bottle-cap unscrewing from **2/3** to **3/3** and plug reinsertion from **0/3** to **2/3**, though decisions and time do not consistently drop.
- Goal-image, self-history, and human-interaction tasks all reach **3/3** without no-context baselines; single-run towel pickup gives GPT-6 Astra **100%** progress versus **30%** Fable 5.1, **20%** Kimi K3.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Off-the-shelf VLMs transfer interaction strategies across embodiments from human video alone, suggesting task-level understanding for robot in-context learning may already exist without dedicated policy training.
> - Context granularity should match task precision: sparse video keyframes convey what and in what order, but contact-sensitive tasks need dense numeric action references to disambiguate motion between keyframes.
> - Better understanding does not ensure reliable execution: precise contact, outcome verification, and inter-arm collision safety stay unsolved, favoring slow VLM agents for reasoning paired with fast VLA/WAM controllers.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

This work addresses a central problem in embodied artificial intelligence: how robots can adapt to tasks and environments that were not represented in their training data. Conventional robot policies are typically trained on large collections of demonstrations, sensorimotor trajectories, or language-action examples. However, no finite dataset can exhaustively describe the object arrangements, physical interactions, and task variations that a robot may encounter after deployment.

The paper places its contribution at the intersection of three research areas. The first is in-context learning (ICL), originally developed in language modeling and later applied to imitation learning and robot policy conditioning. In robotic ICL, demonstrations, goal images, interaction histories, or feedback are supplied at test time, allowing a fixed policy to adapt without gradient updates or persistent changes to task-specific parameters. The second area is vision-language-action modeling, in which multimodal models connect visual and linguistic understanding to executable robot behavior. The third is general-purpose agentic systems that use tools, iterative planning, and execution feedback to complete multistep tasks.

Most prior robotic ICL methods use policies specifically trained for robot control. Examples include systems that condition on robot trajectories, human videos, or geometric demonstrations. By contrast, GPT-Policy investigates whether an off-the-shelf, general-purpose vision-language model can perform this adaptation through prompting and tool use alone. This distinction is important because it separates capabilities that may arise from broad multimodal pretraining from capabilities requiring robot-specific training.

The study is also related to earlier systems that use language models for affordance reasoning, program synthesis, spatial planning, and feedback-based policy refinement. Its specific focus, however, is online action selection from heterogeneous contextual sources under a common physical execution interface.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

The main objective is to determine whether a fixed general-purpose VLM can learn from context during robot deployment and translate that context into executable, verifiable behavior in a new scene. The authors define robotic ICL as adaptation based on demonstrations, examples, or interaction experience provided at test time, without updating the model parameters or introducing persistent task-specific changes.

The paper examines several forms of context because each supplies different information:

- A goal image specifies the desired final arrangement but does not describe how to achieve it.
- Human videos demonstrate a procedure across embodiments, requiring the robot to infer corresponding robot actions.
- Robot videos show a procedure in the same or a related embodiment.
- Robot videos paired with recorded states and actions provide more precise motion and gripper references.
- Self-interaction history records previous observations, actions, and outcomes, allowing the robot to use experience gathered during the current task.
- Online human interaction provides feedback, pointing gestures, turn information, or changes in task state.

The authors seek to answer three broader questions. First, what information does each context type contribute to task understanding and action selection? Second, does contextual information improve success while reducing unnecessary decisions and execution time? Third, do these benefits persist in tasks involving deformable objects, contact-sensitive manipulation, mobile exploration, and human-robot interaction?

A further motivation is to identify the limits of VLM-based control. The authors distinguish high-level task interpretation from low-level physical execution. A model may understand that an object must be grasped, inserted, or arranged while still producing an imprecise pose, failing to establish contact, misjudging whether a grasp succeeded, or causing a collision. The study therefore evaluates not only whether context improves planning but also where failures remain in the perception-action loop.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. GPT-Policy Architecture

GPT-Policy connects a fixed VLM to robot tools through a closed-loop interface. At each decision step, the model receives the task instruction, current camera images, measured robot state, task references, interaction history, and the result of the previous tool request. It then selects a structured tool request, such as a Cartesian motion, a gripper command, a geometric check, or a completion report.

The system contains three principal components. The context compiler selects and organizes task-relevant information. For videos, it retains chronological keyframes, camera-view labels, timestamps, annotations, and, when available, aligned state-action records. The VLM interprets this context and proposes parameterized actions. An embodiment-specific execution layer validates, executes, and reports the outcome of each request.

The model policy is represented conceptually as selecting an action \(a_t\) conditioned on the task, context, current observation, and previous feedback. The robot-tool interface then produces a new observation and execution result. The model parameters remain fixed throughout each task.

#### 3.2. Context Construction

Human demonstrations are represented as selected RGB video frames showing approach, contact, manipulation, release, and final outcome. They contain no robot action labels. Robot demonstrations use keyframes from teleoperated trials and may include measured end-effector poses, gripper states, joint states, and sampled action segments. The “Robot Video” and “Robot Video + Action” conditions use identical visual keyframes; the latter adds aligned numerical records.

The context compiler uses local and global review stages to remove redundant frames while preserving important state transitions. In the robot-action condition, recorded trajectories are not replayed directly. Instead, they serve as references that the VLM adapts to the current object arrangement and robot state.

Interaction history is maintained separately from offline demonstrations. It includes prior observations, tool requests, execution outcomes, and operator feedback. Older visual observations may be limited or summarized to manage context length while retaining the task’s progression.

#### 3.3. Robot Execution

The VLM issues absolute Cartesian tool-center-point poses represented by position and quaternion orientation. Motion requests may contain a single target or a sequence of waypoints. The controller interpolates positions linearly and orientations using quaternion spherical linear interpolation. It then solves inverse kinematics for sampled poses, checks position and orientation residuals, applies joint limits, and uses Ruckig-based timing to constrain velocity, acceleration, and jerk where supported.

Gripper commands are issued separately from Cartesian motion. After execution, the system returns measured state, endpoint errors, settling information, rejection causes, and fresh camera observations. These results allow the VLM to replan. Importantly, accepted motion, stable joints, or a model-generated completion statement are not treated as proof of physical task success.

The experiments use several robot configurations, including two-arm systems and a mobile manipulation platform. The sensing setups use overhead, wrist-mounted, head, and chest RGB cameras. The manuscript states that collision checking is not integrated into the main Cartesian planner.

#### 3.4. Evaluation Design

Each episode begins from a reset scene and ends after success, budget exhaustion, or safety termination. Success is determined by task-specific geometric and semantic criteria. Each condition is repeated three times. The reported metrics are success rate, number of VLM decisions, and total execution time.

The tasks cover human-video imitation, contact-rich manipulation, goal-image arrangement, self-guided exploration, and human-robot interaction. A matched comparison across models is also conducted on red towel pickup using GPT-6 Astra, Fable 5.1, and Kimi K3, although the comparison uses individual runs rather than a statistically powered evaluation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Human Video Context

For both “Pick Red Towel” and “Pick Up Notebook,” the no-video condition achieved zero successes in three trials, whereas the human-video condition achieved two successes out of three. For towel pickup, average decisions decreased from 96.3 to 76.7 and execution time from 24.6 to 18.9 minutes. For notebook pickup, decisions decreased from 94.0 to 66.7 and time from 24.6 to 16.1 minutes.

These results indicate that the VLM can extract procedural information from human behavior and transfer it across embodiments. The robot generated its own robot-specific motions rather than replaying human actions. The demonstrations appear particularly useful for deformable-object handling and for constraining the order of approach, stabilization, grasping, and lifting.

#### 4.2. Robot Video and Action References

For “Unscrew Bottle Cap,” success increased from zero out of three with no demonstration to two out of three with robot video and three out of three with robot video plus action references. The corresponding average decision counts were 71.0, 74.3, and 54.7. For “Remove and Reinsert Plug,” no condition achieved success without additional action references; robot video alone remained at zero out of three, while robot video plus action achieved two out of three.

Action references improved alignment with demonstrated orientations, support postures, and intermediate motion. The paper attributes this effect to the greater temporal density of the action records, which reduces ambiguity between sparse video keyframes. However, action references did not consistently reduce execution time. In some cases, the system made more decisions or spent longer executing failed trials, showing that better procedural guidance does not automatically produce efficient control.

#### 4.3. Goal Images, Self-History, and Interaction

The goal-image condition achieved three out of three successes on both “Arrange T Shape” and “Arrange Fruit,” with average decision counts of 66.7 and 49.0 and execution times of 15.8 and 12.4 minutes, respectively. The images conveyed object identity, relative position, and spacing more directly than text alone. Because no corresponding baseline results are reported for these tasks in the table, the magnitude of the goal-image benefit cannot be isolated.

Self-history produced three out of three successes for both “Lemon to Pink Plate” and “Movable Exploration.” The agent removed a towel to reveal a concealed plate and avoided obstacles during mobile search, suggesting that retained interaction history supported intermediate subgoals and active information gathering. Mobile exploration took 25.53 minutes despite requiring approximately 40 decisions, illustrating that decision count and physical execution time measure different aspects of efficiency.

Online human interaction also achieved three out of three successes for tic-tac-toe and pointed fruit pickup. The system tracked turns, board state, pointing gestures, and stop conditions. In observed games, it selected strategically appropriate moves while respecting turn-taking requirements.

#### 4.4. Model Comparison and Failure Modes

In individual red-towel runs, GPT-6 Astra progressed from 55% completion without video to 100% with human video. Its video-conditioned run took 15.85 minutes and used an estimated 4.956 million tokens, compared with 24.63 minutes and 12.047 million tokens without video. Fable 5.1 and Kimi K3 reached 30% and 20% progress, respectively, but these examples do not establish a reliable model ranking because the sample is very small and task progress is not equivalent to success rate.

The most consequential limitations concern physical execution. The VLM sometimes selected appropriate high-level strategies but failed at precise contact, grasp stability, insertion, outcome verification, or collision avoidance. The authors report repeated inter-arm collisions. IK acceptance and settling feedback were insufficient to guarantee clearance or task completion. These observations demonstrate that contextual reasoning and low-level control reliability remain partially separate problems.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The paper provides an empirical framework for studying robotic ICL with general-purpose VLM agents. Its main contribution is not a newly trained robot policy but a shared interface that allows different kinds of context to be compared under closed-loop physical execution. The results suggest that human videos, action-aligned robot demonstrations, goal images, interaction histories, and live human cues can each guide robot behavior in distinct ways.

If developed further, this approach could reduce the need to collect robot-specific demonstrations for every new task. Human demonstrations may communicate procedures, while goal images and interaction feedback may specify outcomes or changing intentions. A general agent could therefore handle more task variation through context rather than parameter updates.

The findings also define important requirements for deployment. Future systems need independent safety layers, bimanual collision checking, contact and slip detection, reliable physical success verification, faster low-level controllers, active perception, and methods for adapting to changing physical dynamics. The paper’s evidence is limited by small task series, three trials per condition, selected platforms and contexts, incomplete ablations, unknown pretraining effects, and model-provider dependence. Consequently, the results support the feasibility of context-guided adaptation but do not yet establish broad reliability or autonomous safety.
