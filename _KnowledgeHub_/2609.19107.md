# How Model Growth, Recursion, and Boundary Operators Influence Scaling Exponents

> [!summary] Summary
> A prelude-core-coda transformer family showing that architecture can change pretraining scaling exponents, not just constants. Growing depth mid-training by doubling core passes (tied or untied), plus a normalize-and-reinject boundary operator, yields compute multipliers over vanilla transformers that widen with scale, reaching 1.55× at 1e20 FLOPs. A 7.4B grown model matches GPT-3 13B on CORE with roughly 20× less compute.

## Problem

- Conventional wisdom holds that architectural changes only shift scaling-law constants, never exponents, so architecture is largely ignored as a lever for compounding pretraining efficiency gains.
- Prior model-growth and looping studies report gains at one or a few sizes without architecture-specific compute-optimal tuning, and controlled comparisons found recurrence no better or worse than dense transformers.
- Pre-norm transformers suffer the curse of depth: the residual stream grows, deep blocks barely change predictions, and existing fixes improve loss at fixed size but never the exponent.

## Method

- **Prelude-core-coda** family: one network in which a ==core block runs K times== between prelude and coda, isolating ==boundary operator==, ==weight tying== and ==growth== through matched **Vanilla**, **Operator**, **Loop**, **Untied** and **Grow** variants.
- ==Boundary operator Norm(h)+αe==: ==RMS-normalizes the residual stream== and ==re-injects the prelude output== before every core pass and, unlike prior looped transformers, before the coda as well.
- **Loop-Grow** and **Untied-Grow**: double the core passes from two to four at one-half to four-fifths of training, either reusing ==tied weights== or copying and then ==untying cores==, each with its own fitted ==compute-optimal recipe==.

## Results

- On FineWeb ladders from **1e18** to **1e20 FLOPs**, Untied-Grow's compute multiplier over Vanilla rises from **1.30×** to **1.55×** and the exponent improves from **0.111** to **0.117**; tied Loop-Grow reaches **1.36×**.
- The boundary operator alone lifts Operator-1 from **1.12×** to **1.25×** at matched FLOPs, untying adds only a flat constant, and growth raises KL effective depth to **36** versus **24**.
- A **7.4B** Untied-Grow run at **8×** the fitted compute lands on the forecast with **0.387** CORE versus GPT-3 13B's **0.385**; multi-epoch loop scaling matches a weight-decay-tuned baseline with **2.2×** less compute.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Judge new architectures by whether their compute multipliers widen with scale: exponent gains compound while constant gains do not, and reusing another architecture's hyperparameters can hide exponent gains entirely.
> - Usable computational depth, not executed depth, drives efficiency: interventions that remove wasted depth, whether inert blocks or depth paid for before it is needed, plausibly scale better with budget.
> - Under repeated data, models overfit through the parameters they store rather than the blocks they execute, so weight-tied looping adds capacity cheaply and the optimal loop count grows with compute.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

Scaling laws have become a central framework for understanding language-model development. Prior work has shown that validation loss generally decreases according to approximate power laws as training compute, parameter count, and data volume increase. These relationships are commonly used to determine compute-optimal allocations between model size and training tokens. In this framework, an intervention can improve either the **scaling constant**, which produces an approximately fixed efficiency advantage, or the **scaling exponent**, which causes the efficiency advantage to grow with scale.

Most architectural modifications have traditionally been assumed to affect primarily the scaling constant. This paper challenges that assumption by showing that several architectural and training interventions can alter the exponent of the compute–loss relationship. The authors focus on computational depth: the number of transformations that meaningfully affect a model’s predictive distribution under a given compute budget.

The work connects several research areas. First, it builds on standard dense-transformer scaling laws and compute-optimal training. Second, it relates to **model growth**, in which a model is expanded during training by adding or copying layers. Earlier growth studies generally emphasized reducing the cost of training a model at a particular target size, often through checkpoint reuse, rather than changing the asymptotic scaling law. Third, it draws on **looped or recursive transformers**, which repeatedly apply a core block, either with shared weights or with separate weights for each pass. Such models have been studied for parameter efficiency, iterative computation, reasoning, and inference-time compute scaling. Finally, the paper addresses the “curse of depth,” whereby later transformer blocks may make progressively smaller contributions because the residual stream grows with depth.

The paper also considers data-constrained pretraining. As high-quality text becomes scarce relative to available compute, models may need to train for multiple epochs over the same corpus. This creates overfitting problems and changes the trade-off between adding parameters and adding computation through recurrence.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective is to determine whether architectural interventions can modify the compute-optimal scaling exponent during language-model pretraining. The authors ask three related questions:

1. Can increasing model depth during training improve the scaling exponent rather than only the scaling constant?
2. Can recursive weight sharing provide a parameter-efficient form of model growth?
3. Can a boundary operator that normalizes and re-injects an earlier representation make deep computation more usable and thereby improve scaling?

The motivation is based on the hypothesis that the computational depth of a model should increase with available compute, but that ordinary fixed-depth training may use depth inefficiently. The authors identify two potential sources of inefficiency. A model may execute layers that do not yet contribute meaningfully early in training, even though those layers become useful later. Alternatively, a deep transformer may execute many layers whose updates have become too small to substantially change the prediction.

Model growth is intended to address the first problem. The model begins with a smaller number of core passes and adds more passes later in training, when the learned function is presumed to require greater complexity. A boundary operator is intended to address the second problem by normalizing the residual stream and injecting the representation produced by the initial “prelude” blocks at each stage.

The study also seeks to establish methodological standards for comparing scaling laws. Because poor hyperparameter transfer can make an architecture appear to have a worse exponent than it would under proper tuning, the authors fit architecture-specific training recipes rather than applying the vanilla transformer’s settings to all variants.

### 3. Methodology and Experimental Approach

#### 3.1. Unified Architecture Family

All models are expressed as a three-stage **prelude–core–coda** network. The prelude maps the input to a representation \(e\), the core is applied \(K\) times, and the coda produces the final output. If the prelude, core, and coda contain \(P\), \(C\), and \(D\) blocks, respectively, the executed depth is

\[
\ell = P + KC + D.
\]

The authors distinguish executed depth from stored depth. Tied looping can increase executed depth without increasing the number of stored parameters, whereas untied looping stores separate parameters for each pass.

The main boundary operator is

\[
\mathrm{BO}(h,e)=\mathrm{Norm}(h)+\alpha e.
\]

It performs two functions: normalization prevents the residual stream from growing without bound, and injection of \(e\) keeps later computation conditioned on the initial input representation. The operator is applied between core passes and before the coda.

The principal variants are:

- **Vanilla:** A standard pre-norm transformer with one core pass and identity boundaries.
- **Operator-1:** A one-pass model with the boundary operator inserted, isolating the operator’s effect at matched parameters and FLOPs.
- **Loop-2:** Two core passes with tied weights.
- **Untied-2:** Two core passes with independent weights, isolating weight sharing from depth.
- **Loop-Grow:** A tied model that grows from two to four core passes during training.
- **Untied-Grow:** An untied model whose trained core blocks are copied and then independently optimized after the growth transition.
- **Deep Vanilla and Deep Vanilla Grow:** Controls that add depth without the boundary operator.

The three architectural components are scaled proportionally with compute, and the width-to-depth ratio is fixed at 128. The initial growth configuration uses two passes and grows to four passes.

#### 3.2. Training and Scaling Experiments

The models use decoder-only pre-norm transformers with rotary positional embeddings, SwiGLU feed-forward layers, QK normalization, a context length of 2,048 tokens, and a global batch size of 524,288 tokens. The main experiments use FineWeb with the GPT-2 tokenizer. Matrix parameters are optimized with Muon and embeddings and the language-model head with AdamW. Most training runs use eight H100 GPUs.

The authors construct compute-optimal scaling ladders spanning approximately \(10^{18}\) to \(10^{20}\) FLOPs. For each architecture, they independently tune:

1. Base hyperparameters at a reference model size.
2. The optimal number of tokens per stored parameter.
3. The timing of growth, when applicable.
4. A learning-rate scaling rule for increasing model size.

This procedure is important because architecture-specific recipes differ substantially. For example, transferring the vanilla recipe to Operator-1 eliminates much of its measured exponent advantage.

The compute–loss relationship is modeled as

\[
L(C)=E+A(C/C_0)^{-\gamma},
\]

where \(E\) is an irreducible loss floor, \(A\) is a scaling constant, and \(\gamma\) is the scaling exponent. The authors compare architectures using compute multipliers: the amount of compute required by a reference model to reach the loss achieved by a variant.

The study also includes an extrapolation experiment using FineWeb-Edu. A held-out 7.4-billion-parameter Untied-Grow model is trained at roughly eight times the largest fitted compute and evaluated using the CORE benchmark. Additional ablations examine width-only scaling, boundary-operator components, random recurrence, optimizer choice, runtime, and model shape.

#### 3.3. Data-Constrained Experiments

To study repeated-data training, the authors fix a pool of 100 million unique FineWeb tokens and train for ten epochs, producing a total exposure of one billion tokens. They compare two ways to spend additional compute: increasing stored model size or increasing the number of tied core passes. Weight decay is either fixed or tuned across model sizes.

Finally, the authors estimate effective depth using a logit-lens procedure. They decode intermediate residual streams and identify the point at which the intermediate prediction approaches the final output distribution according to a KL-divergence threshold.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Architectural Changes Can Alter Scaling Exponents

The fitted single-epoch exponents are approximately:

- Vanilla: **0.111**
- Operator-1: **0.114**
- Loop-2 and Untied-2: **0.114**
- Loop-Grow: **0.116**
- Untied-Grow: **0.117**

These differences are small in absolute terms but produce increasing compute advantages with scale. Operator-1 improves from approximately **1.12×** efficiency over vanilla at \(10^{18}\) FLOPs to **1.25×** at \(10^{20}\) FLOPs. Untied-Grow improves from approximately **1.30×** to **1.55×** over the same range.

The results indicate that the boundary operator alone can change the exponent. Because Operator-1 matches vanilla in parameter count and FLOPs, this comparison attributes the difference to the normalization-and-injection mechanism rather than to additional computation.

#### 4.2. Model Growth Provides the Largest Exponent Improvement

Untied-Grow improves both the scaling constant and exponent relative to Untied-2. Its advantage over the fixed-depth counterpart grows from roughly **1.09×** to **1.16×** between \(10^{18}\) and \(10^{20}\) FLOPs. Deep Vanilla Grow shows a similar pattern without the boundary operator, indicating that growth and the boundary operator contribute separately.

The held-out 7.4-billion-parameter Untied-Grow model reaches a validation loss consistent with the extrapolated scaling law. It obtains a CORE score of **0.3865**, compared with an estimated **0.3852** for GPT-3 13B. The model uses approximately \(1.23\times10^{21}\) FLOPs, compared with \(2.31\times10^{22}\) FLOPs for the GPT-3 reference. The authors describe this as roughly a 20-fold compute difference, but note that the comparison is indicative rather than controlled because the models use different data and evaluation pipelines.

Using extrapolated fits, the Untied-Grow advantage over vanilla is estimated at **1.8×** at the 7.4B model’s budget and **2.7×** at \(10^{25}\) FLOPs. These larger-scale estimates depend on the assumption that the fitted power laws continue beyond the measured range.

#### 4.3. Weight Sharing Changes the Constant More Than the Exponent

Untied models perform better than tied models at matched compute, but the gap remains approximately constant with scale. Untied-2 is around 1.06–1.08× more compute-efficient than Loop-2, while Untied-Grow is around 1.14–1.16× more efficient than Loop-Grow.

Thus, weight tying does not produce the main exponent improvement in fresh-data training. Instead, it provides a parameter-storage advantage while retaining most of the scaling benefit of growth.

#### 4.4. Looping Is More Useful Under Data Repetition

In the ten-epoch, data-constrained setting, the optimal number of loops increases with compute. Across the tested budgets, the preferred loop count rises from approximately **1.4 to 6.7**. Higher loop counts delay overfitting and produce lower minimum losses.

Scaling loop count at fixed model size reaches the loss of a weight-decay-tuned model-size scaling strategy with approximately **2.2× less compute**. Additional weight-decay tuning provides only a small improvement. The optimal weight decay increases with stored depth but remains relatively stable as the number of tied loops changes, suggesting that overfitting is more closely associated with stored parameters than with executed depth.

#### 4.5. Computational Depth Supports the Interpretation

The KL effective-depth measurements support the proposed mechanism. At \(10^{20}\) FLOPs, Untied-Grow reaches an effective depth of approximately 36, compared with 24 for Untied-2 and lower values for vanilla controls. In multi-epoch training, scaling the loop count produces greater effective depth than increasing stored model size at one pass.

The authors interpret these results as evidence that boundary operators make more executed layers computationally useful, while model growth avoids paying for excessive depth before it is needed.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The paper’s main contribution is conceptual and methodological: it demonstrates that architecture need not affect only the vertical placement of scaling curves. Under carefully tuned compute-optimal training, model growth and boundary operators can change the exponent itself, creating scale-dependent efficiency gains.

If the findings generalize, they could influence how future language-model architectures are evaluated. Architectural proposals may need to be compared across compute ranges rather than at a single model size, with explicit separation between constant-factor and exponent improvements. Model growth could also reduce the cost of training increasingly deep models by postponing additional computation until it becomes useful.

The results have different implications depending on data availability. With fresh data, untied growth is more efficient, while tied growth offers parameter savings at a constant compute cost. With repeated data, tied looping becomes more attractive because it adds computational capacity without adding as many parameters that can overfit.

Several limitations qualify the conclusions. Most fitted results cover approximately \(10^{18}\)–\(10^{20}\) FLOPs, with only one larger held-out run supporting extrapolation. The large downstream comparison with GPT-3 is not controlled for data, tokenizer, evaluation implementation, or training procedure. The experiments also use one principal transformer family, fixed context length and batch size, a particular width–depth rule, and specific optimizers. Consequently, the exponent improvements should be tested across datasets, architectures, training regimes, and larger model scales before being treated as general scaling principles.
