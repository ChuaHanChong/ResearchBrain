# A Comprehensive Survey of Reward Models: Taxonomy, Applications, Challenges, and Future

> [!summary] Summary
> A comprehensive survey consolidates research on Reward Models (RMs) in the Large Language Model (LLM) era, introducing an elaborate taxonomy for their lifecycle components. It details RM types and granularities, explores diverse applications, and identifies challenges alongside future research directions.

## Problem

- Despite the critical role of Reward Models (RMs) in aligning Large Language Models (LLMs) with human values and safety guidelines via RLHF, a comprehensive and dedicated survey specifically focused on RMs in the LLM era was lacking.
- The rapidly expanding and diverse research on RMs lacked systematic organization and an in-depth analysis, making it challenging for both newcomers and experienced researchers to navigate.
- Existing literature touched upon RMs as part of broader discussions on LLM alignment or RLHF limitations, but did not provide the focused, structured overview necessary to address the complexities of RM development and deployment.

## Method

- The paper introduces the first comprehensive survey specifically focused on parameterized ==Reward Models (RMs)== in the Large Language Model (LLM) era, which are used to reflect human preferences.
- An elaborate ==taxonomy== is developed that systematically reviews RMs across three main dimensions: how preference data is collected (Human Preference vs. AI Preference), the mechanisms of reward modeling (Type Level: Discriminative, Generative, Implicit; Granularity Level: ==Outcome-level, Process-level==), and their various usage scenarios in the LLM lifecycle.
- The survey analyzes the current application landscape of RMs, discusses existing benchmarking and evaluation methodologies, and identifies key challenges and open problems while proposing future research directions to facilitate further advancement in the field.

## Results

- The survey confirms the established role of RMs in LLM alignment and identifies that preference data for training RMs can originate from both human annotation and increasingly from AI models, demonstrating high consistency with human judgment.
- A comprehensive RM taxonomy is presented, categorizing models by type (**discriminative, generative, implicit**) and granularity (**outcome-level vs. process-level**), and their diverse applications across the LLM lifecycle (data selection, policy training, inference) are mapped.
- Significant evaluation challenges are identified, including the lack of general and standardized benchmarks, high cost, and sensitivity to input variations; key challenges like data quality, **overoptimization (reward hacking)**, and evaluation biases are highlighted with proposed future directions.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Reward Models (RMs) are indispensable for aligning Large Language Models (LLMs) with human preferences, acting as crucial proxies for human judgment in Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF).
> - Preference data for RMs can be sourced from both human annotations (with ongoing research to improve efficiency and quality) and increasingly from capable AI models, which offer scalability for superhuman tasks.
> - A multi-faceted taxonomy is critical for understanding RMs, classifying them by underlying model type (discriminative, generative, implicit) and the granularity of their feedback (outcome-level vs. process-level), each with distinct advantages and challenges.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The rapid advancement of Large Language Models (LLMs) has revolutionized the field of artificial intelligence, enabling unprecedented capabilities in various natural language processing tasks. However, the sheer scale and complexity of these models necessitate robust "alignment" techniques to ensure their behavior adheres to human values, safety guidelines, and desired performance criteria. Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) has emerged as a cornerstone of this alignment process, primarily by training a "Reward Model (RM)" that serves as a proxy for human preferences. This RM then provides the necessary reward signals for subsequent reinforcement learning to optimize the LLM's behavior.

While RLHF and LLM alignment have garnered significant research interest, the authors identify a critical gap in the existing literature: a dedicated and comprehensive survey *specifically* focused on Reward Models within the LLM era. Previous surveys, such as those by Ji et al. (2023) and Casper et al. (2023), have touched upon RMs as part of broader discussions on LLM alignment or RLHF limitations. However, they lacked the systematic organization and in-depth analysis solely dedicated to RMs that this paper aims to provide.

This survey positions itself as a foundational resource that consolidates the scattered knowledge surrounding RMs. By offering an elaborate taxonomy and exploring RMs from the perspectives of preference collection, reward modeling mechanisms, and practical usage, the authors intend to bring clarity and structure to a complex and rapidly evolving sub-field. The work acknowledges the increasing shift towards AI preferences due to the high cost of human annotation and the growing capabilities of LLMs to mimic human judgment. It also highlights the critical challenges, such as reward hacking and evaluation biases, which are inherent to RM development and deployment. In essence, this survey seeks to fill a crucial void, providing a structured overview that is vital for both newcomers to the field and experienced researchers looking to push the boundaries of LLM alignment and safety.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations behind this comprehensive survey are clearly articulated by the authors:

- **To present the first comprehensive survey specifically focused on Reward Models (RMs) in the LLM era.** The authors explicitly state this as their core contribution, aiming to provide a singular, in-depth resource on a topic that has previously been covered only partially in broader surveys. This objective is motivated by the critical role RMs play in aligning LLMs with human preferences, a challenge that has become paramount with the increasing scale and capabilities of modern language models.
- **To systematically review related works in the field of RMs and introduce an elaborate taxonomy.** A key motivation is to bring order and structure to the diverse and rapidly expanding body of research on RMs. By categorizing RMs based on their lifecycle components – preference collection, reward modeling techniques, and usage – the survey aims to provide a clear framework for understanding the field. This systematic approach is intended to simplify the learning curve for beginners and offer a consolidated reference for experts.
- **To discuss the challenges and future directions, which facilitate further research.** Beyond summarizing existing knowledge, the authors are motivated to identify the current limitations and open problems in RM research. By highlighting issues such as data quality, overoptimization (reward hacking), and evaluation biases, the survey aims to guide future research efforts toward addressing these critical obstacles. Proposing potential research directions is designed to stimulate innovation and accelerate progress in developing more robust, reliable, and aligned LLMs.

In summary, the overarching motivation is to provide a foundational and guiding resource for the research community, enabling a deeper understanding of RMs, fostering new research, and ultimately contributing to the development of more effective and safer LLMs that truly align with human values.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

As a survey paper, the "methodology" is primarily defined by its comprehensive literature review, systematic organization, and analytical synthesis of existing knowledge. The authors' approach can be broken down into several key steps:

- **Defining Scope:** The survey explicitly focuses on "parameterized RMs in the LLM era, which are used to reflect human preferences." This delimits the scope, differentiating it from earlier reward modeling in general reinforcement learning or other AI domains.
- **Taxonomic Organization:** The core of the methodology is the development and application of a novel, elaborate taxonomy for RMs. This taxonomy structures the vast research landscape into three main dimensions:
 - **Preference Collection (§2.1):** This section investigates how preference data is acquired, distinguishing between Human Preference (e.g., direct human annotation, active learning strategies for efficiency and quality) and AI Preference (e.g., RL from AI Feedback, LLM-as-a-judge, synthetic data generation).
 - **Reward Modeling (§2.2):** This delves into the mechanisms by which RMs process preferences. It is further divided into:
 - *Type Level:* Categorizing RMs based on their underlying model types: Discriminative (e.g., sequence classifiers, custom classifiers), Generative (leveraging LLMs' generative capabilities to produce scores or critiques), and Implicit (methods like DPO that optimize policy directly from preferences without an explicit RM).
 - *Granularity Level:* Classifying RMs based on the detail of the reward signal: Outcome-level Reward Models (ORM), which predict overall correctness, and Process-level Reward Models (PRM), which score individual steps in a reasoning process. The advantages and disadvantages of each are discussed.
 - **Usage (§2.3):** This explores how RMs are applied throughout the LLM lifecycle: for Data Selection (filtering and ranking data for fine-tuning), Policy Training (providing feedback signals for RL, including strategies for robustness like ensemble methods and Bayesian approaches), and Inference (ranking outputs, guiding tree searches, dynamic model invocation).
- **Application Landscape Mapping (§3):** The survey identifies and categorizes the diverse domains where RMs are currently employed, including dialogue, reasoning (mathematics, code generation), retrieval & recommendation systems, and emerging multimodal applications (text-to-audio, text-to-image, text-to-video, robotics, games).
- **Benchmarking and Evaluation Analysis (§4):** The authors dedicate a section to the critical aspect of RM evaluation, discussing existing benchmarks (e.g., RewardBench, RM-Bench, ProcessBench) and highlighting the challenges associated with creating general and standardized evaluation metrics due to cost, dependency on policy performance, and sensitivity to input variations.
- **Challenge Identification and Future Direction Proposal (§5, §6, Appendix A.4):** A crucial part of the methodology is a forward-looking analysis. The authors meticulously identify current challenges related to data (quality, bias), training (overoptimization, reward hacking), and evaluation (intrinsic biases). Based on these challenges, they propose concrete future research directions, such as integrating scalar and rule-based rewards, designing rewards for long-horizontal agent tasks, and empowering multimodal domains. They also pose open questions to stimulate further discussion and research.
- **Accessibility:** The paper aims to be accessible to "beginners" while facilitating "future studies" for all researchers, indicating an emphasis on clear explanations and a logical flow of information. The public availability of resources on GitHub further supports this goal by providing practical tools.

This systematic and structured approach allows the survey to provide a comprehensive yet digestible overview of the RM landscape, serving as both an educational resource and a strategic guide for future advancements.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The survey's main "findings" are not experimental results but rather a structured synthesis of the current state of Reward Model research, revealing key trends, classifications, and persistent challenges.

- **Established Role of RMs in LLM Alignment:** The paper confirms that RMs are indispensable for aligning LLMs with human preferences, primarily through Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), acting as crucial proxies for human judgment.
- **Dual Sources of Preference Data:** Preferences for training RMs can originate from both humans and AI.
 - **Human Preference:** Still valuable, with ongoing research focused on improving data collection efficiency (e.g., active learning, sequential comparisons) and quality (e.g., refined instructions, user interfaces).
 - **AI Preference:** Rapidly gaining traction as LLMs become more capable, demonstrating high consistency with human judgment. This offers scalability and addresses evaluation challenges in superhuman tasks (e.g., RLAIF, LLM-as-a-judge, synthetic data generation).
- **Comprehensive RM Taxonomy (Type and Granularity):**
 - **Type Level:** RMs are categorized into:
 - **Discriminative RMs:** Typically using a base model with an MLP head to output scalar rewards or compare pairs (e.g., InternLM2, PairRM).
 - **Generative RMs:** Leverage LLMs' generative capabilities to produce preference scores or textual critiques (e.g., LLM-as-a-judge, Self-Rewarding LMs).
 - **Implicit RMs:** Directly optimize policies based on preference signals without an explicit reward model (e.g., DPO, SLiC-HF), reducing resource costs but sometimes underperforming explicit optimization.
 - **Granularity Level:** RMs vary in the detail of their feedback:
 - **Outcome-level Reward Models (ORMs):** Predict the correctness of a final answer (e.g., Starling-RM). They are flexible but prone to false positives in reasoning tasks.
 - **Process-level Reward Models (PRMs):** Assign scores to each step in a reasoning process (e.g., Math-Shepherd, PRM800K). PRMs offer dense rewards and better control over reasoning paths, mitigating hallucinations, but are costly to train (especially with human annotation) and suffer from scalability issues.
- **Diverse Applications Across LLM Lifecycle:** RMs are integrated at multiple stages:
 - **Data Selection:** Filtering and ranking LLM-generated responses to create high-quality datasets for fine-tuning (e.g., RAFT, RRHF).
 - **Policy Training:** Providing feedback for reinforcement learning (e.g., ODIN, Bayesian RM, ensemble methods) to improve robustness and mitigate issues like out-of-distribution generalization.
 - **Inference:** Ranking multiple outputs or guiding tree search frameworks to enhance reasoning and optimize resource utilization (e.g., Tree-PLV).
- **Widespread Application Domains:** RMs are successfully applied across various domains, including:
 - **Dialogue:** Improving helpfulness, harmlessness, professionalism, and overall impression.
 - **Reasoning:** Enhancing mathematical reasoning and code generation, particularly with PRMs.
 - **Retrieve & Recommendation:** Aligning retrieval processes and improving recommendation explanations.
 - **Multimodal:** Showing potential in text-to-audio, text-to-image, text-to-video generation, and interactive tasks like robotics and games.
- **Significant Evaluation Challenges:** The evaluation of RMs is complex and costly, lacking general and standardized benchmarks. Direct end-to-end RL policy training is expensive, and RMs are sensitive to input variations, making comprehensive assessment difficult. The survey highlights existing benchmarks (RewardBench, ProcessBench) but stresses the ongoing need for more dynamic and multi-faceted testing.
- **Identified Key Challenges:**
 - **Data Quality:** Biases among annotators, noise, and inconsistencies in feedback.
 - **Training Issues:** Overoptimization (reward hacking), where models exploit superficial reward signals rather than achieving true alignment.
 - **Evaluation Bias:** Intrinsic biases in "LLM-as-a-judge" evaluations, such as format or length biases, and preference leakage from synthetic data generators.
- **Crucial Future Directions:**
 - **Hybrid Reward Systems:** Combining scalar RMs with rule-based rewards for robustness and real-world applicability, especially in tasks with clear ground truths.
 - **Reward Design for Agent Tasks:** Developing effective feedback mechanisms for long-horizontal, complex agent tasks involving tool use and planning.
 - **Multimodal Expansion:** Overcoming challenges in collecting multimodal preference data and designing quality reward signals for cross-modal alignment.
- **Open Questions:** The authors conclude by posing critical open questions, such as the sufficiency of rule-based rewards, the comparative effectiveness of Mixture-of-Experts vs. Bradley-Terry models for RMs, and strategies to prevent reward hacking when LLMs surpass human expert performance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This comprehensive survey holds significant value and potential impact across several dimensions of AI research and development, particularly concerning Large Language Models:

- **Consolidating and Structuring Knowledge:** In a rapidly advancing field like LLMs, new research emerges at an overwhelming pace. This survey serves as a vital resource for consolidating disparate knowledge about Reward Models into a coherent, taxonomically organized framework. For beginners, it offers a clear entry point into understanding the complexities of RMs, while for experienced researchers, it provides a structured overview that can highlight overlooked areas or connections.
- **Guiding Future Research Directions:** By systematically identifying current challenges related to data quality, training robustness (e.g., reward hacking mitigation), and evaluation biases, the survey acts as a strategic roadmap for the research community. The proposed future directions—such as integrating rule-based and model-based rewards, designing rewards for complex agent tasks, and extending RMs to multimodal domains—directly address these pain points and inspire new lines of inquiry, potentially accelerating breakthroughs in LLM alignment and capabilities.
- **Promoting Robust and Safer AI:** The core function of RMs is to align LLMs with human values and safety guidelines. By dissecting the challenges of reward hacking and evaluation bias, the paper directly contributes to the ongoing efforts to develop more reliable, ethical, and safe AI systems. Understanding these pitfalls is crucial for building trust in LLMs and ensuring their responsible deployment in sensitive applications.
- **Enhancing Practical Application of LLMs:** The involvement of Ant Group underscores the practical relevance of this work. RMs are not just theoretical constructs; they are essential components in developing and deploying performant LLMs in real-world scenarios. The survey's insights into various applications (dialogue, reasoning, recommendation, multimodal) and deployment strategies (data selection, policy training, inference) will directly benefit practitioners aiming to fine-tune and optimize LLMs for specific tasks.
- **Fostering Standardized Evaluation:** The candid discussion about the nascent state and inherent difficulties in RM evaluation highlights a critical need in the field. By outlining the limitations of current benchmarks and emphasizing the desire for more general and dynamic testing, the survey encourages the development of better, more comprehensive evaluation metrics. Improved benchmarks are fundamental for reproducible research, fair comparison of different RM approaches, and ultimately, for measuring genuine progress in LLM alignment.
- **Stimulating Critical Thinking and Open Dialogue:** The inclusion of "open questions" at the end of the survey is a powerful mechanism for stimulating critical thinking and fostering further academic discourse. Questions about the sufficiency of rule-based rewards, the efficacy of Mixture-of-Experts models, and how to counter reward hacking as LLMs surpass human expert levels encourage deeper theoretical and empirical investigations.

In essence, this survey is more than just a summary; it is a forward-looking analysis that structures, critiques, and guides the burgeoning field of Reward Models in the age of large language models, contributing significantly to both academic understanding and practical AI development.
