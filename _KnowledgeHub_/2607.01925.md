# SPLC: Social Preference Learning for Crowd Robot Navigation

> [!summary] Summary
> The SPLC algorithm introduces an automated mechanism to generate social preference labels for offline reinforcement learning in crowd robot navigation, overcoming the need for handcrafted reward functions and human annotation subjectivity. It enhanced navigation success rates and reduced collision rates across various offline RL baselines in both simulated and real-world crowded environments.

## Problem

- Designing effective handcrafted reward functions for Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) in crowd robot navigation is challenging, often failing to capture nuanced social norms like collective crowd flow or implicit social lanes.
- Traditional reward functions lead to suboptimal, unnatural, or myopic robot behaviors, which are undesirable in complex, dynamic human environments.
- Existing Preference-based Reinforcement Learning (PbRL) methods, even in offline settings, suffer from high human annotation costs and are susceptible to subjectivity and reward bias, limiting scalability and robustness.

## Method

- **SPLC** develops an ==automated social preference feedback mechanism== that generates preference labels for trajectory segments using ==lexicographically prioritized criteria==: "Collision Occurrence", "Goal Progress", and "Risk Exposure".
- Employed a ==preference transformer== to learn a ==non-Markovian reward function== from these automatically generated preference labels, using the ==Bradley-Terry model== and minimizing ==binary cross-entropy loss==.
- Integrated the learned reward model with standard ==offline reinforcement learning== algorithms (==IQL==, ==CQL==, ==TD3BC==) to train robot navigation policies, utilizing the re-rewarded offline dataset.

## Results

- SPLC consistently improved performance across IQL, CQL, and TD3BC offline RL methods in simulations, achieving higher success rates (e.g., SPLC-IQL at **94.60%** vs. HR-IQL at **93.40%**) and lower collision rates (e.g., SPLC-IQL at **5.40%** vs. HR-IQL at **6.20%**).
- Qualitative evaluations showed SPLC-trained robots exhibiting proactive path adjustments, selecting sparser regions, and maintaining appropriate social distancing, demonstrating superior social compliance compared to baseline methods.
- Successfully validated the SPLC-IQL algorithm on a physical TurtleBot4 in a real-world crowded environment with five pedestrians, demonstrating effective transferability and practical collision avoidance behaviors.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Automating the generation of preference labels for trajectory segments can effectively circumvent the high costs and inherent subjectivity of human annotation in reward learning.
> - A hierarchical set of explicit criteria (collision, goal progress, risk exposure) can systematically quantify broad social norms and mitigate reward bias, leading to more robust preference signals.
> - Learning a non-Markovian reward function via a preference transformer from these automated labels enables offline RL algorithms to acquire socially compliant and efficient navigation behaviors.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Crowd robot navigation is a critical area within robotics, essential for applications such as autonomous driving and logistics in environments shared with humans. The primary challenge lies in ensuring safety and efficiency while navigating unpredictable and often uncooperative pedestrian dynamics.

Historically, approaches to crowd robot navigation have included reactive methods like the Social Force Model (SFM), Reciprocal Velocity Obstacles (RVO), and Optimal Reciprocal Collision Avoidance (ORCA). These methods rely on physical and geometric rules for interaction but can suffer from the "robot freezing problem" in dense, dynamic crowds. Another line of research involves predicting pedestrian trajectories before planning, which also faces limitations in highly dynamic scenarios.

Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) emerged as a promising alternative, leveraging deep learning techniques such as Long Short-Term Memory networks (LSTM), self-attention mechanisms, and Transformers to encode interactive feature information. DRL-based methods demonstrated potential in overcoming the limitations of reactive and prediction-based approaches. However, deploying online DRL in real-world human-robot interaction environments presents significant challenges, including high safety risks and considerable costs associated with continuous human-in-the-loop training.

To mitigate these issues, offline Reinforcement Learning (RL) has gained attention. Algorithms such as Implicit Q-Learning (IQL), Conservative Q-Learning (CQL), and TD3BC have shown capabilities in autonomous driving and robotics. Recent work has begun to explore the integration of offline RL into crowd robot navigation, aiming to enhance both safety and practicality.

Despite advancements in offline RL for crowd navigation, a persistent challenge remains in the design of effective reward functions. Traditional methods often rely on handcrafted reward functions, which are limited in their ability to quantify broad social norms. This limitation can lead to suboptimal, unnatural, myopic, or socially undesirable robot behaviors, particularly in complex crowd scenarios where collective flow or implicit social lanes need to be recognized.

This paper addresses this specific reward design challenge by integrating with Preference-based Reinforcement Learning (PbRL). PbRL learns implicit reward functions from comparative feedback on trajectories, thereby capturing human preferences without requiring explicit reward engineering. While online PbRL has been effective, its continuous human querying incurs substantial feedback costs. Offline PbRL has been developed to address this, but still faces challenges related to human annotation costs, the complexity of real-world tasks, and the inherent subjectivity of human annotators, which can lead to reward bias.

The proposed SPLC algorithm positions itself within this landscape by offering an automated approach to generate preference labels for offline PbRL in crowd robot navigation, aiming to overcome the limitations of manual reward design and human annotation subjectivity, thereby fostering socially compliant robot behaviors.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the inherent difficulties in designing effective reward functions for deep reinforcement learning (DRL) algorithms applied to crowd robot navigation, particularly within the offline RL paradigm. Handcrafted reward functions, a common practice, are often insufficient to capture the nuanced and broad social norms required for socially compliant robot behaviors in complex, dynamic human environments. For instance, such functions may guide a robot around a single pedestrian but fail to account for collective crowd flow or social etiquette like following implicit lanes, leading to behaviors that appear unnatural or myopic to humans. The unpredictability and sometimes uncooperative nature of pedestrian motion further complicate the manual design of unbiased and comprehensive reward signals.

While Preference-based Reinforcement Learning (PbRL) offers an alternative by learning rewards from human preferences, existing PbRL methods, even in offline settings, face challenges. They often require extensive human labeling of trajectory preferences, which is costly and susceptible to human subjectivity and potential reward bias. This subjectivity can arise from the complexity of real-world tasks and the varied interpretations of "social compliance" among annotators.

Given these challenges, the key objectives of this work are:

1. **To eliminate the need for detailed, manually handcrafted reward functions** in offline RL-based crowd robot navigation. This aims to simplify the development process and enhance the adaptability of robot navigation policies.
2. **To introduce an automated mechanism for generating preference data**, thereby circumventing the substantial costs and inherent subjectivity associated with human annotation in preference learning.
3. **To develop principled preference evaluation criteria** that explicitly account for the complexities of pedestrian dynamics. These criteria are designed to systematically quantify broad social norms and mitigate reward bias, ensuring that the automatically generated preferences are robust and accurate.
4. **To integrate this automated preference learning approach with offline RL methods** to foster the acquisition of socially compliant navigation behaviors in robots.
5. **To validate the effectiveness of the proposed algorithm** through extensive experiments in both simulated and real-world crowded environments, demonstrating improvements in navigation performance metrics and successful transferability.

In essence, the research aims to provide a robust and scalable solution for teaching robots to navigate crowded spaces in a socially acceptable manner by automating the reward learning process and grounding it in a systematic evaluation of social compliance.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The SPLC (Social Preference Learning for Crowd Robot Navigation) algorithm is designed to address the challenge of reward function design in offline reinforcement learning for socially compliant crowd robot navigation. The methodology primarily consists of formulating the problem, introducing a social preference feedback mechanism with explicit evaluation criteria, and training a reward model using a preference transformer, which is then integrated into standard offline RL algorithms.

**A. Problem Formulation**
The crowd robot navigation problem is framed as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP), defined by (S, A, T, R, Ω, O, γ).

- **State Space (S):** The state at any timestep is represented robot-centrically to enhance generality.
 - **Robot State (s_r):** Includes distance to goal (d_g), preferred velocity (v_pref), radius (¯r), current velocity components (v_x, v_y), and heading angle (θ).
 - **Pedestrian State (s_i):** For each pedestrian `i`, it includes their relative position (˜p_i_x, ˜p_i_y), relative velocity (˜v_i_x, ˜v_i_y), radius (¯r_i), distance from robot (d_i), and sum of robot and pedestrian radii (¯r + ¯r_i).
 - **Joint State (s_jn_t):** Combines the robot state and all pedestrian states at time `t`.
- **Action Space (A):** Actions are defined as translational velocity (v_t ∈ (0, 1)) and rotational velocity (ω_t ∈ (−1, 1)) to facilitate smooth trajectories and align with real robot kinematic specifications.
- **Optimization Objective:** The goal is to maximize the discounted sum of rewards (∑ γ^t r_t), aiming for optimal navigation (reaching the target in minimal time while avoiding collisions).

**B. Social Preference Learning (SPLC)**
The core of SPLC is to learn a reward function without manual engineering, utilizing trajectory preference labeling. This involves an automated social preference feedback mechanism and a preference transformer.

1. **Social Preference Feedback Mechanism:**
 This mechanism automatically generates preference labels for pairs of trajectory segments, circumventing the need for human annotation.
 - **Trajectory Segment (σ):** A sequence of joint states and actions `{(s_jn_1, a_1), ..., (s_jn_L, a_L)}` of a specified length `L`, sampled from an offline dataset `D_s`.
 - **Preference Label (y):** A two-dimensional tuple (e.g., (0.9, 0.1) for strong preference, (0.5-α, 0.5+α) for neutral preference), including a preference risk factor `α`.
 - **Preference Evaluation Criteria:** Three criteria are defined with a lexicographic priority order to determine preference labels: "Collision Occurrence" ← "Goal Progress".
 - **Collision Occurrence (Highest Priority):** Evaluates if a collision occurred within a trajectory segment. A segment without a collision is always preferred over one with a collision (e.g., `y = (0.9, 0.1)`). If both segments have or lack collisions, the mechanism proceeds to "Goal Progress".
 - **Goal Progress (Second Priority):** Quantifies the robot's progress towards its goal, defined as `η = ||p_1 - p_g|| - ||p_L - p_g||`. A segment with greater goal progress (`η_0 > η_1`) is preferred, resulting in a label like `y = (0.7 - α, 0.3 + α)`. If goal progress is equal, a neutral label `y = (0.5 - α, 0.5 + α)` is assigned.
 - **Risk Exposure (Supplement to Goal Progress):** Measures the frequency of risk events (robot entering unsafe proximity to a pedestrian) within a segment (`μ = (1/L) Σ ξ_t`). This criterion is used to calculate the preference risk `α` as `α = 0.1 * tanh((μ_0 - μ_1) / (μ_max - μ_min))`, where `μ_max` and `μ_min` are dataset-wide maximum and minimum risk events. This `α` value is incorporated into the preference labels determined by "Goal Progress," adjusting the strength of preference based on subtle risk differences.

 The generated preference labels `y` and their corresponding trajectory segment pairs `(σ_0, σ_1)` are stored in a preference dataset `D_f`.

2. **Reward Model Learning with Preference Transformer:**
 To learn the reward function `ˆr_ψ,t`, a preference transformer is employed.
 - **Input:** Trajectory segment `σ` is fed into the preference transformer.
 - **Output:** The transformer generates a weighted sum of non-Markovian rewards `Σ w_ψ,t ˆr_ψ,t` and a sequence of preference reward predictions `[ˆr_ψ,1, ..., ˆr_ψ,L]`.
 - **Preference Modeling:** The Bradley-Terry model is used to model the probability of one segment being preferred over another based on their predicted total rewards: `P_ψ(σ_1 ≻ σ_0) = exp(Σ_σ1 w_ψ,t ˆr_ψ,t) / (exp(Σ_σ1 w_ψ,t ˆr_ψ,t) + exp(Σ_σ0 w_ψ,t ˆr_ψ,t))`.
 - **Optimization:** The reward function `ˆr_ψ,t` is updated by minimizing the binary cross-entropy loss `L(ψ)` over the preference dataset `D_f`.

**C. Integration with Offline RL:**
After the reward model `ˆr_ψ,t` is learned, it is used to assign rewards to the transitions in the original offline dataset `D_s`. This transforms the data into `(s_jn_t, a_t, s_jn_t+1, ˆr_ψ,t)` tuples, which are then collected into an experience replay buffer `D_r`. Finally, these reward-augmented transitions are used to train standard offline RL algorithms (e.g., IQL, CQL, TD3BC) to optimize the robot navigation policy. ### 4. Main Findings and Results

The SPLC algorithm was evaluated through extensive experiments in a simulated crowd robot navigation environment and validated with real-world experiments. The evaluation focused on standard performance metrics: Success rate, Collision rate, Timeout rate, and Average navigation time.

**A. Simulation Environment and Dataset:**
A simulation platform was developed with six pedestrians whose motion was governed by ORCA, providing a dynamic and continuously evolving interaction space. The offline dataset used for training was of medium difficulty, characterized by a 76.2% success rate, 23.6% collision rate, and 0.2% timeout rate, with an average navigation time of 11.7 seconds, and a capacity of 5 × 10^5 transitions.

**B. Baselines and Training Settings:**
Three representative offline RL methods—IQL, CQL, and TD3BC—were chosen. These were combined with four different reward function types:
- **HR:** A traditional handcrafted reward function.
- **HPR:** A reward function derived from human-labeled preference data.
- **RPR:** A robust preference-based reward under corrupted preference modeling.
- **SPLC:** The proposed social preference learning approach.
Hyperparameters for the reward model included a max trajectory segment length of 100, 2000 queries, and a query length of 15.

**C. Quantitative Evaluation:**
Results from 500 testing cases demonstrated SPLC's consistent improvements across all three offline RL methods.

- **SPLC-IQL vs. Baselines (HR-IQL, HPR-IQL, RPR-IQL):**
 - SPLC-IQL achieved a 94.60% success rate and 5.40% collision rate, outperforming HR-IQL (93.40% success, 6.20% collision), HPR-IQL (93.20% success, 6.80% collision), and RPR-IQL (92.20% success, 7.80% collision).
 - HR-IQL exhibited the longest navigation time (11.95 s), suggesting myopic behaviors due to the limitations of manual reward design. HPR-IQL showed a slightly lower success rate attributed to the subjectivity of human annotators. RPR-IQL provided stability but without significant success rate changes. SPLC-IQL demonstrated a better quantification of social norms and mitigation of reward bias, enhancing both navigation success and efficiency (11.37 s).

- **SPLC-CQL vs. Baselines (HR-CQL, HPR-CQL, RPR-CQL):**
 - SPLC-CQL achieved the highest success rate of 95.40% and the lowest collision rate of 4.60%. This significantly surpassed HR-CQL (36.40% success, 63.60% collision), which performed poorly due to the combination of algorithm conservativeness and reward biases. HPR-CQL improved performance (82.60% success, 17.40% collision), while RPR-CQL showed further improvements (86.20% success, 13.80% collision).
 - SPLC's social preference feedback mechanism was identified as effectively alleviating reward bias, leading to a more stable and effective CQL policy that balanced efficiency and safety.

- **SPLC-TD3BC vs. Baselines (HR-TD3BC, HPR-TD3BC, RPR-TD3BC):**
 - SPLC-TD3BC achieved the best performance with a 90.60% success rate, 7.40% collision rate, and 11.90 s navigation time. HR-TD3BC showed poor performance (82.40% success, 13.00% collision, 13.70 s time), as handcrafted rewards failed to provide sufficient signal. HPR-TD3BC performed even worse (62.20% success, 7.00% collision, 30.80% timeout, 16.74 s time), indicating insufficient reward modeling. RPR-TD3BC improved slightly but remained inferior.
 - The results indicated that SPLC provided a more discriminative and reliable signal, effectively reducing reward bias and enabling the policy to overcome suboptimal behaviors.

**D. Qualitative Evaluation:**
Visual comparisons of robot trajectories in identical crowd scenarios (Figure 2) further supported the quantitative findings.
- **Baselines (HR, HPR, RPR):** Robots often made myopic decisions, turning into densely crowded areas and resulting in slow movement or collisions. For example, HR-CQL experienced a collision. HPR-CQL and RPR-CQL sometimes approached pedestrians too closely, attempting to pass through rather than detour.
- **SPLC:** Robots trained with SPLC proactively adjusted their paths, selected sparser and safer regions, performed clear bypasses, and maintained appropriate social distancing with early avoidance. This demonstrated SPLC's ability to quantify social norms, incorporate global awareness, and anticipate collective crowd flow, leading to more efficient and socially compliant navigation.

**E. Real-world Experiments:**
The SPLC-IQL algorithm was deployed on a TurtleBot4 for real-world validation.
- **Setup:** The robot used an RPLIDAR-A1 LiDAR with a pedestrian leg detection algorithm for sensing and its built-in odometry for state tracking. Control actions generated by SPLC-IQL on a laptop were executed by the robot.
- **Results:** The robot successfully navigated through a crowd of five pedestrians to its target, demonstrating clear obstacle-avoidance behaviors when pedestrians approached. This validated the effective transferability of the SPLC algorithm from simulation to a physical robot in practical human-robot coexistence settings.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The SPLC algorithm represents an advancement in the field of crowd robot navigation by addressing a fundamental challenge: the design of effective and socially compliant reward functions for offline reinforcement learning. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Automation of Reward Design:** SPLC eliminates the need for manual, intricate design of reward functions, a process that is often labor-intensive, heuristic-driven, and prone to limitations in capturing complex social norms. This automation can accelerate the development and deployment of socially intelligent navigation systems.
2. **Mitigation of Reward Bias and Human Subjectivity:** By introducing an automated social preference feedback mechanism and principled preference evaluation criteria, SPLC reduces the impact of human subjectivity and inherent reward biases, which are common in human-labeled preference data. This leads to more robust and reliable reward signals, enabling robots to learn behaviors that are consistently aligned with broad social expectations.
3. **Enhanced Social Compliance and Safety:** The explicit accounting for pedestrian dynamics through criteria like "Collision Occurrence," "Goal Progress," and "Risk Exposure" enables SPLC to systematically quantify social norms. This fosters the acquisition of navigation policies that are not only efficient but also socially acceptable, leading to fewer collisions, smoother interactions, and a reduced "robot freezing problem."
4. **Improved Performance Across Offline RL Methods:** The consistent performance improvements observed when integrating SPLC with various state-of-the-art offline RL algorithms (IQL, CQL, TD3BC) demonstrate its versatility and compatibility. This suggests that SPLC can serve as a valuable component in a wide range of offline RL-based navigation frameworks.
5. **Validation in Real-world Settings:** The successful transfer of SPLC from simulation to a physical TurtleBot4 robot, where it demonstrated effective navigation and collision avoidance in a real crowd, validates its practical applicability. This sim-to-real capability is crucial for the deployment of intelligent robots in real-world human environments.
6. **Potential for Broader Applications:** The principles of automated preference learning based on quantifiable criteria could extend beyond crowd navigation to other complex human-robot interaction tasks where explicit reward design is difficult or human preferences are nuanced. This could include tasks requiring adaptive behaviors in shared spaces or situations demanding adherence to implicit social rules.

In summary, SPLC contributes a method for learning socially compliant navigation behaviors by automating the generation of preference-based reward functions, thereby enhancing the safety, efficiency, and social acceptability of robots operating in crowded environments. This work offers a pathway toward more adaptive and autonomously learning robotic systems that can seamlessly integrate into human-centric spaces.
