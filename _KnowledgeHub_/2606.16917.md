# Unified Motion-Action Modeling for Heterogeneous Robot Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> The Unified Motion-Action (UMA) Model from Cornell University leverages 3D object motion as a shared interface for visuomotor control and dynamics modeling, enabling a single diffusion transformer to learn from heterogeneous data like action-free videos and robot demonstrations. It demonstrates significant improvements in zero-shot generalization and few-shot adaptation across diverse real-world manipulation tasks.

## Problem

- Existing robotic foundation models (e.g., VLA models, world models) struggle with knowledge transfer across domains and can be brittle outside their training distributions due to operating in pixel or latent visual spaces.
- Prior motion-based methods specialize in either motion-conditioned policies (requiring action labels) or motion prediction (lacking an action interface), preventing the unified utilization of both action-free video and action-labeled robot data.
- There is a lack of structured, embodiment-agnostic representations of physical interaction that are sample-efficient and generic enough to support various data types and learning objectives in robotics.

## Method

- The Unified Motion-Action (**UMA**) Model employs ==3D object motion trajectories== as a shared interface to bridge visuomotor control and dynamics modeling within a single masked generative policy.
- A diffusion transformer architecture is utilized, trained with a flow-matching objective, to jointly denoise masked motion and action tokens, enabling learning from heterogeneous data sources (action-free video, simulated data, robot demonstrations).
- A task encoder, trained with a SimCLR-style contrastive objective, generates a disentangled, spatially invariant task latent that supports flexible inference modes, including motion-conditioned control, dynamics modeling, and few-shot adaptation via soft prompt tuning.

## Results

- UMA consistently outperformed specialized baselines (COIL, UVA) in zero-shot visuomotor control, achieving a 20-25 percentage point increase in success rate across real-world insertion, sweeping, and folding tasks.
- It demonstrated sharper dynamics prediction, reducing the mean squared error for motion prediction from **0.054** (PointWorld) to **0.042**, indicating that joint motion-action training improves dynamics understanding.
- UMA achieved strong few-shot adaptation, matching or surpassing finetuned baselines (e.g., LoRA-finetuned **0.5**) by up to 25 percentage points using soft prompt tuning of its task latent, even when adapted with only action-free human videos.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - 3D object motion trajectories serve as an effective embodiment-agnostic, camera-invariant, and structured intermediate representation, facilitating robust knowledge transfer across diverse data and robot platforms.
> - A masked generative objective within a diffusion transformer allows for seamlessly pretraining on heterogeneous data, where each data source supervises only the available labels (motion-only for videos, motion and action for robot data).
> - A contrastively learned, disentangled task latent is crucial for capturing abstract task intent, enabling rapid and robust few-shot adaptation to new tasks, even with only motion-based supervision.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic learning is progressing towards "robotic foundation models" that can learn from broad data and operate across diverse use cases. Despite advancements in end-to-end and black-box approaches, such as vision-language-action (VLA) models and world models, these methods frequently encounter limitations in transferring knowledge across domains and demonstrate brittleness when deployed outside their training distributions. A significant challenge lies in developing representations of physical interaction that are sufficiently structured to support sample-efficient learning, yet generic enough to be applicable across various settings and robot embodiments.

Object motion has emerged as a promising intermediate representation for grounding visuomotor learning in physical interactions. When represented as 3D point trajectories on object surfaces, motion can be extracted from video without requiring action labels, exists in the same spatial coordinate system as robot controls, and remains comparable across different embodiments and camera views. However, existing methods typically specialize in a single role for this representation. Motion-conditioned policies, for instance, learn to predict robot actions given a target motion but necessitate paired motion-action labels, leaving abundant action-free video data unutilized. Conversely, motion-prediction models forecast trajectories but require a separate downstream controller and lack a direct mechanism to incorporate action labels when robot data is available. Generalist policies often tie dynamics to embodiment-specific states, rendering them incompatible with cross-domain data, or model dynamics in raw pixels, which entangles them with appearance and viewpoint variations. World models, while powerful, typically operate in pixel or latent visual spaces, which can be computationally expensive to roll out and do not inherently provide structured planning signals.

The Unified Motion-Action (UMA) Model addresses these limitations by proposing an approach that leverages 3D object motion as a shared interface to bridge visuomotor control and dynamics modeling within a single framework. Unlike prior methods, UMA jointly models both object motion and robot actions as co-evolving variables. This unified approach allows for the integration of heterogeneous data sources, including action-free human videos, real robot data, and simulated data, under a single masked generative objective. By doing so, UMA aims to overcome the inability of existing specialized methods to simultaneously learn motion from action-free video and exploit action labels when available, thereby contributing to the development of more generalizable and robust robotic learning systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a unified framework, the Unified Motion-Action (UMA) Model, that facilitates heterogeneous robot learning by bridging visuomotor control and dynamics modeling through 3D object motion trajectories. This overarching goal is driven by several specific objectives and motivations:

1. **Unifying Control and Dynamics Modeling:** A core motivation is to create a single model capable of performing both visuomotor control (generating robot actions to achieve a desired object motion) and dynamics modeling (predicting object motion resulting from given robot actions). Existing approaches often specialize in one or the other, necessitating separate models or controllers, which complicates system integration and knowledge transfer.
2. **Leveraging Diverse Data Sources:** Robotic learning is often hampered by the scarcity of high-quality, action-labeled robot data. The authors aim to overcome this by enabling the model to effectively learn from heterogeneous data, including:
 - **Action-free human videos:** A vast and inexpensive source of task demonstrations, currently underutilized by methods requiring action labels.
 - **Real robot demonstrations:** Providing direct supervision for robot actions.
 - **Simulated robot data:** Offering dense, varied action-motion pairs for robust dynamics learning.
 The objective is to allow all these data types to contribute to a shared learning objective, regardless of whether they contain explicit action labels.
3. **Establishing a Structured and Generic Representation:** The motivation behind using 3D object motion trajectories is to find a representation that is both structured (to enable sample-efficient learning and clear physical grounding) and generic (to support transfer across different robot embodiments, camera viewpoints, and types of interactions). Such a representation should be invariant to embodiment-specific details and appearance variations, unlike pixel-based representations, and naturally align with robot control signals.
4. **Enabling Versatile Inference Modes from a Single Model:** The research seeks to demonstrate that the same pretrained model parameters can flexibly support multiple downstream applications without retraining. This includes:
 - **Motion-conditioned visuomotor control:** Guiding robot actions based on a desired object motion.
 - **Motion-based dynamics modeling:** Predicting future object movements given hypothetical robot actions, useful for model predictive control (MPC).
 - **Few-shot task adaptation:** Quickly adapting to new, unseen tasks with minimal demonstrations by tuning a low-dimensional task representation (soft prompt tuning).
5. **Achieving Multi-Task Pretraining Without Manual Task Labels:** Traditional multi-task learning often relies on manually annotated task instructions, which is labor-intensive. The authors are motivated to develop a method that can perform multi-task pretraining by extending hindsight experience replay to motion contexts and incorporating a contrastive objective. This approach aims to disentangle abstract task intent from specific scene geometry, reducing the need for explicit task labels.
6. **Surpassing Specialized Baselines:** A key objective is to empirically demonstrate that the UMA Model, despite its unified and generalist nature, can match or exceed the performance of state-of-the-art methods that are specialized for each individual inference mode (e.g., dedicated policies for visuomotor control or specialized dynamics models). This would validate the efficacy of the unified motion-action modeling paradigm.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Unified Motion-Action (UMA) Model is designed around the principle of treating 3D object motion and robot actions as co-evolving variables within a masked generative objective. This allows for unified pretraining across heterogeneous data sources and supports versatile inference modes.

**Core Framework:**
UMA employs a transformer-based sequence model to jointly predict object motion trajectories and robot action sequences, conditioned on the current observation and a task specification. The crucial element is the flexibility afforded by a masked generative objective, where the mask pattern dictates both the supervision regime during pretraining and the inference mode at deployment.

**Input Representation and Tokenization:**
The model represents observations, object motion, robot actions, and a latent task specification (referred to as the task latent, 'c') as typed tokens that share a common transformer backbone.
- **Object Motion:** Represented as 3D points ($x_{k,t} \in \mathbb{R}^3$) sampled on object surfaces and tracked across time. These trajectories are extracted from monocular video using off-the-shelf trackers for training supervision. At deployment, UMA generates them implicitly. Each ($x_{k,t}$) sample is tokenized via a lightweight Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) incorporating keypoint-identity and time embeddings.
- **Robot Actions:** Robot controls ($a_{t:t+H}$) are encoded into action tokens through a separate input projection.
- **Observations:** The current RGB-D observation ($o_t$) is summarized into observation tokens.
- **Task Latent (c):** A small set of task tokens represents 'c', summarizing the abstract intent of the interaction.

**Task Encoder ($h_\psi$):**
A dedicated task encoder, $h_\psi(o_0, x_c)$, is responsible for generating the fixed-size task latent 'c' from an initial observation ($o_0$) and a partial "reference motion" ($x_c$) drawn from a recorded interaction.
- The initial observation $o_0$ is encoded into geometry-aware point-cloud features using a PointNet++ backbone on 3D positions concatenated with SigLIP 2 image features.
- The reference motion $x_c$, which can be sparse and have non-uniform temporal spacing, is projected via an MLP with time indices normalized to to ensure invariance to the number of timesteps and temporal irregularity.
- These tokens are then cross-attended to the point-cloud features to produce the task latent 'c'. The design ensures 'c' is invariant to specific keypoint/timestep sampling and absolute scene configurations.

**Motion-Action Diffusion Transformer:**
UMA employs a Masked Diffusion Transformer for joint denoising of masked motion and action tokens. It is trained with a flow matching objective.
- **Structured Spatiotemporal Attention:** Following COIL, attention is factorized into:
 - **Spatial attention:** Captures within-timestep motion-action coupling.
 - **Temporal attention:** Connects the same keypoint across different timesteps.
 - **Context attention:** Links target tokens to observation and task tokens.
- **Masked DiT Blocks (Appendix A.2):** A key architectural innovation, these blocks maintain two independent sets of adaptive layer normalization (adaLN) parameters. One set applies specific modulation (scale and shift) appropriate for iteratively denoising *target* (masked) tokens, which are noisy. The other set applies a near-identity modulation to *given* (unmasked) conditioning tokens, preserving their clean state. Both sets are conditioned on the diffusion timestep embedding. This allows for distinct processing without dense global attention.

**Cross-Domain Training via Masked Autoencoding:**
Pretraining involves a masked autoencoding objective (Eq. 1) where random masks are applied to both motion and action streams.
- **Heterogeneous Supervision:**
 - Robot demonstrations with action labels supervise both motion ($L_x$) and action ($L_a$) reconstruction losses.
 - Action-free sources (e.g., human videos, cross-embodiment data with incompatible actions) supervise only the motion reconstruction loss ($L_x$).
 - All data uses the local camera frame, with predicted actions transformed to the robot base frame at deployment.
- **Task Latent Consistency (Contrastive Objective):** A SimCLR-style contrastive objective ($L_c$) is used to train the task encoder $h_\psi$. This objective encourages the task latent 'c' to be invariant to augmentations (keypoint/timestep subsampling, random SE(3) transformations) of the same (initial observation, reference motion) pair, while remaining discriminative across different tasks or motion intents. This disentanglement ensures 'c' captures abstract task intent, not scene-specific details.
- **Full Pretraining Objective:** The total loss is a weighted sum: $L = \lambda_x L_x + \lambda_a L_a + \lambda_c L_c$.

**Versatile Inference:**
At test time, the same pretrained model parameters (except for 'c' in soft prompt tuning) support three main inference modes by altering the masking pattern:
1. **Motion-conditioned visuomotor control (Eq. 5):** Given an initial observation $o_0$ and a target reference motion $x_⋆$, the task encoder produces 'c', and UMA predicts future robot actions. The policy operates in a closed loop, sampling, executing, and replanning actions.
2. **Motion-based dynamics modeling (Eq. 6):** Given a candidate action sequence, UMA predicts the resulting object motion. This mode is suitable for model predictive control (MPC) by evaluating candidate actions against a target motion.
3. **Task inference via soft prompt tuning (Eq. 7):** For new tasks, 'c' is treated as an optimizable free variable while other model parameters are frozen. It is optimized using a small set of demonstrations, supporting both action-supervised and motion-only supervised adaptation.

**Data Pipeline (Appendix B):**
- **Real-World Data:** A four-stage pipeline extracts 3D keypoint trajectories from monocular RGB videos: 1) Camera and depth estimation (MegaSaM), 2) Metric depth alignment (UniDepth), 3) Keypoint sampling (using VLMs and SAM 3 for object segmentation), and 4) 3D point tracking (TAPIP3D).
- **Simulated Data:** Uses a pipeline from COIL to generate randomized tabletop manipulation scenes with a Franka robot, diverse objects, and heuristic action primitives (grasping, tool use).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments evaluate UMA's capabilities across zero-shot generalization and few-shot adaptation settings using real-world tabletop manipulation tasks, with pretraining on a mixture of robot demonstrations, human videos, and simulated data.

**Experimental Setup:**
The evaluation was conducted on real-world tabletop manipulation tasks following the DROID setting, covering rigid object insertion, tool use (sweeping), and deformable material manipulation (folding). All pretraining data sources (DROID, HOI4D, Xperience, and simulated data) were distinct from the real-world test environments. Each task was evaluated over 20 trials with randomized scenes and camera configurations.

**Zero-Shot Generalization:**
1. **Visuomotor Control:** UMA was compared against two baselines: COIL (a 3D motion-conditioned flow-matching policy) and UVA (a joint video-action masked model on raw pixels).
 - UMA consistently outperformed the strongest baseline by 20 to 25 percentage points across all tasks.
 - This indicates that jointly modeling motion and action as coupled variables, rather than using motion solely as a conditioning signal (as in COIL), benefits action prediction. The performance gap with UVA suggests that representing dynamics as 3D object motion, rather than raw pixels, improves effectiveness by reducing appearance variations and aligning more directly with robot actions.
2. **Dynamics Prediction:** UMA was compared with PointWorld, an action-conditioned keypoint dynamics model.
 - UMA reduced the motion-prediction Mean Squared Error (MSE) from 0.054 (PointWorld) to 0.042.
 - This result suggests that joint motion-action training within UMA produces more accurate dynamics predictions even when only motion is queried, implying a sharper understanding of physical interactions.

**Few-Shot Adaptation:**
UMA's ability to adapt to new tasks by optimizing only the task latent 'c' on 25 demonstrations (with frozen model weights) was evaluated under two supervision regimes:
1. **Action Supervision:** With both motion and action reconstruction terms active.
 - UMA matched π_0.5 (a baseline fine-tuned with LoRA) on the Insertion task and surpassed it by 10-25% on Sweeping and Folding. It also outperformed UVA fine-tuned with action supervision.
 - This demonstrates that UMA's low-dimensional task latent can effectively adapt to new tasks, achieving competitive performance despite updating significantly fewer parameters than baselines.
2. **Motion Supervision:** With only the motion-reconstruction term active (using action-free human videos).
 - UMA outperformed UVA fine-tuned with a pixel-reconstruction loss by approximately 25 percentage points across all tasks.
 - This finding indicates that UMA's joint motion-action pretraining offers a more effective mechanism for bridging action-free human videos to robot control compared to pixel-action joint pretraining.

**Cross-Domain Training Analysis:**
Ablation studies on data sources revealed complementary roles:
- **Removal of simulated data (w/o Sim):** Led to a nearly 5x increase in motion-prediction error (MSE 0.042 to 0.208) and up to a 30 percentage point reduction in zero-shot action success. This highlights the importance of dense simulated action-motion pairs for strong motion-action coupling and regularization against out-of-distribution configurations.
- **Removal of human videos (w/o Human):** Had minimal impact on motion prediction (MSE 0.042 to 0.044) but reduced action success, notably on Folding (75% to 65%). This suggests that action-free human videos contribute to task-level diversity, aiding real-world transfer, rather than directly improving dynamics accuracy.

**Ablation and Failure Analysis:**
- **Effects of Contrastive Learning:** Removing the contrastive objective ($L_c$) for task latent training (UMA w/o CL) caused a significant drop of 30-60 percentage points in few-shot success rates. This confirms that $L_c$ is critical for producing a transferable, pose-invariant task latent that captures abstract intent.
- **Model Design Ablation (simulated tasks):**
 - "Action-only Supervision" (no motion prediction) lagged UMA significantly, emphasizing the necessity of jointly predicting motion alongside actions.
 - "Separate Heads" (independent decoder heads) improved over action-only but was still slightly inferior to UMA, indicating benefits of a shared head for motion-action coupling.
 - "Dense Attention" underperformed UMA at matched computational budget, demonstrating the efficiency and effectiveness of structured attention.
- **Failure Analysis:** Execution failures accounted for 82% of errors, while grasping failures constituted 18%. This suggests UMA reliably identifies correct task intent and grasp configurations but struggles with precision during the final action rollout, indicating a need for more motion-action paired data.

**Scalability (Appendix E):**
Analysis of model parameter scale and training data quantity suggests that both factors contribute positively to UMA's performance, with larger models trained on more data achieving superior results.

**Multimodal Task Conditioning (Appendix D, F):**
Qualitative results demonstrate that the same pretrained UMA checkpoint can perform instruction following (from language) and goal reaching (from images) without retraining the denoising transformer, showcasing the flexibility of the learned task latent space.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Unified Motion-Action (UMA) Model contributes to the field of robotic learning by introducing a comprehensive framework that addresses several challenges in developing generalizable and versatile robotic systems. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Advancement in Robotic Foundation Models:** UMA represents a step towards creating more robust and capable robotic foundation models. By unifying visuomotor control and dynamics modeling within a single masked generative framework, it offers a more integrated approach compared to prior specialized methods. This integration can lead to systems with a deeper understanding of physical interaction.
2. **Effective Utilization of Heterogeneous Data:** A key impact of UMA is its ability to effectively leverage diverse data sources, including readily available action-free human videos, alongside real and simulated robot data. This capability is crucial for scaling robot learning, as it reduces the dependency on expensive, time-consuming, and hard-to-collect robot-specific action-labeled data. By tapping into the vast reservoir of human manipulation videos, UMA offers a pathway to imbue robots with a broader range of manipulation skills.
3. **Versatile and Flexible Robotics:** The demonstration that a single pretrained UMA model can perform multiple tasks—zero-shot motion-conditioned control, action-conditioned dynamics prediction, and few-shot task adaptation (even with motion-only supervision)—significantly enhances robotic flexibility. This versatility can streamline the development and deployment of robots in various applications, reducing the need for task-specific models or extensive retraining.
4. **Validation of 3D Object Motion as a Core Representation:** The research strongly validates 3D object motion trajectories as a robust, generic, and structured intermediate representation for physical interactions. This representation abstracts away low-level pixel details and embodiment-specific kinematics, providing a common language for human, simulated, and real-robot interactions. Such a unified representation could become a foundational element for future robot learning architectures.
5. **Improved Generalization and Adaptation:** UMA's consistent outperformance of specialized baselines in both zero-shot and few-shot settings indicates a substantial improvement in generalization capabilities. The effective few-shot adaptation, particularly through soft prompt tuning of a low-dimensional task latent, suggests a more sample-efficient approach to teaching robots new skills, potentially accelerating the deployment of robots in novel or evolving environments.
6. **Insights into Data Synergies:** The ablation studies provide valuable insights into how different data types contribute to overall performance. The finding that simulated data primarily strengthens motion-action coupling and dynamics accuracy, while human videos contribute to task-level diversity and real-world transfer, informs future data collection and curriculum learning strategies for robotic tasks.
7. **Guidance for Future Research:** While demonstrating significant advancements, UMA also highlights areas for future investigation, such as improving action rollout precision, handling embodiment-specific action heads for new robot platforms, and developing more robust methods for managing camera-to-base frame transformations for deployment. These limitations define concrete directions for advancing robot learning.

In summary, UMA's unified approach to motion-action modeling, coupled with its ability to learn from heterogeneous data and adapt to new tasks, positions it as a significant contribution to making robotic systems more intelligent, adaptable, and deployable in complex, unstructured environments.
