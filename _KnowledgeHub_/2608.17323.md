# ORPA: Online Residual Policy Adaptation for Robot Manipulation Control with Human Feedback

> [!summary] Summary
> ORPA augments a frozen pretrained ACT policy with a lightweight feedback-conditioned Feedback Encoder and Policy Updater that predict joint-space residual corrections at runtime, without retraining. On ALOHA precision manipulation tasks, ORPA lifts failure-condition success from 60.0% baseline to over 90%, beating rule-based IK and OLAF corrections.

## Problem

- Imitation-learning policies like ACT reach strong nominal performance but degrade sharply under small execution errors, perturbed object poses, and distribution shifts during deployment.
- Correcting failures via dataset aggregation and full-policy retraining is computationally expensive, needs repeated data collection, and unsuitable for real-time interactive correction.
- Rule-based inverse-kinematics correction assumes identical feedback always yields identical corrective motion, ignoring that needed correction depends on task phase and object context.

## Method

- **ORPA**: ==residual adaptation== module added on top of frozen ==ACT== output, predicting joint-space residual actions that are summed with nominal ACT chunk predictions.
- **Feedback Encoder**: lightweight transformer module mapping discrete human feedback tokens like too left or too high into a latent embedding conditioning the correction.
- **Policy Updater**: transformer module combining feedback embedding, observation, and ACT action chunk to output context-dependent residual corrections, trained via ==MSE== against reference actions.

## Results

- Cube Transfer failure-condition success rose from **60.0%** baseline ACT to **92.3%** discrete-feedback ORPA and **91.7%** continuous-feedback ORPA, versus **81.0%** and **76.0%** for rule-based IK.
- Bimanual Insertion failure-condition success rose from **60.0%** baseline to **80.3%** discrete and **84.7%** continuous ORPA, exceeding OLAF's **78.5%** on Cube Transfer.
- Real-world 30-trial evaluation showed ORPA with perturbed observations raising scores from **0.83/0.79/0.80** baseline to **0.86/0.82/0.83** on transfer, opening, and sorting tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Residual correction conditioned on execution context outperforms fixed geometric rules, since identical feedback needs different correction magnitude at different task phases.
> - Synthetic corrective data generated by perturbing successful demonstrations avoids costly human-in-the-loop data collection for training online adaptation modules.
> - Freezing the base policy and training only a small residual module gives real-time adaptation at negligible overhead compared to full retraining or LLM-based correction.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation has seen substantial advancements through imitation learning (IL), particularly with the emergence of transformer-based policies like Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT). These policies, often combined with low-cost bimanual platforms such as ALOHA, have enabled robots to learn complex, long-horizon manipulation behaviors directly from human demonstrations. Despite these successes, a persistent challenge in deploying such policies in real-world scenarios is their sensitivity to execution errors, environmental variations, and distribution shifts. Small deviations in factors like end-effector position, orientation, or timing can lead to task failure, especially in precision-sensitive tasks like cluttered object grasping or coordinated bimanual manipulation.

Existing strategies to address this lack of robustness typically fall into two categories:
- **Dataset Aggregation and Policy Retraining:** Approaches like DAgger and HG-Dagger iteratively collect new demonstrations or corrective samples to refine the policy. While effective in improving robustness, these methods are computationally intensive, require extensive data collection, and are not suitable for real-time, interactive deployment. They also involve modifying the entire policy network, which can be an overkill for localized execution errors.
- **Rule-Based Geometric Corrections:** This involves translating human feedback (e.g., "too left," "too high") into predefined end-effector offsets and then projecting them back into joint space using inverse kinematics (IK). These methods are computationally efficient but lack flexibility. They operate under the assumption that identical feedback always necessitates an identical corrective action, failing to account for context-dependent factors such as task phase, object configuration, or robot pose.

More recent interactive learning frameworks, such as OLAF and YAY Robot, have explored integrating human verbal corrections. OLAF, for instance, uses language-guided supervision for offline policy refinement. However, these often involve additional inference steps (e.g., with large language models), which can introduce latency, and may still rely on IK pipelines for action translation, inheriting some of their limitations. Furthermore, such approaches may generate corrective decisions at individual execution steps, potentially reducing temporal consistency compared to adjustments over action trajectories.

ORPA positions itself within this landscape by seeking to overcome the limitations of both retraining-based and rule-based methods. It proposes a lightweight, online adaptation framework that augments a *pretrained* policy with a feedback-conditioned residual module. This allows for immediate, context-aware, and joint-space-direct corrective adjustments at runtime, without requiring full policy retraining or fixed geometric rules. By preserving the original policy parameters while learning context-dependent residuals, ORPA aims to enhance the real-time adaptability and robustness of imitation learning policies for robotic manipulation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to develop a framework that enables immediate, feedback-driven correction of robot actions during manipulation tasks without necessitating modifications to the parameters of the underlying control policy. This objective is motivated by several identified shortcomings of current imitation learning (IL) methodologies in real-world deployment.

The specific motivations and objectives include:

- **Addressing Sensitivity to Errors:** Imitation learning policies, particularly those based on transformers like ACT, often exhibit high sensitivity to minor execution errors, environmental variations, and distribution shifts. These small deviations can lead to task failures, especially in precision-sensitive scenarios like cluttered object grasping or narrow-space manipulation. The paper aims to mitigate this by providing a mechanism for real-time error correction.
- **Enabling Real-Time Adaptation:** Current methods for improving policy robustness, such as collecting additional demonstrations and retraining or fine-tuning the entire policy (e.g., dataset aggregation), are computationally expensive and time-consuming. These processes are not suitable for interactive or real-time robotic deployment where immediate corrective action is often required. A key objective is therefore to facilitate prompt adaptation without lengthy offline optimization.
- **Overcoming Limitations of Rule-Based Corrections:** Simple rule-based geometric corrections, which translate feedback into predefined end-effector offsets via inverse kinematics, are computationally efficient but inflexible. They assume a one-to-one mapping between feedback and correction, failing to account for the context-dependent nature of appropriate corrective actions (e.g., varying based on task phase, object configuration, or robot pose). The paper seeks to develop a learning-based approach that can generate context-aware corrections.
- **Preserving Pretrained Policy Performance:** Instead of modifying or retraining the entire underlying policy network, even for localized errors, the proposed framework aims to augment the existing policy. This ensures that the original learned manipulation behaviors are preserved, while a dedicated module handles corrective adjustments.
- **Predicting Joint-Space Residuals Directly:** The objective is to predict corrective adjustments directly in joint space. This eliminates the need for explicit forward and inverse kinematics computations during correction, simplifying the system and potentially improving computational efficiency during runtime compared to methods that require such conversions.
- **Improving Success Rate and Recovery:** Ultimately, the work aims to demonstrate empirical improvements in task success rates and the ability of robots to recover from small perturbations and errors during precision-sensitive manipulation tasks.

In summary, the core motivation is to bridge the gap between high-performing imitation learning policies under ideal conditions and their robust, adaptive deployment in dynamic, error-prone real-world environments, by introducing an efficient, context-aware, and feedback-driven online correction mechanism.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Online Residual Policy Adaptation (ORPA) framework augments a pretrained Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT) policy with a feedback-conditioned module for real-time action refinement. The core principle is to add a learned residual correction to the nominal action produced by the base policy, rather than replacing or retraining the base policy itself.

**A. ORPA Architecture**
The framework consists of three main components:

1. **Pretrained ACT Policy:** This serves as the frozen, underlying manipulation controller. Given a current observation \(o_t\), ACT predicts a chunk of future joint-space actions, \(\hat{a}_{t:t+k}\), representing the nominal manipulation behavior. This policy is not modified during ORPA training or deployment.
2. **Feedback Encoder (FE):** This component is responsible for converting human corrective feedback into a compact latent representation. During both training and evaluation, feedback signals are provided as discrete directional commands (e.g., "too left," "too right," "too high," "good"). Each feedback signal \(f_t\) is first tokenized and then mapped into a learnable embedding space to produce a latent feedback representation \(r_t = f_\psi(f_t)\).
3. **Policy Updater (PU):** This module predicts the residual action correction, \(\Delta a_t\). It receives the encoded feedback representation \(r_t\), the current robot observation \(o_t\), and the nominal action chunk \(\hat{a}_{t:t+k}\) predicted by ACT. The PU, a lightweight Transformer-based component, generates \(\Delta a_t = g_\omega(r_t, o_t, \hat{a}_{t:t+k})\).

The final corrected action \(a^{\text{final}}_{t:t+k}\) is computed by adding the predicted residual correction to the nominal ACT prediction:
\[
a^{\text{final}}_{t:t+k} = \hat{a}_{t:t+k} + \Delta a_t
\]
This residual formulation enables local, corrective adjustments while preserving the original ACT-learned behavior. The corrections are applied directly in joint space, bypassing the need for explicit forward or inverse kinematics computations.

**B. Synthetic Data Generation**
To address the challenge of acquiring large-scale human corrective demonstrations, ORPA employs a synthetic data generation strategy:

1. **Reference Trajectories:** Successful manipulation trajectories are initially collected via teleoperation. These are used to train the base ACT policy and serve as "reference actions."
2. **Controlled Perturbations:** During the data generation phase for ORPA, controlled perturbations are introduced into the environment by modifying object positions and orientations within predefined ranges. These perturbations simulate common execution errors and create "failure scenarios."
3. **Feedback Labeling:** For each perturbation, a corresponding corrective feedback label is assigned based on the direction of the perturbation (e.g., leftward displacement labeled "too left").
4. **Residual Target Computation:** The target residual correction, \(\Delta a^*_t\), is computed as the difference between the action from the original successful (reference) trajectory, \(a^{\text{ref}}_t\), and the action associated with the perturbed execution, \(a^{\text{perturbed}}_t\):
 \[
 \Delta a^*_t = a^{\text{ref}}_t - a^{\text{perturbed}}_t
 \]
This procedure allows for scalable generation of corrective supervision data without requiring additional human teleoperation for corrections.

**C. Training Objective**
ORPA is trained while the pretrained ACT policy remains fixed. The training process involves:

1. **ACT Prediction:** The fixed ACT policy predicts \(\hat{a}_{t:t+k}\) for a given observation \(o_t\).
2. **Feedback Encoding:** The Feedback Encoder processes the feedback signal \(f_t\) to produce \(r_t\).
3. **Residual Prediction:** The Policy Updater predicts \(\Delta a_t\) based on \(r_t\), \(o_t\), and \(\hat{a}_{t:t+k}\).
4. **Final Action:** The corrected action \(\hat{a}^{\text{final}}_{t:t+k}\) is computed.
5. **Loss Calculation:** The Feedback Encoder and Policy Updater are optimized using a mean-squared error (MSE) objective between the corrected action \(\hat{a}^{\text{final}}_{t:t+k}\) and the target reference action \(a^{\text{ref}}_t\).
 \[
 L_{\text{ORPA}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \|\hat{a}^{\text{final}}_i - a^{\text{ref}}_i\|^2
 \]
This focused optimization ensures minimal computational overhead and preserves the integrity of the original ACT policy.

**D. Experimental Setup**
Experiments were conducted using the ALOHA platform, a dual-arm bimanual manipulation system.

- **Simulation Environment:** A MuJoCo-based ALOHA simulator was used, featuring two 6-DOF manipulators with parallel-jaw grippers, a 14-dimensional joint-space action representation, and multi-view RGB observations at 50 Hz.
- **Real-World Platform:** The physical ALOHA system comprised two synchronized 6-DOF manipulators, parallel-jaw grippers, and four synchronized RGB cameras (two wrist-mounted, two external tabletop). Control operated at 50 Hz.
- **Tasks:** Precision-sensitive tasks were selected, including Cube Transfer and Bimanual Insertion in simulation, and Snack Box Transfer, Bottle Cap Opening, and Object Sorting in the real world. These tasks involved precise grasping, coordinated motion, and tight tolerance specifications.
- **Feedback:** Both discrete (e.g., "too left") and continuous feedback types were explored in simulation, while real-world experiments primarily used discrete feedback.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of ORPA was conducted in both simulation and real-world environments, focusing on precision-sensitive bimanual manipulation tasks. The results aim to demonstrate ORPA's ability to improve policy robustness and task recovery using human feedback.

**A. Simulation Results**
Table II summarizes the performance under failure conditions in simulation for two tasks: Cube Transfer and Bimanual Insertion. Under normal conditions, both tasks achieved high success rates (90.0% for Cube Transfer, 84.0% for Bimanual Insertion) with the original ACT policy. However, when spatial perturbations (translation or translation + rotation) were introduced, mimicking execution errors, the original ACT policy's performance significantly degraded.

- **Improved Success Rates with ORPA:** For the Cube Transfer task, the original ACT policy achieved a 60.0% success rate under translational perturbations. ORPA, with discrete feedback, improved this to 92.3%, and with continuous feedback, to 91.7%. When both translational and rotational perturbations were applied, ORPA achieved 85.9% success. Similar improvements were observed in the Bimanual Insertion task, where ACT's 60.0% success rate increased to 80.3% with discrete ORPA feedback and 84.7% with continuous feedback. These results indicate that ORPA consistently enhanced task robustness and recovery.
- **Outperformance of Rule-Based IK:** ORPA consistently outperformed the rule-based Inverse Kinematics (IK) baseline. In the Cube Transfer task, rule-based IK achieved 81.0% with discrete feedback and 76.0% with continuous feedback, both lower than ORPA's performance. The paper attributes this to ORPA's ability to predict context-dependent correction magnitudes based on visual inputs, robot states, and policy outputs, unlike fixed IK corrections.
- **Comparison with OLAF:** ORPA also demonstrated superior performance compared to OLAF, another feedback-guided manipulation framework, which achieved 78.5% in the Cube Transfer task. The paper suggests ORPA's advantages stem from its direct joint-space residual prediction, operation on chunked action trajectories for temporal consistency, and avoidance of additional LLM inference latency and IK conversion pipelines required by OLAF.

**B. Real-World Validation**
Real-world experiments were conducted on the ALOHA platform for Snack Box Transfer, Bottle Cap Opening, and Object Sorting tasks. Task performance was scored on a scale from 0.0 (failure) to 1.0 (full success), with 0.5 for partial completion.

- **Baseline ACT Performance:** The original ACT policy demonstrated strong baseline performance in real-world scenarios, achieving scores of 0.83 for Snack Box Transfer, 0.79 for Bottle Cap Opening, and 0.80 for Object Sorting. This indicates that ACT possesses some inherent robustness and interpolation capabilities, occasionally recovering from minor failures such as re-attempting a grasp.
- **ORPA Improves Real-World Robustness:**
 - **Action Perturbation Only:** When ORPA was trained with action perturbations alone, task scores improved across all tasks (Snack Box Transfer: 0.85, Bottle Cap Opening: 0.81, Object Sorting: 0.81). This suggests that learning residual corrections can effectively address execution failures.
 - **Action + Observation Perturbation:** Incorporating perturbed observations during ORPA training further enhanced performance (Snack Box Transfer: 0.86, Bottle Cap Opening: 0.82, Object Sorting: 0.83). This indicates that a small amount of additional observation-level correction data can contribute to robustness and data efficiency.
- **Challenging Tasks:** The Bottle Cap Opening task remained the most challenging due to its contact-rich interactions, precise alignment, and timing sensitivity. Despite this, ORPA still provided noticeable improvements over the baseline ACT.
- **Context-Aware Adaptation:** Qualitative results (Figure 4) illustrated ORPA's ability to provide context-dependent corrections, refining action trajectories based on feedback without modifying the underlying ACT policy. For instance, it corrected grasping and placement errors in snack box transfer, improved stability in bottle cap opening, and demonstrated robustness across various object configurations in object sorting.
- **Diverse Failure Recovery:** The framework's ability to generate residual corrections directly in joint space allowed recovery from various real-world failure modes beyond simple translation and rotation errors, including timing mismatches and imperfect contact interactions.
- **Inherent ACT Robustness:** The study also noted that some minor perturbations (e.g., "too low" in Figure 5) were within the original ACT policy's recovery capabilities, leading to successful task completion even without explicit ORPA intervention.

In summary, ORPA consistently demonstrated improved task success rates and recovery capabilities compared to baseline policies and alternative correction methods, both in controlled simulation environments and complex real-world manipulation scenarios.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Online Residual Policy Adaptation (ORPA) framework represents a significant contribution to the field of robotic manipulation, particularly in bridging the gap between high-performance imitation learning (IL) policies and their robust deployment in dynamic, real-world environments. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Enhanced Robustness and Reliability for IL Policies:** ORPA directly addresses a critical limitation of current IL policies: their sensitivity to small execution errors, environmental variations, and distribution shifts. By providing a mechanism for real-time, feedback-driven corrections, it significantly improves the success rates and recovery capabilities of robots in precision-sensitive manipulation tasks. This makes IL-trained robots more reliable and dependable in practical applications.

- **Enabling Real-Time Adaptation without Retraining:** One of the most impactful aspects of ORPA is its ability to facilitate immediate action-level refinement without requiring computationally expensive and time-consuming policy retraining or fine-tuning. This is crucial for interactive robotic systems where instant feedback and adaptation are essential, making IL more suitable for dynamic and collaborative human-robot environments. It bypasses the need for repeated data collection and optimization of large neural networks for every minor error.

- **Context-Aware and Flexible Corrections:** Unlike rigid, rule-based inverse kinematics (IK) corrections that apply fixed offsets regardless of the situation, ORPA learns context-dependent residual adjustments. By conditioning corrections on visual observations, robot states, and the current task phase, it can generate appropriate magnitudes and directions for corrective actions. This flexibility allows for more nuanced and effective error recovery that adapts to the specific demands of the manipulation task.

- **Direct Joint-Space Control:** The framework's ability to predict residual adjustments directly in joint space simplifies the control architecture by eliminating the need for explicit forward and inverse kinematics computations during the correction phase. This not only streamlines the process but also potentially reduces computational latency, which is vital for real-time robot control.

- **Data Efficiency through Synthetic Generation:** The methodology for generating corrective training data synthetically from successful demonstration trajectories is a practical advantage. It mitigates the costly and time-consuming process of collecting extensive human corrective demonstrations, making the training of the ORPA modules more scalable and efficient.

- **Facilitating Human-Robot Interaction:** By enabling robots to respond immediately and intelligently to human feedback (e.g., "too left," "too high"), ORPA enhances the interactivity of robotic systems. This allows human operators to intuitively guide and correct robot behavior, fostering a more natural and effective collaboration, and potentially accelerating the deployment of robots in less structured environments.

- **Broader Applicability to Manipulation Tasks:** The successful validation of ORPA on diverse precision-sensitive tasks, ranging from basic cube transfer to complex bimanual insertion and real-world object sorting, suggests its broad applicability. This could generalize to a wide array of robotic manipulation challenges where accuracy and adaptability under unexpected conditions are paramount.

In conclusion, ORPA offers a practical and efficient solution for enhancing the robustness and adaptability of imitation learning policies. Its focus on online, feedback-driven, context-aware residual corrections has the potential to significantly advance the real-world deployability and interactive capabilities of robotic manipulation systems, moving closer to robots that can operate reliably in complex and unpredictable human-centric environments.
