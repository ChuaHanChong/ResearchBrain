# Recovering the View: Benchmarking Physical Active Vision for Occlusion Recovery in Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> BAVO-Bench is a five-task bimanual active-vision benchmark that adds controlled external occlusions under three conditions (Clean, Stage, Random-time). It measures recovery with a counterfactual metric called Camera-attributable Visibility Gain. The companion policy, A-FAR, turns RGB-D into a robot-base point cloud and distills current and future relational structure from a frozen 4D teacher. It beats ManiFlow, ACT and π0.5 under all three conditions.

## Problem

- Existing active-vision manipulation work studies viewpoint needs that come from task geometry or predictable stages, not recovery from unexpected external occlusions such as a hand blocking the view.
- Computational methods that select or synthesize virtual viewpoints depend on reconstruction fidelity, so they fail exactly when task-relevant regions are heavily occluded or poorly observed.
- Task success alone cannot show whether a policy actually recovered a useful view, and a moving camera changes image observations even when the scene is static.

## Method

- **BAVO-Bench**: five multi-stage bimanual tasks on AV-ALOHA with camera-facing occluders, placed at fixed stage transitions for training and at sampled random times for evaluation, plus 500 VR demonstrations.
- ==Camera-attributable Visibility Gain (CVG)==: target visibility along the policy's camera trajectory minus a same-state frozen-camera counterfactual, averaged over a window, isolating recovery due to viewpoint motion alone.
- **A-FAR**: robot-base-frame colored point clouds feed ManiFlow. A ==WorldQueryFormer== predicts future point tokens, supervised by ==D4RT== relational distances and their evolution, and fuses them through a zero-initialized ==gated residual==. The teacher is not used at deployment.

## Results

- A-FAR improves on its ManiFlow backbone's success rate by **5.6**, **3.0** and **3.7 points** under Clean (**51.3%**), Stage (**63.7%**) and Random-time (**18.7%**) occlusion.
- π0.5 reaches **42.0%** Stage success but drops to **4.0%** under Random-time occlusion, despite the highest camera-attributable visibility gain (**34.42 points** vs **29.64** for A-FAR).
- Using moving camera-frame observations cuts A-FAR's average success from **44.7%** to **27.0%** and erases its advantage. Future relational modeling adds **3.7 points** over plain ManiFlow.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Recovering visibility and using it are separate skills: a policy can reveal the target yet still fail the manipulation, so active-vision evaluation needs metrics beyond task success.
> - Putting observations into a fixed robot-base frame matters more than adding predictive modeling. Temporal priors only help once camera-motion-induced variation is removed.
> - Randomizing occlusion timing exposes policies that replay demonstrated stage-specific camera moves instead of reacting to visibility failures, a general caution for imitation-learned recovery skills.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A bimanual robot hands a cube from one arm to the other and places it on a target plate. Partway through, an external obstruction can interrupt the camera's view. A fixed view can be blocked, and a wrist camera's view moves with the manipulation. Physical active vision gives the robot another option: it can move a separate camera to obtain a fresh view and then continue the task. The authors frame recovery from externally disrupted visibility during execution as a distinct problem from choosing views dictated by task geometry or predictable stages. Their benchmark, BAVO-Bench, makes that problem testable. Their policy, A-FAR, controls both camera and manipulation. When visibility failures arrive at unexpected times, A-FAR does better than the ManiFlow policy it builds on. A-FAR does not, however, have the highest visibility gain.

### 2. When the view fails

BAVO-Bench builds on AV-ALOHA, which introduced a dedicated movable camera arm and VR demonstrations for learning task-relevant viewpoints ([AV-ALOHA](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2409.17435)). The paper says the original AV-ALOHA setting primarily studies viewpoint needs arising from task geometry or predictable stages. BAVO-Bench holds the underlying task and control interface fixed. It then introduces external visibility failures during an episode, including at times that do not align with task transitions.

The benchmark has three conditions:

- **Clean** has no artificial benchmark occlusion.
- **Stage Occlusion** begins occluded and changes the target at two predefined task transitions. This is the training-matched condition.
- **Random-time Occlusion** also begins occluded, but it triggers subsequent events at sampled times, not necessarily at those transitions. It may contain more events than Stage, so it is a stronger recovery condition rather than a timing-matched ablation.

Policies are trained only on 100 VR Stage demonstrations per task (500 total) and then evaluated under all three conditions. Success rate is completion of the fifth of five ordered milestones, macro-averaged across tasks.

A separate metric, camera-attributable visibility gain (CVG), measures how much the camera's movement improves visibility. Over a 60-control-step recovery window, CVG compares the policy's camera trajectory with a counterfactual camera frozen at its pose when the occlusion event began. Both views are rendered at the same current scene state. These counterfactual renders are used only for evaluation and do not inform policy actions.

### 3. A policy that looks ahead

A-FAR first lifts RGB-D observations into a colored point cloud in a shared robot-base coordinate frame. In that frame, static geometry does not appear to move merely because the camera moved. During training, a frozen D4RT teacher, a pretrained 4D scene model, supplies relational cues for queried points at the current time and at a later time in demonstration clips. A-FAR learns to predict these future relational features from the current point representation and robot state. It then fuses the predictions into ManiFlow's action pathway. The teacher and future clips are used only in training. At deployment, the policy uses the current RGB-D observation and robot state.

A-FAR: viewpoint-stable 3D observation, training-only 4D teacher, and future-aware action policy. Predicted future tokens are fused into ManiFlow before an action chunk is executed; the teacher branch is removed at deployment.

The results below come from the Random-time Occlusion condition. A-FAR's macro-averaged success rate across the five tasks is **18.7% (±1.2)**, compared with **15.0% (±3.5)** for ManiFlow. Both models were trained only on Stage demonstrations, with three seeds and 20 evaluation episodes per condition. This is a modest absolute increase of 3.7 percentage points. A-FAR's improvement over ManiFlow is reported in all five tasks.

The visibility comparison tells a different story. In the same condition, π0.5 has the highest average CVG (**34.42 pp**, versus A-FAR's **29.64 pp**), but its task success rate is only **4.0%**. The paper also reports a Joint score, the per-task product SR × CVG/100, as a combined summary. A-FAR has the highest average Joint score (**5.78**), compared with **4.42** for ManiFlow, **2.97** for ACT, and **1.41** for π0.5. The comparison suggests that restoring visibility and exploiting the recovered view for manipulation are distinct capabilities.

Ablation, SR (%) under Clean, Stage, and Random-time. The future-modeling variants rise on the reported average. The moving-camera-frame versions have lower averages, and in that frame A-FAR does not exceed ManiFlow.

The authors interpret the shared base frame as a prerequisite for exploiting temporal relational cues.

### 4. Seeing is only half

A robot can change what it sees, but task performance depends on carrying that recovered information into subsequent manipulation. A-FAR's advantage over ManiFlow lies in coupling recovery with task performance, not in maximizing visibility gain.
