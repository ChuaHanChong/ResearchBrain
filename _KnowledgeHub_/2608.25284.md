# Generative Action-Chunk Sampling for Adaptive Stiffness Control in Physical Human-Robot Collaboration

> [!summary] Summary
> An adaptive stiffness framework for physical human-robot collaboration. A force-aware action-chunking transformer trained as a CVAE with an observation-conditioned prior samples multiple future action chunks from RGB images and external joint-torque estimates; the spread among samples continuously modulates joint stiffness and damping, yielding compliance at ambiguous decision points and firm assistance otherwise. Real collaborative transport success reached 0.95 versus 0.83 fixed-stiffness and 0.69 deterministic.

## Problem

- Action-chunking transformers use the mean of a fixed standard-normal prior at test time, emitting one deterministic chunk and never characterizing variation among plausible futures online.
- Force-aware chunking policies improve contact reasoning from torque inputs but generate no action alternatives and never link predictive variability to the robot's physical impedance.
- Variable-impedance methods that adapt compliance rely on a separate risk, semantic, phase, or role model rather than the policy already producing the motion.

## Method

- Multimodal ==force-aware action-chunking transformer== encodes an RGB CLS token plus an MLP-encoded joint-torque token; ==CVAE== training aligns a transformer posterior to a learned ==observation-conditioned prior== via ==KL divergence==, enabling stochastic chunk sampling at inference.
- ==Action-deviation signal==: empirical variance across ten sampled chunks per offset, square-rooted, linearly weighted toward later horizon steps, exponentially smoothed, then min-max normalized against offline-validation bounds.
- ==Adaptive stiffness controller== blends predefined low and high joint stiffness and damping matrices by a coefficient equal to one minus the normalized deviation, passed through a ==slew-rate limiter== at each low-level control step.

## Results

- Real 7-DoF collaborative transport across four directions, **180 trials**: **0.95** average success for the adaptive method against **0.83** for the fixed-stiffness ablation and **0.69** for deterministic FACTR.
- Forward transport showed the largest gap, **0.93** versus **0.27** for the deterministic baseline, whose representative trial accumulated external joint torque until the safety limit halted execution.
- Prior variance rose near **step 250** in latent dimensions **8, 10 and 11** and sampled joint trajectories diverged over **steps 250-300**, coinciding with the direction-determination bifurcation and a stiffness drop.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Disagreement among samples from a generative policy can itself serve as an online control signal, removing the need for a separate risk, phase, or role estimator.
> - Executing one sampled chunk rather than the sample mean preserves a coherent motion hypothesis; lowered stiffness, not action averaging, absorbs the multimodality.
> - The signal is a proxy for demonstrated-motion ambiguity, not calibrated intention probability or epistemic uncertainty, and its scaling bounds need per-task, per-hardware retuning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Physical human-robot interaction (pHRI) presents a challenge in designing robotic systems that can both provide assistance when human intent is clear and remain compliant when future motions are uncertain. Traditional fixed-stiffness controllers struggle with this balance; high stiffness can lead to resistance against unanticipated human motions, while low stiffness can limit the robot's ability to offer physical support. Effective pHRI requires an online adjustment of this balance.

The broader research landscape for this work encompasses three main areas: learning multimodal policies for contact-rich interaction, modeling intention ambiguity in human-robot interaction, and variable impedance control and role adaptation.

In **learning multimodal policies**, behavior cloning techniques enable robots to acquire manipulation policies from demonstrations. However, these methods can suffer from covariate shift during execution, where deviations compound in states outside the training distribution. Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT) addresses this by generating temporally coherent action segments and using a CVAE-based latent representation to encode variation. However, standard ACT inference typically produces a single deterministic action chunk by sampling from the mean of a fixed standard-normal prior, thus not leveraging the latent variation for online control. More recent work, such as Force Torque-Aware Action Chunking Transformer (FTACT) and Force-Attending Curriculum Training for Contact-Rich Policy Learning (FACTR), integrates force or torque observations with visual cues, improving contact reasoning in visually subtle or occluded scenarios. While these policies enhance multimodal perception, they do not directly utilize the variability among multiple sampled action chunks to regulate robot impedance. This paper addresses this gap by combining visual and external joint-torque observations with conditional generative action-chunk prediction.

**Modeling intention ambiguity** is crucial because human motion is inherently variable, and partial observations can precede multiple plausible task outcomes. Probabilistic Movement Primitives (ProMPs) and interaction primitives model distributions over trajectories for intention inference and cooperative tasks. Latent-variable sequence policies extend this probabilistic view to high-dimensional observations and action chunks. The concept that variation among sampled actions can signal ambiguity—small variation for similar futures, large variation for plausible multiple futures—is central to these approaches. This work leverages this concept, using action variation as an online proxy for uncertainty, distinct from calibrated probabilities of human intention or epistemic uncertainty, and directly links this signal to physical robot behavior, a previously underexplored area.

**Variable impedance control** is a standard approach in pHRI, where stiffness and damping are adjusted based on task requirements, demonstrations, or risk estimates. Lower stiffness can improve acceptance of human corrections, while higher stiffness can enhance tracking and assistance. Methods like uncertainty-aware minimal-intervention control and phase-dependent interaction strategies adapt robot intervention strength. More recent advancements, such as OmniVIC and role-allocation approaches, infer impedance from richer contextual representations or explicitly estimate leader-follower roles. This paper differentiates itself by deriving the variable impedance control signal directly from the *same generative policy* that produces the target action chunk. This approach eliminates the need for a separate risk, semantic, phase, or role model, positioning the work at the intersection of force-aware multimodal policy learning, stochastic modeling of human motion alternatives, and variable impedance control, with its distinguishing feature being the continuous adaptation of robot stiffness and damping based on the policy's sampled action variation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the challenges of physical human-robot collaboration (pHRI), where robots need to flexibly adapt their behavior to human partners whose intentions and motions can change dynamically. Current robotic control strategies often fall short in balancing assistance and compliance effectively. When a human's intention is clear, the robot should provide firm assistance. Conversely, when several future motions are plausible, the robot must be compliant to accept human guidance. Maintaining a constant high stiffness can lead to resistance against human corrections, while uniform low stiffness diminishes the physical support the robot can offer. This necessitates an online, adaptive adjustment of the robot's physical impedance.

Existing imitation learning methods, such as ACT, are capable of learning complex manipulation policies from demonstrations and can represent variations in demonstrated behaviors using a CVAE-based latent space. However, during standard inference, ACT typically generates a single deterministic action chunk, thereby not exploiting the inherent variability captured in its latent space for online adaptation. Furthermore, while force-aware policies like FTACT and FACTR improve contact reasoning by integrating force/torque observations, they also do not generate multiple action alternatives online or directly link the variability of these alternatives to robot impedance. This constitutes a significant gap in the current state of pHRI research: the potential of a force-aware generative policy to provide an online compliance signal remains largely unaddressed.

Based on these motivations, the key objectives of this work are:

1. **Develop a Generative Action-Chunking Policy:** To create a policy architecture that is conditioned on both visual (RGB image) and external joint-torque observations. This policy should be capable of sampling multiple plausible future action chunks, thereby explicitly representing the distribution of possible future human actions.
2. **Propose an Adaptive Stiffness Controller:** To design a control mechanism that continuously adjusts robot joint stiffness and damping. This adjustment should be directly driven by the variation observed among the multiple action chunks sampled by the generative policy, where greater variation signals ambiguity (leading to lower stiffness for compliance) and lower variation signals clarity (leading to higher stiffness for assistance).
3. **Integrate Multimodal Perception with Adaptive Control:** To establish a closed-loop system that links multimodal observation (vision and force), generative action-chunk prediction, and low-level adaptive stiffness control within a unified framework.
4. **Evaluate in a Real-World Task:** To empirically validate the proposed framework on a real robot in a collaborative transport task involving multiple directional choices, demonstrating its ability to balance assistance and compliance effectively and improve task success compared to fixed-stiffness and deterministic baselines.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework integrates a generative action-chunking policy with an adaptive stiffness controller. It extends existing multimodal action-chunking architectures like FACTR by incorporating ACT-style CVAE training, but crucially replaces ACT's fixed prior with an observation-conditioned prior. This modification allows the policy to sample multiple future action chunks, from which an online action-deviation signal is derived to dynamically adjust robot stiffness and damping.

**A. Policy Architecture and Formulation**
The policy utilizes a Transformer architecture to predict a chunk of future joint-position actions. At each time step $t$, the robot receives an RGB image $I_t$ and external joint-torque estimates $\tau_t$. These multimodal inputs are processed through modality-specific feature extractors. The RGB image is encoded by a pre-trained Vision Transformer (ViT), from which the learnable CLS token serves as the visual latent representation $z_V$. Concurrently, the external joint-torque estimates are processed by a multilayer perceptron (MLP) encoder to yield a force latent representation $z_F$. These representations are concatenated to form the input token sequence $X_t = [z_V; z_F]$.

A Transformer encoder $h_\theta$ then processes $X_t$ to compute a contextualized multimodal feature representation $H_E$. This $H_E$ acts as the condition for the generative process. To enable stochastic action-chunk generation, the architecture employs a CVAE-based training scheme. During training, an approximate posterior network (a Transformer encoder) estimates the mean $\mu_{q_\phi}$ and log variance $\log \sigma^2_{q_\phi}$ of the latent variable $z$, conditioned on $H_E$ and the ground-truth action chunk $q_{t:t+k}$. Concurrently, an MLP-based prior network estimates $\mu_{p_\psi}$ and $\log \sigma^2_{p_\psi}$ for an observation-conditioned prior distribution $p_\psi(z|H_E)$, which depends only on current observations. During training, the latent variable $z$ is sampled from the approximate posterior, while during inference, it is sampled from the learned observation-conditioned prior. Finally, a Transformer decoder $\pi_\theta$ takes $H_E$, learnable position embeddings, and the sampled latent variable $z$ to compute decoded features $H_D$, which are then projected via an MLP to produce the predicted action chunk $\hat{q}_{t:t+k}$. All actions are z-score normalized during training and inference and transformed back for execution.

**B. Training Procedure**
The network parameters are optimized using a loss function that combines a reconstruction loss ($L_{rec}$) and a Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence loss ($L_{KL}$), weighted by a coefficient $\beta$. $L_{rec}$ is the mean absolute error between predicted and ground-truth normalized action chunks. $L_{KL}$ measures the statistical distance between the approximate posterior and the observation-conditioned prior, enforcing alignment such that the prior can effectively generate appropriate latent variables during inference. A curriculum learning strategy, adapted from FACTR, is employed to prevent overfitting to visual inputs and ensure effective integration of force data.

**C. Adaptive Stiffness Control Based on Action Deviation**
The core of the adaptive stiffness control mechanism is the action-deviation signal. When multimodal observations are compatible with multiple future motions (e.g., at decision points), the policy's sampled actions tend to vary more, producing a larger action-deviation signal. This signal serves as a proxy for uncertainty, prompting the robot to lower its stiffness and become more compliant to human guidance. Conversely, when sampled actions are similar, the signal is small, leading the robot to increase its stiffness to firmly track the intended motion.

To quantify this, during inference, $M=10$ latent variables are sampled from the prior distribution, generating $M$ corresponding action chunks. For each chunk offset $\kappa$, the aggregate empirical action variance $\sigma^2_{t+\kappa}$ is calculated as the sum of squared Euclidean norms of deviations from the mean normalized action, with Bessel's correction applied. The square root of this variance yields the action-deviation magnitude $\sigma_{t+\kappa}$. A temporally weighted action-deviation signal $\sigma_{t,W}$ is then computed, assigning linearly increasing weights to later predictions within the chunk (from $w_{start}=0.1$ to $w_{end}=0.9$) to emphasize emerging alternatives. To enhance stability, an exponential moving average (EMA) with a smoothing factor $\gamma=0.1$ is applied to $\sigma_{t,W}$ to obtain $\sigma_{t,EMA}$. This smoothed signal is then normalized to a range of using empirically determined minimum ($\sigma_{min}=0.061$) and maximum ($\sigma_{max}=0.216$) values from offline validation data.

The normalized action-deviation signal, $\sigma_{t,norm}$, is used to derive a stiffness blending coefficient $\hat{\alpha}_t = 1.0 - \sigma_{t,norm}$. This coefficient dictates the mix between predefined high-stiffness ($K_H, D_H$) and low-stiffness ($K_L, D_L$) parameters. A slew-rate limiter is applied to $\hat{\alpha}_t$ to obtain the actual control coefficient $\alpha_r$, preventing abrupt changes in physical response (max change $\Delta\alpha_{max}=0.005$ per low-level control step). Finally, the robot's stiffness matrix $K_r$ and damping matrix $D_r$ are determined by linear interpolation using $\alpha_r$.

**D. Experimental Environment and Evaluation**
The framework was evaluated in a collaborative transport task using a 7-DoF Franka Research 3 robot arm. A human collaborator and the robot jointly move a light box in one of four target directions: forward, backward, left, or right. The task is divided into three phases: Approach and Lifting (fixed upward motion), Direction Determination and Guidance (human indicates direction, expected high ambiguity), and Transport and Assistance (established direction, expected low ambiguity). External joint-torque estimates from the robot's API and RGB images from an Intel RealSense D435 camera constitute the observations.

Data collection involved 120 demonstration trials (15 for each of four directions under two stiffness settings), divided into 100 training and 20 offline validation trials. The real-world evaluation compared three conditions: the Proposed method (stochastic policy + adaptive stiffness), an Ablation (same stochastic policy + fixed "Medium" stiffness), and a FACTR baseline (separately trained deterministic FACTR + fixed "Medium" stiffness). A total of 180 online evaluation trials (15 for each condition and direction) were conducted. Success was defined by reaching a prespecified displacement in the instructed direction, while failure was recorded for torque-limit shutdowns or movement in unintended directions.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation yielded several findings regarding the latent space dynamics, action chunk generation, and real-world task performance.

**A. Analysis of Latent-Space Variance**
Analysis of the 16-dimensional latent space parameters across 20 offline validation sequences revealed distinct behaviors for the approximate posterior and prior variances. The approximate posterior variance remained close to zero throughout the task, indicating that when the ground-truth future action chunk was available during training, the latent distribution became highly concentrated. In contrast, the observation-conditioned prior variance was larger and exhibited temporal fluctuations. Notably, the prior variance tended to increase around time step 250, particularly in latent dimensions 8, 10, and 11. This interval correlated with the direction-determination phase of the collaborative transport task, suggesting that these latent dimensions were sensitive to behavioral bifurcations or increased ambiguity regarding the intended motion.

**B. Analysis of Sampled Action Chunks**
Further analysis of action chunks sampled from the prior distribution demonstrated that changes in the learned prior's latent distribution directly influenced the diversity of generated actions. Figure 6 illustrates that, especially between steps 250 and 300, a period associated with direction determination, there was pronounced variation among the sampled joint trajectories. This variation was particularly evident in Joints 1, 2, 4, and 7. The co-occurrence of increased prior variance and greater variation among the generated joint trajectories indicates that the learned latent distribution effectively captured and reflected alternative action possibilities during phases of high ambiguity.

**C. Real-World Evaluation**
The proposed framework was evaluated in a real-world collaborative transport task against two baselines. Quantitative results, summarized in Table II, indicate that the proposed method achieved an overall average success rate of 0.95 (17.5 out of 15 trials on average for each direction), compared to 0.83 for the fixed-stiffness ablation and 0.69 for the deterministic FACTR baseline.

- **Directional Performance:** The proposed method demonstrated superior performance across all four transport directions. The largest numerical difference was observed in the "Forward" direction, where the proposed method achieved 14/15 successes, while the ablation recorded 10/15, and the FACTR baseline only 4/15. In "Backward" and "Left" directions, the proposed method and ablation showed similar high success rates. For "Rightward" transport, all methods achieved relatively high success rates (Proposed: 14/15, Ablation: 12/15, FACTR: 12/15).
- **Analysis of Forward Transport (Figure 7):** During representative "Forward" transport trials, the proposed method exhibited an increase in the normalized action-deviation signal, which subsequently led to a reduction in robot stiffness around the bifurcation interval (time steps 250–300). In contrast, the FACTR baseline experienced increasing external joint-torque estimates, culminating in a torque safety limit shutdown. The ablation condition, while completing the task, showed sustained high external joint-torque estimates in the same interval, suggesting continuous resistance from the robot. These observations indicate that the adaptive stiffness mechanism in the proposed method effectively modulated compliance during ambiguous phases, reducing excessive interaction forces.
- **Analysis of Rightward Transport:** In "Rightward" transport, some failures in the ablation and FACTR baseline trials involved unintended transitions into backward motion after initial rightward movement. This was attributed to potential visual ambiguities near task completion, which the policy might misinterpret as another direction-determination phase. In such instances, the proposed method's adaptive stiffness enabled human participants to manually correct the robot's unintended return motion. This physical adjustability limited excessive backward movement and helped maintain the target position, illustrating how adaptive compliance can mitigate the physical consequences of visual ambiguity.

In summary, the results suggest that the proposed framework successfully leverages variation among sampled action chunks as an online control signal for adaptive stiffness, leading to improved task success rates and more compliant, effective human-robot collaboration in the evaluated task.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The significance of this research lies in its demonstration that variability within a generative action policy can serve as a direct, online control signal for regulating robot impedance in physical human-robot interaction (pHRI). This approach offers a novel method for balancing the inherent trade-off between providing physical assistance and ensuring compliance with human guidance.

**A. Core Contribution to pHRI:** The central contribution is the establishment of a direct link between a generative policy's internal representation of future action alternatives and the robot's physical impedance. By using the variation among sampled action chunks to adapt stiffness and damping, the robot can dynamically adjust its level of intervention. This is crucial for collaborative tasks where human intention may be ambiguous at decision points but clear during execution. The system's ability to reduce stiffness during phases of high uncertainty (e.g., direction determination) and increase it when the path is clear contributes to a more intuitive and effective collaboration experience.

**B. Empirical Performance Improvement:** The empirical results demonstrate a notable improvement in task success rates, with the proposed method achieving an average of 0.95 compared to 0.83 for a fixed-stiffness ablation and 0.69 for a deterministic FACTR baseline. This quantitative advantage suggests that adaptive stiffness, as driven by generative action-chunk sampling, translates into tangible benefits in real-world collaborative scenarios. The comparison with the ablation, which uses the same generative policy but fixed stiffness, indicates that the adaptive control mechanism itself provides an empirical advantage in this task, likely due to access to a wider range of stiffness parameters (Low to High) rather than just adaptation around a medium setting.

**C. Mitigation of Interaction Conflicts:** The analysis of external joint-torque estimates and failure modes highlights the impact of adaptive stiffness. Fixed-stiffness conditions exhibited larger or sustained external joint-torque estimates, indicating resistance or conflicts with human inputs. The proposed method, by reducing stiffness during ambiguous phases, allowed for smoother human guidance and mitigated these conflicts, leading to fewer torque-limit shutdowns. Furthermore, in cases of visual ambiguity causing potential policy misinterpretations (e.g., in rightward transport), the increased compliance allowed human partners to physically correct robot actions, preventing task failure.

**D. Implicit Role Adaptation:** The observed behavior can be interpreted as a primitive form of implicit role adaptation. When the generative policy indicates high ambiguity among future actions, the robot becomes more compliant, effectively yielding to human leadership. Conversely, when ambiguity is low, the robot provides firmer assistance, implicitly taking a more supportive role. This adaptation occurs without an explicit semantic model or dedicated leader-follower optimization, deriving the control signal directly from the action generation distribution. This direct connection simplifies the control architecture compared to methods requiring separate high-level reasoning modules.

**E. Future Research Directions:** The paper also outlines several important avenues for future work. Automating the calibration of the action-deviation-to-stiffness mapping would enhance robustness and reduce manual tuning effort. Extending the framework to broader collaborative settings beyond the specific transport task is crucial for demonstrating generalizability. Incorporating explicit epistemic uncertainty measures could improve robustness under out-of-distribution observations and allow for more robust fail-safe compliance strategies. Finally, moving beyond a reactive framework toward active bidirectional collaboration, such as dynamic leader-follower role switching, could lead to even more sophisticated and intuitive human-robot cooperation.

In conclusion, this research presents a method for endowing robots with adaptive compliance, enabling them to navigate the complexities of human intention during physical collaboration. By linking the generative capabilities of a multimodal policy to a low-level impedance controller, the work advances the field towards more responsive, robust, and intuitive human-robot interaction systems.
