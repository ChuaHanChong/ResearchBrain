# Towards Trustworthy Embodied Intelligence: A Systems Framework and Graded Trustworthiness Levels

> [!summary] Summary
> SparkLab@Xspark AI and collaborators present a comprehensive systems framework and a six-level hierarchy (T0-T5) to define and ensure trustworthiness in embodied AI systems. This work bridges the gap between AI capabilities and the necessity for sustained safe and reliable physical operation, offering a structured approach for design, evaluation, and deployment.

## Problem

- Errors in embodied AI systems can lead to severe physical harm or operational failures in real-world environments, unlike purely digital systems where consequences are primarily informational.
- Current research often focuses on isolated task completion or safety mechanisms, lacking a unified, end-to-end framework to address the propagation of failures across different layers of an embodied system.
- There is an absence of structured methods to jointly define and evaluate task capability and safety, characterize failure propagation, or manage the evolution of trustworthiness from laboratory to field deployment.

## Method

- Proposed a **four-layer systems framework** (==Model==, ==System==, ==Evidence==, ==Deployment==) to systematically organize mechanisms for trustworthy embodied intelligence, explicitly designed to address ==cross-layer failure propagation==.
- Introduced a definition of "trustworthy embodied intelligence" as =="sustained safe success"==, emphasizing the joint necessity of task capability and safety within acceptable risk bounds.
- Developed a non-normative hierarchy of six graded trustworthiness levels (**T0-T5**) to assess and communicate the strength of justified trustworthiness claims for specific system configurations and operating conditions.

## Results

- The four-layer framework provides a systematic organization of interdisciplinary mechanisms, detailing responsibilities from model safety and system assurance to evidence generation and deployment governance.
- The **T0-T5** trustworthiness hierarchy establishes a practical, cumulative scale to grade the justified strength of trustworthiness claims across **five dimensions**: capability adequacy, safety, system assurance, evidence sufficiency, and deployment governance.
- A synthesis of prior work and a future research roadmap are presented, highlighting the need for robust integration of AI capabilities with established system assurance foundations for reliable real-world deployment and informing future standardization efforts.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Trustworthiness in embodied AI is an end-to-end property, requiring integrated mechanisms across all system layers to achieve sustained safe success, not just isolated task completion.
> - Failures and assumptions propagate across layers, necessitating explicit consideration of the semantic-physical gap, action-consequence gap, and cross-layer non-compositionality.
> - Trustworthiness claims are inherently bounded to specific contexts and rely on diverse evidence, requiring rigorous lifecycle governance, runtime monitoring, and effective human intervention strategies for validation and maintenance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a critical juncture in the evolution of artificial intelligence, where recent advancements in vision-language-action models, robot foundation models, world models, tactile perception, and whole-body control have significantly expanded the capabilities of embodied systems. These systems are moving AI from purely digital inference to closed-loop physical interaction, enabling robots to perform complex tasks in manipulation, navigation, and human interaction.

However, this transition introduces a fundamental shift in the consequences of error. Unlike digital systems where errors may degrade information or service quality, errors in embodied systems can lead to immediate physical or operational harm, such as unsafe motion, equipment damage, or physical injury. The existing research landscape often focuses on task completion metrics or isolated safety mechanisms. The paper identifies a gap where experimental capability or benchmark performance alone is insufficient to establish trustworthiness for real-world deployment. Factors like distribution shift, sensor degradation, communication delays, software changes, and unanticipated human behavior are common in deployment but often constrained or absent in laboratory and simulation evaluations.

The paper synthesizes perspectives from various fields, including embodied AI, AI safety, robotics, control, cybersecurity, dependable computing, distributed systems, human factors, and engineering standards. It notes that while these fields contribute to different aspects of trustworthiness (e.g., model robustness, runtime safeguards, fault-tolerant control, evaluation protocols, intervention, lifecycle assurance), a unified, end-to-end framework is needed to address the propagation of failures across different layers of an embodied system. This work aims to bridge this interdisciplinary gap by providing a holistic view of trustworthiness for physical AI systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to define, characterize, and provide a comprehensive framework for achieving "trustworthy embodied intelligence." The authors motivate this objective by highlighting the distinct and severe consequences of errors in physical AI systems compared to purely digital ones. Task completion, while a necessary component, is not sufficient to establish trustworthiness when failures can result in irreversible physical harm.

The paper defines trustworthy embodied intelligence as "the sustained capacity to execute specified tasks reliably under environmental and system variation while maintaining risk within acceptable bounds," which it terms "sustained safe success." This objective requires two jointly necessary properties:
1. **Task capability:** Encompassing adequate task success, quality, efficiency, timeliness, robustness, and generalization across the intended task distribution.
2. **Safety:** Ensuring behavior consistent with appropriate safety preferences and explicit constraints on actions, consequences, and authority.

The motivation for developing a new framework and trustworthiness levels stems from several observed deficiencies in current approaches:
- **Layer-specific deficiencies:** The paper argues that individual layers (model, system, evidence, deployment) can each contribute to untrustworthiness. Model errors can lead to unsafe proposals, system failures can turn admissible proposals into unsafe actions, evidence deficiencies can allow unsupported deployment claims, and deployment failures can invalidate previously supported claims.
- **Cross-layer failure propagation:** Critical problems arise from the interdependence of layers, including the semantic-physical gap (abstract instructions lacking physical constraints), the action-consequence gap (same action having different physical outcomes based on state), and cross-layer non-compositionality (individually reliable components failing when integrated). No single layer can establish end-to-end trustworthiness.
- **Lack of structured assessment:** Existing methods lack a unified way to jointly define and evaluate task capability and safety, characterize failure propagation, identify mechanisms for safe execution, substantiate bounded deployment claims, and manage the evolution of trustworthiness from laboratory to field.
- **Need for Bounded Claims:** Trustworthiness is not a universal attribute but is inherently bounded to a specific system configuration, task distribution, embodiment, environment, authority structure, and body of evidence. This necessitates a mechanism to grade the strength of these bounded claims.

To address these motivations, the paper sets out to make four specific contributions:
1. Provide a deployment-oriented definition of trustworthiness.
2. Propose a four-layer systems framework for organizing trustworthiness mechanisms.
3. Synthesize mechanisms, failures, and evidence across these layers, drawing from multiple disciplines.
4. Introduce a graded hierarchy (T0-T5) of trustworthiness levels to support bounded deployment, comparative evaluation, research prioritization, and future standardization.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology of this research involves a conceptual framework design, drawing parallels from established safety-critical domains, and a hierarchical classification system.

#### 3.1. The Four-Layer Systems Framework

The core of the methodology is a novel four-layer framework that organizes the mechanisms necessary for trustworthy embodied intelligence. These layers are defined as functional responsibilities, rather than isolated software modules, and are explicitly designed to be interdependent, addressing cross-layer failure propagation.
- **Model Layer:** This layer is responsible for interpreting observations, task requests, and context to generate state representations, plans, skills, and action proposals. It encompasses learned and conventional components for perception, grounding, reasoning, planning, and world modeling. The model layer aims to produce effective, physically feasible, uncertainty-aware, safety-constrained, and authority-consistent behavior. Mechanisms include grounded perception and state representation, safety-aware reasoning, planning and policy generation (e.g., constrained reinforcement learning, predictive safety filters), world models for long-horizon risk assessment, and ensuring model integrity and governable interfaces.
- **System Layer:** This layer translates model-generated proposals into physical actions through sensing, computation, communication, control, and actuation. It is focused on dependable physical execution, enforcing safety constraints, and containing residual failures. Key aspects include hardware as the physical assurance substrate (sensors, computation, communication, energy, actuation, mechanical design), multi-rate system integration to manage different timescales of operation, fault containment and fallback mechanisms (detection, isolation, degradation, recovery, local protection), and considerations for cyber-physical and distributed execution.
- **Evidence Layer:** This layer is tasked with determining which bounded claims about the integrated system are justified by various forms of evidence. It begins by defining evaluation objects and claims, and emphasizes the joint evaluation of capability and safety using metrics like "safe-success rate" and distinguishing between safe success, safe failure, unsafe success, and unsafe failure. The methodology employs complementary forms of evidence: formal (e.g., temporal specifications), statistical (e.g., confidence intervals), empirical (e.g., simulations, physical experiments), and operational (e.g., deployment records). It advocates for staged evaluation, gradually increasing realism and exposure from simulation to physical evaluation and pilot deployment, and stresses evaluation under stress, failure, and long-horizon conditions. The layer culminates in the concept of assurance cases to connect claims, arguments, assumptions, and evidence.
- **Deployment Layer:** This layer is responsible for maintaining the validity of trustworthiness claims during the operational lifespan of the system. It governs operational authority, human intervention, incident management, and lifecycle change. Mechanisms include defining operational boundaries and runtime admission rules, continuous runtime monitoring of assumptions (e.g., task progress, system health, distribution shift), explicit authority management and effective human control, graceful degradation and recovery strategies, incident response and operational evidence collection, and rigorous change control and revalidation procedures (forming a closed loop of validate → deploy → monitor → diagnose → restrict/update → revalidate).

#### 3.2. Graded Trustworthiness Levels (T0-T5)

To provide a structured way to assess and communicate the strength of trustworthiness, the paper proposes a non-normative hierarchy of six Trustworthy Embodied Intelligence (TEI) levels, from T0 to T5. These levels characterize progressively stronger and more sustained forms of justified trustworthiness for a specific `⟨system version, task, embodiment, operating domain, authority, evidence⟩`.
- **Assessment Dimensions:** Each TEI level is assessed across five cumulative and jointly necessary dimensions: capability adequacy, safety and residual risk, system assurance, evidence sufficiency, and deployment governance.
- **Level Definitions:** The paper provides detailed definitions for each level, from T0 (No Trustworthy Embodied Intelligence) where no substantiated claim exists, through T1 (Constrained), T2 (Partially), T3 (Conditionally), T4 (Highly), to T5 (Sustained Trustworthy Embodied Intelligence), which signifies a governed process for maintaining justified claims under evolving conditions.
- **Transition Logic:** Progression to higher levels requires evidence that increased capability or operational responsibility is matched by stronger system assurance, supporting evidence, and deployment governance. A system can also be downgraded if evidence expires or conditions change.
- **Relation to Existing Taxonomies:** The authors explicitly distinguish their TEI hierarchy from other taxonomies like AgiBot G1-G5 (embodied capability) and SAE L0-L5 (driving automation), emphasizing that TEI levels focus on the *justifiable strength* of a trustworthiness claim rather than just intelligence or autonomy.

The methodology integrates interdisciplinary concepts to provide a holistic framework for understanding, designing, evaluating, and deploying trustworthy embodied AI systems.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper’s main findings and results are encapsulated in its proposed framework and the accompanying trustworthiness hierarchy, along with a synthesis of existing work and identification of future research directions.

#### 4.1. Definition of Trustworthy Embodied Intelligence

A core finding is the comprehensive, deployment-oriented definition of "trustworthy embodied intelligence" as "sustained safe success." This definition underscores that merely completing a task is insufficient; the system must do so reliably under variation, maintaining risk within acceptable bounds. It highlights that trustworthiness is an end-to-end property dependent on both task capability and safety, and is always bounded to specific system configurations, tasks, environments, and evidence.

#### 4.2. The Four-Layer Systems Framework

The central result is the development of a four-layer framework (Model, System, Evidence, Deployment) that systematically organizes the diverse mechanisms required for trustworthiness. This framework explicitly addresses:
- **Model-level challenges:** The need for grounded perception, safety-aware reasoning, robust planning, and governable interfaces that explicitly convey uncertainty and safety preferences.
- **System-level mechanisms:** The critical role of hardware as an assurance substrate, multi-rate system integration, fault containment, fallback behaviors, and cyber-physical security in ensuring dependable physical execution.
- **Evidence-level rigor:** The necessity of joint capability and safety evaluation, using a variety of evidence types (formal, statistical, empirical, operational), and structured assurance cases to substantiate bounded claims. The paper’s proposal for outcomes beyond binary success (safe success, safe failure, unsafe success, unsafe failure) is a key outcome here.
- **Deployment-level governance:** The requirement for runtime monitoring of operational boundaries and assumptions, effective human authority and intervention strategies, robust degradation and recovery processes, incident response protocols, and rigorous change control to maintain trustworthiness over time.

A significant finding is the emphasis on cross-layer interdependence, illustrating how failures and assumptions propagate and how no single layer can guarantee end-to-end trustworthiness. It identifies the "semantic-physical gap," "action-consequence gap," and "cross-layer non-compositionality" as fundamental challenges.

#### 4.3. Graded Trustworthiness Levels (T0-T5)

The paper’s second major result is the introduction of the T0-T5 hierarchy of Trustworthy Embodied Intelligence. This non-normative classification system provides a scale to grade the strength and scope of justified trustworthiness claims, assessed across five dimensions: capability adequacy, safety and residual risk, system assurance, evidence sufficiency, and deployment governance.
- Each level (T0: No Trustworthy; T1: Constrained; T2: Partially; T3: Conditionally; T4: Highly; T5: Sustained) defines progressively more stringent requirements and broader operational responsibility.
- A key insight is that advancing through these levels is not solely about increasing AI intelligence or autonomy, but about the *strength of the substantiated claim* supported by evidence and governance under specific operating conditions. For example, T5 focuses on the governance processes that *sustain* trustworthiness under evolving conditions, rather than universal competence.

#### 4.4. Synthesis of Prior Work and Future Research Roadmap

The paper synthesizes decades of research across disparate fields, tracing the evolution of relevant work from foundations in physical and system assurance to safe learning, general-purpose embodied models, and lifecycle-oriented trustworthiness. It identifies that while model capability has advanced rapidly, the integration of these models with mature system assurance foundations remains underexplored, as do claim-oriented evidence, runtime governance, and assurance under change. The paper concludes by presenting a multi-level future research roadmap (infrastructure, foundation models, trustworthy learning, evaluation, applications), aligning it with existing safety and AI governance standards, and highlighting cross-cutting challenges like continual learning, long-horizon planning, and human alignment.

In essence, the paper provides a structured, interdisciplinary lens through which to view and build trustworthy embodied AI, moving beyond fragmented approaches to a holistic, lifecycle-oriented perspective.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research has several significant implications and potential impacts across the fields of artificial intelligence, robotics, safety engineering, and standardization.

#### 5.1. Providing a Comprehensive and Structured Framework

The proposed four-layer framework offers a unified and interdisciplinary approach to understanding and building trustworthy embodied intelligence. By systematically categorizing the mechanisms and challenges into Model, System, Evidence, and Deployment layers, it moves beyond siloed solutions to a holistic perspective. This structure can serve as a common reference point for researchers, engineers, and policymakers, facilitating more integrated development and assessment practices. It highlights the critical interplay between learned intelligence and dependable physical execution, which is crucial for real-world deployment.

#### 5.2. Enhancing Safety and Reliability in Embodied AI

By explicitly defining "sustained safe success" and detailing the mechanisms required at each layer—from uncertainty-aware model proposals to hardware safeguards, fault containment, and runtime monitoring—the work directly contributes to improving the safety and reliability of embodied systems. The emphasis on cross-layer failure propagation and the need for complementary protection mechanisms can lead to the design of more robust systems that anticipate and mitigate risks more effectively, reducing the likelihood of physical harm or operational failures.

#### 5.3. Guiding Research and Development Priorities

The paper’s synthesis of prior work and identification of open research directions within each layer, coupled with a broader research roadmap, provides a clear agenda for future investigation. This can help to prioritize funding and effort in areas that are currently underexplored, such as physically grounded uncertainty, recovery-centered execution, and assurance-preserving reconfiguration. It encourages researchers to focus not just on increasing capability but also on validating and sustaining trustworthiness throughout the system's lifecycle.

#### 5.4. Facilitating Bounded and Staged Deployment

The concept of "bounded trustworthiness claims" and the T0-T5 hierarchy provide a practical tool for the staged deployment of embodied systems. Instead of a binary "trustworthy/untrustworthy" assessment, stakeholders can use these levels to define the specific conditions, capabilities, and risks under which a system is deemed trustworthy. This enables a more controlled and evidence-based introduction of embodied AI into various applications, starting with highly constrained environments (T1, T2) and progressing to more autonomous and adaptive operations (T3, T4, T5) as evidence and governance mechanisms mature. This approach aligns with best practices in other safety-critical domains, such as autonomous driving.

#### 5.5. Informing Standardization and Regulatory Efforts

The TEI hierarchy serves as an analytical basis for future standardization efforts in embodied AI. As the paper notes, existing standards address parts of the problem, but none fully span the combination of learned intelligence, physical interaction, evaluation, and lifecycle governance. The TEI levels can inform the development of domain-specific profiles that define quantitative thresholds, mandatory safeguards, evaluation requirements, and governance obligations, thus contributing to a more consistent and robust regulatory landscape for embodied AI.

#### 5.6. Promoting a Lifecycle Perspective on Trustworthiness

The research underscores that trustworthiness is not a static property established at design time, but an ongoing process maintained throughout the system's operational lifecycle. The Deployment layer, with its emphasis on runtime monitoring, incident response, and change control, highlights the need for continuous assurance. This perspective encourages developers and operators to implement robust processes for detecting degradation, managing changes, and revalidating claims, fostering a culture of continuous improvement and accountability.

In summary, this paper represents a foundational contribution to the field of embodied AI by providing a much-needed comprehensive framework and a practical hierarchy for assessing and developing trustworthy systems. Its impact is likely to be felt across the design, development, evaluation, deployment, and regulation of physical AI, promoting safer and more reliable integration of these technologies into society.
