# PhysBrain 1.5: From Vision-Language Models to Physical Foundation Models

> [!summary] Summary
> PhysBrain 1.5 is an 8B physical foundation model built on Qwen3-VL-Instruct that expresses language answers, end-effector trajectories, and future RGB/depth/robot-mask states as discrete tokens sharing one vocabulary, backbone, and output head, all trained by masked next-token prediction. Embodied pre-training supervision comes entirely from human interaction video. It averages 72.5 across 28 embodied benchmarks, a new open-source state of the art.

## Problem

- Understanding, action, and prediction capabilities sit in separate specialized models (spatial reasoners, VLAs, world models), so no single backbone serves the whole physical loop.
- Joint training is blocked by mismatched supervision: language plus spatial coordinates, temporally structured motion, and dense visual outputs each conventionally demand their own head and loss.
- Robot action corpora disagree on coordinate frames, units, control frequency, and calibration metadata, making global canonicalization across embodiments unreliable and costly to scale.

## Method

- Unified vocabulary of language, action, and visual tokens over one ==autoregressive== backbone, embedding table, and LM output head, with per-task loss masks selecting which target tokens each objective supervises.
- **ActionPiece** tokenizer (512-token codebook, trained on 28.7M 16-step segments) encodes 10D relative end-effector chunks into 32 tokens; the preceding chunk supplies motion context for implicit system identification.
- Shared 16,384-code ==VQ-VAE== tokenizes future RGB, depth, and robot mask at 128x128, spatially interleaved in RGB-depth-mask order into 770 tokens, with no modality-specific heads or pixel losses.

## Results

- Average **72.5** over 28 embodied benchmarks at 8B: **6.5** points above the best open-source baseline Hy-Embodied-VLM-1.0 (**66.0**), near GPT-6-Astra (**73.3**) and Gemini 3.6 Flash (**73.0**).
- Beats its Qwen3-VL-Instruct base on all 28 benchmarks, ranking first on **14** and top-two on **24**, while general multimodal scores hold roughly level (MMStar **65.6** vs **64.99**).
- Action-token perplexity drops from **17.12** to **5.07** in-distribution and **12.39** to **6.57** on held-out RoboDojo; trajectory and future-frame evidence stays qualitative, with no closed-loop rollouts.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A single next-token objective with loss masks absorbs heterogeneous supervision: added action and future-state targets need no new heads and cost little general multimodal ability.
> - Conditioning on recent action history can substitute for global coordinate canonicalization, letting a model infer each source's convention instead of paying for unreliable frame conversion.
> - Strong understanding benchmarks and falling action perplexity say nothing about closed-loop task success; offline trajectory fit is not evidence of executed performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position within the Broader Research Landscape

PhysBrain 1.5 addresses the development of foundation models for embodied artificial intelligence, where an agent must interpret visual observations, reason about spatial and task relationships, select actions, and anticipate how the environment will change. This area has developed from several related research directions.

General vision-language models provide image and video understanding, language generation, instruction following, and broad commonsense knowledge. However, they commonly lack representations designed for physical interaction, such as end-effector trajectories, object affordances, or future scene states. In parallel, specialized models have been developed for spatial reasoning, grounding, manipulation planning, robot control, and execution assessment. Vision-language-action models, including the π series, GR00T, and Qwen-VLA, connect visual and linguistic inputs to robot actions. World models such as DreamZero and Qwen-RobotWorld focus on predicting future physical states, while systems such as SayCan, Code as Policies, and VoxPoser connect language reasoning to robot skills or spatial control.

More recent embodied foundation models attempt to combine these capabilities in a single system. The paper places PhysBrain 1.5 in this emerging group, alongside HY-Embodied, RynnBrain, MiMo-Embodied, Cosmos, UniVLA, and RynnVLA. Its specific contribution is to unify three output types—language responses, end-effector motion, and future visual states—within one autoregressive model and one discrete token-generation framework.

The work also follows an “understanding-first” strategy. Rather than relying primarily on large collections of robot demonstrations for pre-training, it derives physical supervision from human interaction videos. This approach is intended to address the limited scale, high acquisition cost, and embodiment specificity of robot data while retaining the advantages of human demonstrations for learning task semantics and physical commonsense.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

The central objective is to construct a physical foundation model that supports the complete interaction cycle described by the authors as the **physical loop**: observing an environment, understanding its geometry and task state, generating an action, and predicting the resulting state transition.

The paper identifies several motivations for this design. First, embodied intelligence involves more than visual recognition. A robot must understand object locations, spatial relations, contact regions, affordances, task progress, and the likely consequences of an action. A model that only answers questions about the current image does not directly provide the motion or predictive state representations required for control.

Second, existing capabilities are often trained in separate models with incompatible interfaces. Language reasoning, action prediction, and visual forecasting may use different architectures, supervision formats, and optimization objectives. This separation can make it difficult for semantic knowledge to influence motion generation or for action and state-transition data to improve spatial reasoning. PhysBrain 1.5 therefore aims to make these capabilities share parameters and training signals.

Third, robot data are difficult to scale across embodiments. Different robots use different coordinate systems, action frequencies, control conventions, and sensor configurations. The authors seek a representation that can combine human and robot trajectories without requiring complete global calibration across all sources.

Finally, the work aims to preserve the general multimodal abilities of its base vision-language model. Specialization for robotics can degrade ordinary image, video, document, diagram, or text understanding, so the training mixture includes general language and vision-language instruction data.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Model Architecture

PhysBrain 1.5 is built from the **8-billion-parameter Qwen3-VL-Instruct** model. Its main architectural change is the extension of the language vocabulary into a combined vocabulary containing language tokens, action tokens, and visual-state tokens:

\[
V = V_{\text{lang}} \cup V_{\text{act}} \cup V_{\text{vis}}.
\]

All three task families use the same vision encoder, language-model backbone, token embeddings, and output head. Training uses masked autoregressive next-token prediction. Task-specific formatting and loss masks determine whether the model generates language, action sequences, or future visual-state tokens.

#### 3.2. Action Representation

Human wrist motion is recovered using the Human-as-Humanoid pipeline. Finger motion is omitted, and both human and robot data are represented as short trajectories for the left and right end effectors. Each action chunk contains 16 future steps. Every step is represented by ten values: three-dimensional relative translation, six-dimensional relative rotation, and gripper closure.

The trajectories are discretized using the **ActionPiece** tokenizer. ActionPiece was trained on 28.7 million 16-step trajectory segments and uses a 512-token action vocabulary. In the PhysBrain configuration, one wrist trajectory is encoded into 32 action tokens.

Because the data sources use different local coordinate conventions, the authors do not enforce one globally canonical frame. Instead, the model may receive the immediately preceding action chunk as motion context. This history is intended to help it infer local scale, direction, frequency, and embodiment-specific motion conventions. Action prediction is consequently framed as conditional trajectory continuation rather than isolated regression.

#### 3.3. Future-State Representation

The model predicts a future state consisting of three spatially aligned outputs: an RGB image, a depth map, and a human or robot mask. These modalities correspond to the same future timestamp and image coordinate system.

A shared VQ-VAE tokenizes each modality at 128-by-128 resolution. Each output becomes a 16-by-16 grid containing 256 discrete codes, with a codebook size of 16,384. The model generates the modalities in an interleaved RGB-depth-mask order for each spatial location, producing a sequence of 770 tokens including start and end delimiters. At inference, the sequence is separated into three grids and decoded into an RGB image, depth map, and mask.

#### 3.4. Training Data and Procedure

Training has two stages. The first is physical-aware pre-training, and the second is supervised fine-tuning.

Pre-training uses approximately 30,000 hours of curated human interaction video from egocentric, exocentric, and panoramic sources. These include Ego4D, Ego-Exo4D, EgoDex, EgoLife, EgoVerse, Xperience-10M, Egocentric-10K, and two in-house datasets, PhysBrain-Human and PhysBrain-Ego360. Videos are segmented into task-centered episodes and filtered for visual quality, temporal consistency, and interaction visibility.

From these videos, the authors construct 24.3 million physical perception samples, 31.2 million human-action samples, and 26.8 million future-state samples. Perception supervision includes captions, visual question answering, object detection, segmentation, depth, pointing, counting, temporal grounding, tracking, and multi-view correspondence. Action supervision uses recovered human wrist trajectories. Future-state supervision pairs an earlier observation with a later RGB frame, depth map, and human-part mask.

The pre-training mixture also includes 14.9 million general language and vision-language instruction samples. Fine-tuning adds 6.61 million embodied-understanding samples, 5.4 million action samples, 1.2 million future-state samples, and one million general instruction samples. The action data contain approximately 2,620 hours of robot and simulation trajectories from 17 sources, plus 500 hours of selected human motion data.

The model is trained for one epoch in each stage using bfloat16 precision, Adam optimization, a peak learning rate of \(2 \times 10^{-5}\), cosine decay, sequence packing, and a maximum sequence length of 32,768 tokens.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Embodied Understanding

PhysBrain 1.5 is evaluated on 28 benchmarks covering visual-spatial perception, spatial and multi-view reasoning, embodied cognition and planning, grounding and affordance, and visual trajectory reasoning. It achieves an overall average score of **72.5**.

Among the listed open-source models, it ranks first overall, exceeding Hy-Embodied-VLM-1.0 at 66.0 and the 8B Qwen3-VL-Instruct baseline at 59.5. It is reported to rank first on 14 benchmarks and within the top two on 24. The model outperforms the base Qwen model on all 28 embodied benchmarks.

Its strongest individual results include 90.0 on CV-Bench, 86.2 on MindCube, 81.2 on Q-Spatial-Bench, 76.4 on VLABench, 89.6 on RoboRefit, and 89.8 on VABench Visual Trace. The model performs less strongly on some tasks, including MMSI-Bench, ERQA, RefSpatial-Bench, and several point-localization benchmarks, where proprietary or other specialized models achieve higher scores.

The reported overall score is close to the proprietary comparison models GPT-6-Astra at 73.3 and Gemini 3.6 Flash at 73.0, and exceeds Claude Opus 5 at 67.9. However, the paper notes that the authors independently re-evaluated comparison models using standardized metrics and different official inference settings, so cross-model comparisons should be interpreted in light of those protocol choices.

#### 4.2. General Multimodal Capabilities

The model retains performance broadly comparable to Qwen3-VL-Instruct on general multimodal tasks. It improves over the base model on MMStar, RealWorldQA, and POPE, which measures object hallucination, while scoring slightly lower on MME, VideoMME, and MVBench. It also improves on ChartQA and fine-grained visual recognition through V*, but declines modestly on AI2D, DocVQA, TextVQA, and ScreenSpot.

These results indicate that embodied adaptation does not eliminate the model’s general multimodal functionality, although it produces tradeoffs across individual tasks.

#### 4.3. Action and Future-State Prediction

The paper provides qualitative rather than task-level execution results for action generation. Given an image, instruction, and preceding action chunk, the model predicts the next 16-step trajectory. Visualizations show that predicted trajectories generally follow the direction and broad shape of reference trajectories in object placement, tool use, bimanual manipulation, and out-of-distribution RoboDojo examples.

Action-token perplexity falls from 17.12 to 5.07 on in-distribution held-out episodes and from 12.39 to 6.57 on RoboDojo during pre-training. This suggests improved prediction of action tokens, including on a source excluded from training. Nevertheless, the evaluation does not report closed-loop robot success, collision rates, task completion, or physical execution reliability.

For future-state prediction, qualitative examples show spatially coherent RGB, depth, and robot-mask outputs over a one-second horizon. The predictions preserve major scene layout and object identity while depicting plausible robot motion and task-related changes. No numerical image-generation or state-prediction metrics are reported.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The paper’s main significance lies in its proposed common representation for semantic reasoning, action prediction, and physical-state forecasting. By treating these outputs as discrete sequences under one autoregressive objective, PhysBrain 1.5 offers a relatively simple mechanism for sharing supervision across the physical loop.

The use of human interaction videos is also consequential. Human data provide large-scale information about tasks, objects, manipulation sequences, and state changes before robot-specific fine-tuning. If the approach transfers reliably, it could reduce dependence on costly robot demonstrations and support broader task coverage.

The open-source 8B model, evaluation kit, and model release may facilitate reproducibility and comparison in embodied multimodal research. However, the evidence presented remains strongest for offline understanding and qualitative generation. The paper does not establish closed-loop control performance, real-world safety, long-horizon task success, robustness to sensor variation, or reliable transfer across all robot embodiments. Native coordinate conventions and reliance on recovered or pseudo-labeled data also introduce potential sources of error.

Overall, PhysBrain 1.5 represents an architectural and data-integration contribution toward unified physical foundation models. Its benchmark results support strong embodied understanding, while its action and future-state demonstrations indicate capabilities that require further quantitative and real-robot validation.
