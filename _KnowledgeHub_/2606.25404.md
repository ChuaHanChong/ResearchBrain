# HEART: Coordination of Heterogeneous Expert Agents for Physically Grounded Robotic Task Planning

> [!summary] Summary
> A token-aware multi-LLM framework coordinates specialized expert agents to generate robotic task plans that are both logically valid and physically executable, addressing a common challenge in LLM-based robotics. This approach significantly improved plan success rates for various LLM-based planners by 10-70 percentage points across 40 tasks in three household manipulation scenes, while also reducing token usage by 25-30% through agent specialization.

## Problem

- Single-LLM robotic task planning often generates linguistically plausible but physically inexecutable plans, failing to account for specific robot limitations or environmental realities.
- Existing LLM-based methods require extensive physical descriptions in prompts, leading to increased context lengths, higher computational costs, and potential degradation of reasoning quality.
- Multi-agent LLM frameworks for robotics frequently lack explicit coordination mechanisms under communication constraints, resulting in redundant processing, inefficient token usage, and inconsistent reasoning outcomes.

## Method

- The **HEART** framework introduces a ==token-aware multi-LLM system== that coordinates heterogeneous expert agents via a central allocator through four stages: Task Decomposition, Allocation, Expert Reasoning, and Plan Synthesis.
- It employs five role-specialized expert agents (Capability, Environment, Path, Feasibility, Constraint Reasoners) that process atomic subtasks with role-aligned, filtered environmental data to reduce context size and enhance precision.
- A central allocator assigns subtasks to agents using semantic scoring, a history-sensitive penalty for repeated failures, and capacity planning to adhere to a per-round token budget.

## Results

- The 5-agent HEART configuration achieved higher Plan Success Rates (e.g., **72.0**-**76.0%** on Beechwood and Benevolence scenes) and used approximately 25-**30%** fewer total tokens and half the allocation rounds compared to a single-agent setup.
- HEART's full allocation strategy, including the history-sensitive penalty and capacity planning, achieved approximately **99%** Subtask Success Rate with fewer allocation rounds, demonstrating effective resource management and improved task completion reliability.
- HEART significantly increased Plan Success Rates for both LLM-CoT (from **6.7**–**64.0%** to **60.7**–**77.3%**) and DELTA (from **6.7**–**64.0%** to **74.0**–**86.0%**) planners across 40 tasks in three household manipulation scenes by pre-validating task conditions.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Role specialization significantly enhances planning robustness and efficiency, with the 5-agent configuration achieving higher plan success rates and improved token efficiency compared to fewer agents.
> - Systematic coordination under token budgets, via a history-sensitive penalty and capacity planning, is crucial for improving subtask success rates and reducing allocation rounds by preventing repetitive failures and optimizing resource use.
> - Pre-validation of physical conditions through specialized reasoning reduces common planning failure modes, such as precondition violations from hallucinated affordances, enabling downstream planners to generate more physically executable plans.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) into robotic task planning has gained increasing attention due to their ability to process natural language instructions and perform reasoning without requiring explicit symbolic domain models. This flexibility represents an advancement over classical planning methods, which typically depend on predefined operators and may exhibit limitations when faced with ambiguous instructions.

However, the application of LLMs to robotic task planning in physical environments introduces specific requirements: logical validity (consistent action order and preconditions) and physical executability (robot capabilities, reachability, and feasibility). Current single-LLM approaches often generate plans that are linguistically plausible but may fail upon physical execution. These monolithic LLM systems often require extensive physical descriptions within their prompts, leading to increased context lengths, which can raise computational latency, memory usage, inference costs, and potentially degrade reasoning quality due to diluted attention.

Prior efforts have explored various LLM-based planning paradigms:
- **LLMs as grounders:** Approaches like SayCan, PaLM-E, and SayPlan integrate LLMs with affordances, multimodal perception, or scene graphs to link instructions to the physical environment. However, these methods have been noted for limited precision and reasoning depth.
- **LLMs as translators:** Frameworks that convert planning problems into formal representations, such as PDDL or temporal logics, leverage symbolic solvers for improved logical consistency. Nevertheless, the separation of symbolic reasoning from physical and spatial context in the translation step can lead to challenges in addressing spatial coordination, physical feasibility, and temporal constraints, often resulting in execution failures.

More recently, multi-agent LLM frameworks have emerged, aiming to enhance reasoning consistency in planning. Examples include:
- **RoCo:** Assigns agents per robot for coordinated task and motion planning through dialogue.
- **Collaborative problem formulation:** Frameworks that employ multiple LLMs with complementary roles for joint domain model construction and plan refinement.
- **Policy generation and control synthesis:** Systems like MAS for robotic autonomy and Triple-S for long-horizon policy design.
- **Operational constraint capture:** FlowPlan utilizes multi-step logical flows to incorporate operational constraints.

Despite these advances, many existing multi-agent LLM frameworks for robotics lack explicit mechanisms for coordinating reasoning under communication constraints. This absence can lead to redundant processing, inefficient token usage due to overlapping contexts, and inconsistent reasoning outcomes, which compromise plan validity and executability, particularly under limited token budgets.

The challenge of resource allocation is also a longstanding concern in multi-robot and distributed systems, with prior work in task assignment, market-based optimization, and adaptive scheduling. Related concepts appear in developmental robotics, where socially guided intrinsic motivation frameworks address selective reliance on external guidance under interaction budgets. Recent studies in LLM inference further highlight the benefits of careful compute budgeting for efficiency and robustness.

HEART is positioned to address these identified gaps by proposing a systematic allocation strategy that distributes reasoning questions efficiently among specialized agents, maintains consistency within communication limits, and focuses on producing physically grounded and executable plans through heterogeneous reasoning while adhering to resource constraints.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an LLM-based, instruction-driven robotic task planning framework that generates task plans which are both logically valid and physically executable, while maintaining efficiency under computational and memory resource constraints, specifically a per-round token budget.

The motivation for this work stems from two core challenges observed in existing LLM-based robotic planning approaches:

1. **Ensuring Plan Validity and Executability through Heterogeneous Reasoning:** Robotic tasks in physical environments necessitate adherence to both logical and physical constraints. Logical validity requires consistent action ordering and satisfaction of preconditions (e.g., holding an object before placing it). Physical executability requires that actions are within the robot's capabilities (e.g., mechanical design supports grasping), reachable within its workspace, and physically feasible (e.g., object size and weight are manageable). Single, monolithic LLMs often struggle to integrate these diverse reasoning aspects simultaneously. They may generate plans that appear plausible in natural language but fail to account for specific robot limitations or environmental realities. This leads to issues such as:
 - Generating logically invalid sequences (e.g., attempting to place an object without picking it up).
 - Proposing physically inexecutable actions (e.g., attempting to grasp an object larger than the gripper, or an object in an unreachable location).
 - The need to consider capability, environment, path, feasibility, and constraints jointly for a robust plan.

2. **Maintaining Efficiency in Multi-Agent Collaboration under Resource Constraints:** While multi-LLM approaches offer the potential for deeper and more consistent reasoning, their implementation presents challenges related to computational resources. Including extensive physical descriptions in prompts, necessary for comprehensive reasoning, can significantly expand context lengths. This leads to:
 - Increased computational latency and memory usage.
 - Higher inference costs.
 - Degradation of reasoning quality due to attention dilution over long sequences.
 - Naive multi-LLM approaches, lacking proper coordination and task allocation, often suffer from redundant processing of overlapping contexts, leading to excessive token usage and potentially conflicting reasoning outputs. This inefficiency makes such systems difficult to scale, especially when confronted with strict communication or token budgets.

To address these challenges, the authors propose HEART, aiming to:
- Decompose complex instructions into atomic reasoning tasks.
- Allocate these tasks to role-specialized expert agents.
- Coordinate this multi-agent collaboration under a shared token budget.
- Synthesize the outputs from these agents to produce physically executable and logically valid plans.
By focusing on role-oriented reasoning and constraint-driven plan synthesis, the work seeks to validate critical conditions (capability, reachability, constraints) before plan generation, thereby improving the physical executability of robotic task plans while maintaining computational efficiency.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

HEART operates as a token-aware multi-LLM framework that coordinates heterogeneous expert agents through a central allocator. The pipeline comprises four sequential stages: Task Decomposition, Allocation under Token Budget, Heterogeneous Expert Reasoning, and Plan Synthesis.

**A. Task Decomposition**
The initial stage converts a high-level natural language instruction (I) into a set of atomic reasoning subtasks, denoted as Q = {q_i}. Each subtask q_i is defined by:
- `x_i`: The reasoning question.
- `r_i`: The answer to the question.
- `τ_i`: The task type, chosen from 11 predefined types (Table I, e.g., Robot Capability, Object Discovery, Path Planning, Physical Feasibility, Task Dependency). These types are domain-configurable.
- `p_i`: A local priority (1 being highest), reflecting execution dependency and task importance.
- `stat_i`: The status flag (SUCCESS, PARTIAL, OUT OF SCOPE).
An LLM-based decomposer generates these subtasks by analyzing the instruction and environment data (E), including robot specifications (R) and a 3D scene graph (G). The decomposer follows guidelines to create data-aware questions and avoids requesting unavailable information. Subtasks can be updated during the expert reasoning phase.

**B. Allocation under Token Budget**
A central allocator maps the decomposed subtasks from Q to specialized expert agents A while adhering to a per-round token budget `b`. Each agent `a` is characterized by:
- `d_a`: Its role description.
- `F(a, E)`: Role-aligned and filtered environment data.
- `c_a`: An estimated token usage based on prompt size and past query history.

This stage involves two key mechanisms:
1. **Subtask Assignment with History-Sensitive Penalty:** For each subtask `q_i`, the allocator computes a semantic score `s_i(a)` for each agent `a`. This score is derived from the cosine similarity between the embeddings of the subtask's question (`x_i`) and type (`τ_i`) with the agent's role description (`d_a`). A history-sensitive penalty (`Δ_i(a)`) is subtracted from this score; this penalty increases with prior failed attempts by an agent on a specific subtask and with the most recent failed agent, preventing repetitive assignment to ineffective agents. Each subtask has a maximum trial limit (five in experiments) to avoid infinite retries.
2. **Capacity Planning with Token Budget:** Based on the selected agents `φ(q_i)` for unresolved subtasks, the allocator calculates the demand for each agent type. It then plans agent capacities `y_a` such that the total estimated token consumption `Σ y_a c_a` does not exceed the per-round budget `b`. A greedy strategy is employed, prioritizing agents with higher `c_a` and scheduling subtasks by their priority `p_i`. The allocator outputs the set of assignments `M` for the current round, and token counts and failure histories are updated.

**C. Heterogeneous Expert Reasoning**
In this phase, the assigned subtasks are executed in parallel by the specialized expert agents. Each agent receives only its `role-aligned filtered data F(a, E)`, rather than the full environment, to reduce context size, improve efficiency, and enhance reasoning quality. The five instantiated roles are:
- **Capability Reasoner:** Evaluates robot actions (navigation, manipulation) based on URDF, robot state (kinematics, joint limits, gripper type), and predefined action sets.
- **Environment Reasoner:** Focuses on scene and object understanding using a 3D scene graph, identifying relevant objects, states, and spatial context.
- **Path Reasoner:** Plans high-level navigation sequences based on room adjacency, spatial coordinates, and robot pose.
- **Feasibility Reasoner:** Validates the physical feasibility of actions by combining robot kinematic information (arm length, gripper size) with object properties (dimensions, weight) to assess graspability, reachability, and payload limits.
- **Constraint Reasoner:** Extracts logical and temporal constraints from instructions and environment, deriving action ordering relations and resolving ambiguities.

All agents adhere to a standardized prompting schema, including role-specific system prompts, subtask-specific human prompts, and clear decision guidelines for setting the status flag (`stat_i`: SUCCESS, PARTIAL, OUT OF SCOPE). For `PARTIAL` or `OUT OF SCOPE` responses, agents can rephrase the question and retag the task type for follow-up in subsequent rounds. Each agent maintains a local memory of its interactions to ensure consistency.

**D. Plan Synthesis**
The final stage generates the planning context for a task planner. It integrates all resolved Q&A outcomes (`(x_i, r_i, τ_i)`), preserving partial answers and resolving contradictions (e.g., physical checks overriding capability claims). This validated Q&A block is incorporated into the planner's input (natural language or structured text). HEART is designed to be compatible with any LLM-based or hybrid planner, returning an explicit infeasibility report if no plan is possible.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of HEART involved experiments across three household manipulation scenes (Beechwood, Benevolence, Merom), covering 40 task instructions, using metrics such as Rounds, Token count, Subtask Success Rate (SubSR), Plan Success Rate (Plan SR), and Step Ratio.

**A. Agent Role Ablation**
Comparison of HEART with 5, 3, and 1 specialized agents under a fixed 20k token budget:
- **5-agent configuration:** Achieved the highest Plan SR across all scenes (72.0% on Beechwood, 76.0% on Benevolence, 60.0% on Merom). It demonstrated improved efficiency, using approximately 25-30% fewer total tokens and completing tasks in roughly half the number of allocation rounds compared to the 1-agent setting. This suggests that role specialization enhances planning robustness and efficiency.
- **1-agent configuration:** Exhibited the highest Subtask SR (100.0% on Beechwood and Benevolence) but yielded Plan SRs 12 to 20 percentage points lower than the 5-agent setting. This outcome indicates that a single agent, even with complete context, may answer feasibility questions based on high-level affordance information without performing necessary physical checks against robot hardware, leading to invalid plans.
- **3-agent configuration:** Performed intermediately on Subtask SR but produced the lowest Plan SR, suggesting that broader, less specialized roles introduce inconsistencies that cannot be resolved during plan synthesis.

**B. Allocator Ablation**
This experiment compared HEART's allocator against TypeBased (rule-based) and LLM (general-purpose LLM for allocation) baselines, and its own ablations (without history-sensitive penalty, without capacity planning, or without both) across 10k, 20k, and 40k token budgets on the Benevolence scene.
- **HEART (full):** Consistently achieved a Subtask SR of approximately 99% and the fewest allocation rounds at all budget levels, with comparable total token usage to its component ablations.
- **Impact of components:**
 - Removing the **history-sensitive penalty** resulted in a notable drop in Subtask SR (e.g., from 99.5% to 91.2% at 20k budget), indicating its importance in preventing repetitive, failed assignments.
 - Removing **capacity planning** had a smaller impact on SubSR but saved approximately 1.5 rounds at the tightest 10k budget, demonstrating its role in efficient scheduling under constraints.
- **Baselines:** The LLM baseline incurred 15–25% higher total token usage without superior Subtask SR (around 77% at 20k). The TypeBased baseline, while avoiding routing overhead, showed lower Subtask SR (around 88%) due to its inability to adapt to subtask content not matching its type label.
These results suggest that HEART's allocation strategy effectively balances high subtask success with efficiency by avoiding the overhead of LLM-based routing and the rigidity of fixed-type rules.

**C. Task Planning with Different Planners**
HEART was integrated with two different planners: LLM-CoT (an LLM generating action sequences via chain-of-thought) and DELTA (an LLM-to-PDDL translator with a symbolic solver).
- **Plan SR Improvement:** HEART significantly increased Plan SR for both planners across all scenes:
 - LLM-CoT: From 6.7–64.0% (without HEART) to 60.7–77.3% (with HEART).
 - DELTA: From 6.7–64.0% (without HEART) to 74.0–86.0% (with HEART).
- **Step Ratio:** Plans generated with HEART maintained a Step Ratio close to optimal, indicating efficiency in plan length.
- **Failure Analysis:** Without HEART, the dominant failure mode was precondition violation, often due to hallucinated affordances or incorrect object selection. HEART addressed these by pre-validating capability, feasibility, and constraint conditions during its reasoning stage, leading to higher Plan SR gains, particularly on scenes where physical feasibility reasoning was critical (Benevolence, Merom).
- **Token Consumption:** While HEART increased total token consumption (from 10–43k without HEART to 117–181k with HEART), this represented the upfront cost of validating conditions before plan generation. The planner-side token cost increased only modestly (∼1k for LLM-CoT, ∼4–6k for DELTA).
These findings demonstrate that HEART provides a validated reasoning context that enables diverse LLM-based and hybrid planners to generate more valid and executable plans.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The HEART framework introduces a novel approach to robotic task planning by effectively coordinating heterogeneous Large Language Model (LLM) agents under a token budget. This work makes several contributions to the field of robotics and artificial intelligence:

1. **Bridging the Execution Gap:** HEART addresses a critical limitation of current LLM-based robot planners, which often generate logically sound but physically inexecutable plans. By integrating role-specialized agents that explicitly reason about capability, environment, path, physical feasibility, and task constraints, HEART enables the generation of plans that are robustly valid and executable in physical environments. This capability is significant for the safe and reliable deployment of robots in real-world scenarios.

2. **Enhanced Efficiency in Multi-Agent LLM Systems:** The framework's token-aware allocation strategy, which includes semantic matching, a history-sensitive penalty, and capacity planning, offers a method for managing computational resources in multi-LLM collaborations. By routing subtasks to the most relevant agents and distributing token budgets efficiently, HEART mitigates issues of redundant processing, excessive token usage, and inconsistent reasoning, which commonly arise in uncoordinated multi-agent systems. This efficiency is crucial for the scalability and practical application of LLM-driven robotics.

3. **Modularity and Specialization:** The design of HEART, with its five distinct expert roles, demonstrates the benefits of modularity and specialization in complex reasoning tasks. Each agent focuses on a subset of the problem, operating on role-aligned, filtered data, which can improve reasoning precision and reduce overall context size. This specialized approach aligns with observations that modular, specialized models can be more economical and robust than monolithic ones.

4. **Compatibility with Diverse Planning Paradigms:** HEART's ability to provide a validated reasoning context to both LLM-as-planner (LLM-CoT) and LLM-as-translator (DELTA) systems highlights its flexibility and broad applicability. This allows researchers and practitioners to leverage HEART's benefits regardless of their preferred downstream planning architecture, thereby accelerating the development of more capable robot systems.

5. **Improved Robustness and Reduced Failure Modes:** The systematic validation of conditions (e.g., physical feasibility, action preconditions) during HEART's reasoning stage reduces common planning failure modes, such as precondition violations from hallucinated affordances or incorrect object selection. This pre-validation step enhances the overall robustness of the planning process and increases the success rate of generated plans.

The potential impact of HEART extends to various domains requiring intelligent robotic task execution, particularly in complex household or industrial settings where robots must interact with diverse objects and environments. By enabling LLMs to generate more reliable and physically grounded plans, HEART contributes to advancing autonomous robotics towards more practical and deployable solutions. Future work, potentially extending heterogeneity from the prompt level to the model level (e.g., specialized small or fine-tuned LLMs for each agent), could further optimize resource utilization and reasoning capabilities.
