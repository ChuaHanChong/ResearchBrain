# AIC MLLM: Autonomous Interactive Correction MLLM for Robust Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> This research introduces an Autonomous Interactive Correction (AIC) MLLM framework that enables robots to self-correct low-level SE(3) pose predictions for manipulating articulated objects. It employs multimodal feedback to refine actions, achieving an average success rate of 0.75 on unseen test categories and improving real-world manipulation success from 37.5% to 65%.

## Problem

- Robotic systems frequently encounter failures during interaction with complex, varied real-world environments.
- Existing MLLM-based correction methods primarily address high-level task planning, overlooking low-level SE(3) pose prediction errors critical for atomic manipulation.
- Manipulating articulated objects demands high precision due to their complex kinematics, making them particularly susceptible to low-level pose inaccuracies.

## Method

- An **AIC MLLM** framework is proposed that predicts initial ==manipulation poses== and comprehends specific visual and textual feedback prompts for correction.
- Distinct prompt interfaces are designed to address ==positional errors== (highlighting unmovable parts with masks) and ==rotational errors== (providing joint type and axis direction for gripper adjustment).
- A ==Feedback Information Extraction (FIE)== module automatically detects failure causes and generates appropriate prompts, complemented by a ==Test Time Adaptation (TTA)== strategy for iterative model updates during inference.

## Results

- The AIC MLLM achieved an average success rate of **0.75** on unseen test categories in simulation, surpassing baseline methods and demonstrating strong generalization capabilities.
- Real-world experiments using a Franka Emika robot showed the framework improved manipulation success rates from **37.5%** without correction to **65%** with correction on diverse articulated objects.
- Ablation studies confirmed that both the position and rotation correction modules, as well as the Test Time Adaptation (TTA) strategy, were individually critical for the observed performance gains.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - MLLMs can be effectively trained to interpret specific multimodal feedback (visual masks and text descriptions) for precise, low-level SE(3) pose correction.
> - The combination of both positional and rotational error correction mechanisms is essential and complementary for robust manipulation of complex articulated objects.
> - Iterative self-correction, informed by failed interaction experiences, coupled with test-time adaptation, significantly enhances a robot's ability to generalize and adapt to new object instances and environments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of versatile robotic systems capable of stable interaction with real-world objects is a central aim in embodied artificial intelligence. Despite advancements in generalization strategies, robotic systems frequently encounter failures due to environmental complexity and task variability. Consequently, a crucial area of research involves enabling robots to identify and rectify their erroneous actions.

Recent years have seen the integration of Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) into robotics research, primarily due to their capabilities in common-sense generalization and reasoning. Previous works have explored MLLMs and Large Language Models (LLMs) for understanding, analyzing, and correcting robotic failures. Approaches such as REFLECT have focused on summarizing hierarchical sensor information for LLM-based error explanations and corrections at a high-level, typically addressing task planning. Other systems, like "inner monologue" and DROC, have also investigated human-guided or self-reflective corrections. These methods predominantly operate at the high-level task planning stage, for instance, by adjusting a sequence of actions to achieve a goal.

However, a notable gap exists in the application of MLLMs for correcting low-level SE(3) pose predictions, which are critical for atomic manipulation tasks. Even when a robot's high-level plan is correct, failures can still occur if the precise contact pose for interaction with an object is inaccurate. This issue is particularly pronounced when manipulating articulated objects, which require high precision due to their interconnected parts and multiple degrees of freedom. Existing MLLM-based correction mechanisms have demonstrated limited ability to recover from such low-level pose errors automatically.

This research addresses this specific gap by focusing on autonomous low-level SE(3) pose correction for articulated object manipulation. It aims to enhance a robot's ability to understand object geometry and refine its low-level actions by leveraging failed interaction experiences, a mechanism inspired by human learning processes. The work positions itself as an advancement in robotic failure correction by extending MLLM capabilities to the granular level of physical interaction.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable robotic systems to autonomously reflect on and correct failures in low-level SE(3) pose predictions during manipulation, specifically for articulated objects. This aim is motivated by several considerations:

- **Addressing Low-Level Manipulation Failures:** While MLLMs have been utilized for high-level task planning corrections, their application to correcting precise low-level SE(3) poses has been limited. The paper highlights that even if a robot identifies a high-level task failure (e.g., "failed to open microwave"), it may still lack the capability to automatically adjust the specific contact pose to successfully complete the atomic action (e.g., the act of opening). Such failures at the atomic task level prevent the overall completion of complex manipulation sequences.

- **Challenges of Articulated Object Manipulation:** Articulated objects present unique challenges for robotic manipulation due to their complex kinematics, multiple interconnected parts, and various degrees of freedom (e.g., doors, drawers, lids). These characteristics necessitate highly precise interaction poses, making manipulation tasks involving articulated objects particularly prone to errors. The development of robust correction mechanisms for such objects is therefore identified as an urgent need.

- **Leveraging MLLM Capabilities for Geometric Understanding:** MLLMs have demonstrated generalization and reasoning abilities. The authors propose that these capabilities can be harnessed to allow robots to better understand the geometry of objects and, crucially, to learn from past failed attempts. This includes comprehending which parts of an object are movable or unmovable, and how forces applied at specific points and directions influence object articulation.

- **Mimicking Human Learning from Experience:** The motivation draws inspiration from human cognitive processes, where learning from failed scenarios is an inherent capability. The goal is to imbue robotic systems with a similar capacity, allowing them to utilize erroneous interaction experiences to refine their understanding and improve subsequent actions.

To achieve these objectives, the research proposes the Autonomous Interactive Correction (AIC) MLLM, with several specific goals:

- **Develop a unified MLLM framework:** The MLLM should be capable of both predicting initial manipulation poses and understanding feedback instructions for correction.
- **Design effective feedback prompts:** Create distinct visual and textual prompt types to guide the MLLM in rectifying positional errors (e.g., by highlighting unmovable parts) and rotational errors (e.g., by indicating joint type and axis direction).
- **Implement an adaptive feedback extraction mechanism:** Introduce a Feedback Information Extraction (FIE) module to automatically detect the cause of a manipulation failure and generate the appropriate prompts for the AIC MLLM.
- **Enhance long-term stability and adaptation:** Devise a Test Time Adaptation (TTA) strategy to enable the MLLM to iteratively update itself based on current scene configurations and recent interaction experiences, thereby improving manipulation stability over time.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology introduces the Autonomous Interactive Correction (AIC) MLLM, a framework designed to enable robots to correct low-level SE(3) pose predictions for articulated object manipulation based on past interaction failures. The approach encompasses both a training phase to instill correction capabilities and a testing phase that integrates feedback extraction and adaptive model updates.

#### 3.1. Task Formulation

The manipulation task is defined where a policy model, $\pi$, predicts a manipulation contact pose ($a_{pos}$, $a_{rot}$) given a scene image $I$ and a task instruction $L$. This pose includes both position and rotation components. After contact, the end-effector's axis direction guides the pose trajectory. The model is permitted $N$ opportunities for correction, and during testing, it can update itself using only the current sample.

#### 3.2. Framework Overview

The AIC MLLM operates in two main phases:

- **Training Phase (Section 3.3):** The MLLM is fine-tuned to predict manipulation poses and to comprehend specific feedback prompts, including visual masks and textual descriptions of object parts and axis information. This training enables the model to correct pose predictions based on these prompts.
- **Testing Phase (Section 3.4):** During inference, the model initially predicts a pose. If the interaction fails, a Feedback Information Extraction (FIE) module identifies the error cause. This information is converted into visual and linguistic prompts, which are fed back into the trained MLLM. The MLLM then reflects on the failure and predicts a revised SE(3) pose. To enhance stability and adapt to ongoing configurations, a Test Time Adaptation (TTA) strategy is also employed, allowing the model to update its parameters iteratively during inference.

#### 3.3. Training Phase Details

The training process is designed to equip the MLLM with both manipulation execution and failure correction abilities. The core model architecture is based on LLaMA-Adapter, utilizing a pre-trained CLIP visual encoder and LLaMA-7B as the language model. LoRa adaptation prompts are used, and only these adapters are fine-tuned to preserve general reasoning capabilities while acquiring robotics-specific correction skills.

Two primary prompt interfaces are constructed to convey error information:

- **Positional Error Interface (Section 3.3.1):**
 - **Motivation:** Prevent the robot from attempting to manipulate unmovable parts of an object.
 - **Methodology:** Unmovable parts are visually highlighted with a red mask. The model is trained through a series of Visual Question Answering (VQA) tasks to understand these masks:
 1. **Mask Image Classification:** The model determines if a red mask is present in an image (supervised by cross-entropy loss $L_H$).
 2. **Mask Position Reasoning:** Given $N$ randomly selected pixel points, the model identifies whether each point is on the red mask (supervised by cross-entropy loss $L_P$).
 3. **Correct Based On Mask:** The model learns to predict a new manipulation pose that avoids the masked (unmovable) areas. Direction vectors are discretized into 100 bins (supervised by cross-entropy loss $L_C$).

- **Rotational Error Interface (Section 3.3.2):**
 - **Motivation:** Adjust gripper direction based on the articulated joint's properties (type and axis direction).
 - **Methodology:** Even in failed interactions, objects may exhibit some movement. This movement is used to infer the joint type (prismatic or revolute) and its axis direction.
 - **VQA Task:** The joint type, joint axis direction, current contact point, and normal direction are encoded into a text prompt. The model is then required to predict a new gripper direction (supervised by cross-entropy loss $L_R$).
 - **Robustness Enhancement:** To simulate real-world noise, random rotations of -20° to 20° are injected into the ground truth axis directions during training.
- **Joint Training Objective:** The four tasks are jointly trained under the objective $L = L_H + L_P + L_C + L_R$.

#### 3.4. Testing Phase Details

- **Initial Interaction:** The model predicts a 2D contact point and rotation. The 2D point is converted to 3D using a depth map for initial execution.
- **Feedback Information Extraction (FIE) (Section 3.4.1):**
 - **Position-related Information:** If an interaction fails, the system determines if the contact point was on an unmovable part. VIP-LLaVA is used to query the movability of the predicted contact point (marked by a red dot). If identified as unmovable, SAM segments the smallest mask corresponding to the unmovable part. Multiple failed interactions accumulate multiple red masks.
 - **Rotation-related Information:** The end-effector's trajectory during interaction (20 frames) is analyzed. Joint type classification is performed: a straight trajectory indicates a prismatic joint, while an arc-shaped trajectory suggests a revolute joint. The axis direction is determined by the movement direction for prismatic joints, or by the cross product of trajectory vectors for revolute joints.
- **Model Inference (Reflection & Correction) (Section 3.4.2):**
 - **Failure Cause Identification:** If movement occurs during interaction, the contact point is considered valid, and rotation correction is prioritized. If no movement occurs, even with a normal direction attempt, position correction is deemed necessary.
 - **Position Correction:** A chain-of-thought prompting strategy is used. The model answers questions about the mask presence and location, reflects on why previous attempts failed (e.g., if the point was on an unmovable part), and then predicts a new SE(3) pose. Finally, it evaluates the potential success of the new pose.
 - **Rotation Correction:** If rotation correction is required, the FIE-extracted joint type, joint axis direction, contact point, and normal direction are fed as text prompts. The model then outputs a new gripper direction, keeping the contact point fixed.
- **Test Time Adaptation (TTA) (Section 3.4.3):**
 - **Mechanism:** After each sample inference, the model parameters are updated using the current sample's data. This aims to adapt the model ($\pi_{t-1}$ to $\pi_t$) to the ongoing configuration for subsequent samples.
 - **Update Data:** Ground truth for mask detection (presence and location) and successful corrected poses are used for updating.
 - **Loss Function:** Cross-entropy loss is used. A decaying learning rate prevents catastrophic forgetting of pre-trained knowledge.

#### 3.5. Experimental Setup

Experiments were conducted in a SAPIEN simulated environment using the PartNet-Mobility dataset. A Franka Panda robot with a suction gripper was used. The dataset comprised approximately 12K successful manipulation samples from 20 categories for training ($D_o$), augmented with training information ($D_{aug}$). Testing involved 1K samples from 30 categories (including unseen ones). The evaluation focused on pulling action primitives.

#### 3.6. Evaluation Metrics

Manipulation success rate, defined as the ratio of successful samples to total test samples, was the primary metric. Success for pulling required a movement difference of more than 0.01 units in object pose (or 0.5 relative to total motion range) and a dot product between predicted and actual gripper direction greater than 0.3.

#### 3.7. Baselines and Ablations

Comparisons were made against:
- **UMPNet & FlowBot3D:** Expert suction manipulation models.
- **ManipLLM:** A state-of-the-art MLLM for SE(3) pose prediction.
- **Base model:** The authors' MLLM trained solely on $D_o$ without correction data.

Ablation studies included:
- **Ours-w/o pretrain:** Base model without TTA.
- **Ours-w/o pos:** No position correction, no TTA.
- **Ours-w/o rot:** No rotation correction, no TTA.
- **Ours-w/o tta:** Full AIC MLLM without the TTA module.
- **Ours:** The complete AIC MLLM framework. All ablation studies involving correction modules were allowed four correction attempts.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments, conducted in both simulated and real-world environments, evaluated the effectiveness of the proposed AIC MLLM framework.

- **Improved Performance Through Self-Correction:** The AIC MLLM demonstrated an improvement in manipulation success rates compared to the base model across both training and testing categories (Table 1). As the number of correction attempts increased, the success rate progressively improved, indicating that the model can effectively learn from failed interaction experiences and iteratively refine its actions. This suggests a capacity for continuous information gathering and reflection.

- **Enhanced Generalizability:** The AIC MLLM achieved an average success rate of 0.75 on unseen test categories, surpassing the best baseline method. This result indicates the framework's strong generalization capabilities, implying that the extracted feedback information (e.g., joint properties, unmovable parts) captures universal characteristics of articulated objects rather than being specific to seen categories.

- **Importance of Prompt Understanding:** A comparison between "Ours-w/o pretrain" and "Ours-w/o tta" revealed that fine-tuning the MLLM to understand the custom visual and textual prompts for correction is critical. This training led to substantial performance gains, increasing success rates from 56% to 80% on train categories and from 44% to 76% on test categories. This finding underscores the role of instruction comprehension in the MLLM's ability to utilize feedback effectively.

- **Complementary Nature of Position and Rotation Correction:** Ablation studies (comparing "Ours-w/o pos," "Ours-w/o rot," and "Ours-w/o tta") indicated that both position and rotation correction modules are essential for optimal performance. Removing either type of correction resulted in a performance drop, demonstrating their combined and complementary contribution to achieving successful manipulation.

- **Effectiveness of Test Time Adaptation (TTA):** The inclusion of the TTA module ("Ours" vs. "Ours-w/o tta") generally led to higher or sustained success rates across most unseen test categories and seen train categories. This suggests that TTA enables the model to adapt to new and current scene configurations, enhancing its generalization to a broader range of object instances and environmental variations. While slight performance decrements were noted in a few categories, attributed potentially to small sample sizes, the overall minimal difference in average success rates confirmed TTA's efficacy in mitigating catastrophic forgetting.

- **Real-world Validation:** Real-world experiments using a Franka Emika robotic arm on 8 diverse articulated objects (5 revolute, 3 prismatic) corroborated the simulation findings. The framework's average success rate improved from 37.5% without correction to 65% with correction (Table 3). This demonstrated the method's transferability from simulation to physical robotics and its robustness in real-world scenarios.

- **Feedback Information Extraction (FIE) Robustness:** An additional ablation study comparing the VIP-LLaVA module for mobility detection against ground truth information showed similar performance in simulation. This suggests that the FIE module is robust in identifying unmovable parts and that the overall framework is not overly sensitive to minor inaccuracies from the foundation models used for extraction.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Autonomous Interactive Correction (AIC) MLLM framework represents an advancement in the field of robotic manipulation, particularly concerning robustness and error recovery. The significance and potential impact of this work are multi-faceted:

- **Enhanced Robotic Robustness and Autonomy:** The framework directly addresses the challenge of inevitable failures in real-world robotic interactions. By enabling robots to autonomously reflect on and correct low-level SE(3) pose predictions, it moves beyond high-level planning adjustments to the granular level of physical contact. This capability contributes to more robust and autonomous robotic systems that can operate reliably in complex and dynamic environments.

- **Improved Manipulation of Articulated Objects:** The focus on articulated objects, which are inherently challenging due to their kinematic complexity, is a notable contribution. The method's demonstrated effectiveness in correcting manipulation poses for such objects could lead to advancements in areas requiring fine-grained interaction, such as assembly, repair, and household robotics.

- **Deepening MLLM Integration in Robotics:** This research showcases a practical application of MLLMs for precise, low-level control, extending their utility beyond high-level task planning and semantic understanding. By leveraging MLLMs' reasoning capabilities for geometric inference and corrective action generation based on multimodal feedback, the work contributes to a more integrated and effective use of foundation models in embodied AI.

- **Towards Human-like Learning from Experience:** The interactive correction paradigm, where the robot learns iteratively from its failed attempts, mirrors human-like learning processes. This approach could lead to more adaptive and flexible robotic agents that can continuously improve their performance through experience, rather than relying solely on pre-programmed or extensively pre-trained policies.

- **Practical Applicability and Generalization:** The validation in both simulated and real-world environments, coupled with strong generalization to unseen object categories, indicates the framework's practical applicability. This suggests that the AIC MLLM could be deployed in various real-world scenarios, reducing the need for extensive retraining for every new object or slight environmental variation.

- **Foundation for Future Research:** This work establishes a foundation for several future research directions. These include integrating low-level pose corrections with high-level planning corrections for comprehensive error recovery, exploring the use of temporal data for closed-loop control, and fine-tuning specialized foundation models for object-centric manipulation properties to further enhance performance.

In conclusion, the AIC MLLM offers a pathway for robotic systems to achieve a higher degree of self-sufficiency and reliability in complex manipulation tasks by autonomously correcting low-level interaction failures.
