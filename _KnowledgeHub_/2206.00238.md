# Transferable Reward Learning by Dynamics-Agnostic Discriminator Ensemble

> [!summary] Summary
> A method called Dynamics-Agnostic Discriminator-Ensemble Reward Learning (DARL) learns state-action reward functions from expert demonstrations that remain robust and transferable across environments with varying dynamics. By employing mutual information minimization and a discriminator ensemble, DARL effectively mitigates dynamics and policy dependency in adversarial imitation learning, demonstrating superior performance and high reward consistency across diverse MuJoCo transfer tasks.

## Problem

- Reward functions recovered by existing adversarial imitation learning (AIL) methods are intrinsically coupled with specific training dynamics, hindering their transferability to environments with altered transition rules.
- AIL discriminators suffer from "policy dependency," where they catastrophically forget how to accurately evaluate early, suboptimal policies as they converge, leading to unreliable reward signals for training new policies from scratch.
- Current approaches to learning transferable rewards often rely on restrictive assumptions, such as requiring rewards to be state-only or needing partial knowledge of the reward function, limiting their real-world applicability.

## Method

- To achieve ==dynamics-agnostic reward learning==, **DARL** modifies the discriminator to operate on latent state-action embeddings, ==minimizing mutual information== between these embeddings and the next state.
- A ==discriminator ensemble==, constructed by saving historical discriminators during training, addresses ==policy dependency== by using the mean output of the ensemble as the reward signal, providing robust evaluation across policies at various learning stages.
- The ensemble incorporates ==normalization and clipping== for individual discriminator outputs to stabilize training and prevent the reinforcement learning agent from over-exploiting potentially unreliable early discriminator signals.

## Results

- DARL achieved at least **92%** reward consistency across all tested MuJoCo gravity-transfer tasks, significantly outperforming all baseline methods by providing more accurate directional feedback to agents.
- DARL policies demonstrated superior performance, achieving the highest normalized returns in **15 out of 16** transfer tasks across various MuJoCo environments (e.g., Ant, HalfCheetah) with altered dynamics.
- In action-dependent 'EnergyLim' environments, DARL was the only method to achieve positive returns in **3 out of 4** tasks, showcasing its effectiveness in inferring and utilizing action-dependent reward information.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly decoupling the learned reward from environment dynamics through mutual information minimization between state-action representations and future states is crucial for transferable reward functions.
> - Employing an ensemble of historical discriminators effectively mitigates policy dependency, allowing the learned reward to accurately guide policy learning from diverse starting points without catastrophic forgetting.
> - Transferable state-action dependent reward functions can be effectively learned, challenging the assumption that only state-only rewards are transferable, especially in tasks with significant action dependencies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a fundamental challenge within reinforcement learning (RL) and inverse reinforcement learning (IRL): the recovery of reward functions from expert demonstrations that are robust and transferable across varying environmental dynamics. Reward functions are central to Markov Decision Processes (MDPs), guiding optimal behavior. IRL, which infers these reward functions, has diverse applications, including understanding decision-makers' motivations and enabling counterfactual reasoning. However, existing IRL methods often yield reward functions that lack generalizability across different contexts.

Traditional IRL methods, including those based on adversarial imitation learning (AIL) like Generative Adversarial Imitation Learning (GAIL), are known to learn reward functions that are intrinsically coupled with the specific training dynamics. This coupling limits their transferability to environments where the transition dynamics are altered. Previous attempts to learn transferable rewards often rely on restrictive assumptions, such as requiring partial knowledge of the reward function (e.g., Deep PQR), assuming the reward function is exclusively state-dependent (e.g., Adversarial IRL (AIRL) state-only variant, f-IRL), or necessitating the ability to freely adjust environment dynamics (e.g., Amin and Singh). These assumptions constrain the applicability of such methods.

This paper positions itself within the AIL framework, which has gained attention for its efficiency in learning policies from demonstrations. However, it acknowledges a critical limitation of AIL: the discriminator-derived reward signal is typically non-stationary and non-transferable, unsuitable for training new policies from scratch, particularly in altered environments.

The paper also differentiates its focus from "transfer imitation learning." While transfer imitation learning addresses varying learning conditions, its primary goal is often policy adaptation across different state, action, or dynamics spaces. In contrast, this work concentrates on recovering a fundamental, transferable *reward function* itself, which can then facilitate policy learning, provide insights into expert behavior, and support counterfactual reasoning. The use of mutual information minimization as a technique connects this work to a broader range of applications in unsupervised learning, skill discovery, and exploration within RL.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a method capable of learning transferable reward functions from expert demonstrations, specifically targeting *state-action* dependent rewards. The motivation stems from two critical limitations identified in current adversarial imitation learning (AIL) methods, which hinder the transferability and robustness of learned reward functions: dynamics dependency and policy dependency.

**Dynamics Dependency:**
The core motivation to address dynamics dependency arises from the concept of reward shaping. As established by Ng et al. (1999), an optimal policy with respect to a reward function `r(s_t, a_t, s_{t+1})` remains optimal for a reshaped reward `r̃(s_t, a_t, s_{t+1}) = r(s_t, a_t, s_{t+1}) + γΦ(s_{t+1}) - Φ(s_t)`. Here, `Φ(s)` is an arbitrary state-dependent potential function. This implies that multiple reward functions can rationalize the same expert behavior, making the problem of identifying the "true" reward ill-posed.

In deterministic environments, this shaping can be applied to state-action rewards as `r̃(s_t, a_t) = r(s_t, a_t) + γΦ(T(s_t, a_t)) - Φ(s_t)`, where `T(s_t, a_t)` is the transition function. While this shaping preserves policy optimality under the *original* transition dynamics `T`, it breaks down if the environment dynamics change. If the new environment has different dynamics `T'`, the shaped reward `r̃` might no longer guide the agent to an optimal policy, because `s_{t+1}` from `(s_t, a_t)` under `T'` may not align with `T(s_t, a_t)`. AIL discriminators, without explicit dynamics information, may implicitly learn such shaped rewards by internally forecasting the next state in a latent space. The motivation is to prevent this implicit coupling of the reward function with the training dynamics, thereby ensuring its generalizability to environments with varied transition rules.

**Policy Dependency:**
The motivation to address policy dependency arises from the iterative, adversarial nature of AIL training. In AIL, a discriminator `D` is continually trained to distinguish trajectories generated by the *current* policy `π_t` from expert demonstrations. Concurrently, the policy `π_t` is updated to maximize rewards provided by `D`, aiming to fool it. This dynamic creates a "continual learning" challenge for the discriminator. As `D` adapts to discriminate against `π_t`, it can "catastrophically forget" how to differentiate *earlier* policies `π_1..., π_{t-1}` from the expert.

This means that a discriminator at convergence becomes highly specialized to distinguish policies very close to the expert. If this "final" discriminator is then used to train a *new* policy from scratch (which might start with random, low-performance behavior), it may provide incorrect or uninformative reward signals. It might fail to accurately differentiate the nascent, suboptimal policies from expert behavior, thereby misdirecting the learning process and hindering the achievement of optimal performance. The objective is to ensure the reward signal can accurately evaluate a broader spectrum of policies, from early-stage, low-performing ones to expert-level policies, to facilitate robust policy training.

By addressing these two forms of dependency, the authors aim to produce a state-action reward function that is robust to dynamics transfer and reliable for training new policies from diverse starting points, thereby enhancing the utility of learned rewards in real-world applications.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, Dynamics-Agnostic Discriminator-Ensemble Reward Learning (DARL), extends the Generative Adversarial Imitation Learning (GAIL) framework by introducing specific modifications to the discriminator's learning paradigm and the reward function's formulation.

#### 3.1. Dynamics-Agnostic Discriminator Learning

To decouple the reward function from environment dynamics, DARL modifies the discriminator's input domain. Instead of directly processing state-action pairs `(s, a)`, the discriminator `D` operates on a latent embedding `z`. This `z` is generated by a state encoder `E_s(s)` and an action encoder `E_a(a)`, which map `s` and `a` to their respective embeddings `z_s` and `z_a`, concatenated as `z = [z_s, z_a]`. The choice of separate encoders for state and action is deliberate, aimed at preventing the inadvertent incorporation of dynamics-related information into the embeddings.

The core mechanism for dynamics-agnostic learning is to minimize the mutual information (MI) between the latent embedding `z` and the next state `s'`. By minimizing `I(z; s')`, the encoders are compelled to produce embeddings that contain minimal information predictive of the next state, thereby preventing the discriminator from implicitly learning a shaped reward function that relies on next-state prediction.

Direct minimization of `I(z; s')` is intractable due to unknown posterior `p(s'|z)`. DARL utilizes the variational contrastive log-ratio upper bound (vCLUB) to minimize `I(z; s')`. This involves introducing a transition network `q(s'|z; θ)` parameterized by `θ`, which serves as a variational approximation of `p(s'|z)`. The objective is formulated such that the encoder `E` (with parameters `ϕ`) and the transition network `q` are trained adversarially:
- The encoder `E` is optimized to minimize the vCLUB, which means making `s'` difficult to predict from `z`. This objective has two components: decreasing the likelihood `log q(s'|z)` for the true `s'` (to prevent accurate inference) and increasing the likelihood `log q(s'|z)` for *shuffled* `s'` (to ensure `q` infers incorrect `s'` from `z`). The shuffled `s'` approximation is used for computational efficiency.
- The transition network `q` is optimized to maximize the likelihood `log q(s'|z)`, meaning it tries to accurately predict the next state `s'` from the embedding `z`. This ensures that the upper bound in vCLUB holds.

The overall discriminator loss function incorporates the adversarial training of the discriminator `D`, the encoders `E_s` and `E_a`, and a regularization term `ηL_E(B_π ∪ B_E, ϕ)` based on the encoder loss `L_E`. `L_E` is designed to minimize `I(z; s')` over a combined dataset of policy-generated and expert data. This combined objective ensures `z` is informative for reward prediction but minimally informative for dynamics prediction.

#### 3.2. Discriminator Ensemble

To address the policy dependency issue (where a single discriminator forgets earlier policies), DARL employs an ensemble of historical discriminators.
- **Theoretical Foundation:** The paper presents Online Gradient Descent-GAIL (OGD-GAIL) and a theoretical result (Theorem 2). This theorem demonstrates that if the discriminators are updated via online gradient descent with appropriate step sizes, the *average* discriminator over `T` iterations converges to an optimal discriminator that minimizes the loss across *all historical policies*. This effectively mitigates catastrophic forgetting and ensures the average discriminator can differentiate a wide range of policy performances.
- **Ensemble Construction:** During training, discriminators are periodically saved into a buffer `C` at fixed intervals (`H` iterations).
- **Reward Derivation:** The final reward function `r_origin_E(s, a)` is derived from the *mean output* of all discriminators in `C`. This collective decision-making capability allows the ensemble to provide more robust and accurate reward signals across policies at various stages of learning.
- **Implementation Modifications for Robustness:**
 - **Normalization (Eq. 15, 16):** Discriminators from early training stages tend to output values around 0.5 (difficulty distinguishing), while converged discriminators output values near 0 or 1. This disparity can lead to later-stage discriminators dominating the ensemble reward. A min-max normalization (`D_norm_i`) is applied to each discriminator's output, aligning their output ranges (`[D_min, D_max]`) and ensuring balanced contributions.
 - **Clipping (Eq. 17):** To prevent the reinforcement learning agent from over-exploiting individual discriminators (which might still be unreliable in isolation due to residual policy dependency), the normalized discriminator outputs are clipped at a threshold `c` (`D_norm_clip_i = min(D_norm_i, c)`). This encourages the agent to optimize based on the collective signal rather than overfitting to specific discriminators.
- **Final Reward Function:** The ensemble reward `r_E(s, a)` incorporates these normalization and clipping steps.

#### 3.3. The DARL Algorithm

Algorithm 2 summarizes the DARL training process. It iteratively updates the policy (using PPO with rewards from the current discriminator), updates the discriminator (incorporating the MI minimization loss `L_E`), and updates the transition network `q`. Importantly, at regular intervals `H`, the current parameters of the encoders and discriminator are saved to the ensemble buffer `C`. ### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical studies were conducted across five MuJoCo environments (Hopper, HalfCheetah, Walker2d, Ant, Humanoid) with various dynamics transfers (gravity, dof damping, combined perturbations, limb impairment in DisableAnt) and energy-limited scenarios. Comparisons were made against multiple GAIL variants, deep IRL methods, a directly transferred policy, and an oracle policy.

1. **Policy Dependency Confirmation:**
 - Experiments on HalfCheetah confirmed the existence of policy dependency in standard GAIL. Discriminators learned at convergence showed decreased classification precision for low-return data (representing early, suboptimal policies) compared to high-return data. Consequently, these discriminators incorrectly assigned higher rewards to low-return data, indicating a failure to accurately evaluate diverse policies (Fig. 2).

2. **Alleviating Policy Dependency:**
 - DARL and GAIL-E (a variant using only discriminator ensemble) effectively mitigated the initial drops in derived return for low-return data, showing a more consistent positive correlation with true environment returns (Fig. 3a). DARL exhibited the highest correlation.
 - Analysis of individual discriminators within the ensemble revealed a "relay-like" operation: early discriminators provided accurate rewards for early-stage policies, gradually saturating as later discriminators became more compatible with improving policies. This collective behavior guided policy learning more effectively than a single, final discriminator (Fig. 4).

3. **Alleviating Dynamics Dependency:**
 - When true environment returns were held constant across varying gravity dynamics, the derived return from DARL's reward model remained stable. In contrast, a DARL variant without the MI minimization loss exhibited derived returns that varied with gravity changes, demonstrating dynamics dependency. This confirmed that MI minimization contributes to dynamics-agnostic reward learning (Fig. 5, Fig. 14 in Appendix).

4. **Reward Consistency:**
 - DARL achieved significantly higher reward consistency (at least 92% across all tested MuJoCo gravity-transfer tasks, Table 1). This metric measures the alignment between the learned reward function's guidance and the true reward function's guidance for policy updates. DARL consistently outperformed all baseline methods, indicating its ability to provide more accurate directional feedback to agents in transfer scenarios.

5. **Performance in Various Transfer Tasks:**
 - DARL policies achieved the highest normalized returns (excluding the oracle) in 15 out of 16 transfer tasks across Ant, HalfCheetah, Hopper, and Walker2d environments (Fig. 6). This superior performance, particularly in dynamics transfer tasks, suggests that DARL learns robust and transferable reward functions.
 - DARL frequently surpassed the "transferred policy" baseline, indicating that the learned reward enabled effective policy *adaptation* to new dynamics, rather than merely imitating the original expert's behavior. This highlights the non-trivial nature and value of the learned reward.

6. **Performance in More Difficult Tasks:**
 - In `DisableAnt` (limb impairment), DARL, despite learning a state-action reward function, achieved superior transfer performance compared to methods that learn state-only rewards (f-IRL, MCE-IRL, AIRL-SO). This challenges the assumption that transferable rewards must be state-only, demonstrating DARL's capacity for transferable state-action rewards (Fig. 8).
 - In `Humanoid-v2` (high-dimensional environment), DARL achieved competitive or superior performance against baselines in all four transfer scenarios. While consistently showing high reward consistency (Table 2), its final policy performance did not always exhibit the same dramatic advantage as in other environments (Fig. 7). This suggests potential limitations of underlying RL algorithms (PPO) in robustly training with complex, potentially noisy, recovered rewards in very high-dimensional spaces.

7. **Performance in Action-Dependent Tasks:**
 - In `EnergyLim` environments, where reward functions were heavily action-dependent (increased action cost), DARL was the only method to achieve positive returns in 3 out of 4 tasks (EnergyLimAnt, EnergyLimHalfCheetah, EnergyLimWalker2d). Other methods, especially state-only approaches, failed to correlate actions with rewards, leading to negative returns. This indicates DARL's effectiveness in inferring and utilizing action-dependent reward information (Fig. 9).

8. **Ablation Studies:**
 - **Regularization factor η:** An appropriately chosen `η` was crucial. Small `η` hurt robustness to dynamics transfer, while excessively large `η` eliminated essential reward information, causing performance drops (Fig. 10a).
 - **Discriminator backup interval H:** Smaller `H` (resulting in larger ensemble sizes) consistently led to higher returns, especially in transfer tasks, affirming the benefits of a larger and more diverse discriminator ensemble (Fig. 10b). Memory and time costs of the ensemble were found to be tolerable (Fig. 15 in Appendix).
 - **Reward normalization and clipping:** These techniques significantly improved training stability and asymptotic performance, particularly in transfer scenarios (Fig. 11).
 - **Clipping ratio c:** An optimal value of `c` (around 0.5) was found to balance preventing over-exploitation of individual discriminators without excessively suppressing reward signals (Fig. 12).

9. **Embedding Visualization:**
 - t-SNE visualization of the learned embeddings `z` showed a strong correlation between the embedding space and the true environment reward when the MI loss was applied. Without the MI loss, this correlation was absent, confirming that MI minimization successfully encouraged the encoders to produce reward-relevant but dynamics-agnostic representations (Fig. 13).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant advancement in the field of inverse reinforcement learning (IRL) and adversarial imitation learning (AIL) by effectively addressing two major limitations: dynamics dependency and policy dependency of learned reward functions.

The development of DARL, which learns transferable *state-action* reward functions without restrictive assumptions (such as state-only rewards), expands the practical applicability of IRL. Many real-world tasks inherently involve rewards that depend on both states and actions. By demonstrating superior performance in action-dependent, energy-limited tasks, DARL indicates a greater fidelity to complex reward structures than previous approaches.

The core contributions—mutual information minimization for dynamics agnosticism and a discriminator ensemble for policy independence—enhance the robustness and accuracy of recovered reward functions. The empirical results, particularly the high reward consistency and superior policy performance in diverse dynamics transfer scenarios, suggest that DARL provides a more reliable and generalizable reward signal. This has several potential impacts:

- **Improved Policy Learning in Novel Environments:** Agents trained with DARL's reward functions can better adapt to environments with altered dynamics, leading to higher performance in transfer tasks. This is crucial for applications where training environments may differ from deployment environments, such as robotics operating in varied physical conditions.
- **Enhanced Understanding of Expert Behavior:** By recovering a reward function that more closely aligns with the "true" underlying motivation of an expert, even when environment rules change, DARL can offer deeper insights into decision-making processes. This can aid in fields like human-computer interaction, medical decision support, and financial modeling.
- **Wider Applicability of Imitation Learning:** The ability to learn transferable state-action rewards reduces the need for extensive manual reward engineering and can enable imitation learning in a broader range of complex, dynamics-changing scenarios where state-only assumptions are insufficient.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The identified limitations of DARL in very high-dimensional state spaces and the potential for integrating with other debiasing techniques or more memory-efficient no-regret learning methods provide clear avenues for subsequent research. For instance, addressing the challenges of `p(s'|z)` approximation in image-based state spaces could unlock applications in visual imitation learning.

In summary, DARL contributes a robust methodological framework for learning transferable reward functions, pushing the boundaries of what is achievable with IRL in complex and dynamic environments.
