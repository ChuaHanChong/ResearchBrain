# Compute-Optimal Scaling for Value-Based Deep RL

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at UC Berkeley and Carnegie Mellon University established compute-optimal scaling guidelines for online, value-based deep reinforcement learning, identifying a novel phenomenon called TD-overfitting and providing empirical power laws for allocating resources across model capacity, batch size, and update-to-data ratios.

## Problem

- A lack of comprehensive understanding of compute-optimal scaling laws in value-based deep RL, unlike in NLP and CV where such laws guide resource allocation.
- Difficulty in systematically optimizing resource allocation (model capacity, update-to-data ratio) to maximize sample efficiency given a fixed compute budget in deep RL.
- The unique challenges of TD-learning, such as non-stationary targets and bootstrapping, mean existing supervised learning scaling principles cannot be directly applied.

## Method

- Conducted rigorous empirical studies using ==SAC-based== algorithms (BRO and SimbaV2) across various continuous control environments.
- Systematically varied network width (model capacity), batch size, and update-to-data (UTD) ratio to derive empirical power-law relationships.
- Introduced a novel diagnostic, the "passive critic" experiment, and measured validation TD-error to identify and understand TD-overfitting.

## Results

- Maximal admissible batch size increases with model size and decreases with UTD ratio, allowing larger models to leverage significantly larger batch sizes without TD-overfitting.
- Data efficiency to reach a target performance follows inverse power-law relationships with both UTD ratio and model size, indicating that scaling both contributes to improved sample efficiency.
- Derived empirical power laws show how to optimally partition a total budget between compute and data, and between UTD ratio and model size, for maximizing performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Discovered "TD-overfitting," where reducing training TD-error with large batch sizes harms generalization in small-capacity models due to poor quality TD-targets.
> - The quality of TD-targets, rather than just the network's capacity to memorize, is the primary driver of TD-overfitting, as validated by passive critic experiments.
> - Optimal batch size, UTD ratio, and model size scale predictably as power laws of the total compute budget, allowing for compute-optimal resource allocation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper positions itself within the burgeoning field of "scaling laws" in machine learning, a paradigm that has dramatically transformed areas like natural language processing (NLP) and computer vision (CV). Pioneering works such as Kaplan et al. (2020) demonstrated that performance in these domains often scales predictably with increased model size, data, and compute. This understanding has guided the development of increasingly powerful models like large language models (LLMs).

However, the authors point out a significant gap: reinforcement learning, particularly value-based deep RL, has received comparatively less attention regarding compute-optimal scaling. While scaling in other ML fields has focused on maximizing performance per unit of compute, RL research has largely concentrated on achieving performance with a given set of hyperparameters, rather than systematically optimizing resource allocation. Existing work in RL scaling has explored aspects like the update-to-data (UTD) ratio (Rybkin et al., 2025) or stabilized training for larger models (Nauman et al., 2024b; Lee et al., 2025), but a comprehensive understanding of how model capacity, UTD, and other key hyperparameters interact to dictate compute efficiency has been lacking.

This paper directly addresses this void by extending the scaling law paradigm to value-based deep RL. It aims to develop a principled approach to resource allocation, allowing practitioners to make informed decisions about how to best spend their computational budget. The unique challenges of TD-learning, such as non-stationary targets and bootstrapping, fundamentally differentiate its scaling properties from supervised learning. By investigating these unique dynamics, the paper seeks to provide foundational insights that can enable deep RL to leverage increased compute in a manner similar to other successful modern machine learning approaches, thereby closing a critical gap in the field.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to establish guidelines for **compute-optimal scaling in online, value-based deep reinforcement learning**. Given a fixed compute budget, the authors aim to determine the best strategy for partitioning resources across two main axes: **model capacity** (i.e., the size of the Q-function network) and the **update-to-data (UTD) ratio** (the number of gradient updates per environment data point). The overarching goal is to maximize sample efficiency – achieving the best possible performance with the least amount of environmental interaction – for a given computational cost.

The motivation stems from the high computational expense associated with training large deep learning models, especially in RL where data collection often involves costly interactions with an environment. Without compute-optimal scaling strategies, simply throwing more compute at an RL problem might lead to diminishing returns or even instability. The authors highlight that unlike supervised learning, where scaling laws are relatively well-understood, deep RL's unique challenges (e.g., non-stationary targets, bootstrapping) mean that existing scaling principles cannot be directly transferred.

A specific, crucial objective is to understand the interplay between model size, batch size, and UTD ratio. This involves developing a "mental model" to explain observed phenomena, particularly what the authors term **TD-overfitting**. This phenomenon, previously underexplored in the context of varying model sizes, is central to designing effective scaling strategies. Ultimately, the research aims to move beyond simply finding a "performant set of hyperparameters" for a single setup and instead provide generalized "scaling rules" that can extrapolate to new budgets and diverse RL tasks, enabling practitioners to make informed decisions about resource allocation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a rigorous empirical methodology, systematically investigating the impact of varying key hyperparameters on the performance and stability of value-based deep RL algorithms.

- **Algorithmic Foundation:** The study utilizes **BRO (Bigger, Regularized, Optimistic)** (Nauman et al., 2024b) and **SimbaV2 (Simplicity Bias for Scaling Up Parameters in Deep Reinforcement Learning)** (Lee et al., 2025). These are both Soft Actor-Critic (SAC)-based algorithms, chosen because they are specifically designed to scale well to high network capacities by incorporating regularization and normalization techniques, thus providing a suitable testbed for studying model size effects.
- **Hyperparameter Axes:** The experiments systematically vary three primary hyperparameters:
 - **Network Width (Model Capacity):** Q-function network widths are scaled from 256 to 4096. The authors focus on width over depth, consistent with prior findings suggesting better performance from scaling width in TD-learning.
 - **Batch Size (B):** Values range from 4 to 4096 (in powers of 2).
 - **Update-to-Data (UTD) Ratio ($\sigma$):** Values are 1, 2, 4, 8.
 - Other hyperparameters like learning rate and target network update rate are kept fixed, after initial sensitivity analyses confirm their less critical role in compute-optimal scaling compared to batch size, model size, and UTD.
- **Environments:** Experiments are conducted on challenging continuous control tasks from the **DeepMind Control Suite** (categorized into DMC-medium and DMC-hard) and **HumanoidBench**. This ensures the findings are robust across a diverse set of robotic manipulation and locomotion problems.
- **Compute-Optimal Formalization:** The study formally defines the compute allocation problem based on the framework by Rybkin et al. (2025). Total compute ($\mathcal{C}$) is approximated as proportional to $\sigma \cdot N \cdot \mathcal{D}_J$, where $N$ is model size and $\mathcal{D}_J$ is the data needed to reach performance $J$. Two problems are addressed: (1) minimizing compute to achieve a target performance $J_0$ given a data budget $\mathcal{D}_0$, and (2) maximizing performance for a combined budget $\mathcal{F}_0 = \mathcal{C} + \delta \mathcal{D}$ (where $\delta$ is the relative cost of data).
- **Overfitting Diagnosis:** To measure generalization and identify overfitting unique to TD-learning, the authors measure **validation TD-error** on a held-out set of transitions sampled from an independent validation replay buffer, alongside the standard training TD-error.
- **Passive Critic Experiment:** A diagnostic experiment is designed to isolate the role of TD-target quality in TD-overfitting. A "passive critic" is trained *only* to regress to the TD-targets generated by the main Q-network, without being involved in the online RL loop. By varying the capacity of both the main and passive critics, they can decouple the effects of target quality and the network's ability to fit those targets.
- **Empirical Scaling Models:** The core of the approach involves fitting empirical power-law relationships to model:
 - The "maximal admissible batch size" ($\tilde{B}$) as a function of UTD ratio ($\sigma$) and model size ($N$).
 - The data efficiency ($\mathcal{D}_J$) required to reach a specific performance $J$, as a function of $\sigma$ and $N$.
 - The optimal allocation of compute and data ($\mathcal{C}^*$ and $\mathcal{D}^*$) given a total budget $\mathcal{F}$.
 - The optimal $\sigma^*$ and $N^*$ as functions of the total budget $\mathcal{F}$.
- **Data Processing and Fitting:** Monte Carlo returns are preprocessed by removing post-reset dips and applying isotonic regression to smooth the learning curves, providing a more reliable measure of data efficiency. A robust fitting procedure in log-space, using softplus activations for parameters, is employed to derive the scaling laws. Bootstrap estimates are used to quantify uncertainty and estimate optimal batch sizes.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yields several critical insights and empirical scaling laws for value-based deep RL:

1. **Discovery of TD-Overfitting:** The paper identifies a novel phenomenon termed **TD-overfitting**. Unlike classical supervised learning overfitting, this occurs when reducing training TD-error by using large batch sizes *harms* generalization (increases validation TD-error), particularly for *small-capacity* models. This effect is **absent in large models**, which can effectively leverage larger batch sizes.
2. **Mechanism of TD-Overfitting:** The authors attribute TD-overfitting to the **poor quality of TD-targets** generated by low-capacity Q-networks. Small models, with limited representational capacity, tend to "entangle" features, leading to updates that improve fit on current training data but worsen generalization on unseen state-action pairs. Large batch sizes exacerbate this by providing low-variance, "directed" gradients that cause the small network to more aggressively fit these noisy, non-stationary targets, thereby propagating and amplifying errors across the state space.
3. **Validation with Passive Critic:** The passive critic experiment corroborates this mechanism. When TD-targets are produced by a low-capacity main Q-network, a passive critic (regardless of its own capacity) exhibits high validation TD-error. Conversely, a large main Q-network generates high-quality targets that even smaller passive critics can fit well, leading to lower validation error. This strongly indicates that TD-overfitting is primarily driven by the quality and generalization of the *TD-targets themselves*, rather than just the network's capacity to memorize them.
4. **Scaling Observation 1: Batch Size Selection ($\tilde{B}(\sigma, N)$):** For optimal performance, the maximal admissible batch size ($\tilde{B}$) should increase with model size ($N$) and decrease with the UTD ratio ($\sigma$). This relationship is modeled by an empirical function (Eq. 6.1), showing that larger models allow significantly larger batch sizes without TD-overfitting.
5. **Scaling Observation 2: Optimal Partitioning of Compute for Data Efficiency ($\mathcal{D}_J(\sigma, N)$):** The total amount of data ($\mathcal{D}_J$) required to achieve a target performance $J$ follows an inverse power-law relationship with both UTD ratio and model size (Eq. 7.1). The optimal UTD ($\sigma^*$) and model size ($N^*$) for minimizing compute (or data for a fixed compute) for a given performance target are predictable functions of the data budget, following specific power laws (Eq. 7.2). This means that both scaling model size and UTD ratio contribute to improved sample efficiency.
6. **Scaling Observation 3: Optimal Partitioning Between Data and Compute ($\mathcal{C}^*(\mathcal{F}_0), \mathcal{D}^*(\mathcal{F}_0)$):** For a given total budget $\mathcal{F}_0$ (which combines compute $\mathcal{C}$ and data $\mathcal{D}$ based on a relative cost $\delta$), the optimal allocation between compute and data follows predictable power laws (Eq. 7.3). This allows extrapolation of resource allocation for higher overall budgets.
7. **Scaling Observation 4: Optimal Partitioning of Budget Between UTD and Model Size ($\sigma^*_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{F}_0), N^*_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{F}_0)$):** The optimal UTD ratio ($\sigma^*_{\mathcal{F}}$) and model size ($N^*_{\mathcal{F}}$) also scale predictably as power laws of the total budget $\mathcal{F}_0$ (Eq. 7.4). While the precise coefficients vary by task, these laws provide guidance on how to increase UTD and model size as more resources become available.
8. **Practical Robustness:** The study notes that, for some environments, there exist broad regions of near-equivalent performance, meaning that precisely optimizing hyperparameters might not be necessary to capture most of the gains. However, for other tasks, precise tuning remains crucial, and the framework helps identify these differences. A general heuristic is that scaling model size, paired with a mild increase in UTD, often serves as a good starting point.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research represents a significant step forward in the understanding and practical application of deep reinforcement learning, offering a foundational analysis akin to the scaling laws that revolutionized other areas of machine learning.

- **Foundational Contribution to RL Theory:** By systematically investigating compute-optimal scaling in value-based deep RL, the paper provides a much-needed theoretical and empirical foundation. It fills a crucial gap in the RL literature, offering a comprehensive framework for resource allocation that has been lacking compared to supervised learning.
- **Practical Guidance for Practitioners:** The derived empirical scaling laws and observations offer concrete, actionable guidelines for practitioners. They can use these prescriptions to select optimal batch sizes, UTD ratios, and model capacities given a fixed compute budget, significantly improving the efficiency and performance of their RL agents. This is particularly valuable as RL problems become more complex and resource-intensive.
- **Novel Understanding of TD-Learning Dynamics:** The identification and mechanistic explanation of "TD-overfitting" is a major contribution. This phenomenon, unique to TD-learning due to non-stationary targets, highlights a critical challenge that future RL algorithms must address. Understanding its roots in the quality of TD-targets and the interplay with model capacity opens new avenues for designing more robust, stable, and scalable RL methods.
- **Enabling Larger-Scale RL:** By providing strategies for compute-optimal scaling, this work contributes to pushing the boundaries of deep RL towards larger models and more complex environments. It paves the way for "training TD-learning methods at a scale similar to other modern machine learning approaches," potentially unlocking new capabilities in domains like robotics, visual control, and language-based agents.
- **Optimizing Resource Utilization:** In an era where computational resources are increasingly expensive, this research provides a principled approach to maximize performance per unit of compute. This enables more efficient research and development, as well as deployment of high-performing RL systems in real-world applications.
- **Catalyst for Future Research:** The work explicitly lays out limitations, such as the need to explore other hyperparameters (learning rate, critic update ratio) and extend the analysis to larger-scale domains beyond simulated robotics. This invites future research to build upon these findings, refining the scaling laws and exploring their applicability in broader contexts, ultimately accelerating the progress of deep reinforcement learning.
