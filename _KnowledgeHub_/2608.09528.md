# Robotic Fabric Alignment System for Sewing Using Global Local Weighted ICP

> [!summary] Summary
> An automated robotic system aligns two flat fabric panels before sewing. Global Local Weighted ICP registers each panel to its CAD model using two separate adaptive weight matrices, one for global cut edges and one for local sewing lines, plus a sparsity term that discards occluded unmatched edge points. A suction roller end-effector then picks, repositions, and releases the top panel, achieving millimeter-level alignment.

## Problem

- Existing fabric alignment systems are semi-automatic, relying on rigid templates or fixtures and assuming panels are manually pre-loaded, so none automatically aligns and stacks two panels.
- Standard ICP treats all correspondences uniformly, so fabric cutting errors on the visible cut edge drag the pose estimate and displace the true sewing line.
- Robust registration variants need careful trimming-ratio tuning, dense uniform points, or full visibility, so they break on sparse contours partly occluded by the manipulator.

## Method

- **GLW-ICP** formulates registration with dual feature-aware weight matrices, one for global cut-edge points and one for local sewing-line points, plus a ==Frobenius weight regularizer== under normalization and balancing constraints.
- A four-step ==alternating solver== per iteration: ==k-d tree== correspondence update, closed-form rotation and translation, then closed-form weight update whose regularization parameter is retuned to its analytic upper bound.
- A ==sparsity level== keeps only the k lowest-residual edge weights, zeroing occluded unmatched points; a suction roller end-effector wraps, partially releases, and drags the top panel into alignment.

## Results

- Under occlusion GLW-ICP succeeds on every shape and roughly halves sewing-line Chamfer RMSE versus the only other successful baseline AW-RICP: **0.720** vs **2.084 mm** on shirt, **1.544** vs **3.061 mm** on collar.
- All other baselines fail under occlusion with errors of **53-193 mm**; GLW-ICP converges in **30-45** iterations and **1-3 seconds**, with paired t-tests significant at **p<0.01**.
- Real-world alignment reaches **1.10-1.31 mm**; ablation shows edge-only leaves **136-167 mm** occluded sewing error while sew-only leaves up to **135.8 mm** edge error.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - When one geometric feature is systematically corrupted, add a second semantically-correct feature and let learned weights arbitrate, instead of trimming outliers with a uniform rule.
> - Occlusion is better handled by ratio-based sparsity on residuals than fixed trimming; the optimal retained fraction (0.35 occluded vs 0.95 unoccluded) tracks visible fraction, not material.
> - Adaptive weights demonstrably shift mass away from noise-injected points, so plotting weight trajectories is a cheap diagnostic that a robust estimator behaves as claimed.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Accurate fabric alignment is a fundamental and critical prerequisite for high-quality sewing in garment manufacturing. Traditionally, this process is labor-intensive, time-consuming, and highly dependent on operator skill, often accounting for a significant portion (approximately 75%) of the total sewing operation time. Existing industrial and research efforts to automate parts of this process have largely focused on semi-automatic systems, which still require substantial manual intervention or rely on rigid fixtures and templates.

The broader research landscape includes various approaches to fabric handling and manipulation, such as pin-based, roller-based, brush-based, or shape-conforming gripper end-effectors, and vision-based systems for feature extraction and seam tracking. While these systems contribute to automating specific sub-tasks in structured environments, they often encounter limitations when dealing with the inherent deformability of fabrics, the need for adaptability to diverse fabric shapes, or conditions of partial occlusion.

Specifically, existing pose estimation methods, crucial for identifying fabric position and orientation, face challenges. Vision-based methods can be sensitive to visibility issues like occlusion, folds, or complex textures. Point Cloud Registration (PCR) methods, including variants of the Iterative Closest Point (ICP) algorithm, are commonly used for aligning observed geometry with CAD models. However, standard ICP and many of its variants (e.g., Trimmed-ICP, Sparse-ICP, Coherent Point Drift, Fast and Robust ICP, KSS-ICP) exhibit sensitivities to noise, poor initial alignment, outliers, and perform poorly under partial occlusion. Moreover, many ICP methods treat all correspondences uniformly, failing to differentiate between geometrically distinct features (e.g., global edges versus precise local sewing lines). This can lead to inaccuracies, particularly when fabric cutting errors cause real fabric edges to deviate from ideal CAD geometries.

This research addresses a recognized gap in the field: the absence of a fully automated system capable of both accurately estimating the pose of two fabric pieces (top and bottom panels) and aligning them, especially when portions of the fabric are occluded during manipulation. The work contributes a novel approach to fabric pose estimation that specifically accounts for both global and local geometric features while being robust to partial occlusion and measurement noise, aiming to bridge the gap towards end-to-end automation in apparel manufacturing.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a novel automated robotic system for precise fabric alignment prior to sewing, thereby reducing manual intervention and improving efficiency and consistency in garment production. The motivation for this work stems from several key challenges inherent in current fabric manufacturing processes:

1. **Labor-Intensive and Skill-Dependent Process:** Fabric alignment is a time-consuming task heavily reliant on human dexterity and experience. Automating this step aims to decrease labor costs, reduce operator dependency, and standardize the alignment quality.
2. **Impact of Misalignment on Product Quality:** Even minor misalignments of fabric panels can lead to defects, necessitating rework, and causing production delays. A robust automated system can minimize these errors.
3. **Limitations of Existing Automation:** Previous systems for fabric handling and alignment are often semi-automatic, requiring manual fabric loading onto templates, or are not adaptable to complex or irregularly shaped garment components (e.g., collars, shirt panels). To the best of the authors' knowledge, no existing system can fully automate the alignment and stacking of two fabric pieces, particularly under challenging conditions.
4. **Inadequacies of Current Pose Estimation Techniques:**
 - **Distinction Between Global and Local Features:** Fabrics possess both global features (e.g., cut edges) and local features (e.g., sewing lines). While global edges are visible, they are prone to cutting errors (due to blunt tools, misaligned layers, shrinkage, fraying). Sewing lines, in contrast, represent the true intended seam path and are critical for precise garment construction. Existing pose estimation methods, such as standard ICP, often treat all features uniformly, leading to seam displacement if erroneous edge points are prioritized.
 - **Robustness to Partial Occlusion:** Robotic manipulation inherently involves instances where parts of the fabric panel are occluded by the end-effector. Most conventional ICP methods perform poorly under such conditions, requiring robust algorithms that can effectively handle missing or unreliable data.
 - **Sensitivity to Noise and Outliers:** Fabric properties and sensor measurements can introduce noise and outliers into point cloud data. Traditional ICP variants struggle with these inaccuracies, leading to unstable or incorrect pose estimates.

To address these motivations and challenges, the key objectives are:
- To develop a robotic system capable of automatically estimating the poses of both top and bottom fabric panels that are laid flat and wrinkle-free, regardless of their initial arbitrary positions.
- To design a novel Iterative Closest Point (ICP) variant, termed Global Local Weighted ICP (GLW-ICP), that can robustly align fabric panels by simultaneously considering and appropriately weighting both global edge points and local sewing line points, while effectively managing unmatched points in occluded regions.
- To integrate this advanced pose estimation method with a robotic manipulation system, employing a roller-based end-effector, to accurately pick up, reposition, and precisely align the top fabric panel with the bottom panel.
- To experimentally validate the proposed system's ability to achieve millimeter-level alignment accuracy across various fabric shapes under both occluded and non-occluded conditions.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed system integrates a novel pose estimation algorithm, Global Local Weighted Iterative Closest Point (GLW-ICP), with a robotic manipulation platform to achieve automated fabric alignment.

#### 3.1. System Hardware

The core hardware components include:
- **Manipulator:** A 6-Degrees of Freedom (DoF) Denso VS-087 robotic arm, controlled via an EtherCAT servo controller.
- **Roller End-Effector:** Attached to the robot arm, it features a motor-driven roller with a suction port for fabric pick-up and a force/torque sensor (ATI Axia80-M8). A servo motor with a rotary encoder ensures precise roller rotation.
- **3D Vision System:** A Basler acA1920-155uc camera with a 12mm lens, used for capturing RGB images and detecting fabric features.
- **Control Workstation:** Equipped with a powerful CPU and GPU, running Windows OS and TenAsys INtime 6 OS for robotic control.
The system establishes a clear coordinate system (World, End-effector, Camera, and Panel frames) and represents panel poses using generalized coordinate vectors and homogeneous transformation matrices.

#### 3.2. Automated Fabric Alignment Process Workflow

The alignment process is divided into four main steps:

1. **Step 1: Initial Pose Estimation of Bottom and Top Fabrics:**
 - RGB images of both top and bottom panels (assumed flat and wrinkle-free) are captured.
 - Image processing techniques (binarization, erosion) extract 2D pixel coordinates of fabric edges ($Q_e$) and sewing lines ($Q_s$).
 - These 2D points are projected into 3D world coordinates using known camera intrinsic and extrinsic parameters.
 - The GLW-ICP algorithm is applied to align the extracted fabric point clouds ($Q_e$, $Q_s$) with their corresponding Computer-Aided Design (CAD) model point clouds ($P_e$, $P_s$). This provides initial pose estimates ($W T_T$, $\xi_T$) for the top panel and ($W T_B$, $\xi_B$) for the bottom panel. The system is designed to handle panels with different CAD geometries.

2. **Step 2: Fabric Pick-up and Wrapping:**
 - Based on the estimated top panel pose ($\xi_T$), a predefined grasping point (relative to the CAD model) is identified on the physical top fabric panel.
 - A 6-DoF manipulator, using Inverse Kinematics (IK) and a fifth-order polynomial trajectory, moves the roller end-effector from a home position to the grasping point.
 - Upon reaching the fabric, the end-effector presses down and activates suction to secure the fabric.
 - A coordinated motion involving roller rotation and end-effector translation wraps the fabric smoothly and wrinkle-free around the roller.

3. **Step 3: Wrapped Fabric Manipulation and Partial Release:**
 - After wrapping, the end-effector moves the fabric to an offset pose ($\xi_{offset}$).
 - The orientation of the end-effector is adjusted to match the bottom panel's orientation ($W R_E = W R_B$), while the Z-height is maintained. The position offset is typically set to half the fabric length.
 - At this offset pose, the top panel fabric is partially released onto the workbench. This partial release minimizes subsequent rotational adjustments, mainly requiring translational motion for final alignment, thus reducing the risk of wrinkling.

4. **Step 4: Fabric Pose Re-estimation and Final Alignment:**
 - Due to its deformable nature and the effect of suction, the partially released top fabric panel's pose is re-estimated using the GLW-ICP algorithm. This step extracts point clouds from the partially visible fabric and aligns them with the CAD model.
 - A final fifth-order polynomial trajectory is generated for the end-effector to move the top panel from its current re-estimated pose ($\xi_T(0)$) to the target pose ($\xi_T(t_f) = \xi_B$), where it aligns with the bottom panel.
 - The robot drags the top panel, maintaining suction to prevent relative motion and wrinkles, until alignment is achieved.
 - A controlled fabric release completes the alignment process.

#### 3.3. Global Local Weighted Iterative Closest Point (GLW-ICP) Algorithm

GLW-ICP is a novel point cloud registration method designed to address the challenges of fabric pose estimation under occlusion and with distinct global and local features.

**Optimization Problem Formulation:**
The algorithm seeks to find the optimal rigid transformation ($T^* = [R^*|t^*]$) that minimizes a combined objective function $g$. This function includes:
- **Alignment Terms:** $g_e$ for global edge points and $g_s$ for local sewing line points, quantifying the error between transformed CAD model points and observed fabric points. These are weighted Frobenius norms of error matrices ($E_e, E_s$).
- **Regularization Term:** $g_r$, which penalizes large weight values, promoting smoothness and numerical stability.
The optimization is subject to several constraints:
- **Normalization:** Ensures a fixed total importance distributed between global and local alignment terms.
- **Balancing:** Prevents either global or local alignment terms from dominating the optimization.
- **Orthonormality:** Ensures $R$ remains a valid rotation matrix.
- **Non-negativity:** Constrains all weights to be non-negative.

**Iterative Solution:**
GLW-ICP iteratively refines the transformation and weights in four steps:

1. **Update Correspondence Mapping (C):** At each iteration, for every transformed source point (CAD model point), the nearest neighbor target point (fabric point) is found using a k-d tree algorithm.
2. **Update Translation Vector (t):** With correspondences, rotation, and weights fixed, a closed-form solution for $t$ is derived. Unlike vanilla ICP, GLW-ICP updates $t$ using adaptive weight matrices ($W_e, W_s$) that suppress unreliable points.
3. **Update Rotation Matrix (R):** After updating $t$, the objective function is rewritten in terms of centered points. An optimal $R$ is found by maximizing a trace function involving a cross-covariance matrix ($H$), which combines global and local point pairs with adaptive weights. Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) is used to solve this, with a correction for reflections.
4. **Update Weight Vectors ($W_e, W_s$):** Optimal weight matrices for edge and sewing line points are determined using Lagrange multipliers to satisfy normalization and balancing constraints. These closed-form solutions ($W_e, W_s$) allow dynamic adjustment of feature importance based on alignment error.

**Regularization in GLW-ICP:**
The regularization parameter $\alpha$ controls the trade-off between alignment accuracy and weight smoothness. $\alpha$ is dynamically updated during iterations, influencing the contribution of the alignment error Gram matrix in the weight calculations.

**Occlusion Handling with Sparsity:**
To address occluded regions where fabric edge points may not have corresponding CAD model points, GLW-ICP introduces sparsity into the edge weight matrix ($W_e$). A sparsity level $k$ (expressed as a ratio $k_{ratio}$) specifies the number of edge point pairs with the smallest alignment errors that are assigned non-zero weights. The remaining $n_e - k$ points (likely from occluded regions) are effectively pruned by setting their weights to zero. Bounds for $\alpha$ are derived to ensure these weight conditions are met.

**Convergence Analysis:**
The algorithm ensures convergence by operating on a monotonically decreasing objective function that is bounded below. Each iterative step (correspondence, translation, rotation, weights) is designed to not increase the objective function, leading to a stable pose estimate.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research systematically evaluated the proposed GLW-ICP method and the integrated robotic alignment system through a series of experiments.

#### 4.1. Dataset and Evaluation Metrics

- **Fabric Samples:** Four representative garment panel shapes—collar, rectangle, shirt back, and shirt front—made from plain-weave polyester were used. These covered straight, curved, and mixed edge characteristics common in garment manufacturing.
- **Alignment Scenarios:** Three alignment experiments were conducted: shirt alignment (different geometries), collar alignment (identical panels), and rectangular panel alignment (identical panels). The bottom panel was fixed, and the top panel was manipulated.
- **Visibility Conditions:** Experiments were performed under both unoccluded (entire fabric contour visible) and partially occluded (parts hidden by end-effector) conditions.
- **Metrics:** Bi-directional alignment Chamfer Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) was used to quantify the geometric discrepancy between estimated and ground-truth points for both edge and sewing line features.

#### 4.2. Effect of Sparsity Level ($k_{ratio}$)

- **Unoccluded Conditions:** Increasing the sparsity level ratio ($k_{ratio}$) from 0 to 0.95 consistently decreased Chamfer RMSE for all fabric shapes, improving alignment accuracy. This indicated that retaining more valid edge points provides better geometric information. An optimal $k_{ratio} = 0.95$ was selected for unoccluded cases.
- **Occluded Conditions:** The relationship between $k_{ratio}$ and alignment accuracy was non-monotonic. Accuracy initially improved as $k_{ratio}$ increased from 0 to approximately 0.35, allowing the algorithm to utilize more visible edge points. However, beyond $k_{ratio} = 0.35$, accuracy degraded, as increasing $k_{ratio}$ re-introduced erroneous edge points from occluded areas that lacked valid correspondences. An optimal $k_{ratio} = 0.35$ was selected for occluded cases.
- **Consistency:** The optimal $k_{ratio}$ values for each visibility condition were consistent across all tested fabric shapes.

#### 4.3. Effect of Regularization Parameter ($\alpha$)

- The regularization parameter $\alpha$ was dynamically updated using its upper bound ($\alpha_{ub}$) from equation (52).
- During early iterations, $\alpha$ exhibited a temporary increase before stabilizing. This initial rise was attributed to changes in error distribution as correspondences formed along curved edges, temporarily strengthening the smoothness term to damp weight oscillations. As correspondences stabilized, $\alpha$ converged to a steady value.

#### 4.4. Pose Estimation Comparison with Baseline Methods

GLW-ICP's pose estimation accuracy was compared against several standard and robust ICP variants (ICP, Trimmed-ICP, Sparse-ICP, KSS-ICP, CPD, FRICP, AW-RICP).

- **Overall Performance:** GLW-ICP consistently achieved the lowest Chamfer errors across all fabric shapes and under both unoccluded and partially occluded conditions.
- **Robustness to Occlusion:** GLW-ICP demonstrated consistent success even under partial occlusion. Among the baselines, only AW-RICP also succeeded consistently under occlusion, but GLW-ICP reduced the Chamfer error by at least approximately 50% relative to AW-RICP in occluded scenarios.
- **Failure Modes of Baselines:** Several baseline methods (Sparse-ICP, KSS-ICP, CPD, FRICP, Trimmed-ICP) exhibited failures, including flipping issues or high alignment errors, even in unoccluded settings, particularly for sewing line alignment. Standard ICP also failed under occlusion.
- **Efficiency:** GLW-ICP converged in a moderate number of iterations (typically 30–45) with a total runtime of about 1–3 seconds, demonstrating comparable efficiency to other methods while achieving superior accuracy.
- **Statistical Significance:** Paired t-tests confirmed that GLW-ICP's performance improvements over baseline methods were statistically significant (p < 0.01).

#### 4.5. Validation of Edge and Sewing-Line Complementarity

An experiment simulating cutting errors (Gaussian noise on shoulder-edge points of a shirt panel) validated the complementary roles of global edge and local sewing-line features.
- **Noise Impact:** When noise was introduced to the shoulder edges, using the sewing line ($Q_s$) for alignment (along with the perturbed edge $\tilde{Q}_e$) resulted in significantly better alignment accuracy compared to relying solely on the perturbed shoulder edge points ($\tilde{Q}_{e,shoulder}$).
- **Complementary Role:** This demonstrated that the sewing line, despite being approximately parallel to the edge, provides crucial local constraints that compensate for global edge perturbations, offering a more stable reference for accurate pose estimation and restoring the intended CAD geometry.

#### 4.6. Ablation Study and Adaptive Weighting Validation

- **Ablation Study (Table III):**
 - **Edge-only alignment:** Achieved good alignment for global edges but resulted in large errors for local sewing lines.
 - **Sew-only alignment:** Achieved good alignment for local sewing lines but resulted in large errors for global edges.
 - **GLW-ICP:** Demonstrated the best performance by keeping both sewing line and global edge alignment errors small, highlighting the necessity and effectiveness of jointly considering both feature types.
- **Adaptive Weighting Validation (Fig. 10):**
 - **Noise-Specific Weight Adjustment:** When Gaussian noise was added to specific shoulder parts of the shirt panel, GLW-ICP adaptively decreased the mean weight of the noisy edge points and increased the mean weight of non-noisy edge points as noise level increased. This confirmed that GLW-ICP adjusts weights based on individual point reliability.
 - **Dynamic Feature Balance:** When noise was applied to all edge points, the normalized mean weight of edge points ($\bar{W}_e$) increased, while that of sewing line points ($\bar{W}_s$) decreased. This indicated that GLW-ICP effectively balances the influence of global edge features and local sewing line features dynamically to minimize overall alignment error.

#### 4.7. Fabric Alignment Results

- The full robotic system, executing the four-step workflow, achieved millimeter-level precision in real-world fabric alignment experiments across all tested fabric shapes and visibility conditions. This result further validated the effectiveness and practical suitability of the GLW-ICP method and the integrated robotic system for automated fabric alignment in industrial scenarios.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant advancement in the automation of fabric alignment for apparel manufacturing, addressing a critical bottleneck in the production pipeline.

**Technological Advancements:**
- **Novel Pose Estimation:** The introduction of the Global Local Weighted Iterative Closest Point (GLW-ICP) algorithm represents a methodological contribution to point cloud registration. Its ability to simultaneously and adaptively weigh both global edge features and local sewing line features, while robustly handling partial occlusion and inherent noise, differentiates it from existing ICP variants. This approach more accurately reflects human operators' ability to use both overall shape and precise seam information.
- **Integrated Robotic System:** The development of a complete robotic system, combining advanced vision-based pose estimation with a roller-based end-effector and sophisticated manipulation strategies, demonstrates a practical solution for a complex deformable object handling task.

**Industrial and Economic Impact:**
- **Enhanced Productivity:** By automating a traditionally labor-intensive and time-consuming step, the system has the potential to significantly shorten preparation times and increase overall production throughput in garment factories.
- **Improved Product Quality and Consistency:** Achieving millimeter-level alignment accuracy consistently, even under challenging conditions like partial occlusion, can drastically reduce defects, minimize rework, and improve the quality and consistency of finished garments.
- **Reduced Labor Dependency:** Shifting from manual to automated alignment reduces reliance on highly skilled operators, potentially lowering labor costs and addressing skill shortages in the manufacturing sector.
- **Enabling End-to-End Automation:** This work is a foundational step towards fully automated apparel manufacturing lines, from initial panel preparation to final stitching, which could lead to more agile and efficient production processes.
- **Adaptability:** The demonstrated applicability to various garment components (collar, rectangle, shirt) suggests a broad utility for different production settings and product lines.

**Future Directions:**
- The GLW-ICP method's adaptability to integrate rich surface features (e.g., logos, buttons, stitching patterns) as local information extends its potential beyond simple edge and sewing line alignment to more complex garment pose estimation scenarios.
- Future work on automatically selecting the optimal `k_ratio` based on fabric shape and size will enhance the system's autonomy and reduce the need for manual tuning across diverse garment types and occlusion conditions.
- The planned extension to handle wrinkled fabrics by integrating fabric state estimation methods (e.g., template-mesh-based flattening) promises to further broaden the system's applicability to less controlled initial fabric conditions, moving closer to real-world complexities.

In summary, this research provides a robust, accurate, and practically relevant solution for automated fabric alignment, addressing a longstanding challenge in the apparel industry and paving the way for more comprehensive automation in soft goods manufacturing.
