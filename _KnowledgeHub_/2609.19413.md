# From Rollout to Reset: A Graph-Based Harness for Autonomous Long-Horizon Manipulation Evaluation

> [!summary] Summary
> HALTER is a harness for autonomous real-robot evaluation of long-horizon manipulation that resets the scene by planning over a library of learned atomic VLA reset skills. It builds a spatial scene graph online from RGB-D point clouds and open-vocabulary segmentation, and an LLM reads the graph history to score rollouts, plan resets, and verify them. It reaches 76% reset success versus 52% for AutoEval, cutting operator time 72%.

## Problem

- Real-robot policy evaluation relies on humans resetting the scene between rollouts, consuming operator time and leaving the initial state distribution unspecified, so results reproduce poorly across trials and labs.
- AutoEval automates reset and scoring with one learned per-task reset policy, which only works for single-step tasks; long-horizon rollouts end in combinatorially many terminal states needing combinatorial demonstrations.
- VLM-based checkers read scene state from single post-rollout images, leaving geometry implicit and missing occluded state, so fine spatial relations like in or on are judged unreliably.

## Method

- **Hierarchical reset**: an LLM planner sequences a library of ==atomic reset skills==, each an X-VLA trained on 30-50 demonstrations, so demonstration cost scales with library size, not terminal-state count.
- **Online spatial scene graph**: ==GroundedSAM== masks projected into depth give per-object point clouds at 0.5 Hz; deterministic geometric functions label ==on/in relations==, retaining last-seen state for occluded objects.
- **Shared LLM reasoning interface**: Qwen3.5-397B reads the serialized graph history with ==stage-specific prompts== and few reference cases to score skill completion, plan the reset, and verify per-skill effects and final relations.

## Results

- On four Franka long-horizon tasks, HALTER resets **76.0%** of episodes versus **52.0%** for AutoEval and **65.0%** for motion-planning skills; scoring accuracy **90%** vs **76%**, verification **91%** vs **78%**.
- Across 100 episodes, operator time drops from **88** to **24** minutes (interventions **100** to **25**) versus manual reset; on three held-out tasks with no new demonstrations, HALTER resets **74.7%** against AutoEval's **1.3%**.
- Drawer-Store ablation: replacing the graph with images drops reset SR **88%** to **72%**, rendered point clouds to **40%**; graph updates below 0.2 Hz cut reset SR to **48%** while scoring accuracy stays **96%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Planning over a fixed atomic skill library turns combinatorial terminal-state coverage into demonstration cost bounded by library size, letting resets transfer where monolithic per-task policies collapse.
> - Separating perception from reasoning helps: converting geometry into explicit symbolic relations beats feeding LLMs raw images or 3D renders, so the gain comes from relations, not 3D appearance.
> - Reset state estimation should accumulate history during the rollout, since occluded outcomes like objects inside closed drawers are only observable when they change; post-hoc snapshots cannot recover them.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

Repeated real-robot trials of a task such as opening a drawer, putting a lemon inside, and closing it require putting the scene back before the next trial. Restoring that scene by hand consumes operator time and leaves the starting-state distribution dependent on human judgment. The difficulty grows for long-horizon tasks, because a rollout can stop partway through and leave many different arrangements to undo. HALTER's approach, in ordinary terms, is to observe the scene as it changes, choose a sequence of small known actions to put it back, and then check the result. In the authors' design rationale, demonstrations can then scale with the skill library rather than with the many possible stopping states. The system often restores the scene and sharply reduces the operator's reset burden.

A four-step drawer task has four pictured terminal states: failure to open the drawer, failure while removing the lemon, failure in transit, and failure while closing the drawer. A monolithic reset policy would need demonstrations covering the different terminal states, and a single-image checker can misread spatial relations. ### 2. A graph that remembers the rollout

HALTER builds a scene graph during the rollout. The graph holds named objects, their geometric attributes, and relations such as "on" or "in." As the rollout proceeds, HALTER samples multi-view RGB-D keyframes and segments relevant objects. It then projects the masks into depth to obtain object point clouds and converts that geometry into relations. When an object becomes occluded, HALTER retains its most recent valid observation and updates it when the object is visible again.

An LLM uses this graph history for three jobs:

1. It estimates which task-skill effects occurred.
1. It plans an ordered reset from the current state, using the target relations and the skill library.
1. It supports verification while the robot executes the plan with learned VLA skills. After each skill, the system checks for the expected effect, and it then checks the final scene against the target relations.

A failed check stops the plan or requests human intervention. The graph check is a proxy for checking the physical scene.

The high-level planner chooses which atomic reset skills to call and in what order. The low-level learned skills perform actions such as picking an object or closing a drawer. The design assumes that the library contains what is needed to restore reachable states.

### 3. What the comparisons establish

The main comparison covered four long-horizon tasks on a Franka arm. HALTER reached **76.0% reset success**, compared with **52.0% for AutoEval** and **65.0% for MP skills**. Reset success rate (Reset SR) is the fraction of episodes in which manual annotation finds that the reset restored the target object relations. The average pools 100 episodes, 25 per task. MP skills retains HALTER's components other than its learned skills and uses motion planning to execute the planned skills.

HALTER does not win on every task. On Pot-Cook, MP skills succeeds in 72% of episodes versus HALTER's 48%. The authors suggest that analytic grasps handle the stock pot more reliably.

The practical payoff is less operator time, rather than faster episodes. Over 100 episodes, HALTER uses 24 minutes of operator time, compared with 88 minutes for manual reset, a reported 72% reduction. HALTER's mean cycle time is 121 seconds per episode, longer than AutoEval's 104 seconds, which the authors attribute to extra LLM reasoning between reset skills.

The most direct test of the mechanism is a scene-representation ablation on one task, Drawer-Store. Replacing the graph with images lowers plan validity from 92% to 72%, verification accuracy from 96% to 76%, and Reset SR from 88% to 72%. Rendered point clouds lower those three metrics to 40%, 52%, and 40%. This makes the explicit relations in the graph a plausible contributor beyond raw visual or geometric input. It does not prove that graphs alone explain all performance gains.

### 4. Resetting the next trial

A long rollout can stop in many states, and HALTER uses the observed history to guide a sequence of reusable reset actions. The approach still has requirements. A missing reset skill still needs demonstrations, and task-specific reference cases are still supplied. Executing a reset also remains a long-horizon problem.
