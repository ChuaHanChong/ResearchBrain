# Learning Vision-Guided Quadrupedal Locomotion End-to-End with Cross-Modal Transformers

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from UC San Diego and Tsinghua University developed LocoTransformer, an end-to-end Reinforcement Learning framework that fuses proprioceptive and depth sensor data with a cross-modal Transformer. This approach enables quadrupedal robots to navigate complex environments, showing up to a 92% increase in distance moved and fewer collisions in real-world tasks compared to baseline methods.

## Problem

- Existing learned quadrupedal locomotion policies primarily rely on proprioceptive feedback and extensive domain randomization, leading to "blind" agents that lack proactive obstacle avoidance.
- These blind agents cannot perform long-term environmental planning or consistently avoid large obstacles, which limits their capabilities in complex environments.
- Prior methods incorporating vision often utilize complex hierarchical architectures or pre-defined controllers, hindering end-to-end learning and potentially limiting flexibility.

## Method

- The **LocoTransformer** framework introduces an end-to-end ==Reinforcement Learning== method using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) for vision-guided quadrupedal locomotion.
- It directly fuses high-dimensional depth sensor inputs and proprioceptive states via a ==Transformer==-based architecture, utilizing separate encoders (MLP for proprioception, ConvNet for vision) and a shared Transformer encoder for ==cross-modal== fusion.
- The Transformer's self-attention mechanism processes tokenized proprioceptive and visual features, enabling interaction and information propagation between modalities for sophisticated decision-making.

## Results

- In simulated generalization tasks, LocoTransformer significantly reduced collisions by **290.5%** to **663%** and moved farther compared to baselines.
- During real-world deployment on a Unitree A1 robot, LocoTransformer moved **92%** farther with fewer collisions in indoor obstacle courses and **90%** farther without collisions in forest environments, outperforming the State-Depth-Concat baseline.
- Qualitative analysis of attention maps demonstrated that the LocoTransformer learned to focus on relevant visual cues, such as nearby obstacles for avoidance and distant goals for planning, providing insights into its decision-making.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The Transformer's cross-modal attention mechanism allows the policy to selectively attend to task-relevant visual regions (e.g., obstacles, goal), facilitating proactive planning and reactive adjustments.
> - End-to-end learning with cross-modal Transformers yields superior generalization and robustness for sim-to-real transfer, capturing fundamental object structures rather than overfitting to simulation appearances.
> - Naive concatenation of visual and proprioceptive features is less effective, as the Transformer's ability to model spatial and cross-modal dependencies is crucial for complex terrain navigation and obstacle avoidance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Legged locomotion is a fundamental challenge in robotics, crucial for extending robot capabilities into diverse and complex environments, from daily tasks to planetary exploration. Recent advancements in Deep Reinforcement Learning (RL) have significantly improved the locomotion skills of quadrupedal robots, enabling them to traverse uneven terrain and generalize to real-world conditions like mud and snow. However, a prevalent characteristic of many successful RL approaches in this domain is their reliance on learning robust controllers for "blind" quadrupedal locomotion, primarily using only proprioceptive feedback. These methods often employ extensive domain randomization during training in simulation to enhance generalization.

This paper positions itself by addressing a perceived limitation in these blind RL approaches. While domain randomization can endow agents with robustness to various terrains, it does not enable proactive obstacle avoidance or long-term environmental planning. Human locomotion studies indicate a significant reliance on eye-body coordination and gaze adaptation based on environmental characteristics (e.g., flat versus rough terrain). This observation suggests that equipping robots with visual sensory input could substantially enhance their ability to maneuver complex environments.

Previous works incorporating vision into RL for locomotion have explored various architectures, including Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs) for LiDAR inputs or hierarchical RL frameworks that combine high-level visual guidance with low-level motor control. The present work distinguishes itself by proposing an end-to-end RL method that directly fuses proprioceptive states and high-dimensional depth sensor inputs using a Transformer-based architecture. This approach aims to leverage the complementary nature of immediate proprioceptive feedback and proactive visual information within a single, unified model, without resorting to hierarchical structures or pre-defined controllers, and is presented as the first work to deploy a vision-based RL policy on a real quadrupedal robot for obstacle avoidance in unstructured outdoor environments using cross-modal Transformers.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an end-to-end Reinforcement Learning (RL) method for quadrupedal locomotion that effectively integrates both proprioceptive information and high-dimensional visual depth sensor inputs. The authors propose a Transformer-based model, termed LocoTransformer, to achieve this integration.

The motivation for this work is rooted in several observations and limitations of existing approaches:

1. **Limitations of Blind Locomotion Agents:** Many successful learning-based locomotion methods primarily rely on proprioceptive states and domain randomization to train "blind" agents. While these agents can achieve robust movement on challenging terrains, they lack the ability to proactively perceive and respond to obstacles or anticipate changes in the environment beyond immediate contact. For instance, a blind agent cannot consistently avoid large obstacles as it lacks the long-range perception required to plan alternative trajectories.

2. **Complementary Nature of Sensory Modalities:** The authors hypothesize that proprioceptive states, such as robot pose, IMU readings, and joint rotations, provide precise, immediate feedback crucial for rapid reactive adjustments to the robot's current status. Conversely, visual inputs, particularly from a depth camera, offer critical contextual information about the environment ahead, enabling the agent to plan maneuvers, anticipate terrain changes, and avoid distant obstacles. Fusing these two distinct but complementary modalities is expected to yield a more capable and intelligent locomotion policy.

3. **Inspiration from Biological Systems:** Human locomotion studies have demonstrated that humans heavily rely on eye-body coordination, with gaze patterns adapting to environmental characteristics. This biological insight motivates the integration of visual sensory input to improve quadrupedal locomotion on uneven terrain and in obstacle-rich environments.

4. **Leveraging Transformer Architectures:** The Transformer architecture, known for its success in sequence modeling and multi-modal reasoning, is chosen for its ability to process complementary information from both modalities and its inherent self-attention mechanism. This mechanism allows the model to selectively attend to specific visual regions (e.g., obstacles, uneven ground, goal) that are critical for both short-term reactive decisions and long-term planning, potentially leading to more generalizable and interpretable policies.

5. **End-to-End Learning Without Hierarchical Structures:** The research aims to achieve this multi-modal control in an end-to-end manner, avoiding the complexities associated with hierarchical RL (HRL) or reliance on pre-defined controllers. This simplifies the learning process and allows the policy to directly learn the mapping from fused sensory inputs to motor commands.

In summary, the key objectives are to overcome the limitations of blind locomotion by incorporating vision, to demonstrate the effectiveness of cross-modal Transformers for fusing proprioceptive and visual data in an end-to-end RL framework, and to validate that this approach leads to improved performance, particularly in terms of generalization, in both simulated and real-world challenging environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors propose LocoTransformer, an end-to-end Reinforcement Learning (RL) method for quadrupedal locomotion that integrates proprioceptive states and high-dimensional depth sensor inputs using a Transformer-based model. The interaction between the robot and the environment is modeled as a Markov Decision Process (MDP), and the policy is learned using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO).

#### 3.1. Network Architecture (LocoTransformer)

The LocoTransformer architecture consists of two main components: separate modality encoders and a shared Transformer encoder for multi-modal fusion.

- **Input Modalities:**
 - **Proprioceptive State:** A 93-dimensional vector comprising IMU readings, local joint rotations, and the actions taken by the agent for the last three time steps.
 - **Visual Observations:** Stacked dense depth images from a first-person-view depth camera mounted on the robot's head. Specifically, the most recent four depth images of shape 64x64 are stacked to provide both spatial and temporal visual information.

- **Separate Modality Encoders:**
 - **Proprioceptive Encoder:** An MLP is used to encode the 93-D proprioceptive input into a C-dimensional proprioceptive feature vector ($E_{prop} \in \mathbb{R}^C$). In this implementation, a 2-layer MLP with hidden dimensions (256, 256) is used, projecting to C=128 dimensions.
 - **Visual Encoder:** A ConvNet (following the architecture in Mnih et al., 2015b) processes the stacked depth images into a spatial feature representation ($E_{visual}$) of shape C x N x N (e.g., 128 channels x 4 x 4 spatial maps).

- **Transformer Encoder for Multi-modal Fusion:**
 - **Tokenization:** The proprioceptive features ($E_{prop}$) are projected into a single C-dimensional token embedding ($t_{prop}$) using a linear layer. The spatial visual features ($E_{visual}$) are split into N x N distinct C-dimensional token embeddings ($t_{visual_{i,j}}$), each corresponding to a local visual region. This results in a sequence of N*N + 1 tokens as input to the Transformer encoder.
 - **Shared Transformer Encoder Layers:** A stack of L Transformer encoder layers (L=2 in the main experiments, each with a hidden feature dimension of 256) is used. Each layer comprises a self-attention mechanism, two LayerNormalization (LN) layers with residual connections, and a 2-layer MLP.
 - **Self-Attention Mechanism:** The standard scaled dot-product attention computes interactions between all tokens in the sequence, allowing information propagation between proprioceptive and all visual tokens, as well as spatial attention among visual tokens. This mechanism enables cross-modal reasoning and allows the agent to explicitly attend to relevant visual regions. Residual connections are critical for ensuring stable propagation of proprioceptive information across multiple layers.

- **Projection Head:** After passing through the Transformer encoder layers, information from both modalities is pooled. This is done by first computing the mean of all tokens from the same modality to obtain a single feature vector for each modality. These two feature vectors are then concatenated and passed through another MLP (a 2-layer MLP with hidden dimensions 256, 256) to produce the final output vector, which is used to predict the action distribution (policy) and the value function.

#### 3.2. Training and Reward Function

- **End-to-End Training:** The entire LocoTransformer model is trained end-to-end using PPO, without hierarchical RL or pre-defined controllers. The proprioceptive and visual encoders are shared between the value and policy networks.
- **Reward Function:** A consistent reward function is used across all environments, consisting of:
 - **Forward reward ($\alpha_{forward} R_{forward}$):** Incentivizes forward movement along a task-specific direction.
 - **Energy usage penalty ($\alpha_{energy} R_{energy}$):** Penalizes high motor torques to encourage natural and efficient motion ($-\| \tau \|^2$).
 - **Alive reward ($\alpha_{alive} R_{alive}$):** Provides a positive reward at each time step until termination (e.g., falling or collision), encouraging longer episodes.
 - **Sphere reward ($K \cdot R_{sphere}$):** (Applicable in some environments) A bonus for collecting spheres, where K is the number of spheres collected.
 - Hyperparameters: $\alpha_{forward} = 1$, $\alpha_{energy} = 0.005$, $\alpha_{alive} = 0.1$.

#### 3.3. Experimental Setup

- **Simulation Environments:** Six challenging and diverse simulated environments are designed: Wide Obstacle, Thin Obstacle, Wide Obstacle & Sphere, Thin Obstacle & Sphere, Moving Obstacle, and Mountain. These environments feature varying terrains and obstacles (static, dynamic, with/without reward-giving spheres).
- **Domain Randomization:** To facilitate simulation-to-real transfer, domain randomization is applied during training. This includes varying robot parameters (KP, KD, inertia, friction, mass, motor strength, sensor latency) and introducing noisy visual observations (randomly setting depth readings to maximum values).
- **Real-world Experiments:** Policies trained in simulation are transferred to a Unitree A1 Robot. Experiments are conducted in indoor settings with box obstacles (Indoor & Obs.) and outdoor forest environments with trees and uneven terrain (Forest).
- **Baselines:** The LocoTransformer is compared against:
 - **State-Only:** Uses only proprioceptive states.
 - **Depth-Only:** Uses only visual observations.
 - **State-Depth-Concat:** Concatenates linearly projected visual features with proprioceptive features without a Transformer.
 - **Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning (HRL):** A baseline inspired by Jain et al. (2020), adapted for fair comparison.
 - **Vision-Guided Whole-Body Controller:** A classical control pipeline with a high-level visual RL policy guiding a low-level Model Predictive Control (MPC) and finite state machine (FSM) based controller.
- **Evaluation Metrics:** Mean episode return, distance moved, number of collisions per episode, and sphere reward (where applicable). Real-world evaluations also measure collision count and distance moved over 20-second runs.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings across simulated and real-world evaluations, supporting the efficacy of the LocoTransformer approach.

#### 4.1. Qualitative Analysis: Attention Maps

Visualization of the self-attention weights from the Transformer module revealed that the LocoTransformer learned to attend to task-relevant visual regions.
- In obstacle environments, the agent consistently focused attention on nearby obstacles in its path, suggesting an ability to perceive and plan avoidance maneuvers.
- In the Mountain environment, the agent exhibited alternating attention: first to the immediate terrain for stable foot placement, then to the distant goal destination for longer-term planning. This indicates a capacity for recognizing and utilizing important visual cues for decision-making.

#### 4.2. Navigation on Flat Terrain with Static Obstacles

- **Without Spheres:** Vision-enabled methods (LocoTransformer, HRL, State-Depth-Concat) significantly outperformed the State-Only baseline in both Thin Obstacle and Wide Obstacle environments, demonstrating the benefit of visual information. The Depth-Only baseline showed reasonable performance in simple environments, suggesting it could infer some proprioceptive information from vision. While LocoTransformer achieved comparable training performance to the simpler State-Depth-Concat baseline in these perceptually less complex scenarios, the difference became apparent in generalization.
- **Generalization Performance:** When policies trained on one obstacle type were evaluated on an unseen obstacle type, LocoTransformer exhibited superior generalization. It improved episode return by 69% (Wide Obstacle) and 56% (Thin Obstacle) over State-Depth-Concat, and by 257.6% and 118.2% over HRL. Quantitatively, LocoTransformer moved significantly farther on average and reduced collisions by 290.5% to 663% compared to baselines. The State-Depth-Concat baseline's generalization ability was observed to decrease over training, unlike LocoTransformer, which either plateaued or improved, suggesting better capture of essential environmental information and reduced overfitting.
- **With Spheres:** Introducing spheres, which increased environmental complexity by potentially luring the agent into traps or occluding vision, reduced sample efficiency for all methods. LocoTransformer consistently outperformed both HRL and State-Depth-Concat in final performance and sample efficiency, achieving comparable sphere rewards but longer distances. This indicates LocoTransformer's enhanced capability in modeling complex environments through spatial and cross-modal attention.

#### 4.3. Navigation with Moving Obstacles

In an environment with dynamically moving obstacles, both State-Only and Depth-Only baselines performed poorly. The HRL baseline performed worse than the State-Depth-Concat baseline. While State-Depth-Concat moved farther, LocoTransformer exhibited a more conservative policy, resulting in fewer collisions. This suggests that the Transformer's attention mechanism enabled better tracking and prediction of moving obstacles, facilitating effective avoidance.

#### 4.4. Navigation on Simulated Uneven Terrain (Mountain)

On rugged mountainous terrain, LocoTransformer significantly outperformed all baselines in episode return. The State-Depth-Concat baseline showed minimal improvement over State-Only, suggesting that a naive projection and concatenation of visual features can lead to a loss of critical spatial information. LocoTransformer's ability to better capture both global and local terrain characteristics contributed to its superior performance in these complex environments.

#### 4.5. Ablation Studies

- **Number of Visual Tokens:** Performance positively correlated with the number of visual tokens (N^2), indicating that richer low-level visual information, resulting from smaller receptive fields per token, benefited the model.
- **Number of Transformer Layers:** The model's performance was relatively insensitive to the number of Transformer encoder layers (L=1 or L=2 were found optimal).

#### 4.6. Real-World Experiments

Policies learned in simulation were successfully transferred to a Unitree A1 Robot and evaluated in indoor and forest environments.
- **Performance:** LocoTransformer significantly outperformed the State-Depth-Concat baseline in both scenarios. In the Indoor & Obs. environment, LocoTransformer moved 92% farther with fewer collisions. In the Forest environment, it moved approximately 90% farther without any collisions, whereas the baseline frequently collided with trees and got stuck.
- **Robustness and Generalization:** The real-world deployment demonstrated LocoTransformer's superior robustness and generalization to unseen, complex real-world obstacles and terrains, implying that it captured more fundamental object structures rather than overfitting to simulation appearances.
- **Comparison with Vision-Guided Whole-Body Controller:** An additional comparison showed that while a classical MPC-based whole-body controller could generate stable gaits, it struggled with agile obstacle avoidance. The end-to-end LocoTransformer policy demonstrated greater flexibility, smoother transitions between motions, and better planning for avoidance, leading to fewer collisions, especially in narrow or dynamic real-world scenarios.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research makes several contributions to the field of robotic locomotion and multi-modal reinforcement learning, with potential implications for future robotic applications.

#### 5.1. Advancement in Vision-Guided Legged Locomotion

The work demonstrates that incorporating high-dimensional visual information directly into end-to-end Reinforcement Learning policies significantly improves quadrupedal locomotion capabilities. By moving beyond "blind" agents that rely solely on proprioceptive feedback and domain randomization, the LocoTransformer enables robots to proactively perceive and navigate complex environments with obstacles and uneven terrains, a crucial step towards more autonomous and capable legged robots.

#### 5.2. Efficacy of Cross-Modal Transformers for Robotic Control

The successful application of a Transformer-based architecture for fusing proprioceptive and visual inputs highlights its effectiveness in sequential decision-making tasks within robotics. The LocoTransformer leverages the attention mechanism to enable sophisticated cross-modal reasoning, allowing the policy to simultaneously consider immediate robot status and distant environmental context. This approach could be extended to other multi-modal control problems in robotics.

#### 5.3. Enhanced Generalization and Sim-to-Real Transfer

A key challenge in robotics is the transfer of policies learned in simulation to the real world (sim-to-real). LocoTransformer demonstrated markedly superior generalization performance compared to baselines, not only across different simulated environments but also during real-world deployment. This improved generalization and robustness in unseen real-world scenarios is critical for practical applications of learned robot policies.

#### 5.4. End-to-End Learning Efficiency

By proposing an end-to-end learning framework that avoids the need for hierarchical control structures or pre-defined controllers, the research offers a potentially simpler and more integrated approach to learning complex locomotion skills. This simplification can reduce the manual engineering effort often required for robotic control systems.

#### 5.5. Interpretability through Attention Mechanisms

The ability to visualize attention maps provides a degree of interpretability to the learned policy. Observing how the robot's attention shifts to relevant visual regions (e.g., obstacles, goal locations) offers insights into its decision-making process, which can be valuable for debugging, policy analysis, and improving trust in autonomous systems.

#### 5.6. Foundation for Future Work

The findings suggest new avenues for research, particularly concerning the integration of higher-resolution visual inputs and the application of cross-modal Transformers to even more intricate and dynamic robotic tasks. This could lead to the development of robots with more advanced navigation, manipulation, and interaction capabilities in highly unstructured environments.
