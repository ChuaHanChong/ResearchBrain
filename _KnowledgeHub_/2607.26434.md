# Reinforcement Learning on Cost-Constrained Quadrupedal Hardware

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers demonstrated successful reinforcement learning-based locomotion on a $300 quadrupedal robot by employing an LSTM policy, which intrinsically managed 76 ms transport delays and noisy sensor feedback. This approach resulted in an emergent Central Pattern Generator, enabling robust locomotion with significantly less hand-tuning compared to traditional MLP methods.

## Problem

- Low-cost hardware, such as the $300 Mini Pupper 2, suffers from high transport latencies (>50 ms, specifically 76 ms) and noisy, incomplete motor feedback (position-only servos), creating a wide "sim-to-real gap".
- It is unclear whether to best address these sim-to-real bottlenecks through extensive engineering interventions or by selecting specific neural network architectures that can inherently cope with these constraints.
- Prior delay-aware reinforcement learning approaches for sim-to-real transfer have largely focused on high-frequency brushless actuator systems, leaving extreme low-cost brushed-servo delays largely unaddressed.

## Method

- A systematic comparison of various neural network architectures (==MLP==, **LSTM**, ==GRU==, ==Transformer==) was conducted using a calibrated `DelayedPDActuatorCfg` model in simulation to mimic the low-cost Mini Pupper 2's servo behavior.
- Two main deployment strategies were investigated: an MLP policy augmented with a hand-engineered ==synthetic proportional-derivative (PD) observer==, and an **LSTM** policy paired with a learned, stateless MLP ==servo model== for joint position prediction.
- Policies were trained using ==PPO== in NVIDIA Isaac Lab, and their performance was evaluated on training convergence, deployment complexity, robustness to additional delays, emergent gait characteristics, and qualitative sim-to-real performance on the Mini Pupper 2.

## Results

- The LSTM policy converged to a stable gait in simulation without curriculum, achieving a reward of **271** and requiring only one hardware deployment parameter (a single command gain), a reduction of over **80%** compared to the MLP's six or more parameters.
- The LSTM's emergent CPG generated a consistent **2.8 Hz** trot-like gait on hardware (mean R^2 = **0.77** sinusoidal fit) and maintained frequency and amplitude even with an additional **320 ms** observation delay.
- On the **$300** Mini Pupper 2, the LSTM policy deployed with the learned MLP servo model (which reduced joint prediction error from **0.1-1.8 rad** to **0.003-0.03 rad**) demonstrated robust and directionally compliant locomotion.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The LSTM architecture inherently manages high transport latencies and partial observability, achieving robust sim-to-real transfer with minimal hand-tuning, significantly outperforming MLPs, GRUs, and Transformers.
> - Time-aware LSTM policies, when trained under high delay, spontaneously develop emergent Central Pattern Generators (CPGs) that produce robust, rhythmic motor patterns adaptable to varied delays and terrains without explicit CPG training.
> - An effective two-layer control strategy combines an LSTM policy for long-horizon temporal tasks (gait generation, delay compensation) with a stateless MLP servo model for accurate, short-horizon prediction of joint dynamics.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has advanced the field of quadrupedal locomotion, enabling robots to exhibit robust gaits even in the presence of disturbances. However, much of this progress has been demonstrated on high-cost robotic platforms, such as those employing quasi-direct-drive or elastic actuators, which feature low latency and high control frequencies. These advanced systems typically cost between $10,000 and $150,000.

The broader research landscape, therefore, faces a challenge in replicating these advanced RL capabilities on more affordable hardware. Low-cost platforms, such as the $300 Mini Pupper 2, commonly utilize brushed DC position servos. These actuators introduce significant issues: notably, high transport latencies (e.g., a measured >50 ms, or specifically 76 ms for the Mini Pupper 2), noisy motor feedback, and often provide only position feedback, lacking direct velocity or torque measurements. These factors systematically widen the "sim-to-real gap," making policies trained in simulation difficult to transfer to physical hardware.

Explicitly modeling actuator latency is recognized as necessary for sim-to-real transfer, but prior methods have largely focused on high-frequency brushless systems. Existing delay-resolved RL approaches incorporate action history or disturbance-aware representations to address partial observability, yet few studies have systematically evaluated whether different neural architectures can intrinsically manage the partial observability arising from extreme hardware delays, or if extensive engineering interventions are always required.

This research integrates insights from biological sensorimotor systems, which also operate under significant sensory feedback delays (e.g., 100–160 ms) and limited corrective bandwidth. By framing the problem as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP), the authors explore how different neural network architectures might intrinsically cope with these limitations, aiming to expand the capability of reinforcement learning on cost-constrained hardware and thereby lower the barrier to entry for robotics research and development.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate methods for overcoming low-cost hardware constraints for RL-based quadrupedal locomotion, specifically focusing on the challenges introduced by high transport latencies and noisy, incomplete feedback. The work seeks to close the sim-to-real gap on platforms where these factors are prominent.

The core motivation stems from the high cost and complexity associated with deploying advanced RL policies on state-of-the-art robotic hardware. The authors aim to make sophisticated robotic locomotion more accessible by exploring solutions that do not rely on expensive hardware fidelity but rather on architectural and algorithmic robustness.

The central problem statement is articulated as: "On cost-constrained hardware, is the sim-to-real bottleneck best addressed through network architecture or through training and deployment engineering?" This question is broken down into two specific approaches:

1. **Model-based Observation Bridge:** This involves hand-engineering solutions, such as synthetic proportional-derivative (PD) feedback, to reconstruct missing or delayed state variables for a simpler, feedforward policy architecture (e.g., an MLP). This approach implies a higher burden on deployment engineering to compensate for hardware deficiencies.
2. **Temporal Network Learning:** This involves training a more complex, time-aware neural network (e.g., an LSTM) that can intrinsically learn to manage the delays and missing state dynamics internally, potentially reducing the need for extensive hand-engineered compensations during deployment.

The motivation for considering a time-aware network is rooted in the observation that the delays make the system a POMDP. Since the delay at each timestep can vary, the network needs to internally evaluate its temporal state to maintain robust locomotion. This situation mirrors challenges faced by biological sensorimotor systems, which also contend with significant feedback latencies and noisy information. The research posits that by taking a biologically inspired approach to handling noisy and delayed feedback, it might be possible to develop robust control policies for cost-constrained hardware.

Specifically, the authors are motivated to determine if such a complex architecture (like LSTM) is a worthwhile investment, or if simpler networks combined with engineering interventions are sufficient. They also seek to understand the implications of different recurrent architectures (LSTM, GRU, Transformer) in handling fixed k-step delays and partial observability, hypothesizing that architectures with specific inductive biases, like the LSTM's cell state, might be better suited for delay compensation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed a systematic approach to compare various neural network architectures for RL-based quadrupedal locomotion on a cost-constrained platform.

**A. Training Environment and Actuator Model**
All policies were trained in NVIDIA Isaac Lab (Isaac-Sim) using the `rsl_rl` proximal policy optimization (PPO) implementation. Training was conducted on a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU, utilizing 4098 parallel environments.
A calibrated `DelayedPDActuatorCfg` model was used to simulate the real servo behavior, with parameters matched to the identified delay and amplitude performance of the Mini Pupper 2's brushed DC position servos. Key parameters included:
- Stiffness (Kₚ) = 70, damping (K_d) = 1.2
- Friction = 0.03, armature = 0.005
- Transport delay: 33–43 physics steps at 500 Hz (simulating the 76 ms real-world delay)
- Effort limit = 5.0 Nm, velocity limit overridden to 10.5 rad/s

**B. Observation Space and Reward Shaping**
The observation vector was 60-dimensional, comprising: base linear velocity (3), base angular velocity (3), projected gravity (3), velocity commands (3), relative joint positions (12), joint velocities (12), joint efforts (12), and previous actions (12). The action scale was set to 0.5.
Reward shaping involved tightened tracking standard deviations (σ_lin = 0.1, σ_ang = 0.2) and various penalty weights: foot slip = -0.5, joint position = -0.7, joint torques = -5e-4, action smoothness = -1.0, and base orientation = -5.0.

**C. Network Architectures**
Four different neural network architectures were evaluated: Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM), Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU), and Transformer.
- All architectures shared a common MLP head for actor and critic networks, with layers and ELU activation.
- PPO hyperparameters were kept identical across all architectures (e.g., learning rate = 1e-3, γ = 0.99, λ = 0.95, clip ratio = 0.2, entropy coefficient = 0.005).
- For temporal architectures (LSTM, GRU, Transformer), a recurrent or attention module mapped observations to R^128 before the MLP head.
- Initial training was conducted without curriculum. If necessary, a multi-stage curriculum was introduced.

**D. Deployment Strategies for Hardware**
Deployment on the Mini Pupper 2 hardware addressed two main issues: (1) low-cost servos only providing position feedback (missing velocity/effort), and (2) a 76 ms transport delay and limited tracking bandwidth (degrading above 3 Hz).

- **MLP Deployment:** Required a synthetic PD observer to reconstruct hidden state variables. This involved a 5-step action delay buffer (200 ms at 25 Hz control), simulated PD dynamics with 4 substeps per policy step, and replacement of corrupt/noisy hardware readings with synthetic observations. Only 15 of 60 observation dimensions came from real sensors (25%), while 36 were synthetic (60%) and 3 were heuristic. This approach required tuning six or more parameters (Kₚ, K_d, inertia, delay steps, substeps, effort limit, bias scale, hardware scale).
- **LSTM Deployment:** Operated primarily open-loop at 25 Hz. Instead of real encoders, synthetic joint observations were generated by a learned, per-joint MLP servo network, driven by the policy’s actions. Only 6 of 60 dimensions came from real sensors (IMU angular velocity, projected gravity), with joint effort and base linear velocity zeroed. The primary tuning parameter was a hardware scale (0.75 in practice) applied to servo commands.
 - A two-layer architecture was developed: an LSTM policy for long-horizon temporal tasks (delay compensation, gait generation) and a stateless MLP servo model for short-horizon predicted joint positions. The MLP servo model was trained on 3 loops of open-loop playback data (390 samples per joint) to accurately predict joint positions.

**E. Evaluation Metrics**
Policies were evaluated on:
- **Training Convergence:** Reward achievement and stability in simulation, and curriculum requirements.
- **Deployment Complexity:** Number of hand-tuned parameters and reliance on synthetic observations.
- **Robustness to Delay Perturbation:** Performance when injecting additional observation delays (0, +160, +320 ms) into joint-state channels.
- **Gait Analysis:** Waveform analysis (sinusoidal fit, frequency, amplitude) and attractor dynamics (PCA projections of action and internal state spaces) to identify CPG-like behavior.
- **Observation Ablation:** Sweeping Gaussian noise into joint-state observations and comparing gait performance against zeroing those channels entirely to determine the utility of real-time sensor feedback.
- **Sim-to-Real Performance:** Qualitative assessment of locomotion on the Mini Pupper 2 hardware, including directional compliance and stability.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several key findings regarding architecture suitability, emergent behaviors, and deployment strategies for cost-constrained quadrupedal hardware.

**A. Architecture Comparison and Training Convergence**
- **LSTM:** Converged to a stable gait in simulation without requiring a multi-stage curriculum, achieving a reward of 271. It utilized fewer hardware deployment parameters (1 vs 6+) and relied on fewer real sensor observations (6/60 vs 15/60 for MLP with observer).
- **MLP:** Required a multi-stage curriculum, progressively increasing delay, penalty weights, and action scale, to achieve a comparable reward of 271 and multi-directional command tracking.
- **GRU and Transformer:** Failed to converge within the allocated training budget, even with curriculum. The GRU's failure suggested that simple oscillatory capacity was insufficient; the specific architectural capacity of a linear cell state pathway (as in LSTM) was important for delay compensation. The Transformer's failure was attributed to its need to learn temporal dependencies, in contrast to the LSTM's inductive bias for carrying delayed actions forward.

**B. Emergent Central Pattern Generator (CPG) in LSTM**
- The LSTM policy, despite being trained end-to-end with PPO on a standard locomotion reward, developed an emergent Central Pattern Generator (CPG). This CPG intrinsically generated rhythmic motor patterns that did not require continuous sensory confirmation for their fundamental rhythm. Instead, real IMU input modulated the pattern for balance and terrain adaptation.
- **Gait Waveform Analysis:** On hardware, the LSTM-generated gait exhibited a strong CPG structure. Sinusoidal fit (R^2) across all 12 joints yielded a mean R^2 = 0.77 on hardware (compared to 0.40 in simulation due to high-frequency corrective behaviors). All joints oscillated robustly at approximately 2.8 Hz, uniform across the robot, producing a trot-like pattern.
- **Attractor Dynamics:** PCA projections of the LSTM's cell state and action space revealed stable elliptical limit cycles that persisted across various delay conditions. This indicated an autonomous oscillator. In contrast, the MLP's hidden-layer activations distorted and its action-space orbits collapsed into flat bands under increasing delay, demonstrating its input-driven nature rather than intrinsic rhythmicity.
- **Generalization:** The emergent CPG generalized to out-of-distribution terrains (hills, troughs) in simulation without retraining, adapting via the IMU gravity vector.

**C. Robustness to Observation Delay**
- **LSTM:** Maintained a consistent 5.3 Hz gait frequency and amplitude (approx. -0.30 to 0.30 rad) across all tested extra observation delay conditions (0, +160, +320 ms added to the baseline 76 ms).
- **MLP:** Showed erratic frequency variation (6.6–7.5 Hz), lower amplitude commands (approx. -0.1 to 0.1 rad), and higher baseline jitter, with its amplitude collapsing to 0.16 rad at +320 ms delay.
- This demonstrated the LSTM's superior robustness to significant temporal perturbations due to its CPG-like internal dynamics.

**D. Sim-to-Real Deployment**
- **Observation Ablation:** An experiment sweeping Gaussian noise into joint-state observation channels revealed a crossover point at approximately σ ≈ 0.2 rad. Below this level, noisy real feedback was beneficial, but above it, zeroing the channels (i.e., ignoring feedback) preserved the gait better. Hardware measurements showed position feedback (σ = 0.101 rad) to be marginally useful, while velocity feedback derived from finite-differencing (σ = 3.17 rad/s) was significantly above the crossover, thus corrupting the observation loop. This led to the decision to zero velocity and effort channels for LSTM deployment.
- **Learned Servo Model:** The real servos on the Mini Pupper 2 exhibited behavior that diverged significantly from the analytic PD dynamics used in simulation training. To address this, a learned per-joint MLP servo model was developed. This model, trained on sparse open-loop playback data (390 samples per joint), reduced joint prediction error from 0.1–1.8 rad (analytic PD) to 0.003–0.03 rad, enabling smooth and sustainable locomotion sequences.
- **Optimal Deployment Architecture:** The research established a two-layer deployment architecture: the LSTM policy handles long-horizon temporal tasks (delay compensation, gait generation), while the stateless MLP servo model handles short-horizon servo dynamics prediction. An LSTM-LSTM configuration (recurrent policy with recurrent servo model) failed due to the servo LSTM overfitting on scarce data and accumulating drift during autoregressive rollout, which compounded errors in the policy's observation of its own actions.
- **Hardware Performance:** The LSTM policy, deployed open-loop with a single command gain (0.60–0.75) and an 8 Hz Butterworth low-pass filter on action output, exhibited robust and directionally compliant locomotion on the Mini Pupper 2. Minor hardware-related asymmetries caused a slight clockwise arc during forward walking, consistent across both MLP and LSTM deployments.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research provides a comprehensive demonstration of deploying learned control policies on cost-constrained quadrupedal hardware, specifically addressing the challenges of significant transport latencies and noisy, incomplete sensor feedback.

**A. Lowering the Barrier to Entry:** By successfully implementing RL-based locomotion on a $300 robot with a 76 ms transport delay, the work significantly lowers the financial and technical barrier to entry for robotics research. This accessibility can democratize contributions to the field, which has historically been reliant on expensive, high-fidelity hardware.

**B. Architectural Inductive Bias for Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The study highlights that the sim-to-real gap, particularly when dominated by communication latency rather than model fidelity, can be effectively addressed by selecting neural network architectures with appropriate inductive biases. The LSTM's recurrent cell state, acting as a shift-register for delay compensation, proved superior to MLPs (requiring extensive engineering) and other recurrent architectures like GRUs (lacking specific delay compensation capacity) or Transformers (computationally impractical). This suggests that matching architectural design to the temporal nature of the problem can reduce the need for extensive hand-engineering.

**C. Biological Inspiration and Emergent CPGs:** The finding that time-aware networks (LSTMs) exposed to similar biological constraints (high delay, noise) intrinsically converge to CPG-like solutions is significant. These emergent CPGs demonstrated robustness to out-of-distribution latencies and terrains without explicit CPG training. This suggests that the biological strategy of autonomous rhythm generation is a generalizable solution for rhythmic control tasks subject to substantial transport latency, extending beyond quadrupedal locomotion.

**D. Multi-Layer Control Strategy:** The optimal two-layer deployment architecture, combining an LSTM policy for long-horizon temporal reasoning with a stateless MLP servo model for short-horizon dynamics prediction, offers a principled approach to designing control systems for complex robotic tasks. This strategy emphasizes matching the time horizon and stability requirements for each control layer, recognizing that autoregressive drift in recurrent models can be detrimental for low-data, fast-dynamics tasks.

**E. Practical Deployment Insights:** The detailed analysis of observation noise, the identification of a crossover point where noisy feedback becomes worse than no feedback, and the development of a learned servo model are practical insights directly applicable to deploying RL on real-world, imperfect hardware. The minimalist tuning requirements for the LSTM deployment (a single hardware scale parameter) further underscore its practical advantages over MLP-based approaches.

In conclusion, this research provides both fundamental insights into how neural networks learn under extreme temporal constraints and practical guidelines for building robust and accessible robotic systems, with broad implications for the future of embodied AI.
