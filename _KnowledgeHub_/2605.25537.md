# Action-Prior Denoising for Smooth Real-Time Chunking

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers generalize training-time Real-Time Chunking (RTC) for robot control by introducing a continuous action-prior denoising objective, which models asynchronous execution more accurately. The approach, Soft RTC, achieves a solve rate of 0.809 (N=1) and reduces action jerk by 9.6% (N=2) while maintaining deployment efficiency.

## Problem

- Chunked policies in robot control face real-time execution challenges due to inference latency, leading to jerky motions, localized stalling, or abrupt transitions if chunks are not aligned.
- Existing training-time RTC formulations use a binary mask to distinguish between a fully fixed action prefix and an entirely unconstrained suffix, which oversimplifies the continuous nature of asynchronous chunk overlap.
- This binary distinction in generative models fails to leverage valuable prior information from the preceding action chunk for actions that are editable but should maintain proximity to the previous plan.

## Method

- **Soft RTC** generalizes training-time RTC's binary prefix conditioning into a continuous, token-wise action-prior denoising objective.
- It introduces an `e(d)` parameter to define an editable 'soft-conditioning window' within the chunk, between the committed prefix and the free tail.
- The method modifies the ==flow matching== training objective with token-wise weights `omega_j` that control the level of prior information used for each action, allowing for a gradual relaxation of constraints.

## Results

- Soft RTC (N=1) achieves an overall solve rate of 0.809, comparable to Train Hard RTC (0.815), while Soft RTC (N=2) reduces high-delay action delta by **9.1%** and jerk by **9.6%** relative to Train Hard RTC, maintaining near-naive runtime efficiency.
- Inference-time RTC baselines incur significant runtime overhead (**2.63x**-**2.68x** the naive runtime) for similar or lower solve rates compared to training-time RTC variants.
- Preliminary real-robot validation shows Train Soft RTC provides the lowest commanded-action finite-difference metrics (reducing second and third finite differences by **49.7%** and **52.3%** respectively) and the highest object throughput (5.21 objects/min) for a single-arm sorting task.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Reframing training-time RTC as an action-prior denoising problem with continuous, token-wise weights enables a more nuanced and accurate modeling of asynchronous chunk execution dynamics.
> - Soft RTC effectively exposes a controllable trade-off between task success and motion smoothness, allowing practitioners to tune policy behavior according to specific application needs without incurring deployment overhead.
> - Ablation studies reveal that the most effective improvements in smoothness for Soft RTC are achieved with very short soft-conditioning windows; extended windows tend to over-regularize and degrade task performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is positioned within the field of robot learning, specifically focusing on policies that utilize action chunking for control. Action chunking is a widely adopted strategy in learned robot control, where a policy predicts a sequence of future actions (a "chunk") rather than a single action per control step. This approach helps amortize the computational cost of model inference, improves temporal consistency, and enables larger generative policies to operate at relevant control rates. It is a fundamental component of various modern robot learning architectures, including transformer-based imitation policies like Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT), Behavior Transformer (BeT), VQ-BeT, and BAKU, as well as generative visuomotor policies such as Diffusion Policy.

A persistent challenge with chunked policies is the real-time execution problem, particularly when model inference introduces non-trivial latency. If the next chunk generation is not completed before the currently executing actions run out, the system must either pause (causing delays) or switch to a new, misaligned chunk (leading to abrupt, out-of-distribution transitions due to the new chunk being generated from a stale observation). On physical robots, such issues can manifest as undesirable jerky motions, localized stalling, and delayed or incomplete task execution.

Real-Time Chunking (RTC) was developed to mitigate this by overlapping chunk generation with execution. Inference-time RTC addresses this by conditioning the newly generated chunk on actions already committed by the previous chunk, often through an inpainting-style mechanism. While effective, inference-time RTC incurs additional computational overhead during deployment, which can exacerbate latency issues.

Training-time RTC emerged as a deployment-efficient alternative, simulating inference delay during the learning phase and conditioning the policy on the committed prefix of actions. This approach maintains the efficiency of ordinary policy execution during deployment, as it only involves a lightweight forward pass. However, existing training-time RTC formulations utilize a binary mask, which strictly differentiates between a fully fixed prefix and an entirely unconstrained suffix. This binary distinction does not fully capture the nuanced reality of asynchronous execution, where some early overlap actions are fixed, later overlap actions are still editable but should maintain proximity to the previous plan, and only the non-overlap tail is entirely free.

This paper addresses this limitation by proposing a generalization of training-time RTC. It draws parallels with generative models that denoise action sequences, such as flow-based models (e.g., Flow Matching, Consistency Policy), which RTC typically targets. The work contrasts with concurrent approaches like REMAC and A2C2, which introduce additional corrective modules at deployment. Soft RTC, instead, modifies the training-time conditioning structure of the base flow policy itself, aiming to provide a more integrated solution without additional runtime overhead. The Kinetix simulator, known for its dynamic and reactive physics-based control tasks, serves as the primary benchmark environment, emphasizing the method's relevance to dynamic execution and sensitivity to chunk-boundary artifacts.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to generalize the binary prefix conditioning used in conventional training-time Real-Time Chunking (RTC) into a continuous action-prior denoising objective. This generalization aims to better model the asynchronous execution of action chunks in real-time robotic systems while preserving the fast deployment characteristics of training-time RTC.

The motivation for this objective stems from an identified limitation in existing training-time RTC. While training-time RTC effectively simulates inference delay during learning and conditions on the committed action prefix, its underlying training objective employs a binary mask. This mask rigidly categorizes actions within a chunk into two distinct regions: a "fully fixed" prefix and a "fully free" suffix. This simplification does not accurately reflect the continuous nature of asynchronous chunk execution, which exhibits a more complex overlap geometry:
- **Irrevocably Committed Actions:** The earliest actions within the overlap region are already committed and cannot be altered.
- **Editable but Constrained Actions:** Later actions within the overlap region are still editable by the policy but should ideally remain consistent with the previous chunk's plan to ensure smooth transitions.
- **Fully Unconstrained Actions:** Only actions in the non-overlap "tail" of the chunk are entirely free for the policy to determine based on the latest observation.

From a generative model perspective, the binary mask approach is suboptimal. When a token falls within the overlap region, treating it as if it were unconstrained noise disregards valuable information available from the preceding action chunk. A smoother and more robust delayed controller, instead, should leverage this prior information, learning to denoise from it and gradually relaxing this prior as the token's position moves further from the irrevocably committed prefix.

Therefore, the central question guiding this research is: can the binary prefix conditioning of training-time RTC be extended to a continuous action-prior denoising objective without sacrificing the computational efficiency and fast deployment profile characteristic of training-time RTC?

The paper aims to answer this question by developing Soft RTC, a method that introduces a token-wise overlap profile. This profile specifies that early overlap tokens are perceived by the model as nearly fixed, later overlap tokens are denoised from partially prior-informed states, and only the free postfix tokens adhere to ordinary flow matching. This approach intends to achieve smoother chunk transitions by guiding the policy's learning process with a more nuanced understanding of action commitment and editability within the overlap region, all without requiring architectural modifications to the underlying policy or incurring additional computational costs at deployment.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, Soft RTC, generalizes training-time RTC by introducing a continuous, token-wise action-prior denoising objective. It operates within a chunked policy framework where an action chunk `A_t = [a_t..., a_{t+H-1}]` of horizon `H` is predicted from observation `o_t`. During rollout, `s <= H` actions are executed before replanning, leading to an overlap of `o = H - s` steps between consecutive chunks. The inference delay `d` signifies the number of actions from the previous chunk that must still be executed when the new chunk is generated.

Soft RTC introduces an additional parameter, `e(d)`, which defines the effective conditioning endpoint and determines the extent of the soft-conditioning window within the chunk. This contrasts with hard training-time RTC, which only considers the committed prefix `j < d`. This framework delineates three semantic regions within an action chunk:
1. **Committed prefix:** Actions at indices `j < d`, which are fully fixed.
2. **Soft-conditioning window:** Actions at indices `d <= j < e(d)`, which are editable but informed by an action prior.
3. **Free tail:** Actions at indices `j >= e(d)`, which are unconstrained and follow ordinary flow matching.

If `e(d) = d`, the soft-conditioning window vanishes, reverting to the hard prefix conditioning. If `d = 0` and `e(d) = 0`, the entire chunk is unconstrained, effectively recovering the standard no-RTC training objective.

**Action-Prior Denoising During Training:**
Soft RTC modifies the standard flow matching training objective. In standard flow matching, a neural network `v_theta` is trained to predict the vector field that maps a noisy interpolation between Gaussian noise `epsilon` and the target action chunk `A_t` towards `A_t - epsilon`. Soft RTC introduces token-wise weights `omega_j` (where `omega_j` is in ``) to control the conditioning level for each token `j`.

These weights define how corrupted the input `x_omega[j]` is for each token and how the flow time `tau_omega_j` is blended:
- For `j < d` (committed prefix), `omega_j = 1`. These tokens are fully clamped.
- For `d <= j < e(d)` (soft-conditioning window), `omega_j = g((j-d)/(e(d)-d))`. Here, `g` is a monotone decreasing schedule (linear in the main experiments), which causes the prior information to gradually relax as the token index moves away from the committed prefix.
- For `j >= e(d)` (free tail), `omega_j = 0`. These tokens are fully unconstrained.

The `e(d)` endpoint can be defined in two ways for ablation studies:
- **Delay-scaled endpoint:** `e(d) = min(ceil(lambda * d), h_max)`, where `lambda` is a delay multiplier and `h_max` is the maximum soft-conditioning horizon.
- **Offset endpoint:** `e_off(d) = min(d + L, h_max)`, where `L` is an absolute length after the hard prefix.

The training objective, `L_omega(theta)`, is formulated such that fully clamped tokens (`omega_j = 1`) are masked, while editable tokens (`omega_j < 1`) are trained with a flow-matching target. This allows the model to learn to denoise from a "prior-informed denoising state" (`x_omega[j]`) instead of pure noise for soft-overlap tokens. The amount of prior information is controlled by `omega_j`, which also influences `tau_omega_j`, the blended flow time. This mechanism encourages smoother chunk transitions by incorporating the previous action plan's continuity while allowing for adaptation based on the new observation.

**Inference Rule:**
At inference time, Soft RTC uses the same explicit Euler solver as the base flow policy, ensuring computational efficiency. The key modification is in how the denoiser inputs are prepared at each solver step. Given an observation `o_t`, a reference sequence `Y_t` from the previous chunk, and the realized delay `d`, Soft RTC performs the following steps iteratively for `T` flow steps:
1. Compute the endpoint `e(d)` and token weights `omega_j`.
2. Initialize `x_0` from Gaussian noise `N(0, I)`.
3. For each solver step `k` from `0` to `T-1`:
 - Calculate `t_k = k/T`.
 - Blend the current state `x_k[j]` with the prior `Y_t[j]` using `omega_j`: `tilde_x_k[j] = omega_j * Y_t[j] + (1 - omega_j) * x_k[j]`.
 - Blend the flow time `t_k` using `omega_j`: `tilde_t_k_j = omega_j + (1 - omega_j) * t_k`.
 - Update the state: `x_{k+1} = tilde_x_k + (1/T) * v_theta(o_t, tilde_x_k, tilde_t_k)`.

This inference rule ensures that soft-overlap tokens are continuously guided by the prior `Y_t` throughout the denoising process, rather than being denoised solely from noise. This prior injection helps maintain continuity with the previous chunk while simultaneously adapting the editable parts of the new chunk to the latest observation. The additional computational cost at deployment is limited to lightweight token-wise prior blending.

**Experimental Setup:**
The evaluation was conducted on all 12 large Kinetix levels using full-data expert demonstrations and pre-trained base flow-matching checkpoints. The methods compared include Base Naive, Inference Hard/Soft RTC (as baselines), Train Hard RTC, and Train Soft RTC (N=1 and N=2 operating points). Training-time variants were fine-tuned for one epoch from released base checkpoints. Metrics included task return, solve rate, action delta (mean L2 norm of consecutive action differences), and action jerk (mean L2 norm of the second finite difference). Efficiency was benchmarked by steady-state chunk-generation latency. A preliminary real-robot validation was performed on a single-arm sorting task, evaluating success, completion, throughput, commanded joint actions (finite differences), and policy-call latency.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides a comprehensive evaluation of Soft RTC across simulator and real-robot environments, demonstrating its capacity to offer a controllable trade-off between task performance and motion smoothness while maintaining deployment efficiency.

**Simulator Evaluation on Kinetix Levels:**
- **Performance and Efficiency Frontier (Table I, Figure 4):**
 - **Train Hard RTC** achieved the highest overall solve rate (0.815), serving as a task-performance-oriented baseline for training-time RTC. Its runtime remained close to naive execution (1.07x at batch 1, 1.01x at batch 32).
 - **Train Soft RTC (N=1)**, configured for performance preservation, achieved an overall solve rate of 0.809, which is 0.6 percentage points below Train Hard RTC. Critically, its runtime was nearly identical to naive execution (1.04x at batch 1, 0.95x at batch 32). Statistical analysis indicated heavily overlapping 95% bootstrap intervals for overall solve rate between Train Hard RTC and Train Soft RTC (N=1), suggesting no statistically clean success advantage for either but rather a close practical performance.
 - **Train Soft RTC (N=2)**, a smoothness-oriented operating point, showed a slight reduction in overall solve rate to 0.781. However, it significantly improved motion smoothness:
 - High-delay action delta reduced from 1.403 (Train Hard RTC) to 1.276 (9.1% reduction).
 - High-delay action jerk reduced from 2.416 (Train Hard RTC) to 2.185 (9.6% reduction).
 - This variant also maintained near-naive runtime (1.05x at batch 1, 0.95x at batch 32) and achieved a higher high-delay solve rate (0.702) than both Inference Hard RTC (0.693) and Inference Soft RTC (0.678).
 - **Inference-time RTC Baselines** (Inference Hard RTC, Inference Soft RTC) achieved solve rates of 0.783 and 0.778, respectively. However, they incurred substantial runtime overhead, requiring 2.63x and 2.68x the naive runtime at batch size 1, and 2.19x and 2.21x at batch size 32. Their high-delay jerk metrics were also higher than Train Soft RTC (N=2).
 - **Conclusion:** Soft overlap conditioning effectively exposes a performance-smoothness frontier, allowing for selection of an operating point that prioritizes either task success (N=1) or motion continuity (N=2) while retaining the deployment efficiency of training-time RTC.

**Preliminary Real-Robot Validation (Single-Arm Sorting, Table II):**
- **Task Outcome:** Both training-time RTC policies demonstrated improved task completion over the Base Naive policy.
 - Base Naive: 6/10 successful trials, 33/40 target placements completed, 3.18 objects/min throughput.
 - Train Hard RTC: 9/10 successful trials, 39/40 placements completed, 5.07 objects/min throughput.
 - Train Soft RTC: 8/10 successful trials, 37/40 placements completed, 5.21 objects/min throughput.
 - Qualitative observations indicated that the base policy sometimes exhibited back-and-forth oscillations and stalling, which were mitigated by the RTC-trained policies.
- **Motion Metrics:**
 - **Train Soft RTC** produced the lowest commanded-action finite-difference metrics, indicating smoother motion. Relative to Base, it reduced mean commanded second and third finite differences by approximately 49.7% and 52.3%, respectively. It also exhibited the smallest chunk-boundary action jump (0.0118).
 - **Train Hard RTC** also reduced second and third finite differences (by 37.1% and 39.7%) compared to Base but was less smooth than Train Soft RTC in this particular hardware run.
- **Latency:** Policy call latency for RTC-trained policies (approx. 87 ms) was higher than the base policy (60.6 ms), which was attributed to the deployed checkpoint/server configuration rather than an inference-time correction cost, as these are training-time RTC policies.
- **Conclusion:** Training-time RTC generally improved both task completion and command continuity on the real robot. Train Hard RTC achieved the highest physical completion, while Train Soft RTC provided the lowest commanded-action finite-difference metrics and highest object throughput among the tested policies.

**Ablation Studies (Figure 5):**
- **Delay-Scaled Window Sweep (Figure 5a):** This sweep confirmed that the delay-scaled N=1 rule preserved performance, while the N=2 rule offered the most favorable smoothness-oriented trade-off. Increasing the delay multiplier (`lambda`) beyond 2 further reduced jerk but significantly degraded solve rate. Fixed-length windows were less effective, with longer fixed windows being too aggressive and shorter ones underperforming delay-aware alternatives. This validated Soft RTC as a tunable method for a smoothness-performance frontier.
- **Offset-Length Sweep (Figure 5b):** This experiment isolated the effect of increasing the absolute length (`L`) of the soft-conditioning region after the hard prefix, with `L=0` recovering Hard RTC. As `L` increased, high-delay action delta and jerk decreased monotonically, but task return and solve rate degraded rapidly. Hard RTC (`L=0`) remained the strongest point for task performance. The most effective offset soft variant was `L=1`, showing modest improvements in smoothness with a noticeable but acceptable drop in solve rate. Longer windows (e.g., `L=2` and beyond) led to policies that were over-regularized and performed poorly on task success. This confirmed that while the soft-conditioning window is a valid control knob, its practical utility is confined to very short lengths.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work on Soft RTC presents a significant contribution to the field of learned robot control, particularly for policies operating under real-time constraints and utilizing action chunking. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Principled Generalization of Training-time RTC:** Soft RTC offers a theoretically sound and practically effective method to generalize the binary prefix conditioning of existing training-time RTC. By reframing it as an action-prior denoising problem with continuous, token-wise overlap weights, the method provides a more accurate and nuanced model of asynchronous action chunk execution. This move away from a rigid fixed/free dichotomy addresses a fundamental limitation in prior work.

2. **Exposing a Controllable Smoothness-Performance Frontier:** A key impact of Soft RTC is its ability to expose a continuous trade-off between task success and motion smoothness. Unlike previous methods that often optimize for one aspect, Soft RTC introduces a tunable design parameter (e.g., the delay-scaling multiplier `lambda`). This allows practitioners to select an operating point that aligns with specific application requirements—whether prioritizing peak task completion or demanding exceptionally smooth, low-jerk motion, all within the same policy framework. This flexibility is particularly valuable in real-world robotics where "good enough" performance with high smoothness might be preferred over marginally higher success with jerky motions.

3. **Enhanced Deployment Efficiency:** Crucially, Soft RTC retains the computational efficiency and fast deployment profile characteristic of training-time RTC. Unlike inference-time RTC variants, which introduce significant runtime overhead due to inpainting or complex online guidance mechanisms, Soft RTC's additional operations at inference are lightweight token-wise prior blending. This makes it a viable solution for real-time robotic systems where maintaining low latency during deployment is paramount and cannot be compromised by additional online computation.

4. **Improved Motion Quality and Reliability:** The preliminary real-robot validation, while limited, provides evidence that training-time RTC, and Soft RTC specifically, can lead to tangible improvements in physical task completion and command continuity. By mitigating issues like oscillations, local stalling, and inconsistent chunk handoff (which arise from latency and stale actions), Soft RTC can contribute to more reliable and physically safer robot operation. The observed reductions in commanded-action finite differences (delta and jerk) are direct indicators of improved motion quality, which translates to smoother and potentially more robust robot behavior in dynamic environments.

5. **Practical Design Guidance:** The ablation studies, especially the offset-length sweep, provide valuable practical insights. They demonstrate that while soft conditioning is beneficial, the "useful" part of the smoothness-performance frontier is surprisingly steep and confined to very short soft-conditioning windows. This serves as a critical design lesson for future policy development, guiding researchers and engineers to focus on short, targeted soft-conditioning regions rather than extended ones that quickly over-constrain the learned controller.

6. **Advancement in Generative Model Application:** The work reinforces the utility of generative models, particularly flow-based policies, in robot control. By demonstrating how their denoising objective can be adapted to incorporate complex real-time execution constraints, Soft RTC pushes the boundaries of how these powerful models can be effectively deployed in sensitive, latency-aware robotic applications.

In summary, Soft RTC provides a refined approach to real-time chunking that addresses the complexities of asynchronous execution with a tunable, efficient, and effective action-prior denoising strategy. Its impact lies in offering a practical pathway to more robust, smoother, and deployable learned robotic controllers without sacrificing the benefits of chunked action prediction.
