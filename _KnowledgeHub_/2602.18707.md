# CLASH: Collision Learning via Augmented Sim-to-real Hybridization to Bridge the Reality Gap

> [!summary] Summary
> CLASH builds a hybrid simulator for contact-rich robotics by distilling MuJoCo collision dynamics into a differentiable, parameter-conditioned MLP surrogate, then adapting it with about 10 real collisions via gradient-based contact-parameter identification plus small-step early-stopped fine-tuning. Plugged into MuJoCo as a collision module, it raises post-impact prediction accuracy, cuts CMA-ES search time 42-48%, and doubles real-world sequential pushing success.

## Problem

- Physics engines relax collision solving with linearized friction cones or penalty contacts for speed, producing angular-velocity and post-impact trajectory errors that block direct policy transfer.
- Learned residual or black-box simulator augmentations need large real-world datasets, and fitting a network on a handful of real collisions overfits and generalizes poorly.
- Differentiable simulators suffer unstable contact gradients and mesh-complexity limits, while parameter-identification methods still roll out through the engine, leaving unmodeled effects uncorrected.

## Method

- ==Simulation distillation==: a three-layer ==MLP== surrogate maps pre-collision state plus contact parameters (friction coefficients, restitution damping ratios) to post-collision state, trained by ==MSE== on 100,000 simulated collisions.
- Two-stage real adaptation: frozen-weight ==gradient descent== on the contact parameters identifies them from real impacts, then 10 early-stopped fine-tuning steps absorb residual non-parametric mismatch without overfitting.
- **CLASH** plug-in hybrid simulator: MuJoCo handles all dynamics but hands off to the adapted surrogate whenever mesh-mesh collision is detected, serving ==CMA-ES== model-based search and ==SAC== policy training.

## Results

- Prediction accuracy at **0.1** relative error reaches **0.76** on the semi-cylinder versus **0.64** for MuJoCo and **0.42** for a network trained on real data only; square and triangle also improve.
- Real-world CMA-ES striking cuts position error **35.10%**, **26.58%**, and **26.29%** on semi-cylinder, square, and triangle, while wall-clock search drops from about **27** to **16** seconds.
- SAC policies trained in the hybrid simulator double Route 1 success (**8/10** versus **4/10**) and reach **5/10** on Routes 2 and 3 versus MuJoCo's **2/10** and **1/10**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Pretraining a surrogate on simulator rollouts converts a physics engine into a reusable prior, making 10 real samples enough where direct real-data fitting collapses into overfitting.
> - Splitting the sim-to-real gap into an interpretable identified-parameter part plus a small learned residual beats pure residual learning on data efficiency and interpretability.
> - Replacing only the least-accurate simulator submodule can be faster than the original solver, but untouched effects like torsional friction still cap orientation accuracy.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of robotic policies heavily relies on simulation environments due to their efficiency, scalability, and safety compared to real-world data collection. However, a significant challenge, known as the "sim-to-real gap," often prevents policies trained in simulation from directly transferring to physical robots. This gap stems from various factors, including unmodeled physical effects, sensing latencies, and inaccurate parameter identification in simulators.

Within this broader context, the accurate modeling of contact-rich dynamics, particularly collisions, is identified as a persistent challenge. Physics engines commonly used in robotics, such as MuJoCo, PyBullet, and NVIDIA Isaac Lab, prioritize computational speed and stability, often at the expense of modeling fidelity for complex, discontinuous events like collisions. They frequently employ approximations like penalty-based contacts or linearized friction cones to manage the computational complexity of non-linear complementarity problems that govern rigid-body collisions with friction. These approximations lead to discrepancies in post-impact states, affecting angular velocity changes, frictional dissipation, and subsequent trajectories, which are critical for many manipulation tasks like pushing or striking.

Various strategies have been explored to bridge the sim-to-real gap. These include:
- **System identification:** Methods like those in, and attempt to accurately estimate physical parameters of objects and environments. While effective for some parameters, they may not capture all unmodeled real-world effects.
- **Domain randomization:** Involves training policies across a wide range of simulated parameters to improve robustness, but its effectiveness can be limited if policies rely heavily on specific environmental parameters.
- **Domain adaptation:** Focuses on aligning features or visual representations between simulation and reality.
- **Augmenting simulators with learned models,:** This approach involves using neural networks to learn residual dynamics or directly model aspects of the simulation that are difficult to capture with traditional physics. A key challenge in this area is the typically large amount of real-world data required for training these models. Differentiable simulators like MJX and Warp offer gradient-based optimization for system identification and learning, but can face trade-offs in efficiency, numerical stability, or fidelity for complex contact interactions.

CLASH contributes to the area of augmenting simulators with learned models, specifically targeting collision dynamics. It distinguishes itself by emphasizing data efficiency in leveraging real-world data and by integrating a differentiable surrogate model that distills physical priors from an existing simulator. This approach aims to provide high accuracy with a limited real-data budget, in contrast to methods that require extensive real-world data or struggle with the stability of differentiable physics for complex contacts. The work represents an advancement in creating hybrid simulation environments that are both more accurate and computationally efficient for robotics tasks involving significant contact interactions.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the accuracy of robot policies trained in simulation by effectively bridging the sim-to-real gap, with a specific focus on contact-rich dynamics, particularly collisions.

The motivation for this work arises from several factors:
- **Reliance on Simulation:** Modern robotic strategy development increasingly depends on simulation environments. These platforms offer advantages in terms of cost, safety, and the ability to generate diverse experiences rapidly and at scale. This makes them indispensable for pre-training complex policies before their deployment on physical robots.
- **Persistence of the Sim-to-Real Gap:** Despite advancements in simulation capabilities, the direct transfer of policies from simulation to physical robots remains challenging. Discrepancies arise from unmodeled physical phenomena, sensing limitations, and inaccuracies in identified parameters, collectively contributing to the "sim-to-real gap."
- **Inaccuracy of Collision Modeling:** Collisions are identified as a particularly challenging aspect of physics simulation. Their discontinuous nature and sensitivity to factors like friction make them difficult to model accurately and efficiently. Conventional physics engines, in an effort to maintain real-time performance, often employ simplifications such as penalty-based contact models or linearized friction cones. While computationally efficient, these relaxations introduce errors in critical post-impact outcomes, including changes in angular velocity, frictional energy dissipation, and object trajectories. Since many robot manipulation tasks (e.g., pushing, striking, grasping) inherently involve or are significantly affected by collision dynamics, inaccuracies in their simulation directly impede successful policy transfer.
- **Limitations of Existing Solutions for Data Efficiency:** While various methods exist to address the sim-to-real gap (e.g., system identification, domain randomization, simulator augmentation), many data-driven approaches require substantial amounts of real-world data. Acquiring such data is often costly, time-consuming, and can pose safety risks. Approaches based on purely learning residual dynamics can suffer from model bias or require extensive data. Differentiable simulators, while offering benefits for gradient-based optimization, can encounter issues with efficiency, numerical stability, or fidelity when dealing with complex contact interactions.

To address these motivations, CLASH proposes a framework that seeks to:
- **Develop a data-efficient method:** Learn an accurate collision model using only a minimal number of real-world interactions.
- **Leverage simulated priors:** Distill a base model from an imperfect simulator (like MuJoCo) using large-scale simulated collisions. This step aims to capture foundational physical priors without requiring real-world data for initial learning.
- **Enable efficient adaptation with limited real data:** Utilize gradient-based techniques to identify critical contact parameters from a small set of real collisions. This is followed by targeted, small-step fine-tuning of the learned model to correct remaining discrepancies between simulation and reality, while explicitly avoiding overfitting on scarce data.
- **Integrate a hybrid simulator:** Seamlessly embed the adapted collision model as a plug-in module within a standard physics engine. This approach is intended to provide higher post-impact prediction accuracy while potentially reducing the computational time associated with collision-heavy simulations.
- **Improve downstream task performance:** Demonstrate that policies derived from this hybrid simulator exhibit more robust transfer to the real world, leading to higher success rates in both reinforcement learning and model-based control tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

CLASH (Collision Learning via Augmented Sim-to-real Hybridization) is a data-efficient framework designed to improve collision modeling within physics simulators by integrating a learned surrogate model. The methodology involves a three-stage procedure: simulation distillation, system identification and adaptation, and hybrid simulation.

#### 3.1. System Overview

The core idea is to augment a standard physics simulator by learning a dedicated collision surrogate model. Collisions are defined as short-duration impulsive contacts between objects, distinct from continuous sliding interactions. The framework assumes that post-impact velocities result from a combination of object-object impulses and ground reactions when ground contact is maintained.

#### 3.2. Training Surrogate Base Model (Simulation Distillation)

1. **Parameterization:** The system parameters, denoted as P = (μ, e), include friction coefficients (μ) and damping ratios (e, derived from MuJoCo’s `solref` parameter for restitution behavior). These parameters cover both object-object and object-ground contacts.
2. **Simulation Data Generation:** A large dataset of collision pairs, D_sim = {(S_pre, P, S_post)}, is generated in a physics simulator (MuJoCo). For each sample, experimental parameters (P), collision velocity, and impact point are randomized to define a pre-collision state (S_pre). The simulator then executes the collision, yielding the corresponding post-collision state (S_post).
3. **Model Architecture:** A three-layer Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), denoted f_θ(S_pre, P), is trained as the surrogate base model. This network takes the pre-collision state and system parameters as input and predicts the post-collision state. The focus is on mapping pre- to post-collision velocities.
4. **Optimization:** The MLP is optimized by minimizing the Mean Squared Error (MSE) between its predictions and the simulated post-collision states over D_sim. This process distills the non-linear collision dynamics approximated by the simulator into a differentiable neural network, capturing its implicit physical priors.

#### 3.3. System Identification and Fine-tuning (Real-world Adaptation)

1. **Real-world Data Collection:** A small dataset of real-world collisions, D_real = {(S_pre, S_post)}, is collected using a motion capture system. Parameters are assumed constant during data collection for consistency.
2. **Ground Friction Identification:** For simplicity, friction coefficients between objects and the ground are determined using linear regression based on observed sliding trajectories from the motion capture data.
3. **Parameter Identification:** For the remaining collision-related parameters (e.g., object-object friction and damping), the pre-trained, differentiable surrogate base model (f_θ) is leveraged. With the network weights fixed, these parameters are optimized using gradient-based methods to minimize the MSE loss (L_sys) between the model's predictions and the real-world post-collision states in D_real. This step identifies P_real, the optimal system parameters that best fit the real data through the lens of the pre-trained model.
4. **Fine-tuning:** To further reduce the sim-to-real gap, the surrogate base model's network weights (θ) are fine-tuned using D_real with the identified P_real parameters fixed. To prevent overfitting on the limited real-world data, small-step and early-stopped gradient updates (e.g., 10 iterations) are applied. This adaptation allows the model to learn data-driven residuals that account for unmodeled real-world effects while maintaining the structural priors from the original simulator.

#### 3.4. Hybrid Simulator

1. **Integration:** The adapted collision model is integrated into an existing physics simulator (e.g., MuJoCo) as a plug-in module, replacing its default collision computation.
2. **Operation:** Whenever a collision between two meshes is detected by the simulator, the control is transferred to the learned neural network to predict the post-collision state. For all other dynamics (non-collision phases), the original physics engine continues to operate. This modular design allows seamless integration.
3. **Applications:** The resulting high-fidelity hybrid simulator can be used in two main paradigms:
 - **Model-based Optimization:** Gradient-free optimization algorithms (e.g., CMA-ES) can utilize the hybrid simulator to discover effective open-loop control inputs for single-shot manipulation tasks, benefiting from its increased accuracy.
 - **Reinforcement Learning (RL):** The hybrid simulator provides a more physically plausible training environment, which is expected to improve sim-to-real policy transfer for RL algorithms by reducing the discrepancies between the simulated and real dynamics.

#### 3.5. Experimental Setup

Experiments were conducted using MuJoCo for simulation and a 7-DOF Franka robotic arm for real-world interactions. A motion capture system with 16 cameras recorded real-world data. Objects included a 3D-printed impact block and various target objects, notably a semi-cylinder due to its anisotropic collision behavior. Simulation data consisted of 100,000 collision pairs. Real-world data comprised 110 collisions per setting (across 3 materials and 2 surface conditions), with 10 samples used for training/adaptation and 100 for testing. The MLP had three layers with 128 units and ReLU activations. Training involved 1,000 steps of simulation pre-training, 1,000 steps of parameter optimization, and 10 steps of fine-tuning, all using AdamW.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of the CLASH framework across various evaluations: collision model accuracy, model-based optimization, and reinforcement learning.

#### 4.1. Collision Model Accuracy

The CLASH-trained collision model was evaluated against three baselines: MuJoCo (with grid-searched parameters), MJX (a differentiable simulator), and a neural network trained directly on limited real-world data without simulation pre-training. Prediction accuracy was defined as the proportion of test samples where the relative error between predicted and ground-truth post-collision states was below a threshold α (e.g., 0.1).

- **Superior Performance:** The CLASH model consistently exhibited higher prediction accuracy across all six object-environment interaction settings. For a semi-cylinder (with α=0.1), CLASH achieved an accuracy of 0.76, significantly outperforming MuJoCo (0.64) and the directly trained neural network (0.42).
- **Addressing Baseline Limitations:** MJX, while differentiable, faced stability issues with complex mesh geometries (e.g., semi-cylinder), requiring geometric simplifications that introduced large sim-to-real discrepancies. The direct neural network baseline suffered from severe overfitting and poor generalization due to the scarcity of real-world training data. CLASH avoided these issues by distilling physical priors from large-scale simulation data and then adapting with limited real data, leading to robust performance.

#### 4.2. Model-Based Optimization

The CLASH-trained hybrid simulator was applied to a model-based optimization task where a Franka arm was used to hit a block (semi-cylinder, square, or triangle) to reach a target position and orientation. CMA-ES was used to search for optimal impact parameters within the simulator, which were then executed on the real robot.

- **Reduced Positioning Error:** The hybrid simulator (Ours) significantly reduced positioning errors in real-world executions compared to MuJoCo: 35.10% for the semi-cylinder (0.0159m vs 0.0245m), 26.58% for the square (0.0163m vs 0.0222m), and 26.29% for the triangle (0.0185m vs 0.0251m).
- **Improved Orientation for Semi-Cylinder:** For the semi-cylinder, orientation error was also notably reduced (0.4152 rad vs 0.7882 rad). However, orientation errors for the square and triangle remained comparatively large, which the authors attributed to challenges in accurately modeling torsional-friction effects for these geometries.
- **Computational Efficiency:** Replacing the default collision resolution with the learned model within the hybrid simulator substantially accelerated the CMA-ES wall-clock time. For instance, the average time decreased from approximately 25-28 seconds (MuJoCo) to 13-15 seconds (Hybrid Simulator, Ours), representing a 42-48% reduction, while delivering improved accuracy. Analysis indicated that MuJoCo captured linear velocity decay reasonably well but struggled with angular velocity decay.

#### 4.3. Reinforcement Learning with Hybrid Simulator

A reinforcement learning task involved training a policy to push a semi-cylinder along sequential segments to a target destination, requiring accurate collision and sliding dynamics while maintaining object orientation. A hierarchical approach was used, training a low-level collision policy with SAC for straight segments. Policies were trained in both MuJoCo and the CLASH-trained hybrid simulator and evaluated on three different routes in the real world.

- **Higher Success Rates:** Policies trained using the CLASH-trained hybrid simulator consistently achieved higher Success Rates (SR) and Sub-Goal Completion Rates (SGCR) across all tested routes compared to policies trained with MuJoCo alone. For Route 1, the SR doubled from 4/10 (MuJoCo) to 8/10 (Hybrid Simulator, Ours). Similarly, for Route 3, SR improved from 1/10 to 5/10, and SGCR improved from 13/30 to 21/30.
- **Improved Sim-to-Real Transfer:** These results indicate that the hybrid simulator's more accurate collision predictions lead to policies that transfer more reliably from simulation to the real world, underscoring the importance of high-fidelity collision modeling for complex sequential decision-making tasks in RL.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The CLASH framework represents a significant contribution to the field of robotics, particularly in addressing the long-standing sim-to-real gap, which remains a primary impediment to the widespread deployment of simulation-trained robot policies.

The primary significance of this work lies in its **data-efficient approach to improving simulation fidelity for contact-rich dynamics**. By leveraging large-scale simulated data to distill physical priors into a differentiable surrogate model and then fine-tuning this model with only a handful of real-world interactions (e.g., 10 samples), CLASH offers a practical solution to a common bottleneck. This efficiency in real-data usage is crucial, as real-world data collection is often costly, time-consuming, and carries safety risks. This makes the framework highly applicable for scenarios where extensive real-world experimentation is not feasible.

The **demonstrated improvements in post-impact prediction accuracy** are fundamental. By creating a hybrid simulator that integrates the learned collision model as a plug-in module, CLASH provides a simulation environment that more closely mirrors real-world collision behaviors than standard physics engines like MuJoCo. This higher fidelity directly translates into more reliable predictions, which is critical for tasks sensitive to precise contact interactions.

The **positive impact on downstream robotic tasks** is a key outcome.
- For **model-based control**, the hybrid simulator not only enables the discovery of more effective control inputs that transfer robustly to physical systems but also significantly reduces the wall-clock time for collision-heavy optimization tasks (e.g., a 42-48% reduction in CMA-ES search time). This dual benefit of accuracy and speed can accelerate the development and deployment of precise manipulation strategies.
- For **reinforcement learning (RL)**, policies trained within the CLASH hybrid simulator exhibit superior sim-to-real transfer, evidenced by a doubling of success rates in complex sequential pushing tasks. This indicates that a more accurate underlying physics model leads to the development of more robust and generalizable RL policies, which is essential for translating learned behaviors from controlled simulated environments to the variability of the real world.

Furthermore, the **modular and reusable nature** of the CLASH framework is impactful. Its plug-and-play design suggests that this methodology can be extended to address other known modeling gaps within physics engines, such as torsional friction, rotational dissipation, or compliant contact effects. This offers a generalized recipe for targeted upgrades to existing simulators, allowing researchers and practitioners to focus corrections where modeling errors are most critical.

Potential future impacts of this work include:
- **Accelerated Robotics Development:** By reducing the barrier of entry for accurate simulation and reliable sim-to-real transfer, CLASH can accelerate the iterative design, training, and testing cycles for new robotic systems and complex manipulation skills.
- **Enhanced Robot Capabilities:** More accurate collision modeling could enable robots to perform intricate tasks involving dynamic contacts, such as assembly, dexterous manipulation in cluttered environments, or human-robot interaction where understanding collision consequences is paramount.
- **Foundational for Future Research:** The paper identifies clear avenues for future work, including extending the framework to other dynamics, reducing dependence on the base simulator's initial fidelity, and incorporating scalable geometric representations for broader generalization across object shapes. These directions suggest CLASH could serve as a foundational piece for developing even more advanced, general-purpose hybrid physics engines.

In conclusion, CLASH offers a practical, data-efficient, and demonstrably effective solution to a critical problem in robotics, paving the way for more reliable sim-to-real transfer and ultimately enabling more capable and robust robotic systems.
