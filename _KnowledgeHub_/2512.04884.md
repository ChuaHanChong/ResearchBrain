# Hoi! - A Multimodal Dataset for Force-Grounded, Cross-View Articulated Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers introduced the "Hoi!" dataset, a large-scale multimodal collection of human and robot interactions with articulated objects in diverse environments, featuring synchronized visual, pose, force, and tactile data. This dataset revealed that state-of-the-art methods for articulation estimation, tactile force prediction, and visual force estimation show significant performance degradation in unconstrained, real-world scenarios compared to their reported performance on existing benchmarks.

## Problem

- A "data gap" exists between human-centric video datasets (long-horizon activities) and robotics datasets (short-horizon manipulation primitives), hindering cross-domain transfer.
- Existing articulated object datasets often lack paired motion data, force/tactile measurements, multi-view coverage, or cross-embodiment correspondence necessary for grounded physical interaction research.
- Prior video datasets generally lack integrated multimodal sensing (e.g., forces, tactile cues) crucial for robust physical manipulation and for understanding the relationship between visual appearance and physical interaction.

## Method

- Developed the "Hoi!" dataset, comprising 3048 sequences of human interactions with 381 articulated objects, captured from multi-view RGB-D, proprioceptive, and haptic (force/tactile) modalities.
- Designed a custom "Hoi!" gripper with integrated force-torque and GelSight Digit tactile sensors, allowing capture of robot-grade physical sensing during both human and robotic manipulation.
- Implemented a structured data collection protocol across diverse indoor environments, utilizing high-resolution laser scans for precise 3D scene ground truth and rigorous temporal/spatial alignment of all sensor streams.

## Results

- ArtiPoint's recall on "Hoi!" dropped to 26.9% for prismatic and 57.1% for revolute articulation types, while GPT-5 achieved 71.9% prismatic and 89.7% revolute recall for type prediction from egocentric views.
- Sparsh models for tactile force estimation yielded combined force RMSEs of 3.86 N (DINO) and 4.11 N (DINOv2), a substantial increase from millinewton-level accuracy on their original benchmarks.
- ForceSight's visual force estimation showed an RMSE of 2.23 N for projected forces and 2.57 N for raw forces on "Hoi!", a noticeable degradation from its 0.404 N RMSE on its original dataset.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - State-of-the-art articulation estimation methods, like ArtiPoint and ArtGS, struggle to generalize in cluttered, unconstrained real-world environments, showing significant performance drops (e.g., ArtiPoint's prismatic recall dropped from 68% to 26.9%).
> - Tactile force estimation models, such as Sparsh, exhibit increased error (RMSE of several Newtons) when exposed to out-of-distribution contact geometries and load regimes characteristic of real-world articulated interactions.
> - Visual force estimation models, like ForceSight, show degraded performance (overall RMSE of 2.23 N for projected forces) on the "Hoi!" dataset, particularly for objects requiring higher operating forces, indicating limited exposure to stiff or force-demanding mechanisms in previous datasets.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of computer vision has been transitioning from purely perceptive tasks to dynamic and interactive domains, with a growing focus on interpreting object utility and human-object interactions. This progression has been supported by data-driven methods and the development of large-scale datasets that capture diverse human activities. However, the authors identify a discrepancy in the nature of interactions studied across different research areas. Human-centric video datasets, such as Ego4D or Epic-Kitchens, typically focus on long-horizon activities like cooking or furniture assembly. In contrast, robotics datasets predominantly address short-horizon manipulation primitives, such as pick-and-place or simple drawer opening. This divergence creates a "data gap" that hinders the investigation of cross-domain transfer questions, such as the generalization of interaction-force predictors to human videos or the retargeting of human-demonstrated interactions to robotic grippers.

Articulated furniture, despite its ubiquity in daily life, represents a relatively understudied area in human-robot interaction research. While it is emerging as a tractable challenge in robotics, perception remains a significant obstacle to further progress. Existing datasets for articulated objects, such as those providing static scans or object models, often lack the paired motion data necessary to ground annotations in real interactions. Some datasets, like RBO, offer real RGB-D sequences of human manipulation with limited force measurements, but they are typically small-scale and do not provide multi-view or multi-embodiment coverage.

In the domain of video understanding, egocentric benchmarks have advanced action recognition and activity understanding but generally lack information on applied forces or contact feedback, limiting their direct translation to physical robotic manipulation. Although recent efforts in robotics leverage large video collections to improve policy learning and demonstrate that representations learned from human video can accelerate robot policy learning, these approaches are often restricted to tabletop settings with a low domain gap between demonstration and deployment. They frequently rely primarily on visual inputs, omitting the multimodal richness (e.g., forces and tactile cues) that is crucial for robust physical manipulation. While studies like ForceMimic and RH20T have shown the value of incorporating multimodal signals, particularly force measurements, for robotic manipulation performance, prior video datasets generally lack the integrated multimodal sensing or cross-embodiment correspondence needed for grounded manipulation research.

The "Hoi!" dataset is introduced to address these gaps by jointly capturing vision, force, and tactile signals from both human and robotic embodiments performing identical articulated interactions. This design aims to enable systematic study of the relationship between visual appearance and physical interaction patterns, and to facilitate the transfer of manipulation skills from human demonstrations to robot morphologies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to introduce a dataset that bridges the observed discrepancy between human-centric and robot-centric interaction data by providing force-grounded, cross-view articulated manipulation sequences. This dataset is designed to couple visual observations with "what is done" (actions) and "what is felt" (forces and tactile feedback) during real human interactions.

The key motivations driving the creation of the Hoi! dataset are:
1. **Enabling Multimodal Interaction Understanding**: To provide a rich source of synchronized data across visual (RGB, depth, multi-view), proprioceptive (pose), and haptic (force, tactile) modalities. This comprehensive data aims to foster research into how these modalities interact during manipulation.
2. **Facilitating Cross-Embodiment Transfer Learning**: To allow researchers to investigate how manipulation skills demonstrated by humans can be effectively transferred to robotic systems. This is achieved by recording interactions with the same articulated objects under multiple embodiments, including human hands and various robotic grippers.
3. **Grounding Perception in Physical Interaction**: To move beyond purely visual perception by incorporating detailed force and tactile sensing, thereby enabling the development of methods that can predict and utilize physical interaction forces for robust manipulation.
4. **Addressing Limitations of Existing Datasets**: To overcome the shortcomings of current articulation datasets, which often lack paired motion data, force/tactile measurements, multi-view coverage, or the correspondence between human and robot interactions in complex, "in-the-wild" environments.
5. **Anchoring Interactions to Scene Geometry**: To provide accurate 3D geometric context for interactions by capturing high-resolution laser scans of environments before and after manipulation. This allows for precise spatial grounding of actions and observations.
6. **Supporting Benchmarks for Embodied AI**: To establish a foundation for new benchmarks in areas such as force-from-vision prediction, articulation parameter estimation, cross-view and cross-embodiment transfer, and state-change prediction, which are critical for advancing embodied artificial intelligence.

In summary, the authors' contributions include the development of a novel data capture pipeline utilizing a custom handheld robotic gripper for recording in-the-wild interaction forces, the creation of a large-scale multimodal dataset with extensive annotations, and the provision of interaction-centric annotations supporting benchmarks in various aspects of manipulation learning and embodied reasoning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The "Hoi!" dataset comprises 3048 sequences of human interactions with 381 articulated objects across 38 diverse indoor environments, totaling approximately 48 hours of interaction data. The methodology involves a structured data collection protocol, specialized hardware, and precise temporal and spatial alignment procedures.

#### 3.1. Dataset Scope and Embodiments

The dataset focuses on common articulated household objects such as drawers, doors, fridges, and dishwashers. Each object interaction is captured under four primary embodiments:
1. **Human hand**: Direct human manipulation.
2. **Human hand with a wrist-mounted camera**: Human manipulation with an additional egocentric perspective.
3. **Handheld UMI gripper**: Manipulation using an antipodal gripper designed for universal robot teaching.
4. **Custom Hoi! gripper**: Manipulation with a specially designed gripper equipped with force-torque and tactile sensing.
A small subset of interactions is also recorded using a teleoperated Boston Dynamics Spot robot with body cameras and a wrist-mounted Aria device.

#### 3.2. Multimodal Sensor Suite

Interactions are recorded from multiple synchronized viewpoints and sensor streams:
- **Egocentric View**: Provided by Project Aria glasses, capturing RGB video, SLAM data, eye gaze, and human hand pose.
- **Exocentric Views**: Two static iPhone 13 Pro devices capture third-person RGB video and LiDAR depth.
- **Wrist-Mounted Views**: A ZED Mini stereo camera and an additional Project Aria device are mounted on the Hoi! gripper, providing RGB-D observations and pose tracking from the end-effector perspective. A GoPro camera is used for the UMI gripper.
- **Force and Tactile Sensing**: The custom Hoi! gripper integrates a Bota SensONE 6-DoF force–torque sensor at the wrist and two opposing GelSight Digit sensors on its fingers for high-resolution tactile imaging.
- **3D Scene Ground Truth**: A Leica RTC360 laser scanner is used to capture high-resolution 3D point clouds of each environment both before and after manipulation. These scans provide ground-truth geometry, including static structures and articulation-induced geometry changes, serving as a shared reference frame for spatial calibration.

#### 3.3. Hoi! Gripper Design

The custom-designed Hoi! gripper (Fig. 3) is a 2-finger parallel gripper featuring an antipodal mechanism. It is equipped with:
- **Tactile Sensors**: Two GelSight Digit sensors for capturing high-resolution contact pressure maps.
- **Force-Torque Sensor**: A Bota SensONE 6-DoF force–torque sensor measures interaction forces at the wrist.
- **Actuation**: Powered by a Dynamixel XM430-W350-T motor, with gripping force modulated via a calibrated load cell in its handle during human operation.
- **Onboard Perception**: A wrist-mounted ZED Mini stereo camera and Project Aria device provide visual and pose data.
The gripper system runs on a battery-powered NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano, enabling mobile data collection. The system undergoes full calibration and gravity compensation to isolate interaction forces from internal biases and gravitational effects.

#### 3.4. Data Collection Protocol

Data collection primarily occurred in a furniture store exhibition area, supplemented by recordings in a university lab and private apartments, to ensure realistic "in-the-wild" conditions. Seven human demonstrators performed all interactions.
- **Session Structure**: Each recording session focused on 3 to 11 articulated parts under a single manipulation condition.
- **Synchronization**: A dynamic QR code encoding the current Unix timestamp was displayed at 25 Hz to all camera streams for initial temporal alignment. A static QR code shown to one exocentric camera marked the start of each individual interaction.
- **Interaction Procedure**: Operators performed alternating open/close sequences for all articulated parts.
- **3D Scanning**: For each environment, the Leica RTC360 laser scanner captured 2-5 point clouds, including an unarticulated scene state and states with various parts opened.

#### 3.5. Data Alignment and Annotation

- **Time Alignment**: Post-processing aligns all sensor streams, which run on independent internal clocks. QR codes with Unix timestamps in video streams allow calculation of time offsets relative to a common reference (Aria egocentric camera). This procedure achieves a temporal alignment accuracy of approximately 10-25 ms.
- **Pose Data Generation**: Aria Machine Perception Services (MPS) generate 6-DoF device poses, hand poses, and eye gaze from egocentric Aria streams. For wrist cameras and the Hoi! gripper, attached Aria devices and extrinsic calibrations provide sensor poses. The UMI gripper's GoPro camera is tracked using ORB-SLAM3 with global refinement by GTSAM. Static iPhone camera poses are directly measured.
- **Spatial Alignment**: All recording devices are spatially aligned into a common reference frame using visual localization against the high-resolution 3D laser scans. Dense 2D-3D correspondences are established from the point clouds and panoramic images. High-quality keyframes are selected based on feature density, sharpness, and scene depth, and their 6-DoF poses are estimated using hloc. Robust estimation yields a single rigid transformation aligning each sensor trajectory to the world frame, with accuracy evaluated against Qualisys motion capture data (RMSE position 0.005-0.006m, RMSE rotation 0.012-0.016 rad).
- **Annotations**: Interactions are manually verified after automatic video cutting. Articulation type (prismatic or revolute) and axis are annotated using a tool based on. This tool was extended to include 3D masks of object parts (generated by prompting SAMv2 on panoramic images and lifting to 3D) and language descriptions.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates the utility of the Hoi! dataset through three benchmark tasks designed to assess embodied object understanding: articulated object estimation, tactile force estimation, and visual force estimation.

#### 4.1. In-the-Wild Articulated Object Estimation

This task evaluates the ability of methods to perceive how articulated parts move and estimate their kinematics.
- **Methods Evaluated**: ArtiPoint, ArtGS, and GPT-5 (as a Vision-Language Model for articulation type inference). Depth inputs for Aria were generated using MapAnything and scaled to metric using 3D scene meshes.
- **Results**:
 - **Performance Degradation**: Both ArtiPoint and ArtGS exhibited significantly lower performance on the Hoi! dataset compared to their reported performance on other datasets like Arti4D. For instance, ArtiPoint's recall for prismatic articulation types dropped from 68% (on Arti4D) to 26.9% (on Hoi!), and for revolute from 98% to 57.1%.
 - **Reasons for Degradation**: ArtGS struggled in "in-the-wild" scenarios due to environmental clutter and non-robust object segmentation. ArtiPoint's performance was hampered by noise introduced by scaled monocular depth, which affected its 3D lifting and trajectory filtering.
 - **VLM Performance**: GPT-5 demonstrated surprising robustness in articulation *type* prediction from single RGB views, achieving 71.9% prismatic recall and 89.7% revolute recall from egocentric views.
- **Conclusion**: Current articulation estimation methods appear to be highly dependent on accurate depth information or fail to generalize effectively in cluttered, unconstrained real-world environments with diverse conditions like those in Hoi!.

#### 4.2. Tactile Force Estimation

This task investigates the prediction of normal and tangential forces from gel-based tactile images captured by the Hoi! gripper's GelSight Digit sensors.
- **Ground Truth**: Ground truth forces were derived from temporally aligned force-torque sensor readings and gripper motor-induced gripping forces. Forces were decomposed into normal and tangential components and expressed in an interaction frame. To ensure fair comparison, ground truth magnitudes were clipped to align with the training range of the evaluated model.
- **Method Evaluated**: Two versions of Sparsh, a state-of-the-art method for self-supervised tactile representation learning, using DINO and DINOv2 decoders with a force-estimation head.
- **Results**:
 - **Increased Error**: Sparsh models, which achieved millinewton-level accuracy on their original benchmark, exhibited RMSEs on the order of several Newtons on the Hoi! dataset (e.g., combined force RMSE of 3.86 N for DINO and 4.11 N for DINOv2).
 - **Attributed Causes**: This degradation was primarily attributed to out-of-distribution contact geometries (complex shapes of real handles and furniture parts versus the simple indenters used in training) and out-of-distribution load regimes naturally arising during unconstrained human operation.
- **Conclusion**: Tactile models that perform well in controlled laboratory settings demonstrate significant limitations when generalizing to real-world, unconstrained interactions, highlighting the challenging nature of the Hoi! data.

#### 4.3. Visual Force Estimation

This task evaluates the ability of a model to predict 3D interaction forces and affordances from an RGB-D observation, given a manipulation goal.
- **Ground Truth**: Per-image force–torque measurements from the Hoi! gripper, language goals derived from annotations, and 3D ground-truth trajectories were used. Results were reported for both raw and motion-aligned (projected onto the gripper's linear and rotational velocity directions) force signals to mitigate operator-induced disturbances.
- **Method Evaluated**: ForceSight, a model that predicts force goals for text-guided manipulation. The evaluation was conducted in a zero-shot setting.
- **Results**:
 - **Performance Drop**: ForceSight, which achieved an RMSE of 0.404 N on its original dataset, showed a noticeable performance degradation on Hoi!, with an overall RMSE of 2.23 N for projected forces and 2.57 N for raw forces.
 - **Context-Dependent Failures**: The model struggled particularly in environments with articulated objects requiring higher operating forces, such as kitchens with fridges and ovens (e.g., kitchen\_7 RMSE of 3.531 N) or offices with magnetic drawers (e.g., office\_1 RMSE of 2.325 N).
 - **Benefit of Motion Alignment**: Using projected forces and torques resulted in an improvement in performance, indicating the model's sensitivity to real-world operational disturbances present in raw measurements.
- **Conclusion**: The degradation suggests that existing models and datasets have limited exposure to stiff or force-demanding articulated mechanisms, an underrepresented class in prior work.

#### 4.4. Additional Findings (from Supplementary Material)

- **Multimodal Learning for Real-World Robotics**: A force-centric experiment using the Hoi! dataset to train a visual force predictor integrated into a Boston Dynamics Spot robot's position-force controller demonstrated that the learned force prior substantially improved real-world success rates on stiffer objects (e.g., from 0% to 80% for an oven and 0% to 60% for a dishwasher), highlighting the dataset's utility for multimodal skill learning.
- **Hand Pose Estimation**: An exploratory analysis of hand pose estimation using the method by Pavlakos et al. on Aria MPS data showed an overall PCK@0.15 of 0.699, which is lower than that on more controlled benchmarks. This indicates the challenging nature of egocentric manipulation scenes in the dataset, characterized by fast hand motions, natural behaviors, and lower-resolution imagery.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Hoi! dataset represents a contribution to the fields of computer vision, robotics, and embodied AI by directly addressing a key gap between human-centric and robot-centric interaction data. Its significance and potential impact stem from several aspects:

1. **Bridging the Human-Robot Embodiment Gap**: By providing synchronized, multimodal recordings of the same articulated interactions performed by both human hands and robotic grippers, the dataset creates a unique foundation for understanding how human manipulation skills can be effectively transferred to robots. This facilitates research into creating agents that can learn from human demonstrations and adapt those skills to their own morphology.

2. **Force-Grounded Multimodal Perception**: The integration of rich force and tactile sensing alongside visual and pose data is critical. This enables the development and evaluation of methods that infer and utilize physical interaction forces, which are essential for robust manipulation in unstructured environments. The dataset allows for investigation into previously underexplored modalities, correlating visual cues with haptic feedback.

3. **Challenging Benchmarks for State-of-the-Art Methods**: The evaluations presented in the paper demonstrate that current state-of-the-art methods for articulation estimation, tactile force prediction, and visual force estimation show significant performance degradation when applied to the Hoi! dataset's real-world, unconstrained scenarios. This highlights the limitations of existing models in handling clutter, diverse contact geometries, varied load regimes, and noisy real-world data, thereby establishing new, more realistic benchmarks for future research.

4. **Enabling Generalizable Manipulation and Embodied Reasoning**: By focusing on object-centric understanding—how an object affords manipulation, how it moves, and the physical effort required—the dataset supports the development of more generalizable manipulation policies. This moves research beyond simple pick-and-place tasks toward agents that can truly understand and act upon the physical world in diverse, everyday environments.

5. **Catalyst for Future Research Directions**: The identified limitations of current methods provide clear avenues for future work. This includes developing more robust models for articulation estimation that can handle varying depth quality and clutter, tactile models that generalize to complex contact geometries and varied loads, and visual force estimation models that are resilient to real-world disturbances and capable of handling a wider range of object stiffnesses and interaction forces. The dataset also lays the groundwork for advancing end-to-end policy learning, where perception and action are trained jointly.

6. **Novel Hardware Contribution**: The custom-designed Hoi! gripper, capable of capturing robot-grade physical sensing during human demonstrations, is itself a notable contribution that enables the unique data collection paradigm. Its open-source release will further support community research.

In conclusion, the Hoi! dataset is poised to accelerate progress in embodied AI by providing a comprehensive, force-grounded, and cross-embodiment dataset that bridges critical gaps in current research data. It offers a platform for developing agents that can not only observe but also physically interact with and understand the complexities of articulated objects in real-world settings.
