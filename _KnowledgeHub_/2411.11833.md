# Differentiable GPU-Parallelized Task and Motion Planning

> [!summary] Summary
> cuTAMP is the first GPU-parallelized task-and-motion planner. It treats each candidate plan skeleton as a continuous constraint satisfaction problem and optimizes thousands of particles in parallel with differentiable cost functions, seeded by composed parallel samplers and ordered by a feasibility heuristic. It solves tightly constrained packing problems in seconds where serial sampling planners fail entirely.

## Problem

- Sampling-based TAMP generates each continuous parameter independently and composes by rejection, so tightly interacting constraints like packing demand impractically many samples before one combination survives.
- Optimization-based TAMP solves highly non-convex constrained programs from random initializations, so single serial attempts are costly and often stall in local optima without any feasible solution.
- Prior planners explore one candidate solution at a time and cannot cheaply judge whether a chosen plan skeleton's constraint system is infeasible before wasting refinement effort.

## Method

- ==Parallelized differentiable optimization==: a batch of thousands of particles, each a full assignment of a skeleton's continuous variables, is jointly updated by Adam on relaxed constraint-plus-cost objectives.
- Compositional particle initialization: parallelized samplers for grasps, batched inverse kinematics, and trajectories solve constraint subgraphs in DAG order to seed particles near the solution manifold, with subgraph caching reused across skeletons.
- Plan feasibility heuristic: counts particles satisfying each constraint and heavily penalizes constraints with zero satisfying particles, ordering the skeleton queue and pruning skeletons containing proven-empty constraint subgraphs.

## Results

- On 5-block Tetris packing, sampling solved 0/50 trials even with 4096 particles; **cuTAMP** reached **50/50** in **12.2 seconds**, and Optuna-tuned cost weights hit 50/50 in **5.4 seconds**.
- Optimizing goal cost lowered best packing cost to **44.6** versus **60.0** for pure sampling; on Bookshelf, subgraph caching solved in **1.52 seconds** versus 7.22 seconds for sampling.
- Ablations show uniform-random initialization without sampling is **14x** slower on single-object packing, and only **0.3%** of optimized Tetris particles satisfy every constraint, justifying huge batches.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Parallelism turns a fragile local optimizer into a reliable one: many independently seeded descents cover multiple basins, so success rate climbs with batch size.
> - Initialization matters more than optimizer sophistication; plain first-order Adam suffices once particles start near the solution manifold via composed constraint-subgraph samplers.
> - Batched satisfaction counts double as a cheap infeasibility signal for discrete search, though hand-tuned cost weights generalize unevenly and can overfit one domain.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Long-horizon robot manipulation necessitates integrated reasoning across discrete decisions (e.g., which objects to interact with) and continuous decisions (e.g., specific grasps, placements, and trajectories). This integrated reasoning falls under the domain of Task and Motion Planning (TAMP). TAMP problems are known for presenting significant computational challenges, particularly as the complexity of tasks and the number of interacting constraints increase. This complexity can lead to extensive runtimes and difficulties in achieving high-quality solutions, especially when the solution space is highly constrained and non-convex.

Current TAMP algorithms generally fall into two categories:
- **Sampling-based approaches:** These methods typically decouple continuous parameters, generating samples for each independently. These samples are then composed and evaluated against collective constraints, often through rejection sampling. A limitation of this approach is that when constraints interact tightly, numerous samples may be required to find a feasible solution, leading to inefficiency.
- **Optimization-based approaches:** These methods formulate constraints as analytic functions within a mathematical program, then employ first- or second-order gradient descent to solve for continuous parameters. A primary challenge with this strategy is the highly non-convex nature of such mathematical programs, which possess multiple local optima. This characteristic can make it difficult to locate even a single feasible solution from arbitrary initializations.

Prior research in parallelized motion planning has explored using multiprocessing, vectorization, or GPU acceleration for fundamental operations like forward kinematics, collision checking, and parts of motion planning algorithms (e.g., probabilistic roadmaps and trajectory optimization, as seen in cuRobo). However, these efforts primarily address motion planning, which is a sub-component of the broader TAMP problem.

This work contributes to the TAMP landscape by proposing a novel bilevel algorithm that addresses the computational inefficiencies of existing methods. It integrates elements from both sampling-based and optimization-based TAMP, but critically, it introduces a GPU-parallelized framework. The paper identifies a gap in previous work by being the first to apply massively parallelized differentiable optimization to TAMP, specifically enabling the simultaneous exploration and refinement of thousands of candidate continuous solutions. By leveraging GPU acceleration, the approach aims to overcome the limitations of serial algorithms regarding both solution finding efficiency and quality in complex, constrained environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the computational challenges inherent in Task and Motion Planning (TAMP), particularly in long-horizon robot manipulation tasks involving tight, non-convex constraints. Existing serial TAMP approaches, whether sampling-based or optimization-based, often face limitations that hinder their scalability and efficiency in real-world applications. Sampling-based methods can be computationally intensive when many samples are rejected due to interacting constraints, while optimization-based methods struggle with non-convexity, making them prone to converging to local optima or requiring excessive time to find feasible solutions from random starts.

Against this background, the key objectives of the proposed cuTAMP algorithm are detailed as follows:

1. **Develop a Bilevel TAMP Algorithm Utilizing GPU Parallelism:** The overarching objective is to design and implement a TAMP algorithm that operates in a bilevel manner, separating discrete task planning from continuous motion and parameter planning. The core innovation is to infuse this process with GPU parallelism to enable the simultaneous exploration of a large number of candidate continuous solutions.
2. **Efficiently Explore Continuous Solution Spaces:** The work aims to leverage GPU parallelism to sample an initial batch of solution seeds for a given plan skeleton. This initial sampling phase is designed to populate the parameter space near potential solution manifolds, thereby providing more effective starting points for subsequent optimization.
3. **Apply Differentiable Optimization for Constraint Satisfaction and Cost Minimization:** A central objective is to apply differentiable optimization techniques to the batch of sampled particles. This involves formulating plan constraints and soft objectives as differentiable cost functions. The goal is to iteratively update these particles using gradient-based methods to satisfy plan constraints and minimize solution costs efficiently.
4. **Achieve Rapid Solutions for Highly Constrained Problems:** The research aims to demonstrate that, through massive parallelism, the proposed algorithm can effectively solve highly constrained TAMP problems, including those with non-convex constraints, within short timeframes (e.g., seconds). This objective directly addresses the runtime limitations of serial approaches.
5. **Substantially Outperform Serial TAMP Approaches:** A quantitative objective is to demonstrate that cuTAMP yields significant improvements over serial TAMP methods across several metrics: increasing the number of satisfying solutions found, reducing overall algorithm runtime, and enhancing the quality of the solutions obtained.
6. **Validate on Real-World Robots:** The final objective is to empirically validate the practical applicability and performance of the algorithm by deploying it on physical robot platforms, showcasing its fast planning capabilities for long-horizon manipulation problems in real-world scenarios.

By pursuing these objectives, the research seeks to advance the state-of-the-art in TAMP, making it more computationally tractable and robust for complex robotic tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

cuTAMP operates as a sequence-then-satisfy Task and Motion Planning (TAMP) algorithm. This approach involves two primary stages: first, searching over discrete plan skeletons, and second, for each candidate skeleton, solving the induced continuous Constraint Satisfaction Problem (CSP). A distinguishing feature of cuTAMP is its extensive use of GPU parallelism throughout both stages of continuous parameter exploration and optimization.

### 4. A. cuTAMP Algorithm Overview

The algorithm proceeds in two main stages:

- **Stage 1: Initialize Plans and Particles:** The system first searches for an initial set of `N_s` plan skeletons using a forward best-first search strategy, which incorporates continuous variables into the search tree. For each skeleton `π_i`, an initial batch of `N_b` candidate continuous solutions, termed "particles" `P_i`, is generated through a parallelized sampling process. A `PLANHEURISTIC` is computed for each `(π_i, P_i)` pair, and these are added to a priority queue `Q` ordered by their heuristic values.
- **Stage 2: Loop Over Skeletons and Optimize:** The algorithm iteratively selects plan skeletons and their associated particles from the priority queue `Q`. The particles are then subjected to parallelized differentiable optimization. If a satisfying solution is found within the batch, it is returned. Otherwise, the plan heuristic is recomputed for the optimized particles, and the skeleton (with its updated particles) is pushed back into `Q`. Additionally, new plan skeletons and particles are sampled and added to `Q` to continue the search.

### 5. B. Parallelized Constraint Satisfaction

A core component of cuTAMP is its method for solving the continuous CSP induced by a plan skeleton `π`. This CSP is transformed into an unconstrained optimization problem:

- **CSP as Optimization:** The objective is to find an assignment `x` to the continuous parameters that satisfies all constraints `c ∈ con(π)`, where each constraint `c` is defined by a differentiable real-valued function `J_c(x_param(c))` with a tolerance `ϵ_c`. A particle `x` is satisfying if `J_c(x_param(c)) ≤ ϵ_c` for all constraints.
- **Objective Function:** Hard constraints are relaxed into soft costs. The objective function `J(x)` is a weighted sum of these relaxed hard constraint costs and any soft action costs associated with the plan (Equation 2).
- **Gradient-Based Optimization:** A batch of `N_b` particles `P` is optimized simultaneously. The algorithm minimizes the mean cost `J_batch(P)` over all particles using a gradient-based optimizer (Adam). Gradients are computed efficiently via automatic differentiation. Optimization continues until a stopping criterion (e.g., max time, satisfying particle found) is met.
- **GPU Parallelization:** To handle large batches of particles efficiently, continuous variable assignments are stacked into matrices. Vectorized versions of cost functions are implemented using PyTorch. The approach leverages cuRobo's differentiable collision checkers and kinematics models, which utilize custom CUDA kernels for both forward pass computation and gradient calculation. This enables fast, parallel evaluation of kinematic and collision constraints, producing smooth, informative gradients.

### 6. C. Particle Initialization

To mitigate the problem of local minima in the highly non-convex optimization landscape, cuTAMP employs a compositional sampling strategy for particle initialization:

- **Compositional Sampling:** Instead of random initializations, parallelized samplers are used to generate batches of `N_b` assignments that are "near the solution manifold." These samplers solve subgraphs of the overall constraint network.
- **Sampling Network:** The generations from multiple samplers are composed, forming a directed acyclic graph (DAG) representing dependencies between samplers. For instance, a grasp sampler might generate grasps, which then condition a robot configuration sampler (using parallelized inverse kinematics), which in turn conditions a trajectory sampler.
- **Sample Reuse:** To reduce redundant computation, outputs of samplers are cached and reused across different plan skeletons that share common subgraphs.

### 7. D. Approximating Plan Skeleton Feasibility

To guide the discrete search over plan skeletons and avoid computationally expensive refinement of infeasible skeletons, cuTAMP utilizes a plan feasibility heuristic:

- **Heuristic `H(π, P)`:** This heuristic estimates the feasibility of a plan skeleton `π` by evaluating the number of satisfying particles for each constraint `c` in the initial batch `P`. If no particles satisfy a constraint, a large negative penalty `Λ_penalty` is applied. This measure (Equation 5) provides an indication of how likely a skeleton is to be satisfiable.
- **Pruning Skeletons:** The algorithm identifies constraints that are "likely unsatisfiable" (zero satisfying particles after sampling). New plan skeletons containing these unsatisfiable constraint subgraphs are pruned from consideration by the task planner, saving computational effort. Periodically, particles for failed subgraphs are re-initialized to confirm unsatisfiability, and pruned skeletons can be re-added if a counterexample (satisfying particle) is found.

### 8. E. Theoretical Analysis

A simplified version of cuTAMP, where particles are initialized independently and randomly with positive probability density and without the plan skeleton feasibility heuristic, is proven to be probabilistically complete. This indicates that for robustly feasible problems, the algorithm will find a correct solution with probability one in finite time.

### 9. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of cuTAMP was conducted on a range of simulated TAMP problems with varying levels of complexity, from simple object packing to highly constrained multi-object arrangements and tool-use scenarios. The performance was compared against serial sampling-based (SAMPLING) and optimization-based (OPTIMIZATION) baselines, both effectively run with a single particle (`N_b=1`). Ablation studies examined the impact of particle batch size (`N_b`), cost weights (`λ`), and subgraph caching.

### 10. A. Solving TAMP Problems with Obstruction

- **Single Object Packing:** In this relatively simple task, all methods successfully found solutions. SAMPLING with small `N_b` was sufficient. OPTIMIZATION with uniform random initialization performed poorly at `N_b=1` (4/30 coverage), underscoring the importance of effective initialization, though its performance improved with larger batch sizes. cuTAMP and SAMPLING achieved full coverage and the fastest solution times (around 0.1-0.16s for optimal `N_b` values between 64 and 1024), indicating that particle initialization via conditional sampling contributes significantly to efficiency.
- **Bookshelf Problem:** This task involved packing books, potentially requiring moving an obstacle. cuTAMP variants demonstrated a 45% faster runtime than SAMPLING and achieved full coverage with smaller batch sizes. The plan feasibility heuristic effectively prioritized skeletons that involved moving the obstacle. Subgraph caching improved cuTAMP's runtime by 28%, speeding up particle initialization across skeletons.

### 11. B. Optimizing Goal Costs

When minimizing the goal distance between four objects, cuTAMP with explicit goal cost optimization substantially outperformed SAMPLING in reducing the final objective cost. While SAMPLING could find satisfying solutions, its ability to achieve tight packing (low distance cost) was limited. The experiments showed that while increasing the weight `λ` for the goal cost could lead to lower final costs, it could also decrease the number of satisfying particles if the goal cost conflicted with hard constraints. This highlights the trade-off between strict constraint satisfaction and soft objective minimization.

### 12. C. Solving Highly-Constrained Problems (Tetris)

The Tetris domain, involving packing 5 non-convex blocks into a tight region, presented a highly constrained and complex problem with a 90-dimensional parameter space.
- SAMPLING completely failed to find solutions (0/50 coverage) even with large batch sizes.
- Untuned cuTAMP successfully found solutions (e.g., 50/50 coverage at `N_b=4096` in ~12 seconds), demonstrating the effectiveness of combining sampling and parallel differentiable optimization. This indicates that optimizing thousands of candidate solutions in parallel is crucial for such difficult problems, even if only a small fraction (0.3%) of optimized particles are ultimately satisfying.
- Automated tuning of cost weights using Optuna significantly improved performance. Tuned cuTAMP more than halved the solution time (e.g., from ~12s to ~5.4s for `N_b=1024`) and nearly tripled the number of satisfying particles, suggesting that optimized cost weighting can further enhance the algorithm's robustness and efficiency.
- Runtime analysis showed that cuTAMP's runtime remained relatively constant for batch sizes up to 512 or 1024 particles before scaling linearly, affirming the benefits of GPU parallelism.

### 13. D. Efficiently Searching over Plan Skeletons (Stick Button)

In the Stick Button problem, a robot (Franka) needed to press buttons, some of which were out of direct reach, necessitating the use of a stick tool. This scenario involved numerous infeasible plan skeletons.
- cuTAMP autonomously inferred the need to use the stick, leveraging the plan feasibility heuristic to avoid optimizing likely infeasible skeletons. The accuracy of this heuristic increased with larger batch sizes.
- cuTAMP with subgraph caching was over 3 times faster than SAMPLING, emphasizing the efficiency gains from caching and reusing sampled subgraphs. Subgraph caching significantly accelerated particle initialization and the transition to the optimization phase.
- The system also demonstrated cross-embodiment generalization, implicitly adapting to the kinematics of different robots (Franka vs. UR5), where the UR5 could directly reach all buttons without the stick.

### 14. E. Real Robot Experiments

cuTAMP was successfully deployed on a UR5 arm and a Kinova Gen3 robot.
- It achieved tight object packing by minimizing goal distances.
- It autonomously reasoned to move obstructing objects out of the way for stacking tasks.
- It supported joint optimization of grasps, placements, and full robot trajectories parameterized as knot points.
- These real-world demonstrations validated the algorithm's fast planning capabilities (seconds from perception to action).

### 15. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of cuTAMP represents a notable advancement in the field of Task and Motion Planning (TAMP) for robotics. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Enhanced Computational Efficiency and Scalability:** cuTAMP is presented as the first GPU-parallelized TAMP algorithm, directly addressing a critical bottleneck in existing serial TAMP approaches. By enabling the simultaneous exploration and optimization of thousands of candidate continuous solutions, the method significantly reduces planning runtime, particularly for complex and highly constrained problems. This improved efficiency allows for faster deployment and iteration in robotic systems.
- **Improved Solution Quality and Robustness:** The integration of compositional sampling for particle initialization with differentiable, gradient-based optimization offers a robust strategy for navigating highly non-convex solution spaces. This combination helps overcome the limitations of single-shot optimization (prone to local minima) and pure sampling (inefficient for tight constraints), leading to a higher likelihood of finding feasible and high-quality solutions, as demonstrated in challenging Tetris-like packing tasks.
- **Leveraging Modern Hardware:** The effective utilization of GPU architectures for massively parallel computation taps into the capabilities of contemporary hardware. This approach is positioned to benefit from ongoing advancements in GPU technology, suggesting a pathway for continuous performance improvements in future TAMP systems.
- **Practical Applicability in Real-World Robotics:** The successful deployment of cuTAMP on real UR5 and Kinova Gen3 robots for diverse manipulation tasks, including object packing, obstacle avoidance, and tool use, demonstrates its practical utility. Its ability to plan in seconds from perception to action indicates potential for broader adoption in industrial, service, and research robotics requiring complex, long-horizon manipulation capabilities.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** cuTAMP provides a modular framework that can serve as a basis for further research. This includes exploring more advanced optimization techniques (e.g., augmented Lagrangians for dynamic constraint scaling), strategies for particle pruning, integrating GPU-accelerated task planning (e.g., via SAT solvers), extending to planning under uncertainty (stochastic actions, partial observability), and tackling more complex non-prehensile and contact-rich manipulation tasks. The theoretical analysis confirming probabilistic completeness also provides a strong foundation for further theoretical investigations.

In essence, cuTAMP's contribution lies in making complex, integrated task and motion planning more computationally tractable and robust, thereby enabling robots to perform sophisticated manipulation tasks with greater efficiency and reliability in dynamic, constrained environments.
