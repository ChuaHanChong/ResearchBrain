# Perturbation-Based Epistemic Uncertainty for Failure Detection in Vision-Language-Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> PFD is a training-free failure-detection framework for pretrained vision-language-action policies. It injects random low-rank Gaussian perturbations into selected transformer MLP down-projection weights, builds several local model variants, fixes the generation noise, and scores epistemic uncertainty as variance across their predicted action chunks, temporally smoothed and thresholded by split conformal calibration. On LIBERO-PRO it reaches the best average AUROC of 0.866 and balanced accuracy of 0.760.

## Problem

- Regression and flow-based action heads emit no explicit predictive probabilities, so token entropy or perplexity uncertainty measures from autoregressive policies do not apply.
- Stochastic action sampling varies only generation noise under fixed weights, capturing aleatoric action-mode variability rather than parameter uncertainty that matters under distribution shift.
- Deep ensembles and fine-tuned multi-model flow ensembles need training and storing several full parameter sets, which is prohibitive for large pretrained foundation policies.

## Method

- ==Low-rank weight perturbation==: each variant adds a rank-64 ==Gaussian factorization== scaled by strength sigma over square-root input dimension, cutting perturbation parameters to order rank times dimensions.
- Perturbation set placement: perturb ==MLP down-projections== in all transformer layers except the first, targeting the vision encoder or the VLM expert preceding the action expert.
- Uncertainty score: fix generation noise across variants, average per-variant action variance over chunk horizon and action dimensions, smooth over a sliding window, and threshold by ==split conformal calibration==.

## Results

- Averaged over twelve LIBERO-PRO shift suites, PFD reaches **0.866** AUROC and **0.760** balanced accuracy, beating stochastic sampling at **0.814** and **0.712** on every suite in AUROC.
- Gains are largest on Object-Pos, where AUROC rises from **0.484** to **0.640**; representation baselines swing wildly, with PCA-KMeans spanning **0.948** down to **0.362**.
- Real-world black-cup object shift gives PFD **0.862** AUROC and **0.825** balanced accuracy, second to RND at **0.948** but above ACE at **0.716** and sampling at **0.803**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Perturbing model weights and holding sampling noise fixed isolates epistemic from aleatoric uncertainty, a separation that matters more as distribution shift grows.
> - Perturbation strength is model-specific and must be tuned on in-distribution data: the best sigma was 0.6 for one policy and 0.05 for another.
> - Representation novelty detectors can beat action-disagreement scores in narrow single-task settings, since novelty then correlates tightly with failure but generalizes poorly across suites.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A robot can keep confidently executing an old action pattern when objects move, even though the instruction and intended task stay the same. In one LIBERO-PRO Spatial-Pos rollout, the target position was shifted. The robot grasped a bowl but placed it at the memorized original plate location instead of the shifted target. The authors' approach is to compare the same policy under small weight variations and treat disagreement among the variants' action predictions as a warning signal. In this example, that warning score crossed its calibrated threshold before the task failed.

Two representative cases. Left: the score crosses the calibrated threshold at t=12, before the depicted failure at t=21. Right: after visual attributes change, the robot opens the drawer but fails to grasp the target bowl; crossing at t=12, depicted failure at t=29. These are illustrative examples, not aggregate performance.

### 2. Small weight changes, a different question

Vision-language-action (VLA) robots translate a camera view, robot state, and language instruction into continuous actions. Token-generating systems expose probabilities, but regression- and flow-based policies do not necessarily expose probabilities that can serve as confidence scores.

The method, PFD, targets **epistemic uncertainty**, meaning uncertainty about what the model knows. This differs from the variation in actions produced by sampling a fixed model. PFD works in steps:

- Given the current image, state, and instruction, it creates several variants of the pretrained policy by adding random low-rank perturbations to selected transformer weights. When the policy is stochastic, each variant uses the same action-generation noise.
- It compares the predicted action chunks and averages their disagreement across chunk steps and action dimensions.
- It smooths the score over a five-step sliding window and compares it with a threshold calibrated using successful in-distribution rollouts.

The robot executes the base, unperturbed policy's actions. The perturbed variants are used only to estimate uncertainty. The method is training-free, but that does not make it cost-free. It uses additional perturbed forward passes, and the paper does not establish lower elapsed inference time.

Schematic of the proposed signal path. Weight-perturbed variants of a pretrained VLA feed an action-disagreement block, which produces a timestep-level failure score compared with a calibrated threshold.

### 3. Against action sampling on LIBERO-PRO

LIBERO-PRO preserves task semantics while shifting object positions, environment appearance, or non-essential object attributes. The main test used π₀.₅ across 12 shift suites. PFD was compared with stochastic action sampling, which varies flow-generation noise while holding the model fixed.

Two metrics were reported:

- **AUROC** measures how well rollout-level scores separate successful from failed rollouts across thresholds.
- **Balanced accuracy** averages the failure-detection rate and successful-rollout recognition rate at the chosen threshold.

Averaged across suites, PFD scores 0.866 AUROC and 0.760 balanced accuracy, compared with sampling's 0.814 and 0.712. PFD beats sampling in AUROC on all 12 suites, with the largest gain on Object-Pos, from 0.484 to 0.640. It leads averaged results for Position and Object shifts but ranks second for Environment shifts.

Better separation does not mean earlier warnings. Normalized detection time averages 55.65 for PFD and 49.34 for sampling, where lower is earlier. These averages use different valid subsets because results with TPR or TNR below 0.4 are excluded.

The stated conformal false-alarm guarantee applies only to successful in-distribution rollouts under exchangeability. It does not cover shifted conditions, so the shifted-condition results are empirical.

AUROC and balanced accuracy for Position, Environment, and Object shifts, comparing PCA-KMeans, RND, ACE, Sampling, and PFD (blue). PFD is best in the Position and Object categories on both measures. In the Environment category, it trails ACE in AUROC and RND in balanced accuracy.

### 4. A narrower real-robot check

The physical test used one task, "pick up the cup and place it in the box." The π₀.₅ policy was fine-tuned on green-cup demonstrations and tested with an unseen black cup. The test set had 50 rollouts (30 successful, 20 failed), evaluated offline on cached policy inputs. Representation-based RND achieved the best AUROC and balanced accuracy (0.948, 0.908). PFD ranked second on both (0.862, 0.825), exceeding ACE and stochastic sampling.

### 5. Disagreement as a warning

A robot can repeat a memorized action despite a shifted scene. For continuous-action VLAs, the paper shows that a useful uncertainty signal can come from probing the model's weights, in addition to sampling its actions. The evidence is strongest on LIBERO-PRO against action sampling. In the single real-world object-shift test, PFD was competitive but ranked second to RND.
