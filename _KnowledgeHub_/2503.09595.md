# PISA Experiments: Exploring Physics Post-Training for Video Diffusion Models by Watching Stuff Drop

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from New York University and Intel Labs developed the PISA benchmark and applied Physics Supervised Fine-Tuning (PSFT) and Object Reward Optimization (ORO) to improve the physical accuracy of video diffusion models when simulating falling objects. Their post-training approach, using a relatively small simulated dataset, significantly reduced trajectory errors and produced more physically plausible motion compared to state-of-the-art baselines like Open-Sora.

## Problem

- Current state-of-the-art video generation models frequently produce visually convincing content that fails to accurately represent fundamental laws of physics.
- General pre-training on vast datasets does not reliably enable video models to reproduce basic physics principles like gravity.
- There is a need for video generation models to act as reliable "world simulators" for applications like robotics and autonomous driving, requiring physically accurate content.

## Method

- Introduced the **PISA** (Physics in Simulated and Actual environments) benchmark, a standardized evaluation platform focused on ==object freefall== using both real-world and simulated datasets.
- Developed ==Physics Supervised Fine-Tuning (PSFT)== to refine pre-trained video diffusion models by fine-tuning them on a dedicated simulated dataset of object-dropping videos.
- Proposed ==Object Reward Optimization (ORO)==, a novel technique that uses reward signals based on ==segmentation, optical flow, and depth== information to guide the model toward generating more physically plausible object motion.

## Results

- The post-trained models, utilizing **PSFT** and **ORO**, significantly outperformed all baseline video generation models on the PISA benchmark metrics for physical accuracy.
- **PSFT** on a small simulated dataset significantly improved the physical accuracy of the **Open-Sora** model, reducing trajectory errors compared to baselines.
- **ORO** further enhanced physical accuracy, with different reward functions targeting specific aspects like object shape or motion accuracy, though generalization and distributional alignment remain challenges.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - State-of-the-art video generation models, despite their visual quality, consistently fail to accurately simulate basic physics, such as object freefall.
> - Pre-training on massive datasets is insufficient to ensure physical accuracy; dedicated post-training on physics-specific data significantly improves a model's ability to generate physically accurate motion.
> - Leveraging the representational power of pre-trained models is essential for effective physics refinement; training from scratch on physics datasets does not yield comparable results.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Introduction

In recent years, video generation models have made remarkable strides in creating visually impressive content. Models like Sora (OpenAI), Kling (Kuaishou), and Gen-3 (Runway) showcase unprecedented abilities to generate high-quality videos that appear realistic at first glance. However, a critical challenge persists: while these videos may look visually convincing, they often fail to accurately represent the fundamental laws of physics that govern our world.

The research team from New York University and Intel Labs addresses this gap in their paper "PISA Experiments: Exploring Physics Post-Training for Video Diffusion Models by Watching Stuff Drop." Their work examines a seemingly simple yet surprisingly challenging aspect of physics for AI models: accurately simulating objects in freefall. While humans intuitively understand that objects fall at predictable rates due to gravity, state-of-the-art video generation models often produce physically implausible motion when generating videos of falling objects.

This paper presents a compelling argument that although pre-training on massive datasets provides these models with general content creation capabilities, a dedicated post-training phase focusing specifically on physics is necessary to produce physically accurate simulations. This approach draws parallels to the evolution of Large Language Models (LLMs), which undergo post-training processes like instruction tuning and RLHF to enhance their alignment with specific tasks.

### 2. The Physics Challenge in Video Generation

The authors identify a key limitation in current video generation models: despite being trained on vast datasets that implicitly contain examples of physical phenomena, these models fail to reliably reproduce basic physics principles such as gravity. This suggests that general pre-training alone is insufficient to ensure physical accuracy in generated videos.

For video generation models to function as reliable "world simulators" for applications in robotics, autonomous driving, or scientific simulations, they must generate not just visually convincing content but physically accurate content that adheres to the laws of physics. This represents a significant challenge for current models and is the central problem addressed by this research.

The paper makes a compelling case for the necessity of post-training specifically targeted at improving physical accuracy. Just as large language models undergo instruction tuning and reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) to improve their capabilities in specific domains, video generation models require similar dedicated refinement to accurately represent physical phenomena.

### 3. The PISA Benchmark

To systematically evaluate the physical accuracy of video generation models, the researchers introduce the PISA (Physics in Simulated and Actual environments) benchmark. This benchmark focuses specifically on object freefall, a fundamental physical phenomenon governed by gravity.

The PISA benchmark consists of two complementary datasets:

1. **Real-world dataset**: Videos of various objects being dropped in different environments, captured at 120 FPS to ensure detailed tracking of motion
2. **Simulated dataset**: Computer-generated videos of object freefall created using the Kubric physics simulation engine, providing perfect ground truth annotations

The benchmark includes various objects (bottles, boxes, balls, etc.) in diverse environments (kitchens, offices, outdoor spaces), as illustrated in Figure 1. The variety of objects is shown in a word cloud in Figure 3, with bottles and boxes being the most common object types.

The benchmark employs three main metrics to evaluate physical accuracy:

1. **Trajectory L2**: Measures the squared Euclidean distance between predicted and ground truth object trajectories
2. **Chamfer Distance (CD)**: Computes the distance between predicted and ground truth object shapes
3. **Intersection over Union (IoU)**: Evaluates the overlap between predicted and ground truth object masks

These metrics provide a comprehensive assessment of how accurately a model reproduces both the motion trajectory and the physical appearance of falling objects.

### 4. Methodology

The research methodology involves several key components:

#### 4.1. Baseline Evaluation

The authors first evaluated several state-of-the-art video generation models on the PISA benchmark to establish baseline performance. These included both open-source models like Open-Sora and commercial models such as Kling. This evaluation revealed that even the most advanced video generation models struggle to accurately simulate object freefall.

#### 4.2. Data Collection and Annotation

The researchers created a comprehensive dataset for training and evaluation:

1. **Real-world data**: Carefully collected videos of objects being dropped, using a smartphone camera recording at 120 FPS (Figure 2)
2. **Simulated data**: Generated using the Kubric physics engine with precise control over physical parameters and perfect ground truth annotations

The simulated data was particularly valuable as it provided perfect ground truth for object trajectories, segmentation masks, and depth information, which are difficult to obtain in real-world videos.

#### 4.3. Physics Supervised Fine-Tuning (PSFT)

The core technique introduced is Physics Supervised Fine-Tuning (PSFT), where the researchers fine-tuned the Open-Sora model on the simulated dataset of object-dropping videos. This approach leverages the pre-trained capabilities of the model while refining its understanding of physics.

#### 4.4. Object Reward Optimization (ORO)

To further enhance physical accuracy, the authors developed a novel technique called Object Reward Optimization (ORO). This approach uses reward signals based on segmentation, optical flow, and depth information to guide the model toward generating more physically plausible object motion.

### 5. Key Findings

The evaluation of baseline models revealed several important insights:

1. **Current models fail at physics**: State-of-the-art video generation models, despite their impressive visual quality, consistently fail to accurately simulate the physics of falling objects.

2. **Pre-training is insufficient**: Although these models have been trained on massive datasets that implicitly contain examples of physical phenomena, they fail to reliably reproduce basic physics principles.

3. **Post-training works**: The experiments demonstrated that post-training on a relatively small dataset of simulated videos can significantly improve a model's ability to generate physically accurate object motion.

### 6. Physics Supervised Fine Tuning

The Physics Supervised Fine-Tuning (PSFT) approach proved remarkably effective. By fine-tuning the Open-Sora model on just a few thousand simulated videos of falling objects, the researchers were able to significantly improve its physical accuracy.

The effectiveness of PSFT is illustrated in Figure 4, which shows a qualitative comparison between ground truth videos, outputs from baseline models, and outputs from the post-trained model. The post-trained model clearly produces more physically plausible object motion.

Quantitative results further confirmed the efficacy of PSFT:

1. The fine-tuned model outperformed all baseline models on the PISA benchmark metrics.
2. The improvement was substantial, with the post-trained model achieving significantly lower trajectory errors compared to the baseline models.

A crucial finding was the importance of pre-training. When the researchers tried to train a model from scratch (without pre-training) on the physics dataset, it failed to achieve comparable results. This suggests that leveraging the representational power of pre-trained models is essential for success. This dynamic is illustrated in Figure 6, which shows the learning curves for models with and without pre-training.

### 7. Object Reward Optimization

The Object Reward Optimization (ORO) framework represents another significant contribution of this research. This approach uses reward signals to further refine the physical accuracy of generated videos.

The researchers explored three different reward functions:

1. **Segmentation-based rewards**: Computes IoU between generated and ground truth object masks
2. **Optical flow-based rewards**: Calculates the difference between generated and ground truth optical flow fields
3. **Depth-based rewards**: Measures the difference between generated and ground truth depth maps

Each reward function targets a different aspect of physical accuracy. The segmentation-based reward focuses on object shape, the optical flow-based reward targets motion accuracy, and the depth-based reward emphasizes spatial consistency.

The experiments showed that ORO can further enhance the physical accuracy of the post-trained model, with different reward functions providing improvements in different aspects of physical accuracy. This suggests that a combination of reward functions could potentially yield the best overall results.

### 8. Generalization Capabilities

An important question addressed by the research is how well the post-trained model generalizes to scenarios outside its training distribution. The researchers evaluated the model's performance on scenarios with objects dropping from unseen depths and heights.

The results showed that while the post-trained model performs better than baseline models on out-of-distribution scenarios, its performance degrades compared to in-distribution cases. This indicates that generalization remains a challenge for these models.

To better understand the model's generalization capabilities, the researchers analyzed its performance from a distributional perspective. They examined whether the model could consistently generate physically plausible motion across a range of initial conditions.

The distributional analysis revealed a key insight: the post-trained model generates object motion that is consistent with a valid 3D world state, but there is a misalignment between the generated and ground truth distributions of dropping time. This suggests that while post-training improves per-sample accuracy, distributional alignment remains a challenge.

### 9. Distributional Analysis

One of the most interesting aspects of the research is the analysis of perspective ambiguity in falling object videos. When viewing a 2D video of a falling object, there is inherent ambiguity about the object's distance from the camera, as illustrated in Figure 8. This ambiguity leads to a distribution of possible dropping times that are consistent with the laws of physics.

The researchers examined whether the post-trained model captures this distribution of possibilities. Their analysis, shown in Figure 10, revealed that while the model generates physically plausible motion, there is a misalignment between the generated and ground truth distributions of dropping times.

This finding suggests an important direction for future research: improving the distributional alignment of post-trained models to better capture the range of physically valid outcomes.

### 10. Significance and Impact

The significance of this research extends beyond the specific task of modeling object freefall. The findings have broad implications for the development of video generation models as reliable world simulators:

1. **Highlighting the importance of physics post-training**: The research demonstrates that dedicated post-training is essential for adapting pre-trained video generation models to tasks requiring physical accuracy.

2. **Providing a valuable benchmark**: The PISA benchmark offers a standardized evaluation platform for assessing and comparing the physical accuracy of different video generation models.

3. **Introducing effective techniques**: PSFT and ORO provide practical approaches for improving the physical accuracy of video generation models.

4. **Identifying key limitations and challenges**: The research highlights important limitations in current approaches, such as generalization to unseen scenarios and distributional alignment, providing valuable directions for future research.

These contributions collectively advance the goal of developing video generation models that can serve as reliable world simulators for applications in robotics, autonomous driving, virtual reality, and scientific simulation.

### 11. Conclusion

The research presented in "PISA Experiments" makes a compelling case for the importance of physics post-training in video generation models. By focusing on the specific task of object freefall, the authors demonstrate that even state-of-the-art models struggle with basic physics, but that targeted post-training can significantly improve their physical accuracy.

The introduced techniques—Physics Supervised Fine-Tuning (PSFT) and Object Reward Optimization (ORO)—provide effective approaches for enhancing the physical accuracy of video generation models. The PISA benchmark offers a valuable tool for evaluating and advancing research in this area.

The findings also highlight important challenges and directions for future research, including improving generalization to unseen scenarios and achieving better distributional alignment. Addressing these challenges will be crucial for realizing the vision of video generation models as reliable world simulators.

As video generation technology continues to advance, the insights and methodologies presented in this research will play an important role in ensuring that these models not only generate visually convincing content but also accurately represent the physical laws that govern our world.
