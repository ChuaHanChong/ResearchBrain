# RoamFlow: Reinforcement-Aligned One-Step Action MeanFlow Policy for Image-Goal Navigation

> [!summary] Summary
> RoamFlow, a generative framework from Nanyang Technological University, introduces a one-step action MeanFlow policy for image-goal navigation. It integrates a two-stage training process combining imitation learning with reinforcement learning, achieving superior navigation performance and real-time inference on both simulated environments and a physical robot.

## Problem

- Existing generative navigation policies suffer from high inference latency due to iterative generation processes, limiting real-time deployment on resource-constrained platforms.
- Most generative navigation policies rely solely on imitation learning, which struggles to correct suboptimal expert behaviors or adapt to novel environments, leading to accumulated long-horizon errors.
- There is a trade-off between aggressively distilling generative models for speed and maintaining trajectory quality and diversity.

## Method

- **RoamFlow** proposes a ==MeanFlow-based generative policy==, which models ==interval-level displacement== (an average velocity field) to enable the generation of complete trajectory sequences in a single or very few transport steps, boosting efficiency.
- A ==two-stage training framework== combines initial offline ==imitation learning== for stable policy acquisition with online ==reinforcement learning== (clipped ==PPO==) for task-driven refinement and improved adaptability to novel situations.
- A ==trajectory evaluator== is incorporated to score and select the most feasible and desirable action sequences from multiple generated candidates, enhancing safety and robustness during execution.

## Results

- In Habitat simulations, RoamFlow achieved **68.7%** Success Rate and **61.9%** SPL on Gibson, outperforming the strongest generative baseline (NavDP) by **9.4%** SR and **14.4%** SPL, while exhibiting significantly lower inference time (**19.6ms** vs **61.1ms**).
- Ablation studies confirmed that both the imitation learning and reinforcement learning stages are crucial, with RL improving SR from **51.3%** to **68.7%** and reducing Collision Rate from **25.9%** to **10.9%**.
- On a physical Unitree Go2 quadruped robot, RoamFlow achieved a **100%** success rate and the lowest collision rate (**0.10 C/run**) among all baselines, demonstrating real-time inference speed (**37.2ms**) below the 10 Hz requirement.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Modeling interval-level displacement via MeanFlow enables efficient, few-step trajectory generation, effectively overcoming the inference latency of traditional iterative generative models.
> - The synergistic combination of imitation learning for foundational skills and reinforcement learning for task-alignment and adaptation significantly improves policy robustness and safety beyond purely imitation-based approaches.
> - A lightweight trajectory evaluator acts as an effective safety filter, improving the reliability of multimodal trajectory predictions by selecting the most suitable option, especially regarding collision avoidance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Image-goal navigation is a fundamental problem in embodied robotics, requiring an autonomous agent to reach a target location specified by a visual image using onboard sensory observations. Traditionally, many approaches in this domain have relied on end-to-end reinforcement learning (RL) policies. These policies directly map perceptual observations to single-step actions. While robust to noise and environmental uncertainty, such step-wise action prediction struggles with long-horizon dependencies, often resulting in myopic behaviors and suboptimal, inefficient trajectories in complex environments that demand multi-step planning and global reasoning.

To mitigate these limitations, recent research has explored generative frameworks that reframe navigation as a trajectory distribution modeling problem. These generative policies synthesize coherent multi-step action sequences rather than isolated actions, aiming to improve long-horizon coherence and enable multimodal trajectory prediction. Examples include diffusion-based methods like NoMaD, NaviDiffusor, and NaviBridger, as well as conditional flow matching methods such as FlowNav. These methods demonstrate an improved capacity for generating more holistic and diverse navigation plans.

However, existing generative navigation policies face two primary challenges. First, their inference efficiency is often limited by inherently iterative generation processes. Diffusion models require multiple denoising steps, and flow-based methods depend on step-wise numerical integration of learned velocity fields. These iterative procedures introduce significant computational latency, which can hinder real-time deployment on resource-constrained robotic platforms. While distillation techniques have been proposed to accelerate these models, aggressive compression frequently compromises trajectory quality and diversity. Second, most generative navigation policies primarily utilize imitation learning. Although offline demonstrations provide valuable trajectory priors, imitation learning alone may not be sufficient to correct suboptimal behaviors present in expert data or adapt effectively to novel, unseen environments during deployment. This can lead to the accumulation of long-horizon errors and a misalignment between trajectory generation and actual task-level objectives, such as efficient goal-reaching and collision avoidance.

RoamFlow addresses these two specific challenges by proposing a generative navigation framework that integrates efficient, few-step trajectory generation with a two-stage training strategy involving reinforcement learning for task-driven refinement. The work positions itself as an advancement in generative navigation that seeks to overcome the trade-off between inference speed and task performance, thereby making these advanced navigation capabilities more practical for real-world robotic applications.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a generative navigation framework capable of efficient, few-step trajectory generation while maintaining strong navigation performance under real-time constraints for image-goal navigation. The authors aim to produce a policy that is both computationally lightweight for deployment on resource-limited robotic platforms and highly effective in navigating complex environments.

This objective is motivated by specific limitations identified in the current state of generative navigation policies:

1. **Inference Latency of Generative Models:** Existing generative navigation policies, including diffusion-based and flow-based methods, inherently involve iterative sampling or numerical integration during inference. Diffusion models require numerous denoising steps, while flow-based models typically integrate learned velocity fields over time. This iterative nature introduces considerable computational overhead and latency, making these methods challenging to deploy in real-time on robots with limited processing capabilities. The authors seek a generative mechanism that can synthesize trajectories with significantly fewer computational steps, ideally in a single or very few forward passes.

2. **Limitations of Imitation Learning in Generative Policies:** Most current generative navigation policies are trained predominantly through imitation learning on offline expert datasets. While imitation learning is effective for learning fundamental behaviors, it struggles to correct suboptimal expert behaviors or adapt to unexpected situations and novel environments encountered during live deployment. Policies trained purely via imitation may accumulate errors over long horizons and fail to optimize trajectory generation directly against task-level objectives, such as maximizing goal-reaching success or minimizing collisions. This motivates the need for a training paradigm that can refine the policy beyond the confines of offline demonstrations.

To address these motivations, RoamFlow sets out to achieve the following specific goals:
- To propose a MeanFlow-based generative policy that models interval-level displacement, thereby enabling efficient trajectory generation with a reduced number of transport steps.
- To introduce a two-stage training framework that combines the stability of offline imitation learning for initial policy acquisition with the adaptability and task-alignment capabilities of online reinforcement learning.
- To validate the proposed framework's efficiency and performance in both high-fidelity simulation environments and real-world robotic platforms, demonstrating its practical applicability.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

RoamFlow's methodology is structured around a novel generative architecture combined with a two-stage training pipeline.

**A. RoamFlow Architecture**

The RoamFlow architecture consists of three main components:

1. **Observation Encoding:** The policy processes the current RGB image ($I^{rgb}_{obs}$), current depth image ($I^{depth}_{obs}$), and a goal RGB image ($I^{rgb}_{goal}$). An EfficientNet-B0 backbone independently encodes $I^{rgb}_{obs}$ and $I^{depth}_{obs}$ into corresponding embeddings, which are then concatenated and linearly projected. Concurrently, $I^{rgb}_{obs}$ and $I^{rgb}_{goal}$ are concatenated and fed into another EfficientNet-B0 encoder to generate a goal embedding. These two resulting embeddings are then concatenated and processed by a Transformer block to yield a joint latent representation ($C_t$), which encapsulates both the current environmental state and the visual goal.

2. **RoamFlow Policy (MeanFlow-based Generator):** This component is responsible for generating trajectory action sequences. It leverages the latent representation $C_t$ from the Transformer, incorporating observation conditions through FiLM (Feature-wise Linear Modulation). The system takes Gaussian noise as input, and a UNet architecture is employed to predict the mean velocity field. Crucially, RoamFlow formulates trajectory generation as a *single-step process*, directly mapping the initial stochastic noise to a complete trajectory sequence ($A_t$). This design aims to enhance generation efficiency significantly compared to iterative methods.

3. **Trajectory Evaluator:** An MLP-based head serves as the trajectory evaluator. Its inputs include visual token representations from the Transformer and *multiple* candidate trajectories generated by the RoamFlow Policy. The evaluator assigns a scalar score to each trajectory, indicating its feasibility and desirability. During inference, the trajectory with the highest score is selected for execution. This mechanism intends to enhance robustness by allowing selection among multimodal predictions, mitigating potential instability from diverse outputs, and prioritizing safe, goal-directed behaviors.

**B. Offline Policy Learning (Stage I)**

The initial stage involves training the MeanFlow generator and the trajectory evaluator using imitation learning on an expert dataset.

1. **MeanFlow Generator:**
 - Traditional Conditional Flow Matching (CFM) methods model trajectory generation as a continuous transport process, mapping a noise prior to expert trajectories via an instantaneous velocity field $v(x_t, t)$. This requires numerical integration over multiple steps.
 - RoamFlow adopts **MeanFlow**, which predicts an *average velocity field* $u(x_t, r, t)$ over a time interval $[r, t]$, representing the normalized displacement. This approach models interval-level displacement rather than instantaneous velocity.
 - A neural network $u_\theta$ is trained to predict this average velocity field, conditioned on observations. The objective function $L_{MF} = E_{x_t,r,t}[\|u_\theta(x_t, r, t|Z) - u(x_t, r, t)\|^2]$ encourages the policy to capture interval-level motion, enabling trajectory synthesis with significantly fewer transport steps, ideally a single forward pass, to improve inference speed.

2. **Trajectory Evaluator:**
 - The evaluator $s_\phi(o, \tau)$ is trained via a regression loss $L_{eval} = E_{(o,\tau)}[(s_\phi(o, \tau) - \tilde{s}(\tau))^2]$.
 - The target score $\tilde{s}(\tau)$ for a trajectory $\tau$ combines safety and smoothness considerations: $\tilde{s}(\tau) = s_{safe}(\tau) - \lambda s_{smooth}(\tau)$.
 - $s_{safe}(\tau)$ penalizes trajectories that come too close to obstacles (using a smooth barrier function $\psi$) and encourages increasing clearance.
 - $s_{smooth}(\tau)$ penalizes abrupt changes in actions, measured by the squared norm of the second derivative of the action sequence.
 - During inference, the evaluator selects $\tau^* = \text{argmax}_{\tau_i} s_\phi(o, \tau_i)$ from multiple generated proposals, acting as a safety-aware selection layer.

**C. Online Policy Refinement (Stage II)**

Following offline pre-training, the policy undergoes online refinement using reinforcement learning to improve task-level performance.

1. **Stochastic Transport Process for RL:**
 - The deterministic MeanFlow formulation is extended to a stochastic process by introducing learnable noise into the transport dynamics. A learnable noise network $g_{\theta'}$ predicts the transition variance, resulting in Gaussian transitions for intermediate trajectory states $X_{k+1_t} \sim N(\mu^k_{\bar{\theta}}, \Sigma^k_{\theta'})$.
 - This transforms the deterministic transport into a discrete-time Markov chain over latent trajectory states, allowing for likelihood-based policy optimization. The trajectory distribution's log-likelihood is factorized as a product of these transition densities.
 - At inference time, the stochastic transport chain reverts to a deterministic policy mapping $\tau_t = f_\theta(X_{0_t}, Z_t) = X_{0_t} + U_\theta(X_{0_t}, Z_t)$. This ensures fast, deterministic action selection while leveraging the RL-learned trajectory distribution. The Transformer parameters and evaluator parameters are typically frozen during this stage.

2. **Reward Function:** The agent's learning is guided by a composite reward function $r_t = r_{success_t} + r_{slack_t} + r_{progress_t} + r_{collision_t}$.
 - $r_{success_t}$: Positive reward upon reaching the goal.
 - $r_{slack_t}$: Constant per-step penalty to encourage faster task completion.
 - $r_{progress_t}$: Measures the change in distance to the goal, promoting efficient movement.
 - $r_{collision_t}$: Penalty applied when a collision occurs, encouraging safety.

3. **Optimization:** The trajectory generation policy is optimized using the clipped Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) objective, $L_{PPO}$, which utilizes estimated advantages and importance ratios to stabilize training and improve policy performance without large, destructive updates.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates RoamFlow extensively in both simulation and real-world robotic experiments, using standard image-goal navigation metrics: Success Rate (SR), Success weighted by Path Length (SPL), Collision Rate (CR), and Inference Time (ms).

**A. Simulation Results (Habitat Environment)**

- **Baseline Comparison (Table I):** RoamFlow was compared against several generative navigation baselines, including diffusion-based methods (NoMaD, NaviDiffusor, NavDP, NaviBridger) and flow-based methods (FlowNav), along with their distilled variants.
 - RoamFlow achieved the highest performance on the Gibson validation split, with an SR of 68.7% and SPL of 61.9%. This represents an increase of 9.4% in SR and 14.4% in SPL compared to the strongest generative baseline, NavDP.
 - It demonstrated improved safety, reducing CR to 10.9% on Gibson, lower than all baselines.
 - Crucially, RoamFlow exhibited the lowest inference time at 19.6ms on Gibson (19.1ms on MP3D), significantly faster than other generative methods (e.g., NavDP at 61.1ms, NaviDiffusor at 89.5ms) and even outperforming distilled baselines (e.g., NoMaD + Distillation at 28.1ms, FlowNav + Distillation at 21.2ms).
 - Similar trends were observed for cross-domain generalization on the MP3D dataset.
 - The reward curve during RL training (Figure 3) indicated that RoamFlow achieved the highest and most stable average episode reward, suggesting efficient convergence and superior final policy quality.

- **Ablation Study (Table II):** An ablation study investigated the contribution of key components:
 - **Effect of IL Stage:** Training without the imitation learning (IL) pre-training stage resulted in significantly lower SR (13.2% vs. 68.7%) and SPL (11.2% vs. 61.9%) and much higher CR (61.3% vs. 10.9%) on Gibson. This demonstrates that IL provides crucial initial learning for basic navigation behaviors.
 - **Effect of RL Stage:** When omitting the reinforcement learning (RL) fine-tuning stage, performance decreased across all metrics (e.g., SR dropped from 68.7% to 51.3%, CR increased from 10.9% to 25.9% on Gibson). This confirms that RL effectively refines the policy, enhancing robustness and safety through task-oriented rewards and direct environment interaction.
 - **Effect of Trajectory Evaluator:** Comparing RoamFlow's evaluator-guided selection with random trajectory selection, the evaluator consistently reduced the CR (10.9% vs. 17.5% on Gibson) and improved SR and SPL. This indicates the evaluator acts as a lightweight safety filter, improving trajectory reliability.

**B. Physical Experiments (Real-World Robot)**

- RoamFlow was deployed on a Unitree Go2 quadruped robot equipped with an NVIDIA Jetson Orin NX.
- In real-world tests across three distinct scenarios, RoamFlow achieved a 100% Success Rate, significantly higher than all baselines (ranging from 70% to 90%).
- It demonstrated superior safety with the lowest average number of collisions per run (0.10 C/run) compared to baselines (0.25 to 0.70 C/run).
- The inference time for RoamFlow on the physical robot was 37.2ms, which was the lowest among all tested methods, including distilled baselines. This performance was consistently below the 100ms required for real-time operation at 10 Hz.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

RoamFlow represents an advancement in image-goal navigation for embodied robotics, primarily by addressing the critical challenges of inference efficiency and task-level performance in generative policies.

The introduction of a MeanFlow-based generative policy, which predicts an average velocity field, enables the generation of complete trajectories in a single or very few transport steps. This mechanism significantly reduces computational latency, a major bottleneck for deploying advanced generative models on resource-constrained robotic platforms. The demonstrated inference times of under 20ms in simulation and under 40ms on a real-world edge device (Jetson Orin NX) indicate a practical solution for real-time autonomous navigation.

Furthermore, the two-stage training strategy, combining offline imitation learning with online reinforcement learning, offers a robust approach to policy development. Imitation learning provides a stable initial policy with fundamental navigation skills, while the subsequent reinforcement learning stage refines the policy to explicitly align with task-level objectives, such as maximizing success, minimizing path length, and avoiding collisions. This hybrid approach allows the policy to transcend the limitations of purely imitation-based methods, leading to higher success rates, improved path efficiency, and enhanced safety in complex and novel environments. The ablation studies empirically confirm the complementary benefits of each training stage and the utility of the trajectory evaluator as a lightweight safety filter.

The successful deployment of RoamFlow on a physical quadruped robot, achieving a 100% success rate with minimal collisions and real-time inference, demonstrates its practical viability. This work contributes to the development of more capable and reliable autonomous agents, paving the way for wider adoption of generative navigation solutions in real-world applications where both performance and computational efficiency are paramount. The methodology presented in RoamFlow, particularly the efficient MeanFlow trajectory generation and the IL-RL alignment strategy, could potentially be extended to other sequential decision-making tasks in robotics.
