# Learning robust perceptive locomotion for quadrupedal robots in the wild

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at ETH Zurich and collaborators developed a locomotion controller for quadrupedal robots that robustly integrates exteroceptive and proprioceptive perception. This enabled the ANYmal robot to navigate complex, unstructured outdoor environments with high speed and efficiency, demonstrating zero falls across various challenging terrains including a 2.2km mountain hike and subterranean environments.

## Problem

- Legged robots historically struggle to match animal agility and robustness when navigating complex, unstructured environments.
- Proprioceptive locomotion is robust but cannot anticipate terrain, limiting speed and efficiency; perceptive locomotion anticipates terrain but is vulnerable to perception failures.
- The challenge lies in developing a controller that seamlessly integrates both perception types, maintaining anticipatory benefits while being robust to perception inaccuracies or failures.

## Method

- A ==recurrent belief encoder== with an ==attention mechanism== constructs an integrated ==belief state== by dynamically balancing proprioceptive and exteroceptive information.
- A ==teacher-student learning== framework leverages a "teacher" policy with ==privileged information== (trained via ==PPO==) to train a "student" policy that operates with realistic, noisy inputs.
- Comprehensive ==domain randomization== techniques ensure robust ==simulation-to-reality transfer==, allowing policies trained in simulation to work effectively on real robots without fine-tuning.

## Results

- The ANYmal robot completed a **2.2km mountain hike** with **120m elevation gain** and navigated diverse challenging environments including underground caves, deep snow, and urban settings with **zero falls**.
- The perceptive controller successfully traversed steps up to **30cm high** and completed obstacle courses **more than twice as fast** as proprioceptive baselines (**33 seconds vs. 75+ seconds**).
- The approach maintained robust locomotion when sensors were partially or completely occluded, over reflective surfaces, and adapted gait patterns on slippery terrain.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A belief state generated by a recurrent encoder can effectively integrate and dynamically weight proprioceptive and exteroceptive information, adapting to perception reliability.
> - Privileged learning, combined with a teacher-student framework, enables the training of robust controllers that can handle real-world sensor noise and failures.
> - Sophisticated noise modeling and comprehensive domain randomization in simulation facilitate zero-shot sim-to-real transfer for complex locomotion policies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Introduction

Legged robots offer significant advantages over wheeled or tracked vehicles when navigating unstructured environments, yet they have historically struggled to match the agility and robustness of their animal counterparts in challenging terrain. The paper "Learning Robust Perceptive Locomotion for Quadrupedal Robots in the Wild" addresses this challenge by developing an innovative approach that enables quadrupedal robots to navigate complex, unstructured environments with remarkable speed and efficiency.

The research, conducted by Takahiro Miki and colleagues from ETH Zurich, KAIST, and Intel, presents a solution to the critical problem of integrating exteroceptive (external) and proprioceptive (internal) perception for robust quadrupedal locomotion. This integration allows robots to anticipate and adapt to terrain features while maintaining stability and efficiency - a capability essential for autonomous operation in remote or hazardous environments.

### 2. Research Context and Challenges

Legged locomotion research has traditionally followed two main approaches:

1. **Proprioceptive locomotion**: Relying solely on internal sensing (joint positions, torques, IMU data), these controllers react to terrain only after physical contact. While robust to perception failures, they limit speed and efficiency as they cannot anticipate terrain features.

2. **Perceptive locomotion**: Incorporating external sensing (cameras, LiDAR) allows anticipation of terrain features but introduces vulnerability to perception failures in challenging conditions like snow, reflective surfaces, or changing lighting.

The fundamental challenge addressed by this research is developing a controller that seamlessly integrates both perception types, maintaining the benefits of anticipatory perception while being robust to perception failures or inaccuracies.

### 3. Technical Approach

The researchers' solution hinges on three key innovations:

1. **Recurrent encoder with attention mechanism**: A neural network architecture that constructs an integrated "belief state" by dynamically balancing proprioceptive and exteroceptive information.

2. **Teacher-student learning framework**: Leverages privileged learning where a "teacher" policy with perfect information trains a "student" policy that operates with realistic, noisy inputs.

3. **Robust simulation-to-reality transfer**: Employs comprehensive domain randomization techniques to ensure policies trained in simulation work effectively on real robots without fine-tuning.

### 4. Methodology

The methodology involves three distinct phases:

1. **Teacher Policy Training**: A reinforcement learning policy is trained in simulation with access to privileged information (perfect terrain data, friction coefficients) unavailable in the real world. Using the PPO algorithm, this policy learns optimal behaviors for various terrains.

2. **Student Policy Training**: A student policy learns to imitate the teacher's actions without privileged information. This policy uses a recurrent belief encoder with a gated attention mechanism to combine proprioceptive data with noisy exteroceptive inputs. The training includes both behavior cloning (imitating the teacher) and reconstruction losses (encouraging informative internal representations).

3. **Deployment**: The student policy is deployed on the ANYmal quadrupedal robot. The robot uses onboard LiDAR and depth cameras to construct an elevation map, from which height readings are sampled and fed to the control policy at 50Hz.

The belief encoder is particularly innovative. As shown in Figure 2, it uses a gated recurrent neural network to create a belief state that:
- Maintains history of previous observations
- Dynamically weights the importance of exteroceptive information
- Updates its internal model when perception proves unreliable

The researchers developed sophisticated noise models to simulate real-world perception challenges.

### 5. Key Results

The controller demonstrated exceptional performance across diverse challenging environments:

1. **Alpine hiking**: The robot completed a 2.2km mountain hike on Etzel Mountain in Switzerland, navigating steep terrain with 120m elevation gain without human assistance.

2. **Underground navigation**: Successfully deployed in subterranean environments during the DARPA Subterranean Challenge, navigating tunnels, caves, and urban underground settings.

3. **Extreme conditions**: Demonstrated robust locomotion in deep snow, over reflective surfaces, through dense vegetation, and across urban obstacles.

4. **Zero falls**: Remarkably, the robot experienced zero falls during all deployments across these varied terrains.

### 6. Performance Evaluation

Controlled experiments demonstrated significant advantages over proprioceptive baselines:

1. **Step traversal**: The perceptive controller successfully navigated steps up to 30cm high, while the proprioceptive baseline failed beyond 20cm.

2. **Obstacle course**: The perceptive controller completed obstacle courses more than twice as fast as the proprioceptive baseline (33 seconds vs. 75+ seconds with assistance).

3. **Command tracking**: The controller demonstrated superior velocity tracking across forward, lateral, and yaw dimensions, particularly when navigating obstacles.

4. **Terrain adaptation**: Quantitative evaluation showed significantly higher success rates across various terrain configurations, including grid patterns and stairs of different dimensions.

### 7. Handling Perceptual Challenges

A key strength of the approach is its robustness to perceptual challenges that typically confound autonomous robots:

1. **Soft and transparent obstacles**: The controller successfully navigated both cushioned obstacles and transparent barriers that confuse typical elevation sensors.

2. **Sensor occlusions**: Maintained robust locomotion even when sensors were partially or completely covered.

3. **Slippery surfaces**: Adapted gait patterns when encountering low-friction surfaces, as shown in the dynamic friction coefficient estimation.

The belief state visualization revealed how the controller integrates and updates its internal model when confronted with misleading perceptual information. When terrain perception is reliable, the controller leans heavily on exteroceptive information to anticipate features. When perception becomes unreliable, it smoothly transitions to rely more on proprioceptive feedback.

### 8. Real-World Applications

The robust perceptive locomotion capabilities demonstrated in this research have significant real-world applications:

1. **Search and rescue**: The robot's ability to navigate challenging terrain autonomously makes it valuable for disaster response scenarios.

2. **Industrial inspection**: Demonstrated in underground environments, the robot can access hazardous areas unsafe for humans.

3. **Exploration**: The system's robustness to varied environmental conditions enables exploration of remote or extreme locations.

4. **DARPA Subterranean Challenge**: The controller was successfully deployed as the default locomotion system for team Cerberus, which won first prize in the finals, demonstrating its practical value in a demanding real-world scenario.

### 9. Significance and Impact

This research represents a significant advancement in legged robotics for several reasons:

1. **Integrated perception paradigm**: The belief-based approach provides a principled solution to the long-standing challenge of integrating exteroceptive and proprioceptive information for robust locomotion.

2. **Zero-shot sim-to-real transfer**: The ability to transfer policies directly from simulation to real robots without fine-tuning dramatically reduces development time and costs.

3. **Expanded operational domain**: The demonstrated robustness across diverse environments significantly expands the practical operational domain of legged robots.

4. **Computational efficiency**: Despite the sophisticated perception processing, the controller runs in real-time on onboard computing hardware, making it practical for field deployment.

The neural network architecture's ability to learn appropriate weighting of different information sources based on context provides a powerful and generalizable approach to robot control under perceptual uncertainty.

### 10. Conclusion

The research by Miki et al. presents a significant advance in legged robotics by developing a robust perceptive locomotion controller that effectively integrates exteroceptive and proprioceptive information. Through a teacher-student learning framework and recurrent belief encoder, the approach enables quadrupedal robots to traverse challenging, unstructured environments with unprecedented speed and reliability.

The controller's successful deployment across alpine, forest, underground, and urban environments—with zero falls during all tests—demonstrates its exceptional robustness. Its ability to handle perceptual challenges like deep snow, reflective surfaces, and sensor occlusions makes it particularly valuable for real-world applications.

This work brings legged robots closer to the agility and adaptability of their animal counterparts, opening new possibilities for deploying autonomous robots in environments previously considered too challenging for consistent operation.
