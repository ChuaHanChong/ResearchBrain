# A Differential and Pointwise Control Approach to Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> The paper introduces Differential Reinforcement Learning, a continuous-time optimal control framework for RL, leading to the Differential Policy Optimization (dfPO) algorithm. This approach, which inherently embeds physics-informed priors via a Hamiltonian structure, consistently surpassed 12 baselines in various scientific computing tasks, including surface modeling, grid control, and molecular dynamics, particularly under low-data conditions.

## Problem

- Traditional reinforcement learning (RL) methods in scientific computing suffer from poor sample efficiency and often produce physically inconsistent solutions due to a lack of integrated physical laws.
- Existing model-based and model-free RL algorithms often require explicit reward functions/derivatives, mid-course re-planning capabilities, or struggle to incorporate physics-informed priors under limited data conditions.
- A general absence of robust theoretical guarantees, such as pointwise convergence or competitive regret bounds, for RL in continuous state-action spaces limits its reliability in scientific applications.

## Method

- The authors reformulated RL from a discrete Markov Decision Process into a continuous-time optimal control problem, leveraging ==Pontryagin's Maximum Principle== to derive a differential dual formulation with a Hamiltonian structure.
- A novel Differential Policy Optimization (**dfPO**) algorithm was developed, which is a stage-wise method that learns a dynamics operator by refining local movement operators along a trajectory, rather than global value functions.
- Physics-informed inductive biases were implicitly embedded into the policy's structure through a canonical symplectic matrix, `S`, ensuring learned trajectories adhere to underlying physical dynamics without explicit constraints.

## Results

- dfPO consistently outperformed **12** diverse RL baselines in three scientific computing tasks (surface modeling, grid control, molecular dynamics), achieving the lowest final mean evaluation costs under low-data conditions.
- The framework provides strong theoretical guarantees, including pointwise convergence for the learned policy, bounding the expected error between the learned and true optimal dynamics at each individual timestep.
- For a restricted hypothesis space, dfPO achieved a dimension-independent regret bound of O(K^(5/6)), demonstrating competitive performance against optimal bounds in continuous domains, especially with limited data.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Reframing reinforcement learning within a differential, continuous-time optimal control framework naturally leads to a Hamiltonian structure that inherently embeds physics-informed inductive biases.
> - A pointwise, stage-wise policy optimization approach, which refines local movement operators, significantly enhances sample efficiency and provides trajectory-consistent updates for continuous state-action spaces.
> - Rigorous theoretical guarantees, including pointwise convergence and competitive regret bounds, can be established for continuous-time RL when approached through a differential control perspective.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has demonstrated successes in various fields, including robotics, biological sciences, and control systems. However, its application in scientific computing faces persistent challenges, primarily concerning sample efficiency, the integration of physical consistency, and the lack of robust theoretical guarantees. Standard RL methodologies often require extensive trial-and-error exploration with sparse feedback, leading to high data demands that are often impractical in scientific simulations where data generation is costly. Furthermore, conventional RL typically does not inherently incorporate physical laws or structural priors, which can result in physically inconsistent or suboptimal solutions when applied to systems governed by continuous dynamics.

Model-based RL (MBRL) attempts to improve sample efficiency by learning a model of the environment. However, many MBRL algorithms, such as Stochastic Value Gradients (SVG) or iterative Linear Quadratic Regulator (iLQR), necessitate explicit access to the reward function and/or its derivatives, which are often unavailable in scientific computing settings where rewards are implicitly defined by complex simulations. Additionally, some trajectory-based planners assume the ability to re-plan from intermediate time steps, a capability often absent in scientific problems that require generating complete trajectories from an initial state without mid-course resets. These limitations often lead researchers to resort to model-free RL combined with customized reward shaping. However, these approaches still struggle to incorporate physics-informed priors or achieve optimal policies with limited data budgets.

This paper proposes a fundamentally different approach by reframing RL within a continuous-time control framework. Instead of discrete-step cumulative reward optimization, the authors interpret trajectory returns as integrals and introduce a differential dual formulation. This approach naturally gives rise to a Hamiltonian structure, which embeds inductive biases aligned with physical dynamics, even in the absence of explicit physical constraints. This positions the work as an attempt to bridge the gap between traditional RL and continuous-time optimal control theory, aiming to address the specific shortcomings of existing RL methods when applied to complex, physics-governed scientific domains.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to address the limitations of reinforcement learning in continuous state-action spaces within scientific computing. Specifically, the authors aim to overcome challenges related to poor sample efficiency and the lack of pathwise physical consistency.

The detailed objectives and motivations are as follows:

1. **Reformulate RL using a Continuous-Time Control Perspective:** Existing RL methods are predominantly based on discrete Markov Decision Processes (MDPs). The authors are motivated by the inherent continuous nature of many scientific phenomena. Their objective is to reformulate the RL problem from a continuous-time optimal control perspective, treating trajectory returns as integrals over time. This foundational shift is intended to provide a more natural and mathematically consistent framework for continuous state-action spaces.

2. **Introduce a Differential Dual Formulation and Hamiltonian Structure:** Building on the continuous-time reformulation, a key objective is to invoke Pontryagin's Maximum Principle to derive a differential dual formulation. This dual formulation introduces an adjoint variable and leads to a Hamiltonian structure. The motivation for this is that a Hamiltonian framework inherently encodes physics priors, ensuring that learned trajectories maintain consistency with underlying physical dynamics without requiring explicit constraints or complex reward shaping. This is crucial for scientific computing tasks where physical realism is paramount.

3. **Develop a Pointwise, Stage-wise Policy Optimization Algorithm (dfPO):** To implement the proposed Differential RL framework, the authors aim to develop an algorithm called Differential Policy Optimization (dfPO). Unlike traditional RL algorithms that rely on global value function estimates, dfPO is designed to be a pointwise, stage-wise algorithm. Its objective is to refine local movement operators along a trajectory, allowing for updates that are closely aligned with the system's dynamics at each timestep. This local approach is motivated by the need to improve sample efficiency by minimizing redundant relearning and avoiding costly, physically misaligned exploration, particularly in scientific settings with constrained simulation budgets.

4. **Establish Rigorous Theoretical Guarantees:** A significant motivation is to provide robust theoretical foundations for the new approach. The authors aim to establish pointwise convergence guarantees for dfPO, a property not typically available in standard RL algorithms. Additionally, they seek to derive competitive theoretical regret bounds, providing a formal measure of the algorithm's performance relative to an optimal policy, especially under limited data conditions. These theoretical contributions are intended to enhance the credibility and understanding of the proposed method.

5. **Empirically Validate Performance on Scientific Computing Tasks:** The ultimate objective is to demonstrate the practical utility and effectiveness of Differential RL and dfPO. The motivation here is to evaluate the algorithm on representative scientific computing tasks that involve complex dynamics, implicit objectives, and limited data—conditions under which traditional RL methods often underperform. The chosen tasks (surface modeling, grid control, and molecular dynamics) represent diverse applications where physical consistency and sample efficiency are critical.

In essence, the motivation is to develop an RL framework that is inherently physics-informed, sample-efficient, and theoretically sound, thereby expanding the applicability and reliability of RL in data-intensive scientific and engineering disciplines.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of this work lies in reformulating reinforcement learning from a continuous-time control perspective, leading to a differential dual formulation and a novel policy optimization algorithm.

**Problem Reformulation: Differential Reinforcement Learning**

1. **Continuous-Time Control Objective:** The standard discrete-time Markov Decision Process (MDP) formulation, which maximizes a sum of discrete rewards, is transformed into a continuous-time optimal control problem. This involves approximating the discrete sum with a time integral, aiming to maximize the expected integral of rewards `∫ r(s_t, a_t)dt` subject to continuous-time dynamics `ṡ_t = f(s_t, a_t)`.

2. **Hamiltonian Dual Formulation:** Instead of directly solving this constrained optimization problem, Pontryagin's Maximum Principle is invoked. This introduces a dual formulation analogous to Hamiltonian mechanics. An adjoint variable `p` is introduced, and the Hamiltonian function `H(s, p, a)` is defined as `pᵀf(s, a) - r(s, a)`.

3. **Optimal Action and Reduced Hamiltonian:** The optimal action `a*(s, p)` is determined by the stationarity condition `∂H/∂a = 0`. Substituting this optimal action back into `H` yields the reduced Hamiltonian `h(s, p) := H(s, p, a*(s, p))`. This allows the optimal path to be expressed solely in terms of the state `s` and adjoint variable `p`.

4. **Differential Dual System:** The state `s` and adjoint variable `p` are coupled into a composite vector `x = (s, p)`, forming an extended phase space. The evolution of the system is then described by the differential dual system: `ẋ = S∇h(x)`, where `S` is the canonical symplectic matrix `[[0, I], [-I, 0]]`. This formulation encodes the system's evolution through a Hamiltonian gradient flow.

5. **Dynamics Operator Learning:** The continuous differential system is discretized to obtain an iterative update rule: `x_n+1 = x_n + Δt S∇h(x_n) := G(x_n)`. Here, `G` represents the dynamics operator. The learning objective becomes to discover an optimal operator `G` that generates the optimal trajectory through successive applications.

6. **Score Function Approximation:** The reduced Hamiltonian `h(x)` is approximated by a learnable score function `g(x)`. This reparameterization shifts the learning objective towards trajectory-consistent updates. The policy `G_θ` is then parameterized as `G_θ = Id + Δt S∇g_θ`, where `Id` is the identity operator and `∇g_θ` is obtained via automatic differentiation. This structure implicitly embeds a physics prior through the symplectic form `S`.

7. **Application to Energy-Based Objectives:** For scientific computing tasks often characterized by energy-based objectives, a common reward form is `r(s, a) = 1/2 ||a||² - F(s)`. In this scenario, the stationarity condition `∂H/∂a = 0` implies `p = a*`, establishing a direct correspondence between the adjoint variable and the action. The score function `g(s, p)` can then be defined as `1/2 ||p||² - r(s, p)`.

**Differential Policy Optimization (dfPO) Algorithm**

dfPO (Algorithm 1) is a stage-wise algorithm designed to iteratively update the policy `G_θ_k` (parameterized by `θ`) by refining the score function `g_θ_k`.

1. **Initialization:** The algorithm initializes an empty replay memory queue `M`, a stage counter `k=1`, and a random scoring function `g_θ_0`. The initial policy `G_θ_0` is derived from `g_θ_0`.

2. **Iterative Policy Refinement:** The algorithm proceeds in stages until `k` reaches `H` (the number of steps per episode):
 - **Sample Collection:** At each stage `k`, `N_k` starting points are sampled (from an initial distribution `ρ_0`), and the environment `B` is queried using the current policy `G_θ_k-1` to generate `N_k` sample trajectories `(G_θ_k-1^(j)(X_i))_j=0^(H-1)` along with their associated scores `(g(G_θ_k-1^(j)(X_i)))_j=0^(H-1)`.
 - **Memory Update:** Labeled samples of the form `(x, y)` are added to `M`. Crucially, this includes `(G_θ_k-1^(k-1)(X_i), g(G_θ_k-1^(k-1)))` for the current stage's end-point, and `(G_θ_k-1^(j)(X_i), g_θ_k-1(G_θ_k-1^(j)(X_i)))` for all preceding timesteps `j < k-1`. The latter step ensures that the new policy `G_θ_k` does not significantly deviate from `G_θ_k-1` on states where the previous policy already performed well, promoting consistency across policy updates.
 - **Neural Network Training:** A neural network `g_θ_k` (from a hypothesis space `H_k`) is trained at stage `k` using the labeled samples from `M`. A smooth L1 loss function is used for this training.
 - **Policy Update:** The new policy `G_θ_k` is derived from the trained `g_θ_k` using automatic differentiation: `G_θ_k = Id + Δt S∇g_θ_k`. The stage counter `k` is then incremented.

3. **Output:** After `H` stages, the final policy `G_θ_H-1` is output.

The algorithm's "time-expansion" nature and emphasis on pointwise estimation aim to provide a more local and sample-efficient approach compared to global value function updates in standard RL.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yields both theoretical guarantees and empirical performance demonstrations for the proposed Differential Reinforcement Learning framework and its associated algorithm, dfPO.

**Theoretical Contributions:**

1. **Pointwise Convergence Guarantees (Theorem 3.2):** The paper establishes that dfPO provides pointwise convergence for the learned policy. For a given threshold error `ε`, probability `δ`, and episode length `H`, and assuming Lipschitz continuity for the true dynamics `G` and policy approximator `G_θ_k`, the expected error between the `j`-th step of the learned policy `G_θ_k` and the true optimal dynamics `G` is bounded: `E_X ||G^(j)_θ_k(X) - G^(j)(X)|| < (jL^j ε) / (L-1)` for `1 ≤ j ≤ k`. This is a property generally not available in standard RL, indicating that the learned policy accurately approximates the optimal trajectory at each individual timestep.

2. **Sample Complexity:**
 - **General Neural Network Approximators (Corollary 3.3):** For a general hypothesis space of neural network approximators with bounded weights and biases, the number of training samples `N_k` required at each stage `k` scales as `O(ε^-(2d+4))`, where `d` is the dimension of the extended state `x = (s, p)`.
 - **Restricted Hypothesis Space (Corollary 3.4):** When the hypothesis space is restricted to functions `h` such that `h - g` (where `g` is the true score function) and `h - g_θ_k-1` are both p-weakly convex and linearly bounded, the sample complexity improves to `N_k = O(ε^-6)`, making it independent of the dimension `d`.

3. **Convergence with Infinitesimal Step Size (Corollary 3.5):** The paper shows that even when the discretization time step `Δt` approaches zero, dfPO's training converges, with error bounds approaching zero, provided `N_k` scales appropriately with `Δt`.

4. **Regret Bound Analysis (Corollary 3.6):** For a fixed and relatively small episode length `H`, the regret bound for dfPO is derived as `O(K^((μ-1)/μ))`, where `K` is the total number of episodes and `μ` is the exponent in the sample complexity `N_k = O(ε^-μ)`. Specifically:
 - For the general neural network case (μ = 2d+4), the regret bound is `O(K^((2d+3)/(2d+4)))`.
 - For the special case with a restricted hypothesis space (μ = 6), the regret bound is `O(K^(5/6))`. This dimension-independent rate is competitive with optimal bounds in continuous domains under similar structural assumptions. The regret bound is derived from the tighter pointwise policy error guarantees, ensuring that the learned policy is near-optimal at each timestep, which can mitigate issues like reward hacking.

**Empirical Performance:**

dfPO was evaluated on three representative scientific computing tasks:

1. **Surface Modeling:** Optimizing evolving surfaces based on geometric or physical properties (e.g., smoothness, curvature).
2. **Grid-based Modeling:** Control on coarse spatial grids with evaluation on refined grids, typical of multiscale PDE-constrained problems.
3. **Molecular Dynamics:** Guiding molecular conformations (octa-alanine molecule) to low-energy states based on atomic interactions.

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Conditions:** Low-data regimes (100,000 sample steps for surface and grid modeling, 5,000 for molecular dynamics due to high evaluation costs), physics-constrained environments, and implicit, simulation-defined objectives.
- **Baselines:** 12 common RL algorithms, including 6 standard (S-TRPO, S-PPO, S-SAC, S-DDPG, S-CrossQ, S-TQC) and 6 energy-reshaped variants (TRPO, PPO, SAC, DDPG, CrossQ, TQC).
- **Metrics:** Final mean evaluation cost (F(s) at the terminal step), with lower values indicating better performance.

**Key Empirical Results (Table 1, Figure 1):**

- **Consistent Superiority:** dfPO consistently outperformed all 12 baselines across all three scientific computing tasks, achieving the lowest final mean evaluation costs.
- **Baseline Performance:** No single baseline demonstrated overall dominance; some performed better than others on specific tasks (e.g., CrossQ, TQC, DDPG, TRPO intermittently ranked second or third).
- **Reward Shaping Impact:** Energy-reshaped variants generally showed improved performance over their standard counterparts, indicating the benefit of incorporating some physics-informed structure, but they still did not match dfPO's performance.
- **PPO Underperformance:** PPO generally underperformed across all tasks, which the authors suggest might be due to its simplified approximations compared to TRPO, leading to reduced stability or weaker inductive bias for physics-aligned problems.
- **Variance and Convergence:** dfPO exhibited competitive exploration patterns with moderate variance, converging to lower objective values more consistently than other methods.
- **Ablation Study (Table 2, Table 5 in Appendix):** Ablation studies on various hyperparameters for both dfPO and baselines confirmed dfPO's robustness, as hyperparameter changes did not substantially alter its relative performance advantage.
- **Computational Complexity:** The computational complexity was analyzed to be `O(H^2 ε^-6)` for the restricted hypothesis space, highlighting its efficiency given the number of steps and target error.
- **Standard RL Tasks (Appendix Table 8):** While not the main focus, dfPO also showed reasonable performance on standard continuous-control tasks like Pendulum, Mountain Car, and CartPole.

In summary, the research presents a theoretically grounded and empirically validated RL framework that specifically addresses the unique demands of scientific computing problems, demonstrating superior performance in challenging, data-scarce, and physics-governed environments.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces a significant advancement in the application of reinforcement learning to scientific computing, offering both a novel theoretical framework and a practical algorithm.

#### 5.1. Significance

1. **Bridging Optimal Control and RL:** The introduction of Differential Reinforcement Learning (Differential RL) is significant because it rigorously bridges the gap between continuous-time optimal control theory, particularly Pontryagin's Maximum Principle and Hamiltonian mechanics, and modern reinforcement learning. This reformulation provides a fresh perspective for analyzing and solving continuous state-action space RL problems.
2. **Inherent Physics-Informed Priors:** A core contribution is the natural embedding of physics-informed inductive biases through the Hamiltonian structure derived from the differential dual formulation. Unlike traditional RL methods that often require explicit constraints, customized reward shaping, or pre-learned physics models, Differential RL inherently promotes physically consistent trajectories. This is crucial for scientific and engineering domains where solutions must adhere to fundamental physical laws.
3. **Pointwise Control and Sample Efficiency:** The proposed Differential Policy Optimization (dfPO) algorithm's focus on learning local movement operators and its stage-wise, pointwise updates are significant for improving sample efficiency. By refining policy behavior locally along the trajectory, dfPO minimizes redundant exploration and aligns updates with system dynamics at each timestep, which is vital in scenarios where data acquisition (e.g., high-fidelity simulations) is costly.
4. **Novel Theoretical Guarantees:** The establishment of pointwise convergence guarantees for dfPO is a notable theoretical contribution. This property, uncommon in conventional RL, provides a finer-grained understanding of the algorithm's learning process, assuring that the learned policy is near-optimal at individual timesteps, not just in terms of cumulative reward. The derived competitive regret bounds, particularly the dimension-independent `O(K^(5/6))` for a restricted hypothesis space, further strengthens the theoretical foundation.
5. **Empirical Superiority in Scientific Domains:** The consistent outperformance of dfPO against 12 standard and reward-shaped RL baselines across diverse scientific computing tasks (surface modeling, grid control, molecular dynamics) under low-data and physics-constrained conditions demonstrates its practical utility and robustness in challenging real-world scenarios.

#### 5.2. Potential Impact

1. **Enhanced Scientific Discovery and Engineering Design:** By enabling more effective and reliable application of RL to complex physical systems, this work has the potential to accelerate scientific discovery and engineering design processes. This could include optimizing materials properties, designing more efficient aerodynamic structures, controlling complex multi-scale systems governed by PDEs, or exploring molecular conformations for drug discovery and protein engineering.
2. **Reduced Computational Costs:** The improved sample efficiency inherent in dfPO can significantly reduce the computational cost of applying RL in scientific domains, where simulations are often expensive and time-consuming. This could make previously intractable problems amenable to RL-based solutions.
3. **Foundation for Future Research:** The framework provides a theoretical bridge between control theory and RL, potentially inspiring new lines of research in areas such as adaptive discretization methods for continuous-time RL, developing specialized neural network architectures for Hamiltonian dynamics, and exploring the interplay between classical mechanics and deep learning.
4. **Broader Applicability of Physics-Informed AI:** This work contributes to the growing field of physics-informed artificial intelligence, offering a generalizable approach for embedding domain knowledge implicitly into learning systems. This could extend beyond the current scientific computing tasks to other areas like robotics, climate modeling, or medical imaging, where physical consistency is essential for reliable and safe operation.
5. **Robustness and Interpretability:** Pointwise convergence guarantees and inherent physical consistency can lead to more robust and potentially more interpretable policies, as their behavior is grounded in underlying physical principles, making them more trustworthy for critical applications.
