# ELASTIC: Efficiently Learning to Adaptively Scale Test-Time Compute for Generative Control Policies

> [!summary] Summary
> An algorithm called ELASTIC learns to adaptively scale test-time computation for Generative Control Policies (GCPs) in robotics, dynamically adjusting sequential denoising steps and parallel action samples. This approach consistently enhanced task success while reducing inference latency by up to 34% on real-world robot manipulation, outperforming fixed compute baselines.

## Problem

- Generative Control Policies (GCPs) benefit from test-time scaling (sequential refinement, parallel exploration) but existing methods use fixed, conservative compute allocations.
- Optimal compute allocation between sequential and parallel scaling is highly dependent on the robot's state, the specific task, and the base policy's characteristics, leading to inefficient fixed settings.
- Increased test-time computation directly translates to control latency, which is a critical limitation for real-time and reactive robotic systems.

## Method

- **ELASTIC** frames adaptive compute allocation as a ==meta-Markov Decision Process (meta-MDP)==, learning a ==meta-policy== to make decisions on sequential strides and parallel sample pruning at each denoising iteration.
- It employs a reward function that balances terminal task performance (estimated via an ==environment-level Q-function==) against penalties for both sequential and parallel computational costs.
- A ==hybrid reinforcement learning== approach is used for sample-efficient training, combining ==offline critic pretraining== with online rollouts augmented by ==counterfactual compute allocations==.

## Results

- ELASTIC consistently Pareto-dominated fixed and sequential-only scaling baselines in simulated tasks, achieving higher success rates for matched compute budgets and adaptively discovering task-specific bottlenecks.
- On real-world robot manipulation with a Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model, ELASTIC matched the performance of full Best-of-N scaling while reducing wall-clock inference latency by **34%**.
- Qualitative analysis showed the meta-policy adaptively increased sequential steps for precision (e.g., grasping) and parallel samples for mode selection (e.g., divergent actions) during different phases of tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Adaptive, state-dependent allocation of both sequential denoising steps and parallel action samples is crucial for optimizing GCP performance and efficiency, consistently outperforming static or single-axis scaling.
> - The learned meta-policy can autonomously identify and respond to task-specific bottlenecks, prioritizing precision through sequential steps when needed, or exploring multimodal actions via parallel samples.
> - The system allows base GCPs to compensate for inherent weaknesses or uncertainties by dynamically allocating more resources in states where the policy is less competent or faces ambiguity.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is situated within the field of robot control, specifically focusing on the efficient deployment and performance enhancement of Generative Control Policies (GCPs). GCPs, such as diffusion policies and flow-based Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, have demonstrated efficacy in generating complex and multimodal action distributions for robots. A key characteristic of these models is their inherent capability for "test-time scaling," where performance can be improved by allocating additional computation during inference, rather than through further training.

The paper identifies two primary axes for this test-time scaling:
1. **Sequential Scaling:** Involves increasing the number of denoising steps to refine generated actions, leading to greater precision.
2. **Parallel Scaling:** Entails sampling and denoising multiple candidate actions concurrently to explore different modes of the policy's action distribution, often followed by a verifier to select the optimal candidate (e.g., Best-of-N search).

The broader research landscape has explored these axes individually. Prior work on sequential scaling in robotics with diffusion policies includes distillation methods (which require retraining), advanced ODE solvers, and adaptive denoising schedules based on robot state using variance estimation or reinforcement learning. Similarly, parallel scaling has been investigated through Best-of-N search, often coupled with learning-to-search methods and verifiers for improving performance or aligning with preferences. Some methods attempt to reduce parallel overhead via early stopping, but often limit interventions to initial noise samples or neglect sequential denoising length.

A gap in existing research is the joint and adaptive allocation of both sequential and parallel compute. Current approaches typically employ fixed compute settings, which are often conservative and inefficient. The optimal allocation is complex, varying significantly based on the task, the robot's current state, and the base policy's characteristics. For instance, a task requiring precise contact might benefit more from sequential refinement, while a task with multiple viable action modes might necessitate broader parallel exploration. Critically, in robotics, increased test-time computation directly contributes to control latency, which is undesirable for real-time, reactive systems. While some work like ZIP-RC explores adaptive compute in language models, it relies on introspective signals (next-token logits) that do not have direct analogs in GCPs due to the real-world consequences of robotic actions and the nature of intermediate denoising updates.

This paper addresses this gap by proposing a method for learning state-dependent, joint compute allocation for GCPs, aiming to optimize task success while adhering to compute budgets and mitigating latency.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the inherent inefficiencies and performance limitations of fixed test-time compute allocations in Generative Control Policies (GCPs). While GCPs offer the benefit of test-time scaling—improving performance by adding computation during inference—determining the optimal way to allocate this computation is a non-trivial challenge.

The authors highlight several factors that make fixed compute allocations suboptimal:
- **State-Dependency:** The ideal balance between sequential refinement and parallel exploration can vary significantly with the robot's current state. For example, early in a manipulation task, broad parallel exploration might be beneficial to identify a correct approach, while closer to contact, finer sequential refinement might be critical for precision.
- **Task-Dependency:** Different tasks have different requirements. A task demanding high precision (e.g., peg insertion) might prioritize sequential steps, whereas a task with ambiguous goals or multiple valid action paths might benefit more from parallel sampling to explore modes.
- **Policy-Dependency:** The base policy's own competence and uncertainty can influence the optimal compute strategy. A policy uncertain about which action mode to select benefits more from parallel exploration, while one prone to imprecise actions benefits from sequential refinement.
- **Latency Constraints:** In real-time robotic control, test-time computation directly translates into control latency. Maximally scaling both sequential and parallel compute to guarantee performance is often impractical due to hardware limitations and response time requirements. Fixed, conservative settings lead to inefficient resource utilization.

Given these complexities, the overarching objective of this research is to develop an algorithm, named ELASTIC (Efficiently Learning to Adaptively Scale Test-time Compute for Generative Control Policies), that learns to adaptively allocate test-time computation for GCPs. Specifically, ELASTIC aims to:
1. **Learn state-dependent compute schedules:** The algorithm should dynamically adjust the number of sequential denoising steps and parallel samples based on the robot's current state, task, and policy characteristics.
2. **Maximize task success:** The adaptive allocation should lead to higher task completion rates.
3. **Minimize compute costs/latency:** The system should achieve high performance while minimizing the computational resources used, thereby reducing control latency.

The core motivation is to move beyond static, inefficient compute strategies towards an intelligent, adaptive system that leverages the full potential of GCPs for robust and responsive robotic control.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The ELASTIC algorithm frames the problem of adaptive test-time compute allocation as a meta-Markov Decision Process (meta-MDP). This meta-MDP is designed to learn a "meta-policy" that interacts with a frozen, pretrained robot policy (GCP) and makes decisions regarding sequential steps and parallel samples at each denoising iteration.

**Meta-MDP Formulation:**
- **States ($S$)**: A meta-state $s_k$ at denoising step $k$ concatenates the physical state ($x$), the $N$ partially-denoised action samples ($u_{1:N}^k$), their respective denoising times ($\tau_{1:N}^k$), and a mask ($m_{1:N}^k$) indicating active samples. The physical state $x$ remains constant throughout a single denoising process.
- **Actions ($A$)**: A meta-action $a$ is a vector of strides $\Delta\tau_{1:N}^k$, representing how much each active sample advances in its denoising process. Setting $\Delta\tau^{(i)}_k = 0$ for a sample $i$ effectively prunes or stops that sample, allowing for adaptive parallel scaling. This action space enables joint optimization of sequential (stride length) and parallel (sample pruning) scaling.
- **Transition ($T$)**: The transition function describes how the meta-state evolves. Active action samples are updated via the pretrained $\pi_{\text{GCP}}$ using the chosen strides. Pruned samples or fully denoised samples retain their state. Once all samples are either fully denoised or pruned, the un-pruned candidates are evaluated by an external verifier $Q_\phi$, and the highest-scoring action is executed in the physical environment, which then updates the physical state $x$.

**Reward Design:**
The meta-policy's reward function $r(\xi)$ is designed to balance task performance with computational cost:
$r(\xi) = Q_{\text{task}}(x, u) - \alpha L(\xi) - \beta (P(\xi) - 1)$
- **Task Performance Reward ($Q_{\text{task}}$)**: To address the challenging credit assignment problem in long-horizon tasks and across the nested denoising process, an environment-level Q-function ($Q_{\text{task}}$) estimates the expected return over the remainder of the real-world episode. This terminal value estimate is used as the task reward, folding downstream consequences into a single value and preserving policy optimality. For parallel scaling, $Q_\phi = Q_{\text{task}}$ also serves as the verifier for action selection.
- **Compute Reward**: Penalizes both sequential and parallel compute expenditures:
 - **Sequential Cost ($L(\xi)$)**: Defined as the length of the slowest-finishing sample's denoising trajectory, $L(\xi) = \max_{i \in [N]} \ell^{(i)}$.
 - **Parallel Cost ($P(\xi)$)**: Represents the time-averaged number of active samples, $P(\xi) = (\sum_{i=1}^N \ell^{(i)}(\xi)) / L(\xi)$.
- **Coefficients ($\alpha, \beta$)**: These hyperparameters allow flexible trade-offs between task performance and the costs associated with sequential and parallel computation, respectively.

**Solving the Meta-MDP:**
The meta-MDP is solved using Soft Actor-Critic (SAC), training a meta-policy $\pi_{\text{meta}}$ (actor) and a meta-critic $Q_{\text{meta}}$.

**Key Design Decisions for Efficient Meta-Policy Learning:**
1. **Permutation Equivariance/Invariance**: Since the N denoising samples are unordered, the meta-policy architecture uses attention over the sample set. This ensures the actor's denoising decisions are equivariant to sample permutations and the critic's value estimates are invariant.
2. **Centralized Actor and Critic**: A centralized design is employed to enable coordinated decision-making across samples. A decentralized approach would likely lead to symmetric decisions, hindering behaviors like pruning less promising samples.
3. **Physical State Conditioning**: The meta-policy's decisions are conditioned on the physical state $x$ by applying FiLM conditioning to the frozen base policy's observation embedding. This allows the compute allocation to adapt to the real-world context.

**Hybrid Reinforcement Learning for Sample Efficiency:**
To mitigate the challenges of exploration and sample efficiency in solving the meta-MDP, ELASTIC adopts a hybrid RL approach:
- **Phase 1: Offline Critic Pretraining**: The meta-critic is initially pretrained on an offline buffer generated from rollouts of the base GCP using various fixed denoising schedules.
- **Phase 2: Hybrid RL**: Online rollouts are conducted with the meta-policy controlling compute. The replay buffer is augmented with **counterfactual compute allocations**. This involves running the $\pi_{\text{GCP}}$ with all $N$ candidates active at a fixed stride and then subsampling these candidates to simulate different parallel budgets. Synthetic terminal transitions are generated for these subsamples, providing diverse (L, P) cost-reward tuples without additional environment interaction.

The overall inference process (Algorithm 1) involves the meta-policy iteratively deciding strides for all samples, pruning samples if $\Delta\tau=0$, running the GCP denoising step for active samples, and finally selecting an action via the verifier $Q_\phi$ once all active samples are processed.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates ELASTIC across simulated manipulation benchmarks with diffusion policies and on real-world robot manipulation with a Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model ($\pi_{0.5}$), demonstrating its capabilities against fixed and single-axis scaling baselines.

#### 4.1. ELASTIC's Test-time Compute Beats Fixed Allocation with Diffusion Policies (Simulation)

- **Setup**: Evaluated on six simulated tasks (PUSHT, PUSHT OBSTACLE, SQUARE PH, SQUARE MH, CAN PAIRED, CAN REVERSE) designed to surface train-test mismatches or mode ambiguities. Diffusion Policy was used as $\pi_{\text{GCP}}$. A verifier $Q_\phi$ was trained via Bellman Residual Minimization. Experiments were conducted under three different ($\alpha, \beta$) cost regimes to explore scenarios where parallel or sequential overheads are bottlenecks. Metrics included success rate, average sequential steps ($L$), and average parallel samples ($P$).
- **Performance vs. Baselines**: ELASTIC consistently Pareto-dominated fixed and single-axis (sequential-only) scaling baselines at matched compute budgets. The largest performance gains were observed on tasks where $\pi_{\text{GCP}}$ was trained on diverse or imperfect data (e.g., CAN PAIRED, SQUARE MH). The "Fixed" baseline, which used ELASTIC's average ($L, P$) but without state-dependence, was consistently outperformed, indicating the value of adaptive allocation.
- **Adaptive Bottleneck Discovery**:
 - **PUSHT**: Primarily benefited from sequential steps, with minimal gains from parallel samples.
 - **CAN and PUSHT OBSTACLE**: Sequential scaling benefits saturated quickly, while parallel samples significantly improved success by addressing mode selection issues.
 - **Qualitative Analysis (CAN PAIRED, Figure 3)**: ELASTIC learned to increase sequential steps during the grasping phase (for precision) and subsequently increased parallel candidates when the base policy's actions diverged into multiple modes (for mode selection), then reduced both once the mode ambiguity was resolved. This adaptive strategy allowed ELASTIC to achieve success with less total compute than full Best-of-N scaling.
 - **Adaptation to Base Policy Performance (SQUARE MH vs. PH, Figure 9)**: ELASTIC adjusted compute based on the quality of the base policy. The Mixed Human (MH) checkpoint, trained on lower-quality data, received more sequential steps and parallel samples throughout the trajectory compared to the Proficient Human (PH) checkpoint, demonstrating ELASTIC's ability to compensate for policy weaknesses.

#### 4.2. ELASTIC Yields Efficient Test-time Scaling with Vision-Language-Action Models (Simulation & Hardware)

- **Setup**: Used the $\pi_{0.5}$ VLA model. Simulation on LIBERO-10 benchmark. Real-world task involved a Franka Emika arm placing a purple mug in a basket, chosen for its challenge in object discrimination and precise placement. V-GPS was fine-tuned as the verifier $Q_\phi$. Wall-clock latency was measured.
- **LIBERO-10 (Simulation)**:
 - ELASTIC matched the performance improvements of Sequential-only scaling but with 6% less inference latency than the Base VLA (which uses a fixed 10 sequential steps).
 - Allocation patterns varied significantly by task and state (Figures 4, 12). Tasks with high base policy success showed low parallelism, while tasks where the base policy benefited from the verifier (e.g., tasks 8 and 9) showed higher parallelism near grasp and place states.
- **Real-World Robot Manipulation (Hardware)**:
 - ELASTIC matched the performance of full Best-of-N scaling (with V-GPS) while reducing wall-clock inference latency by 34%.
 - **Adaptive Schedule (Figure 6)**: The meta-policy adaptively allocated more parallel samples at the start (to resolve ambiguity of which mug to pick) and during the placing action. Sequential steps varied less but showed peaks near grasping and prior to placing for precision.
 - The results suggested that state conditioning becomes more valuable at smaller compute budgets, as ELASTIC outperformed a comparable fixed allocation baseline.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces ELASTIC, an algorithm that addresses the challenge of efficiently allocating test-time computation for Generative Control Policies (GCPs) in robotics. The findings demonstrate that adaptive, state-dependent compute scheduling can significantly improve task success and efficiency compared to fixed or single-axis scaling approaches.

The significance and potential impact of this work are multi-faceted:

- **Enhanced Robotic Performance and Efficiency**: By learning to dynamically adjust sequential denoising steps and parallel action samples, ELASTIC enables robots to achieve higher task success rates while simultaneously reducing computational overhead and wall-clock latency. This is particularly crucial for real-time, reactive robotic systems where prompt decision-making and low latency are critical.
- **Autonomous Resource Management**: The meta-policy learns to autonomously identify and respond to bottlenecks in task execution. For instance, it can prioritize sequential refinement when precision is required (e.g., grasping) and switch to parallel exploration when dealing with multimodal action distributions or ambiguities (e.g., selecting among multiple objects or avoiding obstacles). This reduces the need for manual tuning of compute parameters.
- **Improved Generalization for GCPs**: GCPs, like diffusion policies and Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, benefit from this adaptive scaling by being able to perform robustly even in scenarios that deviate from their training data distribution or when trained on imperfect demonstrations. ELASTIC allows the base policy to compensate for its inherent weaknesses or uncertainties in a state-dependent manner.
- **Broader Applicability**: The meta-MDP formulation and hybrid RL training approach are designed to be generic, making ELASTIC applicable across different types of GCPs (diffusion models, flow-based models) and a variety of manipulation tasks, from simple pushes to complex pick-and-place operations involving language conditioning.
- **Foundation for Future Adaptive Systems**: This work lays a foundation for more sophisticated adaptive inference systems in AI, particularly in domains where computational resources are constrained, and decision-making quality is paramount. It demonstrates how a learned meta-controller can optimize the execution of another, more complex policy.

**Limitations:**
The paper identifies the primary limiting factor for ELASTIC's effectiveness as the **quality of the learned verifier ($Q_\phi$)**. A noisy or inaccurate verifier can mislead the meta-policy, diminishing its ability to distinguish between high-quality and low-quality actions, thereby hurting both meta-policy optimization and the benefits of parallel scaling. The authors acknowledge that the development of general-purpose reward models and verifiers is an active area of research, suggesting that improvements in this domain will directly enhance ELASTIC's performance.

Another limitation is that the **meta-policy is currently trained per task**, which can be resource-intensive, especially for multi-task policies like VLAs. A promising future direction suggested by the authors is to investigate whether the meta-policy can generalize to unseen environments by conditioning on the base policy's internal features and action samples, thereby reducing the need for task-specific training.

Despite these limitations, ELASTIC represents a step towards making advanced generative control policies more practical and efficient for deployment in complex, real-world robotic applications.
