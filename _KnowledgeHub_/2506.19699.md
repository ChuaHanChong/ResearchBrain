# UniTac-NV: A Unified Tactile Representation For Non-Vision-Based Tactile Sensors

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Imperial College London introduce UniTac-NV, a framework that unifies tactile data from non-vision-based sensors into a shared latent space. This approach facilitates cross-sensor data transfer and enables generalizable algorithms for downstream tasks, maintaining high reconstruction fidelity and supporting contact geometry estimation.

## Problem

- The significant diversity in sensing mechanisms and data modalities among non-vision-based tactile sensors hinders the development of generalizable algorithms.
- Sensor obsolescence challenges the continuity and relevance of research built on specific, often discontinued, tactile hardware.
- Limited research exists on representation learning and cross-sensor information sharing specifically for non-vision-based tactile sensors, unlike their vision-based counterparts.

## Method

- Developed **UniTac-NV**, a ==Cross-Sensor Tactile Representation Autoencoder== featuring distinct encoders for each sensor type and a ==single shared decoder==.
- Employed a ==sample-matched training== strategy where paired contact interactions from different sensors are simultaneously fed, with a total reconstruction loss calculated across ==self- and cross-reconstructions==.
- The model maps raw sensor data from varied formats (e.g., 72 readings from Xela uSkin, 27 from Contactile PapillArray) into a unified ==16-dimensional latent space==.

## Results

- Achieved high data reconstruction performance for 'seen' objects (SSIM typically > **0.95**, low NMAE) for both self-reconstruction and cross-sensor transfer between uSkin and PapillArray.
- t-SNE visualizations demonstrated significant alignment of latent representations for matched samples and enhanced separability of object material and shape in the unified latent space post-training.
- In a contact geometry estimation task on an 'unseen' irregular object, self-latent evaluations yielded average errors of **0.353 mm** (uSkin) and **0.397 mm** (PapillArray), while cross-sensor latent transfer resulted in increased errors of approximately **0.6 mm**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Implicit alignment of latent spaces is achieved through reconstruction loss on sample-matched data, enabling the unified representation to retain physical properties like object material and shape.
> - The unified latent space enables downstream tasks, such as contact geometry estimation, to generalize to unseen objects and even across different sensor types without re-training the task-specific model.
> - While a unified latent space is achievable, the fidelity of cross-sensor information transfer for complex tasks is ultimately constrained by the inherent morphological and functional differences of the underlying sensor hardware.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Tactile sensing is recognized as a crucial component for enhancing robotic perception, particularly in scenarios where visual information is limited, such as in occluded environments or low-light conditions. Tactile data can also provide insights into object properties inherently difficult to infer visually, including detailed surface shape, friction, stiffness, and texture. Consequently, tactile sensing has demonstrated utility across various robotic applications, including in-hand manipulation, object identification, characterization, and grasp stability estimation.

Despite the demonstrated importance and potential of tactile sensing, the development and widespread adoption of generalizable tactile algorithms have been hindered. A primary reason for this limitation is the considerable diversity in sensing mechanisms and modalities among currently available tactile sensors. For instance, sensors like the DIGIT capture high-resolution RGB images of surface deformations, while others like the Contactile PapillArray provide 3-DoF force-torque readings from discrete sensing units. This heterogeneity makes it challenging to develop algorithms that can operate effectively across different sensor types without extensive re-training or adaptation. Additionally, the issue of sensor obsolescence, exemplified by once-popular sensors like BioTac and TakkTile becoming unavailable, poses a significant challenge to the continuity and relevance of research built upon specific hardware.

In recent years, research efforts have explored methods for cross-sensor transfer, particularly among *vision-based* tactile sensors such as DIGIT, GelSight, and TacTip. These sensors typically employ cameras to detect micrometer-level elastomer deformations and produce image-based outputs. Their shared internal structures and comparable data formats have facilitated the development of generalizable approaches, including self-supervised pre-training on large datasets and contrastive learning techniques to align data representations. This has enabled data collected on one vision-based sensor to be effectively transferred and utilized by others.

However, vision-based tactile sensors possess inherent limitations, including relatively low operating frequencies (often 30 Hz), high latencies (100-200 ms), and bulky form factors (around 40 mm). These characteristics can render them unsuitable for high-speed dynamic tasks or for comprehensive integration into robotic hands. In such contexts, *non-vision-based* tactile sensors often offer advantages. For example, the Xela uSkin provides various configurations with a thin profile, suitable for covering large robotic hand surfaces, while the Contactile PapillArray offers high temporal resolution (up to 1000 Hz) for dynamic force measurement and slip detection.

Despite the practical advantages of non-vision-based sensors in certain applications, there has been limited research focusing on representation learning and cross-sensor information sharing for this category of tactile sensors. This gap means that the benefits of cross-sensor generalization observed in vision-based systems have not been widely extended to their non-vision-based counterparts. This work directly addresses this research gap by proposing a framework for unifying tactile data representations across non-vision-based sensors, aiming to foster more generalizable tactile algorithms and overcome issues related to sensor diversity and obsolescence in this domain.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a unified tactile representation that can bridge data across diverse *non-vision-based* tactile sensors. This aims to enable generalizable algorithms and facilitate data transfer in the tactile sensing domain, which currently faces significant challenges due to sensor heterogeneity and obsolescence.

The key motivations driving this work include:

- **Addressing the Gap in Non-Vision-Based Tactile Sensor Generalization:** Existing efforts for cross-sensor transfer have largely focused on vision-based tactile sensors. This paper explicitly targets non-vision-based sensors, which, despite their distinct advantages (e.g., higher operating frequencies, lower latencies, thinner form factors), lack similar frameworks for data unification and generalization.
- **Enabling Generalizable Tactile Algorithms:** The diversity of tactile sensing mechanisms and output modalities currently necessitates sensor-specific algorithm development, hindering the broader applicability of tactile research. By creating a sensor-agnostic latent space, the research aims to pave the way for algorithms that are less dependent on specific sensor hardware.
- **Mitigating Sensor Obsolescence:** The rapid evolution and discontinuation of tactile sensor models can render previous research challenging to replicate or continue. A unified representation would allow for the transfer of learned knowledge and algorithms to new or different sensors, thereby preserving the utility of research even as hardware changes.
- **Facilitating Data Sharing and Transfer:** A common, unified representation would allow data collected from one type of non-vision-based tactile sensor to be directly utilized or translated for another. This can significantly reduce the data collection burden and enhance the efficiency of research and development.
- **Supporting Downstream Applications without Retraining:** A core motivation is to demonstrate that downstream robotic tasks, such as contact geometry estimation, can leverage the unified latent representation directly. This would allow models trained on data from one sensor's latent space to be applied to another sensor's latent space without requiring re-training of the task-specific model, thereby improving efficiency and versatility in robotic systems.
- **Implicit Learning of Sensor-Agnostic Physical Properties:** The proposed training strategy is designed to implicitly align latent representations, ensuring they capture underlying physical properties of contact interactions (e.g., shape, material) rather than sensor-specific noise or artifacts.

By addressing these motivations, the UniTac-NV framework seeks to enhance the accessibility, applicability, and longevity of tactile sensing research and deployment in robotics.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces UniTac-NV, a Cross-Sensor Tactile Representation Autoencoder, designed to unify tactile data from non-vision-based sensors into a shared latent space.

**A. The UniTac-NV Autoencoder Model**

1. **Multi-Encoder Architecture:** The UniTac-NV model is an encoder-decoder network. It features a distinct encoder for each tactile sensor type involved in the unification process. These encoders map raw sensor data into a common, low-dimensional latent space. A single, shared decoder then reconstructs tactile readings from this latent space for all sensors.
 - **Encoder Structure:** Each encoder takes flattened raw sensor data as input. For the Xela uSkin (uSPa 46), this involves 72 readings (4x6 sensing units, 3-DoF each), and for the Contactile PapillArray, 27 readings (3x3 sensing units, 3-DoF each). Each encoder consists of two hidden dense layers (64 and 48 units), followed by a projection layer to a 16-dimensional latent space. Dropout layers are integrated between all layers to prevent overfitting.
 - **Shared Decoder Structure:** The shared decoder processes the 16-dimensional latent data through two hidden dense layers (64 and 96 units). Its output is a combined 99-dimensional vector, representing the reconstructed readings for both the uSkin (72 dimensions) and the PapillArray (27 dimensions).

2. **Sample-Matched Training:** The core of the UniTac-NV approach lies in its training strategy. During each training pass, the model is fed *matching contact interactions* collected from different sensors. This means that data samples corresponding to the same object shape and pressing forces, but recorded by different sensors, are processed simultaneously.
 - **Loss Function:** The encoders produce individual latent representations for each sensor's input. The shared decoder then uses these latent representations to generate four distinct reconstructions: two *self-reconstructions* (each sensor's latent representation reconstructing its own input) and two *cross-reconstructions* (each latent representation reconstructing the input from the *other* sensor). The total loss function is calculated as the sum of the Mean Absolute Errors (MAE) for these four reconstructions. This structure implicitly aligns the latent spaces, as the decoder requires consistent latent features to accurately reconstruct the paired input data, regardless of the originating sensor.

3. **Single-Encoder Inference:** After training, the model provides sensor-specific encoders and a shared decoder. For practical applications, tactile data from a specific sensor can be fed into its corresponding encoder to obtain the unified latent representation. This latent representation can then be used directly for downstream tasks or passed through the shared decoder to translate the data into the format of any other sensor included in the training.

**B. Hardware Setup**

The experimental setup utilized a UR5e robotic arm, equipped with Robotiq Force Copilot software, to execute consistent and force-monitored movements during data collection. A Thorlabs MB1224 optical breadboard secured experiment objects at known positions using a 3D-printed adapter, allowing for precise 90-degree object rotations.

Two non-vision-based tactile sensors were employed, both operating at 100 Hz:
- **Xela uSkin (uSPa 46):** A Hall-effect-based sensor with a 30 mm × 50 mm sensing area, featuring 4x6 sensing units, each detecting 3-DoF force. A thin elastic sheet covers the units, creating a continuous sensing surface.
- **Contactile PapillArray:** A sensor with a 24 mm × 24 mm sensing area, employing 3x3 3-DoF optical force sensing units. Unlike the uSkin, its units are directly exposed, and the central unit is slightly protruded.
Customized sensor adapters ensured that the contact surface centers of both sensors maintained an identical pose relative to the UR5e's tool flange, ensuring consistency during data collection.

**C. Experiment Objects**

The study utilized a set of seven 3D-printed objects:
- **Six "Seen" Objects:** These were hollow prisms (circular, square, hexagonal bases), each printed in two materials: rigid PLA and soft TPU. The hollowness of the soft TPU versions allowed for deformation under force.
- **One "Unseen" Object:** An irregular rigid PLA prismatic object featuring six corners with angles varying from 80 to 160 degrees. This object was used to test the model's generalization capabilities to novel geometries.

**D. Data Collection**

A standardized and reproducible data collection procedure was implemented:
1. **Pressing Sequence:** Sensors were pressed horizontally against objects at a constant height, targeting the object's centerline, along a 90-degree arc. Each press was force-controlled, stopping once a 10 N z-axis force was detected by the UR5e's force-torque sensor. A total of 91 presses were performed with 1-degree angular steps for each object. For the irregular object, this was repeated four times with 90-degree object rotations to ensure full outline coverage.
2. **Sensor Interchange:** This entire pressing sequence was repeated for both the uSkin and PapillArray to acquire matching tactile data for identical contact interactions.
3. **Data Extraction:** From the time-series data, 25 specific target z-axis forces (ranging from 4 to 10 N in 0.25 N intervals) were selected. Tactile data frames closest to these force targets were extracted for each sensor, approach angle, and object. This resulted in a dataset of 2,275 matched contact interactions per object between the two sensors.

**E. Data Preparation & Model Training**

- **Data Splitting:** Data from the six "seen" objects were divided into 90% training and 10% testing sets using a stratified approach. For each object, 10% of the approaching angles were randomly selected, and all corresponding data samples (across varying pressing forces) were allocated to the test set to prevent data leakage. All data collected from the "unseen" irregular object were entirely allocated to the testing set. This ensures paired samples with consistent object, angle, and force for both sensors.
- **Training Parameters:** The UniTac-NV model was trained for 1000 epochs on the training set using the Adam optimizer, with a learning rate of 5e-4, a dropout rate of 0.007, and a batch size of 64.

**F. Metrics**

To evaluate model performance, two primary metrics were used for data reconstruction quality, and one for latent space alignment:
- **Normalized Mean Absolute Error (NMAE):** Quantifies the average absolute difference between original (X) and reconstructed ( $\hat{X}$ ) sensor data, normalized by the range of the data.
- **Structural Similarity Index (SSIM):** Measures discrepancies in spatial force distribution and relative magnitudes, calculated per force channel (x, y, z) and averaged. It considers luminance, contrast, and structure components.
- **Manhattan Distance:** Used to quantify the alignment quality between latent spaces generated from matched data samples by each sensor's encoder.

**G. Downstream Task: Contact Geometry Estimation**

To demonstrate the practical utility of the unified latent space, a contact geometry estimation task was performed on the "unseen" irregular object.
- **Downstream Data Preparation:** 10% of the data from the unseen object (920 samples) formed the test set, with the remaining 8,280 samples used for training. Ground truths for contact shape were derived from the object's STL file: a 2D outline was extracted, rotated to align the pressing trajectory with the y-axis, and 11 evenly spaced points along a 14 mm sensing range (centered at the contact origin) were sampled.
- **Downstream Model & Training:** A Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) was employed, consisting of four hidden dense layers (64, 128, 64, 32 units) with dropout layers. This MLP mapped the 16-dimensional latent representations (obtained from the previously trained UniTac-NV encoders) to an 11-dimensional geometry vector. Training involved an L1 loss function, Adam optimizer (learning rate 5e-5, weight decay 1e-3), and 80 epochs with a batch size of 64. Separate MLP models were trained using latent spaces from the uSkin and PapillArray, and then evaluated on test sets from both sensors.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of UniTac-NV focused on assessing implicit latent space alignment, data reconstruction fidelity (self-reconstruction and cross-sensor transfer), and the utility of the unified representation in a downstream task.

**A. Implicit Latent Space Alignment & Separation**

- **Alignment During Training:** The training process successfully reduced both the reconstruction errors (Normalized MAE) and the Manhattan distance between latent space representations of matched data samples from different sensors. These metrics, calculated on the unseen test set, consistently decreased over 1000 epochs, indicating that the network learns to align the latent spaces implicitly.
- **Visualization of Latent Space:** t-SNE visualizations of the latent space after the first and final training epochs demonstrate this alignment. Initially, latent representations from different sensors for the same contact interaction are disparate. After training, these representations become significantly more aligned. Furthermore, the latent spaces also exhibited enhanced separability with respect to object material (rigid vs. soft) and object shape, implying that the unified representation retains crucial physical characteristics in a sensor-agnostic manner. This alignment and separation were achieved implicitly through the reconstruction loss, without explicit alignment or separation terms in the objective function.

**B. Data Reconstruction Results: Self-Reconstruction and Cross-Sensor Transfer**

- **Visual Fidelity:** A selection of qualitative examples of reconstructed tactile data (quiver plots) shows that most reconstructions, for both seen and unseen objects, were visually similar to or indistinguishable from the original sensor readings.
- **Quantitative Performance (Seen Objects):** For the six "seen" objects (combinations of circular, square, hexagonal prisms in PLA and TPU), UniTac-NV achieved high reconstruction performance. Self-reconstructions (e.g., uSkin to uSkin, PapillArray to PapillArray) consistently yielded high Structural Similarity Index (SSIM) values, generally above 0.95, and low Normalized Mean Absolute Error (NMAE). Cross-sensor transfers (uSkin to PapillArray, PapillArray to uSkin) also performed well, with SSIM values typically above 0.95, showing only slightly elevated NMAE compared to individual autoencoders trained separately on each sensor. This indicates that the unified latent space effectively preserves information for known contact types.
- **Quantitative Performance (Unseen Irregular Object):** On the more complex, "unseen" irregular object, self-reconstructions maintained strong performance, with uSkin to uSkin achieving an NMAE of 0.111 and SSIM of 0.862, and PapillArray to PapillArray achieving an NMAE of 0.036 and SSIM of 0.982. This suggests good generalization of information preservation within the same sensor's representation.
 - **Cross-Sensor Performance for Unseen Object:** While transferring uSkin data to the PapillArray (upsampling from 3x3 to 4x6 taxels in essence) still performed reasonably well (NMAE 0.136, SSIM 0.858), the transfer from PapillArray to uSkin showed a noticeable degradation (NMAE 0.318, SSIM 0.581). This disparity is attributed to the inherent differences in sensor design, particularly the PapillArray's lower taxel density and the need for significant upsampling when reconstructing uSkin's higher-resolution data.

**C. Downstream Task: Contact Geometry Estimation**

- **Overall Performance:** The downstream task of estimating local contact geometry for the unseen irregular object yielded satisfactory results across all scenarios (single-sensor and cross-sensor latent inference), producing recognizable object shapes. This indicates that the unified latent space, despite being trained on different objects, successfully captures sufficient geometric information for generalization.
- **Performance Disparity in Cross-Sensor Transfer:**
 - When the MLP model was trained and tested using latent representations from the *same* sensor (e.g., uSkin latent for training and uSkin latent for inference), the average estimation errors were 0.353 mm for uSkin and 0.397 mm for PapillArray.
 - When cross-sensor latent transfer was involved (e.g., trained on uSkin latent, tested on PapillArray latent, or vice versa), the average errors increased to 0.637 mm and 0.666 mm, respectively.
- **Limitations and Interpretations:** This performance reduction in cross-sensor downstream tasks suggests that while a shared latent space is achieved, the information truly transferable between sensors is limited by their inherent physical and morphological differences. For instance, the PapillArray's exposed, slightly protruded sensing nubs are better suited for capturing specific shear force dynamics and 3D contact information compared to the uSkin's larger, flatter sensing surface. Consequently, only information common to both sensor modalities appears to be effectively transferred for task execution, highlighting that fundamental hardware capabilities remain a factor in downstream task performance across different sensor types. Specific complex features, such as sharp 80° corners resulting in minimal contact, were challenging for both self-reconstruction and cross-transfer, leading to reduced fidelity in local contact identification.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents UniTac-NV, a framework that addresses a significant challenge in tactile sensing by enabling a unified representation for non-vision-based tactile sensors. This work carries several implications for the broader field of robotics and tactile sensing:

- **Proof of Concept for Non-Vision-Based Tactile Generalization:** The study successfully demonstrates the feasibility of achieving cross-sensor generalization for non-vision-based tactile sensors, a domain previously less explored compared to vision-based counterparts. This validates the approach of using an encoder-decoder architecture with sample-matched training to create a sensor-agnostic latent space.
- **Enabling Generalizable Tactile Algorithms:** By providing a unified, sensor-agnostic representation, UniTac-NV reduces the dependency of tactile algorithms on specific hardware modalities. This can significantly accelerate the development and deployment of tactile-enabled robotic systems by allowing researchers to focus on core algorithmic challenges rather than sensor-specific adaptations.
- **Mitigating Sensor Diversity and Obsolescence Challenges:** The ability to transfer tactile data and even task-specific models between different sensor types helps to mitigate issues arising from the wide variety of available sensors and the rapid obsolescence of specific models. Research efforts can have a longer lifespan, and algorithms developed on one platform may be readily adapted for others, preserving scientific investment.
- **Facilitating Data Sharing and Reduced Data Collection:** The unified latent space enables data collected with one sensor to be translated and utilized by another, potentially reducing the extensive time and resources typically required for data collection across multiple tactile sensor platforms. This fosters a more collaborative and efficient research environment.
- **Practical Utility in Downstream Tasks:** The demonstration of contact geometry estimation using the unified latent space highlights the practical utility of the framework. The ability to directly apply downstream models trained on one sensor's latent representation to another without retraining represents a step towards more robust and flexible robotic control and perception systems. This capability is particularly valuable in scenarios where a robot might need to switch between different tactile end-effectors or adapt to new sensor technologies.
- **Insights into Information Transfer Limitations:** The observed performance variability in cross-sensor transfer, particularly for the downstream geometry estimation task, offers valuable insights. It suggests that while a unified latent space can be learned, the effectiveness of transferring information for complex tasks is constrained by the inherent morphological and functional differences between sensors. This points towards a crucial area for future research: understanding which specific physical properties are truly shared across different tactile modalities and how to best capture and transfer them.
- **Contribution of a Reproducible Data Collection Procedure:** The detailed, reproducible data collection procedure proposed in this paper provides a valuable resource for the tactile sensing community. It allows for consistent data acquisition, facilitating the expansion of datasets with new contact interactions and sensor types, which is essential for advancing generalizable tactile AI.

The work serves as a foundational step, and its identified limitations, such as the dataset's scope and the impact of sensor morphological differences on transfer fidelity, clearly outline pathways for future research. Expanding the framework with a larger, more diverse dataset, incorporating additional non-vision-based and potentially vision-based sensors, and exploring a wider range of object properties and interaction dynamics are critical next steps to further enhance the robustness and information preservation capabilities of unified tactile representations.
