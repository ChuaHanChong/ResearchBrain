# Rubrics as Rewards: Reinforcement Learning Beyond Verifiable Domains

> [!summary] Summary
> The 'Rubrics as Rewards' (RaR) framework enables reinforcement learning for large language models (LLMs) in complex, real-world reasoning tasks that traditionally lack single verifiable answers. This approach uses instance-specific, LLM-generated rubrics as structured reward signals, resulting in improved model performance and enhanced LLM judge alignment with human preferences across various tasks.

## Problem

- Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) is constrained to tasks with clear, binary correctness signals, limiting its application in nuanced, real-world reasoning domains.
- Existing reward mechanisms for complex, subjective tasks (e.g., preference models) often lead to learning superficial artifacts, are costly to annotate, and lack transparency.
- A scalable and interpretable method for providing multi-criteria feedback to LLMs in expert domains, such as medicine and science, is needed where 'correctness' is multifaceted.

## Method

- Introduces the ==Rubrics as Rewards (RaR) framework==, formalizing instance-specific rubrics as the core mechanism to generate structured reward signals for on-policy reinforcement learning.
- Leverages powerful LLMs (e.g., GPT-4o) to synthetically generate ==dynamic, instance-specific rubrics== at scale, grounded by golden reference answers, for comprehensive and weighted feedback.
- Explores two reward aggregation strategies—==Explicit== (weighted sum of criteria) and ==Implicit== (holistic LLM-as-judge scoring)—to convert multi-criteria rubric feedback into a single scalar reward for GRPO-based policy updates.

## Results

- RaR-Implicit achieved relative performance gains of up to **31%** on rubric-based evaluations (HealthBench) and up to **7%** on verifiable multiple-choice tasks (GPQA-Diamond) over Likert-based baselines.
- Policies trained with LLM-generated, reference-guided synthetic rubrics performed comparably to those trained with human-authored rubrics, validating the scalability and quality of the rubric generation process.
- Rubric guidance consistently improved LLM-judge pairwise preference accuracy with human judgments across all judge model sizes, with the most notable improvements observed for smaller judge models.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Effective reinforcement learning in complex, non-verifiable domains critically depends on instance-specific rubrics that are tailored to the prompt's context, rather than generic criteria.
> - Structured rubric guidance significantly improves the alignment of LLM judges (especially smaller models) with human preferences, indicating rubrics act as valuable scaffolding for nuanced evaluation.
> - Implicit aggregation of rubric criteria by a powerful LLM judge generally yields stronger training outcomes than explicit weighting, suggesting advanced LLMs can effectively integrate complex criteria.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of large language models (LLMs) has seen remarkable advancements, with Reinforcement Learning (RL) playing a critical role in aligning these models with human preferences and improving their performance on specific tasks. **Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR)** has emerged as a particularly effective paradigm for tasks where correctness can be objectively verified, such as mathematical problem-solving or code generation. In these domains, reward models can often be replaced by deterministic scoring functions or test cases that automatically provide binary feedback on correctness.

However, a significant challenge in the broader LLM research landscape is extending these RLVR successes to **unstructured, real-world reasoning tasks**. These tasks, prevalent in expert domains like medicine, law, or complex scientific inquiry, often lack a single "ground truth" answer. Instead, their evaluation depends on **nuanced, multi-criteria judgments** that cannot be reduced to simple binary correctness.

Current approaches to address this gap have their limitations:
- **Preference-based reward models (e.g., through Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback - RLHF):** While powerful, these models are prone to learning superficial artifacts (e.g., response length, formatting quirks, annotator biases) rather than deep reasoning. They also require extensive and costly human pairwise comparisons, which can be an operational overhead and limit scalability.
- **Generative reward models or models leveraging internal confidence estimates:** These are active areas of research, but a general-purpose and reliably specified reward signal for subjective, multi-criteria tasks remains elusive.
- **Expansion of verifiable datasets:** While efforts to broaden RLVR datasets beyond STEM domains are ongoing, they primarily focus on finding more *verifiable* tasks, not on addressing the core challenge of *subjective, multi-criteria evaluation*.

This paper introduces **Rubrics as Rewards (RaR)** as a **paradigm shift** that aims to carve out a valuable middle ground between the simplicity of verifiable rewards and the expressiveness (and challenges) of coarse preference rankings. It builds upon recent trends in using **instance-specific rubrics** for nuanced evaluation in expert domains (e.g., HealthBench, Pathak et al.), but critically, it extends their application beyond mere evaluation to directly serve as **structured reward signals for on-policy RL training**.

By converting rubrics into dynamic, instance-specific reward functions, RaR directly addresses the challenge of providing scalable, interpretable, and multi-criteria feedback in domains where objective correctness is multifaceted. This work distinguishes itself from concurrent research that primarily uses rubrics for conditioning preference data (e.g., for DPO or safety alignment) or for grading outputs, by directly integrating them into the on-policy learning loop for **expert-level reasoning tasks**. Thus, RaR represents a significant step towards making reinforcement learning more applicable and effective in complex, real-world scenarios where human-like judgment is paramount.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core motivation behind the "Rubrics as Rewards" (RaR) framework stems from the recognized gap in applying traditional Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) to complex, real-world reasoning tasks. While RLVR excels in domains with clear, binary correctness signals like mathematics or coding, its efficacy diminishes in fields requiring nuanced, multi-criteria human judgment.

The **key objectives** of this research are:

1. **Extend RLVR beyond Verifiable Domains:** The primary objective is to overcome the limitations of RLVR in environments lacking single, objectively verifiable answers. This entails developing a method that can provide effective reward signals for tasks characterized by subjective, multi-criteria evaluation, such as medical diagnosis explanations or complex scientific problem-solving.
2. **Introduce Rubrics as Core Reward Mechanisms:** To achieve the first objective, the authors aim to formalize and implement rubrics—structured sets of criteria—as the central mechanism for generating reward signals in an on-policy reinforcement learning framework. This moves beyond merely using rubrics for post-hoc evaluation and integrates them directly into the learning process.
3. **Develop Instance-Specific Rubric Generation:** Recognize that generic rubrics are insufficient for complex tasks, the paper aims to develop a scalable method for synthesizing instance-specific rubrics. These rubrics are designed to be dynamic and tailored to individual prompts, capturing unique contextual requirements and potential failure modes.
4. **Evaluate Reward Aggregation Strategies:** Investigate and compare different computational approaches for converting multi-dimensional rubric feedback into a single, scalar reward signal suitable for RL training. This includes both explicit, weighted aggregation and implicit, LLM-driven holistic scoring.
5. **Demonstrate Improved Model Performance and Generalization:** Empirically validate that models trained with rubric-based rewards significantly outperform traditional Likert-based or reference-guided baselines across various evaluation settings, including both rubric-scored and verifiable multiple-choice tasks.
6. **Enhance LLM Judge Alignment and Robustness:** Show that providing structured rubric guidance to LLM judges improves their alignment with human preferences and reduces the performance variance observed across different judge model scales, especially benefiting smaller judge models.
7. **Release Datasets for Public Use:** Contribute new datasets (RaR-Medicine and RaR-Science) comprising prompts and their corresponding synthetic, instance-specific rubrics to foster further research in this area.

The **motivation** for these objectives is multifaceted:
- **Addressing Reward Signal Limitations:** Existing reward mechanisms for non-verifiable domains are either too sparse (like binary correctness) or prone to "reward hacking" and overfitting superficial characteristics (like preference models). Rubrics offer a more robust, transparent, and structured form of supervision.
- **Interpretability and Modularity:** Rubrics decompose complex notions of "goodness" into human-interpretable subgoals. This provides a clearer, more modular, and transparent feedback mechanism compared to opaque scalar scores from preference models, facilitating debugging and targeted improvements.
- **Scalability:** By leveraging powerful LLMs to generate instance-specific rubrics, the framework aims to provide a scalable alternative to costly human annotation for complex reward modeling.
- **Bridging the Gap to Real-World Applications:** By enabling RL for subjective domains, RaR aims to unlock the potential of LLMs to perform expert-level reasoning in high-stakes fields like healthcare, where nuanced understanding and explanation are critical.

In essence, the authors are motivated to create a principled, scalable, and interpretable method for training LLMs to excel in the vast, complex landscape of human reasoning that lies beyond simple verifiable facts.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The "Rubrics as Rewards" (RaR) framework introduces an on-policy reinforcement learning method that leverages instance-specific rubrics as structured reward signals. The methodology can be broken down into several key components: problem formulation, reward aggregation strategies, rubric generation, and training/evaluation setup.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The paper formalizes a structured reward function for domains without single ground-truth answers. For an input prompt `x` and a generated response `ŷ` from a policy `πθ`, the reward `r(x, ŷ)` is defined based on a set of `k` rubric items `{(w_j, c_j)}`. Each rubric item `j` has a weight `w_j` and a binary correctness function `c_j(x, ŷ)` which evaluates whether the response satisfies that specific criterion. This formulation generalizes RLVR, where `k=1` and `c_1` is a simple verifiable correctness check.

#### 3.2. Reward Aggregation Strategies

Two main strategies are explored to convert the multi-criteria rubric feedback into a single scalar reward:

- **Explicit Aggregation (RaR-EXPLICIT):** Each rubric criterion `c_j` is independently evaluated by an LLM-as-judge (e.g., gpt-4o-mini). The final normalized reward is calculated as a weighted sum of the satisfied criteria:
 `r(x, ŷ) = (∑_j w_j ⋅ c_j(x, ŷ)) / (∑_j w_j)`
 In experiments, categorical labels ("Essential," "Important," "Optional," "Pitfall") are manually mapped to numerical weights (1.0, 0.7, 0.3, 0.9 respectively), where pitfalls contribute positively if avoided/satisfied.
- **Implicit Aggregation (RaR-IMPLICIT):** All rubric criteria, along with their categorical weights, are provided to an LLM-as-judge. This judge then holistically evaluates the response and outputs a single scalar Likert-scale rating (1-10), normalized to. This approach delegates the complex aggregation logic to the LLM itself, potentially capturing nuanced interactions between criteria.

#### 3.3. Rubric Generation

This is a critical component, as the quality of the rubrics directly impacts the effectiveness of the reward signal. The authors outline four desiderata for effective rubric generation:
1. **Grounded in Expert Guidance:** Rubrics should reflect domain expertise, ideally derived from human experts or high-quality proxies.
2. **Comprehensive Coverage:** Rubrics should cover multiple dimensions of response quality (accuracy, coherence, completeness, safety, etc.), including "pitfall" criteria for common errors.
3. **Criterion Importance:** Rubrics must differentiate between more and less critical aspects, either through categorical tags or explicit numeric weights.
4. **Self-Contained Evaluation:** Each rubric item should be independently actionable and understandable without external context.

Given the scarcity of human-annotated rubric datasets, the methodology employs **strong LLMs (GPT-4o, o3-mini)** to generate instance-specific rubrics at scale. This process conditions rubric generation on **golden reference answers** from the underlying datasets, which serve as proxies for expert supervision. Each generated rubric contains 7–20 self-contained items, each assigned a categorical weight (Essential, Important, Optional, Pitfall). The resulting datasets are released as RaR-Medicine and RaR-Science.

#### 3.4. Training Details

- **Reinforcement Learning Algorithm:** GRPO (Generalized Reinforcement Policy Optimization) is used, an on-policy RL algorithm.
- **Base Policy:** Qwen2.5-7B is selected as the base language model to be fine-tuned.
- **Compute:** Training runs are executed on a single node with 8 NVIDIA H100 GPUs.
- **Training Loop:**
 1. **Response Generation:** For each prompt, `k=16` responses are sampled from the current policy `πθ`.
 2. **Reward Computation:** A separate, strong LLM-as-judge (gpt-4o-mini) assigns rewards to the sampled responses based on the instance-specific rubrics, using either the Explicit or Implicit Aggregation strategy.
 3. **Policy Update:** The policy weights are updated using GRPO based on these computed rewards.

#### 3.5. Baselines and Evaluation Setup

- **Rubric-Free Baselines:**
 - **OFF-THE-SHELF:** Untrained Qwen2.5-7B and an instruction-tuned variant (Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct).
 - **DIRECT-LIKERT:** LLM-as-judge provides a direct 1-10 Likert score (normalized) for each response.
 - **REFERENCE-LIKERT:** LLM-as-judge compares the generated response against a human/strong-LLM reference answer and assigns a 1-10 Likert score.
- **Rubric-Guided Baselines:**
 - **RaR-PREDEFINED:** Uses a fixed set of generic rubrics for all prompts with uniform weighting (no instance-specific synthesis). This is included to highlight the importance of specific rubrics.
- **Evaluation Settings:**
 - **Rubric-Based Evaluation:** HealthBench (5,000 clinical conversations) uses physician-authored rubrics to assess safety and helpfulness, reporting overall and per-axis scores. A subset (HealthBench-1k) is used for ablations.
 - **Multiple-Choice Evaluation:** GPQA-Diamond (scientific reasoning) evaluates factual accuracy, with performance measured as mean accuracy over 10 runs with 95% confidence intervals.
 - **LLM-Judge Alignment Evaluation:** A paired evaluation set from HealthBench (practitioner-approved vs. perturbed answers) assesses how well LLM judges (of varying sizes) align with human preferences (pairwise preference accuracy), comparing rubric-guided vs. rubric-free scoring.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study presents compelling evidence for the effectiveness of the Rubrics as Rewards (RaR) framework, demonstrating significant improvements across various evaluation metrics and shedding light on the crucial aspects of rubric design and application.

1. **Superior Performance Across Domains:**
 - **HealthBench (Rubric-Based, Free-Form):** RaR-Implicit consistently outperforms the Direct-Likert baseline with relative gains of up to 31%. It also shows strong competitive advantages over Reference-Likert. All instance-specific rubric-guided variants achieve higher overall scores than both the base and instruction-tuned policies.
 - **GPQA-Diamond (Multiple-Choice):** RaR-Implicit achieves relative gains of up to 7% over Direct-Likert. This finding is particularly important as it demonstrates that skills learned from rubric-based training generalize effectively beyond rubric-guided evaluations to tasks with verifiable answers, indicating a deeper improvement in reasoning capabilities.

2. **Importance of Instance-Specific Rubrics:**
 - The RaR-Predefined variant, which uses a fixed list of generic rubrics, significantly underperforms compared to instance-specific RaR variants. This highlights that effective training requires rubrics tailored to the specific context of each prompt, as generic criteria often fail to capture task-specific requirements and common failure modes, leading to misaligned reward signals.

3. **Effectiveness of Implicit Aggregation:**
 - Among the rubric-guided methods, RaR-Implicit (where the LLM judge holistically aggregates criteria) generally yields the strongest results. This suggests that powerful LLM judges are capable of effectively integrating multiple criteria to produce a nuanced scalar reward, potentially overcoming the brittleness and difficulty of manually tuning fixed weights in RaR-Explicit. While explicit weighting offers control and interpretability, the study suggests implicit aggregation provides better adaptability.

4. **Enhanced Alignment with Human Preferences Across Model Scales:**
 - An ablation study on LLM judge alignment reveals that rubric guidance consistently improves the pairwise preference accuracy of LLM judges with human preferences, regardless of judge model size. The most pronounced gains are observed for smaller judge models, effectively narrowing the performance gap between them and larger, more capable models. This indicates that explicit, context-specific criteria act as valuable scaffolding, enabling judges to better distinguish subtle quality differences than direct Likert scoring alone. This finding has significant implications for reducing the cost and expertise required for effective reward modeling.

5. **Critical Role of Expert Guidance in Rubric Generation:**
 - The quality of synthetic rubrics is profoundly influenced by the inclusion of expert guidance, specifically through the use of reference answers during rubric generation. Rubrics developed *with reference answers* achieve significantly higher accuracy and lead to better downstream policy performance compared to those generated without. Notably, policies trained with synthetic rubrics generated with reference answers yield comparable performance to those trained with human-authored rubrics (e.g., RaR-Implicit-Synthetic vs. RaR-Implicit-Human on HealthBench-1k). This underscores that while LLM-generated rubrics are scalable, grounding them with expert knowledge (even through proxies like high-quality reference answers) is essential for capturing subtle human preferences.

6. **Insights into Rubric Design Elements:**
 - A study on rubric design elements (on HealthBench-1k) indicates that incorporating a broader range of criteria (beyond "Essential-Only") generally leads to better learning outcomes, suggesting that richer evaluation signals are beneficial. Interestingly, the study found minimal performance differences from explicitly including categorical weights or specific "Pitfall" criteria during training. This might be due to the inherent difficulty in synthetically generating truly effective pitfall criteria that accurately anticipate common model failures without human intuition.

7. **Impact of LLM Expertise on Rubric Quality (Reference-Free):**
 - When rubrics are generated *without reference answers*, larger and more capable LLMs (e.g., GPT-4o) produce more effective rubrics than smaller models. However, even the best reference-free rubrics still fall short of those generated with expert (reference-guided) supervision. This reinforces the idea that while LLM capabilities are important for rubric generation, external grounding remains paramount for achieving optimal training signals.

In summary, RaR demonstrates itself as a robust and effective framework for training LLMs in complex, non-verifiable domains, primarily driven by the strategic use of instance-specific, expert-grounded rubrics.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Rubrics as Rewards" (RaR) framework represents a significant advancement in the field of large language model alignment and reinforcement learning, with several important implications and potential impacts:

1. **Expanded Applicability of Reinforcement Learning:** RaR effectively extends the reach of Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) beyond well-defined, binary-correct domains (like math and coding) to the far broader and more challenging landscape of real-world, nuanced reasoning tasks. This opens doors for applying sophisticated RL techniques to fields such as medicine, law, scientific research, and complex dialogue systems, where evaluation is inherently multi-criteria and subjective.

2. **Bridging the Gap in Reward Modeling:** The framework offers a principled and scalable "middle ground" between sparse verifiable rewards and often unreliable or biased preference-based reward models. By structuring feedback through explicit criteria, RaR mitigates issues like reward hacking and overfitting to superficial characteristics, leading to more robust and genuinely aligned models.

3. **Improved Model Performance and Generalization:** The demonstrated performance gains on both rubric-scored (HealthBench) and verifiable multiple-choice (GPQA-Diamond) tasks highlight that RaR not only aligns models with specific evaluation rubrics but also enhances their underlying reasoning capabilities, leading to better generalization across diverse evaluation formats.

4. **Enhanced Transparency and Interpretability:** By decomposing the concept of a "good response" into modular, human-interpretable criteria, RaR makes the reward signal far more transparent than opaque scalar scores from preference models. This interpretability is crucial for understanding why a model performs well or poorly, facilitating debugging, targeted improvements, and fostering trust in AI systems, especially in high-stakes applications like healthcare.

5. **Democratization of LLM Fine-tuning and Evaluation:** The finding that rubric guidance significantly improves the alignment of smaller LLM judges with human preferences is profoundly impactful. It suggests that specialized, high-quality reward modeling might become less dependent on access to the largest and most expensive frontier models. This could lower the barriers to entry for researchers and developers, enabling more efficient and cost-effective fine-tuning of specialized LLMs even with more modest computational resources.

6. **Foundation for Advanced AI Systems:** The ability to provide structured, multi-criteria feedback is essential for developing more sophisticated AI agents that can navigate complex tasks requiring nuanced decision-making, ethical considerations, and adaptability to diverse user needs. RaR provides a robust mechanism for instilling these qualities during post-training.

7. **Contribution of New Datasets:** The release of RaR-Medicine and RaR-Science datasets, complete with instance-specific, LLM-generated rubrics, provides valuable resources for the research community. These datasets can accelerate further exploration into structured reward learning, multi-criteria evaluation, and LLM alignment in expert domains.

8. **Future Research Avenues:** RaR opens up numerous avenues for future work, including exploring more advanced and dynamic rubric aggregation strategies (e.g., learned weights, curriculum-based weighting), extending the framework to other agentic tasks (dialogue, tool use), and investigating specialized evaluators beyond generic LLM judges.

In essence, "Rubrics as Rewards" lays a crucial foundation for making LLMs not just factually correct, but truly intelligent, nuanced, and aligned with complex human values and expert judgments across the full spectrum of real-world reasoning.
