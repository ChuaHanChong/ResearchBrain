# Unlocking Location Intelligence: A Survey from Deep Learning to The LLM Era

> [!summary] Summary
> This survey paper provides a comprehensive review of geospatial representation learning, covering advancements from deep learning to large language models (LLMs). It introduces a novel three-dimensional taxonomy to systematically organize research and highlights the evolving landscape of Location Intelligence.

## Problem

- The rapidly evolving field of Location Intelligence (LI) lacked a systematic and up-to-date review that encompasses both deep learning and emerging Large Language Model (LLM) paradigms.
- Existing surveys often had limited scope, focusing on specific data types or methodologies, and failed to adequately integrate multimodal approaches or recent breakthroughs like Transformers and LLMs.
- There was no clear, structured taxonomy to categorize and compare the diverse research approaches in geospatial representation learning, hindering a holistic understanding of the field's progress.

## Method

- The authors conducted a comprehensive literature review of ==geospatial representation learning==, tracing its evolution through the deep learning and LLM eras.
- A novel ==three-dimensional taxonomy== was introduced to categorize representative works based on methodology (single, dual, multiple view), modality, and downstream tasks.
- The survey framed geospatial representation learning within a complete pipeline, analyzing it from data, methodological (location/region representation learning), and application perspectives.

## Results

- Identified spatial data (POIs, satellite imagery) as the most prevalent data modality (**67%**) in geospatial representation learning, followed by mobility data (**18%**).
- Showcased a clear progression from single-view to dual-view and multi-view multimodal integration methods, significantly enhancing representation learning for various geospatial tasks.
- Demonstrated an expanding application landscape for learned representations in predicting socioeconomic indicators, managing regions and POIs, and advanced location sensing.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Location Intelligence has experienced two transformative phases: first driven by deep neural networks for structured data, and now by LLMs for cross-modal reasoning and unstructured geo-textual data.
> - The field is increasingly moving towards multimodal integration, with contrastive learning and Graph Neural Networks being key techniques for combining diverse geospatial data modalities.
> - While LLMs are emerging enhancers, the GeoAI domain currently lacks dedicated foundation models, unified benchmarks, and large-scale, comprehensive multimodal geospatial datasets.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of geospatial analysis is undergoing significant transformation, driven by the proliferation of multimodal geospatial data and advancements in Artificial Intelligence (AI). Traditional Geographic Information Systems (GIS) have been foundational for spatial operations, but the emergence of Geospatial AI (GeoAI), which integrates deep learning with spatial science, has expanded capabilities in modeling geographic phenomena. Geospatial Representation Learning (GRL) is a core component of this shift, focusing on automatically extracting latent structures, semantic patterns, and geographic dependencies from diverse location-centric data.

This survey paper positions itself within the evolving landscape of GRL by synthesizing developments across two significant technological phases: the deep learning era and the emerging Large Language Model (LLM) and multimodal foundation model paradigm. The deep learning era saw successes in automated feature extraction from structured and semi-structured data (e.g., satellite imagery, GPS trajectories) using architectures like Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), Graph Neural Networks (GNNs), and Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs). The advent of LLMs introduces capabilities for cross-modal geospatial reasoning and processing unstructured geo-textual data, addressing limitations of earlier approaches.

The authors identify a gap in existing literature, noting that previous surveys often focus on specific data types (e.g., self-supervised learning for location/region data, graph neural networks for urban computing, visual and mapping data) or predate recent advancements in Transformers, multimodal pretraining, and LLMs. This work aims to provide a more comprehensive and up-to-date examination, offering a unified synthesis of GRL methodologies under a coherent taxonomy that integrates both deep learning and LLM-driven approaches. It serves as an exploration of the field's current state and a guide for future innovation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this survey is to present a comprehensive review of geospatial representation learning, covering its evolution from deep learning methodologies to the contemporary large language model (LLM) era. The authors aim to organize this extensive field into a structured taxonomy that encompasses data, methodological, and application perspectives.

The motivation for this work stems from several fundamental challenges and developments within GRL:

- **Ubiquitous Geospatial Data Growth:** The exponential increase in multimodal geospatial data (e.g., satellite imagery, human mobility records, geo-textual information) necessitates advanced methods for extracting actionable insights and understanding geographic phenomena.
- **Limitations of Traditional GIS:** While effective for basic spatial operations, traditional GIS requires augmentation by more sophisticated AI techniques to model the increasing complexity of geographic data.
- **Deep Learning Advancements:** Deep Neural Networks have demonstrated considerable success in automating feature extraction from structured and semi-structured geospatial data, enabling various applications. However, these methods often struggle with unstructured data and complex cross-modal reasoning.
- **Emergence of LLMs:** The recent integration of LLMs and multimodal foundation models presents a transformative opportunity for GRL, particularly in processing unstructured textual data, enabling cross-modal alignment, and enhancing spatial reasoning with pretrained knowledge. Despite their potential, LLMs are not yet extensively adopted in GRL, highlighting a need for focused exploration.
- **Persistent Challenges in GRL:**
 - **Heterogeneity of Geospatial Data:** Geospatial data originates from highly diverse modalities, varying in semantic richness, spatial scale, and sampling density, making it difficult to construct unified representation spaces.
 - **Generalization Across Regions:** Spatial heterogeneity, scale mismatch, and geographic domain shift hinder robust generalization of models across different regions, where urban morphology, mobility patterns, and socioeconomic conditions can vary significantly.
 - **Cross-Modal Alignment and Spatial Grounding:** Reliably aligning information from different modalities and grounding it spatially is challenging, especially with conflicting signals or missing data.
 - **Limited Incorporation of Geographic Knowledge:** Explicit geographic knowledge, such as topological constraints, spatial hierarchies, and physical laws, is often not integrated into GRL models in a principled manner.
 - **LLM-Specific Issues:** The application of LLMs to geospatial tasks introduces new challenges, including geographic hallucination, spatial biases, limited grounding, and the absence of geospatial inductive mechanisms.
- **Lack of a Unified, Up-to-Date Survey:** Existing surveys provide fragmented views, often focusing on specific data types or methodologies, and generally predate the latest advancements in LLMs and multimodal foundation models.

To address these motivations, the authors propose to:
1. Introduce a unified and fine-grained taxonomy for GRL, integrating data modalities, representation methodologies, and fusion architectures.
2. Synthesize methods across the deep learning and LLM eras, tracing the evolution of modeling strategies.
3. Analytically review geospatial data modalities and their implications for GRL challenges.
4. Offer an outlook on GRL in the LLM era, assessing current systems, identifying limitations, and outlining future research directions, including geospatial foundation models and agent-based urban reasoning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors structure their comprehensive survey of Geospatial Representation Learning (GRL) using a three-dimensional taxonomy: (1) data perspective, (2) methodological perspective, and (3) application perspective. This framework systematically categorizes and analyzes contemporary techniques, spanning from deep learning to the LLM era.

#### 3.1. Data Perspective

The survey begins by classifying geospatial data into four primary categories, highlighting their distinct roles in understanding geospatial dynamics:
- **Spatial Data:** Includes Points of Interest (POIs), check-in activities, satellite imagery, street view imagery, and geospatial vectors (e.g., OpenStreetMap nodes, ways, relations). These data types provide details about locations and regions, capturing physical layouts, land use, and semantic information.
- **Mobility Data:** Reflects human movement patterns within cities, primarily represented by taxi trips, subway ridership, and other trajectory data. This category captures dynamic spatiotemporal interactions.
- **Social Media Data:** Comprises user-generated content, such as geo-tagged imagery and descriptive texts (e.g., social media geo-tags, online encyclopedias, user reviews). This multimodal data offers insights into human perception, urban vibrancy, and contextual details, with growing importance for LLM integration.
- **Socio-Economic Attributes:** High-level semantic indicators used to quantify human populations, economic activities, and environmental interactions. Examples include land usage, demographic data, crime statistics, income, poverty, and carbon emissions, often serving as labels for downstream predictive tasks.

The authors provide statistics on data usage frequency, noting spatial data (67%) as dominant, followed by mobility data (18%) and social media data (13%). They also observe a focus on developed cities (e.g., Beijing, New York) due to established infrastructure and open data initiatives.

#### 3.2. Methodology Perspective

This section forms the core of the survey, detailing how different data modalities are processed and fused to generate geospatial representations (embeddings). Methods are categorized based on the complexity of views used in learning, further divided into location-level and region-level views.

- **Data-centric View (4.1 in paper):**
 - **Single View:** Methods that project diverse spatial data modalities into a unified representational space using a single data source.
 - *Single View Location Embedding:* Focuses on specific geographical coordinates, often using environmental contexts (Loc2Vec) or POI distributional differences (Place2Vec).
 - *Single View Region Embedding:* Extracts and refines single-modal attributes for urban regions. Key modalities include satellite imagery (e.g., READ, Tile2Vec for economic scales, land cover), mobility data (e.g., HDGE, MGFN for dynamic patterns), and others like POI data (HGI) or trajectories (MTE).
 - **Dual View:** Integrates data from two distinct modalities to overcome single-modality limitations.
 - *Dual View Location Embedding:* Often combines geographic location data with visual data (satellite or geo-tagged imagery, e.g., Geo-SSL, SatCLIP, GeoCLIP) or textual data (e.g., GeoLLM, LLMGeovec with OSM context). Trajectory data (TALE) and geographic context (MGeo) are also explored.
 - *Dual View Region Embedding:* Exemplifies two main trajectories:
 - *POI + Mobility:* Integrates static functional attributes from POIs with dynamic activity patterns from mobility data, often using graph-based methods (e.g., MV-PN, CGAL, MVURE with GCNs/GATs) or contrastive learning (ReCP, ReMVC).
 - *Visual Imagery + Others:* Combines satellite or street-view imagery with other modalities. Satellite imagery is enhanced by POI data (PG-SimCLR, MMGR), knowledge graphs (KnowCL), or textual descriptions (UrbanCLIP). Street-view imagery is combined with POIs (Urban2Vec) or text (USPM).
 - **Multiple View:** Approaches that integrate three or more data modalities to achieve enhanced representations, acknowledging the trade-off between information richness and computational cost. Examples include combining satellite imagery, POIs, and human mobility (RegionEncoder), or further incorporating street-level visual data (M3G, Geo-Tile2Vec), or leveraging textual descriptions from LLMs as proxies (UrbanVLP). Land Usage is also integrated as an auxiliary feature (HAFusion, HUGAT, FlexiReg).

- **Representation Learning Methodology / Implementation (4.2 in paper):**
 The survey details the underlying machine learning techniques:
 - **Contrastive Learning:** A prevalent unsupervised method (e.g., SimCLR, InfoNCE, Triplet Loss) that pulls similar samples closer and pushes dissimilar samples apart in embedding space. It is widely used for intra-modal representation and inter-modal alignment across single, dual, and multiple-view scenarios (e.g., Loc2Vec, Geo-SSL, SatCLIP, Tile2vec, UrbanVLP).
 - **Graph Neural Networks (GNNs):** Specifically designed for graph-structured data, GNNs (e.g., GCN, GAT) are crucial for capturing complex relationships and non-local dependencies in mobility data, urban networks, and POI interactions. They are extensively used in region embedding, particularly in dual and multiple-view settings (e.g., HDGE, MV-PN, MVURE, HREP).
 - **Large Language Models (LLMs):** Leveraged for their world knowledge and text processing capabilities. In GRL, LLMs extract geographical information from text using prompts and meta-information (e.g., GeoLLM, LLMGeovec), generating intermediate embeddings to augment other tasks.
 - **Other Methods:** Includes retrieval-augmented matching (RANGE, G3, Georanker), transformer encoders and masking strategies (UrbanFusion, MGeo), reinforcement learning (GLOBE), and diffusion models (Zhang et al.).

#### 3.3. Application Perspective

The learned geospatial representations are applied to various tasks:
- **Socioeconomic Indicator Prediction:** Forecasting regional GDP, poverty, crime, mobility popularity, house prices, population, energy consumption, and air quality using learned embeddings. This has evolved from urban-scale predictions to broader and global scales, especially with multi-modal fusion and LLM integration.
- **Region and POI Management:** Utilizing embeddings to detect and quantify functional similarities between regions and locations, leading to automatic clustering of regions into functional groups and identification of special areas (e.g., ZE-Mob, ReFound).
- **Location Sensing:** Encompassing location understanding (enhancing features for GeoAI tasks like visitor flow prediction, transportation mode classification, spatiotemporal prediction) and geo-localization (predicting precise GPS coordinates from image content). Geo-localization has progressed from classification/retrieval to representation-based alignment, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), and multi-agent collaborative reasoning (e.g., GeoCLIP, G3, GeoRanker, GLOBE).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The survey presents a structured overview of the geospatial representation learning (GRL) domain, highlighting its evolution, key methodologies, and applications. The main findings and results are encapsulated in the comprehensive taxonomy and the detailed analysis of trends and challenges.

#### 4.1. Comprehensive Taxonomy and Evolution of GRL

The paper establishes a three-dimensional taxonomy (data, methodology, application) that systematically organizes GRL research. This taxonomy reveals the progression of GRL through two distinct technological eras:
- **Deep Learning Era:** Characterized by the extensive use of CNNs, GNNs, and RNNs for structured and semi-structured geospatial data. This era established foundations for automated feature extraction from satellite imagery, urban networks, and trajectories.
- **LLM Era:** Represents a shift towards integrating unstructured geo-textual data, leveraging pre-trained knowledge, and enabling cross-modal reasoning through large language models and multimodal foundation models. This era is still emerging but shows significant potential to redefine GRL.

#### 4.2. Diverse Data Modalities and Their Contributions

The survey systematically reviews the diverse data modalities crucial for GRL, categorizing them into spatial data, mobility data, social media data, and socio-economic attributes. It identifies spatial data (e.g., POIs, satellite imagery) as the most frequently used category, followed by mobility data. The increasing role of social media data, particularly geo-tagged text, is noted as a promising direction in the LLM era. The analysis underscores how characteristics of these modalities shape the challenges and opportunities in GRL.

#### 4.3. Methodological Advancements in Representation Learning

The paper details the core methodological approaches, organized by "views" (single, dual, multiple) and underlying techniques:
- **Single-View Approaches:** Provide foundational representations, with satellite imagery and mobility data being prominent for region embeddings, while Loc2Vec and Place2Vec initiated location embeddings.
- **Dual-View Approaches:** Represent a significant advancement, integrating two complementary modalities (e.g., location + visual imagery, POI + mobility). This fusion enhances the semantic richness and dynamic understanding of geospatial entities. Visual data combined with location coordinates and POI data combined with mobility patterns are identified as dominant paradigms in dual-view region embedding.
- **Multiple-View Approaches:** Further leverage three or more modalities to achieve more comprehensive representations, acknowledging the complexity of urban systems (e.g., satellite imagery + POI + mobility, or text as a substitute for POI/mobility).
- **Key Techniques:**
 - **Contrastive Learning:** Emerges as a pivotal and versatile technique for both intra-modal representation learning and inter-modal information alignment, particularly effective in integrating visual and other modalities.
 - **Graph Neural Networks (GNNs):** Are fundamental for modeling dynamic spatiotemporal processes and complex relationships within urban networks, especially for mobility data and POI interactions.
 - **Large Language Models (LLMs):** Show potential in extracting geographical knowledge from text, augmenting existing embeddings, and enhancing spatial reasoning, although their integration is still in early stages.

#### 4.4. Broadening Application Spectrum

GRL has expanded its utility across various domains:
- **Socioeconomic Indicator Prediction:** Applications have moved beyond initial urban-scale predictions (crime rates, house prices) to broader and global-scale estimations of poverty, population density, GDP, and air quality, largely due to multi-modal data fusion and LLM integration.
- **Region and POI Management:** GRL enables data-driven approaches to understand urban functions, cluster regions, and identify specific locations, which complements traditional administrative boundaries.
- **Location Sensing:** This includes enhancing location understanding for various GeoAI tasks (e.g., visitor flow, transportation mode prediction) and advancing geo-localization, transitioning towards sophisticated representation-based alignment, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), and multi-agent reasoning.

#### 4.5. Identified Challenges and Future Directions in the LLM Era

The survey explicitly outlines current limitations and promising avenues for future research, particularly in the context of LLMs:
- **LLM Deficiencies:** Challenges related to geographic hallucination, spatial biases, limited grounding, and lack of geospatial inductive mechanisms in LLMs are highlighted.
- **Emerging Trends:** The development of unified benchmarking frameworks, strategies to integrate LLMs with real-world urban and physical knowledge (data customization, architectural integration, domain-specific reasoning), incentivizing LLM reasoning via reinforcement learning (e.g., GRPO for geospatial reasoning), and the conceptualization of geospatial foundation models (GFMs) are identified as critical directions.
- **General Future Directions:** The need for standardized datasets, codebases, and evaluation metrics is emphasized to facilitate reproducibility and comparability. Further investigation into the differential contributions of various modalities to downstream tasks is called for. The shift towards continuous and physics-informed representation learning (e.g., Implicit Neural Representations) is proposed to overcome limitations of spatial discretization. Finally, architectures like Mixture of Experts (MoE) and agentic learning are suggested to handle data heterogeneity and enable distributed geospatial analysis, alongside ethical considerations regarding fairness, equity, and privacy.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This survey holds significant implications for the advancement of Geospatial Representation Learning (GRL) and the broader field of GeoAI. Its significance stems from providing a structured, comprehensive, and forward-looking analysis of a rapidly evolving domain.

#### 5.1. Providing a Unified Framework and Roadmap

By introducing a novel three-dimensional taxonomy (data, methodology, application), the survey offers a coherent organizational framework that synthesizes disparate research efforts across deep learning and the emerging LLM era. This taxonomy clarifies the landscape of GRL, making it more accessible for new researchers and providing a structured "roadmap for further innovation." It helps to understand how different components of GRL interact and where future research can be most impactful.

#### 5.2. Bridging Deep Learning and LLM Paradigms

The paper critically analyzes the transition of GRL from traditional deep learning approaches to the LLM and foundation model era. This historical perspective not only contextualizes current advancements but also highlights the transformative potential of LLMs in addressing previous limitations, particularly in processing unstructured geo-textual data and enabling sophisticated cross-modal geospatial reasoning. This integration is crucial for developing more robust and intelligent location-based systems and services (LBS).

#### 5.3. Addressing Fundamental Challenges in GeoAI

The detailed discussion of persistent challenges in GRL—such as data heterogeneity, generalization across diverse regions, cross-modal alignment, spatial grounding, and the nuanced issues of geographic hallucination and spatial biases in LLMs—is instrumental. By explicitly articulating these problems, the survey guides researchers toward critical areas requiring innovation. Solutions in these areas could significantly enhance the reliability and applicability of GeoAI systems.

#### 5.4. Driving Advancements in Key Applications

The comprehensive overview of applications, including socioeconomic indicator prediction, region and POI management, and location sensing/geo-localization, showcases the practical utility of GRL. Improvements in these areas can directly impact:
- **Urban Planning and Governance:** Better predictions of socioeconomic indicators (e.g., poverty, crime, population) can inform policy-making, resource allocation, and sustainable urban development.
- **Environmental Monitoring and Climate Change:** Enhanced representations can support analysis of land use changes, carbon emissions, and climate patterns.
- **Intelligent Transportation Systems:** Improved understanding of mobility patterns and location prediction can optimize traffic flow and public transport.
- **Real Estate and Business Analytics:** More accurate house price predictions and regional popularity insights can aid investment and business strategies.

#### 5.5. Shaping Future Research Directions

The "Future Directions" section is particularly impactful, outlining several critical avenues:
- **Standardization:** Emphasizing the need for standardized datasets, codebases, and evaluation metrics will foster reproducibility and accelerate research progress.
- **Modality Contribution Analysis:** Understanding the specific contributions of different data modalities will optimize data acquisition and model design.
- **Continuous and Physics-informed Learning:** Moving beyond discrete spatial units to continuous and physics-informed models (e.g., INRs) could overcome fundamental limitations like the Modifiable Areal Unit Problem (MAUP) and enhance consistency with geographic laws.
- **Advanced Architectures:** Exploring Mixture of Experts (MoE) and agentic learning frameworks offers solutions for handling data heterogeneity and distributed geospatial analysis, paving the way for more adaptable and scalable systems.
- **Ethical Considerations:** The explicit focus on fairness, equity, and ethics is crucial as GRL systems increasingly influence public policy. Developing privacy-preserving and debiasing techniques is essential to prevent representation inequality and reinforcement of historical prejudices, ensuring responsible deployment of GeoAI.

In conclusion, this survey serves as a foundational reference for the GRL community. By meticulously organizing past achievements and clearly articulating future challenges and opportunities, it is positioned to guide significant theoretical and practical advancements in extracting actionable insights from complex geospatial data in the age of foundation models.
