# HoRD: Robust Humanoid Control via History-Conditioned Reinforcement Learning and Online Distillation

> [!summary] Summary
> A framework called HoRD enables robust torque-level humanoid control, deriving stable whole-body behaviors from sparse motion commands and adapting to varying physical dynamics through history-conditioned reinforcement learning. This approach achieved over a 2x reduction in motion tracking errors and maintained an 86% success rate during zero-shot transfer to an unseen physics engine.

## Problem

- Difficulty in translating high-level, sparse motion commands into robust, torque-level humanoid control, especially under distribution shift.
- Existing control policies overfit to specific training dynamics and fail under domain shifts or when transferred to unseen physics engines.
- Inability of current methods to adapt online to unobserved dynamic changes and model mismatches in real-world environments.

## Method

- A two-stage teacher-student distillation framework for learning robust control, processing sparse motion commands through a shared history-conditioned mechanism.
- A ==Standardized Sparse-Joint Representation (SSJR)== to abstract motion data and unify fragmented datasets, enabling policies to operate directly from sparse keypoint commands.
- A History-Conditioned Dynamics Representation (HCDR) module that infers latent environmental dynamics from recent state-action history for real-time online adaptation.

## Results

- Achieved a **90.7%** success rate and over a **2x** reduction in motion tracking errors (E_g-mpjpe of 102mm) compared to baselines in the training environment.
- Demonstrated strong zero-shot transfer capabilities, maintaining an **86.0%** success rate in an unseen physics engine (Genesis) without retraining, significantly outperforming other methods.
- Exhibited robust online adaptation and disturbance recovery, successfully recovering from **85.2%** of external pushes in zero-shot deployment in the Genesis environment.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Extensive episode-level domain randomization combined with online dynamics inference via HCDR is crucial for achieving strong zero-shot generalization to unseen physics engines.
> - Learning from sparse, standardized motion commands (SSJR) facilitates data scalability and cross-platform transfer, effectively bridging high-level planning with low-level torque control.
> - A teacher-student distillation approach enables the efficient transfer of robust control knowledge from a privileged expert, trained with full observations, to a deployment-ready student policy with sparse observations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of humanoid robotics is progressing towards autonomous agents that can perform complex tasks in dynamic, human-centric environments. Recent advancements in high-level planning and abstraction, such as vision-language-action (VLA) systems and learning-based planning, have improved the semantic reasoning and task specification capabilities of robots. These high-level commands often translate into goal-conditioned or multi-task reinforcement learning (RL) problems.

However, a fundamental challenge persists in mapping these high-level specifications to stable, torque-level humanoid control, particularly under conditions of distribution shift. This shift can occur when test-time dynamics, contact properties, or physics engines differ from those used during training. Current physics-based control policies are typically trained on dense motion-capture datasets within a single simulator with fixed dynamics parameters. This approach presents two limitations:
1. **Limited and sparse training distribution:** Motion datasets often cover a narrow range of skeleton definitions, frame rates, and input modalities, which can lead to incomplete coverage of the motion manifold and hinder scalable learning.
2. **Overfitting and catastrophic failure under domain shift:** Policies trained in a specific environment tend to overfit to its dynamics, exhibiting significant performance degradation or failure when exposed to different motion patterns, terrains, or physics engines. For example, recent methods like MaskedMimic, while capable of visually plausible motion synthesis, have shown limited generalization robustness when evaluated in different physics engines.

Existing methods for physics-guided policy learning often require dense reference trajectories (e.g., full joint-space motion targets) during both training and deployment. This creates a deployment gap, as real-world motion planners or high-level controllers typically provide only sparse keypoint commands. Furthermore, heterogeneous motion sources frequently use incompatible skeleton definitions and temporal resolutions, complicating integration. While approaches like domain randomization have been used to improve generalization, they are often insufficient to cover all possible dynamic variations, and aggressive randomization can slow convergence for complex humanoid systems. Target-domain adaptation methods, though effective, necessitate additional interaction and fine-tuning in the target domain, which is often impractical for real-world deployment.

HoRD addresses these limitations by introducing a framework that aims to bridge the gap between high-level motion specifications and robust torque-level control. It specifically targets the challenges of data fragmentation, requiring sparse command inputs, and achieving robust generalization and online adaptation under varying physical dynamics and environments, including zero-shot transfer to unseen simulators.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this work stems from the observed gap in current humanoid control methods: the lack of a unified, robust representation that can translate high-level motion specifications into torque-level control, while generalizing across diverse data sources and physical environments. The authors identify three specific requirements that current methods do not adequately address:

1. **Ability to track human-like motions from sparse motion commands:** Real-world applications often provide high-level motion instructions as sparse keypoint trajectories (e.g., 3D positions of key joints), rather than complete joint-space data. Existing policies frequently require dense reference trajectories, creating a mismatch with practical deployment scenarios.
2. **Robust generalization under distribution shifts:** Humanoid robots need to operate reliably in environments where physical dynamics (e.g., contact properties, actuator delays, mass distributions) or the underlying simulation engine might differ from training conditions. Current methods often overfit to training dynamics, leading to performance drops or failures in new domains.
3. **Online adaptation to unobserved dynamic changes:** Small model mismatches or unanticipated changes in the environment (e.g., surface friction, external perturbations) can accumulate into large tracking errors and destabilize torque control over long horizons. Policies need to adapt in real-time to these latent, unobserved dynamics.

To address these motivations, HoRD sets forth the following key objectives:
- To propose a two-stage learning framework that can map sparse root-relative 3D joint keypoint trajectories to torque-level humanoid control.
- To achieve strong cross-domain generalization, enabling a single policy to adapt zero-shot to unseen domains without per-domain retraining.
- To introduce a history-conditioned reinforcement learning (RL) framework capable of inferring latent dynamics context from recent state-action trajectories, facilitating online adaptation.
- To develop a standardized sparse-joint representation (SSJR) that unifies fragmented motion datasets and allows policies to operate directly from sparse keypoint commands.
- To demonstrate superior robustness and transfer capabilities compared to existing baselines, particularly under unseen domains and external perturbations.
- To contribute a large-scale trajectory dataset of diverse humanoid motions to support future research and benchmarking.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

HoRD proposes a two-stage learning framework designed for robust, anticipatory humanoid control that generalizes across domains under partial observability. The framework conceptualizes the control problem as a Contextual Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (C-POMDP).

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

Each episode is associated with an unobserved, episode-specific physical parameter vector ψ^(e) that dictates physical properties. The robot receives future motion intentions (Yt) and environmental context (gt), and maintains historical interactions (Ht) consisting of recent state-action pairs. The goal is to learn a policy (π) that generates adaptive proportional-derivative (PD) targets (αt) by leveraging a temporal memory embedding (mt) derived from Ht, to maximize performance across diverse conditions.

#### 3.2. Two-Stage Learning Framework: Teacher-Student Distillation

The framework employs a teacher-student distillation paradigm:
- **Shared Mechanism:** Both teacher and student policies utilize a shared mechanism for processing historical interactions. They collect a history window Ht = {(s_t-k, α_t-k)} of recent state-action pairs. This history is processed by the **HCDR** module to produce a temporal memory embedding mt, which captures latent dynamics information and enables adaptive control.
- **Stage I: Full-Observation Expert (Teacher) Training:**
 - An expert policy (π⋆) is trained in simulation using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO).
 - It has privileged access to the full state (s_full_t), dense future motion intent (Y_full_t) from high-frequency motion capture data, and the temporal memory embedding (mt).
 - The expert learns to predict control modulation signals (α^(1)_t) by maximizing expected discounted returns across domain-randomized episodes (Equation 3), thus handling a wide range of latent dynamics and environment variations.
- **Stage II: Sparse-Observation Student Training:**
 - The expert's behavior is distilled into a student policy (π_θ2) designed for realistic sensory constraints.
 - The student receives sparse observations (s_sparse_t) and sparse future motion cues (Y_sparse_t). It uses the same temporal memory embedding (mt) and the **SSJR** representation for motion commands.
 - The student predicts control modulation signals (α^(2)_t) and is trained via supervised distillation to match the expert's actions, minimizing the mean squared error (MSE) between their outputs (Equation 5).

#### 3.3. SSJR: Standardized Sparse-Joint Representation

This module addresses data fragmentation and the requirement for dense reference motions.
- **Motion Abstraction:** SSJR represents future motion intentions (Yt) using a sparse set of key joints (e.g., from SMPL-X) in a canonical human skeletal model. Commands are q_cmd_j,t+h for specific joints and lookahead horizons. This abstracts away platform-specific details.
- **Policy Integration:** The student policy directly receives these sparse SSJR commands, along with proprioception and environmental context, to output torque modulation signals.
- **Advantages:** Enables data scalability by utilizing large human motion datasets, supports cross-platform transfer by abstracting robot-specific actuation, and facilitates anticipatory control when combined with HCDR and domain randomization.

#### 3.4. Episode-Level Domain Randomization

To achieve robust cross-domain generalization, HoRD employs an episode-level domain randomization strategy.
- A parameter vector ψ^(e) is sampled at the beginning of each episode and remains fixed for its duration. This encourages the policy to infer latent physical properties.
- Randomization is applied across four dimensions: (1) inertial properties (e.g., link masses, joint damping), (2) contact dynamics (e.g., ground friction, stiffness), (3) probabilistic system latency delays in control execution, and (4) periodic random external forces to the robot's torso. This exposes the agent to diverse and temporally consistent dynamics shifts, relevant for sim-to-sim transfer and real-world deployment.

#### 3.5. HCDR: History-Conditioned Dynamics Representation

HCDR is a module designed for real-time adaptation to latent environment parameters (ψ^(e)).
- **Latent Dynamics Inference:** HCDR uses a Query-Transformer (Q-Former) architecture to model historical interactions (Ht). Learnable latent tokens (m0) encode a prior over dynamics. The temporal memory embedding (mt) is computed via cross-attention between these prior tokens and Ht (Equation 8), selectively extracting informative history components.
- **Adaptive Modulation:** The resulting mt serves as a compact dynamics fingerprint. By conditioning the control modulation (αt) on mt, the policy can adapt its behavior (gait, balance, torque outputs) based on the environment's recent responses to actions, enabling online system identification and adaptive control without direct access to latent parameters.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments evaluated HoRD on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot with 29 actuated degrees of freedom, controlling it at 50 Hz. Training was conducted in IsaacLab, with zero-shot transfer evaluation to the Genesis physics engine. HoRD was compared against MaskedMimic, OmniH2O, ExBody2, and Hover, using metrics such as success rate (Succ.), global pose error (E_g-mpjpe), and local pose error (E_mpjpe).

#### 4.1. In-Domain Performance

- **IsaacLab (ID):** HoRD achieved a success rate of 90.7% with significantly lower motion tracking errors (E_g-mpjpe: 102mm, E_mpjpe: 76mm). This represented more than a 2x reduction in errors compared to the strongest physics-based baselines.
- **IsaacLab + Domain Randomization (ID):** Under additional observation noise and perturbations at test time, HoRD maintained strong performance (88.4% success rate, E_g-mpjpe: 124mm, E_mpjpe: 87mm), consistently outperforming all baselines.
- MaskedMimic exhibited low performance (32.1% success) and failed under domain randomization (<10% success), consistent with its focus on motion synthesis rather than robust torque-level execution. Other baselines experienced noticeable degradation with domain randomization.

#### 4.2. Cross-Domain Generalization

- **Genesis (Out-of-Distribution, OOD):** When transferred to Genesis, an unseen physics engine, most baselines showed substantial performance degradation. MaskedMimic failed entirely. In contrast, HoRD demonstrated strong zero-shot transfer, achieving an 86.0% success rate without retraining.
- **Genesis + Domain Randomization (OOD):** Even with additional domain randomization applied at test time in Genesis, HoRD retained high performance (84.4% success rate), while all baselines degraded significantly to near-failure levels. This robustness was attributed to HoRD's extensive training under domain randomization and the online dynamics inference capabilities of HCDR.

#### 4.3. Representative Motion Case Studies

- Qualitative analysis (Figure 2) on motions like common walk, side walk, get-up, punch, high kick, and martial arts in the unseen Genesis environment showed that HoRD produced temporally stable, human-like trajectories while maintaining balance. Challenging motions with rapid center-of-mass shifts, such as high kick, remained stable.
- Quantitative pose error comparisons (Figure 3) revealed that HoRD achieved lower errors on most motions, particularly those requiring sustained tracking and whole-body coordination over longer horizons.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies

- **HoRD w/o D (without Domain Randomization):** Removing domain randomization from training caused severe performance collapse under test-time perturbations or simulator changes (e.g., <10% success in IsaacLab+DR and Genesis environments), affirming its necessity for robust generalization.
- **HoRD w/o H (without HCDR):** This variant maintained competitive performance on the training distribution but degraded sharply under distribution shift (e.g., <10% success in Genesis, 70.5% in IsaacLab+DR compared to HoRD's 88.4%), indicating HCDR's critical role in online adaptation to latent dynamics.

#### 4.5. Online Adaptation on Different Terrain Surfaces

- Evaluated in the Genesis simulator under zero-shot transfer on flat ground, smooth slopes, and rough slopes, HoRD maintained robust performance.
- Success rates were 86.2% on flat ground, 85.8% on smooth slopes, and 84.2% on rough slopes, demonstrating graceful degradation with increasing difficulty and adaptive foot placement and body posture.

#### 4.6. Disturbance Recovery

- When subjected to random external pushes in the zero-shot Genesis environment, HoRD recovered successfully in 85.2% of disturbed executions.
- This performance surpassed strong baselines like OmniH2O (71.8%) and ExBody2 (72.1%), indicating that HoRD performs closed-loop execution and can effectively re-stabilize after unexpected perturbations.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

HoRD introduces a framework for mapping sparse motion commands to robust torque-level humanoid control. By integrating a standardized sparse-joint representation (SSJR) with history-conditioned dynamics adaptation (HCDR), the framework enables the learning of stable whole-body behaviors that generalize across substantial dynamics variations. HoRD significantly outperforms existing baselines in tracking fidelity, stability, and zero-shot transfer capabilities, facilitated by data-efficient distillation from a domain-randomized expert. This work indicates that lightweight learned policies utilizing standardized representations can effectively bridge the gap between high-level planners and deployment-ready control systems.

The potential impact of this research is multi-faceted:
- **Embodied AI and Robotics:** It can benefit embodied AI research and, in the longer term, assistive and service robotics. By reducing the engineering and computational overhead associated with retraining or re-tuning controllers across different robot platforms and deployment conditions, HoRD could accelerate development and deployment in these areas.
- **Standardization and Benchmarking:** The release of a large-scale trajectory dataset and evaluation scripts supports reproducible benchmarking, fostering further research and development in the community.
- **Enhanced Robustness and Generalization:** The ability to operate from sparse motion commands and adapt online to distribution shifts makes humanoid control more practical and adaptable to real-world scenarios, where precise, dense motion data or static environments are often unavailable.
- **Safety Considerations:** The advancement in capable and transferable humanoid control policies also raises safety considerations. The authors emphasize the importance of responsible deployment, advocating for extensive validation beyond simulation, the integration of safety constraints (e.g., action limiting, emergency stops, monitoring), and human oversight on physical platforms. They also acknowledge that robustness may vary across different robot embodiments, sensing configurations, and operating conditions, highlighting the need for future work to audit reliability across diverse settings and strengthen evaluation protocols for sim-to-real transfer and safety.
