# Dissecting Training-Free Uncertainty Estimation in Multimodal Large Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> A systematic study of training-free uncertainty estimation for multimodal LLMs. It groups existing estimators into token-level, verbalized, and semantic families, then benchmarks them over eight models and five visual question answering datasets using AUROC, coverage at risk, and a new calibrated hallucination-detection accuracy metric. The headline finding is that the winning family depends on response length, and no method reaches safe-deployment coverage.

## Problem

- Uncertainty estimation is mostly validated on unimodal language models and short-answer question answering, leaving its transfer to visual ambiguity and varied answer formats untested.
- Prior multimodal evaluations cover a narrow set of models, so practitioners lack guidance on how reliability shifts with model family, generation, and scale.
- Standard hallucination-detection accuracy assumes a fixed known threshold, which does not reflect deployment where an abstention threshold must be calibrated from limited held-out data.

## Method

- A ==three-family taxonomy== of training-free estimators: ==verbalized abstention== and numeric confidence prompts, ==token-level entropy== and ==negative log-likelihood== aggregated by mean or max, and ==semantic-clustering entropy==.
- **Calibrated Hallucination Detection Accuracy**, a deployment-time metric that fits one threshold on a small pooled calibration subset, then scores detection accuracy on the full evaluation set.
- An evaluation grid crossing two model families, two generations and two scales against five benchmarks binned into ==short, sentence and long regimes==, adding a released **FSVQA200** subset and an **MM-Vet Long** variant.

## Results

- Max entropy at temperature 1.0 leads short answers with AUROC **0.76**, verbalized abstention leads sentence-length responses at **0.67** AUROC and **73.4** CHDA, semantic methods lead long-form at **0.72** to **0.73**.
- Method choice drives ranking quality (AUROC spans **0.58** to **0.73**) while the base model drives coverage (Cov@20 spans **0.11** to **0.39**); verbal confidence exceeds **0.9** on **79.4%** of predictions and fails to parse **10.3%** of the time.
- Best coverage at 20% risk reaches only **0.45** against a perfect-estimator ceiling of **0.84**, just **33** to **63%** of achievable coverage; raising semantic samples from 5 to 20 lifts AUROC only **0.66** to **0.68**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Match the uncertainty signal to output length: one token's entropy cannot score a long answer, and one abstention token cannot localize many separate sub-claims.
> - Estimate entropy at a higher temperature than you decode at, since low-temperature logits sharpen and collapse the signal; one extra forward pass recovers it cheaply.
> - Sampling diversity, not image blurring or text rephrasing, carries most of the semantic-uncertainty signal, so expensive multi-sample pipelines often buy little over token-level estimates.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A vision-language model can give a fluent but wrong answer about an image. In one example from the paper, the question is "What will the girl on the right write on the board?" The ground truth is **14**, and the model answers **9**. A useful uncertainty estimate could help a system decide when to answer, abstain, request human review, verify, or route a query elsewhere.

The authors do not assume that one confidence measure fits every answer. Instead, they compare existing training-free methods from three families across eight models and five VQA benchmarks. The strongest approach changes with the length of the answer. Token-level entropy is strongest for short answers, prompted abstention for sentence-length answers, and semantic methods for long answers.

### 2. Three ways to ask "how sure?"

**Verbalized** methods prompt the model to give a numerical confidence or to say "UNKNOWN" when uncertain. They are cheap, requiring one forward pass, but they depend on the model's self-calibration.

**Token-level** methods use the model's next-token probability distribution. Entropy measures uncertainty across possible next tokens. Negative log-likelihood measures the probability of the token actually produced. Token scores are aggregated by mean or maximum. These methods require access to model internals.

**Semantic** methods sample multiple answers and group semantically equivalent responses into clusters. They then measure variation over meanings rather than wording. Semantic methods require repeated inference, an entailment model, and clustering.

The study covers InternVL generations 2 and 3.5 and QwenVL generations 2 and 3, at 2B and 7–8B sizes. It uses five benchmarks grouped into short, sentence-length, and long answers.

### 3. The right signal depends on the answer

AUROC measures how well a score ranks incorrect answers as more uncertain than correct ones. A score of .5 is random, and 1.0 is perfect.

Results are aggregated across models and benchmarks within each length group:

- **Short answers:** Max Entropy at T=1 reaches AUROC .76, at a cost of 2× forward passes per sample.
- **Sentence-length answers:** Verbal Abstain reaches .67, at 1×.
- **Long answers:** Semantic Entropy with ten samples reaches .72, at 11×.

These are aggregate findings, not guarantees for every model or dataset. Other metrics do not always select the same row. For sentence-length answers, VASE has Cov@20 .45, slightly above Verbal Abstain's .43. For long answers, VL-Uncertainty has the highest AUROC in the table.73.

Table 1: AUROC, Cov@20, and CHDA for each method in the Short, Sentence, and Long regimes, with forward-pass cost per sample. The results are aggregate, not per model or benchmark.

The authors offer explanations for this pattern. At the usual low inference temperature of .1, the token distribution is sharp and entropy collapses. A separate T=1 pass broadens the signal and improves short-answer ranking, at an extra forward-pass cost. Short answers can also repeat identically across semantic samples, which collapses them to one cluster. For long responses, uncertainty can concern multiple subclaims. The authors argue that meaning-level variation across sampled answers gives a more useful signal than the entropy of any single token.

A controlled comparison supports this pattern. MM-Vet Long is a variant of MM-Vet that asks for complete, multi-sentence answers. Base accuracies are comparable, at 50.4% versus 46.6%. Max Entropy at T=1 moves from AUROC .77 on MM-Vet to .60 on MM-Vet Long, while Semantic Entropy moves from .69 to .71. This is consistent with the length account, but it does not establish that length is the only factor affecting performance.

### 4. A signal is not a safety guarantee

Cov@20 is the maximum fraction of samples answered while keeping errors among answered samples at or below 20%. The best aggregate coverage is .45 for sentence-length answers.43 for short answers, and .21 for long answers. Under a hypothetical perfect-ranking estimator, the corresponding ceilings are .84.71, and .63. The gap illustrates how often current methods fail to flag confidently wrong answers.

The contribution is a comparative study and practical taxonomy that matches uncertainty signals to answer length. It does not propose a new estimator. The findings concern training-free methods on small-to-medium models and general-purpose benchmarks, and they do not amount to a demonstrated safety guarantee.
