# LookOut: Real-World Humanoid Egocentric Navigation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Stanford University introduced LookOut, a framework for predicting future 6D head poses from egocentric video, which supports real-world humanoid navigation amidst static and dynamic obstacles. The approach, aided by a new scalable dataset, shows improved collision avoidance and active information-gathering behaviors over existing methods.

## Problem

- Existing egocentric navigation research often operates under the assumption of static environments, limiting applicability in dynamic real-world scenarios.
- Current navigation models frequently neglect active information gathering behaviors, such as head rotations, which are critical for human perception and safe navigation.
- A significant lack of large-scale, multi-modal, and appropriately labeled training data hinders the development of robust real-world humanoid navigation policies.

## Method

- A novel problem formulation that predicts a sequence of future 6D head poses (3D translation and 3D rotation) from posed egocentric video observations.
- The **LookOut** model architecture, which integrates pre-trained DINO features, a parameter-free unprojection into a 3D voxel grid, and a Bird's ==Eye View (BEV)== projection for efficient semantic and geometric reasoning.
- A scalable data collection pipeline using Project Aria glasses to create the Aria Navigation Dataset (AND), comprising real-world human navigation data with diverse static and dynamic obstacles.

## Results

- LookOut demonstrated superior head pose prediction accuracy and achieved higher non-collision scores for both static and dynamic obstacles compared to several baselines.
- The model learned to exhibit human-like active information-gathering behaviors, such as scanning the environment, and adaptive responses like waiting for clear paths or rerouting around dynamic obstacles.
- Ablation studies confirmed that DINO features and the intermediate 3D feature space are critical components for the model's robust performance in predicting collision-free trajectories.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly modeling 6D head poses enables learning human-like active perception and information-gathering behaviors, which are crucial for safe navigation in dynamic environments.
> - Robust semantic features (from DINO) combined with an intermediate 3D feature space, efficiently projected to a BEV, are effective for understanding egocentric video without explicit depth sensors.
> - A dedicated and scalable real-world data collection methodology is essential for training and evaluating navigation policies that account for complex, dynamic human-environment interactions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of embodied navigation for artificial agents, particularly in complex real-world environments, is an active area of research with applications in humanoid robotics, virtual/augmented reality (VR/AR), and assistive technologies. Existing research has approached this problem from several perspectives, each with distinct focuses and limitations that this work aims to address.

Vision-Language Navigation (VLN) studies typically involve guiding an agent to a specified goal location using natural language instructions. These efforts often focus on long-term path planning and are predominantly conducted in simulated, largely static environments. Examples include Point-Goal, Object-Goal, Image-Goal, and Language-Goal Navigation, which prioritize goal localization and path planning rather than immediate collision-free locomotion in dynamic settings.

Robotic social navigation research, on the other hand, focuses on enabling robots to navigate safely and compliantly in dynamic environments, often interacting with humans. These systems commonly target wheeled or legged robots, which possess different action spaces, observation distributions, scales, speeds, and morphologies compared to humans or humanoid robots. While some studies for humanoid robots exist, they frequently rely on classical methods with specific sensor inputs (e.g., laser and stereo vision) and are often designed for simplified or controlled environments.

More recently, research has explored egocentric navigation for humans, predicting future trajectories or full-body poses from first-person observations. Works such as EgoNav and EgoCast contribute to this area by forecasting human trajectories or full-body poses from head- or chest-mounted cameras. However, a consistent limitation across these approaches is the assumption of static environments, which simplifies the challenge of real-world dynamic obstacle avoidance. Furthermore, these methods generally do not explicitly model or learn the active information-gathering behaviors, such as head turns to scan the environment, which are integral to human navigation strategies.

Another significant challenge in developing robust real-world navigation policies is the scarcity of appropriate multi-modal labeled training data. Deploying complex sensor suites on humanoid robots for data collection is logistically difficult and expensive. While various egocentric datasets exist, they often lack camera pose annotations, focus on diverse activities unrelated to navigation, or do not capture real-world scenarios with dense static and dynamic obstacles. Synthetic data generation attempts have produced simplified and sometimes unnatural motions. Autonomous driving datasets provide extensive 3D tracking of traffic but lack egocentric pedestrian data. Recent datasets leveraging devices like Project Aria glasses offer rich multi-modal egocentric data but often focus on single-human activities or collaborative interactions, not real-world navigation involving collision avoidance with diverse agents.

This work addresses these gaps by:
1. Focusing on **humanoid egocentric navigation** in **real-world dynamic environments**, moving beyond simulated or static settings.
2. Incorporating **active information gathering** (6D head pose prediction, including rotations) as a core aspect of navigation behavior.
3. Proposing a **scalable data collection pipeline** for real-world multi-modal data using Project Aria glasses, thereby mitigating the data scarcity problem.

By tackling these challenges, the paper aims to contribute towards more robust, human-like, and deployable navigation policies for applications requiring a human form factor in complex, dynamic scenarios.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a computational framework capable of predicting a sequence of future 6D head poses from egocentric video observations, enabling real-world humanoid navigation that accounts for both static and dynamic obstacles. This involves predicting both head translations and rotations to capture human-like active information-gathering behaviors.

The motivation for pursuing this objective stems from several identified limitations in the current state of embodied navigation research and the demands of real-world applications:

1. **Addressing Real-World Dynamic Environments:** Many existing navigation policies, particularly those focusing on human egocentric navigation, operate under the assumption of static environments. This simplification limits their applicability in practical scenarios where dynamic obstacles (e.g., pedestrians, vehicles) are prevalent and necessitate real-time collision avoidance. The authors are motivated to create a policy that can navigate effectively amidst such complexities.

2. **Modeling Active Information Gathering:** Human navigation is characterized by active perception, where individuals frequently adjust their head orientation to gather critical visual information from the environment. Examples include looking left and right before crossing a road, or scanning the ground for hazards. Current navigation models often neglect this aspect, predicting only translational trajectories. The researchers are motivated to incorporate 3D head rotations into the prediction task, thereby enabling the model to learn and replicate these vital information-gathering behaviors, which are particularly important given the limited field-of-view of typical camera sensors.

3. **Bridging the Data Gap for Humanoid Navigation:** A significant impediment to developing robust real-world navigation policies for humanoid systems is the lack of large-scale, multi-modal, and appropriately labeled training data. Traditional methods for collecting such data, involving complex sensor suites or teleoperated robots, are often labor-intensive, expensive, and difficult to scale. The motivation here is to devise a streamlined, easily deployable data collection methodology that can capture naturalistic human navigation behaviors in diverse, dynamic real-world settings, thereby providing a valuable resource for training and evaluation.

4. **Enabling Advanced Robotic and AR/VR Applications:** The ability to predict safe, human-like navigation trajectories from egocentric observations is crucial for several advanced applications. In humanoid robotics, such a policy would allow robots to navigate more autonomously and safely in human-centric environments. For VR/AR, it could facilitate more natural and immersive experiences by anticipating user movement or guiding assistive navigation systems. The development of such a capability is seen as a foundational step towards more intelligent and context-aware embodied AI.

In summary, the core motivation is to move beyond existing limitations to develop a comprehensive, real-world deployable humanoid egocentric navigation policy that not only avoids collisions but also mimics human-like perceptual strategies, supported by a scalable data collection infrastructure.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology presented in this paper encompasses a novel problem formulation, a model architecture for 6D head pose prediction, and a dedicated data collection pipeline along with a new dataset.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The task is formulated as predicting a sequence of future 6D head poses from a posed egocentric video. Given an input egocentric video $X \in \mathbb{R}^{T_1 \times H \times W \times 3}$ (RGB frames) and its corresponding past 6D head pose sequence $H_{1:T_1} = \{h_1, \dots, h_{T_1}\} \in \mathbb{R}^{T_1 \times 9}$, the objective is to predict the future 6D head pose sequence $H_{T_1+1:T_1+T_2} = \{\hat{h}_{T_1+1}, \dots, \hat{h}_{T_1+T_2}\} \in \mathbb{R}^{T_2 \times 9}$. Each head pose $h_t = [t_t | r_t]$ includes a 3D translation component $t_t$ and a 6D continuous rotation representation $r_t$. In the experiments, both the input and prediction horizons are set to $T_1 = T_2 = 8$ frames. The head poses are defined within a head-centered canonical frame.

#### 3.2. LookOut Model Architecture

The proposed model, named LookOut, is designed to extract semantic and geometric information from a monocular video stream without relying on explicit depth or LiDAR input. Its architecture consists of several key components:

1. **DINO Feature Encoding:** For strong open-vocabulary semantic modeling, the model utilizes a pre-trained DINO2 (`dinov2_vits14_reg`) encoder. This encoder processes each input RGB frame (down-sampled to 224x224 pixels) to generate a sequence of 2D DINO features $F_{2D} \in \mathbb{R}^{T_1 \times 16 \times 16 \times 384}$.

2. **Parameter-Free Unprojection:** To aggregate information and reason in 3D space, the model employs a "Parameter-Free Unprojection" strategy. A voxel grid of 3D points is defined in a canonical frame. These 3D points are then projected onto the pixel space of each input frame. For each 3D point, its feature encoding is obtained by bilinearly interpolating the 2D DINO feature map at its projected 2D coordinates. This process yields a sequence of 3D DINO feature volumes.

3. **Temporal Aggregation:** The sequence of 3D DINO feature volumes is then aggregated across the time dimension to form a single, comprehensive 3D feature volume $F_{3D} \in \mathbb{R}^{Z \times Y \times X \times 384}$, where $Z=X=96$ and $Y=32$ represent the spatial resolutions of the voxel grid in the Y-up canonical frame. Average pooling across time is used for this aggregation.

4. **BEV Projection:** To reduce computational cost and optimize reasoning, the 3D feature volume $F_{3D}$ is projected into a Bird's Eye View (BEV) representation. This is achieved by "squeezing" the up-axis (Y-axis) using a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) that projects the flattened channel dimension (384 $\times$ Y) to a fixed channel dimension (384). The result is a BEV feature map $F_{BEV} \in \mathbb{R}^{Z \times X \times 384}$.

5. **BEV Net:** The BEV feature map is further processed by a BEV Net, which consists of 11 sequential BEV modules. Each module applies a 2D convolution, Layer Normalization, and an MLP with GELU activation. The hidden dimension increases from 384 to 1540, while the spatial dimension is reduced to 3x3.

6. **Trajectory Prediction:** Finally, the features from the BEV Net undergo spatial average pooling. A 3-layer MLP with Layer Normalization and GELU activation then takes these pooled features to predict the future 6D head pose sequence $\hat{H}_{T_1+1:T_1+T_2}$.

#### 3.3. Loss Function

The model is supervised using a combined L1 loss on both the translation and rotation components of the head poses. The loss function is defined as:
$L = \frac{1}{T_2} \cdot \sum_{t=T_1+1}^{T_1+T_2} (\lambda_{trans} \cdot ||t_t - \hat{t}_t||_1 + \lambda_{rot} \cdot ||R_t \hat{R}_t^{-1} - I||_1)$
where $R_t$ and $\hat{R}_t$ are rotation matrices converted from the 6D rotation representation, $I$ is the identity matrix, and $\lambda_{trans} = \lambda_{rot} = 1$ in the experiments.

#### 3.4. Implementation Details

A head-centered canonical frame is used, which is parallel to the ground plane, faces forward, and is centered on the current head pose $h_{T_1}$. This allows the model to predict future poses in a relative sense. The model is trained using the AdamW optimizer with a weight decay of 0.05. A OneCycle learning rate scheduler with a linear annealing strategy is employed, and training runs for 700k steps with a batch size of 4.

#### 3.5. Aria Navigation Dataset (AND)

Recognizing the absence of suitable datasets, the authors contribute the Aria Navigation Dataset (AND), collected using a dedicated pipeline.

1. **Hardware:** The data collection relies solely on a pair of Project Aria glasses, chosen for their lightweight, non-intrusive nature, cost-effectiveness, and ease of setup.

2. **Recording Process:** Human subjects use the Aria Studio mobile app to activate RGB, SLAM (two monochrome cameras), eye-tracking cameras, and IMU, barometer, and GPS sensors, all operating at 20fps. Subjects are instructed to follow specific information-gathering strategies (e.g., checking for vehicles before crossing roads) to ensure consistent behavior capture.

3. **Data Processing:** Raw data in VRS format is processed using Aria Machine Perception Services (MPS) to obtain accurate 6D head pose trajectories and scene point clouds. Raw fisheye RGB frames are undistorted. Sequences are segmented into (T1+T2)-long clips with a stride and dilation factor of 6 frames. Dynamic objects are filtered during SLAM reconstruction, and further filtering is applied to remove noisy points from the point cloud.

4. **Privacy:** Measures such as blurring faces using a state-of-the-art de-identification algorithm are implemented to ensure privacy compliance with Project Aria research guidelines.

5. **Dataset Statistics:** The AND comprises approximately 4 hours of recorded data, resulting in 274k RGB frames and 36k processed clips. The data was collected across 18 diverse, densely populated real-world locations including university campuses, city downtowns, and parks, during times of dense traffic, ensuring a variety of static and dynamic obstacles.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of LookOut involved quantitative comparisons with baselines, an ablation study, and qualitative assessments of learned behaviors. All results were obtained on a held-out set consisting of environments unseen during training.

#### 4.1. Quantitative Evaluation

**Metrics:**
- **Head Pose Prediction Accuracy:** L1 losses on translation ($L_{1 \text{ trans}}$) and rotation ($L_{1 \text{ rot}}$).
- **Collision Avoidance:** Non-collision scores for static ($Col_{stt k}$) and dynamic ($Col_{dyn k}$) obstacles, measured as the percentage of predictions at least $k$ centimeters away from the closest obstacle. $Col_{\ast avg}$ represents the average over $k \in \{15, 25, 35\}$. Static obstacles are derived from SLAM-reconstructed point clouds, while dynamic obstacles are identified using monocular metric depth estimation (Depth Pro) and DINOv2 + Mask2Former semantic segmentation for "person" pixels. Ground-truth (GT) non-collision scores are provided as reference.

**Comparison with Baselines:**
Since no directly comparable prior work exists for this novel problem formulation, several baselines were implemented or adapted:
1. **Constant Velocity (Const Vel):** Extrapolates future head translations and rotations using velocities from the last two input steps.
2. **Linear Extrapolation (Lin Ext):** Fits a linear regression model to past translation and rotation sequences.
3. **EgoCast:** Adapted by repurposing its transformer-based forecasting module to predict future 6D head poses from past head poses and egocentric video.
4. **A$^*$+Linear Extrapolation (A$^*$+Lin Ext):** Uses linear extrapolation for rotations and an A$^*$ algorithm for translations. The A$^*$ algorithm discretizes the SLAM point cloud into an occupancy grid, with the ground-truth future head translation as the goal, and applies a maximum velocity constraint. For fair comparison, a variant of LookOut was also tested with goal input.

**Results (No-goal Setting):**
LookOut demonstrated superior performance across all metrics compared to the Constant Velocity, Linear Extrapolation, and adapted EgoCast baselines (Table 1).
- $L_{1 \text{ trans}}$: LookOut (0.17) significantly outperformed EgoCast (0.34), Const Vel (0.41), and Lin Ext (0.45).
- $L_{1 \text{ rot}}$: LookOut (0.16) also showed better rotation prediction accuracy than EgoCast (0.63), Const Vel (0.77), and Lin Ext (1.21).
- Non-collision Scores: LookOut consistently achieved the highest non-collision scores for both static and dynamic obstacles across all $k$ values, approaching ground-truth levels more closely than other methods. For example, $Col_{stt 15}$: LookOut (91.3) vs. EgoCast (90.5); $Col_{dyn 15}$: LookOut (97.2) vs. EgoCast (94.6). This indicates LookOut's effectiveness in learning collision-free paths.

**Results (Goal-conditioned Setting):**
When a goal position was provided:
- A$^*$+Lin Ext achieved near-perfect static non-collision scores (e.g., $Col_{stt 15}=98.8$, $Col_{stt 25}=100.0$), as static obstacles were explicitly modeled in its pathfinding. However, its performance on dynamic obstacles was notably poor ($Col_{dyn 15}=82.4$), as they were not represented in its occupancy grid.
- LookOut (+goal) further improved its prediction accuracy ($L_{1 \text{ trans}}=0.11$, $L_{1 \text{ rot}}=0.15$) and maintained high non-collision scores for both static and dynamic obstacles, generally outperforming A$^*$+Lin Ext on dynamic collision avoidance.

#### 4.2. Ablation Study

An ablation study (Table 2) investigated the contribution of key model designs and input modalities:
- **Multi-modal Support:**
 - Adding Point Cloud Data (PCD) or Depth information to RGB input generally improved non-collision metrics. For instance, RGB+PCD yielded $Col_{stt avg}=87.8, Col_{dyn avg}=90.1$, and RGB+Depth yielded $Col_{stt avg}=87.4, Col_{dyn avg}=91.4$, demonstrating the benefit of explicit geometric information. Depth input was particularly helpful for avoiding dynamic obstacles, which are not captured in the SLAM-reconstructed point cloud.
- **Model Design:**
 - **Effectiveness of DINO features:** A variant without DINO (w/o DINO) performed significantly worse ($L_{1 \text{ trans}}=0.35, L_{1 \text{ rot}}=0.67$), underscoring the importance of DINO's semantic encoding capabilities.
 - **3D Feature Space:** A 2D-Only variant (temporally pooling 2D DINO features directly) had reduced performance ($L_{1 \text{ trans}}=0.26, L_{1 \text{ rot}}=0.44$), indicating that the intermediate 3D feature space provides a valuable explicit geometric understanding.
 - **BEV Projection vs. 3D Convolutions:** A variant directly applying 3D convolutions on $F_{3D}$ (3D Conv) performed on par with LookOut (0.17 $L_{1 \text{ trans}}$, 0.19 $L_{1 \text{ rot}}$), but was noted to be more computationally expensive. This validates the BEV projection as an efficient and effective design choice.

#### 4.3. Qualitative Evaluation

Visualizations of model predictions and ground-truths demonstrated several desirable behaviors:
- **Collision Avoidance:** LookOut consistently predicted collision-free paths around both static environmental elements and dynamic obstacles like pedestrians.
- **Information-Gathering:** The model learned human-like head rotations for active information gathering, such as turning the head to check road conditions or potential traffic.
- **Adaptive Behaviors:** The model exhibited complex behaviors such as waiting or slowing down when no clear path was available, rerouting around newly appeared obstacles, and adapting its predictions based on new visual cues. For instance, paths shifted to avoid a newly appearing person.
- **BEV Visualizations:** Bird's Eye View visualizations confirmed that the predicted translational trajectories respected environmental constraints when overlaid on a height map representation of the static environment.

#### 4.4. Failure Cases and Limitations

The authors identified limitations:
- **Lack of Generative Modeling:** In situations with multimodal futures (e.g., multiple equally plausible paths to avoid pedestrians), the model tends to regress to the mean, potentially producing an averaged, suboptimal, or collisive path. This suggests a limitation in handling inherent uncertainty or multiple valid choices in navigation.
- **Generalization to Unseen Scenarios:** The model struggled to predict specific behaviors (e.g., looking down to check for a rail) if those scenarios or objects were not represented in the training data, indicating a need for more diverse training data to improve generalization to novel environmental features.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of embodied artificial intelligence and robotics through several key advancements, with implications for enhanced navigation capabilities in complex real-world settings.

Firstly, the introduction of **6D head pose trajectory prediction from posed egocentric observations in dynamic environments** represents a novel and challenging task formulation. By explicitly modeling both translations and rotations, the work moves beyond previous approaches that largely focused on translational movement in static scenes. This emphasis on active information gathering through head movements is a significant step towards developing more human-like and perceptually intelligent navigation policies, which can actively seek out relevant visual cues for safer and more efficient path planning, particularly in scenarios with limited sensor fields-of-view.

Secondly, the proposed **LookOut model** offers a methodological contribution by demonstrating an effective approach to integrating semantic and geometric understanding from monocular egocentric video. Leveraging pre-trained DINO features for robust semantic encoding and a parameter-free unprojection strategy for 3D geometric reasoning allows the model to achieve strong performance without requiring explicit depth sensors, making it adaptable to a wider range of hardware. The BEV projection strategy further enhances computational efficiency while maintaining performance.

Thirdly, the development of a **scalable data collection pipeline using Project Aria glasses** and the resulting **Aria Navigation Dataset (AND)** address a critical data gap in the field. The ease of setup, non-intrusive nature, and multi-modal capture capabilities of the Project Aria platform facilitate the collection of extensive, naturalistic human navigation data in diverse, dynamic real-world environments. This dataset, comprising 4 hours of real-world sessions across 18 densely trafficked locations, provides a valuable resource for the research community, enabling further development and evaluation of real-world humanoid navigation policies that account for static and dynamic obstacles.

The demonstrated ability of LookOut to learn diverse human-like navigation behaviors, such as waiting for clear paths, rerouting around dynamic obstacles, and actively scanning the environment, indicates progress towards more autonomous and adaptive intelligent agents.

The potential impact of this work extends across several domains:
- **Humanoid Robotics:** The developed policies could enable humanoid robots to navigate more safely and naturally in human-centric environments, interacting effectively with both static and dynamic elements. This can facilitate deployment in logistics, assistance, or social interaction scenarios.
- **VR/AR Applications:** By predicting future head poses and incorporating human-like information gathering, the research can contribute to more immersive and intuitive VR/AR experiences, potentially enhancing user comfort and interaction within virtual or augmented spaces. It could also support assistive navigation systems that provide guidance aligned with natural human perception.
- **Embodied AI Research:** The novel task, model, and dataset provide a foundation for future research in embodied AI, particularly in areas like multimodal motion forecasting, robust scene understanding from egocentric views, and learning complex human-environment interactions. The identified limitations regarding generative modeling for multimodal futures and generalization to unseen scenarios also open clear avenues for subsequent investigations.

Overall, this research represents a step towards creating robust, perceptually-aware, and human-like navigation capabilities for artificial agents operating in complex real-world environments.
