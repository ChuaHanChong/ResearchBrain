# Chameleon: Episodic Memory for Long-Horizon Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Nanyang Technological University developed Chameleon, an episodic memory architecture for robotic manipulation that addresses perceptual aliasing in long-horizon tasks. On a real-robot dataset, Chameleon achieved a 100.0% decision success rate for episodic recall, 73.5% for spatial tracking, and 72.2% for sequential manipulation, significantly outperforming existing baselines.

## Problem

- Robotic manipulation tasks often involve perceptual aliasing, making decisions non-Markovian at the observation level and requiring access to historical context.
- Current memory systems either discard critical fine-grained perceptual cues through semantic compression or suffer from interference due to similarity-based retrieval of visually similar but decision-irrelevant past events.
- The inability to form disambiguated, indexable event representations and perform goal-directed retrieval limits long-horizon robotic control.

## Method

- An architecture named **Chameleon**, drawing inspiration from human episodic memory, is introduced with Perception, Memory, and Policy modules for closed-loop robotic manipulation.
- The Perception module creates geometry-grounded, view-consistent tokens using dorsal and ventral streams with epipolar and learned geometric biases for cross-view enhancement.
- The Memory module employs spatiotemporal anchors, multi-timescale episodic states, and a "HoloHead" imagination objective to achieve disambiguated encoding and goal-directed retrieval for a compact decision state.

## Results

- Chameleon demonstrated superior performance on the Camo-Dataset, achieving **100.0%** Decision Success Rate (DSR) in episodic recall, **73.5%** DSR in spatial tracking, and **72.2%** DSR in sequential manipulation, significantly outperforming baselines.
- Ablation studies confirmed that the structured memory, dorsal stream for geometry-biased perception, and the HoloHead's latent imagination objective are critical for the architecture's effectiveness.
- Qualitative mechanistic validation showed that the system's decision state supports pattern separation by clustering visually aliased states and pattern completion through consistent predictive rollouts, encoding task-relevant historical information.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Perceptual aliasing renders memory essential for robotic manipulation, as optimal actions depend on interaction history, not just current observations.
> - Bio-inspired memory components like geometry-grounded disambiguating encoding and a latent imagination objective (HoloHead) are crucial for robust pattern separation and goal-directed recall in robotic episodic memory.
> - A hierarchical episodic memory system, generating a compact decision state, can effectively condition a rectified-flow policy for stable, long-horizon control trajectories.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation is increasingly focusing on long-horizon tasks that necessitate advanced memory capabilities. Existing literature, particularly recent benchmarks like LIBERO-10, MemoryBench, and MIKASA-Robo, has highlighted a recurring challenge: robots frequently encounter scenarios where information critical for a future action is observed significantly earlier than the action's execution and is no longer directly perceptible at the decision point. This phenomenon is termed "observation–action delay" and renders manipulation tasks non-Markovian within the policy's immediate observation space, as the optimal action depends on past events rather than the current input alone.

Prior approaches to equipping robots with memory include utilizing recurrent hidden states, extending observation windows, or employing external memory banks. While these methods have demonstrated benefits across various scales and complexities, they often face limitations when confronted with observation–action delay. Compact memory summaries can inadvertently discard fine-grained event details necessary for precise control. Furthermore, similarity-based retrieval mechanisms, common in many memory-augmented policies, might retrieve visually plausible but control-irrelevant past observations. A fundamental gap identified in the broader landscape is the need for memory mechanisms that not only store and retrieve information but also process it into an "action-ready" state, directly usable for current control decisions.

Chameleon addresses this specific bottleneck by proposing a "control-indexed prospective memory" architecture. Unlike general-purpose memory systems that may prioritize visual similarity or comprehensive scene representation, Chameleon emphasizes the ability to retrieve and interpret memory traces based on their relevance to an ongoing control decision. This work positions itself as advancing the understanding and implementation of "policy-facing memory," where memory's utility is defined by its ability to facilitate future action rather than solely by its storage or descriptive capacity. It draws functional inspiration from aspects of human episodic memory (e.g., pattern separation, cue-dependent retrieval, goal-directed prediction) to establish a set of requirements for embodied memory that are directly relevant to solving observation–action delay in visuomotor manipulation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable robotic agents to effectively manage "observation–action delay" in visuomotor manipulation tasks. This delay occurs when critical information required for a future action is observed at an earlier time step but is no longer directly observable or unambiguous by the time the action needs to be executed. For instance, in a shell game, a robot must remember which cup initially contained a ball, even after the cups have been shuffled and the ball is hidden. The motivation stems from the recognition that current policies often fail in such non-Markovian scenarios because their memory systems are not adequately designed to preserve, retrieve, and utilize context-dependent information over time for control purposes.

To address this, the authors identify three key requirements for an effective policy-facing memory system, drawing conceptual parallels from human episodic memory:

1. **Separability**: The system must be capable of storing distinct, fine-grained event traces even when the underlying observations are perceptually similar or overlapping. This prevents similar experiences from collapsing into a single, undifferentiated memory state, thereby preserving the unique distinctions that might be crucial for future actions. For example, if multiple identical objects are involved, the memory must distinguish which specific object was involved in a particular event.
2. **Addressability**: The memory system needs a mechanism to actively query and retrieve the specific past trace that is relevant to the *current* decision or control task, rather than merely recalling the most recent or visually similar information. This implies an intelligent indexing system that can link current control needs to specific historical events.
3. **Prospectiveness**: Recalled information must be consolidated into a working state that is directly interpretable and actionable by the policy for *future* control. It is not sufficient for memory to be merely descriptive of past events; it must actively prepare the policy for upcoming actions and decisions, effectively making the past "action-ready."

The core motivation is to instantiate these three principles within a visuomotor policy to allow robots to learn and perform complex, long-horizon manipulation tasks where decisions are fundamentally dependent on previously observed, but currently unavailable, information. The research aims to move beyond generic memory augmentation by explicitly designing memory to be "control-indexed" and "prospective," thereby tackling the specific challenges posed by observation–action delay.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Chameleon is presented as a causal visuomotor policy with approximately 60 million trainable parameters, designed to implement control-indexed prospective memory. The policy processes RGB camera views, proprioceptive data, and optional language instructions at each timestep to predict a short horizon of future actions. Its architecture is structured around a write–propagate–address–consolidate loop.

#### 3.1. Embodied Event Tokens

To preserve detailed information for future control, Chameleon avoids immediate pooling of observations into a global descriptor. Instead, it converts each timestep's input into a set of "embodied event tokens." Visual inputs from multiple cameras are processed by a DP-style patch encoder to generate patch-level visual tokens. Proprioceptive data and language instructions (encoded by a frozen DistilBERT model) are projected into the same token space. These concatenated visual, proprioceptive, and language tokens form `Z_0_t`, which serves as the input for the memory module.

#### 3.2. Control-Indexed Prospective Memory Cell

The memory module consists of `L` causal layers, each executing four sequential operations:

1. **Event Binding**: At the beginning of each layer, event tokens (`Z_ℓ_t`) interact through a residual self-attention block (`Mixer_ℓ`). This internal interaction allows visual, proprioceptive, and language tokens to contextualize each other, forming a richer "bound event" (`¯Z_ℓ_t`) before temporal propagation. This mechanism constitutes the memory "write" interface.

2. **Token-Grounded Trace Propagation (Separability)**: To maintain distinct histories, Chameleon propagates a bank of "token-grounded traces" (`E_ℓ`) rather than a single recurrent vector. A shared token-wise temporal kernel, implemented using a selective state-space model (`SSM_ℓ_slow`), processes each individual token stream along the causal time axis. This design ensures that `N` separable histories are preserved, where `N` is the total number of event tokens. The recurrence is strictly causal, with `e_ℓ_t,i` (trace for token `i` at time `t`) depending only on `¯Z_ℓ_1:t,i`.

3. **Control Index and Control Context (Addressability)**: For effective retrieval, the policy generates a "control index" (`c_ℓ_t`) from the bound proprioceptive and language tokens. This index, representing the body and task-specific query, is then refined into a "control context" (`u_ℓ_t`) by attending over the current bound event tokens (`¯Z_ℓ_t`). This refinement step integrates present-scene evidence with the task and body context, forming a query that is specifically tailored to the current control decision.

4. **Control-Indexed Recall and Working-State Consolidation**: The `control context (u_ℓ_t)` queries the `token-grounded traces (E_ℓ_t)` via attention to recall a "recalled trace" (`r_ℓ_t`). This query-based mechanism enables addressable recall, where the same trace bank can yield different content depending on the current control context. The `recalled trace (r_ℓ_t)` is then fused with the `control context (u_ℓ_t)` to update a "fast working state" (`h_ℓ_t`) through another selective state-space model (`SSM_ℓ_fast`). This fast stream maintains the policy-facing working state, which is then written back into the event tokens for the next layer's processing. The final prospective working state after all layers is `h_t = h_L_t`.

#### 3.3. Prospective Training with Control-JEPA

To address the credit-assignment problem inherent in observation–action delay, Chameleon employs "Control-JEPA" (Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture adapted for control). This training objective encourages the causal working state `h_t` to predict future control contexts, rather than merely reconstructing past observations or full world states. An EMA (Exponential Moving Average) target branch encodes future embodied event tokens into target control contexts (`˜u_t+k`). A predictor receives `h_t` and a horizon embedding to predict `ˆu_t+k`. The `L_CJEP` objective uses a smooth-L1 alignment loss between `ˆu_t+k` and the stop-gradient `˜u_t+k`, along with a variance regularization term to prevent representation collapse. The horizon set `K` spans both near-term and delayed control contexts.

#### 3.4. Memory-Conditioned Action Policy

The final prospective working state `h_t` is used to condition a transformer rectified-flow head for continuous action generation. The policy is trained with a clean-endpoint rectified-flow objective, where it predicts the ground-truth action horizon `A_t:t+H-1` from a noised action chunk and `h_t`. During inference, actions are generated by iteratively updating a Gaussian-noise-initialized action chunk along the rectified-flow path. The overall loss combines the action loss (`L_act`) and the Control-JEPA loss (`L_CJEP`) with a weighting factor `α`.

#### 3.5. Evaluation Strategy

The evaluation proceeds in three stages:
1. **Diagnostic**: Introduction of `Camo-Dataset`, a real-robot UR5 benchmark designed to isolate observation–action delay, differentiating between memory-dependent decision success (DSR) and execution success (MSR).
2. **Generalization**: Testing on public long-horizon memory benchmarks: LIBERO-10, MemoryBench, and MIKASA-Robo.
3. **Mechanistic Analysis**: Using representation probes and ablation studies to investigate whether separability, addressability, and prospectiveness are realized and functionally necessary for the observed performance gains.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of Chameleon demonstrates its capabilities across diagnostic real-robot scenarios, public benchmarks, and through mechanistic analyses.

#### 4.1. Real-Robot Diagnostic Results on Camo-Dataset

On the newly introduced Camo-Dataset, designed to specifically highlight observation–action delay, Chameleon showed substantial performance improvements. The average decision success rate (DSR) and end-to-end success rate (SR) improved from 22.5%/21.3% for Diffusion Policy, the strongest matched baseline, to 80.8%/71.3%. This indicates Chameleon's ability to correctly make history-dependent choices in visually ambiguous situations.

Ablation studies on Camo-Dataset provided evidence for the necessity of Chameleon's proposed mechanisms:
- **Separability**: Removing the memory module entirely (`w/o memory`) resulted in DSR/SR of 20.4%/17.6%. Replacing token-grounded traces with a vanilla Mamba memory (`Vanilla Mamba memory`) yielded 35.9%/22.2%. These results suggest that preserving distinct, token-grounded traces is more effective than undifferentiated recurrent memory or no memory.
- **Addressability**: Using a `similarity retrieval bank` instead of control-indexed recall led to 23.3%/18.5% DSR/SR. Removing the `control index` entirely resulted in 48.8%/26.0% DSR/SR. These comparisons indicate that memory recall benefits from being conditioned on the current control state rather than relying solely on visual similarity.
- **Prospectiveness**: Removing the `Control-JEPA` objective reduced the SR from 71.3% to 52.8%, although decision signal was partially retained (DSR of 71.6%). This suggests that training the working state to predict future control contexts enhances its reliability for downstream actions.

#### 4.2. Public Long-Horizon and Memory Benchmarks

Chameleon demonstrated competitive and, in several cases, leading performance on established public benchmarks:
- **MemoryBench**: Chameleon achieved 97.3% ± 4.5% success rate. This score either matches or surpasses the original spatial-memory baselines and compares favorably with the strongest reported memory-augmented VLA results, even under modified protocols.
- **LIBERO-10**: Chameleon reached 87.1% ± 0.8% success rate. This represents an improvement over standard diffusion-policy and discrete-action baselines and multiple larger Vision-Language-Action (VLA) systems.
- **MIKASA-Robo**: Chameleon achieved the highest reported performance in both tested settings. For the 5-task mixed-policy setting, it improved the average success rate from 67.8% (GMP baseline) to 75.1% ± 1.4%. In the 2-task specialist setting, it achieved 95.6% ± 1.0%, exceeding the previous best of 86.5% (DP-VPWEM).

#### 4.3. Mechanistic Probes

Probes conducted on Camo-Dataset provided direct evidence for the realization of the proposed memory properties:
- **Separability**: Hidden-variable decoding from the final policy-facing memory state (`h_L_t`) at aliased decision frames was consistently more accurate than decoding from current event tokens (`Z_0_t`). For instance, on "Play shell game," memory decoding achieved 83.3% accuracy compared to 46.7% for current tokens. On "Add various seasonings," the difference was 98.5% vs. 37.4%. Visualizations also showed memory trajectories remaining organized by initial object identity even after visual aliasing.
- **Addressability**: Counterfactual trace editing experiments in "Add various seasonings" demonstrated that policy choice accuracy was sensitive to manipulations of the *relevant* trace within the trace bank, but remained stable when *irrelevant* traces were removed. For example, fixing the control query and swapping the relevant trace still led to 87% choice accuracy (following the swapped trace), while masking the relevant trace dropped accuracy to 40%. This supports that recall is selected by control relevance rather than mere temporal proximity or visual similarity.
- **Prospectiveness**: Probes predicting future target endpoints or subgoal modes from the working state `h_t` showed that Chameleon exposed this future control information earlier and more reliably compared to the `w/o Control-JEPA` ablation. This indicates that the Control-JEPA objective effectively shapes `h_t` into an action-ready state.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of robotic manipulation by formally defining and addressing "observation–action delay" as a critical control-level memory failure. It highlights that in complex, long-horizon tasks, the utility of memory for robots is not merely about storage capacity or visual similarity matching, but rather its ability to provide action-ready information at the precise moment it is required for decision-making.

The introduction of **Chameleon**, a visuomotor policy incorporating "control-indexed prospective memory," offers a novel architectural paradigm for embodied memory. By instantiating the principles of separability, addressability, and prospectiveness, Chameleon provides a structured approach to memory that prioritizes the actionable utility of past information. The demonstrated performance gains on both a custom diagnostic real-robot benchmark (Camo-Dataset) and a suite of public long-horizon memory benchmarks (LIBERO-10, MemoryBench, MIKASA-Robo) indicate that this design philosophy is effective. The fact that Chameleon achieves state-of-the-art performance among same-size models and surpasses several larger Vision-Language-Action (VLA) baselines suggests an efficiency in its memory utilization.

The mechanistic probes and ablation studies lend empirical support to the functional necessity of the proposed memory properties. This detailed analysis provides insights into *how* the memory system contributes to improved performance, moving beyond black-box effectiveness to a more nuanced understanding of embodied memory function. Such insights can inform the design of future robotic AI systems.

The potential impact of this work extends to enhancing the autonomy and capability of robots in real-world applications where tasks involve sequential dependencies, occlusions, and temporal gaps between observation and action. By enabling robots to robustly remember and utilize past, non-Markovian information for control, Chameleon could facilitate more reliable performance in domains such as manufacturing, service robotics, and assistive technologies, where long-term task planning and error recovery are crucial.

Future directions for this research include exploring the scalability of control-indexed memory across diverse embodiments, sensor configurations, and task families. Its potential integration as a reusable module within foundation-scale robot policies could lead to improved cross-task, cross-embodiment, and cross-environment generalization. Furthermore, coupling prospective memory with active perception could enable robots to proactively acquire or refresh critical evidence before it becomes essential for an upcoming action, thereby enhancing robustness and adaptability.
