# Ambient Diffusion Policy: Imitation Learning from Suboptimal Data in Robotics

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at MIT developed Ambient Diffusion Policy, an imitation learning method that integrates suboptimal robot data by selectively allowing its contribution to training at specific noise levels. This approach improves policy performance and robustness, outperforming baselines like data filtering and co-training on tasks ranging from maze navigation to large-scale robotic manipulation.

## Problem

- High-quality robot data is expensive and difficult to acquire, while abundant suboptimal data is often discarded or improperly utilized by existing imitation learning methods.
- Current data filtering techniques waste potentially useful information, and simple co-training often introduces bias from detrimental features present in suboptimal datasets.
- Existing methods struggle to distinguish between useful signals and detrimental noise in heterogeneous robotics datasets, requiring complex strategies or high proportions of quality data.

## Method

- Introduces **Ambient Diffusion Policy**, which selectively allows ==suboptimal data== to contribute to training within a Diffusion Policy framework based on the diffusion noise level.
- Leverages the hierarchical learning properties of diffusion models, where high noise levels correspond to learning global planning and low noise levels correspond to fine-grained motion primitives.
- Utilizes a classifier-based annotation method to determine `t_min` and `t_max` parameters for suboptimal samples, defining specific noise ranges where they are admissible for training.

## Results

- Ambient Diffusion Policy consistently outperformed data filtering and co-training baselines across controlled experiments (noisy trajectories, sim-to-real, task mismatch) and large-scale Open X-Embodiment datasets.
- Achieved smoother trajectories and higher success rates; for instance, in a 2D maze, it yielded **99.5%** success with 31.0 average squared acceleration, compared to co-training's **99.4%** success with 62.2.
- Demonstrated scalability and reduced sensitivity to dataset re-weighting on Open X-Embodiment, achieving **83%** task completion for table cleaning with 50 high-quality demos, outperforming co-training (**71%**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Robot action data exhibits a spectral power law, which induces a global-to-local hierarchy in action diffusion; high noise levels mask local details, while low noise levels reveal fine-grained dynamics.
> - The spectral power law and derived locality property of optimal denoisers justify noise-dependent data usage, enabling the policy to learn useful features from suboptimal data while ignoring detrimental ones.
> - Selectively using suboptimal data at appropriate noise levels acts as a novel co-training axis, allowing policies to learn shared high-level plans from diverse data or shared local motion primitives from misaligned data.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robotics research, particularly in imitation learning, increasingly relies on large and diverse datasets to train robust and generalizable policies. However, obtaining high-quality, task-specific robot data is often resource-intensive, requiring specialized equipment, expert teleoperators, and significant time investment. In contrast, suboptimal datasets, comprising lower-quality demonstrations, out-of-distribution examples, or failed attempts, are abundant. These suboptimal data sources arise from various scenarios, including variations in teleoperator skill, sensor noise, sim-to-real discrepancies, cross-embodiment data, and task mismatches.

Current approaches to handle such heterogeneous datasets in robotics typically involve either discarding low-quality samples, which is wasteful of potentially useful information, or co-training on all available data by weighting the contribution of different datasets to the loss function. Data filtering, while ensuring high-quality input, removes a significant portion of available data. Co-training methods, while leveraging more data, can introduce bias into the learned policy if the suboptimal distributions contain harmful features that are not adequately differentiated from meaningful ones. Existing methods in this area often face challenges in separating useful signals from detrimental noise in suboptimal data, requiring complex multi-run strategies, knowledge of corruption types, or relying on high proportions of quality data.

This work addresses the underexplored challenge of effectively leveraging arbitrary suboptimal or shifted distributions in robotics. It draws inspiration from "Ambient Diffusion Omni," a framework previously applied in computer vision and protein design, to propose a method that integrates suboptimal data in a principled manner during the diffusion training process.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust and principled method for imitation learning in robotics that can effectively utilize abundant suboptimal data alongside limited high-quality data. The central motivation stems from the recognition that while high-quality robot data is expensive to acquire, suboptimal data sources are plentiful and contain potentially valuable learning signals that are often discarded or improperly utilized by existing methods.

The specific motivations and objectives include:
- **Overcoming limitations of existing methods:** Current practices of data filtering are deemed wasteful, and co-training often fails to distinguish between useful and harmful features in suboptimal samples, leading to biased policies. The research aims to mitigate these issues.
- **Extracting useful features from suboptimal data:** The core intuition is that suboptimal and high-quality samples may differ in some features (e.g., precision of manipulation) but align in others (e.g., high-level plan). The goal is to devise a mechanism that selectively extracts only the useful features from suboptimal data, without propagating the detrimental ones.
- **Introducing noise-dependent data usage:** The paper proposes a novel axis for co-training in robotics: noise-dependent data usage. This is motivated by the observation that Diffusion Policies learn different features of actions at varying noise levels – high-level planning at high noise and fine-grained motion primitives at low noise. By controlling when suboptimal data contributes to training based on noise levels, the policy can selectively learn features where suboptimal data aligns with the target distribution.
- **Establishing theoretical justification:** The work seeks to rigorously justify its approach by demonstrating fundamental properties of robot action data. Specifically, it empirically shows that robot action data exhibits a spectral power law, which induces a global-to-local hierarchy and a locality property in action diffusion. These properties are then formalized theoretically using a simplified model.
- **Demonstrating generality and scalability:** A key objective is to validate the proposed method across various types of action suboptimality (noisy trajectories, sim-to-real gap, task mismatch) and to demonstrate its scalability to large, heterogeneous datasets like Open X-Embodiment (OXE), showcasing its practical applicability in real-world robotics.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, Ambient Diffusion Policy, is a principled approach for imitation learning from suboptimal data in robotics. It operates by selectively allowing suboptimal data to contribute to training at specific noise levels within a Diffusion Policy framework.

**Background: Diffusion Policy and Problem Setting**
The work builds upon Diffusion Policy, which learns a conditional denoising process over action space to approximate the target policy $\pi(A|O)$. The policy learns a family of denoisers $h_\theta(A_t, O, t)$ that predict a clean action $A_0$ from a noisy version $A_t = A_0 + \sigma(t)Z$. The training minimizes a denoising loss between the predicted and clean actions.
The problem setting involves a small, high-quality dataset $D_p$ from a target distribution $p$, and a larger dataset $D_q$ from a shifted or suboptimal distribution $q$. The goal is to improve the denoisers' estimation of $E_p[A_0 | A_t, O]$ by leveraging $D_q$.

**Core Idea: Ambient Diffusion Omni and Noise-Dependent Data Usage**
Inspired by Ambient Diffusion Omni, the method restricts the contribution of suboptimal samples from $D_q$ to specific diffusion times $t \in [0, t_{max}) \cup (t_{min}, T]$. The underlying principle is that adding noise (increasing $t$) makes distributions $p$ and $q$ contract towards each other, meaning they become indistinguishable at sufficiently high noise levels. Conversely, at low noise levels, specific local features might align even if global features differ.

**Distributional Properties of Robot Data**
The paper empirically establishes two key properties of robot action data that justify Ambient Diffusion Policy:
1. **Spectral Power Law:** Robot action data exhibits a spectral power law, meaning its power spectral density (PSD) decays approximately as $|f|^{-\alpha}$ (where $f$ is frequency, $\alpha > 0$). This implies that energy is concentrated in low-frequency components.
 - **Implications for Diffusion Policy:** This spectral structure induces a global-to-local hierarchy in action diffusion. Denoisers learn high-level, global planning at high noise levels (low frequencies) and fine-grained, local motion primitives at low noise levels (high frequencies).
 - **Implications for Ambient Diffusion Policy:** If differences between $p$ and $q$ are concentrated in high-frequency (local) features, then $D_q$ can be safely used at high noise levels to learn useful global features, as the local suboptimality is masked by noise.
2. **Locality:** An optimal denoiser exhibits locality if its output at each coordinate depends primarily on a small receptive field in the noisy input. The paper empirically shows this in robotics, where each action output is most sensitive to its temporal neighbors.
 - **Implications for Ambient Diffusion Policy:** If $p$ and $q$ share local motion primitives but differ globally, $D_q$ can be used at low noise levels (where $t < t_{max}$) to learn these local action primitives, as the denoiser ignores the global (incorrect) structure of the suboptimal data.

**Theoretical Foundations**
The empirical observations are supported by theoretical results for a simplified model (zero-mean stationary Gaussians):
- **Theorem 1 (Spectral Power Law implies fast contraction through noise):** If $p$ and $q$ agree on low frequencies and have power-law tails, then the KL divergence and Total Variation distance between their noisy versions ($p_t$ and $q_t$) contract quickly as noise increases, independently of the original distance. This supports the use of $D_q$ for $t > t_{min}$.
- **Theorem 2 (Spectral Power Law implies locality of the optimal denoiser):** For a spectral power law with exponent $\alpha > 1$, the optimal denoiser is functionally myopic at low noise levels. It can reconstruct a signal from a local spatial neighborhood, justifying the use of $D_q$ for $t < t_{max}$ when local features align.

**Ambient Diffusion Policy Algorithm**
The algorithm has two phases:
1. **Phase 1: Dataset Annotation:** This phase assigns $t_{min}$ and $t_{max}$ parameters for suboptimal samples in $D_q$.
 - $t_{min}$: Determined by training a binary classifier $c_\phi(A_t, t)$ to predict if a noisy action $A_t$ came from $p_t$ or $q_t$. $t_{min}$ is the minimum noise level at which the classifier cannot reliably distinguish $p_t$ from $q_t$ (i.e., $E_{A_t \sim q_t} [c_\phi(A_t, t)] > 0.5 - \tau$). A theoretical guarantee (Theorem 4) links classifier performance to the distributional closeness of $p_t$ and $q_t$.
 - $t_{max}$: Can be annotated similarly using a classifier or empirically determined. For many experiments, $t_{max}=0$ was used, meaning suboptimal data was only used at high noise.
 - Annotation granularity can vary (per dataset, per sample, per partition).
2. **Phase 2: Training:** The training loop is similar to Diffusion Policy but with a custom data sampler. For each training step, a batch of diffusion times $t^{(i)} \sim U[0, T]$ is sampled. Samples from $D_p$ are always used. Suboptimal samples from $D_q$ are only admissible if $t^{(i)} \in [0, t_{max}) \cup (t_{min}, T]$. The policy parameters are updated to minimize the denoising loss or, optionally, the Ambient loss (which predicts $x_{t_{min}}$ instead of $x_0$).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments evaluated Ambient Diffusion Policy across controlled settings and large-scale real-world datasets, consistently demonstrating its effectiveness compared to data filtering and co-training baselines.

**Controlled Experiments:**

- **Noisy Trajectories (2D Maze & 7-DoF Neural Motion Planning):**
 - **2D Maze:** When learning to navigate a maze, Ambient Diffusion Policy achieved a 99.5% success rate (comparable to co-training at 99.4%) but produced substantially smoother trajectories (31.0 average squared acceleration) than co-training (62.2). Data filtering (using only high-quality GCS data) resulted in 57.5% success. The smoother trajectories from Ambient were attributed to restricting the jittery RRT data at low diffusion times, preventing the policy from learning non-smooth behaviors. Ambient's Pareto frontier for the "smoothness vs. success rate" trade-off dominated co-training.
 - **7-DoF Neural Motion Planning:** Similar trends were observed. Ambient achieved a 65.9% success rate, outperforming co-training (59.9%) and data filtering (46.0%), while maintaining significantly greater smoothness (31.4 vs. 42.7 for co-training). These results suggest Ambient can leverage large-scale suboptimal (e.g., sampling-based) datasets for general planning while ensuring high-quality, smooth robot motions.
 - **Ablation (D_q only at high noise):** A policy exclusively trained on $D_q$ for $t \in (t_{min}, T]$ and $D_p$ for $t \in [0, t_{min}]$ in the maze task matched Ambient's performance, supporting the hierarchical learning claim.

- **Sim-to-Real Distribution Shift (Planar Pushing):**
 - Ambient Diffusion Policy, in its various configurations, consistently outperformed the co-training baseline on a planar pushing task involving a sim-to-real (sim-to-target) gap. The best Ambient variant, using per-datapoint $t_{min}$ annotation, achieved a 93.5% success rate, compared to 84.5% for co-training and 56.5% for data filtering.
 - This highlights Ambient's ability to extract useful high-level strategies from simulated data while ignoring low-level dynamic discrepancies. Locality ($t_{max} > 0$) did not provide additional benefits, suggesting that sim-to-real differences affect low-level contact dynamics rather than local motion primitives.

- **Task Mismatch (Block Sorting):**
 - In a task where $D_p$ showed correct block sorting and $D_q$ showed opposite sorting, Ambient (leveraging locality, with $t_{max} > 0$) achieved near-perfect scores on both "logic" (98.2%) and "motion" (95.0%) metrics, leading to a 93.3% overall success rate. Co-training performed poorly (26.0% logic, 87.2% motion, 22.7% success) because it learned the incorrect task logic from $D_q$.
 - Ambient's success was attributed to learning local motion primitives (grasping) from $D_q$ at low diffusion times ($t < t_{max}$) while ignoring the incorrect global task logic.

- **Finetuning Comparison:**
 - Finetuning an Ambient base policy consistently outperformed finetuning a co-trained base policy. In some cases, the Ambient base policy itself outperformed a finetuned co-trained policy, suggesting that Ambient policies are inherently less susceptible to harmful features in suboptimal data.

**Scaling Experiments (Open X-Embodiment - OXE):**

- **Table Cleaning:** With 50 high-quality demos, the best Ambient policy achieved an 83% task completion rate, outperforming co-training (71%) and data filtering (68.2%). With 150 high-quality demos, Ambient still outperformed co-training (87% vs. 82%). Ambient continued to improve as the amount of suboptimal data scaled (from MS++ to COXE), whereas co-training plateaud.
- **Tower Building:** With 35 high-quality demos, Ambient policies built towers 84% taller on average than data filtering and 33% taller than co-training.
- **Dataset Re-weighting Ablation:** Ambient policies were significantly less sensitive to dataset re-weighting than co-training policies. Unweighted co-trained policies were too unstable to evaluate, whereas unweighted Ambient policies still performed effectively (at most 9% worse than weighted Ambient). This indicates that Ambient's noise-dependent data usage reduces the criticality of precise dataset weighting.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Ambient Diffusion Policy introduces a novel and principled approach to imitation learning from suboptimal data in robotics, addressing a fundamental and persistent challenge in the field. Its significance and potential impact are multifold:

- **Increased Utility of Suboptimal Data:** The method effectively extracts valuable learning signals from diverse sources of suboptimal data that might otherwise be discarded or lead to compromised policy performance. This significantly expands the pool of usable data for training robot policies, which is crucial given the high cost and scarcity of pristine, task-specific robot data.
- **Enhanced Policy Performance and Robustness:** By selectively utilizing suboptimal data, Ambient Diffusion Policy consistently outperforms traditional data filtering and co-training baselines across various tasks and types of suboptimality. It achieves higher success rates while maintaining desirable properties like motion smoothness, and demonstrates robustness to distribution shifts and scaling of suboptimal data.
- **Novel Design Axis for Co-training:** The introduction of noise-dependent data usage as a design axis for co-training provides a new paradigm for integrating heterogeneous datasets. This approach is more nuanced than simple re-weighting, allowing policies to selectively learn features at appropriate levels of abstraction (global planning at high noise, local motion primitives at low noise).
- **Theoretical Justification of Data Properties:** The empirical demonstration and theoretical formalization of the spectral power law in robot action data, along with its implications for a global-to-local hierarchy and locality in action diffusion, contribute to a deeper understanding of robot data characteristics and their interaction with diffusion models. This theoretical grounding provides a strong foundation for future research in robot learning.
- **Scalability to Real-World Datasets:** The successful application and superior performance on large, heterogeneous datasets like Open X-Embodiment underscore the method's practical applicability and potential to advance the state-of-the-art in training generalist robot policies. Its reduced sensitivity to dataset re-weighting also offers practical benefits in real-world deployments.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The paper acknowledges several limitations, including:
- The current method primarily addresses distribution shifts in the action space, with observation space shifts remaining an open challenge. Initial explorations into noising observations or classifier-free guidance did not yield significant improvements.
- The classifier-based annotation method for $t_{min}$ and $t_{max}$ does not always outperform manual hyperparameter sweeps, suggesting room for improvement in automated data annotation strategies.
- The theoretical foundations are currently limited to simplified Gaussian models, and extending these results to a broader class of distributions would provide further insights.
- The method is computationally more expensive than data filtering due to training on more data, though the benefits often outweigh this cost.
- The need for users to partition data into $D_p$ and $D_q$ highlights a dependency on manual design decisions for downstream performance, emphasizing the need for a more principled understanding of data quality in robotics.

Future research directions include addressing observation-space shifts, developing more robust and automated annotation methods for $t_{min}$ and $t_{max}$, extending the theoretical analysis to more general distributions, and exploring principled approaches for defining and partitioning high-quality versus suboptimal data for various learning goals (e.g., task-specific vs. generalist pretraining).
