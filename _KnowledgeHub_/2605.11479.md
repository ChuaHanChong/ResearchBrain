# Offline Policy Evaluation for Manipulation Policies via Discounted Liveness Formulation

> [!summary] Summary
> A framework for offline policy evaluation, termed 'discounted liveness formulation with two-stage bootstrapping,' was developed to accurately assess robotic manipulation policies under sparse rewards, truncation bias, and non-monotonic task progression. This approach significantly improved success and composite metrics across various manipulation tasks by providing a more optimistic and nuanced understanding of policy capabilities.

## Problem

- Traditional policy evaluation methods struggle with sparse, binary rewards in real-world manipulation tasks, failing to capture intermediate progress.
- Finite-horizon robotic experiments lead to 'truncation bias,' where timed-out episodes are pessimistically labeled as failures, underestimating policy performance.
- Modern manipulation policies often exhibit non-monotonic task progression and recovery behaviors, which are poorly modeled by standard cumulative sparse reward formulations.

## Method

- Reformulates policy evaluation as a **discounted liveness problem**, where a state's value represents its semantic distance to task completion over a finite horizon, rather than cumulative reward.
- Introduces a ==two-stage bootstrapped evaluation framework==: Stage 1 computes ==anchor values== from successful trajectories, and Stage 2 iteratively propagates these optimistic values to similar states in truncated episodes.
- Utilizes a ==Bellman operator with a `min` function== to inherently accommodate non-monotonic task progression, allowing values to reflect temporary setbacks and subsequent recoveries.

## Results

- Achieved statistically significant improvements in **success** and **composite** evaluation metrics across three case studies: LIBERO Pick and Place (VLA model), Square Peg and Hole Insertion (diffusion policy), and hardware-based Cloth Folding (human demos).
- Qualitatively demonstrated the value function's ability to track non-monotonic task progression, showing value dips during setbacks and recoveries, and to predict impending failures based on visual cues.
- Ablation studies confirmed that the bootstrapping mechanism substantially increased success accuracy by correcting pessimistic values, and the method exhibited robustness to different image encoders and demonstrated sample efficiency with smaller datasets.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Defining policy value as the expected minimum target function value (liveness) over a horizon effectively captures semantic progress and recovery dynamics in sparse reward settings.
> - A two-stage bootstrapping mechanism can mitigate truncation bias by propagating success signals from observed successful trajectories to semantically similar states in otherwise truncated or 'failed' episodes.
> - The use of a pessimistic Bellman operator with a `min` function naturally models scenarios where task progress is not monotonic, allowing for temporary increases in value during setbacks if recovery is possible.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics is experiencing rapid advancements, particularly with the emergence of generalist policies powered by large-scale models and datasets. As these policies are deployed in increasingly complex real-world manipulation tasks, robust and reliable policy evaluation has become a critical challenge. Policy evaluation is fundamental for iteratively improving policies, assessing their readiness for deployment, and guiding their refinement.

Traditional reinforcement learning (RL) frameworks, such as Monte Carlo (MC) and Temporal Difference (TD) learning, are commonly used for policy evaluation. These methods aim to compute a value function that quantifies a policy's expected performance from a given state. However, the application of these classical methods to modern manipulation policies faces several challenges:
1. **Sparse Rewards:** Real-world manipulation tasks often provide only binary success/failure signals, lacking dense reward structures that are beneficial for conventional RL.
2. **Finite-Horizon Rollouts and Truncation Bias:** Practical robotic experiments are necessarily time-limited. Episodes often terminate due to manual time-outs rather than explicit task completion or definitive failure. This introduces "truncation bias," where timed-out episodes are pessimistically assumed as failures, leading to underestimation of policy performance. Standard value estimation methods, which often rely on infinite-horizon assumptions, are ill-suited for this.
3. **Non-Monotonic Task Progression and Recovery Behaviors:** Advanced manipulation policies frequently exhibit recovery behaviors, meaning they can experience setbacks (e.g., dropping an object) but still recover and complete the task. This leads to non-monotonic task progression within an episode, which cumulative sparse reward formulations inherently struggle to model accurately.

This work addresses these specific limitations by reformulating the policy evaluation problem within the context of manipulation, proposing a method designed to be robust to sparse rewards, truncation bias, and non-monotonic task progression. It aims to provide a more accurate and conservative assessment of a policy's ability to complete a task, thus contributing to the development and deployment of increasingly capable robotic systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust framework for *offline policy evaluation* for robotic manipulation policies, specifically targeting common challenges encountered in real-world scenarios. The core motivations stem from the inherent difficulties of applying standard policy evaluation methods to these contexts:

1. **Accurate Task Progression Measurement in Sparse Reward Settings:** In many manipulation tasks, rewards are sparse, typically a binary signal indicating task completion. Standard value functions, which accumulate discounted rewards, struggle to capture intermediate task progress or semantic closeness to goal completion when only sparse signals are available. The authors aim to define a value function that inherently reflects this semantic distance, allowing for evaluation beyond a simple binary outcome.
2. **Mitigating Truncation Bias from Finite-Horizon Rollouts:** Real-world robotic experiments are constrained by time, leading to episodes that are often manually terminated (timed out) rather than reaching a definitive success or failure state. This indistinguishability between policy failure and premature termination in offline datasets introduces a "truncation bias," where timed-out episodes are pessimistically labeled as failures. This leads to an underestimation of the policy's true capabilities. A key objective is to reduce this bias, enabling a more accurate assessment of policy performance even with incomplete data.
3. **Handling Non-Monotonic Task Progression and Recovery Behaviors:** Modern manipulation policies can exhibit sophisticated recovery strategies. For instance, a robot might temporarily drop an object but then re-grasp it and complete the task. Such non-monotonic progress challenges standard reward accumulation models that implicitly assume continuous forward progress. The research seeks a formulation that can naturally accommodate these fluctuations in task progress, where a state might momentarily move "further" from the goal but still be recoverable.

To achieve these objectives, the authors propose reformulating policy evaluation as a "liveness problem" within a Markov Decision Process (MDP) framework, aiming for a value function that represents the semantic distance to task completion rather than cumulative reward.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology reformulates offline policy evaluation as a discounted liveness problem and employs a two-stage bootstrapped evaluation framework to address sparse rewards, non-monotonic task progression, and truncation bias.

**A. Policy Evaluation as a Discounted Liveness Problem**

The core idea is to shift from evaluating cumulative sparse rewards to evaluating the "liveness" of a state, defined as its semantic distance to task completion.
1. **Liveness Problem Formulation:** The traditional MDP framework M = (S, A, P, R, γ) is modified by replacing the reward function R(·) with a "target function" `l(s)`. This function is intended to encode how far a state `s` is from a target set `L`, with `l(s) ≤ 0` for states `s ∈ L`.
2. **Value Function Definition:** The value `V_π(s)` of a state `s` under policy `π` is defined as the expected minimum target function value achieved over a finite horizon `N` steps:
 `V_π(s) = E_{ξ∼Ξ_{π,P}^s} [min_{k∈{0,1,...,N}} l(ξ(k))]`
 where `ξ` is a state trajectory from `s`. This means the value reflects the closest the policy gets to the target set during the rollout.
3. **Bellman Operator for Liveness:** Through dynamic programming and the application of Jensen's inequality, an inequality for `V_π(s)` is derived:
 `V_π(s) ≤ E_π [E_P [min {l(s), V_π(s')}] ]`
 This inequality implies that any fixed point of the corresponding equality defines a conservative (pessimistic) estimate of the true liveness value. The authors deliberately adopt this pessimistic estimate:
 `Ṽ_π(s) = E_π [E_P [min {l(s), Ṽ_π(s')}] ]`
4. **Practical Approximation with Discount Factor:** For offline policy evaluation, the expectations over `π` and `P` are approximated by empirical distributions from the dataset `D`. To induce a contraction mapping for stable learning and to incorporate the notion of "steps to task completion," a discount factor `γ ∈ (0, 1)` is introduced, leading to the discounted Bellman operator:
 `Ṽ_π(s) = (1 - γ) + γ min{l(s), Ṽ_π(s')}`
 The target function `l(s)` is initially defined by a binary sparse reward: `l(s) = -1` if `s` is a goal state (task completion) and `l(s) = 1` otherwise. This formulation relates the value `Ṽ_π(s)` to the number of steps `k` remaining to task completion by `k = log_γ((1 - Ṽ_π(s))/2)`. However, this initial `l(s)` only captures monotonic task progression.

**B. Refining the Target Function via Bootstrapping (Two-Stage Framework)**

To address non-monotonic task progression and truncation bias, a two-stage bootstrapping mechanism is introduced to refine the target function `l(s)`.

1. **Stage 1: Computing "Anchor" Values:**
 - The dataset `D` is divided into successful episodes `D'` (reaching a goal state) and timed-out episodes `D \ D'`.
 - For all states in `D'` that precede observed task completion, their values are computed using the Bellman operator from Eq. 7. These values are considered "well-defined" as they are grounded in observed success.
 - Let `V̄_π` be a value function that holds these well-defined values. For any state `s` not found in `D'` (i.e., not a descendant of a goal state in a successful episode), `V̄_π(s)` is set to `1` (the pessimistic default).
 - This `V̄_π` function acts as "anchors," providing initial, more optimistic value estimates for states associated with successful trajectories.

2. **Stage 2: Bootstrapping and Iterative Evaluation:**
 - The target function `l(s)` is bootstrapped using `V̄_π(s)`. This means that for any state `s`, its "immediate" target value is now `V̄_π(s)`.
 - The Bellman operator is then applied to the entire dataset `D` using this bootstrapped target function:
 `Ṽ_π(s) = (1 - γ) + γ min{V̄_π(s), Ṽ_π(s')}`
 - The `min` operator is crucial here: if a state `s` in a timed-out episode (`V̄_π(s) = 1`) has a successor `s'` whose value `Ṽ_π(s')` has been updated (e.g., because `s'` is similar to a state in a successful episode), then `min{V̄_π(s), Ṽ_π(s')}` will take the lower (more optimistic) value `Ṽ_π(s')`. This propagates success signals backward through seemingly failed trajectories, effectively overriding the pessimistic time-out assumption.

**C. Reduction of Truncation Bias:**
The bootstrapping mechanism directly addresses truncation bias. Episodes that would traditionally be labeled as failures due to time-outs can now have states assigned more optimistic values if those states are sufficiently similar to states observed in successful (or successfully recoverable) trajectories. This mechanism provides a more accurate representation of the policy's true capabilities, even when episodes are truncated.

**D. Implementation Details:**
Value functions are parameterized as neural networks. Inputs to these networks are concatenated latent embeddings of image observations (from models like SigLIP2) and proprioception states. Prioritized experience replay is used during training.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The method was evaluated across three distinct case studies: a LIBERO Pick and Place task (Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model), a Robomimic Square Peg and Hole Insertion task (diffusion policy), and a hardware-based Cloth Folding task (human demonstrations). Performance was compared against four baselines: Ours-NB (ablation without bootstrapping), TD(0), MC, and MC-D (Distributional Monte Carlo).

**A. Evaluation Metrics:**
- **Success Metric (↑):** For successful sub-episodes of length `N`, the percentage of frames where the estimated steps-to-goal is less than `N`. A high percentage indicates correct prediction of goal reachability within the actual duration.
- **Failure Metric (↑):** For timed-out episodes, the percentage of frames where the inferred steps-to-success exceeds a standard task horizon `N_s`. A high percentage indicates correct prediction of unreachability within a typical task horizon.
- **Composite Metric (↑):** The arithmetic mean of the success and failure metrics, balancing both aspects.
Standard classification metrics (e.g., precision, recall) were deemed unsuitable due to the ambiguity of binary labels in intermediate states with recovery behaviors or time-outs.

**B. Quantitative Results:**

- **Overall Performance (Full Dataset):**
 - **LIBERO-Spatial (Pick and Place):** The proposed method ("Ours") achieved the highest success (0.9796 ± 0.0039) and composite (0.9519 ± 0.0017) metrics, while maintaining a competitive failure metric (0.9242 ± 0.0037). Baselines (TD(0), MC, MC-D) generally performed worse on success and composite metrics, though some had slightly higher failure metrics (e.g., MC at 0.9993).
 - **Square Peg and Hole Insertion:** "Ours" again showed the best success (0.7278 ± 0.0242) and composite (0.8096 ± 0.0017) metrics, with a competitive failure metric (0.8914 ± 0.0233). Performance for all methods was generally lower in this task compared to LIBERO-Spatial, which the authors attributed to fewer visual cues and lack of trained recovery behaviors in the diffusion policy.
 - **Cloth Folding (Hardware):** "Ours" significantly outperformed all baselines on the success metric (0.6429 ± 0.0304 vs. 0.3038 ± 0.0120 for Ours-NB and lower for others) and composite metric (0.7828 ± 0.0164), while having a slightly lower failure metric (0.9227 ± 0.0039 compared to Ours-NB at 0.9713). This task, characterized by frequent human recovery behaviors and noisy labels, demonstrated the strong effectiveness of the bootstrapping mechanism.
- **Statistical Significance:** The proposed method showed statistically significant improvements over all baselines on both success and composite metrics across all case studies and dataset sizes (α = 0.05).

**C. Qualitative Observations:**

- **Non-Monotonic Progression (Fig. 1a - LIBERO):** The value function correctly decreased as the robot picked up the bowl, sharply increased when the bowl slipped (indicating a setback), and then decreased again as the policy recovered and completed the task. This demonstrates the method's ability to capture recovery behaviors.
- **Predictive Capability (Fig. 1b - Square):** The value function showed a high value (close to 1, indicating failure) even before a slip occurred, due to poor gripper alignment. This suggests the function learned to associate visual cues of impending failure.
- **"Unexpected" Failures/Recoveries (Fig. 4 - Square):** In cases where a poor grasp unexpectedly led to success or a good grasp led to a slip, the value function reacted to the event as it happened rather than preemptively predicting it, indicating a reliance on strong semantic cues.
- **Stagnant Progress (Fig. 1c - Cloth Folding):** During initial struggles to grasp the cloth, the value oscillated near zero, reflecting slow task progression. Once a successful grasp was established, the value dropped sharply towards -1.

**D. Ablation Studies:**

- **Bootstrapping (Ours vs. Ours-NB):** Ours-NB (without bootstrapping) consistently underperformed "Ours" on success and composite metrics, but often showed slightly better performance on the failure metric. This supports that bootstrapping increases success accuracy by being more optimistic, with a minor trade-off on failure prediction.
- **Dataset Size:** As dataset size increased, "Ours" improved on both success and failure metrics, and variance decreased. Notably, "Ours" with smaller datasets (e.g., 50 or 100 episodes) still maintained a significantly better success metric and competitive failure metric compared to baselines with full datasets, suggesting sample efficiency.
- **Embedding Space (Image Encoder):** Testing with SigLIP2, CLIP, and DINOv2 encoders for the cloth folding task showed comparable performance across all metrics. This indicates the method is largely robust to the choice of a high-capacity pre-trained image encoder, as long as task-relevant features are preserved.

**E. Discussion of Observations:**

- The consistent high success metric for "Ours" is attributed to the bootstrapping mechanism, which corrects pessimistic values in timed-out episodes by associating states with similar states in successful trajectories. This "optimism" is learned based on latent/pixel space similarity.
- The `min` operator helps the network learn non-monotonic task progression by allowing temporary increases in value during setbacks that are then reduced upon recovery.
- The slightly lower failure metric is a consequence of the bootstrapping mechanism, as some states in timed-out episodes, if visually similar to successful states, might be optimistically mislabeled. This effect is more pronounced in environments with fewer distinctive visual cues.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work makes significant contributions to the field of robotic policy evaluation by proposing a novel framework that directly addresses critical limitations of existing methods in real-world manipulation tasks.

**A. Addressing Core Challenges:**
- **Robustness to Sparse Rewards:** By reformulating policy evaluation as a liveness problem, the method moves beyond cumulative sparse rewards to capture semantic task progression more accurately. The value function directly reflects the "distance" to task completion, providing a richer signal than binary success/failure.
- **Mitigation of Truncation Bias:** The two-stage bootstrapping mechanism is a central innovation for handling finite-horizon rollouts and the resulting truncation bias. By propagating success signals backward from identified "anchor" states in successful episodes, it prevents the pessimistic mislabeling of recoverable time-outs, leading to a more realistic assessment of policy capabilities. This is particularly valuable for policies that exhibit recovery behaviors.
- **Modeling Non-Monotonic Task Progression:** The method inherently accommodates policies that exhibit setbacks and recoveries, a common characteristic in complex manipulation. The value function can increase during a setback and decrease upon recovery, providing a dynamic reflection of task progress.

**B. Practical Implications for Robotic Development:**
- **Improved Policy Assessment:** The ability to reliably evaluate policies under sparse rewards and with non-monotonic progression offers developers a more nuanced tool for understanding policy performance. This is crucial for iterating on policy design, debugging, and making informed decisions about deployment readiness.
- **Utility in Data-Constrained Settings:** The ablation studies indicate that the method remains effective even with smaller datasets, which is important for real-world robotics where data collection can be expensive and time-consuming.
- **Foundation for Future Work:** The liveness-based formulation and bootstrapping mechanism provide a new perspective for policy evaluation that can potentially be extended to other challenging areas in robotics and control.

**C. Limitations and Future Directions:**
The paper acknowledges several limitations:
- **Over-optimism from State Overlap:** The learned state similarity for bootstrapping can lead to over-optimistic value estimates for some timed-out states, especially if visual cues are ambiguous. Future work could explore structured boundaries for state similarity.
- **Dataset Dependency:** The performance is highly dependent on the quality and composition of the training dataset, and biases can emerge if the data distribution is imbalanced or unrepresentative.
- **Performance with Non-Recovery Policies:** The bootstrapping mechanism is less effective for policies that do not exhibit recovery behaviors, as there are fewer "optimistic" states to propagate.

Future research could focus on defining structured boundaries for state similarity to mitigate over-optimism, improving data efficiency, and evaluating the method across a wider range of visuomotor policy architectures.

In summary, this research presents a method for offline policy evaluation that offers a more robust and informative assessment of manipulation policies by specifically addressing key challenges in sparse reward, finite-horizon, and non-monotonic task environments.
