# Out-of-Distribution Recovery with Object-Centric Keypoint Inverse Policy for Visuomotor Imitation Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Object-Centric Recovery (OCR) augments any base visuomotor BC policy with a plug-in recovery policy: it fits a GMM over training-data object keypoints, follows its density gradient to plan a return trajectory, and executes it via a keypoint inverse policy, switching control back to the base policy once in-distribution. No extra data collection needed; improves OOD success by 77.7% over base policy across sim and real tasks.

## Problem

- Behavior cloning policies rely on large labeled demonstration coverage and fail unpredictably once objects enter spatially out-of-distribution regions not seen in training.
- Existing OOD fixes require costly online data aggregation from human experts or extra reinforcement-learning correction steps, adding deployment burden.
- Prior state-recovery work like Back to the Manifold recovers robot end-effector states, not task-relevant object states, missing the object-centric failure mode.

## Method

- **Object Manifold Estimation** fits a per-keypoint ==Gaussian Mixture Model== over object keypoint positions extracted from training demonstrations via pose estimation.
- **Object-Recovery Vectors** compute the ==GMM density gradient== at test-time keypoints, rescaled by a ==negative-exponential function== so recovery speeds up when far from the manifold.
- **Keypoint Inverse Policy**, trained on ==object-frame-zeroed== keypoint-action sequences from the same base dataset, converts planned recovery keypoint trajectories into robot actions, joined to the base policy via a ==density-activated switch==.

## Results

- Joint OCR policy reached **93%** success on Push-T and **80%** on Robomimic Square in OOD regions, versus **10%** and **0%** for the base policy alone.
- Real Franka bottle-pick task: OCR achieved **70%** OOD success versus **0%** for base policy, even exceeding the base policy's own **60%** in-distribution rate.
- Continual learning with OCR-collected OOD demonstrations raised augmented base policy OOD success to **80%** (Push-T) and **76%** (Square) while preserving or improving ID performance (**90%**->**97%** on Push-T).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Recovery can be squeezed entirely from existing training data's object-keypoint manifold gradient, avoiding costly new online data collection for OOD robustness.
> - Zeroing sequences to the object's initial pose frame lets an inverse policy generalize keypoint-to-action mappings to any object pose, in- or out-of-distribution, from limited demonstrations.
> - A recovery policy that nudges objects toward denser training regions can double as an autonomous data-collection engine for lifelong policy improvement without degrading in-distribution performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robot learning, particularly through Imitation Learning (IL), has demonstrated capabilities in deploying robotic systems for various tasks. Within IL, Behavior Cloning (BC) is a widely adopted approach that involves learning control policies directly from expert demonstrations. BC offers a relatively straightforward method for policy acquisition, avoiding the need for explicit environmental modeling. However, a known limitation of BC is its susceptibility to the covariate shift problem. This issue arises when the robot encounters states or observations that deviate significantly from the training data distribution, leading to unpredictable or suboptimal behavior in out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios.

Addressing OOD challenges in BC typically necessitates one of two general strategies: increasing data coverage or implementing corrective mechanisms. Data aggregation methods, such as DAgger, involve online interaction to gather additional expert data in encountered OOD states, which can be laborious and time-consuming. Alternatively, corrective mechanisms might involve human intervention or the use of reinforcement learning (RL) agents, which introduce further complexities or computational overhead. The offline RL community also addresses distribution shifts, often by penalizing actions far from the data or by attempting to recover to training data regions, but still grapples with extrapolation errors from limited data. Approaches combining BC with RL (BC+RL) also represent a strategy for improving robustness beyond the initial dataset.

This work specifically addresses the OOD problem in visuomotor policy learning by proposing a recovery framework that aims to leverage existing training data more effectively, without requiring additional online data collection or extensive human effort. It differentiates itself from prior work that focused on robot state recovery (e.g., "Back to the Manifold") by adopting an object-centric perspective. This focus on task-relevant object states is proposed as a more generalizable strategy for manipulation tasks. The use of object-centric representations, particularly keypoints derived from pose estimation, aligns with other recent research demonstrating the benefits of such representations for policy learning and manipulation. The framework also aims to be agnostic to the specific base BC policy used, allowing for broad applicability. By focusing on returning OOD object states to the training distribution, this research attempts to bridge the gap between the strong in-distribution performance of BC and the need for robust generalization in real-world, dynamic environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the robustness of visuomotor policies in out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios without requiring additional data collection. Specifically, the authors aim to develop an object-centric recovery (OCR) framework that enables a robot to successfully complete tasks even when task-relevant objects are initialized or move into unfamiliar spatial states.

The motivation for this objective stems from several challenges inherent in current visuomotor policy learning, particularly those based on Behavior Cloning (BC):

1. **Covariate Shift Problem**: BC methods are known to suffer from covariate shift. They are highly dependent on the coverage of their training data. When a robot encounters an observation (visual or state) that is significantly different from those seen during training—an OOD scenario—the learned policy may produce unreliable or incorrect actions, leading to task failure.
2. **Laborious Data Collection**: Addressing OOD scenarios in traditional BC often involves collecting more labeled data in the new, unfamiliar states. This process, typically through teleoperation or kinesthetic teaching, is labor-intensive, time-consuming, and an added burden on system deployment. The authors are motivated to circumvent this by extracting maximum utility from the existing training dataset.
3. **Limitations of Existing Recovery Methods**: While previous methods have explored recovery from OOD states, many focus on the robot's own state rather than task-relevant objects. Approaches like data aggregation or reinforcement learning for recovery also introduce additional online interaction or computational demands. The authors seek a simpler, more integrated add-on mechanism that works with an existing base BC policy.
4. **Enhancing Real-World Robustness**: For robotic systems to be reliably deployed in real-world environments, they must be capable of handling unexpected variations and initial conditions. The current fragility of BC policies in OOD settings limits their practical utility. The research is motivated by the need to develop a framework that improves task success rates under such conditions, thus making visuomotor policies more robust.
5. **Agnosticism and Flexibility**: The researchers aim to develop a recovery framework that can be seamlessly integrated with any existing visuomotor BC policy, regardless of its specific architecture or learning approach. This agnosticism would enhance the framework's adaptability and utility across different imitation learning implementations and future advancements.
6. **Potential for Continual Learning**: A secondary motivation is to explore whether the proposed recovery mechanism can autonomously generate useful data for continual learning. By effectively guiding the system back to in-distribution regions from OOD states, the framework could potentially collect new demonstrations that expand the training data coverage, thereby incrementally improving the base policy's generalization capabilities over time.

In essence, the core motivation is to achieve "out-of-distribution recovery with object-centric control" by inferring a recovery policy from the gradient of the object keypoint manifold in the original training data, allowing BC policies to maintain high performance in a broader range of real-world conditions.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Object-Centric Recovery (OCR) framework is designed as an add-on to augment a base visuomotor policy trained via Behavior Cloning (BC). The framework's core idea is to identify when a task-relevant object is in an out-of-distribution (OOD) state and then execute recovery actions to guide it back into the training data manifold, where the base policy can reliably take over. The method comprises several integrated components.

**A. Base BC Policy**
The framework assumes the existence of a generic visuomotor policy, $\pi_b$, that has been trained using standard behavior cloning objectives on a dataset $D_b = \{(o_t^{(i)}, a_t^{(i)}, p_t^{(i)})\}$ consisting of observations, robot actions, and robot proprioception. This base policy is expected to perform well within its training data distribution (ID).

**B. Object-Centric Recovery Policy Components**

1. **Keypoint Generation**: To represent object poses in a manner suitable for distribution modeling, the framework employs artificial object keypoints. For each observation frame $o_t^{(i)}$ in the training dataset, an off-the-shelf 6D object pose estimator (e.g., FoundationPose) is used to determine the object's pose, $T_{obj,t}^{(i)}$. A predefined set of arbitrary 3D keypoints is then transformed by this pose into their 3D world coordinates, $\rho_t^{(i)} = \{\rho_{k,t}^{(i)}\}_{k=1}^n$. These transformed keypoints, along with object poses, robot actions, and proprioception, form a new dataset $D_{rec}$ used specifically for the recovery mechanism.

2. **Object Manifold Estimation**: The distribution of task-relevant object keypoints within the training dataset is modeled using Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs). For each individual keypoint $k$, a GMM is fitted to its 3D positions across all time steps and demonstrations in $D_{rec}$. The GMM $p(\rho_{k,t}^{(i)}|\theta_k) = \sum_{m=1}^M \lambda_{k,m} \mathcal{N}(\rho_{k,t}^{(i)}|\mu_{k,m}, \Sigma_{k,m})$ is learned using Expectation-Maximization, where $\theta_k$ represents the parameters (mixing coefficients $\lambda_{k,m}$, means $\mu_{k,m}$, and covariances $\Sigma_{k,m}$) for $M$ Gaussian components. The collection of these GMM parameters, $\Theta = \{\theta_k\}_{k=1}^n$, collectively approximates the probability distribution of the object keypoints within the training manifold.

3. **Keypoint Inverse Policy ($\pi_{inv}$)**: A specialized policy, $\pi_{inv}$, is trained to translate a sequence of desired object keypoint trajectories and the robot's current proprioception into a sequence of robot actions. This policy is trained on the same $D_{rec}$ dataset. To mitigate distribution shift issues during training and allow for more generalized manipulation, a "zero-out" strategy is employed. Instead of learning directly from raw keypoint trajectories, the input keypoint sequences and corresponding action sequences are transformed to be relative to the initial object pose of each sequence, i.e., using $(T_{obj,j}^{(i)})^{-1}$. This normalization makes the policy learn to execute object motions from an "identity" frame, effectively compressing the input domain and enhancing data efficiency. At test time, a desired trajectory in the current object frame is provided, and the policy outputs robot actions in that frame, which are then transformed back to the robot's frame for execution.

4. **Object-Recovery Vectors**: At inference time, the current keypoints $\rho_{test}$ of the object are obtained. For each keypoint $k$, its probability density $\eta_{test,k} = p(\rho_{test,k}|\theta_k)$ and the gradient $\delta_{test,k} = \nabla p(\rho_{test,k}|\theta_k)$ with respect to the GMM are computed. To ensure that recovery speed decreases as the object approaches higher density regions (i.e., closer to ID), the magnitude of the gradient is modified by a monotonically decreasing negative exponential function, $q(x) = e^{\varphi - x / \eta}$, resulting in $\delta_{mod,k}$. The final mean recovery gradient $\delta_{rec}$ and mean density $\eta_{rec}$ are calculated by averaging across all keypoints.

5. **Recovery Keypoint Planner**: The object recovery vector $\delta_{rec}$ is used to generate a naive recovery keypoint trajectory. To ensure feasibility and account for the robot's ability to reach and manipulate the object, a heuristic planner is introduced. This planner incorporates a "delay function," $df(d_{pos})$, which determines how many initial steps of the object trajectory should be "delayed" before the object starts moving. The delay is calculated based on the Euclidean distance $d_{pos}$ between the robot end-effector and the object, within predefined minimum and maximum manipulation distances ($d_{min}$, $d_{max}$). This function provides the robot with sufficient time to approach the object before initiating its movement. The resulting planned trajectory $\zeta_{rec}^L$ guides the object towards higher density regions of the manifold.

6. **Final Policy (Joint Policy)**: The base policy $\pi_b$ and the recovery policy $\pi_{inv}$ are combined using a density-activated switch. The mean keypoint density $\eta_{rec}$ acts as a confidence measure. If $\eta_{rec}$ falls below a tunable threshold $\epsilon_{rec}$ (indicating an OOD state), the recovery pipeline is activated, and $\pi_{inv}$ generates actions based on the planned recovery trajectory $\zeta_{rec}^L$. Otherwise, if $\eta_{rec} \ge \epsilon_{rec}$ (indicating an ID state), the base policy $\pi_b$ takes control and executes actions based on visual observations and proprioception. This seamless switching mechanism ensures that the system either recovers from OOD states or efficiently executes the task in ID states.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The Object-Centric Recovery (OCR) framework was systematically evaluated across three distinct task scenarios: a simulated 2D non-prehensile task (Push-T), a simulated 3D prehensile task (Robomimic Square), and a real-robot prehensile task (Bottle Pick and Place). For each task, specific in-distribution (ID) and out-of-distribution (OOD) regions were defined based on where the initial object poses were located during training versus testing. The baseline for comparison was a vision-input U-Net-based diffusion policy, which also served as the base BC policy within the OCR framework.

**A. Performance in Out-of-Distribution Scenarios**

The experimental results consistently demonstrated the effectiveness of the OCR framework in improving task success rates in OOD scenarios:

- **Push-T Task (Simulated, Non-Prehensile)**:
 - The base policy achieved a 10% success rate in OOD regions, primarily due to accidental interactions.
 - The OCR joint policy achieved a 93% task success rate in OOD regions. This indicates a substantial improvement attributed to the recovery actions guiding the T-shaped block back into the ID region, where the base policy successfully completed the task.

- **Robomimic Square Task (Simulated, Prehensile)**:
 - The base policy exhibited a 0% success rate in OOD scenarios, as it consistently failed to grasp the object when initialized outside its training distribution.
 - The OCR joint policy achieved an 80% task success rate in OOD regions. The recovery mechanism enabled the robot to grasp the object in OOD, manipulate it for recovery, and then hand over control to the base policy within the ID region.

- **Bottle Pick and Place Task (Real Robot, Prehensile)**:
 - The base policy, similar to the Square task, showed a 0% success rate in OOD scenarios, failing to interact with the bottle.
 - The OCR joint policy achieved a 70% task success rate in OOD regions.
 - Notably, the OCR joint policy's 70% OOD success rate was higher than the base policy's 60% ID success rate for this task. The authors hypothesized this might be due to OCR's tendency to consistently move objects towards regions of high training density, which could complement the base policy even in some ID cases.

Overall, across the three evaluated tasks, the OCR framework achieved an average OOD success rate of 81.0%, representing an improvement of 77.7% over the base policy's OOD performance.

**B. Continual Learning Capacity**

The research also investigated OCR's capacity to autonomously collect demonstrations for continual learning, with the goal of improving the base policy's OOD performance without degrading its ID performance.

- **Methodology**: OCR was used to autonomously collect augmented demonstration data ($D_{aug}$) in OOD regions. The environment was initialized OOD, and the OCR joint policy rolled out actions until the object reached the ID threshold. These collected demonstrations were then appended to the original training dataset ($D_b$), and the base policy was incrementally retrained.

- **Results**:
 - **Push-T Task**: The augmented base policy achieved an 80% task completion rate in the original OOD regions (up from 10% for the original base policy). Its ID performance also improved from 90% to 97%.
 - **Square Task**: The augmented base policy achieved a 76% task completion rate in the original OOD regions (up from 0% for the original base policy). Its ID performance remained at 87%.

These results indicate that the OCR framework can effectively collect valuable OOD data for incremental training. The augmented base policies showed significant improvements in OOD performance while maintaining or even enhancing their performance in the original ID scenarios. The authors suggested that OCR-generated demonstrations contribute actions that converge the object to the base policy's operational space, acting as a complement rather than a replacement.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Object-Centric Recovery (OCR) framework represents a contribution to improving the robustness of visuomotor policies, particularly those derived from Behavior Cloning (BC), in real-world applications. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Enhanced Robustness in Out-of-Distribution Scenarios**: The primary impact is the significant improvement in task success rates when task-relevant objects are in OOD states. By providing a mechanism to guide objects back to familiar regions, the framework mitigates the inherent fragility of BC policies to covariate shift, making robotic systems more reliable in varied and unpredictable environments. The observed average improvement of 77.7% in OOD success rates across diverse tasks highlights this capability.

2. **Reduced Data Collection Burden**: A key aspect of the framework is its ability to recover from OOD situations without requiring additional online data collection or expert demonstrations for the OOD regions. This addresses a significant practical bottleneck in robot learning, reducing the labor and cost associated with deploying and maintaining robust robotic systems. The recovery policy is learned solely from the original training data used for the base policy.

3. **Agnosticism to Base Policy**: The OCR framework is designed as an add-on that is agnostic to the specific architecture or method of the base visuomotor BC policy. This flexibility allows it to be integrated with various existing and future imitation learning implementations, broadening its applicability and potentially accelerating the development of robust robotic control.

4. **Enabling Continual Learning**: The demonstrated capacity of OCR to autonomously collect valuable demonstrations in OOD regions for incremental training has implications for continual learning. This self-improvement loop allows the base policy to expand its operational range over time without human intervention, fostering long-term adaptability and reducing the need for periodic manual retraining. This could lead to more autonomous robot systems that learn and adapt throughout their operational lifespan.

5. **Object-Centric Generalization**: By focusing on recovering task-relevant objects rather than general robot states, the approach offers a potentially more effective and generalizable strategy for object manipulation tasks. This object-centric perspective aligns with the way humans often reason about and interact with their environment, which could lead to more intuitive and robust robot behaviors.

Despite its benefits, the authors acknowledge limitations that also suggest avenues for future impact: the reliance on explicit object poses currently restricts the framework to rigid, non-deformable objects. Future work extending OCR to more flexible scene representations (e.g., articulated or deformable objects) could further expand its applicability. Additionally, the use of state-based distribution manifold estimation as a proxy for the true visual distribution and potential inconsistencies in 3D keypoint representation are areas for refinement. Addressing these limitations could unlock even broader utility and higher accuracy, further cementing the framework's role in advancing visuomotor policy robustness for real-world scenarios.
