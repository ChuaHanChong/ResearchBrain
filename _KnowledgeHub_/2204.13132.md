# HRDA: Context-Aware High-Resolution Domain-Adaptive Semantic Segmentation

> [!summary] Summary
> HRDA, developed by researchers from ETH Zurich and MPI for Informatics, presents a multi-resolution framework for unsupervised domain adaptation in semantic segmentation that efficiently integrates high-resolution details and low-resolution contextual information. This approach achieved new state-of-the-art performance on GTA→Cityscapes and Synthia→Cityscapes benchmarks, with improvements of 5.5 mIoU and 4.9 mIoU respectively.

## Problem

- Pixel-wise annotations for semantic segmentation are exceptionally time-consuming and expensive to acquire, necessitating methods like Unsupervised Domain Adaptation (UDA).
- UDA methods typically consume substantial GPU memory, leading to downscaling input images which results in loss of fine details, poor recognition of small objects, and inaccurate boundaries.
- Training with high-resolution crops helps details but restricts the learning of long-range context, which is crucial and often domain-invariant for effective UDA.

## Method

- A multi-resolution framework that utilizes a large ==low-resolution (LR) context crop== and a smaller ==high-resolution (HR) detail crop==, sharing the same network weights for efficiency.
- A learned ==scale attention== mechanism that dynamically fuses predictions from LR context and HR detail crops, indicating the trustworthiness of each scale for different regions.
- High-quality pseudo-labels for the target domain are generated using an ==overlapping sliding window inference== strategy on HR context crops, ensuring robust and detailed self-training.

## Results

- Achieved new state-of-the-art performance on UDA benchmarks: **73.8 mIoU** on GTA→Cityscapes (a **5.5 mIoU** gain) and **65.8 mIoU** on Synthia→Cityscapes (a **4.9 mIoU** gain), with improvements across most semantic classes, particularly small objects.
- Demonstrated general applicability by improving various established UDA methods (e.g., Entropy Minimization, Adversarial Training, DACS) by at least **+2.4 mIoU** when integrated, indicating broad compatibility and modularity.
- Ablation studies confirmed the learned scale attention as the most critical component, contributing **+3.0 mIoU**, while robust pseudo-label generation via overlapping sliding windows (**+0.9 mIoU**) and detail crop supervision (**+1.4 mIoU**) also provided substantial gains.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Different object scales benefit from specific input resolutions during domain adaptation; high-resolution inputs are essential for small objects and fine details, while low-resolution inputs are more effective for adapting large 'stuff' regions by reducing domain-specific texture interference.
> - Long-range contextual information plays a more critical role in UDA than in supervised learning, as it often contains more domain-robust clues, particularly for large objects and scene layout.
> - Dynamically combining multi-resolution information via learned scale attention is more effective than fixed fusion or single-resolution training, enabling adaptive blending of detail and context for superior UDA performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

The field of computer vision frequently utilizes neural networks, but their training often necessitates extensive annotated datasets. For tasks such as semantic segmentation, this annotation process is labor-intensive, requiring pixel-wise labels for entire images, which can take over an hour per image. To mitigate this annotation burden, various methods have emerged, including weakly-supervised learning, semi-supervised learning, and unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA). This work specifically focuses on UDA.

UDA for semantic segmentation aims to adapt a model trained on a source domain (e.g., synthetic data with readily available labels) to a target domain (e.g., real-world data without target annotations). This is crucial because neural networks are susceptible to performance degradation when encountering domain shifts between training and deployment environments.

A significant challenge in UDA for semantic segmentation stems from its computational demands. UDA methods typically consume more GPU memory than standard supervised learning approaches due to the involvement of multiple domains, additional networks (e.g., teacher models, domain discriminators), and supplementary loss functions. Consequently, many prior UDA methods for semantic segmentation commonly process downscaled images (e.g., half the full resolution for Cityscapes) to manage GPU memory constraints. This practice introduces a performance gap compared to supervised methods that often operate on full-resolution images.

The use of low-resolution inputs presents limitations, specifically in recognizing small objects and preserving fine segmentation details, such as distant traffic lights or pedestrian limbs. While training with random crops of high-resolution images can address these detail-oriented issues, it restricts the model's ability to learn long-range context dependencies. Long-range context and scene layout information are often considered domain-robust (e.g., a rider on a bicycle, a sidewalk alongside a road) and are therefore valuable for UDA. Conversely, high-resolution inputs can be disadvantageous for adapting large "stuff" regions, such as sidewalks, as they may contain overly detailed, domain-specific textures that hinder effective domain adaptation. Low-resolution inputs, in such cases, might "hide" these distracting details while still providing sufficient information for recognizing large regions across domains.

This research, HRDA, positions itself within this landscape by addressing the trade-off between resolution and contextual information in UDA for semantic segmentation. It proposes a multi-resolution training approach designed to leverage the advantages of both high-resolution detail and low-resolution context while maintaining manageable GPU memory usage, thereby aiming to narrow the performance gap between UDA and supervised learning.

### 2. Key objectives and motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA) framework for semantic segmentation that can effectively utilize high-resolution image details and long-range contextual information, while simultaneously addressing the GPU memory constraints inherent in UDA training.

The motivation for pursuing this objective is multifaceted:

1. **High Cost of Pixel-wise Annotations:** Semantic segmentation requires precise pixel-level labels, which are exceptionally time-consuming and expensive to acquire for real-world datasets. This expense necessitates methods like UDA that can reduce or eliminate the need for target domain annotations.
2. **GPU Memory Limitations in UDA:** Unsupervised domain adaptation methods typically demand substantial GPU memory. This is due to the need to process images from both source and target domains, often involving additional network components (e.g., teacher models, domain discriminators) and complex loss functions. Current UDA semantic segmentation approaches predominantly mitigate this by downscaling input images, which is a key divergence from supervised training methodologies.
3. **Deficiencies of Low-Resolution Inputs:** Relying solely on downscaled images for UDA results in several performance limitations:
 - **Loss of Fine Details:** Small objects (e.g., distant traffic lights, poles) are often poorly recognized or entirely missed.
 - **Inaccurate Boundaries:** Fine segmentation details (e.g., the precise outline of a pedestrian, thin structures) are difficult to preserve, leading to coarser predictions.
4. **Limitations of High-Resolution Crop-based Training:** While training with random high-resolution crops can address the detail preservation issue, it introduces another drawback:
 - **Limited Long-Range Context:** Cropping restricts the receptive field, preventing the model from learning extensive long-range context dependencies. This is problematic for UDA because long-range context (e.g., scene layout, relationships between large objects) is often more domain-robust than local textures, making it valuable for adapting across domains.
5. **Detrimental Effects of High-Resolution for "Stuff" Regions:** For large, amorphous regions like roads or sidewalks (often termed "stuff" classes), high-resolution inputs can sometimes be counterproductive in UDA. These regions at high resolution may contain overly detailed, domain-specific textures that are not generalizable across domains, causing the model to overfit to source domain specifics rather than learning domain-invariant features. Low-resolution inputs can "hide" these distracting details, facilitating adaptation for such regions.
6. **Need for a Balanced Approach:** The motivation arises from the need to combine the advantages of high-resolution processing (for fine details and small objects) and low-resolution processing (for broad context and large "stuff" regions), while circumventing the high memory costs of full high-resolution inputs and the context limitations of isolated high-resolution crops. This suggests a multi-resolution strategy that can intelligently select or fuse information from different scales based on the content.

By addressing these issues, the HRDA framework aims to enable more effective and robust domain adaptation for semantic segmentation, improving performance across various object scales and scene types without prohibitive computational resource requirements.

### 3. Methodology and approach

HRDA introduces a multi-resolution framework for UDA semantic segmentation designed to integrate fine details from high-resolution (HR) inputs and long-range contextual information from low-resolution (LR) inputs, while maintaining GPU memory efficiency. This is achieved through a combination of tailored crop strategies, a learned scale attention mechanism, and robust pseudo-label generation.

#### 3.1. Context and Detail Crop

The core of HRDA's memory-efficient approach lies in its use of two distinct crops: a large LR context crop and a small HR detail crop.

- **Rationale for Two Crops:**
 - Directly training with full HR images for UDA is often infeasible due to the significant GPU memory overhead from multiple domains, additional networks, and losses.
 - Previous UDA methods typically rely on LR inputs, which compromise the recognition of small objects and fine details.
 - Random HR cropping helps with details but limits long-range context, which is crucial and often domain-invariant for UDA.
 - For large "stuff" regions, LR inputs can be advantageous by abstracting away domain-specific HR textures that might hinder adaptation.
- **Crop Generation:**
 - **LR Context Crop (`x_c`):** This crop is generated by first cropping a region (`x_c,HR`) from the original full HR image (`x_HR`) and then bilinearly downsampling it by a factor `s` (default `s=2`). The bounding box for this crop (`b_c`) is randomly sampled from the image dimensions, ensuring its coordinates are divisible by `k = s * o` (where `o` is the network's output stride) to facilitate later alignment. The purpose of the context crop is to capture broad scene layout and adapt large objects.
 - **HR Detail Crop (`x_d`):** This crop is randomly sampled *from within the region of the LR context crop* (`x_c,HR`), specifically before its downsampling. It is kept at high resolution. Its bounding box (`b_d`) is also sampled with divisibility by `k`. This nesting is essential for fusing predictions. The detail crop focuses on fine segmentation details and adapting small objects.
- **Dimensions:** By default, both crop dimensions are set to `h_c = w_c = h_d = w_d = 512`. With a context downscale factor `s=2`, the context crop effectively covers four times more spatial content than the detail crop at half the resolution.
- **Shared Weights:** The feature encoder (`f_E`) and semantic decoder (`f_S`) are shared between both LR and HR inputs. This design choice contributes to memory efficiency and enhances the network's robustness across different resolutions.

#### 3.2. Multi-Resolution Fusion

To optimally combine the strengths of both crops, HRDA employs a learned scale attention mechanism.

- **Problem:** LR context is good for large objects, while HR detail is good for small objects. A fixed fusion might be suboptimal.
- **Solution: Learned Scale Attention:** A separate scale attention decoder (`f_A`) takes features from the LR context crop to predict an attention map (`a_c`). This map, passed through a sigmoid function (`σ`), indicates the trustworthiness of LR vs. HR predictions for each region (1 for HR focus, 0 for LR focus). The attention is derived from the context crop due to its broader scene understanding.
- **Fusion Process:**
 - The detail prediction (`ŷ_d`) is aligned with the context prediction (`ŷ_c`) by zero-padding it to the size of the upsampled context crop.
 - The attention map (`a_c`) is masked (`a'_c`) such that it is 0 outside the detail crop's region (where no HR detail prediction is available).
 - The final fused prediction (`ŷ_c,F`) is an attention-weighted sum of the (upsampled) LR context prediction and the (aligned) HR detail prediction.
- **Loss Functions:**
 - The supervised source loss (`L_S_HRDA`) and unsupervised target loss (`L_T_HRDA`) are modified to supervise both the fused multi-scale prediction and, additionally, the detail crop prediction. The detail crop loss weight (`λ_d`) is empirically set to 0.1. This additional supervision helps learn robust HR features.
- **Benefit:** The learned attention allows the model to adapt objects at their most suitable scale, for example, prioritizing HR for small objects and LR for large, stuff-like regions. This helps prevent overfitting to domain-specific textures at high resolution for large objects while ensuring detail preservation for small ones.

#### 3.3. Pseudo-Label Generation with Overlapping Sliding Window

For self-training on the target domain, HRDA requires high-quality pseudo-labels.

- **HR Prediction for Context Crop:** Since the multi-resolution fusion for pseudo-label generation requires an HR prediction (`ŷ_T_c,HR`) for the *entire* context crop (not just the detail crop region), and direct full HR processing during training is memory-intensive, an overlapping sliding window mechanism is used.
- **Sliding Window Inference:** During pseudo-label generation, the teacher network (`g_ϕ`) processes the HR context crop (`x_T_c,HR`) by sliding windows of size `h_d × w_d` (detail crop size) with an overlap. This generates multiple overlapping HR predictions, which are then averaged to enhance robustness. This process is efficient as windows can be processed in parallel.
- **Full Image Inference:** For model validation or deployment, the entire image (`x_HR`) is segmented using an overlapping sliding window approach, where each window corresponds to the size of the `sh_c × sw_c` context crop, and the HRDA prediction within each window is generated as described above.

**Implementation Details (from Section 5.1):**

- **Datasets:** Target: Cityscapes (2048x1024). Source: GTA (2560x1440) and Synthia (2560x1520), resized to maintain source-target scale ratios.
- **Network Architecture:** Uses DAFormer as the base, consisting of an MiT-B5 encoder and a context-aware feature fusion decoder. The scale attention decoder is a lightweight SegFormer MLP. For evaluating other UDA methods, a ResNet101 backbone with a DeepLabV2 decoder is used.
- **Training Parameters:** Follows DAFormer's self-training strategy with AdamW optimizer, linear learning rate warmup, batch size 2, and DACS data augmentation. For adversarial training, SGD is used. Default crop sizes are 512x512 with context downscale `s=2`, and detail loss weight `λ_d=0.1`. Experiments were conducted on a Titan RTX GPU.

### 4. Main findings and results

The HRDA framework demonstrated notable advancements in unsupervised domain adaptation for semantic segmentation, as evidenced by its performance metrics and ablation studies.

#### 4.1. Comparison with State-of-the-Art UDA Methods

HRDA established a new state-of-the-art performance on benchmark datasets:
- **GTA→Cityscapes:** Achieved 73.8 mIoU, representing a 5.5 mIoU improvement over the previous best method (DAFormer).
- **Synthia→Cityscapes:** Achieved 65.8 mIoU, marking a 4.9 mIoU improvement over the previous best method (DAFormer).
The improvements were observed across nearly all semantic classes for both datasets. Particularly high gains were recorded for classes requiring fine segmentation details, such as "pole," "traffic light," "traffic sign," "person," "rider," "motorbike," and "bike." Larger classes like "truck," "bus," and "train" also showed benefit. Qualitative examples supported these quantitative findings, illustrating better segmentation of small objects and clearer boundaries.

#### 4.2. HRDA for Different UDA Methods

HRDA exhibited general applicability by consistently improving the performance of various established UDA methods when integrated. Across three additional representative UDA methods (Entropy Minimization, Adversarial Training, and DACS), HRDA yielded performance gains of at least +2.4 mIoU. The largest improvements were observed with self-training methods (e.g., DACS and DAFormer), indicating that HRDA's high-quality pseudo-labels positively reinforce the UDA process. The framework was also shown to be compatible with different network architectures, including DeepLabV2 and DAFormer.

#### 4.3. Influence of Resolution and Crop Size on UDA

An analysis of resolution and crop size revealed their impact on UDA performance:
- **Resolution and Crop Size:** Both increased resolution and larger crop sizes generally enhance performance for UDA and supervised learning.
- **Importance of Context for UDA:** A larger crop size proved to be more critical for UDA than for supervised learning. For instance, a four-fold smaller LR crop reduced UDA performance by 39% compared to a 14% reduction for supervised training. This suggests that long-range context clues, often being more domain-invariant, are crucial for effective UDA, particularly for challenging classes like "wall," "fence," "truck," "bus," and "train."
- **Resolution-Specific Adaptation:** Higher input resolution improved UDA performance for small objects (e.g., "pole," "traffic light," "traffic sign," "person," "motorbike," "bicycle"). Conversely, some large "stuff" classes (e.g., "road," "sidewalk," "terrain") experienced decreased performance at higher resolutions, supporting the hypothesis that large objects are more effectively adapted at LR due to fewer confounding domain-specific textures, while small objects benefit from HR.

#### 4.4. Combining Crops from Multiple Resolutions with HRDA

- **Multi-Resolution Advantage:** Training with multiple resolutions within HRDA consistently outperformed both LR-only and HR-only training by +3.4 mIoU, indicating the efficacy of the multi-resolution fusion with learned scale attention.
- **Context Crop Size Impact:** Increasing the LR context crop size while keeping the HR detail crop fixed further improved performance by +5.3 mIoU, demonstrating the benefit of combining large LR context with small HR detail crops.
- **Detail Crop Size Impact:** Even with a significantly smaller HR detail crop (HR_0.25) combined with a large LR context crop, HRDA surpassed training with only the context crop by +2.1 mIoU. This illustrates that the multi-resolution fusion effectively leverages the detail crop's strengths while compensating for its limited context. Further increasing the detail crop size also yielded additional gains.
- **Cruciality of Real HR:** Using a bilinearly upsampled LR crop as a "detail crop" improved performance over an LR detail crop, but still lagged behind a genuine HR detail crop by -1.9 mIoU, confirming that the performance gains stem from both increased context and authentic high-resolution details.
- **Comparison with Naive HR:** HRDA outperformed a naive large HR crop (HR_0.75) with comparable GPU memory footprint by +3.8 mIoU. Even when HRDA's crop sizes were reduced to match the memory footprint of naive HR_0.75, HRDA still achieved a +1.3 mIoU gain with 40% less GPU memory, highlighting its efficiency and the benefits of its multi-resolution fusion.

#### 4.5. HRDA Component Ablations

- **Learned Scale Attention:** This was identified as the most critical component, providing a +3.0 mIoU improvement over a naive average fusion of multi-scale predictions. This underscores the importance of dynamically learning which scale is optimal for different image regions.
- **Overlapping Sliding Window (Pseudo-Label Generation):** Generating pseudo-labels with robust context views through overlapping slide detail crops contributed an additional +0.9 mIoU.
- **Detail Crop Supervision:** Providing additional supervision for the detail crop during training yielded a further +1.4 mIoU gain.

#### 4.6. Qualitative Analysis

Visual examples confirmed that LR predictions were more effective for large objects (e.g., bus, sidewalk), whereas HR predictions were superior for small objects and fine details. The learned scale attention mechanism adapted its focus accordingly, concentrating on LR for large objects and HR for small objects and segmentation borders, thus effectively combining their respective strengths.

#### 4.7. Training and Inference Time (from Supplementary Material)

- **Training Time:** HRDA training required 32 hours on a Titan RTX GPU.
- **Inference Time:** Standard HRDA inference operated at 0.8 images/second, consuming 3.3 TFLOPs and 9.4 GB of GPU memory.
- **Efficient Variants:** For applications prioritizing faster inference, non-overlapping sliding window inference achieved 2.2 images/second with 1.8 TFLOPs and 3.2 GB GPU memory, while still maintaining 73.4 mIoU. Additionally, knowledge distillation into a faster single-scale DeepLabV2 model resulted in 70.4 mIoU at 3.4 images/second, 1.4 TFLOPs, and 1.3 GB. Both efficient variants surpassed the previous SOTA performance of DAFormer (68.3 mIoU).

### 5. Significance and potential impact

The HRDA framework represents a substantial advancement in the field of unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA) for semantic segmentation, addressing a critical technical challenge and establishing new performance benchmarks.

**Significance:**

- **Overcoming Resolution-Context Trade-off:** The work systematically investigates and effectively resolves the long-standing challenge in UDA of balancing high-resolution detail preservation with long-range context understanding under GPU memory constraints. It demonstrates that a multi-resolution approach with learned scale attention can combine these elements efficiently.
- **New State-of-the-Art Performance:** HRDA significantly elevates the performance of UDA semantic segmentation on standard benchmarks (GTA→Cityscapes and Synthia→Cityscapes) by considerable margins (5.5 mIoU and 4.9 mIoU, respectively). This establishes a new high-water mark for the field.
- **Generalizability and Modularity:** The framework is designed to be applicable to various existing UDA strategies and network architectures. Its demonstrated consistent improvements across different UDA methods highlight its modularity and broad utility.
- **Insights into Scale-Dependent Adaptation:** The research provides empirical evidence that different object scales benefit from different input resolutions during domain adaptation. Small objects and fine details benefit from high-resolution inputs, while large "stuff" regions might be better adapted using low-resolution inputs due to less interference from domain-specific textures. The learned scale attention mechanism effectively exploits this insight.
- **Memory Efficiency:** By combining nested large LR context crops with small HR detail crops, HRDA achieves its performance gains while maintaining a manageable GPU memory footprint, making high-resolution UDA more feasible.

**Potential Impact:**

- **Reduced Annotation Burden:** By improving the efficacy of UDA, HRDA can further reduce the need for costly and labor-intensive pixel-wise annotations in real-world target domains. This would accelerate the deployment of semantic segmentation models in new environments.
- **Enhanced Real-World Applications:** The ability to accurately segment small objects and preserve fine details, combined with robust context understanding, is crucial for applications like autonomous driving, robotics, and medical imaging. HRDA's improved performance in these areas could lead to more reliable and safer systems.
- **Inspiration for Future UDA Research:** The findings on multi-resolution learning, learned scale attention, and the specific benefits of LR vs. HR inputs for different object types can guide future research in UDA, leading to further innovations in architecture design and training strategies.
- **Standard for High-Resolution UDA:** HRDA establishes a precedent and a strong baseline for future efforts aiming to tackle high-resolution semantic segmentation in UDA, pushing the boundaries of what is achievable.
- **Practical Implementations:** The public availability of the implementation provides a direct resource for researchers and practitioners to build upon this work and integrate its benefits into their own projects.
