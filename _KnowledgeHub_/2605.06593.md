# ReActor: Reinforcement Learning for Physics-Aware Motion Retargeting

> [!summary] Summary
> ReActor, a framework from Disney Research, uses a bilevel optimization scheme to adapt human kinematic motion to diverse robotic morphologies by integrating reinforcement learning with physics simulation. This approach generates physically plausible motions with zero ground or self-penetration, which significantly improves downstream reinforcement learning success rates by up to 15.22 percentage points on various humanoid and quadruped robots.

## Problem

- Existing motion retargeting methods often generate physically implausible motions (e.g., foot sliding, self-penetration) that degrade downstream imitation learning performance.
- A significant 'embodiment gap' exists between human motion data and robots with fundamentally different kinematic structures and dynamics, making direct application challenging.
- Current methods frequently require extensive manual tuning, predefined contact patterns, or large paired datasets, limiting their scalability and generalization across diverse morphologies.

## Method

- **ReActor** uses a ==bilevel optimization== framework that jointly optimizes learnable retargeting parameters (upper-level) and trains a ==motion tracking policy== via reinforcement learning (lower-level) within a physics simulation.
- A ==simplified gradient estimation== for the upper-level loss is derived, avoiding the computationally intensive implicit function theorem typically required for bilevel problems with an RL-based lower-level.
- The system requires only ==sparse semantic rigid-body correspondences== and automatically identifies optimal retargeting parameters, including local position/orientation adjustments and a per-motion vertical offset.

## Results

- ReActor achieved **zero ground and self-penetration** (0.00% time, 0.00 cm depth) and significantly reduced foot sliding (e.g., **0.17 cm/s** on G1) and floating compared to GMR and OmniRetarget baselines.
- Policies trained with ReActor-retargeted data consistently achieved higher downstream RL success rates (e.g., **97.45%** for G1, **95.07%** for Lima) compared to baselines (79.85–95.51%) — up to **+15.22 pp** improvement.
- The method demonstrated effective generalization to unseen motion data from diverse datasets and successfully applied retargeted motions to quadruped robots and physical hardware in real-time interactive settings.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating motion retargeting directly within a physics simulation, combined with bilevel optimization, inherently ensures physical plausibility, accounts for contact dynamics, and significantly reduces artifacts like ground or self-penetration.
> - A computationally tractable, simplified gradient estimation enables the practical application of bilevel optimization to complex reinforcement learning problems for motion retargeting.
> - The iterative refinement of both retargeting parameters and the RL policy leads to more natural and physically consistent robot motions, directly correlating with improved performance in downstream imitation learning tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of character animation and robotics heavily relies on motion data, frequently utilizing it as reference trajectories for imitation learning with deep reinforcement learning (RL). This data is often sourced from human motion capture or reconstructed from video. A significant challenge arises when these human-derived motions need to be adapted, or "retargeted," to target embodiments such as robots or virtual characters that possess substantially different kinematic structures, body shapes, mass distributions, and actuation mechanisms. This adaptation process, known as motion retargeting, is crucial for bridging the "embodiment gap."

Existing motion retargeting methods broadly fall into two categories: optimization-based and learning-based approaches. Optimization-based methods aim to minimize pose discrepancies between source and target motions. However, these methods often require predefined contact patterns, are susceptible to converging in local minima, and demand considerable manual tuning to scale effectively across diverse motion datasets. Learning-based methods, on the other hand, learn direct mappings from source to target motions. These approaches typically necessitate large datasets of source-target pairs and have primarily been applied to characters with idealized joint structures, thereby often sidestepping the complexities associated with physical characters and real-world physics.

A common limitation across both categories is their propensity to generate physically implausible motions. These artifacts include foot sliding, self-penetration, and abrupt joint movements. Such inconsistencies are a primary factor contributing to performance degradation in subsequent tasks, particularly during the training of RL policies for imitation learning. Specialized methods have been developed for humanoid robots to address these artifacts, sometimes employing differentiable simulation to optimize for additional effects like vibration suppression. While recent tools have aimed to streamline the sim-to-real transfer process, many remain largely kinematic in nature and struggle with temporal coherence. Some physics-based approaches for retargeting, like Differentiable Optimal Control (DOC), share a focus on physical plausibility but may differ in aspects such as collision avoidance, contact pattern requirements, or scalability to large datasets.

This work distinguishes itself from conventional motion imitation frameworks (which focus on tracking a *given* kinematic reference) by addressing the *preceding* problem of generating a *suitable* reference motion to track. It directly tackles the embodiment gap between human source and robot target. The proposed approach embeds motion retargeting within a physics simulation using a bilevel optimization framework. This integration enables the system to inherently respect physical limitations and account for discontinuous contact dynamics, features often lacking in purely kinematic or data-driven methods. Furthermore, by using a reinforcement learning controller at the lower level, it can handle non-differentiable objectives and scale to massive datasets with a single retargeting policy, unlike many prior approaches that require extensive, per-motion tuning or fail to generalize across diverse morphologies. The use of bilevel optimization, while gaining traction in RL problems, is applied here with a simplified gradient estimation tailored to the retargeting problem structure, distinguishing it from methods relying on the implicit function theorem and its computational demands.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust, physics-aware motion retargeting framework that can effectively transfer human kinematic reference motions to diverse robotic morphologies. The overarching goal is to produce physically plausible, artifact-free target motions that significantly enhance the success and robustness of downstream imitation learning tasks, particularly those involving deep reinforcement learning.

More specifically, the key objectives include:
- To establish a bilevel optimization framework that jointly adapts parameterized reference motions to a target robot's morphology while simultaneously training a reinforcement learning policy to track these adapted motions. This joint optimization is intended to mitigate conflicts between the reference motion and the robot's physical constraints.
- To ensure the generated motions are physically plausible by integrating the retargeting process directly within a physics simulation, thereby inherently respecting physical limitations, accommodating discontinuous contact dynamics, and allowing for non-differentiable objectives.
- To minimize common retargeting artifacts such as foot sliding, self-collisions, and dynamically infeasible movements, which are known to degrade the performance of downstream RL policy training.
- To reduce the manual effort required by users, necessitating only a sparse set of semantic rigid-body correspondences between source and target embodiments, and automatically identifying optimal retargeting parameters.
- To validate the method's effectiveness across various target morphologies, including humanoids and quadrupeds, demonstrating its applicability to characters that differ significantly from a human source.
- To demonstrate the framework's scalability to large motion datasets through the training of a single retargeting policy that can be applied to diverse motions.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges in current motion retargeting practices:
- **Physical Inconsistencies:** Existing retargeting methods frequently yield motions that are physically inconsistent, characterized by artifacts like foot sliding, self-penetration, and abrupt joint movements. These artifacts hinder the effective training of physically simulated characters and robots.
- **Degradation of Downstream Tasks:** Such physically implausible motions act as a primary source of performance degradation in downstream tasks, particularly in reinforcement learning for imitation, where accurate and feasible reference trajectories are critical.
- **Embodiment Gap:** Human motion data, while abundant, is difficult to directly apply to robots due to fundamental differences in kinematic structure, body shape, mass distribution, and actuation mechanisms. A robust method is needed to bridge this gap effectively.
- **Manual Effort and Scalability:** Many optimization-based methods require predefined contact patterns and extensive manual tuning, making them labor-intensive and difficult to scale across large and diverse motion datasets. Learning-based methods often require substantial paired data, which can be challenging to acquire for novel morphologies.
- **Need for Robust Reference Generation:** While motion imitation frameworks exist, there is a preceding need for a method to generate a *suitable* and *physically consistent* reference motion to track, rather than simply tracking an arbitrary, potentially implausible, input.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of this work is a bilevel optimization framework designed to retarget human motions onto diverse robot morphologies. This framework jointly optimizes retargeting parameters in an upper level and trains a motion tracking policy via reinforcement learning in a lower level.

**Bilevel Optimization Structure:**
The problem is formulated as minimizing an upper-level loss function `L(p, 𝝓*(p))` with respect to retargeting parameters `p`, where `𝝓*(p)` represents the optimal policy parameters that maximize a lower-level reward function `R(p, 𝝓)` for a given `p`. The upper-level loss `L` quantifies the error between the parameterized reference motion `g_t` and the simulated state `s*_t` resulting from the optimal policy rollout.

**Upper-Level Optimization:**
A practical challenge in bilevel optimization, particularly with RL in the lower level, is the computational cost of waiting for the lower-level problem to converge. The authors adopt a single-loop bilevel optimization algorithm, specifically a Two-Timescale Approximation (TTSA), which updates both upper- and lower-level decision variables concurrently.
A key aspect of this approach is a simplified gradient estimation for the upper-level loss. Instead of employing the implicit function theorem, which typically requires computing the inverse Hessian of the lower-level problem (complex for RL), the authors derive an approximate gradient `(1 − 𝛼) 𝜕_g_t ℓ d_p g_t`. This derivation relies on two assumptions: first, that the loss function depends strictly on the difference between the parameterized reference `g_t` and the simulated state `s*_t`, implying `𝜕_s*_t ℓ = −𝜕_g_t ℓ`; and second, that the sensitivity of the optimal trajectories `s*_t` to changes in `g_t` can be approximated as `𝛼I`, where `𝛼` is a scaling factor between 0 and 1. This simplification renders the gradient computationally tractable. The gradient estimate is then computed using sampled data from the current policy rollouts.

**Retargeting Parameterization:**
User input is minimized by requiring only sparse, semantic rigid-body correspondences between the source (e.g., human SMPL model) and target robot morphologies in a nominal configuration (e.g., T-pose). A global scaling factor `s` is automatically derived from the root height ratio between the source and target. Nominal transformations (`x_b^nom`, `R_b^nom`) are then computed to represent the relative offset from the scaled source body frame to the target body frame in the source's local coordinates.
To allow for fine-tuning during optimization, the framework introduces learnable parameters: `p_b^pos` and `p_b^ori` for local position and orientation adjustments, and `p_z` for a per-motion vertical offset to correct residual floating or penetration artifacts present in the source motion. These parameters, along with the nominal transformations, define the full parameterized mapping from the source motion's global position, orientation, linear velocity, and angular velocity to the target robot's corresponding quantities (`g_t`). The optimization parameters are constrained within a convex set `P` to limit their deviations.
The upper-level loss function `ℓ(g_t − s_t)` is a weighted sum of individual loss terms for each source-target rigid-body pair. These terms include squared norm differences for position, linear velocity, and angular velocity, and the squared geodesic difference for orientation. For robots with fewer degrees of freedom, the orientation error can be decomposed into "swing" and "twist" components, allowing the system to ignore rotations about unactuated axes.

**Lower-Level Reinforcement Learning:**
The lower level employs reinforcement learning to train a policy `𝜋_𝝓` that tracks the parameterized reference motion `g_t`. Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is used, with policy and value functions modeled by multi-layer perceptron (MLP) networks.
The action space consists of joint position setpoints for PD controllers (`a_jts^t`) and auxiliary wrenches (forces and torques) applied directly to the robot's root (`w_rt^t`). This auxiliary wrench, which is penalized in the reward function, aids in policy generalization across diverse, challenging motions. A continuous deadband is applied to the wrench action to encourage sparsity.
The observation space `o_t` includes the robot's proprioceptive state (root height, projected gravity vector, linear and angular velocities), joint positions and velocities, and past actions. A retargeting phase variable `𝜓_t` is introduced to manage the initial learning phase, allowing the policy to learn to reach the reference pose from a randomized initial configuration before full retargeting begins.
Traditional Reference State Initialization (RSI) is not feasible due to morphological differences. Instead, the robot's root state is set to that of the source, and joint positions are sampled from a Gaussian distribution around the nominal robot configuration. During the initial phase where `𝜓_t < 1`, the reference motion is paused, and the policy prepares for tracking. Trajectory segments from this phase are excluded from the upper-level optimization.
An adaptive motion sampling strategy is utilized to prioritize training on more challenging motion clips, identified by early episode terminations. The total reward `r_t` is a combination of tracking rewards (based on the same loss terms as the upper-level, scaled by `𝜓_t`) and regularization rewards that penalize excessive joint torques, encourage smooth actions, and penalize the use of auxiliary wrenches to promote physical plausibility.
Simulations are performed using Isaac Sim, running 4,096 environment instances in parallel on a single GPU for efficient training. The method is trained for 20,000 iterations, typically taking approximately 6 hours.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates the proposed "ReActor" method through comparisons with state-of-the-art baselines, ablation studies, and demonstrations across various use cases.

**Baseline Comparison:**
ReActor was compared against two contemporary human motion retargeting methods, GMR [Araujo et al. 2025] and OmniRetarget [Yang et al. 2025a], on two humanoid robots: Unitree G1 and a custom small-scale robot named Lima.

- **Kinematic Evaluation:** ReActor demonstrated superior performance across all kinematic metrics on both humanoid platforms (Table 4).
 - **Ground and Self-Penetration:** ReActor achieved zero ground penetration and self-penetration (0.00% time, 0.00 cm depth) for both robots. In contrast, GMR and OmniRetarget exhibited measurable ground penetration (GMR: 0.53% time, 2.74 cm depth on G1) and self-penetration (GMR: 0.07% time, 5.63 cm depth on G1; OmniRetarget: 0.12% time, 3.27 cm depth on G1). This difference is attributed to ReActor's integration with physics simulation, which explicitly accounts for contact dynamics.
 - **Foot Sliding and Floating:** ReActor significantly reduced foot sliding (e.g., 0.17 cm/s on G1) and foot floating (e.g., 0.12 cm on G1) compared to baselines. Baselines often struggled with these artifacts; GMR did not apply height correction, leading to penetration and floating, while OmniRetarget's strict height correction sometimes resulted in floating due to conflicting objectives (Figure 5).
 - **Local Minima:** Optimization-based baselines were observed to converge to local minima, particularly near kinematic singularities or joint limits, leading to severe artifacts and rendering retargeted motions unusable. ReActor avoided these issues.
 - **Generalization to Diverse Morphologies:** OmniRetarget showed degraded performance on the Lima platform, suggesting challenges with non-uniform scaling and non-standard root alignment without specific tuning. GMR performed better on Lima than G1 in some aspects.
 - **Computational Cost:** The training and retargeting time for ReActor on the AMASS dataset (approximately 6.5 hours on a GPU) was comparable to the baselines running on a CPU.

- **Downstream RL Performance:** A key finding was the direct correlation between retargeted motion quality and the success of downstream Reinforcement Learning. Policies trained on data retargeted by ReActor consistently achieved higher success rates (e.g., G1: 97.45%, Lima: 95.07%) compared to policies trained with baseline-retargeted data (G1: 89.93-95.51%, Lima: 79.85-91.23%). Furthermore, these policies exhibited smaller root position, root orientation, and joint position Root Mean Squared Errors (RMSEs).

**Ablation Studies:**

- **Impact of Bilevel Optimization (Parameterization):** Training curves and qualitative comparisons (Figure 6, 7) demonstrated that the bilevel optimization framework, which iteratively refines both the retargeting parameters and the RL policy, consistently reduced tracking loss and led to higher tracking rewards. This resulted in more natural and physically consistent robot motions with fewer artifacts. The method also proved robust to sparse correspondences (e.g., only root and end effectors) and intentional mapping mismatches. The inclusion of positional targets was found to be beneficial for motions requiring precise timing, such as jumping.
- **Generalization to Unseen Data:** A policy trained on the full AMASS dataset generalized effectively to unseen motions from the 100STYLE dataset. The tracking errors on the test set were comparable to a policy specifically trained on the 100STYLE training split (Table 5), indicating that ReActor can produce a single policy for reuse across diverse datasets and applications.
- **External Force Penalty:** Analysis of the external force penalty weight (Figure 8) revealed a trade-off: higher penalties encouraged greater physical realism but could lead to failures on very challenging motions, while weaker penalties improved retargeting success at the cost of physical plausibility. This parameter acts as a tuning knob for balancing these objectives.

**Use Cases:**

- **Quadruped Retargeting:** ReActor demonstrated versatility by successfully retargeting human motions onto a quadruped robot (ANYmal D), preserving the visual appearance of the motion despite significant morphological differences. This application highlighted the need for more nuanced reward tuning as the embodiment gap increases.
- **Interactive Animation:** The retargeting policy was deployed in an interactive setting, allowing artists to modify motion sequences in real time (88.3 Hz) with instantaneous retargeting to the robot morphology. This capability supports responsive motion editing and potential applications in live motion capture sessions.
- **Physical Robot Control:** Goal-conditioned tracking policies, trained with ReActor-retargeted data, were successfully deployed on the physical Lima robot. This validated the sim-to-real transfer capabilities of the pipeline, confirming that ReActor produces motion references suitable for real-world robotic deployment within hardware limits.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "ReActor" framework represents a notable advancement in the field of motion retargeting, offering a solution to a long-standing challenge in robotics and character animation. Its significance stems from several key contributions:

- **Enhanced Physical Plausibility:** By deeply integrating retargeting with physics simulation and reinforcement learning through a bilevel optimization framework, the method inherently accounts for physical constraints, contact dynamics, and self-collisions. This design leads to physically plausible motions that are largely free from common artifacts like foot sliding and self-penetration, which are prevalent in many existing methods.
- **Improved Downstream RL Performance:** The research demonstrates that the high kinematic quality of motions produced by ReActor directly translates to significantly improved performance and robustness for downstream reinforcement learning tasks. This is a critical factor for training agile and complex behaviors in physically simulated characters and real-world robots.
- **Reduced Manual Effort and Increased Scalability:** ReActor requires minimal user input, relying only on sparse semantic rigid-body correspondences, and automates the identification of optimal retargeting parameters. This reduces the need for extensive manual tuning and domain-specific knowledge, making the method more scalable to large datasets and applicable across diverse morphologies.
- **Computational Efficiency in Bilevel Optimization:** The derivation of a simplified, computationally tractable gradient estimate for the upper-level loss addresses a significant challenge in applying bilevel optimization to reinforcement learning problems, where differentiating through complex, non-linear policies can be prohibitive.
- **Versatility Across Morphologies and Applications:** The method's successful application to humanoids of different scales and even a quadruped, both in simulation and on physical hardware, highlights its versatility. This suggests broad applicability in various domains, from robot control and animation to virtual character design.
- **Real-time Interaction Capability:** The ability to perform real-time retargeting in an interactive setting opens up new possibilities for artists and developers, allowing for dynamic motion editing and potential integration into live performance capture workflows.

The potential impact of this work is substantial across several domains:

- **Robotics:** ReActor can significantly accelerate the development of advanced robotic skills, particularly for humanoid and quadrupedal robots. By providing robust and physically consistent reference motions, it lowers the barrier for applying imitation learning to complex, real-world robotic tasks, improving sim-to-real transfer success. This could lead to more agile, human-like robot locomotion and manipulation.
- **Computer Graphics and Animation:** For animators, the framework offers a powerful tool for generating high-quality physics-based character animation from human motion capture data, even for highly dissimilar characters. The real-time interactive capability could streamline animation workflows and enable more dynamic content creation. It also has implications for training generative motion models by providing cleaner, physically consistent reference data.
- **Virtual Reality and Gaming:** The ability to retarget motions to diverse virtual avatars while maintaining physical plausibility could enhance the immersion and realism in virtual reality experiences and video games, especially for physics-driven character controllers.
- **Foundational Research in AI/RL:** The bilevel optimization formulation, particularly its efficient gradient estimation, offers a promising direction for future research in artificial intelligence and reinforcement learning. The authors suggest its potential for more complex scenarios where reference tracking needs to be balanced with auxiliary objectives like obstacle avoidance, manipulation, or even automated robot design. This integrated approach, rather than decoupled steps, could yield more holistic and robust solutions in complex AI systems.

While ReActor establishes a robust foundation, acknowledging current limitations (such as the tuning of external force penalties and the handling of physically impossible motions) provides clear avenues for future research, including exploring time-varying parameterizations and automating semantic correspondence selection.
