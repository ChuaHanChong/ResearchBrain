# Learning Dynamics of LLM Finetuning

> [!summary] Summary
> The paper presents a learning dynamics framework to analyze LLM fine-tuning, explaining phenomena like hallucination and text degeneration through a "squeezing effect" where negative gradients on unlikely tokens disproportionately boost already confident ones. A proposed "extend" SFT method, which trains on both preferred and rejected examples, mitigates this effect and improves alignment performance.

## Problem

- Understanding the step-wise evolution of LLM predictions during fine-tuning (SFT, DPO) beyond just end-state analysis.
- Explaining counter-intuitive behaviors like hallucination, text degeneration, and confidence decay observed during Direct Preference Optimization (DPO).
- Identifying underlying mechanisms behind the success or failure of different LLM alignment algorithms.

## Method

- Extends the =="learning dynamics" framework== to LLMs, decomposing gradient updates into Activation, ==empirical Neural Tangent Kernel (eNTK)==, and a loss-derived residual term.
- Applies this framework to SFT and DPO, deriving specific update rules for each algorithm to analyze their impact on output probabilities.
- Identifies and formally characterizes the =="squeezing effect"== to explain how negative gradients on low-probability tokens lead to probability mass being disproportionately pushed to the already most confident token.

## Results

- Empirical analyses confirm that SFT increases confidence in target responses and unrelated preferred responses, while DPO causes a general confidence decay, validating the proposed dynamics.
- The "squeezing effect" is shown to be a key mechanism explaining text degeneration and the observed confidence dynamics in DPO.
- A proposed "extend" SFT method, which includes training on rejected responses during SFT, effectively mitigates the squeezing effect and leads to demonstrably higher win rates in LLM evaluations.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - SFT typically pulls up preferred response confidence but also indirectly increases confidence in preferred responses from *other* training examples, explaining a form of hallucination.
> - Off-policy DPO leads to a general *decrease* in confidence across most responses due to the "squeezing effect," where large negative gradients on unlikely tokens disproportionately boost already confident ones.
> - On-policy DPO variants perform better because their rejected responses are sampled from the current model, residing in higher probability regions and thus mitigating the extreme squeezing effect.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of Large Language Models (LLMs) has seen explosive growth, driven by their remarkable capabilities across diverse tasks. However, effectively aligning these powerful models with human intentions and preferences often requires intricate fine-tuning processes, such as Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), or more broadly, Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF). While these methods empirically improve LLM performance, the underlying *mechanisms* of how models evolve during these fine-tuning stages are not fully understood.

Existing analyses of LLM fine-tuning predominantly focus on:
- **Training Targets:** What the models are optimized for (e.g., maximizing likelihood, ranking preferences).
- **End-of-Training Status:** Analyzing the final model's capabilities and limitations.
- **Reinforcement Learning Analogies:** Interpreting fine-tuning as an RL problem, especially for preference alignment.

This paper distinguishes itself by adopting a **dynamical perspective**. It aims to understand the *step-wise evolution* of LLM predictions during fine-tuning, directly addressing how changes in training examples influence predictions on *other* examples. This approach is rooted in the broader study of "learning dynamics" in deep learning, which has been successfully applied to explain phenomena like generalization, memorization, and concept formation in various neural network architectures.

By extending this framework to the high-dimensional, sequential nature of LLMs, the authors bridge a significant gap. They provide a **uniform interpretation** for several puzzling and counter-intuitive observations frequently reported in LLM research, such as:
- **Hallucination:** Why LLMs might generate factual inaccuracies or answers irrelevant to the prompt, sometimes drawing from other training contexts.
- **Text Degeneration:** The tendency of LLMs to repeat phrases or produce overly confident, generic outputs, particularly after preference tuning.
- **Confidence Decay in DPO:** The perplexing observation that confidence in *both* preferred and rejected responses can decrease during Direct Preference Optimization, despite its success in alignment.
- **On-policy vs. Off-policy Performance Gap:** Providing a mechanistic explanation for why on-policy fine-tuning methods often outperform their off-policy counterparts.

The paper directly engages with and offers explanations for findings from other recent works (e.g., Rafailov et al. on DPO, Holtzman et al. on degeneration, Gekhman et al. on hallucination), positioning itself as a foundational step towards a more principled understanding of LLM behavior during adaptation. It moves beyond purely empirical observations or high-level analogies to offer a mechanistic, gradient-based explanation for complex phenomena, paving the way for more robust and interpretable LLM alignment strategies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to **systematically analyze the learning dynamics of Large Language Model (LLM) fine-tuning**, providing a unified framework to understand how these models evolve during adaptation. This overarching goal is driven by several key motivations:

1. **Demystify LLM Fine-tuning:** While LLM fine-tuning methods like SFT and DPO are widely used and empirically successful, the intricate mechanisms governing how models learn and change during these processes are not fully understood. Existing analyses often focus on the end-state of training or high-level comparisons, lacking a granular, step-by-step interpretation of parameter updates and their effects on predictions.

2. **Explain Counter-Intuitive Phenomena:** The field of LLMs is replete with puzzling behaviors observed during or after fine-tuning. The authors specifically aim to provide mechanistic explanations for:
 - **Hallucination:** Why LLMs might start "hallucinating" or providing information from irrelevant contexts after instruction tuning.
 - **Text Degeneration/Repetitive Outputs:** The tendency for models to generate unhelpful, repetitive phrases, especially post-preference tuning.
 - **DPO Confidence Decay:** The observation that confidence in *both* chosen and rejected responses can paradoxically decrease during Direct Preference Optimization.

3. **Uncover the Roots of Algorithmic Success/Failure:** By understanding the dynamics, the paper seeks to shed light on why certain fine-tuning algorithms (e.g., on-policy DPO variants) perform better or are more stable than others. This involves identifying underlying "effects" (like the "squeezing effect" proposed) that govern the distribution of probability mass across the model's output space.

4. **Inspire Improved Alignment Methods:** The ultimate motivation is to translate these mechanistic insights into practical advancements. By deeply understanding *how* models learn, researchers can devise more effective, robust, and safer fine-tuning algorithms. The paper explicitly states its goal to inspire a simple, effective method to improve alignment performance based on its dynamic analysis.

In essence, the paper seeks to provide a theoretical and empirical microscope on LLM fine-tuning, moving beyond superficial observations to reveal the fundamental forces at play during model adaptation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the paper's methodology lies in extending and applying the concept of "learning dynamics" to Large Language Models. This involves a precise decomposition of how a single gradient update, derived from a specific training example, influences the model's predictions on other examples.

1. **Formalizing Learning Dynamics (General Case):**
 - The paper begins by recalling the general formulation of learning dynamics, which describes the change in a model's prediction, $\Delta \log \pi_t(y | x_o)$, after a single gradient descent (GD) update on a training example $(x_u, y_u)$.
 - This change is decomposed into three key components:
 - **`A_t(x_o)` (Activation Matrix):** Reflects the model's current predicted probability distribution for the observed example $x_o$. For Softmax outputs, it's $I - \mathbf{1}\pi_t^\top(x_o)$, essentially determining how changes in logits map to changes in log-probabilities.
 - **`K_t(x_o, x_u)` (Empirical Neural Tangent Kernel - eNTK):** Represents a model-specific similarity measure between the observing input $x_o$ and the updating input $x_u$. It's the product of gradients of logits w.r.t. parameters for $x_o$ and $x_u$. The authors rely on the assumption that the *relative* influence (or similarity captured by eNTK) between different examples remains *relatively stable* during training, which they empirically verify.
 - **`G_t(x_u, y_u)` (Gradient of Loss / Residual Term):** This term provides the "energy and direction" for the model's adaptation, derived directly from the loss function and the training example $(x_u, y_u)$.

2. **Adapting to LLM Fine-tuning:**
 - **Handling Sequential Data and High Dimensionality:** LLMs process sequences and generate outputs token-by-token. The authors address this by treating the concatenated prompt and response ($\chi = [x; y]$) as the input and using a "teacher forcing" mechanism (where previous ground-truth tokens are provided during prediction for loss calculation). This allows them to apply a similar multi-label classification decomposition, with causal masking incorporated into the model's backbone ($h_\theta$). The eNTK term, $K_t(\chi_o, \chi_u)$, then captures similarity between full prompt-response sequences.
 - **Deriving `G_t` for Specific Algorithms:**
 - **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT):** For Negative Log-Likelihood (NLL) loss, $G_t^{SFT}(\chi_u) = \pi_{\theta_t}(y | \chi_u) - y_u$, representing the difference between the model's current prediction and the one-hot target.
 - **Direct Preference Optimization (DPO):** For DPO's sigmoid-based loss, the derivation yields two `G` terms, $G_t^{DPO+}$ and $G_t^{DPO-}$, associated with the preferred ($y_+$) and rejected ($y_-$) responses, respectively. These terms are scaled by a factor $\beta(1-a)$, where `a` relates to the sigmoid output and reflects how well the model currently separates $y_+$ and $y_-$. This reveals that DPO imposes both a "pull-up" pressure on $y_+$ and a "push-down" pressure on $y_-$. The paper also extends this to other RL-free methods like IPO, SLiC, and SPPO (in appendix).

3. **Introducing the "Squeezing Effect":**
 - A significant part of the methodology is the theoretical and empirical identification of the "squeezing effect." This describes how large *negative* gradients applied to Softmax-based probability distributions can cause probability mass from low-confidence (or "valley") regions to be redistributed disproportionately to the *already most confident* output, rather than to other less confident but still plausible options.
 - This effect is analytically proven for a simple logistic regression model and then hypothesized to apply to LLMs, particularly during DPO, explaining the observed general confidence decay.

4. **Experimental Validation:**
 - **Models:** Pythia (410M to 2.8B) and Qwen1.5 (0.5B, 1.8B) models are used, representing different sizes and pre-training recipes.
 - **Datasets:** Antropic-HH and UltraFeedback are used for fine-tuning.
 - **Probing Dataset (`D_prob`):** This is a crucial element. For selected prompts, the authors generate various types of responses: the original preferred/rejected, their rephrased versions (semantic/format-preserving by ChatGPT/self-rephrase), irrelevant but human-like responses (from other train/test examples, random sentences), and non-human-like random token sequences. Tracking the log-likelihood of these diverse responses over training epochs provides fine-grained insights into how different regions of the output space are affected.
 - **Metrics:** Log-likelihood of different response types, and derived metrics like `LBK_uo` (lower bound of eNTK) and `SignDelta_uo` to track influence and direction of changes.
 - **Ablation/Intervention:** A simple "extend" SFT method is proposed where (x, y-) pairs are *also* used in the initial SFT stage, aiming to mitigate the squeezing effect by elevating the prior probability of rejected responses. This method's effectiveness is then evaluated via human-like LLM evaluators (ChatGPT, Claude3) to assess alignment win rates.

This multi-faceted approach combines theoretical decomposition, specific algorithmic derivations, a novel conceptual explanation ("squeezing effect"), and detailed empirical validation with carefully designed probing strategies and a practical intervention.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper presents several key findings based on its learning dynamics framework and experimental validations:

1. **SFT Learning Dynamics:**
 - **Direct Pull-Up:** As expected, the confidence (log-probability) of the directly supervised preferred responses ($y_u^+$) consistently increases throughout SFT.
 - **Indirect Influence on Similar Responses:** Responses "similar" to $y_u^+$ (e.g., rephrased $y_u^+$, original rejected responses $y_u^-$, or other preferred responses from the training set $y_j^+$) initially show a slight increase in confidence. This is attributed to the indirect "pull-up" pressure through the eNTK term, as they share some learned features or patterns with $y_u^+$.
 - **Global Push-Down Dominance:** After an initial phase, the confidence of most *other* responses (including those initially slightly increasing) eventually decreases. This is explained by the fundamental property that total probability mass must sum to one; as $y_u^+$'s probability increases significantly, it necessitates a "push-down" on all other options.
 - **Hallucination Explained:** The confidence of preferred responses to *other* questions from the training set ($y_{j \neq u}^+$) consistently increases, albeit at a slower rate than $y_u^+$. This provides a mechanistic explanation for why SFT can sometimes lead to models answering questions with facts or phrases relevant to different, unrelated training examples—a form of hallucination.
 - **Format vs. Semantics Bias:** Observations suggest that LLMs during SFT might prioritize matching the *format* of preferred responses more than their exact *semantics* (e.g., GPT-f rephrases showing higher confidence than GPT-s rephrases), implying a potential "fingerprint" of LLM-preferred idioms.

2. **Off-Policy DPO Learning Dynamics and the "Squeezing Effect":**
 - **Confidence Decay in DPO:** Unlike SFT, where $y_u^+$ confidence usually increases, DPO shows a general *decrease* in confidence for almost *all* observed responses, including $y_u^+$. While the margin ($\pi_\theta(y_u^+) - \pi_\theta(y_u^-)$) still increases, the absolute log-probabilities drop significantly.
 - **The "Squeezing Effect":** This phenomenon is explained by the novel "squeezing effect." When a large *negative* gradient is applied to a token that is *already unlikely* (i.e., in a "valley" region of the probability distribution), the probability mass taken from this token is not evenly redistributed. Instead, it is predominantly "squeezed" towards the *single most confident token* in the model's current vocabulary, making the distribution peakier.
 - **Causes of Squeezing in DPO:** Since off-policy DPO uses pre-collected $y_u^-$ which are often low-probability under the current model (especially if fine-tuned extensively by SFT), applying a negative gradient to these "valley" regions triggers a strong squeezing effect. This forces the model to become extremely confident on its *already most preferred* tokens, leading to a "degeneration" where it might repeat simple, highly probable phrases (as observed by Holtzman et al.).
 - **Exacerbation by Prior SFT:** The longer a model is SFT-trained before DPO, the more "peaky" its initial distribution becomes, and the lower the probability of many $y_u^-$. This further intensifies the squeezing effect during subsequent DPO, leading to faster confidence decay for *all* responses.
 - **On-Policy Benefits Explained:** The paper suggests that on-policy DPO variants perform better because their rejected responses ($y_u^-$) are sampled from the current model, meaning they are likely in *higher* probability regions. Applying negative gradients to these higher-probability regions (akin to "peaky on peak" in the logistic regression example) mitigates the extreme squeezing effect observed with "peaky on valley" scenarios.

3. **Mitigating the Squeezing Effect (Proposed Method):**
 - **"Extend" SFT:** The authors propose a simple modification: during the initial SFT phase, train the model on *both* $(x, y_u^+)$ and $(x, y_u^-)$ pairs. This aims to increase the base probability of $y_u^-$ before DPO, preventing it from being in a "valley" region.
 - **Empirical Success:** Experiments show that this "extend" SFT indeed mitigates the squeezing effect during the subsequent DPO phase (slower confidence decay for all responses, lower "argmax confidence" increase).
 - **Improved Alignment:** When evaluated using LLM judges (ChatGPT, Claude3), models trained with "extend" SFT followed by DPO significantly outperform baseline SFT-then-DPO models in terms of win rate, demonstrating practical improvement in alignment performance.

These findings collectively offer a nuanced and mechanistic understanding of LLM fine-tuning, explaining previously mysterious behaviors and pointing towards new directions for algorithmic improvement.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research paper offers substantial significance and potential impact across theoretical understanding, practical LLM development, and broader machine learning research:

1. **Fundamental Understanding of LLM Learning:** By successfully applying the framework of learning dynamics to LLM fine-tuning, the paper provides a **novel and powerful lens** through which to understand how these complex models adapt. It moves beyond black-box observations to offer mechanistic, gradient-based explanations for changes in model behavior, which is crucial for building more reliable and predictable AI systems.

2. **Unified Explanation for Diverse Phenomena:** The paper's framework allows for a **uniform interpretation** of several previously disparate and puzzling observations in LLM fine-tuning, including hallucination, text degeneration (repeating phrases), and the peculiar confidence decay in DPO. This synthesis consolidates empirical findings under a single theoretical umbrella, making the field more coherent.

3. **Addressing Key LLM Alignment Challenges:**
 - **Hallucination Mitigation:** The explanation for how specific types of hallucination (e.g., using responses from other training examples) are strengthened during SFT provides actionable insight. Future work could explore mechanisms to weaken unwanted cross-example influence.
 - **Degeneration Prevention:** The "squeezing effect" mechanistically explains the origin of text degeneration. This understanding opens avenues for designing DPO variants or regularization techniques that specifically counteract this effect, leading to more diverse and coherent model outputs.

4. **Insights into Preference Optimization (DPO):** The analysis of the "squeezing effect" and its relationship to the "valley" region of the probability distribution offers a **critical insight into the performance discrepancies** between off-policy and on-policy DPO variants. It suggests that on-policy sampling implicitly mitigates the harmful effects of negative gradients by ensuring $y_-$ samples are less likely to be in "deep valley" regions. This understanding can guide the design of future preference optimization algorithms.

5. **Direct Practical Improvement:** The paper's analysis is not just theoretical; it directly **inspires a simple, effective method ("Extend" SFT)** to improve alignment performance. By augmenting SFT with rejected examples, the method mitigates the squeezing effect and leads to demonstrably higher win rates against strong baselines. This demonstrates the immediate utility of a deep dynamic understanding in practical LLM development. This straightforward technique could be readily adopted and further refined (e.g., by incorporating more diverse "unpreferred" responses).

6. **Broader Applicability of "Squeezing Effect":** The concept of the "squeezing effect" caused by large negative gradients on unlikely outcomes is a general phenomenon observed in Softmax-based models. This insight could have implications beyond LLMs, informing research in areas like machine unlearning, generative adversarial networks (GANs), or any deep learning system where negative gradients are applied to specific parts of a probability distribution.

In summary, this research significantly advances our understanding of LLM fine-tuning by providing a mechanistic, gradient-level explanation for complex behaviors. Its contributions are both theoretically profound and practically impactful, offering clear pathways for developing more robust, interpretable, and aligned large language models.
