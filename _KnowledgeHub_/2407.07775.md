# Mobility VLA: Multimodal Instruction Navigation with Long-Context VLMs and Topological Graphs

> [!summary] Summary
> Mobility VLA, developed by Google DeepMind, enables robots to understand and navigate based on complex multimodal instructions by combining the semantic reasoning of long-context Vision-Language Models with robust topological graphs. The system demonstrated high success rates in real-world environments, including 85% for multimodal instructions in an office, and showed that long-context VLMs are crucial for high-level understanding while a classical low-level controller is essential for reliable action execution.

## Problem

- Existing robot navigation methods like Object Goal Navigation (ObjNav) and Vision-Language Navigation (VLN) often lack the ability to handle complex, multimodal instructions or require extensive real-time exploration.
- Large Vision-Language Models (VLMs) struggle with practical embodied navigation due to context length limitations when processing entire environment representations and their inability to output low-level robot actions directly.
- Robots needed a more intuitive and human-like interaction paradigm for navigation, allowing for combined natural language and visual cues, and common-sense reasoning for ambiguous instructions.

## Method

- ==Introduces Multimodal Instruction Navigation== with Tours (MINT), which uses an easily collectable demonstration tour video of the environment as prior knowledge.
- Employs a hierarchical Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policy: an offline phase constructs a topological graph from the tour video, and an online phase executes the navigation.
- A long-context VLM (Gemini 1.5 Pro) performs high-level semantic goal finding by identifying a target frame from the tour based on multimodal user instructions, while a low-level policy uses the topological graph for robust localization, path planning, and waypoint action generation.

## Results

- Mobility VLA achieved high end-to-end success rates in real-world environments: **80%** for reasoning-required/free instructions and **85%** for multimodal instructions in an office setting, and **100%** in a home-like environment using smartphone-recorded tours.
- The system's high-level goal finding significantly outperformed CLIP-based retrieval and text-only VLM baselines (e.g., **90%** vs. **30%** for multimodal tasks) by effectively leveraging long-context VLMs.
- Direct VLM prompting for actions resulted in **0%** end-to-end navigation success, validating the necessity of a separate topological graph-based low-level controller which consistently achieved **100%** success for goal reaching.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Long-context Vision-Language Models (VLMs) are crucial for high-level semantic understanding of complex, multimodal instructions, as evidenced by drastic performance drops when context length is constrained.
> - Directly prompting VLMs for low-level robot actions is ineffective and inefficient for navigation due to their lack of embodied control understanding and slow inference for real-time operation.
> - A hierarchical approach, combining VLM for high-level goal identification with a robust topological graph-based low-level policy, effectively bridges the gap between semantic understanding and precise robot action.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robot navigation research has historically evolved from simple point-to-point movement in pre-mapped environments to more semantically aware capabilities. Early classical navigation methods focused on metric coordinates and relied on precise maps and path-planning algorithms like D*. While robust, these systems lacked intuitive user interaction, requiring specific coordinate inputs.

A significant leap came with **Object Goal Navigation (ObjNav)** and **Vision-Language Navigation (VLN)**, which enabled robots to understand open-vocabulary language instructions (e.g., "Go to the couch") or identify target objects. These approaches often necessitate an active exploration phase, as the robot typically operates without comprehensive prior knowledge of the environment. More recent work has explored multimodal inputs (combining language and images) for navigation and the use of memory or past explorations to build semantic scene representations.

The emergence of **large Vision-Language Models (VLMs)**, such as Gemini and GPT-4V, marks a new era. These models, trained on web-scale multimodal data, exhibit impressive capabilities in language and image understanding, common-sense reasoning, and even some zero or few-shot navigation abilities. However, VLMs face two primary challenges in practical navigation:
1. **Context Length Limitation:** Many VLMs cannot process the vast number of images required to represent large environments comprehensively. This limits their ability to build a high-fidelity understanding of the entire navigation space.
2. **Action Generation Mismatch:** VLMs are not inherently trained to output low-level robot actions (e.g., twist velocities or precise waypoints). Directly prompting them for actions often leads to out-of-distribution queries and unsatisfactory zero-shot performance.

Mobility VLA introduces **Multimodal Instruction Navigation with Tours (MINT)** as a novel paradigm that addresses these limitations. MINT explicitly leverages a "demonstration tour video" as an environment prior, which can be easily collected (e.g., by teleoperation or a smartphone). This bypasses the need for extensive real-time exploration, a common bottleneck in previous approaches. By combining long-context VLMs with a robust, classical low-level navigation policy based on topological graphs, Mobility VLA effectively bridges the gap between high-level multimodal understanding and reliable robot action generation. It aims to make robots truly useful by allowing them to accept complex, multimodal, and colloquial instructions, going beyond simple object or location names. This work aligns with the trend of integrating powerful foundation models into embodied AI, while also acknowledging the practical need for robust, traditional robotics components.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to enable intelligent agents to understand complex multimodal instructions (natural language and images) and perform useful navigation tasks in real-world environments. The authors identify several key motivations for pursuing this goal and developing Mobility VLA:

1. **Lifting Navigation to Multimodal Space:** Current ObjNav and VLN primarily rely on natural language. The authors aim for a "leap forward" by allowing robots to accept simultaneous natural language and image instructions. This enables more intuitive and human-like interaction, such as a user asking "Where should I return this?" while holding an object. This is referred to as **Multimodal Instruction Navigation (MIN)**.
2. **Leveraging Demonstration Tours (MINT):** Recognizing that full environment exploration is often time-consuming or unnecessary, the paper introduces MINT. The motivation for using demonstration tours includes:
 - **Ease of Collection:** Users can teleoperate a robot or simply record a video with a smartphone, making deployment straightforward.
 - **Alignment with User Practice:** It mirrors how humans might introduce a new robot to their home.
 - **Safety and Privacy:** Restricting robot movement to a pre-defined zone can be desirable in certain contexts.
3. **Addressing VLM Limitations for Embodied Navigation:** Despite their impressive capabilities, VLMs alone are insufficient for MINT due to:
 - **Context Length:** The inability of many VLMs to process a large number of input images from a comprehensive tour video limits environment understanding, especially in large spaces.
 - **Action Generation:** VLMs are not inherently designed to output precise robot actions, leading to poor zero-shot navigation performance if directly prompted for control commands.
4. **Enabling Complex Reasoning and Colloquial Instructions:** The motivation is to enable robots to handle instructions that require common-sense reasoning (e.g., "I want to store something out of sight from the public eye. Where should I go?") or are inherently multimodal (e.g., "Where should I return this?" with an accompanying image of the item). Previous methods were not designed for such complex, ambiguous, or multimodal inputs.

In essence, Mobility VLA seeks to overcome the limitations of existing navigation paradigms and raw VLM application by proposing a hierarchical system that leverages the strengths of long-context VLMs for high-level semantic understanding and a classical topological graph-based approach for robust low-level action generation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Mobility VLA is designed as a **hierarchical Vision-Language-Action (VLA) navigation policy** that integrates advanced large models with traditional robotics techniques. Its core components operate in both offline and online phases.

#### 3.1. Offline Phase: Demonstration Tour and Topological Graph Generation

- **Demonstration Tour Collection:** A video tour of the environment (`F`) is collected. This can be done via teleoperation of the robot or simply by recording with a smartphone while walking. Optional natural language narratives (`N`) can be added to specific frames during the tour for richer semantic context.
- **Topological Graph Construction:** From the collected tour video, a topological graph `G = (V, E)` is constructed offline.
 - **Vertex Creation:** Each frame `f_i` from the tour video corresponds to a vertex `v_i` in the graph.
 - **Pose Estimation:** COLMAP, an off-the-shelf Structure-from-Motion (SfM) pipeline, is used to estimate the approximate 6-Degree-of-Freedom camera pose for each frame, which is stored in the corresponding vertex.
 - **Edge Creation:** A directed edge is added between vertices if the target vertex is "in front of" the source vertex (within 90 degrees of the source's pose) and within a 2-meter proximity. This simpler approach, compared to traditional PRMs, efficiently captures environmental connectivity based on the tour trajectory.

#### 3.2. Online Phase: Hierarchical Policy Execution

##### 3.2.1. High-Level Goal Finding with Long-Context Multimodal VLMs

- **Input:** The high-level policy takes the full demonstration tour video (`F`, `N`) and the multimodal user instruction (`d` for text, `I` for image) as input.
- **Prompt Engineering:** A carefully designed prompt `P(F, N, d, I)` is constructed. This prompt interleaves text (including frame narratives and user instructions) and images (from the tour frames and the user's instruction image). The goal is to leverage the VLM's common-sense reasoning and multimodal understanding to identify a specific goal frame from the tour video.
- **VLM Role:** A long-context VLM (specifically Gemini 1.5 Pro) is employed to process this extensive multimodal input. The VLM's task is to understand the user's intent (even if colloquial or ambiguous) and identify the integer index `g` of the tour frame that best satisfies the instruction. This effectively translates a semantic goal into a visually grounded waypoint within the tour.

##### 3.2.2. Low-Level Goal Reaching using Topological Graphs

- **Inputs:** Once the goal frame index `g` is identified, the low-level policy takes the offline-constructed topological graph `G`, the robot's current camera observation `O`, and the goal frame index `g` as input.
- **Real-time Localization:** At each timestep, a real-time hierarchical visual localization system is used to:
 - Estimate the robot's current pose `T` by localizing `O` within the pre-built 3D landmarks and reference images from COLMAP.
 - Identify the closest start vertex `v_s` in `G` to the robot's current pose.
- **Path Planning:** If the robot has not reached the goal frame (`v_s` != `v_g`), Dijkstra's algorithm is used to compute the shortest path `S` on the topological graph from `v_s` to the goal vertex `v_g`.
- **Waypoint Action Generation:** The low-level policy then calculates a waypoint action `a` (longitudinal translation ∆x, lateral translation ∆y, and rotation ∆θ in the robot-centric frame) based on the relative pose between the robot's current pose `T` and the next vertex `v_1` in the shortest path `S`.
- **Action Execution:** This waypoint action `a` is then executed by the robot's embodiment-specific mechanism, which typically involves a Model Predictive Control (MPC) based algorithm to drive the robot while avoiding obstacles.

This hierarchical approach effectively decouples the complex semantic reasoning from precise low-level control, allowing each component to leverage its strengths. The long-context VLM handles the "what" and "where" (semantic understanding and goal identification), while the topological graph and localization system handle the "how" (path planning and robust execution).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted by the authors demonstrate the effectiveness and critical design choices of Mobility VLA, addressing three key research questions (RQs).

**RQ1: Does Mobility VLA perform well in MINT in the real world?**
- **High End-to-End Success Rate:** Mobility VLA achieved high success rates in an 836m² real-world office environment and a home-like setting. In the office, it showed an 80% success rate for "Reasoning-Free" and "Reasoning-Required" instructions, and an impressive 85% for "Multimodal" instructions (Table 2). This is notable as "Reasoning-Required" and "Multimodal" tasks were previously infeasible for most navigation systems.
- **Challenges with Small Objects:** Success for "Small Objects" was lower (40%), attributed to the limited resolution of the tour video, making fine-grained object identification difficult.
- **Robust Low-Level Goal Reaching:** The low-level policy consistently achieved 100% success rate in the real world, even with demonstration tours recorded months prior under varying conditions (Table 2), highlighting the robustness of the topological graph and localization.
- **Personalization:** Mobility VLA successfully incorporated personalization narratives from the tour, guiding different users to appropriate "temp desk" locations based on their identity.
- **Scalability in Simulation:** Large-scale simulations confirmed high end-to-end success rates (90%) for Reasoning-Free and Reasoning-Required tasks, suggesting scalability.
- **Generalization and Ease of Use:** A proof-of-concept in a home-like environment using a smartphone-recorded tour yielded 100% success rate, demonstrating the system's generality and unprecedented ease of deployment for everyday users.

**RQ2: Does Mobility VLA outperform alternatives thanks to the use of long-context VLM?**
- **Superior High-Level Goal Finding:** Mobility VLA significantly outperformed baseline methods in high-level goal finding (Table 3).
 - **CLIP-based retrieval:** Achieved low success rates across all categories (e.g., 20% for Multimodal), indicating its limitations in handling complex instructions or finding semantically relevant regions beyond direct visual similarity.
 - **Text-Only Mobility VLA:** Performed better than CLIP-based retrieval but still notably worse than Mobility VLA (e.g., 30% for Multimodal vs. 90% for ours), highlighting the critical importance of multimodal understanding from VLMs.
- **Criticality of Long-Context VLMs:** Experiments demonstrated that only Gemini 1.5 Pro, with its 1M token context length, achieved satisfactory success rates. Reducing the tour video frame rate (to fit smaller context windows of models like GPT-4V or GPT-4o) drastically decreased performance (Table 4), confirming that processing the full context of the environment is crucial. This underscores that large, long-context VLMs are *enabling technology* for MINT.

**RQ3: Is the topological graph necessary? Can VLMs produce actions directly?**
- **Topological Graphs are Critical:** A direct comparison in simulation showed that prompting Gemini 1.5 Pro to output waypoint actions directly resulted in a 0% end-to-end success rate (Table 5). The VLM consistently defaulted to a "move forward" action regardless of observation, indicating its inability to perform zero-shot navigation control without specific training or a suitable action space.
- **Efficiency Benefits:** The hierarchical approach with a topological graph is also significantly more efficient. Mobility VLA's low-level policy operates at 0.19 seconds per step, whereas direct VLM prompting was prohibitively slow at 25.90 seconds per step, due to the need to upload all tour images at every inference call. This validates the hierarchical design for practical deployment.

In summary, Mobility VLA demonstrates robust performance on complex, previously unsolved navigation tasks by effectively combining the high-level reasoning of long-context VLMs with the reliability of topological graph-based low-level navigation. The findings strongly support the necessity of both long-context VLMs for semantic understanding and a separate, robust low-level controller for efficient and accurate action generation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

Mobility VLA represents a significant advancement in embodied AI, particularly in the domain of robot navigation. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **New Paradigm for Human-Robot Interaction:** By proposing **Multimodal Instruction Navigation (MIN)** and its variant **MINT**, the paper introduces a more natural and intuitive way for humans to interact with robots. The ability to combine natural language with visual cues (e.g., holding an object while asking "Where should I return this?") mirrors human communication more closely, making robots more accessible and user-friendly for complex, real-world tasks.
2. **Enabling Complex Reasoning in Navigation:** Mobility VLA is the first system to robustly handle navigation tasks requiring complex common-sense reasoning ("I want to store something out of sight from the public eye. Where should I go?") and truly multimodal instructions. This capability expands the range of tasks robots can perform autonomously and interactively.
3. **Leveraging Foundation Models Effectively:** The work effectively integrates the power of large, pre-trained Vision-Language Models (specifically long-context VLMs like Gemini 1.5 Pro) into a practical robotic system. It highlights a successful strategy for overcoming the inherent limitations of VLMs (context length and direct action generation) by adopting a hierarchical architecture, where the VLM performs high-level semantic goal finding, and a classical module handles low-level control. This provides a blueprint for how future foundation models can be deployed in embodied AI.
4. **Ease of Deployment and Generalization:** The demonstration of using a smartphone to collect the tour and achieving 100% success in a home-like environment showcases Mobility VLA's remarkable ease of deployment and generalization. This "walk-around-and-tell" setup drastically lowers the barrier to entry for setting up robots in new environments, moving beyond specialized mapping and programming.
5. **Robustness in Real-World Conditions:** The system's high success rates in a large, cluttered real-world office, even with tours collected months prior under changing conditions, speaks to its practical robustness. The 100% success rate of the low-level goal reaching policy further solidifies its reliability.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The authors acknowledge a few limitations:
- **Lack of Exploration:** The current system relies entirely on a pre-recorded demonstration tour and does not autonomously explore unknown parts of the environment. However, they suggest that existing exploration algorithms could be integrated to augment the tour.
- **VLM Inference Time:** The 10-30 seconds inference time for the high-level VLM can lead to awkward waiting periods for users. The authors propose caching the demonstration tour to significantly speed up inference.

Future work includes deploying Mobility VLA on various robot embodiments, given its light onboard compute requirements (VLMs are cloud-based) and reliance on only RGB camera observations. More ambitiously, the authors hint at the VLM's potential to generate high-level action plans for complex, compound instructions beyond simple navigation (e.g., "Do they have my favorite drink today?" involving navigation, inspection, and reporting), opening doors for more sophisticated human-robot collaboration.

Overall, Mobility VLA pushes the boundaries of intuitive robot navigation, demonstrating how sophisticated AI models, when strategically integrated with robust classical robotics, can unlock unprecedented levels of usability and intelligence in real-world robotic applications.
