# Adaptive Action Chunking at Inference-time for Vision-Language-Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> A new inference-time strategy, Adaptive Action Chunking (AAC), allows Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models to dynamically determine action chunk sizes based on predictive uncertainty. This method enhances task success rates on RoboCasa Kitchen and LIBERO benchmarks in simulation, achieving an average 2.3% and 0.9% increase respectively, and improves real-world robot manipulation by 15% across several tasks.

## Problem

- Fixed action chunk sizes in Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models necessitate a trade-off between reactivity to new environmental information (requiring small chunks) and temporal consistency/computational efficiency (benefiting from large chunks).
- The optimal action chunk size varies considerably across different robotic manipulation tasks and even within distinct phases of a single task, making manual empirical tuning challenging and inherently suboptimal.
- Previous adaptive chunking approaches often rely on hand-crafted value functions, require task-specific reward signals unavailable in real-world scenarios, or do not offer true dynamic adaptation, limiting their generalization and practical deployment.

## Method

- Introduces **AAC** (Adaptive Action Chunking), an ==inference-time strategy== that dynamically selects the optimal action chunk size based on the VLA model's predictive uncertainty, without requiring additional training or architectural modifications.
- Utilizes ==action entropy== as a metric to quantify the uncertainty of predicted actions; lower entropy suggests higher reliability for longer action chunks, while higher entropy indicates uncertain predictions warranting smaller chunk sizes and more frequent replanning.
- Calculates average action entropy by sampling multiple candidate action chunks in parallel, using ==Gaussian differential entropy== for continuous control components (translation, rotation) and ==discrete entropy== for discrete components (gripper), then identifies the optimal chunk size where the increase in average entropy is maximal.

## Results

- In simulation, **AAC** improved average success rates by **2.3%** on RoboCasa Kitchen (**62.0%** vs **59.7%**) and **0.9%** on LIBERO (**95.0%** vs **94.1%**), notably a **4%** increase in LIBERO-Long tasks.
- Real-world experiments on a robot showed a **15%** average increase in success rate (from **67.0%** to **82.0%**) across banana pick-and-place, emergency button pressing, and long-horizon toy manipulation tasks.
- AAC enhanced robustness in out-of-distribution scenarios and improved operational safety in real-world deployments by preventing collisions and enabling smoother, more precise actions when predictions indicated high uncertainty.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Action entropy, derived from parallel sampling of predicted actions, provides a robust and model-agnostic indicator of a VLA model's predictive uncertainty for both continuous and discrete control outputs.
> - Dynamically adjusting action chunk sizes based on this uncertainty enables VLA models to adapt their control granularity to specific task phases, leveraging larger chunks for confident, coarse movements and smaller ones for critical, fine-grained manipulations.
> - The point of maximum differential in average action entropy, combined with a minimum action magnitude constraint, effectively determines an optimal chunk size at inference time, balancing temporal consistency with reactivity to environmental changes.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models represent a significant area of research in robotics, aiming to enhance robotic manipulation capabilities by generating control actions from various data modalities such as vision, language instructions, and proprioception, often augmented with tactile, force, or 3D information. These models, inspired by the success of large pre-trained vision-language models (VLMs), leverage diverse, robot-specific datasets to learn complex policies.

A key technique employed in many VLA models, particularly those based on imitation learning like ACT and Diffusion Policy, is "action chunking." This involves executing a sequence of actions without intermediate replanning, which has been shown to improve success rates by reducing compounding errors over time. Consequently, action chunking has become a standard paradigm in VLA models, especially those with diffusion-based action heads.

However, a prevalent challenge in current VLA models is the empirical selection of a fixed action chunk size at inference-time. This approach presents a dilemma: a large chunk size can decrease the model's responsiveness to new information or unexpected environmental changes, potentially leading to errors. Conversely, a small chunk size necessitates more frequent replanning, which can increase computational overhead, compromise temporal consistency, and potentially lead to "mode-jumping" or jerky behavior due to discontinuities between rapidly re-evaluated chunks. The paper illustrates this problem by showing that the optimal fixed chunk size varies significantly across different manipulation tasks, making empirical tuning difficult and often suboptimal for diverse applications.

Prior efforts to address limitations of fixed chunking include:
- **Temporal consistency enhancement:** Methods like ACT integrate current and past predicted actions using Exponential Moving Average (EMA). BID and TV-BID employ search strategies to select optimal chunks from candidates based on coherence and contrast scores, and SGAC selectively updates chunks using cosine similarity. These methods, however, generally still operate with a fixed chunk size throughout an episode.
- **Adaptive chunk size selection (learning-based):** More recent studies have explored adaptive chunk size selection using reinforcement learning. For instance, some approaches determine whether to execute a single action or an entire chunk based on a regret value function, while others introduce learnable modules to predict chunk size. A common limitation of these methods is their reliance on handcrafted value functions or task-specific reward signals, which can restrict their generalization across tasks and limit applicability in real-world scenarios where such signals may not be readily available.

This research aims to address the aforementioned limitations by proposing an Adaptive Action Chunking (AAC) strategy that dynamically determines the chunk size at inference-time. This approach seeks to balance reactivity and consistency without requiring additional training or architectural modifications to the underlying VLA model, thereby offering a more scalable and practical solution for various manipulation tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from the observed limitations of fixed action chunking strategies in Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models during robotic manipulation. Current VLA models predominantly rely on an empirically chosen, static chunk length at inference-time. This fixed-size approach presents several critical issues:

1. **Suboptimal Performance Across Diverse Tasks:** The optimal action chunk size is not universal; it varies significantly depending on the specific manipulation task. As demonstrated in the paper with experiments on the GR00T N1.5 model across RoboCasa Kitchen tasks (Figure 1), different tasks exhibit varying success rates with different fixed chunk sizes. A size that performs well for one task might be suboptimal or lead to failure in another. For instance, tasks requiring fine-grained control might benefit from smaller chunks, while long-range transport might prefer larger chunks for efficiency.

2. **Trade-off Between Reactivity and Consistency:** A large, fixed chunk size can diminish the robot's ability to react promptly to unexpected events or new information from the environment, leading to reduced adaptability and potential task failures. Conversely, an excessively small chunk size, while increasing reactivity, can compromise temporal consistency, lead to jerky movements due to frequent replanning, and increase computational load, thereby hindering overall efficiency. The empirical determination of a fixed chunk size often struggles to optimally balance these competing demands.

3. **Lack of Scalability and Generalizability:** The necessity for extensive empirical tuning of chunk sizes for each new policy or task makes current approaches inefficient and difficult to scale to a wide range of manipulation scenarios. This manual tuning process is time-consuming and does not generalize well, limiting the practical deployment of VLA models in dynamic, open-world environments.

4. **Limitations of Existing Adaptive Methods:** While some prior work has explored adaptive chunking using reinforcement learning, these methods often depend on handcrafted value functions or task-specific reward signals. Such dependencies restrict their generalization capabilities and their applicability in real-world settings where obtaining dense reward signals is challenging.

Given these challenges, the key objective of this work is to propose a novel, simple, and effective Adaptive Action Chunking (AAC) strategy. This strategy aims to dynamically determine the optimal action chunk size at each inference timestep by leveraging action entropy as a cue. The goal is to achieve a better balance between the model's reactivity (by reducing chunk size when uncertainty is high) and temporal consistency/computational efficiency (by increasing chunk size when predictions are reliable), without requiring additional training or modifications to the VLA model's architecture. This approach seeks to enhance the robustness, scalability, and overall performance of VLA models across a diverse spectrum of robotic manipulation tasks in both simulated and real-world environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed Adaptive Action Chunking (AAC) strategy operates entirely at inference-time, dynamically adjusting the action chunk size based on the predicted action's uncertainty. The method is designed to be applicable to VLA models utilizing a diffusion-based action head, with GR00T N1.5 serving as the baseline for implementation in this study.

**Core Principle: Action Entropy as a Cue**
The fundamental idea behind AAC is that the uncertainty of a predicted action can serve as an indicator for deciding the appropriate chunk size.
- **High Uncertainty:** When the model's predictions exhibit high uncertainty (high action entropy), it implies that the predicted actions are of lower quality or reliability. In such scenarios, AAC reduces the chunk size, leading to more frequent replanning and thus increasing the model's reactivity to new observations.
- **Low Uncertainty:** Conversely, when the predictions are more confident (low action entropy), longer action chunks can be executed. This enhances temporal consistency and improves inference efficiency by reducing the frequency of replanning cycles.

**Baseline VLA Model (GR00T N1.5)**
GR00T N1.5 is a VLA foundation model that utilizes a Vision-Language Model (VLM) to extract vision-language features ($\phi_t$) and a Diffusion Transformer (DiT) module as its action head ($V_\theta$) for flow-matching. During training, it minimizes a flow-matching loss to approximate a flow vector field. At inference-time, it generates action chunks through K-step iterative denoising from random noise using forward Euler integration. The proposed AAC strategy integrates with this inference process.

**Action Entropy Calculation**
The action space in robotics can involve both discrete (e.g., gripper control) and continuous (e.g., translation, rotation) dimensions. AAC accounts for both:

1. **Discrete Control Entropy ($E_{dis}$):** For discrete actions, such as gripper control (open/close, G $\in$ {0, 1}), Shannon entropy is used:
 $E_{dis} = - \sum_{a \in A} p(a) \log(p(a))$
 To estimate $p(a)$, the probability of a specific discrete action (e.g., gripper close), N candidate action chunks are sampled in parallel. The probability is then estimated as the count of occurrences (c) of that action among the N samples, divided by N (i.e., $p(a) = c/N$).

2. **Continuous Control Entropy ($E_t$, $E_r$):** For continuous actions, such as 3-DOF translation offsets ($\Delta x, \Delta y, \Delta z$) and 3-DOF rotation offsets ($\Delta R_x, \Delta R_y, \Delta R_z$), Gaussian Differential entropy is employed:
 $E_t = \frac{1}{2} \log[(2\pi e)^d \det(\Sigma_t)]$
 where $d$ is the dimensionality of the continuous action (e.g., 3 for translation), and $\det(\Sigma_t)$ is the determinant of the covariance matrix. The covariance matrix ($\Sigma_t$) is estimated from the N parallel-sampled candidate continuous actions at timestep $t$.

**Average Action Entropy ($\bar{E}_h$):** For a given chunk size $h$, the average action entropy $\bar{E}_h$ is computed by summing the entropies of all continuous and discrete action components (translation, rotation, gripper) for each timestep within the chunk, then averaging over the chunk length:
$\bar{E}_h = \frac{1}{h} \sum_{i=t}^{t+h-1} \sum_{j \in \{t,r,g\}} E_j^i$
where $E_j^i$ represents the entropy of the $j$-th action component at the $i$-th timestep.

**Optimal Chunk Size Determination ($h^*$):**
The optimal chunk size $h^*$ is determined by identifying the point where the average action entropy exhibits its maximum increase, while also ensuring a minimum action magnitude. This is formulated as:
$h^* = \max(\arg \max_h (\bar{E}_{h+1} - \bar{E}_h), \xi)$

Here:
- $\arg \max_h (\bar{E}_{h+1} - \bar{E}_h)$ finds the chunk size $h$ where the difference in average entropy between $h+1$ and $h$ is maximized. This indicates a point where the uncertainty significantly increases, suggesting that actions beyond this point might be unreliable.
- $\xi$ (xi) is a dynamic lower bound for the chunk size. Its purpose is to guarantee a minimum action magnitude, balancing temporal consistency and computational efficiency even in high-uncertainty scenarios.
 - $\xi = \arg \min_l (m(l) > \alpha)$
 - $m(l)$ is the sum of component magnitudes (translation, rotation, gripper) for a chunk of length $l$.
 - **Translational magnitude ($m_t(l)$):** Euclidean norm of the sum of translation vectors over $l$ steps.
 - **Rotational magnitude ($m_r(l)$):** Accumulation of rotational offsets via sequential quaternion composition over $l$ steps.
 - **Gripper magnitude ($m_g(l)$):** Set to 1 if the gripper state changes within the $l$ steps, and 0 otherwise.
 - $\alpha$ is a predefined minimum movement energy of the robot arm, set to 3 in the experiments.

**Inference-time Algorithm (Algorithm 1):**
1. **Generate Candidates:** Given N candidate action chunks predicted in parallel, each containing H future timesteps.
2. **Compute Individual Entropies:** For each timestep $i$ from 1 to H, compute the entropies for continuous translations ($E^i_t$), rotations ($E^i_r$), and discrete gripper control ($E^i_g$) using their respective formulas (Eq. 3 and 2).
3. **Compute Average Chunk Entropies:** For each possible chunk size $h$ from 1 to H, calculate the average action entropy $\bar{E}_h$ using Eq. (4).
4. **Determine Optimal Chunk Size:** Find the optimal chunk size $h^*$ using Eq. (5), which incorporates the maximum differential point of average entropy and the minimum action magnitude constraint $\xi$.
5. **Execution:** The first $h^*$ actions of the predicted chunk are executed, and the process repeats at the next observation timestep.

This methodology allows AAC to adaptively execute larger chunks for efficient coarse movements when confidence is high, and smaller chunks for precise, reactive control during critical manipulation phases, all without requiring any changes to the underlying VLA model's training pipeline or architecture.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research conducted extensive experiments to evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed Adaptive Action Chunking (AAC) strategy across both simulated and real-world robotic manipulation tasks. The findings demonstrate improvements over state-of-the-art alternatives.

#### 4.1. Simulation Experiments

##### 4.1.1. Benchmarks and Setup

- **RoboCasa Kitchen:** A benchmark with 24 kitchen manipulation tasks categorized into Relocation (10), Container (6), Rotation (5), and Button (3) tasks.
- **LIBERO:** A robot simulation benchmark comprising four task suites: LIBERO-Spatial, LIBERO-Object, LIBERO-Goal, and LIBERO-Long, with 10 tasks each.
- **LIBERO-Pro:** Used for evaluating robustness in out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios with position perturbations.
- **Baseline:** GR00T N1.5, which typically uses a fixed action horizon of 16 for training and inference. Comparisons were also made against GR00T with different fixed inference chunk sizes (h=2, 4, 8, 12).
- **Training:** Models were fine-tuned from publicly available GR00T N1.5 checkpoints on 100 MimicGen demo trajectories for RoboCasa (90 epochs) and jointly on all 40 LIBERO tasks (90 epochs). Eight NVIDIA A800 GPUs were used.
- **Evaluation:** 100 rollouts per task for RoboCasa, 50 per task for LIBERO.

##### 4.1.2. Quantitative Results

- **Comparison with Vanilla GR00T (Fixed h=16):**
 - **RoboCasa:** AAC improved the average task success rate by 2.3%, from 59.7% to 62.0%. Notably, the 'Rotation' task set, requiring fine-grained control, saw a significant increase from 57.6% to 61.4%. Improvements in 'Button' tasks were modest, consistent with their simpler action requirements.
 - **LIBERO:** AAC achieved a 0.9% average gain in success rate across the four task suites, from 94.1% to 95.0%. The most challenging 'LIBERO-Long' suite, involving multiple subtasks and longer action trajectories, showed a 4% improvement, indicating AAC's effectiveness for complex, long-horizon tasks.
- **Comparison with Fixed-size GR00T:** The experiments validated that a single fixed chunk size is suboptimal across different tasks. For example, LIBERO-Spatial performed best with h=4, while LIBERO-Goal achieved the highest success with h=16. AAC consistently outperformed all fixed-size baselines on average by adaptively selecting chunk sizes, eliminating the need for manual tuning.
- **Scalability to Different Backbones:** AAC was applied to the $\pi^{0.5}$ VLA backbone on LIBERO. It improved the average success rate from 97.0% to 97.9%, demonstrating its generalization capability across different diffusion-based VLA backbones.
- **Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Scenarios:** On the LIBERO-Pro benchmark with position perturbations, AAC enhanced robustness. For GR00T, the average success rate increased from 3.9% to 6.3%. For $\pi^{0.5}$, it improved from 30.9% to 34.8%, even as overall success rates decreased with increasing perturbation levels.
- **Impact of Number of Samples for Entropy Estimation:** The success rate improved continuously as the number of samples for estimating action entropy increased from 1 to 20 (94.1% to 95.0% on LIBERO). Beyond 20 samples, the improvement was marginal. Consequently, 20 samples were chosen for balancing computational efficiency and success rate.
- **Computational Efficiency:** The additional computational cost introduced by AAC due to parallel sampling of N candidate action chunks was deemed negligible. With 20 samples, AAC introduced approximately a 20 ms inference delay on a single A800 GPU.

##### 4.1.3. Qualitative Results

- **Distribution of Chunk Size Decisions (Figures 3 and 4):** Rollouts of chunk sizes chosen by AAC in a LIBERO-Spatial task (e.g., "Pick up the black bowl...") revealed an adaptive pattern aligning with human intuition.
 - Large chunk sizes were observed during initial phases (t < 20) and transportation stages (t > 50) when the gripper was far from the object or moving it to the target, allowing for fast, coarse movements.
 - Smaller chunk sizes were selected during critical manipulation stages (30 < t < 50), such as approaching and grasping the object, enabling precise, fine control with high-frequency observations and replanning.

#### 4.2. Real-world Experiments

##### 4.2.1. Setup

- **Hardware:** Realman single-arm robot with a Mycobot gripper, visual observations from wrist and side RGB views.
- **Data Collection:** 50 human-teleoperated demonstrations per task.
- **Training:** GR00T N1.5 checkpoint fine-tuned on each task for 300 epochs.
- **Evaluation:** 20 trials per task, with a limit of 600 action execution steps per trial.

##### 4.2.2. Tasks

1. **Banana Pick-and-Place:** Picking a banana and placing it into a randomly positioned rattan basket.
2. **Emergency Button Pressing (E-Button):** Pressing an E-Button, challenging due to unseen button positions during evaluation.
3. **Long-Horizon Toy Manipulation:** Picking a randomly placed toy, placing it into a drawer, and then closing the drawer.

##### 4.2.3. Results

- **Improved Real-world Task Success Rates:** AAC consistently outperformed the baseline GR00T across all three real-world tasks, achieving an average performance gain of 15% (from 67.0% to 82.0%).
 - **Banana Pick-and-Place:** AAC improved success rates from 70.0% to 90.0%, attributed to better gripper alignment for precise grasping.
 - **E-Button Pressing:** Success rates increased from 65.0% to 75.0%, indicating better generalization to near-unseen button locations.
 - **Long-Horizon Toy Manipulation:** Success rates improved from 65.0% to 80.0%, primarily due to higher success in the toy-picking stage, which benefits from adaptive chunking.
- **Improved Action Accuracy and Safety (Figure 6):** Qualitative observations showed that AAC prevented collisions with the tabletop, which occurred with vanilla GR00T due to low-quality actions. AAC was able to smoothly stop at an appropriate lowest point by filtering high-entropy or uncertain actions, demonstrating its potential for high-precision manipulation and improved safety.

In summary, the experimental results consistently demonstrate that AAC provides a robust and effective solution for dynamically adjusting action chunk sizes, leading to improved task success rates, enhanced precision, and better generalization across a wide array of simulated and real-world robotic manipulation tasks, without requiring additional model training.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Adaptive Action Chunking (AAC) strategy addresses a fundamental challenge in Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models: the critical trade-off between reactivity and temporal consistency imposed by fixed action chunk sizes. By proposing an inference-time adaptive mechanism based on action entropy, this work offers several significant contributions and potential impacts:

1. **Enhanced Performance and Robustness:** The consistent improvements in success rates across a diverse range of simulated benchmarks (RoboCasa, LIBERO) and real-world robotic tasks highlight the practical efficacy of AAC. The ability to adapt chunk sizes based on action uncertainty directly translates to more precise, reliable, and robust robotic manipulation, especially for complex and long-horizon tasks requiring both coarse and fine movements.

2. **Elimination of Manual Tuning:** Current VLA models often require laborious and empirical tuning of chunk sizes, which is task-dependent and non-scalable. AAC automates this process by dynamically determining the optimal chunk size, thereby reducing development overhead and enabling VLA models to be more easily deployed across varied scenarios without extensive manual recalibration.

3. **Improved Reactivity and Consistency:** The core mechanism of AAC—executing smaller chunks in high-uncertainty situations and larger chunks in confident ones—allows for a dynamic balance between reactivity to new information and maintaining temporal consistency. This adaptive behavior mirrors human intuition, where caution is exercised in uncertain situations, and confident, broader movements are made when the path is clear. This can lead to smoother and more intelligent robotic behaviors.

4. **Increased Safety and Precision:** Qualitative results in real-world experiments demonstrated AAC's ability to prevent collisions by adjusting chunk sizes to filter high-entropy, potentially low-quality actions. This capability is critical for safe deployment of robots in human-centric environments, as it enhances the precision and reliability of physical interactions.

5. **Generalizability and Scalability:** AAC is designed to operate solely at inference-time without requiring architectural modifications or additional training of the VLA model. This characteristic makes it highly generalizable to different diffusion-based VLA backbones (as demonstrated with $\pi^{0.5}$) and scalable to new tasks and environments. Its effectiveness in out-of-distribution scenarios further underscores its robustness.

6. **Foundation for Future Research:** The principle of using action entropy as a cue for adaptive control decisions opens avenues for further research in embodied AI. This approach could inspire similar uncertainty-aware strategies for other aspects of robotic control, such as adaptive replanning frequencies, state-dependent sensor fusion, or even human-robot interaction where the robot's confidence could influence its communicative behavior.

In conclusion, the Adaptive Action Chunking strategy presented in this paper represents a step towards more intelligent and autonomous robotic systems. By providing a simple yet effective method for dynamic control adjustment at inference-time, it enhances the practical applicability, reliability, and safety of VLA models, potentially influencing the design of future general-purpose robotic manipulation systems.
