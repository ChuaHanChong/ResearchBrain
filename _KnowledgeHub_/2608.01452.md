# DynamicManip: Enabling Dynamic Manipulation from a Single Static Demonstration

> [!summary] Summary
> DynamicManip, developed by researchers from Sun Yat-sen University and collaborators, enables robots to perform complex dynamic manipulation tasks by generating diverse training data from just one static demonstration. Its adaptive policy significantly reduces policy-query latency by 32.9% while improving mean task success rates by 18.4 percentage points on various dynamic benchmarks.

## Problem

- Learning robust policies for dynamic robot manipulation is challenging due to the need for continuous perception, tracking, and rapid real-time responses.
- Collecting sufficient high-quality, diverse training data for dynamic tasks is extremely time-consuming and expensive due to the combinatorial complexity of object motions and interaction timings.
- Existing policies often prioritize precision over inference speed, making them less suitable for the low-latency requirements of dynamic environments.

## Method

- A ==static-to-dynamic data augmentation== pipeline synthesizes diverse dynamic manipulation episodes from a single static demonstration by reconstructing geometries, extracting keyframes, and applying dynamic trajectory editing via ==phase operators==.
- A ==dynamic-aware adaptive== imitation learning policy, incorporating a stage classification head, dynamically adjusts its inference frequency and action execution length based on the predicted interaction stage to optimize for real-time performance.
- A new **DynamicManip** Benchmark is introduced, featuring diverse dynamic tasks in simulation and real-world, with a latency-aware automatic evaluation system to assess policies under realistic conditions.

## Results

- Policies trained on DynamicManip's augmented data (from one static demonstration) achieved comparable or superior success rates to those trained on **50-200** manually collected dynamic demonstrations, reducing human data collection effort by orders of magnitude.
- The dynamic-aware adaptive policy decreased mean policy-query latency by **32.9%** and increased mean task success rates by **18.4 percentage points** across five simulation tasks compared to a 3D-Diffusion-Policy baseline.
- Ablation studies confirmed that all components of the data augmentation pipeline cumulatively improve performance, and adaptive inference scheduling consistently outperformed non-adaptive strategies.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Diverse and physically consistent dynamic training data can be efficiently synthesized from minimal static input by intelligently reusing local interaction patterns and dynamically transforming object motions and robot trajectories.
> - Dynamically adjusting policy inference parameters, such as DDIM denoising schedules and action-chunk execution lengths, based on the robot's current interaction stage significantly improves both real-time responsiveness and task success.
> - The combination of a robust data generation pipeline and an adaptive, stage-aware policy is crucial for bridging the gap between limited data and successful execution in complex dynamic manipulation scenarios.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of general-purpose robots capable of operating in human environments is a prominent goal in robotics. A key component of this vision is robot manipulation, which has seen advancements through methods like Imitation Learning (IL) and Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. These approaches have demonstrated capabilities in learning from expert demonstrations and predicting action sequences. However, much of the existing research and benchmarks primarily focus on static or slowly changing environments, where target objects remain stationary or exhibit minor movements. In such scenarios, data collection is generally more manageable, and policies can tolerate moderate inference latency.

Dynamic manipulation, which involves interacting with objects in rapid or unpredictable motion (e.g., catching a falling object or sorting items on a conveyor belt), presents a significant challenge. This domain demands continuous perception, tracking, and rapid response through closed-loop perception-action cycles, imposing higher data burdens due to the need to capture diverse object motions, interaction timings, and temporal alignments. Additionally, real-time model inference is critical to cope with rapid state variations, a requirement not always met by traditional methods that may prioritize precision over inference speed.

Existing work on dynamic manipulation is comparatively limited and often focuses on structured scenarios with predictable motion, such as conveyor-belt environments (e.g., DBC-TFP, GEM). While concurrent VLA approaches (e.g., RDT-2, RTVLA, VLASH) have shown real-time interaction with fast-moving targets, they often operate with large contact margins, lacking the precision required for fine manipulation. Efforts like DynamicVLA have attempted to address these issues, but a gap remains between current robotic capabilities and the demands of general real-world dynamic manipulation.

Data collection and augmentation are critical for learning robust manipulation policies, but obtaining high-quality, diverse data for dynamic tasks is particularly expensive due to the combinatorial variation in object dynamics. Existing data augmentation methods, such as 2D visual augmentation, implicit 3D reconstruction, and geometric transformations, often face limitations. 2D methods do not enforce 3D consistency, implicit 3D methods may not adapt action trajectories, geometric approaches are typically restricted to rigid global transformations, and simulation-based methods can suffer from sim-to-real discrepancies. Trajectory-level editing approaches, like DemoGen, have explored variations for tabletop tasks but generally focus on quasi-static changes, not the complex temporal dynamics of dynamic manipulation.

DynamicManip positions itself within this landscape by addressing two fundamental challenges: the substantial data requirements imposed by the combinatorial complexity of dynamic scenarios and the need for real-time and accurate policy execution in the face of rapid dynamic variations. It proposes a method for synthesizing diverse, physically consistent dynamic demonstration data from a minimal static input and an adaptive policy that can execute these tasks efficiently, aiming to bridge the gap in current robotic manipulation capabilities for dynamic environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching goal of this research is to enable robots to effectively perform dynamic manipulation in complex and dynamic environments, where objects are in motion or require rapid interaction adjustments. This capability is considered critical for deploying robots in everyday human environments and integrating them into daily workflows.

The motivation for this work stems from two primary challenges inherent in learning models for dynamic manipulation tasks:
1. **Combinatorial Complexity and Data Requirements:** Dynamic scenarios involve a vast range of possible object velocities, trajectories, and robot response timings. This combinatorial explosion necessitates substantial amounts of diverse training data. However, collecting such data through manual teleoperation is inherently difficult, time-consuming, and expensive, making it a significant bottleneck for developing robust dynamic manipulation policies.
2. **Real-time Policy Execution:** Dynamic tasks demand policies that can perceive, track, and respond to rapidly evolving object states in real-time. Traditional robot manipulation methods often train on quasi-static datasets and may compromise inference speed to achieve precision. This trade-off renders them less suitable for dynamic environments where low-latency and continuous closed-loop perception-action cycles are essential for successful task completion.

To address these challenges, DynamicManip establishes three key objectives:
1. **Efficient Data Augmentation:** To mitigate the data scarcity problem, the first objective is to develop a static-to-dynamic data augmentation pipeline. This pipeline aims to synthesize diverse dynamic manipulation demonstrations from a single, minimally collected static demonstration, while preserving robot behavior patterns and underlying physical consistency.
2. **Low-Latency Adaptive Policy:** To overcome real-time execution limitations, the second objective is to introduce a dynamic-aware adaptive imitation learning policy. This policy is designed to adjust its inference frequency and computational effort according to the evolving task dynamics, thereby enabling responsive and effective dynamic manipulation with improved efficiency.
3. **Comprehensive Benchmark Development:** To facilitate systematic research and objective evaluation in dynamic manipulation, the third objective is to construct a dedicated dynamic manipulation benchmark. This benchmark includes diverse dynamic tasks and an automatic evaluation system to ensure scalable and consistent assessment of dynamic manipulation policies.

Through these objectives, the research aims to significantly improve data efficiency and real-time performance in dynamic manipulation tasks, contributing to the development of more capable and deployable robotic systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

DynamicManip addresses the challenges of dynamic manipulation by integrating an efficient data generation pipeline with a low-latency, adaptive policy. The methodology is structured around two main components: a static-to-dynamic data augmentation pipeline and a dynamic-aware imitation learning policy, supported by a new benchmark.

#### 3.1. Static-to-Dynamic Data Augmentation

The data augmentation pipeline converts a single static task demonstration into a diverse set of dynamic episodes. This process operates on the principle that local interaction patterns from a static demonstration can be reused and adapted to dynamic scenarios by modifying object motions, phase anchors, and transition targets. The pipeline involves three main steps:

1. **Geometric Reconstruction:**
 - **Object Geometry:** A 3D CAD mesh of the manipulated object is generated from an RGB image and aligned to the observed object point cloud. This alignment uses OBB-based coarse initialization followed by multi-resolution ICP refinement. The aligned mesh is then resampled into a canonical object point cloud, allowing coherent transformations.
 - **Robot Geometry:** The robot's geometry is obtained from its known URDF model, and its point cloud is rendered based on joint configurations via forward kinematics.
 - This step provides a complete, consistent, and editable 3D scene representation.

2. **Keyframe Extraction:** The static demonstration is decomposed into reusable phase segments by sparsely annotating keyframes. These keyframes delineate phases such as approach, interaction, and disengagement (labeled intuitively as start, grasp, interact, recover), serving as anchors for transforming and recomposing motion segments.

3. **Dynamic Trajectory Editing:** This is the core of the augmentation, where the system synthesizes dynamic episodes by reusing source interaction segments under newly sampled object poses, motions, and transition targets. Task-specific augmentation parameters (ξk) are sampled from a distribution (D) to guide the process. Four phase operators are instantiated and composed:
 - **Static Object Acquisition (Φ_static):** For initial static grasping phases, a rigid transformation (T_static_k) is applied to the entire segment, increasing spatial diversity while preserving relative robot-object motion.
 - **Dynamic Target Alignment (Φ_dyn):** For aligning the end-effector with a moving object or predicted target, a time-varying interaction target (g_k(t)) is computed based on synthesized object motion (T_k_obj(t)) and task-specific rules (e.g., object-frame relative pose, predicted landing point). A motion planner then generates a smooth, kinematically feasible trajectory to reach these dynamic target-conditioned poses.
 - **Contact Interaction Execution (Φ_inter):** For short, contact-rich motions (e.g., dynamic grasping, striking), the local interaction motion from the static demonstration is replayed relative to a new anchor frame (A_k), which is determined by the preceding Φ_dyn phase. This preserves fine local motion patterns at different object positions.
 - **Pose-Conditioned Transition (Φ_trans):** For movements between segments (e.g., object transport, returning to reset), a motion planner generates a feasible trajectory from the current state to a transformed target pose (e_k_key = T_trans_k * e_key), connecting edited interaction segments continuously.
 These phase operators are composed to form complete dynamic trajectories (e.g., Φ_static → (Φ_dyn → Φ_inter → Φ_trans)L). Each timestep is automatically assigned a stage label corresponding to the phase operator that generated it.

#### 3.2. Dynamic-aware Imitation Learning Policy

The policy is trained on the augmented, stage-labeled data to achieve both accuracy and responsiveness.

1. **Dynamic-stage Awareness Learning:** A lightweight classification head (h_ϕ) is attached to the shared observation encoder of a diffusion-based policy. This head predicts the current interaction stage (ℓ_t ∈ {Φ_static, Φ_dyn, Φ_inter, Φ_trans}) from visual observations. It is supervised by a cross-entropy loss (L_stage) using the automatically generated stage labels. The total training objective combines this auxiliary loss with the primary diffusion loss (L_diff): L = L_diff + λL_stage, where λ balances action fidelity and stage awareness.

2. **Dynamic-adaptive Inference:** During deployment, the trained stage classifier predicts the current interaction stage. Based on this prediction, the policy dynamically adjusts its computational allocation for DDIM sampling and action execution:
 - **Denoising Schedule (TaylorSeer Acceleration):** The policy adaptively controls the frequency of full forward computations in the diffusion model. For intermediate denoising steps, a Taylor-based acceleration mechanism approximates residual block outputs, skipping expensive forward passes and reducing inference latency.
 - **Action-Chunk Execution Length:** This parameter (n_exec) dictates how many predicted actions from an action chunk are executed before the next policy query. Shorter chunks provide more frequent feedback, while longer chunks reduce inference calls.
 - **Heuristic Stage-conditioned Rules:** Specific parameters for TaylorSeer (computation interval τ, expansion order K) and action-chunk length (n_exec) are assigned based on the predicted stage:
 - **Φ_static:** Conservative schedule (frequent recomputation, short n_exec) to minimize early grasp failures.
 - **Φ_dyn:** Aggressive Taylor configuration (large τ, short n_exec) for minimal closed-loop latency to track fast-moving targets.
 - **Φ_inter:** Conservative setting (smaller τ, longer n_exec) to prioritize temporal coherence and smoothness for contact quality.
 - **Φ_trans:** Most aggressive acceleration (largest τ, higher K, full n_exec) to minimize computational overhead during non-critical movements.

#### 3.3. DynamicManip Benchmark

To support evaluation, a benchmark is developed on the RoboTwin 2.0 platform.
- **Tasks:** Five diverse dynamic tasks are designed for simulation (Dynamic Tapping, Belt Picking, Object Catching, Mole Whacking, Goal Blocking) and four for the real world (Dynamic Tapping, Belt Picking, Boat Loading, Bottle Catching).
- **Data Collection:** The RoboTwin 2.0 pipeline is extended with a dynamic-aware heuristic planner and a contact-release mechanism to enable the robot to track and interact with moving objects effectively.
- **Latency-Aware Automatic Evaluation:** A task-specific evaluator detects success using physical signatures and reports metrics like success rates and response latency. Crucially, the simulator advances by a duration proportional to the policy inference time (N_delay = round(t_infer/∆t_sim)) before applying actions, simulating a more realistic real-time scenario.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were conducted in both simulation and real-world environments to evaluate the framework's data efficiency, real-time performance, and the contributions of its key components.

#### 4.1. Data Efficiency (Q1: Can Augmented Data Match or Exceed Human Demonstrations)

- **Simulation Analysis (Table 1):** Policies trained on DynamicManip's augmented episodes, derived from a single static demonstration, achieved success rates comparable to or higher than policies trained on significantly larger sets of manually collected simulated dynamic demonstrations. For instance, policies trained on 50 or 200 augmented episodes (from 1 static demo) often surpassed the performance of policies trained on 50 or 200 directly collected simulated demonstrations. This suggests the augmentation pipeline effectively expands limited static data into diverse and useful dynamic training data.
- **Real-world Validation (Table 2):** In real-world settings, starting from a single static demonstration, increasing the number of augmented episodes consistently improved policy performance. Policies trained on 200 augmented episodes from one static demonstration achieved success rates that surpassed models trained on 50 manually teleoperated dynamic demonstrations across all evaluated tasks (Dynamic Tapping, Belt Picking, Boat Loading, Bottle Catching).
- **Human Effort and Collection Time (Table 3):** The DynamicManip pipeline substantially reduced human involvement. A single static demonstration required approximately 30 seconds of human teleoperation and about 10 minutes for pre-generation processing. The subsequent automated generation of 200 augmented episodes required roughly 30 minutes of machine time with no human intervention. In contrast, collecting 50 to 200 manual dynamic demonstrations typically required 2 human operators and between 100 to 400 minutes of human time.

**Summary for Q1:** DynamicManip successfully synthesizes effective dynamic training data from a single static demonstration. Policies trained on this augmented data achieve performance comparable to, and in some cases exceeding, that of policies trained on significantly larger volumes of conventionally collected dynamic demonstrations, both in simulation and real-world scenarios. This is achieved with a notable reduction in human data collection effort and cost.

#### 4.2. Real-Time Performance of the Dynamic-Aware Policy (Q2: How does our dynamic-aware policy perform in terms of reaction speeds and task success rates)

- **Latency and Success Rate Comparison (Table 4):** Compared against the 3D-Diffusion-Policy (DP3) baseline under a latency-aware evaluation system, DynamicManip demonstrated improvements across five simulation tasks.
 - **Latency:** DynamicManip reduced the mean policy-query latency by 32.9%. For example, in Dynamic Tapping, latency dropped from 60.0ms (DP3) to 38.0ms (Ours), and in Object Catching, from 61.4ms to 36.0ms.
 - **Success Rate:** The mean success rate increased by 18.4 percentage points. Notable improvements included Goal Blocking (+46 percentage points, from 42% to 88%) and Dynamic Tapping (+22 percentage points, from 20% to 42%).
- **Conclusion for Q2:** The stage-aware adaptive inference mechanism within DynamicManip improved real-time responsiveness significantly (lower latency) while simultaneously enhancing task success rates across diverse dynamic manipulation tasks under a realistic latency-aware execution protocol.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies on Data Generation and Inference Scheduling (Q3: How do the key data-generation components and inference-scheduling variants affect task performance)

- **Cumulative Data Generation (Table 5):** Ablation studies on data generation components (Spatial Only, Quasi-static, Planned Motion, Local Interaction Replay) showed that each successive addition contributed positively to task performance (success rate). Planned dynamic motion provided the largest incremental gain, with local interaction replay offering further improvement. This indicates the complementary nature and effectiveness of the various components of the data augmentation pipeline.
- **Inference Scheduling (Table 5):** When comparing different inference scheduling strategies using the same trained policy checkpoint, the "predicted-stage scheduling" strategy (DynamicManip's adaptive approach) outperformed uniform DDIM sampling, fixed Taylor acceleration, and short-chunk execution variants on both evaluated tasks (Object Catching and Goal Blocking). This suggests that adaptively adjusting denoising, Taylor acceleration, and action-chunk length based on the predicted interaction stage is more effective than fixed schedules.

**Summary for Q3:** The detailed ablation studies confirmed that all components of the data generation pipeline contribute to improved performance. Furthermore, the dynamic-aware inference scheduling, which adaptively adjusts computational parameters based on the interaction stage, is more effective than non-adaptive or partially adaptive approaches.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The DynamicManip framework presents a significant advancement in the field of robot manipulation, particularly for tasks in dynamic and unstructured environments. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Addressing Core Bottlenecks:** The work directly tackles two fundamental challenges in dynamic robot manipulation: the scarcity of diverse training data and the critical need for low-latency, real-time policy execution. By providing solutions to these bottlenecks, DynamicManip contributes to making dynamic manipulation more feasible and robust.

2. **Enhanced Data Efficiency:** The static-to-dynamic data augmentation pipeline represents a major step towards data-efficient robot learning. The ability to synthesize diverse, physically plausible dynamic demonstrations from a single static human demonstration substantially reduces the manual effort, time, and cost associated with data collection. This can democratize access to training data for complex dynamic tasks, accelerating research and development in the field.

3. **Improved Real-Time Performance and Reliability:** The dynamic-aware adaptive policy, with its stage-aware inference and Taylor-based acceleration, demonstrates marked improvements in both policy-query latency and task success rates. By adaptively allocating computational resources, the policy achieves faster reaction times without compromising precision, which is crucial for successful interaction with fast-moving or rapidly changing objects in real-world scenarios. This directly contributes to more reliable and responsive robotic systems.

4. **Advancement in Generalizability:** The method of preserving local robot-object interaction patterns while varying object motion, spatial placement, and interaction timing via the augmentation pipeline, helps policies learn the underlying dynamics robustly. This approach has the potential to enhance the generalizability of learned manipulation skills to novel dynamic situations.

5. **New Research Standard and Benchmarking:** The introduction of the DynamicManip Benchmark, featuring diverse dynamic tasks and a latency-aware automatic evaluation system, provides a standardized and scalable platform for assessing policies. This benchmark can foster future research by enabling more consistent, objective, and realistic comparisons of different dynamic manipulation approaches.

6. **Paving the Way for Real-World Deployment:** By effectively bridging the gap between minimal static data and complex reactive execution, DynamicManip takes a robust and scalable step towards deploying robots in complex, unstructured, and fast-paced human environments. This includes applications such as assisting with household chores involving dynamic objects, industrial sorting on conveyor belts, or even tasks requiring interception of moving items.

In conclusion, DynamicManip offers a comprehensive framework that addresses critical limitations in current robot manipulation capabilities. Its dual focus on efficient data augmentation and adaptive, low-latency control has the potential to significantly advance the practicality and robustness of robots operating in dynamic real-world settings.
