# TAGA: Terrain-aware Active Gaze Learning for Generalizable Agile Humanoid Locomotion

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the National University of Singapore, Zhejiang University, and South China University of Technology developed TAGA, a framework for agile humanoid locomotion that integrates multimodal perception with an emergent active gaze mechanism. The system enabled a Unitree G1 humanoid robot to traverse a 120 cm gap, representing a 50% increase over previous reported maximums for perceptive humanoids, while maintaining stability across diverse challenging terrains.

## Problem

- Humanoid locomotion in complex terrains requires balancing wide perceptual coverage for anticipatory planning with precise local geometry understanding for reliable foot placement.
- Existing perceptive methods struggle with computational costs of broad coverage or miss critical local terrain information directly beneath the robot.
- Effectively integrating complementary long-range visual cues with accurate local geometric details has been a challenge, with many approaches loosely combining or relying on single sources.

## Method

- **TAGA** integrates egocentric depth vision (for long-range preview), height scans (for precise local geometry), and proprioception into a unified sensing and control pipeline.
- It introduces an emergent, anticipatory ==active gaze== module that dynamically predicts a task-relevant ==Region of Interest== (ROI) within the local height scan, guiding the policy's attention.
- The system employs a hierarchical neural network architecture with visuomotor fusion, a ==Mixture-of-Experts== action decoder, and is trained using PPO with Adversarial Motion Priors and extensive domain randomization.

## Results

- The TAGA framework achieved a **120 cm** gap traversal distance on a physical Unitree G1 humanoid robot, surpassing previous reported maximums for perceptive humanoids by **50%**.
- It demonstrated robust and generalizable locomotion across diverse challenging terrains, including sparse stepping stones (**70 cm** spacing), narrow beams, stairs, and unstructured outdoor environments, even under perceptual disturbances and external pushes.
- The active gaze module enabled performance comparable to processing a full height scan but with **65.2%** lower training cost, while significantly outperforming fixed-gaze policies on tasks requiring distant foothold planning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - An anticipatory, task-relevant active gaze mechanism can emerge purely through reinforcement learning, without explicit supervision, dynamically focusing perception to improve information density and decision-making.
> - Multimodal fusion of depth vision and local height scans provides complementary information crucial for both long-range anticipatory planning and precise foothold placement in discontinuous terrains.
> - Adversarial Motion Priors contribute to more natural, coordinated, and dynamically stable locomotion, which enhances robustness to sim-to-real variations and real-world disturbances.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Humanoid locomotion in complex, unstructured environments requires robots to perceive their surroundings, assess terrain traversability, and adapt their motion strategies in real-time. This presents a significant challenge, particularly in balancing wide perceptual coverage for anticipatory planning with precise local geometry understanding for reliable foot placement.

Existing perceptive locomotion methods generally fall into two categories:
- **Mapping-based methods:** These approaches utilize explicit geometric terrain representations such as height scans, elevation maps, or voxel grids. They typically provide strong local terrain awareness and enable precise foothold placement for challenging tasks like stepping stones and gap traversal. However, their computational cost often increases with an expanded perception range, limiting the trade-off between local detail and broad coverage. Their performance can also be susceptible to map quality issues, localization uncertainty, occlusions, or deficient perception.
- **Vision-based methods:** These methods directly map raw depth images or RGB-D inputs to actions, reducing the reliance on explicit terrain reconstruction. They have facilitated agile locomotion in various challenging terrains. However, forward-facing depth images can miss crucial terrain information directly beneath or near the robot's feet, which is necessary for precise sparse foothold placement. Additionally, recurrent memory mechanisms used in these approaches often struggle to retain fine-grained geometric details over extended horizons.

The current research indicates that these two perceptual paradigms offer complementary information: vision provides look-ahead awareness for distant terrain, while height scans offer accurate local geometry for foot placement. Many existing approaches either rely on a single source or combine them loosely, which may not fully leverage their combined strengths or align long-range visual cues with local geometric details effectively.

Drawing inspiration from human and animal locomotion, where gaze is actively directed towards task-relevant regions, this work proposes an active perception mechanism. This mechanism aims to address the limitations of current systems by dynamically determining where to focus perception and which information is most relevant for subsequent movements. Prior work in active perception has explored selective terrain encoding and exteroceptive-proprioceptive fusion, and adaptive perception for robustness. However, these methods often emphasize short-horizon reasoning, leaving the proactive allocation of perception across both nearby footholds and future terrain structures relatively underexplored. TAGA aims to bridge this gap by integrating multimodal perception with an emergent, anticipatory active gaze mechanism for generalizable agile humanoid locomotion.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research is the observation that agile humanoid locomotion across diverse and challenging terrain necessitates both extensive perceptual coverage and precise understanding of local geometry. This demand is often difficult to meet simultaneously under tight onboard computational constraints. The authors are inspired by biological systems, specifically how humans and animals selectively focus their gaze on task-relevant terrain features (e.g., footholds, gaps, obstacles) based on the immediate situation, thereby enhancing information density and decision-making.

The key objectives of the TAGA framework are:

1. **Develop a unified sensing and control pipeline:** To integrate egocentric depth vision, height scans, and proprioception for humanoids operating in challenging terrains with onboard computation. This aims to leverage the complementary strengths of these modalities: vision for long-range preview and height scans for precise local geometry.
2. **Enable emergent, active gaze behavior:** To facilitate the learning of an anticipatory active gaze mechanism without explicit supervision or pre-defined gaze targets. This mechanism should guide the policy to dynamically select and attend to the most task-relevant regions of the height scan based on multimodal cues (vision and proprioception).
3. **Improve information density and efficiency:** By selectively attending to relevant terrain regions and routing these localized observations to the downstream locomotion policy, the framework aims to increase the effective information density of observations. This approach seeks to reduce interference from irrelevant terrain data and simplify the learning process for the locomotion policy, particularly under computational constraints.
4. **Enhance robustness and generalization:** To develop a policy that demonstrates robust and generalizable locomotion across a diverse range of challenging terrains, including large gaps, narrow beams, sparse stepping stones, stairs, and unstructured outdoor environments, even under perceptual disturbances and environmental interference.
5. **Achieve competitive real-world performance:** To deploy the trained policy on a physical humanoid robot and demonstrate leading performance metrics in real-world scenarios, particularly in tasks such as gap traversal and sparse foothold navigation, compared to previously reported methods.

In essence, the research is motivated by the need for more intelligent and efficient perception in humanoid robots to enable agile movement in complex environments, mirroring the adaptive perceptual strategies observed in nature.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

TAGA formulates humanoid perceptive locomotion as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP). The policy, $\pi(a_t|o_t)$, maps a multimodal observation $o_t$ to an action $a_t$, representing target joint positions at 50 Hz, which are then tracked by a low-level 200 Hz PD controller.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The observation $o_t$ consists of:
- **Proprioception history ($p_H_t$):** A five-frame history of the robot's internal state, including torso angular velocity ($\omega_{b,t}$), projected gravity vector ($g_{b,t}$), joint positions ($q_t$) and velocities ($\dot{q}_t$), previous action ($a_{t-1}$), and command ($c_t = (v^x_{cmd,t}, v^y_{cmd,t}, \dot{\psi}_{cmd,t})$).
- **Depth image ($d_t \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times 36 \times 64}$):** A forward-facing depth image providing long-range terrain preview.
- **Height scan ($h_{xyz,t} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 21 \times 21}$):** A local, egocentric height scan representing the terrain geometry around the robot's feet.

#### 3.2. Neural Network Design (TAGA Architecture)

The TAGA policy is parameterized by a neural network with a hierarchical gaze mechanism (Figure 3).
- **Initial Encoders:**
 - A CNN-based **depth encoder ($\phi_d$)** processes the depth image $d_t$ to produce a visual embedding $e_{d,t} \in \mathbb{R}^{128}$, capturing distant terrain awareness.
 - An MLP-based **proprioception encoder ($\phi_p$)** processes the proprioception history $p_{H,t}$ to produce a proprioceptive embedding $e_{p,t} \in \mathbb{R}^{128}$, encoding the robot's dynamic state and command context.

- **Task-Relevant Active Gaze Module:**
 - This module is designed to filter irrelevant terrain information and direct perception to the most informative region for the next locomotion step.
 - A lightweight **prediction head ($f_{roi}$)** takes the visual and proprioceptive embeddings as input and predicts a normalized two-dimensional gaze location $r_t = f_{roi}([e_{d,t}, e_{p,t}])$, where $r_t \in^2$.
 - The predicted gaze location $r_t$ is then mapped onto the full local height-scan grid ($h_{xyz,t}$) to crop a compact terrain patch, $\tilde{h}_{xyz,t} = \text{Crop}(h_{xyz,t}, r_t)$, with a size $K=11$. The cropping operation is implemented using differentiable bilinear grid sampling, allowing end-to-end optimization with the policy.
 - A **boundary penalty ($L_{roi}$)** is applied to discourage the gaze from fixating near the edges of the height scan.

- **Visuomotor Fusion Encoder:**
 - This component further emphasizes terrain cues relevant to the immediate locomotion decision.
 - A lightweight CNN extracts features from the cropped height scan $\tilde{h}_{xyz,t}$. Corresponding spatial coordinates are embedded by an MLP. These are fused to obtain pointwise terrain embeddings $E_{m,t} \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times K \times 128}$, serving as key and value vectors.
 - A **projection head ($f_{proj}$)** generates a query vector from the concatenated visual and proprioceptive embeddings $([e_{p,t}, e_{d,t}])$.
 - A multi-head cross-attention layer combines the query and terrain embeddings to produce a **fusion embedding ($e_{pg,t} \in \mathbb{R}^{128}$)**, representing a terrain-aware visuomotor representation.

- **Action Decoder:**
 - Implemented as a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) module with $N_e = 5$ experts, designed to increase policy expressiveness through adaptive action composition.
 - The actor input is $e_{\pi,t} = [e_{p,t}, e_{pg,t}]$.
 - A gating network $g(\cdot)$ computes soft expert weights $\alpha_{i,t} = \text{Softmax}(g(e_{\pi,t}))_i$.
 - The final action $a_t$ is a weighted combination of expert outputs: $a_t = \sum_{i=1}^{N_e} \alpha_{i,t} E_i(e_{\pi,t})$. This allows input-dependent adaptation of expert contributions.

#### 3.3. Training TAGA Policy via Reinforcement Learning

TAGA is trained using asymmetric actor-critic Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) in massively parallel simulation (Isaac Lab) on NVIDIA GPUs. The actor receives partial observations, while the critic has access to privileged information (ground-truth base linear velocity and the full uncropped height scan).
- **Terrain Design and Curriculum:** A diverse set of challenging terrains is used, including gaps, stairs, sparse footholds, narrow beams, obstacles, and slopes. Each terrain type has 10 difficulty levels. Robots are progressively promoted to harder terrains upon successful traversal and demoted upon failure.
- **Task Reward and Safety Constraints:** The base task reward ($r^t_{env}$) combines command tracking, posture and joint regularization, contact-related terms, and safety-oriented penalties. An **Adversarial Motion Prior (AMP) reward ($r^t_{AMP}$)**, derived from a discriminator trained on motion capture data (AMASS), encourages human-like gait and stable control. Early termination conditions are applied for physically unsafe states (e.g., illegal non-foot contacts, excessive torso tilt, low base height, high hip-link acceleration during foot contact).
- **Two-stage Training:**
 1. **Stage 1:** Policy is trained in a clean simulation without observation noise or domain randomization to efficiently acquire core locomotion skills.
 2. **Stage 2 (Fine-tuning):** The policy is fine-tuned from the Stage 1 checkpoint with a reduced entropy coefficient and extensive deployment-oriented domain randomization. This includes variations in actuation (payload, CoM perturbations, motor delays, friction, restitution), external pushes, and various observation degradations (proprioceptive noise, depth camera artifacts, height scan noise and drift, terrain perturbations).
- **Loss Function:** The full training objective combines the PPO surrogate loss on the augmented reward $\tilde{r} = r^t_{env} + \eta r^t_{AMP}$ with a value loss ($L_{value}$), an entropy bonus ($H(\pi_\theta)$), and two auxiliary terms:
 - **MoE–Terrain Contrastive Loss ($L_{con}$):** Encourages terrain-aware soft routing in the MoE by aligning the actor-gate embedding with a full height-scan embedding in a shared prototype space.
 - **Gaze Boundary Penalty ($L_{roi}$):** Prevents the learned gaze from degenerately selecting crops near the height-scan boundary by penalizing ROI centers too close to the edge.
- **Symmetry Augmentation:** Proprioceptive observations, height scans, AMP states, and actions are mirrored, and horizontally flipped depth views are used to encourage symmetric locomotion and improve training efficiency.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The TAGA framework was evaluated extensively in both simulation and real-world environments, demonstrating capabilities in agile and robust humanoid locomotion across diverse challenging terrains.

#### 4.1. Simulation Evaluation

Simulation experiments were conducted to assess locomotion capabilities and robustness, addressing three specific questions:
- **Q1: Complementary Nature of Multimodal Perception:**
 - Comparison of TAGA with CReF (vision-based baseline) and TAGA-HSOnly (height scan only).
 - TAGA demonstrated performance comparable to or surpassing CReF on gaps, beams, and high platforms, and substantially outperformed it on stepping stones. This suggests that vision alone is insufficient for tasks requiring precise foot placement on sparse footholds, where accurate local geometry (provided by height scans) is crucial.
 - TAGA-HSOnly struggled with gaps, indicating that height-scan-only perception lacks the anticipatory preview needed for distant discontinuities and landing regions.
 - These results support the hypothesis that vision and height scans provide complementary information, with vision offering anticipatory preview and height scans providing local geometric precision.
 - Under out-of-distribution (OOD) terrain challenges (C1, C2), TAGA achieved success rates of 93.70% and 93.90% respectively, indicating strong robustness and generalization across novel terrain combinations.

- **Q2: Advantages of the Active Gaze Module:**
 - Visualization of the learned gaze behavior (Figure 4) showed dynamic shifts in the predicted Region of Interest (ROI). During gap crossing, the gaze shifted from the current support region to the opposite edge, resembling anticipatory gaze in humans. On stepping stones and beams, the gaze moved towards sparse footholds or traversable strips, while on continuous terrains, it remained local to cover nearby height changes.
 - Ablation studies confirmed the gaze module's effectiveness. TAGA achieved performance comparable to TAGA-FullScan (which processes the entire height scan) but with 65.2% lower training cost.
 - TAGA-InactiveGaze, which used a fixed ROI, exhibited significant performance degradation on gaps and stepping stones, where distant footholds often lay outside its static perceptual window. This demonstrates the critical role of dynamically selected, task-relevant terrain regions.

- **Q3: Impact of AMP Priors on Motion Quality:**
 - The removal of AMP priors (TAGA-NoAMP) did not substantially reduce task completion rates across terrains.
 - However, TAGA-NoAMP policies displayed noticeable degradation in motion quality, characterized by unnatural movements such as inward knee collapse and short, shuffling steps, particularly during turning and terrain transitions (Figure 5).
 - In real-world deployment, TAGA-NoAMP proved less robust to sim-to-real variations and small disturbances, indicating that AMP priors contribute to more natural, coordinated, and dynamically stable locomotion beneficial for hardware robustness.

#### 4.2. Real-World Evaluation

TAGA was deployed on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot with onboard inference (NVIDIA Jetson Orin) for real-world validation.
- **Indoor Evaluation:**
 - TAGA demonstrated capabilities in highly discontinuous indoor terrains requiring precise foothold planning and momentum generation.
 - The robot successfully traversed 120 cm gaps, which is the largest reported real-world gap-crossing distance among perceptive humanoid locomotion systems, surpassing previous bests by 50%.
 - It balanced on narrow footholds and traversed sparse blocks with 50-70 cm spacing and 10 cm height variation.
 - Emergent terrain-adaptive behaviors were observed, such as pushing off from the edge of a block for extra momentum during gap crossing and knee bending to absorb impact upon landing, all without explicit hand-crafted motions.
- **Outdoor Evaluation:**
 - TAGA was tested in outdoor and low-light environments, which introduced varied ground materials, changing illumination, background clutter, depth degradation, sensor noise, and occluded footholds (e.g., in tall grass).
 - Despite these challenges, the robot maintained stable locomotion across various outdoor terrain types, demonstrating robustness to real-world perception noise and contact variations.
 - The system also exhibited stability under external disturbances (kicks and pushes), absorbing perturbations, recovering balance, and continuing locomotion.
- **Comparative Performance (Table 2):** TAGA demonstrated competitive performance across a wider range of real-world terrain categories compared to many existing perceptive humanoid and biped locomotion methods, notably achieving leading metrics for gap crossing (120 cm) and sparse foothold traversal (70 cm spacing on uneven terrain).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The TAGA framework presents a notable advancement in the field of agile humanoid locomotion, particularly in its approach to integrating multimodal perception with an adaptive attention mechanism.

**Key significance points include:**
- **Emergent Active Gaze:** A primary contribution is the demonstration that an anticipatory, task-relevant active gaze mechanism can emerge purely through reinforcement learning, without requiring explicit supervision or pre-defined gaze targets. This finding suggests a pathway for more autonomous and adaptable robotic perception systems, reducing the need for extensive hand-engineering of perceptual strategies.
- **Enhanced Perceptual Efficiency:** By dynamically cropping and focusing on task-relevant regions of the height scan, TAGA addresses the trade-off between wide perceptual coverage and fine-grained local geometry understanding. This approach increases the effective information density of observations while operating within onboard computational constraints, making it practical for real-world deployment on resource-limited hardware.
- **Robustness and Generalizability:** The system exhibits high levels of robustness to perceptual disturbances, environmental interference, and dynamic variations, as demonstrated by its performance in challenging real-world outdoor conditions and under external pushes. Its generalization across diverse and novel terrains in simulation indicates a learned understanding of locomotion principles rather than mere memorization of specific terrain features.
- **State-of-the-Art Hardware Performance:** The deployment on a Unitree G1 robot showcased leading real-world performance metrics, notably achieving the largest reported gap traversal distance (1.2 m) and robust sparse foothold traversal (70 cm spacing) among perceptive humanoid locomotion systems. These accomplishments push the boundaries of current humanoid capabilities in navigating highly discontinuous environments.
- **Improved Motion Quality with AMP:** While not directly tied to perception, the integration of Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP) significantly contributes to the naturalness, coordination, and dynamic stability of the robot's gait, which is crucial for successful and durable real-world operation.

**Potential impact:**
The development of TAGA has several potential impacts. It offers a more efficient and effective paradigm for robot perception in complex environments, where selective attention can reduce computational load and improve decision-making. This could accelerate the deployment of humanoids in unstructured settings such as disaster relief, construction, exploration, or logistics, where navigating uneven and discontinuous terrain is common. The emergent nature of the gaze mechanism could inspire future research in self-supervised perceptual learning for robotics. Furthermore, the robust performance on real hardware, especially the demonstrated capabilities in extreme gap crossing and sparse foothold negotiation, establishes a new benchmark for agile humanoid locomotion and provides a foundation for developing even more versatile and autonomous robotic systems. The methodology also highlights the benefits of combining high-level vision with precise local geometry, a strategy that could be adopted and adapted across various types of legged robots.
