# Differentiable Material Point Method for the Control of Deformable Objects

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from ZHAW and ETH Zürich developed a differentiable Material Point Method (MPM) simulator, adapted with Runge-Kutta 4 integration, to accurately model mechanical energy in deformable objects at coarser resolutions. This simulator enabled gradient-based trajectory optimization for active damping, achieving faster kinetic energy reduction and improved computational efficiency compared to a sampling-based method.

## Problem

- Controlling deformable objects is challenging due to their complex, non-linear dynamics and effectively infinite-dimensional state and configuration spaces.
- Existing differentiable Material Point Method (MPM) simulators suffer from significant numerical dissipation at low spatial resolutions, leading to inaccurate mechanical energy modeling.
- Dynamic control problems with time-varying objectives, such as active damping, were underexplored with differentiable physics simulators that prioritize energy accuracy.

## Method

- A modified ==Fluid Implicit Particle (FLIP) Material Point Method (MPM)== was developed, which rearranges the algorithm and incorporates ==Runge-Kutta-4 (RK4) integration== for particle state advancement.
- The simulator was implemented using the ==JAX== framework to enable ==automatic differentiation== and leverage GPU acceleration for efficient gradient computation.
- ==Gradient-based trajectory optimization==, utilizing the ==Adam optimizer==, was applied to minimize kinetic energy for active damping by adjusting control inputs over time.

## Results

- The developed MPM with RK4 robustly conserved total mechanical energy, whereas a standard FLIP MPM exhibited **88%** energy dissipation within 10 seconds.
- In an active damping task, the proposed method reduced kinetic energy by **80%** approximately **2.7 times faster** than the MPPI baseline and achieved a **20%** lower mean kinetic energy in the damped state.
- The differentiable simulator demonstrated significantly higher computational efficiency, completing trajectory optimization in **17 minutes** compared to MPPI's **9.5 hours** for the same task, representing a **33x** speedup.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Incorporating Runge-Kutta-4 (RK4) integration into the MPM scheme effectively mitigates numerical dissipation, allowing for accurate mechanical energy conservation at coarser spatial resolutions.
> - Differentiable simulation, coupled with gradient-based optimization, provides a more efficient and precise approach for dynamic control of deformable objects compared to sampling-based methods.
> - Tailoring physics simulators to prioritize physical accuracy, such as energy conservation, is essential for their effective application in optimal control scenarios.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Controlling the deformation of flexible objects represents a significant challenge in robotics and control engineering due to their non-linear dynamics and effectively infinite-dimensional state and configuration spaces. This complexity limits the direct applicability of many traditional control methods, especially for scenarios involving large, non-linear deformations or materials with varied properties.

Historically, various approaches have been explored. Early methods involved discretizing objects into finite elements (FEM) to derive locally valid Jacobians for inversion-based control or finding non-linear control laws by analytically solving simplified models, such as the cantilever beam equation. While efficient for specific, simple cases, these methods often require ad-hoc derivations and struggle with generalization to complex shapes or diverse material models.

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has emerged as an alternative, but its application to deformable object control often necessitates extensive and costly training data. Learning from demonstrations, a more data-efficient variant, tends to yield control policies that are restricted by the specific demonstrations used and lack generalizability. For higher-level planning, some approaches embed object states into learned latent spaces, facilitating planning which is then transformed back into physical actions. However, these methods typically focus on moving objects between static configurations and often do not incorporate dynamic object or goal behaviors.

More recently, the field has seen the rise of differentiable simulations, which offer the ability to compute gradients with respect to simulation parameters and states. This capability has opened new avenues for material identification and control. Examples include differentiable Position-Based Dynamics (PBD) and Discrete Elastic Rod (DER) simulations for controlling rope or cable shapes. However, PBD, primarily developed for computer graphics, may sacrifice physical realism, while DER is limited to one-dimensional objects, restricting its general applicability. Differentiable Finite Element Method (FEM) simulators offer precision but can be computationally expensive and may face challenges with large deformations. Several differentiable Material Point Method (MPM) simulators have also been introduced, some focusing on computer graphics performance and others providing benchmarks for manipulation tasks.

This work specifically positions itself within the context of differentiable MPM. The authors identify a gap in existing differentiable simulator research: a lack of comprehensive analysis and demonstration of their use in *dynamic control problems* with *time-varying objectives*, as opposed to static configuration goals. Furthermore, they note that many existing differentiable MPM variants prioritize performance over accuracy, particularly regarding physical properties like energy conservation, or are computationally intensive (like FEM). This paper aims to address these limitations by proposing a differentiable MPM simulator explicitly tailored for control applications, emphasizing accurate mechanical energy modeling at coarser spatial resolutions and its application to dynamic control tasks like active damping.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a differentiable Material Point Method (MPM) simulator specifically designed for optimal control applications involving deformable objects. This overarching goal is driven by several key motivations and aims to address identified shortcomings in the broader research landscape.

The intrinsic challenge in controlling flexible objects stems from their complex, non-linear dynamics and the high dimensionality of their configuration space. Traditional control methods often struggle with generalization to complex shapes or materials, while data-driven approaches like reinforcement learning demand substantial training data, making them costly to implement for many real-world scenarios.

The authors motivate their focus on differentiable simulation by highlighting its potential for gradient-based optimization, which can be significantly more efficient than sampling-based methods and capable of finding more optimal solutions. However, they observe that existing differentiable simulators, particularly those based on MPM, often originate from computer graphics, leading to implementations that may prioritize computational speed at the expense of physical realism or accuracy, especially concerning energy conservation.

Specifically, the paper identifies a critical issue with standard MPM implementations, such as FLIP MPM, at low spatial resolutions: they suffer from strong numerical dissipation. This dissipation causes an erroneous damping of the system, preventing the accurate simulation of mechanical energy and thus limiting their utility for control applications where capturing stability properties and energy dynamics is crucial. This issue necessitates either prohibitively high-resolution simulations, leading to excessive computational times, or an adaptation of the method.

Based on these motivations, the paper articulates three main contributions, which also serve as specific objectives:

1. **Presentation of a control-tailored MPM simulator:** To develop and present an MPM-based simulator specifically adapted for optimal control problems involving deformable objects, moving beyond general-purpose or graphics-focused implementations.
2. **Modification for differentiability, GPU acceleration, and energy accuracy:** To build upon standard MPM by modifying its implementation to ensure differentiability, enable acceleration through GPUs, and accurately model the system’s mechanical energy, particularly at coarser spatial resolutions, by minimizing numerical dissipation.
3. **Demonstration in a dynamic control scenario:** To showcase a control approach using the proposed differentiable simulator for an active damping problem—a dynamic control task previously underexplored with such simulators. This objective also includes comparing its performance against a baseline sampling-based control method (Model Predictive Path Integral, MPPI) to evaluate its effectiveness and efficiency.

In essence, the research aims to bridge the gap between accurate physical simulation and efficient gradient-based control for complex deformable objects, particularly in dynamic scenarios where energy conservation is paramount.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research centers on developing a differentiable Material Point Method (MPM) simulator and applying it to an optimal control problem for deformable objects.

**Problem Formulation:**
The underlying physical system is modeled using continuum mechanics, where the deformable body's motion is governed by non-linear partial differential equations. To make this problem tractable for control, the body is discretized into a finite number of particles within an MPM framework, transforming the continuous system into a discrete-time optimal control problem. The objective is to find a control trajectory `u = {u₀..., u_T-1}` that minimizes a time-varying, state-dependent cost `Σ c_t(S_t, u_t)`, subject to the system's discrete-time evolution `S_t+1 = f(S_t, u_t)` and boundary conditions. The control inputs `u_t` can be applied either as discretized body forces or as enforced boundary conditions.

**Material Point Method (MPM) Adaptation:**
The paper utilizes a modified version of the standard Fluid Implicit Particle (FLIP) update-stress-last (USL) MPM. A critical adaptation addresses the numerical dissipation inherent in standard MPMs at low spatial resolutions, which leads to inaccurate energy conservation.
Instead of the typical two Euler integration steps, the proposed method rearranges the algorithm to only calculate the derivatives of the particle state `S_t = [x_p, v_p, F_p]^T`. This re-formulation expresses the system as a first-order ordinary differential equation `˙S_t = ˜f(S_t, t)`, allowing for integration with explicit or implicit solvers. Specifically, **Runge-Kutta-4 (RK4)** is used for integration, which significantly reduces numerical dissipation compared to standard Euler integration, enabling accurate energy conservation even at coarser spatial resolutions.

The adapted algorithm (Algorithm 1) consists of four main steps at each time step to evaluate particle derivatives:
1. **Particle to Grid (P2G):** Transfer particle masses and momenta to grid nodes.
2. **Grid-to-Particle-to-Grid (G2P2G):** Compute derivatives of particle deformation gradient (`˙F_p`) and position (`˙x_p`) from grid velocities. Calculate particle stresses (`σ_p`) using a material model. Finally, determine node forces (`f_I`) from stresses and body forces.
3. **Grid Operations:** Calculate node accelerations (`a_I`).
4. **Grid to Particle (G2P):** Use node accelerations to find derivatives of particle velocities (`˙v_p`).
These derivatives `(˙x_p, ˙v_p, ˙F_p)` are then used by the RK4 integrator to advance the particle states.

**Differentiable Implementation:**
The simulator is implemented to leverage Automatic Differentiation (AD). The JAX framework is chosen for its maturity, active development, and compatibility with GPU parallelization, enabling efficient gradient computation for Python code.
The simulation rollout is structured hierarchically. An inner simulation loop advances the system by `N` steps, accumulating a partial cost `C_τ^N`. An outer loop then collects the gradients of this cost with respect to the optimization variables (`θ_τ`, which represent control inputs in this work) using AD and performs an optimization step. This allows for gradient calculation over batches of `N` time steps, balancing accuracy and computational cost. The Adam optimizer is used for robust gradient-based optimization.

**Material Model:**
The material behavior is modeled as hyperelastic using the Saint Venant-Kirchhoff model, which defines stress based on the Green strain tensor. To incorporate realistic damping, a strain-rate dependent damping model is added, resulting in a combined stress that includes both elastic and dissipative components.

**Numerical Experiments - Active Damping Scenario:**
1. **Energy Conservation Verification:** A preliminary experiment simulates a flexing hyperelastic beam in isolation to compare the total energy conservation of the proposed MPM implementation (with RK4) against a standard FLIP MPM. The goal is to demonstrate the reduced numerical dissipation.
2. **Trajectory Optimization for Active Damping:**
 - **Setup:** A 2D hyperelastic rope is simulated under gravity. Its left end is fixed, and the right end is fixed horizontally but can be controlled in the vertical (y) direction via a prescribed velocity (`v_t^y`).
 - **Objective:** After an initial disturbance excites oscillations in the rope, the goal is to optimize a control velocity trajectory (`u`) for 1.6 seconds to minimize the kinetic energy, thereby actively damping the oscillations.
 - **Cost Function:** The state cost `c_t` is defined as half the mass times the squared velocity of the particles, representing the kinetic energy.
 - **Control Optimization:** The control sequence is discretized into 32 values, held for 50 ms each. Optimization is performed in 0.4-second intervals (`N=8` in the inner loop) using Adam for 50 steps.
 - **Baseline Comparison (MPPI):** The performance of the differentiable simulator's trajectory optimization is compared against a Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) controller. MPPI, a sampling-based reactive control method, samples 2000 noisy control trajectories per step with a horizon of `H=10` control steps to approximate the optimal control input in a receding horizon approach.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings based on numerical experiments conducted with the proposed differentiable Material Point Method (MPM) simulator.

**Energy Conservation:**
A critical verification involved simulating a flexing hyperelastic beam in an isolated environment to assess energy conservation.
- The proposed MPM implementation, which uses Runge-Kutta-4 (RK4) integration, demonstrated robust conservation of total mechanical energy (sum of strain and kinetic energy) over a 10-second simulation period and longer time horizons. Any energy loss observed was negligible, indicating minimal numerical dissipation.
- In contrast, a standard FLIP MPM implementation, even with a four times smaller integration step, exhibited significant energy dissipation. It lost 88% of the total energy within 10 seconds.
- This comparison indicates that the proposed control-oriented differentiable simulator is better suited for simulating low-resolution problems over extended time horizons where accurate energy dynamics are essential.

**Trajectory Optimization for Active Damping:**
The differentiable simulator was applied to an active damping problem involving a 2D hyperelastic rope. After an initial disturbance, the simulator optimized a velocity trajectory for the rope's right end to minimize kinetic energy.
- **Performance of Proposed Method:** The optimization process yielded an oscillatory velocity profile that effectively damped the rope's oscillations, reducing the kinetic energy to near zero levels. The resulting energy trajectory was smooth.
- **Performance of MPPI Baseline:** The Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) controller also achieved significant damping. However, its stochastic, sampling-based nature introduced visible noise into the system's energy trajectory.
- **Numerical Metrics Comparison (Table I):**
 - **Time to 80% Energy Reduction:** The proposed method achieved an 80% reduction in kinetic energy (from the maximal disturbance-induced level of 20.9 J to 4.19 J) in 120 ms. The MPPI controller required 323 ms for the same reduction, making the proposed method approximately 2.7 times faster.
 - **Time to 90% Energy Reduction:** For a 90% reduction (to 2.09 J), the proposed method took 271 ms, while MPPI took 461 ms, indicating the proposed method was approximately 1.7 times faster.
 - **Damped Mean Kinetic Energy:** In the interval between 1 and 2 seconds after the disturbance, the proposed method resulted in a mean kinetic energy of 0.8307 J. The MPPI controller yielded a mean kinetic energy of 1.0383 J, which is approximately 20% higher than the proposed method. For reference, the undamped system (No Action) had a mean kinetic energy of 5.2298 J in the same interval.
 - The superior performance of the gradient-based optimization is attributed to its ability to iteratively minimize the cost function and reach a deeper minimum, contrasting with MPPI's reliance on a finite number of sampled trajectories.

**Computational Efficiency:**
- The differentiable simulator's trajectory optimization (open-loop) required approximately 17 minutes to complete on a compute cluster with a Nvidia H200 GPU and 8 CPU cores.
- The MPPI controller (closed-loop) required approximately 9.5 hours (570 minutes) under the same computing conditions, even with parallelized trajectory simulations. This indicates that the proposed method is approximately 33 times more computationally efficient for the evaluated task.
- The authors estimate that running the proposed optimization in a closed-loop setting would take roughly 2.8 hours (10,000 seconds), which would still represent a more than 3-fold speedup compared to the MPPI controller.

In summary, the results demonstrate that the adapted differentiable MPM, with its improved energy conservation and efficient gradient computation, enables trajectory optimization that is both faster and achieves a lower energy state for active damping compared to a sampling-based MPPI approach.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a differentiable Material Point Method (MPM) simulator tailored for control applications, which carries several implications for the field of deformable object control and robotics.

One significant aspect of this work is the **improvement in physical realism and accuracy** of MPM for control purposes. By modifying the standard MPM algorithm and incorporating Runge-Kutta 4 (RK4) integration, the authors have effectively minimized numerical dissipation. This allows the simulator to accurately model mechanical energy conservation even at coarser spatial resolutions. For control systems that rely on precise physical dynamics, such as those involving stability or energy regulation, this enhancement is crucial as it permits simulations to be both computationally efficient and physically representative.

The demonstration of this differentiable simulator in an **active damping problem** highlights its utility for dynamic control tasks, which represents a step beyond applications primarily focused on achieving static configurations. This capability suggests broader applicability for scenarios requiring continuous adjustment and stabilization of deformable bodies.

Furthermore, the research showcases the **computational and performance advantages of gradient-based optimization** over sampling-based methods like Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI). The proposed method achieved faster damping and resulted in a 20% lower mean kinetic energy while consuming significantly less computation time (approximately 3% of the time required by MPPI). This efficiency is important for real-time control systems and for exploring complex control policies that might be intractable with more computationally demanding approaches. The ability to use automatic differentiation to compute gradients efficiently is a cornerstone of this improvement, allowing for more robust and precise optimization.

The potential impact of this work extends to several domains:
- **Robotics:** It could enable more precise and efficient control of soft robots, flexible manipulators, or in tasks involving the manipulation of deformable objects such as ropes, fabrics, or biological tissues in surgical robotics.
- **Manufacturing:** Applications in processes requiring precise shaping or handling of pliable materials, where control over deformation is critical.
- **Computer Graphics and Simulation:** While adapted for control, the improved energy conservation properties of the MPM could also benefit simulations in graphics that demand higher physical accuracy.

The authors note that future work will focus on improving computational efficiency further and demonstrating control on real physical systems. This indicates that while the current application is primarily in simulation, the framework is designed with eventual real-world deployment in mind, paving the way for more sophisticated and efficient robotic interaction with the deformable world. The development of a robust, differentiable, and energy-conserving physics engine for deformable bodies, coupled with efficient optimization techniques, is a foundational step towards achieving such advanced capabilities.
