# STDArm: Transferring Visuomotor Policies From Static Data Training to Dynamic Robot Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> STDArm is a real-time action correction framework that enables visuomotor policies, trained on static data, to operate robustly on dynamic mobile robots. It achieves centimeter-level operational precision in mobile manipulation tasks, significantly improving success rates on platforms like mobile tables, frames, and drones compared to uncorrected policies.

## Problem

- Difficulty in collecting high-quality human demonstrations for training visuomotor policies on unstable mobile platforms due to complex operator requirements.
- Significant performance degradation of visuomotor policies on mobile robots caused by processing delays leading to outdated actions and uncompensated platform motion disturbances.
- Limited onboard computing resources on small mobile robots and challenges in generalizing policies across diverse mobile robotic systems with different locomotion characteristics.

## Method

- A real-time action correction framework, **STDArm**, augments existing visuomotor policies trained on static data by compensating for platform motion disturbances and processing delays.
- The framework decouples the potentially low-frequency policy network inference from the high-frequency control loop using an ==Action Manager== that performs ==temporal ensemble and interpolation==.
- A ==Stabilizer module==, incorporating a lightweight pose prediction network and ==online latency estimation==, adjusts policy actions in real-time to compensate for the mobile platform's motion and system latencies.

## Results

- STDArm significantly improves success rates of static-trained visuomotor policies on dynamic platforms, e.g., increasing Pick and Place success on a mobile table from **0%** to **53.3%** and 'Get the Delivery' on a drone from **13.3%** to **40%**.
- The framework achieves centimeter-level operational precision across various dynamic mobile platforms (wheeled robots, leadscrew, drone) and different base policies (Diffusion Policy, ACT).
- Ablation studies confirm the necessity of each STDArm component, particularly the action manager and stabilizer with latency estimation, for robust performance in dynamic environments.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Visuomotor policies trained on static data can be effectively transferred to dynamic mobile platforms without extensive re-training or modification.
> - Real-time action compensation, incorporating platform motion prediction and latency estimation, is crucial for achieving high precision on unstable robots.
> - Decoupling the policy's primary function (skill learning) from dynamic compensation allows for broader applicability and simplifies development for mobile manipulation systems.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper positions itself within the rapidly evolving field of visuomotor policies and mobile manipulation. Visuomotor policies, which directly map visual observations to robot actions, have gained prominence due to their effectiveness in acquiring complex tasks through imitation learning, eliminating the need for traditional modular pipeline designs. This approach has led to remarkable capabilities in stationary robotic systems, as evidenced by tasks like battery insertion, food preparation, and textile handling.

However, the broader research landscape faces significant hurdles when attempting to deploy these policies on dynamic mobile platforms such as quadruped robots, drones, or humanoids. These challenges include:
1. **Data Collection Difficulty:** High-quality human demonstrations, typically collected via teleoperation, are straightforward for static robots but become immensely challenging for dynamic platforms due to their inherent mobility and instability. Skilled operators are required to compensate for base movement, making data collection complex.
2. **Processing Delays and Outdated Actions:** Policy inference on computationally constrained edge devices often leads to significant delays. The actions generated can become outdated by the time they are executed, especially on a moving robot.
3. **Temporal Consistency vs. Responsiveness:** Current visuomotor policies often use action chunking for temporal consistency, which generates multiple actions for sequential execution. While useful, this reduces the robot's responsiveness to rapid environmental changes.
4. **Generalization Across Platforms:** Policies trained on specific mobile robots often struggle to generalize to different systems due to unique locomotion characteristics.

Prior work in mobile manipulation, such as MobileALOHA and UMI on Legs, has addressed efficient data collection and policy deployment for certain mobile platforms. MobileALOHA focuses on wheeled dual-arm systems for static operations, while UMI on Legs extends to legged robots. However, highly dynamic platforms like drones present heightened challenges for precise and robust control dueability of their bases. Methods like Diffusion Policy and ACT have shown promise in learning complex policies, but their direct deployment on dynamic platforms remains problematic due to the aforementioned issues.

STDArm directly addresses a critical gap: **the transfer of visuomotor policies *trained under static conditions* to *dynamic mobile platforms* with minimal cost and modifications.** Instead of training policies specifically for dynamic environments (which is data-intensive and complex) or solely focusing on accelerating policy inference (which might not fully compensate for physical delays), STDArm proposes a real-time action correction framework. This framework augments existing static-trained policies by compensating for platform motion disturbances and processing delays. This approach is distinct as it leverages existing, readily available static demonstration data, thereby significantly reducing the barriers to deploying advanced manipulation capabilities on a wider range of mobile robots. It is designed to be compatible with various learning-based algorithms, ensuring broad applicability and flexibility within the imitation learning paradigm.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind STDArm stems from the increasing demand for deploying advanced visuomotor policies on mobile robotic platforms like quadruped robots and drones, which operate in inherently dynamic environments. While visuomotor policies have shown remarkable capabilities in controlled, static settings, their transition to real-world mobile scenarios is hindered by several significant barriers:

- **Difficulty of High-Quality Data Collection on Dynamic Platforms:** The process of collecting human demonstrations, crucial for training visuomotor policies, becomes exceedingly complex and challenging when performed on unstable mobile robots. Operators must simultaneously manage the robot's inherent movement and the manipulation task, making data acquisition a formidable hurdle.
- **Impact of Platform Motion and Processing Delays:** Mobile robots exhibit active movement and inherent wobbling, which introduce disturbances. Furthermore, performing policy inference on computationally constrained onboard devices leads to significant processing delays. These delays result in "outdated" actions that do not account for the robot's latest position or environmental state, leading to degraded performance, as visually demonstrated in Fig. 1.
- **Limited Onboard Computing Resources:** Small mobile robots often have limited computational power, making it difficult to run complex policy networks in real-time while also handling motion compensation.
- **Generalization Across Diverse Robotic Systems:** Policies trained for one mobile robot often lack generalization to others with different locomotion characteristics.

Given these challenges, the **key objective of STDArm** is to develop a system that enables the direct transfer of visuomotor policies—originally trained using data collected under static conditions—to dynamic mobile platforms. This transfer must occur with "negligible cost" (meaning without needing extensive re-training or modification of the base policy) and must preserve the original policy's manipulation capabilities. Ultimately, the system aims to achieve "centimeter-level operational precision" in mobile manipulation tasks, ensuring robust and accurate performance even when the robotic base is in motion. This objective seeks to unlock the potential of visuomotor policies for a wider range of real-world applications on dynamic platforms by simplifying the development and deployment process.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

STDArm proposes a real-time action correction framework designed to transfer visuomotor policies from static data training to dynamic robot manipulation. The core idea is to augment the policy's output with high-frequency observations of the robot's position and use a lightweight network to perform real-time action corrections, thereby compensating for platform motion. The system operates entirely on edge devices.

The methodology is structured around three main modules:

1. **Policy Network (Section III-A):**
 - **Role:** Serves as the foundational visuomotor policy (e.g., Diffusion Policy (DP) or ACT), trained offline on static data.
 - **Input/Output:** Takes `m_o` steps of visual observations (images `I`) and end-effector poses (`S`) as input, and predicts an action sequence `u` for the next `m_a` time steps (end-effector position).
 - **Execution:** Runs in a dedicated subprocess to decouple its potentially low-frequency inference from the high-frequency control loop, continuously generating action sequences.

2. **Action Manager (Section III-B):**
 - **Purpose:** Decouples the policy network's asynchronous inference from the real-time control loop and boosts the control frequency.
 - **Temporal Ensemble:** Maintains an action buffer `A` and merges newly generated action sequences `u` with its contents. To prevent "action backtracking" (repeatedly executing past actions due to inconsistent policy inference), it aligns actions by timestamp and then refines the alignment by comparing action similarity using Euclidean distance over a search range. The buffer is updated using a temporal ensemble approach, where new actions are merged with existing ones via exponentially decaying weights, inspired by ACT.
 - **Action Interpolation:** Since the policy network's output frequency is lower than the required execution frequency, linear interpolation is applied to generate actions at finer time steps (`Aτ`), ensuring smooth, high-frequency control (e.g., 50Hz). The continuous merging into the action buffer ensures interpolation is always internal, avoiding risky extrapolation.

3. **Stabilizer (Section III-C):**
 - **Purpose:** Compensates for the impact of the robot's motion (active movement or wobble) on manipulation by predicting future motion and adjusting actions.
 - **Pose Prediction Module:**
 - **SLAM:** Utilizes a real-time, high-frequency visual SLAM system (e.g., Intel Realsense T265 stereo camera with IMU) to obtain a sequence of high-frequency poses of the mobile platform.
 - **Lightweight Prediction Network (M):** A computationally efficient neural network (parallel LSTM and GRU outputs concatenated and passed through a fully connected layer) takes a sequence of past relative poses (`ΔP_-l0:-1`) as input. It directly predicts future relative poses (`ΔP*_1:l1`) to estimate the platform's future motion.
 - **Action Compensation:** Calculates an action offset `δ` for a target action `Aτ`. This offset accounts for the time interval between action generation and execution (`Δtτ`) and the total system latency (`Δt`). The offset is computed using the inverse of the relative transformation between the pose at action generation and the predicted future pose, considering the extrinsic parameters (`E`) between the visual SLAM and arm coordinate systems. The final compensated action (`A'τ`) is derived by applying this offset to the action from the Action Manager.

4. **Latency Estimation (Section III-D):**
 - **Purpose:** Online calibration of system parameters, specifically the total latency (`Δt`) from pose estimation to action execution.
 - **Procedure:** Before mission execution, a "warm-up routine" is performed. The robot holds a red marker, and a fixed monocular camera tracks its positional variance while the robot's base moves. A linear search identifies the latency value that minimizes the marker's movement, using the velocity ratio between the marker's pixel-plane motion and the VSLAM-estimated platform motion as the evaluation metric. This ensures accurate compensation by the stabilizer.

**Hardware and Tasks:** The system is evaluated across four reconfigurable hardware configurations: MT-3DoF (Mobile Table), MF-5DoF (Mobile Aluminum Frame), LS-3DoF (Programmable Leadscrew), and UAV-3DoF (DJI Matrice 300 drone), all powered by an NVIDIA Jetson AGX Xavier. Three tasks with increasing precision requirements are designed: Pick and Place, Stack Cylinder, and Get the Delivery. Data collection involved 50 static sequences for policy training and 5 minutes of motion data for the pose prediction network. Experiments for mobile scenarios involved manual shaking (double-blind) for MT/MF, a programmable leadscrew for LS (for controlled ablation), and leveraging the drone's natural instability for UAV.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental results comprehensively demonstrate STDArm's efficacy in enabling visuomotor policies trained on static data to perform robustly on dynamic mobile platforms.

1. **Baseline Performance Degradation in Dynamic Environments:**
 - Under static conditions, baseline policies (Diffusion Policy (DP) and ACT) achieved high success rates (e.g., 100% for ACT on MF-5DoF in all static tasks, 73.3-100% for DP on MT-3DoF).
 - However, when these baseline policies were deployed on dynamic platforms without STDArm, their performance severely degraded. For precision-demanding tasks like Pick and Place and Stack Cylinder, DP on MT-3DoF showed 0% success, and ACT on MF-5DoF achieved only 6.7% and 13.3% respectively. For "Get the Delivery" in dynamic settings, DP managed 20% on MT-3DoF and ACT 26.7% on MF-5DoF. Crucially, on the highly unstable UAV-3DoF, DP achieved only a 13.3% success rate.

2. **Significant Performance Recovery and Centimeter-Level Precision with STDArm:**
 - The integration of STDArm resulted in substantial performance recovery, achieving success rates comparable to or even better than the baselines in static conditions.
 - For MT-3DoF (DP-based), success rates in dynamic Pick and Place and Stack Cylinder increased from 0% to 53.3%. Get the Delivery improved from 20% to 93.3%.
 - For MF-5DoF (ACT-based), dynamic Pick and Place improved from 6.7% to 53.3%, Stack Cylinder from 13.3% to 66.7%, and Get the Delivery from 26.7% to 86.7%.
 - On the UAV-3DoF, STDArm boosted the success rate for "Get the Delivery" from a meager 13.3% to 40%, representing a 200% improvement.
 - These results collectively demonstrate STDArm's ability to achieve "centimeter-level operational precision" during robot motion, as required by tasks like Stack Cylinder (4 cm precision).

3. **Adaptability Across Platforms and Policies:**
 - STDArm's effectiveness was validated across two different robotic arm configurations (3-DoF and 5-DoF), four mobile platforms (mobile table, mobile frame, leadscrew, and drone), and two distinct visuomotor policies (Diffusion Policy and ACT). This highlights its plug-and-play capability and generalizability to various hardware and learning-based approaches.
 - The system successfully handled diverse sources of instability, from manual perturbations to the inherent aerodynamic uncertainties of a drone.

4. **Ablation Studies Confirm Component Contributions:**
 - Ablation studies on the "Get the Delivery" task confirmed the incremental contributions of STDArm's modules. Starting from a static policy baseline, adding the action manager alone provided a noticeable improvement (e.g., from 20% to 40% on MT-3DoF). However, the most significant gain was observed when the full STDArm system, including the latency estimation module, was integrated, demonstrating the necessity of accurate latency compensation.
 - Further detailed ablation using the LS-3DoF (programmable leadscrew for controlled motion) specifically highlighted the critical roles of temporal ensemble, latency estimation, and action interpolation within the action manager and stabilizer.

5. **Visual Evidence of End-Effector Stability:**
 - An end-effector holding task visually demonstrated STDArm's contribution to stability. Without correction, the end-effector's movement mirrored the base motion. With "latency=0s" correction, improvement was noted but still showed significant movement due to uncompensated delays. Only with the full STDArm, including the pose prediction module, was the end-effector movement drastically reduced, confirming effective mitigation of delays and compensation for base motion.

In summary, the main findings unequivocally establish STDArm as a robust and generalizable solution for deploying static-trained visuomotor policies on dynamic mobile robots, effectively overcoming challenges posed by platform motion and system latency to achieve high precision.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of STDArm holds significant implications for the field of robotics, particularly in extending the applicability and reach of visuomotor policies to real-world, dynamic environments.

1. **Enabling Cost-Effective Deployment of Visuomotor Policies:** One of the most significant impacts of STDArm is its ability to facilitate the direct deployment of policies trained under static conditions onto mobile platforms. This eliminates the need for expensive, time-consuming, and technically challenging data collection processes on dynamic robots. By leveraging readily available static demonstration data, STDArm offers a cost-efficient pathway for researchers and practitioners to equip mobile robots with advanced manipulation capabilities, significantly lowering the barrier to entry for many applications.

2. **Broadened Applicability of Mobile Manipulation:** STDArm's proven effectiveness across diverse mobile platforms, including wheeled robots and, notably, drones, expands the potential operational envelope for visuomotor policies. This advancement is crucial for tasks requiring manipulation in environments where the robot itself is in motion or inherently unstable (e.g., aerial inspection and repair, logistics in warehouses with mobile bases, search and rescue scenarios with legged robots). The ability to perform centimeter-level precise tasks on a moving drone opens up new frontiers for unmanned aerial manipulation.

3. **Robustness to Real-World Challenges:** The system's capacity to compensate for platform motion disturbances, processing delays, and asynchronous control loops directly addresses critical real-world challenges that often hinder the transition of lab-trained policies to practical deployment. This inherent robustness makes robotic systems more reliable and effective in unstructured and dynamic settings.

4. **Decoupling Policy Learning from Dynamic Compensation:** By providing a generalizable framework for real-time action correction, STDArm allows policy learning research to focus primarily on acquiring complex manipulation skills from demonstrations, without the added complexity of making the policy itself robust to platform dynamics. The STDArm layer handles the dynamic compensation, serving as a versatile "plug-and-play" solution that can augment various existing visuomotor policies (e.g., Diffusion Policy, ACT).

5. **Foundation for Future Research:** The paper clearly outlines its limitations, particularly that STDArm does not address the generalization challenges of the foundational policy network itself when faced with inevitable changes in camera perspective or background during robot movement. This acknowledgment provides a clear direction for future research, prompting further work on policies that are inherently more robust to visual changes in dynamic environments, while STDArm continues to handle the physical motion compensation.

In conclusion, STDArm represents a vital step forward in bridging the gap between sophisticated lab-trained visuomotor policies and their practical, high-precision deployment on dynamic mobile robotic platforms. Its cost-effectiveness, generalizability, and demonstrated real-time precision position it as a foundational contribution that could accelerate the widespread adoption of advanced mobile manipulation capabilities across various industries and applications.
