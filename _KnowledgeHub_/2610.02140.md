# Finetuning with Sampling: SFT Learns Better Than You Think

> [!summary] Summary
> Projection sampling is a Metropolis-Hastings procedure that rewrites off-policy expert traces into information-equivalent traces closer to the base model's own distribution. It targets the KL information projection of the base model onto correct trajectories. Plain SFT on these boosted traces generalizes better and forgets less than RL and on-policy self-distillation across chemistry, math, and medical tasks. On a 3B model, MATH500 accuracy rises from 24.5% to 58.2%.

## Problem

- SFT on off-policy expert traces generalizes weakly and causes catastrophic forgetting, because off-policy data drives large updates that pull the model far from its base distribution.
- On-policy RL needs the model to find successful rollouts itself, so hard problems yield zero reward variance and no learning signal, blocking genuinely new capabilities.
- Bridging methods such as importance-weighted SFT, KL-anchored SFT, and expert-guided GRPO change the learning objective rather than the data, so the off-policy distribution mismatch stays unresolved.

## Method

- ==Information-projection== target: the ideal training distribution is the base model restricted to trajectories equivalent to the expert trace, which is the ==KL==-closest distribution that keeps the expert's information.
- **Projection sampling**: a block-wise ==Metropolis-Hastings== sampler adapted from ==power sampling== and initialized at the expert trace. By the ==data processing inequality==, each step cannot increase KL to the base model.
- ==Information-preserving proposal==: cut the current trace at a random index, then let the base model, prompted with the expert solution in context, continue in its own words. In practice, a candidate is accepted when its likelihood is higher.

## Results

- Chemistry on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct: sampling SFT reaches **66.0%** versus **61.8%** for both SFT and OPSD. Prior-capability average falls only from **59.7%** to **58.6%**, the least forgetting of any method.
- Math on Qwen2.5-3B: MATH Levels 3-5 reach **49.5%** versus **45.7%** for GRPO and **47.0%** for UFT. MATH500 reaches **58.2%** versus **31.3%** for GRPO, and adding GRPO afterward raises it to **65.2%**.
- Across **0-10** MCMC steps, KL to the base model falls monotonically while accuracy rises. A zero-shot rewrite scores **61.3%**. Qwen finetuned on Olmo-boosted data drops to **57.3%**, below vanilla SFT.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - SFT's forgetting and weak generalization come mostly from off-policy data, not from the SFT objective itself. Reshaping the data can replace on-policy learning algorithms.
> - Boosted data must match the model being trained. Traces made on-policy for one model hurt another, so the gain comes from distribution alignment, not from generically cleaner rewrites.
> - Sampling compute is a separate scaling axis. A one-time MCMC spend on training data buys learnability and can seed RL on hard problems that otherwise give no learning signal.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Place in the Broader Research Landscape

This paper addresses a central question in large language model posttraining: how to teach a model new capabilities while limiting damage to its existing ones. The two principal approaches discussed are supervised finetuning (SFT) and reinforcement learning (RL). SFT learns from expert-written or expert-generated examples, including examples the model itself may not be able to produce. This provides a direct learning signal, but prior work has associated off-policy SFT with weak generalization and catastrophic forgetting. RL, by contrast, typically learns from the model’s own generated outputs and their rewards. Its on-policy character has been linked to better generalization and retention, but it can struggle when the model rarely generates successful answers and therefore receives little useful reward signal.

The authors situate their work among methods that try to combine the advantages of both approaches. Some methods incorporate expert information into RL rollouts, while others use self-distillation to train on outputs from a teacher conditioned on privileged information. Other approaches modify the SFT objective to account for the difference between training and model-generated data. This paper takes a different route: it leaves the SFT objective unchanged and transforms the training examples before finetuning.

The work also draws on research using Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods to alter language-model sampling distributions. In contrast to methods that use MCMC to improve inference-time responses, the authors apply sampling in advance to create a training dataset that is intended to be more compatible with a particular base model.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

The paper’s main objective is to determine whether off-policy expert traces can be made more suitable for SFT by changing the examples rather than the learning objective. The authors argue that expert data contains information the base model may lack, but that a large mismatch between the expert traces and the model’s existing distribution can make that information difficult to learn from. Their proposed solution is to preserve the task-relevant content of the traces while making them more likely under the base model.

This proposal responds to a limitation on each side of the SFT–RL comparison. SFT can learn from demonstrations even when the model cannot independently produce a correct trajectory. However, fitting directly to those demonstrations may induce large behavioral changes and impair previously acquired skills. RL can constrain updates to model-generated outputs, but that advantage is less useful when a model cannot solve the task often enough to generate a learning signal. The authors therefore seek a training-data construction method that retains expert supervision while moving examples toward the model’s own distribution.

The study tests this idea across scientific problem solving, mathematical reasoning, and medical question answering. It asks whether the resulting SFT can improve task performance, generalize beyond the finetuning examples, retain prior capabilities, and produce behavior that reflects new learning rather than only increased likelihood of answers the base model already knew how to generate.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Target distribution

For each query, let the expert trajectory be one example of a broader set of trajectories that convey equivalent information. The paper denotes this set by \(C\). In the idealized formulation, a trajectory belongs to \(C\) if it preserves the relevant correctness or semantic content of the expert trace. The precise interpretation depends on the task: for math or science, the trajectory should lead to a correct answer using the expert information; for factual or open-ended tasks, it should preserve the relevant content.

The authors define the desired distribution as the base model’s distribution restricted to \(C\), and normalized. In other words, among trajectories that preserve the expert information, the target favors those the base model itself assigns higher probability to. They motivate this target as the distribution closest to the base model under the forward KL divergence, subject to the constraint that outputs remain within the information-preserving set. The expert dataset supplies starting points, while the target describes the desired distribution of transformed examples.

#### 3.2. Projection sampling

To sample approximately from this target, the paper introduces projection sampling, based on Metropolis–Hastings. Starting from an expert trace, the algorithm proposes alternative traces and accepts or rejects them according to their probabilities under the base model and the proposal distribution. In the ideal algorithm, this process preserves the target distribution as its stationary distribution. A theoretical result also shows that successive MCMC steps do not increase the KL divergence between the sample-generating distribution and the base model, under the stated assumptions.

For language-model sequences, directly resampling complete trajectories can be costly and may mix slowly. The implementation therefore proceeds incrementally in blocks. It builds up a sequence in blocks of 32 tokens, using the current prefix to initialize each extension, and runs MCMC updates over prefixes and completions. The reported experiments use up to 10 MCMC steps and a maximum sequence length of 1,856 tokens.

The proposal mechanism uses the base model conditioned on a prompt containing the original expert solution and a partial candidate response. The prompt asks the model to continue the partial response in its own words while remaining consistent with the expert solution and reaching the same correct answer. This is intended to preserve information while allowing the resulting wording and reasoning trajectory to be more natural for the base model.

A practical qualification is important: the appendix reports that the implementation approximates the Metropolis–Hastings acceptance test. Rather than computing the full proposal-probability ratio in every case, it accepts a candidate when it has higher likelihood than the current one. This makes the procedure more aggressive and means that the implemented sampler is not identical to exact Metropolis–Hastings. The reported accuracy of the transformed datasets ranges from 93.94% to 95.86%, depending on task and model.

#### 3.3. Evaluation design

The experiments cover three tasks. Chemistry uses 1,800 training and 600 test questions from the Chemistry L-3 subset of SciKnowEval, with expert trajectories generated by GPT-5. Math uses the hardest levels of the MATH dataset, with 8,230 training and 1,024 test problems and existing expert solutions. Medical question answering uses the HuatuoGPT-o1 SFT dataset for training and 1,000 held-out questions from its RL dataset for evaluation.

The models include Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct for chemistry and medical QA, Qwen2.5-3B for math, and, in additional chemistry experiments, Olmo-3-7B-Instruct. Baselines include vanilla SFT, a one-pass rewrite of expert traces, on-policy self-distillation (OPSD), and, for math, GRPO and UFT. The authors evaluate task accuracy and retention on other benchmarks, including MMLU, GPQA, AMC, MATH500, and GSM8K. Medical answers are assessed using an automated GPT-5-mini grader.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Task performance and retention

For chemistry with Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, sampling SFT reaches 66.0% accuracy, compared with 61.8% for vanilla SFT and OPSD, and 61.3% for rewrite SFT. It also records 69.2% on MMLU, compared with 68.7% for the base model. Its average score across the listed prior-capability benchmarks is 0.586, below the base model’s 0.597 but higher than vanilla SFT’s 0.520 and OPSD’s 0.568. Thus, in this setting, sampling SFT improves chemistry performance and limits the retention loss relative to the other finetuning approaches.

The math results show the strongest gains over several task-specific measures. Sampling SFT achieves 49.5% on the held-out MATH levels 3–5, compared with 47.0% for UFT and 45.7% for GRPO. It scores 58.2% on MATH500, substantially above the other listed methods, and 78.2% on GSM8K. Its average across the listed prior-capability benchmarks is 0.420, close to the base model’s 0.422. Initializing RL from the sampling-SFT checkpoint raises MATH levels 3–5 accuracy to 54.5%, MATH500 to 65.2%, and GSM8K to 83.0%.

For medical QA, sampling SFT reaches 45.8% accuracy, compared with 44.8% for vanilla SFT and 46.6% for OPSD. It therefore does not lead on the new-task measure in this experiment. Its average prior-capability score is 0.516, compared with 0.597 for the base model, 0.353 for vanilla SFT, and 0.501 for OPSD. Sampling SFT improves retention relative to vanilla SFT, but the results also show that its advantage over other methods is not uniform across every metric.

On Olmo-3-7B-Instruct chemistry, sampling SFT reaches 58.3%, while OPSD reaches 59.7%. Sampling SFT has the highest average among the listed prior-capability scores, at 0.617. Together, these results suggest that the relative benefit depends on task, model, and evaluation measure rather than appearing in every comparison.

#### 4.2. Additional analyses

The authors report that transformed traces have higher average likelihood under their corresponding base models than the original expert traces, consistent with the intended shift toward the model distribution. They also find, in an Olmo chemistry pass@k analysis, that sampling SFT remains ahead of both the base model and OPSD as the number of sampled answers increases. This is evidence that the gains are not limited to improving a single sampled response, though the result is specific to that setting.

In a chemistry scaling experiment, increasing the number of MCMC steps is associated with decreasing divergence from the base-model distribution and rising downstream accuracy. The authors also report that transforming examples for one model and using them to finetune a different model performs poorly relative to model-matched sampling. This supports the paper’s claim that the procedure is tailored to the base model’s distribution.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The paper presents data transformation as a potential way to combine the direct supervision available in SFT with some of the distributional advantages associated with on-policy learning. If the approach proves reliable across additional models and tasks, it could help make expert demonstrations useful in cases where RL has difficulty producing successful rollouts, while reducing the amount of mismatch between training examples and the model being updated.

The method also frames sampling as a possible component of a broader posttraining pipeline, rather than solely an inference-time technique. The authors suggest extensions to self-distillation and RL on hard tasks, where expert information could guide the construction of model-compatible training examples.

The evidence remains bounded by the experiments reported. Results vary across domains, the practical sampler uses an approximate acceptance rule, and information preservation is high but not perfect. The paper also relies on automated grading for the open-ended medical task and reports single-shot benchmark performance; the supplied results do not establish performance across broader deployment conditions. Accordingly, projection sampling is best understood as a promising approach to preparing expert data for finetuning, with its generality and costs requiring further evaluation.
