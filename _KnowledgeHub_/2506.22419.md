# The Automated LLM Speedrunning Benchmark: Reproducing NanoGPT Improvements

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Meta introduce the Automated LLM Speedrunning Benchmark to evaluate AI agents' capacity for reproducing documented improvements in LLM training efficiency. The study finds that even state-of-the-art models and scaffolds struggle to consistently replicate human-achieved speedups, often recovering less than half of the original performance gains even with explicit guidance.

## Problem

- Automated scientific discovery requires AI agents to reliably reproduce existing experimental findings, a capability currently underevaluated.
- There is a lack of specialized benchmarks focusing on sequential, code-level reproducibility of complex improvements within dynamic fields like LLM training.
- Understanding the limitations of current LLM-based AI research agents in this foundational task is crucial for advancing autonomous research.

## Method

- Developed the ==Automated LLM Speedrunning Benchmark==, comprising 19 tasks derived from the NanoGPT Speedrun, where agents reproduce successive LLM training time records from previous versions.
- Implemented a flexible ==AI agent scaffold (AIDE framework)==, enabling iterative code generation, execution, and analysis using various LLM backbones.
- Agents are evaluated primarily by the ==Fraction of Speedup Recovered (FSR)==, measuring their ability to match human-achieved performance, with different levels of textual and pseudocode hints available.

## Results

- AI agents recovered under **20%** of the speedup on average without hints; the best model (o3-mini) achieved up to **46%** FSR with a combination of hints and Multi-AIDE search.
- o3-mini generally outperformed other LLMs, while Gemini-2.5-Pro and Claude-3.7-Sonnet showed near **0%** FSR in interquartile mean evaluation, and DeepSeek-R1 benefited from combined hints.
- Performance declined sharply in cumulative tasks (o3-mini's FSR dropped from **60%** for the 2nd record to **20%** for the 3rd), and external documentation negatively impacted results.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Current LLM agents struggle to reproduce scientific improvements, achieving less than 20% of the speedup without hints, highlighting a fundamental challenge for automated science.
> - Explicit hints, especially pseudocode, are crucial for improving agent performance, though the best agents still recover less than half of the human-achieved speedups.
> - Agents exhibit difficulty integrating external knowledge and show significant performance degradation when attempting to cumulatively reproduce multiple successive findings.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated at the intersection of large language models, automated scientific discovery, and machine learning reproducibility. The advent of highly capable LLMs has spurred a burgeoning field of "automated science," aiming to leverage AI agents to accelerate research processes, from hypothesis generation to experimental implementation and analysis. However, a cornerstone of reliable scientific progress is reproducibility – the ability to consistently re-obtain previously reported results. This paper uniquely positions itself by focusing on automating this crucial aspect within the dynamic field of LLM development itself.

The paper differentiates itself from existing ML reproducibility benchmarks in several key ways:
- **Focus on LLM Research:** Unlike general reproducibility benchmarks (e.g., CORE-bench, PaperBench) that span diverse machine learning domains, this benchmark specifically targets improvements in LLM training. This makes the tasks directly relevant to the core capabilities of the AI agents being developed.
- **Sequential Reproducibility:** A significant novelty is the evaluation of an agent's ability to reproduce a *cumulative series* of scientific findings. Most prior benchmarks assess single-shot reproduction of individual papers or codebases. The NanoGPT Speedrun, with its successive records, provides a natural "research arc" where improvements build upon previous ones. This tests an agent's capacity for sustained, iterative scientific progress, mirroring real-world research.
- **Actionable, Code-Level Ground Truth:** The NanoGPT Speedrun provides clear, code-level changes and a unified success metric (training time to target loss on fixed hardware). This contrasts with benchmarks requiring interpretation of natural language papers into code, which often lack precise, runnable ground truth for evaluation.
- **Diverse Optimization Types:** The speedrun improvements encompass a wide range of code changes, from high-level algorithmic advancements (e.g., new optimizers like Muon) to hardware-aware optimizations (e.g., mixed precision, efficient attention variants), making the benchmark realistic and challenging.

By providing a specialized, sequential, and highly actionable benchmark for reproducibility in LLM training, this work aims to fill a critical gap in evaluating and driving the development of more capable and reliable AI research agents.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to **evaluate the ability of current AI agents, particularly those leveraging advanced reasoning LLMs, to reproduce existing scientific findings in the domain of Large Language Model training.** This evaluation is carried out through the introduction of a novel benchmark called the "Automated LLM Speedrunning Benchmark."

The motivation behind this objective is multifaceted:
- **Foundational for Automated Science:** The authors argue that automated reproducibility is a "critical component" and a "necessary (but not sufficient) skill" for realizing the broader ambition of automated scientific discovery. Without the ability to reliably reimplement experiments based on existing descriptions, AI agents cannot build trust in findings, validate hypotheses, or scale up experimental testing effectively.
- **Testing AI Research Agents:** The paper aims to understand whether current AI research agents, defined as combinations of LLMs and iterative scaffolds, can succeed at this prerequisite task of reproducing prior discoveries. This addresses the significant speculation about LLM-based systems potentially leading to recursive self-improvement in AI research.
- **Leveraging Community Efforts:** The choice of the NanoGPT Speedrun as the basis for the benchmark is strategic. This community-driven competition provides a rich, real-world dataset of incremental improvements to LLM training efficiency, complete with code implementations, measured performance, and detailed changelogs. This makes the benchmark both "accessible and realistic for the frontier problem of improving LLM training."
- **Unified and Reproducible Metric:** The NanoGPT Speedrun offers a clear, objective success metric (wall-clock time to reach a target validation loss on fixed hardware), simplifying evaluation, fair comparisons, and cross-task analysis.

In essence, the paper seeks to establish a clear measure of how close current AI systems are to automating scientific reproduction, thereby identifying critical bottlenecks for future development towards truly autonomous research agents.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the methodology revolves around the **Automated LLM Speedrunning Benchmark** and a **flexible search scaffold** for AI agents.

#### 3.1. The Automated LLM Speedrunning Benchmark Design

- **Source:** The benchmark is derived from the community-driven NanoGPT Speedrun, which tracks successive records in minimizing the training wall-time of a GPT-2 model (Karpathy's nanoGPT) to a target validation loss on an 8xH100 node.
- **Tasks:** Each task in the benchmark corresponds to reproducing a successive speedrun record (R_i) starting from the previous record (R_{i-1}). There are 19 such tasks, excluding one due to a PyTorch upgrade.
- **Input Formats:** For each task, the AI agent is provided with:
 - The previous record's training script (R_i).
 - Optional "hints" describing the improvements of the next record (R_{i+1}). These hints come in three formats, increasing in detail:
 - **Level 1 (Pseudocode):** A pseudocode description of the code changes.
 - **Level 2 (Text Description):** A natural-language description of the changes.
 - **Level 3 (Mini-paper):** A paper-like summary of the changes, including theoretical justifications and implementation details.
- **Evaluation Metrics:**
 - **Fraction of Speedup Recovered (FSR):** The primary metric, calculated as `(t_i - t'_{i+1}) / (t_i - t_{i+1})`, where `t_i` is the time for the current record, `t_{i+1}` is the time for the target next record, and `t'_{i+1}` is the time achieved by the agent's solution. FSR quantifies how much of the human-achieved speedup the agent recovers.
 - **Record Reproduction vs. Optimization:** The benchmark defines two types of tasks: "Record reproduction tasks" (with hints) and "Record optimization tasks" (without hints), where the agent must discover improvements independently.
 - **Code Similarity:** Secondary metrics include normalized L2 embedding distance (using SFR-Embedding-Code 2B) and an LLM-as-judge score to assess how closely the agent's code resembles the human solution, beyond just performance.

#### 3.2. Agent Scaffolds

The paper provides and uses a "flexible search scaffold" which extends AIDE (AI-Driven Exploration) for iterative solution refinement. This scaffold operates in a search tree where each node represents a solution instance.
- **Stages:** Each search step involves three stages:
 1. **Implementation:** An LLM (the "coder" submodule) generates or modifies code based on prompts, task description, and optional hints. Aider is used for diff-based edits.
 2. **Execution:** The generated code is run on a fixed hardware configuration (single 8xH100 node) to measure its training time and identify bugs.
 3. **Analysis:** An LLM (the "analyzer" submodule) summarizes the execution results and identifies key performance indicators and errors.
- **Search Variants:** The scaffold supports different search strategies:
 - **Flat (Best-of-M):** Generates M initial solutions and picks the best. No iterative refinement.
 - **Tree:** Iteratively improves from the best-performing node, with a fixed branching factor. No explicit debugging.
 - **Forest:** Similar to Tree but generates N0 initial solutions before iterative improvement.
 - **AIDE:** Incorporates explicit debugging steps, randomly selecting buggy nodes to fix with a probability `p_debug`.
 - **Multi-AIDE:** Combines AIDE's debugging with a branching factor for improvement steps.
- **Models:** The study evaluates four frontier LLMs as backbones for the coder and analyzer: DeepSeek-R1, o3-mini (OpenAI), Gemini-2.5-Pro, and Claude-3.7-Sonnet.
- **Budget and Setup:** All search variants are given the same budget of M=20 search steps. Experiments are run across 19 records, 6 hint regimes, 5 search variants, and 4 models, with 3 seeds per variation, totaling 6,840 agent runs.

The hints (Levels 1-3) were manually drafted and verified by human experts to ensure correctness and relevance, detailed in Appendix D.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments reveal significant challenges for current LLM agents in reproducing scientific improvements in LLM training, even with detailed guidance.

- **Struggle with Reproducibility:** Even with state-of-the-art LLMs and scaffolds, agents "struggle to improve ground-truth records to match the speedups of the next ground-truth record." Without hints, agents recover less than 20% of the speed-up on average (Figure 1, Figure 4).
- **Impact of Hints:** Hints are "necessary for inducing greater values of FSR." The most useful individual hint format is pseudocode (Level 1), enabling o3-mini to recover approximately 40% of the speedup. Combinations of hints (L1+L2+L3) sometimes offer further benefits, pushing o3-mini to 46% FSR with Multi-AIDE (Table 3).
- **Model Performance Differences:**
 - **o3-mini:** Generally performs best in terms of mean FSR across most hint levels, demonstrating better iteration on previous solutions, especially with branching scaffolds.
 - **DeepSeek-R1:** Surprisingly, its performance can degrade with individual hints compared to no-hint settings, suggesting difficulty in implementing complex changes. However, it benefits from *combined* hints (L1+L2+L3), achieving a notable 41% FSR with Multi-AIDE, indicating it leverages longer contexts more effectively.
 - **Gemini-2.5-Pro & Claude-3.7-Sonnet:** These models generally show the lowest performance (close to 0 FSR in IQM evaluation), lagging behind even the open-weight DeepSeek-R1.
- **Search Scaffold Effectiveness:**
 - Flat search (best-of-M) often performs comparably to or outperforms iterated search scaffolds (Tree, Forest, AIDE) for individual hint levels.
 - Explicit debug steps (in AIDE variants) do not consistently provide a significant benefit over simple iterative improvement (Tree, Forest).
 - Multi-AIDE search, which combines branching with debugging, emerges as the best-performing scaffold in the interquartile mean (IQM) evaluation (Figure 5).
- **Analysis of Search Trees (Buggy Nodes):**
 - Flat search leads to a higher proportion of buggy nodes, indicating that initial solutions are often incorrect (Figure 8).
 - DeepSeek-R1 agents generate more buggy nodes under AIDE and Multi-AIDE, suggesting less capability in self-correction compared to o3-mini.
 - Gemini-2.5-Pro generates fewer buggy nodes but also achieves lower FSR, implying it produces robust but less effective code.
 - Claude-3.7-Sonnet generates significantly more buggy nodes, often leading to a high fraction of non-working solutions, explaining its poor overall IQM despite occasional FSR improvements.
- **Correlation with Code Similarity:** A modest positive correlation is observed between higher FSR and increased code similarity (L2 embedding distance recovered) to the human solution, especially for richer hint formats. LLM-as-judge scores also show a clear positive correlation with FSR (Figure C.2).
- **External Knowledge Integration Challenge:** Providing external documentation (FlexAttention docs) as an additional hint for a challenging record (R12) *negatively impacts* performance for both DeepSeek-R1 and o3-mini (Table 4). This suggests current models struggle to correctly exploit external knowledge not in their training corpus for complex tasks.
- **Cumulative Performance Degradation:** When agents are tasked with cumulatively reproducing successive records (building on their *own* previous output), performance drops sharply. o3-mini recovers 60% FSR for the second record but only 20% for the third, and virtually no speedup by the fourth record (Figure 9). This highlights a critical limitation in maintaining improvement over a longer research arc.
- **Later Records are Harder:** Agents consistently find it more difficult to reproduce later speedrun records, which often involve more complex or subtle optimizations (Figure 6, Figure C.1).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Automated LLM Speedrunning Benchmark and its findings carry significant implications for the development of AI research agents and the broader landscape of automated scientific discovery.

#### 5.1. Significance

- **Highlighting a Critical Bottleneck:** The study clearly demonstrates that automated reproducibility, a foundational skill for any autonomous research agent, remains a significant challenge for even leading LLMs and scaffolds. The observed struggle to replicate known innovations, even with explicit hints, underscores the gap between current AI capabilities and the aspiration of self-improving or fully autonomous scientific research.
- **Realistic and Actionable Evaluation:** By grounding the benchmark in the real-world, highly competitive NanoGPT Speedrun, the authors provide a valuable and challenging testbed. The sequential nature of the tasks, coupled with clear code-level ground truth and diverse hint formats, makes it a robust tool for measuring incremental progress in AI agent capabilities.
- **Insights into LLM Strengths and Weaknesses:** The detailed analysis of different LLMs, search scaffolds, and hint levels provides crucial insights. For instance, the findings on context length, the effectiveness of pseudocode, the varying ability to debug, and the surprising negative impact of external knowledge offer actionable directions for improving LLM architectures and agent designs.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The identified limitations (scaling external knowledge, disentangling memorization/generalization, semantic diffs, generalizing beyond LLM training) directly point to crucial avenues for future research in AI for science.

#### 5.2. Potential Impact

- **Accelerating AI Research Agents:** The benchmark provides a non-saturated measure for tracking progress in AI agents. Its availability (open-sourced code) can spur dedicated development efforts towards agents that can reliably reproduce and ultimately *innovate* in complex code-based scientific domains.
- **Enhancing Scientific Progress:** If AI agents can reliably reproduce experiments, it could drastically increase the pace of scientific validation, accelerate hypothesis testing, and make research findings more trustworthy and scalable across various fields (e.g., healthcare, climate science).
- **Economic Growth:** The acceleration of scientific discovery, facilitated by advanced AI research agents, could lead to novel technologies and breakthroughs, driving economic growth.
- **Facilitating Safety and Alignment Research:** As LLM capabilities advance, understanding and controlling their behavior becomes paramount. Agents capable of open-ended AI research tasks introduce new risks. The benchmark contributes by offering a structured way to evaluate specific aspects of agent autonomy, which is critical for parallel advancements in monitoring, aligning, and controlling such models for responsible deployment.
- **Shift Towards AI-driven Innovation:** While the current results show limitations, the benchmark lays the groundwork for a future where AI agents might not just reproduce but also *outpace* human records in specific scientific domains, eventually leading to true automated scientific discovery. This would fundamentally change how research is conducted.

In conclusion, this work serves as a critical diagnostic tool, revealing that while AI agents show promise in scientific domains, overcoming the challenge of reliable automated reproducibility is a fundamental step that current models are yet to master, serving as a clear call to action for the AI research community.
