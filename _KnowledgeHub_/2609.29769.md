# JEV vs. LLMs as Rubric Judges: Cheaper, Faster, and Wrong in the Same Places

> [!summary] Summary
> Jev is a typed classifier that returns probabilities over permitted answers without generating any text. The paper tests it as a rubric judge against three flash-tier LLM judges on 5,003 pairs from nine panels. Jev's accuracy differs significantly in only 8 of 27 paired comparisons, the LLM judges cost 29 to 325 times as much, and correlated errors cap any cascade gain at 1.5 points.

## Problem

- Per-criterion LLM rubric graders make one call per unit-criterion pair, so evaluation cost and wall time scale with units times criteria.
- Prior automatic evaluation reports correlation with rater means, which cannot register a constant shift, and high percent agreement can hide large judge-human score differences.
- Cascade and jury schemes assume cheap and expensive judges fail independently, but error correlation across vendors is rarely measured against a label-matched independence baseline.

## Method

- Three ==Jev== framings: ==Noul== returns a yes-probability thresholded at 0.5, ==Choice== takes the argmax over supplied option labels, and ==Score== rounds an expected level; a wrapper prepends verdict definitions.
- One shared ==AutoRubric== criterion-grader harness runs all judges on identical criterion texts across nine panels from seven benchmarks, scoring exact accuracy, mean quadratic-weighted kappa, and a per-criterion offset.
- Post-hoc replays on recorded verdicts: leave-one-out constant level shifts, Jev-first cascades under oracle and cross-fitted thresholds, median juries, and repeated-error rates against a criterion-and-label-matched independence baseline.

## Results

- Jev Choice led RiceChem with **81.0%** verdict accuracy against **76.1 to 79.2%** for the LLM judges, while Gemini's largest graded lead was **16.4** points on ELLIPSE.
- Over nine panels Jev Choice cost **$0.063** and ran **29** seconds; Luna cost **29 times** as much, DeepSeek **66 times**, Gemini **325 times**, taking **859 to 6,298** seconds.
- On Jev's 84 most confident errors the LLM judges repeated its wrong answer **96.0%** of the time against a **50.3%** independence baseline; cross-fitted cascades gained at most **1.5** points.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Cheap-judge cascades buy cost savings, not accuracy: cross-vendor error correlation caps the gain, so report repeated-error rates against a label-matched independence baseline.
> - Judge-judge agreement exceeding judge-label agreement signals a shared offset; a per-criterion constant shift removes most of the gap while leaving rank order unchanged.
> - Judges track raters best when level descriptions name observable features; norm-referenced or corpus-relative scales need exemplars, since rater conventions never reach a zero-shot judge.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

Grading a submission against a rubric means judging it one criterion at a time. A conventional grader makes one LLM call for each submission–criterion pair, so more submissions and rubric criteria multiply calls and costs. This study tests a model that answers questions about an input with probabilities over a fixed set of allowed answers, rather than generating text. The authors call this a typed classifier, and their system is Jev. One request can carry a submission and all its criteria, and return structured verdicts and confidence scores. The result is a tradeoff, not a blanket replacement: Jev's accuracy depends on whether the criteria are binary or graded.

Filled markers are separated comparisons, hollow markers are not separated, and a ring marks a binary panel. Points fall on both sides of zero, and Gemini's ELLIPSE result stands out high above zero.

### 2. Cost against accuracy

The authors compare Jev with three flash-tier LLM judges: GPT-5.6 Luna, Gemini 3.8 Flash, and DeepSeek V4.1 Flash. The test set has 5,003 pairs in nine panels drawn from seven public benchmarks. Jev's request carried a unit's criteria together, where a unit is one submission. Each LLM judge was called once per pair.

To compare two judges, the authors resampled units 10,000 times. A comparison is separated when its 95% interval excludes zero. Parity requires the 90% interval to fit within a five-point margin chosen after the verdicts were collected. A comparison with neither separation nor parity is inconclusive.

Under these rules, Jev's accuracy was significantly separated from an LLM judge's in 8 of 27 paired comparisons, before Holm correction. Most of the remaining comparisons were inconclusive, which is not evidence of equivalence. Jev's leads were mostly on binary criteria, and its deficits were on graded criteria, chiefly against Gemini. RiceChem, for example, evaluates chemistry answers against binary rubric criteria. On its 819 binary criterion pairs, Jev Choice had 81.0% verdict accuracy, the share of pairs where the predicted verdict matches the label. The LLM judges ranged from 76.1% to 79.2%. Jev Choice is never the most accurate matched judge on a graded panel.

The efficiency comparison is where the gap is largest. For the nine-panel run, Jev Choice cost $0.063, while Gemini cost $20.587, or 325 times as much. Wall time was 29 seconds for Jev Choice and 950 seconds for Gemini, about 33 times as long. These wall times reflect the study's API concurrency and run conditions and are not a normalized latency comparison. A batched LLM judge and a run without reasoning were not tested.

### 3. Why a cascade adds little

The proposed cascade works like this: Jev answers every pair, its confident verdicts are kept, and the less confident pairs go to an LLM fallback. Confidence ranks Jev's answers but does not guarantee that an answer is correct. On six of seven graded panels, confidence had AUROC 0.57–0.70 for distinguishing Jev's correct answers from its incorrect ones. ELLIPSE was the exception, at 0.49.

The weakness appears among Jev's mistakes. A repeated error is an LLM judge giving Jev's same wrong answer. The independence baseline asks how often that LLM gives Jev's wrong answer on other pairs with the same criterion and label. The authors sampled Jev's most confident graded-panel errors. On those, the three LLM judges repeated its wrong answer on 242 of 252 verdicts (96.0%), while the baseline predicted 126.7 repeats.

Split human labels may account for some of these apparent errors. Of the 72 sampled confident errors on panels with multiple raters, only 10 had unanimous labels. On HealthBench, 11 of 12 separately sampled errors had unanimous physician labels.

By Jev Choice confidence band: (a) Jev's error rate; (b) the share of LLM verdicts on Jev-wrong pairs that repeat Jev's wrong answer, observed (solid) against the independence baseline (dashed). ELLIPSE is black and the other graded panels are grey. In the highest band, observed repeats range from 80.5% to 92.8%, against baselines from 33.6% to 63.7%.

The authors tested cascades by replaying recorded verdicts rather than running new ones. With cross-fitted thresholds, the largest average gain over the best single judge was 1.5 percentage points, and cascades fell below that judge on six panels. Oracle thresholds raised the maximum gain to 2.0.

### 4. The boundary

The study establishes a measured tradeoff. Jev costs far less in this study's run conditions, and its accuracy leads come mostly on binary criteria. For confidence-gated cascades, the fallback judges often repeat Jev's errors, especially among its most confident mistakes. These findings hold for the tested rubric panels and flash-tier judge configurations. They do not show how Jev fares against the best obtainable LLM judge.
