# From Generalist to Specialist Representation

> [!summary] Summary
> A theoretical framework for identifiable learning of task-relevant representations is introduced, providing nonparametric guarantees for discovering temporal task structures and disentangling task-relevant latent variables within observations. The method demonstrated superior accuracy in identifying temporal task structures on synthetic and real-world datasets, while achieving clear disentanglement of relevant features in experiments.

## Problem

- Developing efficient and robust "specialist" representations for AI systems, as "generalist" models often contain superfluous or entangled information.
- The absence of identifiability guarantees for recovering true underlying task-relevant latent representations, especially in general, nonparametric scenarios without strong prior assumptions.
- Existing identifiability theories are often too restrictive, focusing on full recovery of all latent variables or requiring interventions, which limits applicability to real-world, observation-only settings.

## Method

- A hierarchical theoretical framework that first identifies the ==temporal structure== of tasks across observation sequences, and then disentangles task-relevant latent variables within each time step.
- Introduces a novel conditional independence test and Algorithm 1 for unsupervised discovery of temporal task structures, even with disconnected segments and arbitrary task interleaving.
- Applies sparsity regularization to the estimated task-latent structure, providing nonparametric identifiability guarantees (Theorem 2) for separating task-relevant latent variables from irrelevant ones.

## Results

- The proposed Algorithm 1 for temporal task structure identification consistently outperformed baseline methods, achieving higher accuracy and Matthews Correlation Coefficient on synthetic data and the highest mean Average Precision on the SportsHHI video dataset.
- Quantitative evaluation on synthetic data demonstrated that the estimated task-relevant latent variables accurately captured ground-truth relevant information while effectively disentangling it from irrelevant factors, evidenced by high and low R-squared values respectively.
- Qualitative results on realistic vision tasks showed that sparsity regularization enabled precise control over task-specific attributes in image generation, confirming the interpretability and disentanglement of learned task-relevant representations.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Conceptualizing tasks as colliders that induce conditional dependence between relevant time steps enables their unsupervised identification from observed data.
> - Focusing on *task-relevant identifiability* (recovering a subset of latents) rather than full identifiability allows for more general and weaker theoretical assumptions, increasing practical applicability.
> - Sparsity regularization on the mapping from latent states to tasks is a sufficient condition for provably disentangling task-relevant latent variables from irrelevant ones in a nonparametric setting.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of machine learning increasingly relies on learning latent representations from high-dimensional observations to facilitate understanding and action in complex environments. While "world models" aim to compress sensory input into low-dimensional features that capture dynamics, there is a recognized need for task-relevant representations. These "specialist" representations are designed to retain only the information essential for a particular task, offering benefits in terms of efficiency and robustness compared to "generalist" representations that model the entire environment.

A central challenge in latent representation learning, including for task-relevant settings, is *identifiability*. Identifiability refers to the asymptotic guarantee of recovering the true underlying representation, independent of the amount of data or computational resources. Without it, a learned representation may be an arbitrary transformation of the true latent variables, leading to ambiguity where task-relevant factors remain entangled with irrelevant ones.

Existing theoretical work on identifiability provides conditions primarily within classical linear models (e.g., factor analysis, ICA, tensor decomposition) or more recent nonlinear theories. Nonlinear ICA often relies on auxiliary information across domains or time, or constrains the mixing function class. Causal representation learning frequently derives identifiability from interventional data or counterfactual views, which require some control over the data-generating process. More general settings have been explored, sometimes assuming Boolean latent and observed variables. These existing conditions often focus on *full identifiability* of all latent variables, which can be overly restrictive for many applications where only a subset of factors is relevant to a specific task.

This paper addresses a gap in the literature by focusing on *task-relevant identifiability* in a highly general, nonparametric setting. It seeks to establish theoretical guarantees for recovering task-relevant representations without relying on interventions, parametric forms, or strong structural constraints, particularly when dealing with complex temporal dynamics like disconnected sequences and arbitrarily interleaving tasks. The work distinguishes itself by providing general nonparametric identifiability guarantees for task-relevant components rather than full component-wise recovery, and by doing so across both temporal task structure and within-step latent disentanglement.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to establish a theoretical framework for the identifiable learning of task-relevant representations, thereby providing a principled pathway from generalist to specialist models. This overarching goal is motivated by several key considerations:

- **Efficiency and Robustness:** In many real-world applications, tasks depend on only a subset of latent factors. For example, autonomous driving requires vehicle and pedestrian positions, but not car color. Learning representations that selectively retain task-relevant information can lead to more efficient and robust downstream applications, such as planning, transfer learning, and generalization.
- **The Problem of Identifiability:** Without identifiability, learned representations cannot be guaranteed to correspond meaningfully to the ground truth. This ambiguity introduces irreducible uncertainty into a machine's internal model of the world, potentially limiting the capabilities of AI systems and posing risks in high-stakes domains. Existing identifiability theories often require strong assumptions that may not hold in general scenarios.
- **Limitations of Full Identifiability:** Most prior theoretical work aims for full identifiability, meaning the recovery of all latent variables component-wise. However, in many practical settings, such comprehensive recovery is not necessary. Tasks often depend on a specific subset of latent factors. This paper proposes that shifting the focus to *task-relevant identifiability* can enable weaker assumptions while still directly supporting practical applications.
- **Challenges in General Settings:** The paper seeks to provide identifiability guarantees in a fully nonparametric setting, without relying on interventions, specific parametric forms for generative or transition functions, or restrictive structural constraints. This includes scenarios with complex temporal dynamics, such as sequences lacking strict temporal dependence (e.g., disconnected steps), and tasks with arbitrarily complex and interleaving structures (appearing, disappearing, and reappearing). Addressing these generalities without auxiliary information or strong priors presents a significant theoretical challenge.

To achieve its primary objective, the paper breaks down the problem into two distinct, yet interconnected, sub-objectives:

1. **Identify Temporal Task Structure:** The first objective is to determine *which tasks are active at which time steps*. This involves recovering the structure linking time segments and tasks in a fully unsupervised manner. This step is considered foundational because understanding task engagement across time is necessary before attempting to disentangle task-relevant latents within individual steps.
2. **Disentangle Task-Relevant Latent Representations:** The second objective is to, within each identified time step, isolate the components of the latent state that are *relevant to the associated tasks* from those that are irrelevant. This aims to obtain a minimal yet sufficient representation for each task.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research develops a hierarchical theoretical framework and corresponding algorithmic procedures to address the identified objectives.

**Generative Process Model:**
The framework models observations `{o_t}` as generated by latent states `{s_t}` through unknown, nonlinear diffeomorphism functions `o_t = f_t(s_t)`. Actions `{a_t}` are also generated by latent states (`a_t = π_t(s_t, η_t)`). The model allows for varying temporal connectivity, where `s_t` can influence `s_{t+1}` and `a_t` can influence `s_{t+1}` if the boundary `t → t+1` is connected; otherwise, these influences are omitted. Tasks `{g_i}` are defined as colliders among different time steps, meaning `s_t → a_t → g_i` if time step `t` is relevant to task `g_i`. This definition of tasks as colliders is justified by the argument that it correctly induces conditional dependence between time steps within a task, capturing their interdependent nature as parts of a coherent strategy, unlike confounder or mediator structures which would imply conditional independence. All generative functions and distributions are assumed to be hidden and nonparametric.

**Learning Temporal Task Structure (Section 3):**
The first part of the methodology focuses on identifying the temporal structure between time steps and tasks.
- **Temporal Segmentation:** The sequence `{s_t}` is partitioned into `N` contiguous segments `S = {S_1..., S_N}` of equal length `L ≥ 2`. The assumption is that all states within a segment share the same set of active tasks, and each task `g_i` appears in at least two segments. The segmentation does not require prior semantic knowledge but ensures minimal temporal coherence.
- **Conditional Independence (CI) Test:** The core of the temporal task structure identification relies on a novel conditional independence test.
 - **Band Conditioning Set (Z_band):** For testing the relevance of task `g_i` to segments `S_k` and `S_v` (where `k < v`), a specific conditioning set `Z_band(k, v, i)` is defined. This set includes the immediate boundary states of `S_k` and `S_v` (e.g., `s_{kL-1}, s_{kL+1}, s_{vL-1}, s_{vL+1}`) and the task variable `g_i`.
 - **Theorem 1:** This theorem formally states that task `g_i` is relevant to segments `S_k` and `S_v` if and only if `s_{kL}` is conditionally dependent on `s_{vL}` given `Z_band(k, v, i)`. The proof sketch indicates that conditioning on the immediate boundary states blocks purely temporal dynamics paths, forcing any remaining dependence between `s_{kL}` and `s_{vL}` to be transmitted through the shared task `g_i`, acting as a d-connecting path.
 - **Corollary 1:** This extends Theorem 1, demonstrating that any pair of states `s_j` and `s_q` within `S_k` and `S_v`, respectively, can be used for the CI test, not just the boundary states, provided `L > 2`. This highlights the robustness of the identifiability to the specific choice of representative states within a segment.
- **Algorithm 1 (Global Task Structure Discovery):** This algorithm systematically applies the pairwise CI tests from Theorem 1 across all segment pairs and candidate tasks. By aggregating these results, it builds the complete set of tasks relevant to each segment `T(S_k)` and, consequently, to each individual time step `T(t)`. Proposition 1 guarantees that this algorithm exactly recovers the temporal task structure under the specified conditions.
- **Practical Considerations:** The method is nonparametric, testable directly in the observed space (due to CI preservation under diffeomorphism), and robust to arbitrary task interleaving and temporal disconnections. The paper also discusses how the method can be extended to scenarios where tasks are not given but inferred as latent variables. Computational complexity is noted to scale linearly with the number of segments, allowing for a controllable accuracy-scalability trade-off.

**Learning Task-Relevant Representation (Section 4):**
After identifying the temporal task structure, the methodology moves to disentangling task-relevant latent variables within each time step.
- **Generalist Model Limitations (Proposition 2):** The paper first analyzes what happens without explicit regularization for task relevance. It proves that a "generalist" model, while observationally equivalent, tends to learn an overcomplete representation. Specifically, for a mapping `u` from `s` to task `g`, the number of estimated task-relevant latent variables (as indicated by the support of the Jacobian `J_u_hat`) will be greater than or equal to the number of true task-relevant variables (`||I(J_u_hat)|| ≥ ||I(J_u)||`). This means generalist models may contain superfluous dimensions and entangled relevant/irrelevant factors. The proof relies on assumptions regarding the linear independence of Jacobian row vectors over a set of points.
- **Specialist Model with Sparsity Regularization (Theorem 2):** To overcome the limitations of generalist models, the method proposes using sparsity regularization on the estimated task–latent structure.
 - **Sparsity Constraint:** By adding a constraint that the estimated number of task-relevant latent variables is not larger than the true number (`||I(J_u_hat)|| ≤ ||I(J_u)||`), in conjunction with the result from Proposition 2, an exact match in the support of the Jacobians can be achieved.
 - **Disentanglement Guarantee:** Theorem 2 establishes that with such sparsity regularization, under some permutation, the estimated task-relevant latent variables (`s_hat_{t,π(I_k)}`) are an invertible function of *only* the ground-truth task-relevant latent variables (`s_{t,I_k}`). This implies a provable disentanglement of task-relevant variables from irrelevant ones.
- **Nonparametric and i.i.d. Applicability:** This result is significant because it provides identifiability guarantees in a nonparametric setting, covering even i.i.d. data (due to allowing arbitrary disconnections), which is generally more challenging than scenarios exploiting temporal or domain shifts. Unlike prior work that often requires strong functional assumptions or graphical criteria for full component-wise identifiability, this approach focuses on identifying task-relevant latents as a subgroup, relaxing assumptions.

**Experimental Implementation (Section 5 and Appendix C):**
- **CMI Surrogate:** For practical high-dimensional CI testing, conditional mutual information (CMI) is used as a surrogate, estimated using a task-conditioned representation and a conditional InfoNCE objective (e.g., `I(s_{kL}; s_{vL} | c_i)` where `c_i` is a learned representation of `Z_band`).
- **Synthetic Data:** Linear Gaussian SCMs are used to generate data for evaluating temporal task structure identification, varying the number of time steps and tasks, with random disconnections and interleaving.
- **Real-World Data:** The SportsHHI video dataset is used for evaluating temporal task structure in complex human-human interaction videos. Pretrained CLIP encoders and variational autoencoders are utilized for feature extraction and latent state estimation.
- **Task-Relevant Representation Evaluation:** Synthetic data with MLP/Leaky ReLU generative functions is used to quantify disentanglement using R-squared values. Realistic vision tasks (e.g., cat images with accessories) are used for qualitative evaluation of human interpretability of disentangled latents.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical evaluations generally support the theoretical claims regarding both temporal task structure learning and task-relevant representation learning.

**Identifiability of Temporal Task Structure:**
- **Synthetic Data Performance:** The proposed Algorithm 1 for temporal task structure identification consistently demonstrated superior performance compared to baselines (classical CCA, Group Lasso, and SelTask) across various settings. When varying the number of time steps (T from 8 to 20, with T/5 tasks) and the number of tasks (M from 2 to 10, with 20 time steps), Algorithm 1 achieved higher accuracy and Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC). The performance of all methods decreased as problem complexity increased (larger T or M), but the performance gap favoring Algorithm 1 persisted.
- **Real-World Data Performance (SportsHHI):** On the SportsHHI video dataset, which features complex, multi-individual human interactions with overlapping and intricate task structures, the proposed method achieved the highest mean Average Precision (mAP) for discovering corresponding task labels. It outperformed baselines, including applying the algorithm to observed variables directly ("Base"), LEAP (a method for identifiable latent temporal representations), Slowfast, and VitB. This indicates that a principled approach to modeling general temporal task structures is effective in real-world scenarios.

**Identifiability of Task-Relevant Representation:**
- **Synthetic Data Disentanglement:** Experiments on synthetic data, designed to test the recovery of task-relevant latent representations, showed a clear separation between relevant and irrelevant components. The R-squared values indicated that the estimated task-relevant parts accurately captured information from the ground-truth relevant parts (high R-squared), while simultaneously disentangling them from irrelevant factors (low R-squared for irrelevant parts). These results provide quantitative validation for the identifiability theory regarding information preservation and irrelevance disentanglement.
- **Realistic Vision Qualitative Results:** Qualitative evaluations on a cat image dataset, where tasks involved specific attributes (e.g., "wearing eyeglasses," "wearing a hat," "wearing a tie"), demonstrated the practical benefit of sparsity regularization. With sparsity, the model successfully isolated latent variables corresponding to the intended task attributes, leading to precise control over these features in generated images. Without sparsity, irrelevant factors, such as color, became entangled with the target tasks, resulting in unintended changes. Similar qualitative findings were presented for a dog image dataset. This visual evidence supports the role of sparsity in achieving disentangled and interpretable task-relevant representations.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research marks a notable contribution to the theoretical foundations of representation learning by initiating a formal investigation into task-relevant world representations and the transition from generalist to specialist models.

- **Novel Identifiability Guarantees:** The paper provides, to its knowledge, the first general nonparametric identifiability guarantees for two critical aspects:
 1. **Temporal Task Structure:** Proving that the structure between time steps and tasks can be identified in a fully unsupervised manner, even in highly challenging settings with disconnected sequences, arbitrary task interleaving, and no strict temporal dependence.
 2. **Task-Relevant Latent Representation:** Demonstrating that within each time step, task-relevant latent variables can be disentangled from irrelevant ones under a simple sparsity regularization, without relying on interventions, parametric forms, or restrictive functional/structural constraints.
- **Principled Pathway to Specialist Models:** By establishing these hierarchical identifiability guarantees (task structure across time, and task-relevant latents within each step), the work offers a principled justification for why and how specialist models, guided by appropriate regularization, can achieve disentangled and minimal representations where generalist models might produce overcomplete or entangled ones. This provides a formal underpinning for the development of AI systems that can effectively compress information and achieve fidelity to underlying task-relevant factors.
- **Broad Applicability:** The nonparametric nature of the results and the robustness to complex temporal dynamics (including i.i.d. settings) suggest broad applicability across various domains. The ability to identify hidden temporal task structures and disentangle task-relevant features is critical for developing more robust, efficient, and interpretable AI systems, particularly in areas like robotics, autonomous driving, and complex system modeling.
- **Advancement of Theoretical Understanding:** The research highlights the utility of focusing on partial identifiability (task-relevant components) rather than full identifiability, potentially opening new methodological avenues for a wider range of latent-variable problems where isolating specific factors is crucial. It also encourages a stronger emphasis on identifiability within the machine learning community, suggesting that theoretical insights can lead to significant advancements beyond purely empirical gains.
- **Future Directions:** The paper identifies avenues for future work, including analyzing the finite-sample regime (as identifiability is an asymptotic property) and exploring novel identifiability-inspired architectural designs. These directions indicate a continuing research agenda stemming from the foundational work presented.
