# Humanoid Locomotion as Next Token Prediction

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at UC Berkeley developed a method for humanoid locomotion by reframing it as a next token prediction task, similar to large language models. This approach uses a transformer model trained on diverse datasets to generate robust locomotion policies, achieving zero-shot transfer to a full-sized humanoid robot that navigated varied terrains in San Francisco.

## Problem

- Developing effective, adaptable, and generalizable locomotion for humanoid robots remains a long-standing challenge.
- Traditional methods like model-based control and reinforcement learning often face limitations in adaptability, generalization, and data requirements.
- Difficulty in transferring locomotion policies from simulation to diverse real-world environments without extensive fine-tuning.

## Method

- Reframes humanoid locomotion as a next token prediction task, where a causal transformer model predicts future robot actions.
- Treats the robot's sensory inputs and motor outputs as tokens in a sequential stream for the transformer to process.
- Trains the model on diverse data sources, including incomplete human motion capture and video data, by using mask tokens for unavailable information.

## Results

- Achieved zero-shot transfer of policies trained in simulation to a real-world Digit humanoid robot, enabling navigation across various terrains in San Francisco.
- Produced smoother and more consistent gait quality in simulation compared to reinforcement learning baselines, while maintaining comparable trajectory following performance.
- Demonstrated robustness to disturbances and generalization to new commands (e.g., walking backward) in real-world deployment, showing benefits from diverse data training.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Humanoid locomotion can be effectively modeled as a sequence prediction problem, analogous to natural language processing.
> - Transformer architectures can capture complex sensorimotor patterns and long-range dependencies, enabling learning from both complete and incomplete training data.
> - Performance scales with increased training data, context length, and model size, similar to observations in large language models.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Introduction

Enabling humanoid robots to walk effectively has been one of the most challenging problems in robotics for decades. Traditional approaches have relied on model-based control or reinforcement learning (RL), both of which come with limitations in terms of adaptability, generalization, and data requirements. A team of researchers from UC Berkeley has proposed a revolutionary approach to this problem: treating humanoid locomotion as a next token prediction task, similar to how large language models (LLMs) predict the next word in a sentence.

This innovative perspective reimagines robot control as a sequence modeling problem, where the transformer architecture—which has revolutionized natural language processing—is applied to predict the next robot action based on the sequence of previous sensor readings and actions. The researchers have demonstrated that this approach can lead to robust locomotion policies capable of zero-shot transfer to real-world environments, successfully navigating the streets of San Francisco with a full-sized humanoid robot.

### 2. The Next Token Prediction Paradigm

The core insight of this research is reframing humanoid locomotion as a next token prediction problem. In this paradigm:

1. The robot's sensory inputs and motor outputs are treated as tokens in a sequence.
2. A transformer model is trained to predict the next motor command given the history of previous sensory inputs and motor commands.
3. The model learns to capture the underlying patterns and dynamics of effective locomotion from diverse data sources.

This approach differs fundamentally from traditional methods:

```
Traditional RL: Maximize expected reward through trial and error
Model-based control: Leverage dynamics models for optimal control
Next token prediction: Learn to model the distribution of effective sensorimotor sequences
```

By leveraging the transformer architecture's ability to model long-range dependencies and complex patterns in sequential data, the researchers can train a model that captures the underlying dynamics of humanoid locomotion without explicitly encoding physical laws or reward functions.

The power of this approach lies in its ability to learn from both complete and incomplete data. As shown in Figure 2, the transformer can be trained with mask tokens in place of missing data, allowing it to learn from diverse sources that might have different available modalities.

### 3. Data Diversity and Learning from Multiple Sources

One of the most innovative aspects of this research is leveraging multiple data sources with varying levels of completeness:

The researchers combined:

1. **Simulation-based data**: Complete trajectories from both neural network policies trained with RL and model-based controllers
2. **Motion capture data**: Human movement recordings with partial information
3. **Internet videos**: Human locomotion videos processed with pose estimation, containing visual data but no action information

This data diversity is crucial because it allows the model to learn from both "perfect" examples (simulation) and "natural" examples (human movement), creating a policy that blends precision with the natural fluidity of human locomotion.

The ability to train on incomplete data is particularly revolutionary, as it opens the door to using vast amounts of readily available human motion data from sources like YouTube. These videos provide valuable examples of human locomotion but lack the corresponding motor command information. By using mask tokens for the missing action data, the model can still extract useful patterns from these incomplete trajectories.

### 4. Methodology

The methodology consists of several key components:

1. **Data Collection and Preprocessing**: Gathering diverse locomotion data from simulations, motion capture, and videos. The data is tokenized into discrete timesteps containing sensor readings and motor commands.

2. **Transformer Architecture**: A causal transformer model is employed for next token prediction, with the architecture adapted for sensorimotor data rather than text:

 ```
   Input: [s_1, a_1, s_2, a_2, ..., s_t]
   Output: Prediction of a_t
   ```

 Where `s_t` represents sensor readings at time t, and `a_t` represents actions at time t.

3. **Modality-Aligned Prediction**: The model is trained to predict tokens of the same modality as the input token, maintaining the structured relationship between sensors and actions.

4. **Handling Missing Data**: Mask tokens are used to represent missing data (typically action data from human videos), allowing the model to learn from incomplete trajectories.

5. **Autoregressive Inference**: During deployment, the model predicts actions autoregressively based on the current and historical sensor readings.

The training objective is to minimize the negative log-likelihood of the data:

$$L = -\sum_{i=1}^{N} \log p(x_i | x_{<i})$$

Where $x_i$ represents the tokens in the dataset, and $p(x_i | x_{<i})$ is the probability assigned by the model to token $x_i$ given the preceding tokens $x_{<i}$.

### 5. Scaling Properties

An important finding of this research is that performance improves with scaling of various factors, similar to what has been observed in large language models.

The research shows clear improvements when:

1. **Increasing training data**: More trajectories lead to better performance, with error decreasing as the number of training trajectories increases from 1K to 10K.

2. **Extending context length**: Longer historical context improves performance, with the ability to consider more timesteps (from 16 to 48) resulting in lower tracking errors.

3. **Enlarging model size**: Larger models with more parameters (from 1M to 8M) demonstrate better performance, confirming the benefits of model scaling.

These scaling properties suggest that the approach could benefit from even larger datasets and models in the future, potentially leading to even more capable locomotion policies.

### 6. Performance Evaluation

The researchers evaluated their approach both in simulation and in real-world settings. In simulation, they compared the gait quality and trajectory following capabilities of their method against traditional RL approaches.

As shown in Figure 5, the next token prediction approach (right) produces smoother, more consistent knee joint movements compared to the RL baseline (left), indicating better gait quality.

The trajectory following capabilities of the approach were also tested:

Figure 6 demonstrates that the proposed approach can achieve trajectory following performance comparable to RL-based methods, with both policies showing similar abilities to follow commands at different angles and distances.

One particularly interesting finding was the strong correlation between the model's prediction loss and its tracking error:

This correlation (r=0.87) suggests that the prediction loss on held-out validation data is a good proxy for the expected performance of the controller, allowing researchers to estimate controller performance without expensive deployment tests.

### 7. Real World Deployment

The most impressive achievement of this research is the successful zero-shot transfer to a real humanoid robot. The policy trained entirely in simulation was deployed on a full-sized Digit humanoid robot, which was able to navigate various locations in San Francisco without any additional fine-tuning.

The researchers found that their approach outperformed traditional methods in several key aspects:

1. **Handling diverse terrains**: The robot successfully navigated different surfaces, including pavement, grass, and slightly uneven terrain.

2. **Generalization to new commands**: The policy generalized to commanded behaviors not seen during training, such as walking backward.

3. **Robustness**: The policy demonstrated robustness to disturbances and environmental variations.

4. **Data efficiency**: Training with a combination of complete and incomplete data sources led to better performance than training only on complete data.

This successful real-world deployment provides strong evidence for the effectiveness of the next token prediction paradigm for humanoid locomotion.

### 8. Limitations and Future Work

Despite its impressive results, the research has several limitations that point to opportunities for future work:

1. **Dynamics gap**: While zero-shot transfer was successful, there's still a gap between simulation and reality that could be addressed through improved simulation or domain adaptation techniques.

2. **Limited behaviors**: The current implementation focuses primarily on locomotion. Future work could expand to more complex behaviors like manipulation or interaction with objects.

3. **Model size constraints**: The models used are relatively small compared to state-of-the-art language models. Scaling to larger models might yield further improvements but requires managing computational constraints for real-time control.

4. **Data diversity**: While diverse, the data sources used still represent a limited set of locomotion scenarios. Incorporating even more diverse data, potentially from real robot experiences, could improve robustness and generalization.

Future research directions could include:

- Integrating vision data directly into the model for more informed environmental interaction
- Expanding to multi-task learning across different robot behaviors
- Exploring hybrid approaches that combine the strengths of next token prediction with model-based control or reinforcement learning

### 9. Conclusion

The research demonstrates a groundbreaking approach to humanoid locomotion by reframing it as a next token prediction problem. This novel perspective allows for leveraging the power of transformer architectures and diverse data sources, including incomplete trajectories from human videos.

The approach shows promising scaling properties similar to those observed in language models, suggesting that performance could continue to improve with larger datasets, longer context lengths, and bigger models. The successful zero-shot transfer to a real humanoid robot navigating the streets of San Francisco represents a significant advancement in robotics.

By treating locomotion as a sequence modeling problem, this research opens new avenues for robot learning that move beyond traditional reinforcement learning and model-based control approaches. It suggests that the generative modeling techniques that have revolutionized natural language processing and image generation may also transform how robots learn to move and interact with the world.

This paradigm shift could accelerate progress in humanoid robotics by enabling the use of internet-scale data for training and reducing the need for carefully designed reward functions or precise dynamics models. As such, it represents an important step toward more generalizable and adaptable robotic systems.
