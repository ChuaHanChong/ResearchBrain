# FAMOS: Feed-Forward 3D Articulation Modeling from Sparse Observations

> [!summary] Summary
> FAMOS is a feed-forward model that predicts movable-part segmentation and joint parameters from a sparse, unordered set of partial point clouds showing an object in different articulation states. A Multi-state Articulation Transformer alternates state-wise and global attention, an observed articulation span loss forces cross-observation reasoning, and procedural assets expand training data. It beats feed-forward and optimization-based baselines on three benchmarks while running about 3,000x faster than optimization.

## Problem

- Test-time optimization methods need dense multi-view captures per articulation state, often a known part count, and costly per-object fitting that limits scalability.
- Feed-forward methods infer articulation from a single observation, which is intrinsically ambiguous, so they lean on category-level shape priors and generalize poorly to unseen geometry or partial inputs.
- Video and multi-state methods require temporally ordered motion, complete geometry, or known part counts, while existing articulated-object datasets remain too small and narrow for training.

## Method

- **Multi-state Articulation Transformer**: frozen ==PartField== features plus geometric embeddings, no state-index embedding, alternating per-observation and global attention; part queries decode cross-state segmentation and ==Plücker-coordinate== joint axes.
- ==Observed articulation span loss==: an auxiliary l1 target on each part's max-minus-min joint value across visible inputs, which cannot be inferred from one view, blocking the single-observation shortcut.
- ==Procedural asset generator==: six analytic primitives assembled into 15 object families with 1-8 movable parts, producing self-annotated assets on the fly (about 3 ms each) for pre-training and 1:1 curated mixing.

## Results

- With two input states, segmentation F1 reaches **98.4**/**71.9**/**92.5** on PartNet-Mobility/ACD/ArtiCraft versus **93.6**/**43.7**/**76.6** for re-trained multi-state Particulate; optimization baselines ReArt and ArtGS stay below **60**.
- Strict motion F1 (type, axis under 5 degrees, origin) reaches **98.4**, **64.5**, **90.0**, beating Particulate by **8.1**, **27.8**, **21.5** points over more matched parts; inference **0.1 s** versus **182-297 s**.
- On ACD, removing span loss drops MAO F1 from **47.3** to **41.6**, and register attention instead of global drops it to **39.2**; procedural pre-training plus 1:1 mixing lifts it to **64.5**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Supervising only state-invariant targets lets multi-input models collapse onto one input; add an auxiliary target defined over the whole set, like observed span, to force aggregation.
> - The largest gain comes from a second observed state, which exposes motion; motion evidence, not shape priors, drives cross-dataset generalization in articulation estimation.
> - Procedural primitive assets and curated artist assets are complementary: generators add articulation diversity, real assets add shape statistics; too much procedural data overfits to primitive structure.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

FAMOS addresses 3D understanding of articulated objects, including objects with doors, drawers, lids, handles, hinges, and sliding components. This problem is relevant to robotic manipulation, embodied artificial intelligence, augmented and virtual reality, simulation, and the creation of interactable digital twins.

Existing research falls into three broad categories. First, optimization-based approaches reconstruct object-specific articulated models from dense multi-view observations. These methods can produce detailed geometry and motion estimates, but generally require substantial test-time computation, multiple views, several articulation states, and sometimes prior knowledge of the number of movable parts. Their per-object optimization makes them difficult to apply at scale.

Second, video-based approaches estimate articulation from temporal sequences, including monocular interaction videos and 4D point-cloud data. These methods benefit from observing motion directly, but often depend on point tracking, temporal ordering, or continuous object motion. Occlusions and simultaneous movement of multiple parts can cause tracking errors that propagate through the sequence.

Third, feed-forward methods directly predict articulation properties from a single image, mesh, or point cloud. They are computationally efficient, but a single observation does not usually provide enough evidence to distinguish between multiple plausible kinematic explanations. These approaches therefore rely heavily on category-level geometric priors learned from training data. Their performance can degrade on unseen object geometries or incomplete point clouds.

FAMOS occupies an intermediate position. It retains the speed and scalability of feed-forward inference while using multiple sparse observations of the same object in different states. Unlike video-based systems, its inputs are unordered and do not need to show continuous motion. Unlike dense reconstruction methods, it operates on partial point clouds and does not require extensive multi-view scanning. The paper’s central premise is that even a small number of casually captured observations can provide motion evidence that reduces the ambiguity inherent in single-state articulation estimation.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective is to jointly infer movable-part segmentation and kinematic parameters from a sparse, unordered set of partial point clouds. For each object, FAMOS predicts:

1. The number of object parts, including a static base and movable components.
2. A consistent segmentation of points into parts across all observations.
3. The joint type of each part, classified as static, revolute, or prismatic.
4. The axis and origin of revolute joints.
5. The direction of prismatic joints.

The method is designed to accept a variable number of observations, including a single observation. This flexibility is important because practical captures may vary substantially in the number of views and articulation states available.

A central motivation is to make the model use all available observations rather than treating each state independently or relying primarily on shape priors. The authors identify a training shortcut: joint type, axis, and origin are object-intrinsic properties and can sometimes be predicted from the geometry of one state. If these are the only supervised outputs, a model may ignore additional observations during training. FAMOS therefore introduces an auxiliary “observed articulation span” target. This target measures the range of motion exhibited by each part across the input observations. Since the span depends on the entire observation set, predicting it requires the model to identify the same part across states and reason about its relative configurations.

The work also responds to limitations in available training data. Existing articulated-object datasets have limited scale, class diversity, and structural variety. The authors consequently develop a procedural generator that creates annotated articulated assets from geometric primitives. This provides unlimited synthetic training examples with exact part and joint labels.

### 3. Methodology and Technical Approach

#### 3.1. Problem Representation

The input consists of \(S\) partial point clouds, one for each observation. Each point cloud contains \(N\) points in a shared coordinate frame. In the standard experiments, the model uses up to four states and 2,048 points per state. The point clouds may differ in viewpoint, visible geometry, and articulation state.

For a revolute joint, the model predicts a unit rotation axis and an origin on that axis. These are represented using Plücker coordinates. For a prismatic joint, the model predicts a unit translation direction. The number of parts is not provided in advance. Instead, FAMOS uses a fixed upper bound of 16 learnable part queries and determines the active number of parts from the queries that receive assigned points.

#### 3.2. Point Encoding and Part Queries

Each observation is processed independently by a frozen PartField feature backbone, which produces part-aware features for individual points. The model also computes sinusoidal embeddings of point coordinates and surface normals. These appearance-independent geometric features are concatenated with the PartField features and projected into transformer tokens.

The model deliberately does not add an observation-index or state embedding to point tokens. This design prevents the network from associating a particular input position with a fixed object state and supports a variable number of unordered observations.

A set of learnable part queries represents candidate object parts. The queries have no predefined semantic identities. During training, predicted queries are matched to ground-truth parts with the Hungarian algorithm using segmentation likelihood across all observations.

#### 3.3. Multi-State Articulation Transformer

The main architecture is a six-layer Multi-state Articulation Transformer with a hidden dimension of 768 and 12 attention heads. Its layers alternate between state-wise and global attention.

In the state-wise stage, point tokens within each observation attend to other points from that same observation. This allows each partial point cloud to build local geometric context. The part queries also self-attend, allowing them to coordinate their candidate part assignments.

In the global stage, all point tokens from all observations and the part queries are concatenated into one sequence. This enables direct point-to-point interaction across observations, as well as query-to-point information exchange. Direct global interaction is intended to help establish correspondences when surfaces are only partially visible or differ substantially across viewpoints and articulation states.

This differs from the Particulate baseline, where information is exchanged through shared queries but point tokens do not directly interact across states. The FAMOS ablation results indicate that this direct global interaction is important for motion estimation.

#### 3.4. Prediction Heads and Training Losses

A segmentation head produces scores for each point-query pair. The query dimension is normalized with a softmax, assigning each point to one predicted part. Because the same query is used across all observations, the resulting segmentation is intended to remain consistent across states.

Additional query-level heads predict joint type, revolute-joint Plücker coordinates, prismatic direction, and observed articulation span. The complete training loss combines segmentation, joint-type classification, revolute-parameter regression, prismatic-direction regression, and observed-span regression. All terms use equal weights in the reported implementation.

The observed span is computed for each part visible in at least two states. For revolute joints it is the difference between the largest and smallest observed joint angles; for prismatic joints it is the corresponding displacement range. The target is independent of an unobserved canonical rest pose, which makes it well-defined for arbitrary observation sets.

#### 3.5. Procedural Data Generation

The generator assembles objects from six analytic primitives, including boxes, cylinders, hollow cylinders, half-tori, spheres, and holed panels. It creates 15 object families, such as cabinets, doors, boxes, laptops, ovens, washing machines, fans, scissors, and trolleys. Dimensions, part counts, joint placements, and structural variants are randomized, producing objects with one to eight movable parts.

Because the geometry and kinematic structure are generated analytically, segmentation and joint annotations are available without manual labeling. Assets are generated in memory during training. The model is first pretrained for 50,000 steps on procedural data and then trained for 80,000 additional steps using a balanced mixture of procedural assets and curated data from PartNet-Mobility and GRScenes.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

FAMOS is evaluated on PartNet-Mobility, ACD, and ArtiCraft-10K. PartNet-Mobility is treated as the in-distribution benchmark, while ACD and ArtiCraft-10K assess generalization to different object geometries and articulation structures.

For movable-part segmentation with two input states, FAMOS achieves F1 scores of 98.4 on PartNet-Mobility, 71.9 on ACD, and 92.5 on ArtiCraft-10K. The re-trained multi-state Particulate baseline achieves 93.6, 43.7, and 76.6, respectively. The largest differences occur on the cross-dataset benchmarks, where FAMOS improves by 28.2 and 15.9 percentage points. The results indicate that motion evidence and the broader procedural training distribution are particularly useful under geometric and structural distribution shift.

For motion estimation under the strictest criterion, which requires correct joint type, axis error below five degrees, and origin error below 0.05 of the object scale, FAMOS obtains F1 scores of 98.4 on PartNet-Mobility, 64.5 on ACD, and 90.0 on ArtiCraft-10K. The corresponding Particulate scores are 90.3, 36.7, and 68.5. FAMOS also matches more parts, particularly on ACD, where it matches 947 parts compared with 572 for Particulate. This reduces the likelihood that its lower average errors result from evaluating only a small set of easy detections.

In the single-state setting, FAMOS remains ahead of the re-trained Particulate baseline. Its segmentation F1 scores are 95.2, 68.5, and 89.9 on the three benchmarks, compared with 90.4, 43.4, and 75.1 for Particulate. Thus, although the architecture is designed to exploit multiple states, it also retains a useful object-centric prior when motion evidence is unavailable.

The ablations support both principal design choices. Removing global attention reduces motion-estimation MAO F1 from 47.3 to 39.2 on ACD. Removing the span loss reduces it to 41.6. The number of observations also matters: the largest improvement occurs when moving from one to two states, while additional states provide smaller gains before performance saturates.

Procedural training contributes substantially. On ACD, training only on curated data gives segmentation F1 and MAO F1 values of 53.2 and 47.3. Procedural pretraining raises these to 65.3 and 58.2, and the final balanced mixture raises them further to 71.9 and 64.5. A balanced procedural-to-curated ratio performs better than ratios dominated by procedural data, suggesting that synthetic structural diversity and realistic curated geometry provide complementary information.

FAMOS has an inference time of approximately 0.1 seconds per object using four states, compared with 182 seconds for ReArt and 297 seconds for ArtGS under the reported hardware and input conditions. The model also produces qualitative predictions on real-world captures reconstructed with VGGT-Ω, despite being trained only on synthetic and curated 3D assets. These demonstrations are qualitative rather than a quantitative real-world benchmark.

### 5. Significance, Potential Impact, and Limitations

FAMOS provides a practical formulation for estimating articulated structure when only a small number of casual observations are available. Its feed-forward design could support robotic perception, rapid simulation-model construction, interactive 3D applications, and scalable digital-twin generation. The observed-span objective is also relevant beyond this specific architecture because it offers a way to supervise set-level motion evidence without requiring a canonical rest pose.

The procedural generator addresses a recurring limitation in 3D learning: annotated articulated assets are difficult to collect at scale. Its use of on-the-fly generation could be adapted to other articulated-object tasks, although the current families remain limited compared with the diversity of real-world objects.

The paper identifies several constraints. Performance decreases for object classes substantially different from the training distribution, for objects with many small movable parts, and for heavily corrupted upstream 3D reconstructions. The method focuses on depth-one kinematic structures in which movable parts are directly attached to a static base; it does not address deeper articulated chains. It also assumes 1-DoF revolute or prismatic joints and depends on an external system to lift monocular images into a shared 3D coordinate frame. Consequently, future work must evaluate robustness to reconstruction errors, expand training to broader object classes and hierarchical kinematics, and establish quantitative performance on real-world captures.
