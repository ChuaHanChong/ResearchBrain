# Capturing Individual Human Preferences with Reward Features

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Google DeepMind developed a Reward-Feature Model (RFM) and a theoretical framework to personalize reward functions, addressing the limitations of aggregated human preferences in RLHF. This model efficiently captures diverse user tastes and rapidly adapts to new users with minimal data, demonstrating improved inter-user test accuracy and enhanced control over Large Language Model outputs compared to non-adaptive methods.

## Problem

- Traditional Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) methods typically aggregate human preferences into a single reward function, implicitly assuming homogeneous user tastes.
- This 'one-size-fits-all' approach leads to sub-optimal or unsatisfactory experiences for a significant portion of users when preferences are diverse, particularly in subjective domains like Large Language Model (LLM) responses.
- Existing adaptive methods often lack a formal theoretical foundation for personalized reward learning and struggle with efficient, few-shot adaptation to new, unseen users.

## Method

- The paper formalizes and analyzes ==user-aware reward== learning within an empirical risk minimization framework, accounting for the non-identically and independently distributed (non-i.i.d.) nature of preference data from distinct human raters.
- A Reward-Feature Model (RFM) architecture is proposed, where an individual's reward function is an inner product of a shared feature vector (learned across all users) and a user-specific weight vector.
- During adaptation, for new users, the shared feature parameters are frozen, and only the user-specific weight vector is efficiently learned via convex logistic regression using a small number of new preference examples.

## Results

- Empirical evaluations with synthetic users confirmed that RFM's inter-user test accuracy consistently improved with an increasing number of diverse training raters and demonstrated robustness to heterogeneous preferences.
- RFM effectively adapted to new users with minimal data, achieving around **79%** accuracy with 90 adaptation examples and maintaining above-chance performance (**0.5481** accuracy) even with a single example.
- In 'best-of-n' selection tasks, RFM(32) consistently outperformed non-adaptive baselines and other adaptive methods, including leading LLMs using in-context personalization, in steering LLM outputs to match individual user preferences.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Theoretical PAC bounds reveal that the approximation error for inter-user generalization is primarily driven by the number of diverse raters (m) rather than merely the number of examples per rater (n), with `O(1/√m)` dependence.
> - Adaptive reward models are theoretically advantageous over user-agnostic models when user preferences exhibit significant heterogeneity, enabling a lower optimal population loss.
> - The RFM architecture allows for efficient few-shot personalization by learning general reward features shared across users and reducing user-specific adaptation to a simple, convex optimization problem.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated within the field of Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), a methodology widely employed in training large language models (LLMs) and other AI agents when an explicit reward function is not readily available. The standard practice in RLHF involves aggregating human feedback to derive a single reward function, which implicitly assumes that preferences are homogeneous across the population.

The paper identifies a limitation in this conventional approach: when human preferences exhibit significant diversity or disagreement, a single aggregated reward function may not adequately capture the nuances required to satisfy a broad user base. This can lead to a substantial portion of users experiencing unsatisfactory agent behavior, particularly in subjective domains like LLM responses.

The work builds upon the foundational principles of empirical risk minimization and Bradley-Terry models, which are established methods for learning from pairwise preferences. It extends these concepts by addressing the non-identically and independently distributed (non-i.i.d.) nature of preference data when individual raters are considered. By formalizing and analyzing the problem of learning user-specific reward models, the paper contributes to the theoretical understanding of personalized reward learning.

Practically, this research positions itself among efforts to develop adaptive reward models capable of specializing agent behavior to individual users, rather than seeking a compromise across a diverse population. It acknowledges and differentiates itself from existing adaptive approaches, including those focusing on aggregating preferences into a single policy or using in-context learning, and highlights concurrent work by Shenfeld et al. and Bose et al. that propose similar architectural ideas. This paper's contribution lies in its formal theoretical analysis of the learning problem, its proposed architecture, and its empirical validation against various baselines and in a setting with simulated real-world user heterogeneity.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the observation that current Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) methods typically utilize a single reward function derived from aggregated human preferences. This approach implicitly assumes a high degree of homogeneity in user preferences, which is frequently not the case in real-world applications, particularly for Large Language Models (LLMs). When preferences are heterogeneous, such aggregated models can lead to a significant portion of users experiencing sub-optimal or unsatisfactory interactions. The paper illustrates this with a scenario where even a slight majority preference (51% vs. 49%) can result in nearly half the users being consistently dissatisfied.

Against this background, the paper outlines several key objectives:

1. **Formalize and Analyze User-Aware Reward Learning:** A core objective is to formally define and analyze the problem of learning a reward model that can be specialized to individual users, including those who did not contribute to the initial training data. This involves moving beyond the traditional i.i.d. assumption for preference data and developing a theoretical framework to understand the factors influencing approximation error.
 - **Motivation:** Such a formal analysis is necessary to understand the theoretical trade-offs involved in collecting pairwise preference data and to guide strategies for resource allocation (e.g., number of raters vs. examples per rater). It also provides a basis for evaluating the potential benefits and limitations of adaptive reward models.

2. **Develop a Concrete Adaptive Reward Model Architecture:** The paper aims to propose a practical and principled architecture for an adaptive reward model. This architecture should be capable of capturing the subjective criteria underlying individual human preferences, even if users cannot explicitly articulate these criteria. A critical requirement is the ability to adapt quickly and reliably to new, unseen users with minimal additional data.
 - **Motivation:** Bridging the gap between theoretical understanding and practical implementation, this objective seeks to provide a concrete solution for personalizing LLMs. The emphasis on rapid adaptation with few examples (few-shot learning) is motivated by practical constraints in deployment scenarios, where collecting extensive feedback from every new user is often infeasible.

3. **Empirically Validate the Proposed Approach:** The research seeks to present experimental evidence that illustrates the theoretical findings and demonstrates the performance of the proposed adaptive reward model architecture. This includes comparisons against non-adaptive baselines (representing current practice) and other adaptive methods.
 - **Motivation:** Empirical validation is crucial to confirm the practical utility of the theoretical insights and the proposed architecture. The experiments are designed to show how the model's benefits increase with factors such as the number of raters and the heterogeneity of preferences, as predicted by the theoretical analysis. It also aims to demonstrate that the proposed architecture offers advantages over existing adaptive counterparts.

In essence, the paper is motivated by the need to move beyond "one-size-fits-all" AI models towards systems that can dynamically adjust to individual user preferences, thereby enhancing user satisfaction and addressing the limitations of generalized reward functions in diverse user populations.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research comprises both theoretical analysis and empirical experimentation to address the challenge of personalizing reward models for individual human preferences.

**Theoretical Framework:**
The paper frames the problem within the principle of empirical risk minimization. It defines the goal as minimizing a generalization loss `L_D(c)` for a classifier `c` that models human preferences `P(y ≻ y' | x, h)`. Unlike standard settings, the data is not assumed to be identically and independently distributed (i.i.d.). Instead, data is collected from `m` distinct human raters, with `n` examples provided by each rater, resulting in a dataset `S`. Each tuple `(h_i, x_ij, y_ij, y'_ij, z_ij)` is labeled with a rater ID `h_i`.

1. **PAC Bound for Single Classifier (Proposition 1):** The paper derives a Probably Approximately Correct (PAC) bound to quantify the difference between the true generalization loss `L_D(c)` and the empirical loss `L_S(c)`. This bound `ϵ` reveals dependencies on:
 - The total number of raters (`m`).
 - The number of examples provided by each rater (`n`).
 - Two variance terms: `E[V(ℓ_c|H)]`, which measures the average loss variation per user, and `V(E[ℓ_c|H])`, which measures how the average loss varies across users.
 The analysis shows that `ϵ` is `O(1/√m)` and approaches a constant as `n` tends to infinity for a fixed `m`. This suggests that increasing the number of diverse raters (`m`) is more effective for generalization across users than merely increasing the number of examples per rater (`n`), for a fixed total budget. Specifically, minimizing the bound for a fixed total `k=nm` examples suggests setting `n=1` and `m=k`, implying many raters each providing few examples is optimal.

2. **PAC Bound for Hypothesis Class (Proposition 2):** The analysis is extended to bound the difference `L_D(˜c*) − L_D(c*)`, where `c*` is the optimal classifier in the hypothesis class `C` and `˜c*` is the empirical minimizer. This bound incorporates the "capacity" or complexity of the classifier set `C`, measured by its covering number `|C_α|`. The bound grows logarithmically with `|C_α|`, indicating that reasonable increases in model complexity do not incur excessively high costs in sample complexity.

3. **Comparison of User-Agnostic vs. User-Aware Models:** The theoretical framework is used to formally demonstrate when an adaptive model (`C`) is advantageous over a non-adaptive model (`C_0`). It shows that `C` can achieve a lower optimal population loss `L_D(c*)` when user preferences are heterogeneous (i.e., `V(E[ℓ_{C0}|H])` is large), even if it might increase the required sample complexity for the same generalization error in homogeneous settings.

**Reward-Feature Model (RFM) Architecture:**
The paper proposes a specific architecture for adaptive reward models called Reward-Feature Models (RFMs).
1. **Structure:** RFMs assume that an individual's reward function `r_h(x, y)` can be expressed as an inner product between a shared feature vector `ϕ_θ(x, y)` and a user-specific weight vector `w_h`: `r_h(x, y) = ⟨ϕ_θ(x, y), w_h⟩`.
 - `ϕ_θ(x, y)`: A parameterized function (e.g., a neural network) that extracts `d` general reward features from the context `x` and response `y`. The parameters `θ` are shared across all users.
 - `w_h`: A `d`-dimensional vector of user-specific coefficients, indicating how much user `h` values each feature.

2. **Training Phase:**
 - The `ϕ_θ` parameters (shared across all users) and the `w_h` vectors for the *known* training raters `ˆh ∈ ˆH` are learned jointly.
 - This is achieved by minimizing a logistic loss function derived from the Bradley-Terry model, effectively maximizing the likelihood of observed pairwise preferences `z_ij`.
 - The goal is for `ϕ_θ` to capture features common to the training group, which should generalize to the broader population. It emphasizes `e ≫ d`, where `e` is the number of shared parameters and `d` is the dimension of the feature space, to enable efficient adaptation.

3. **Adaptation Phase:**
 - For a *new, unseen* user `h ∈ H \ ˆH`, the `ϕ_θ` parameters are *frozen*.
 - Only the user-specific weight vector `w_h` is learned using a small set of preference examples `S_h` provided by that new user.
 - This adaptation problem simplifies to logistic regression, a convex optimization problem that can be solved efficiently with a limited number of examples.

**Advantages of RFM:**
The RFM architecture is chosen for several reasons: its convex adaptation problem, leveraging well-understood linear algebra techniques; potential for interpretability of features; ease of incorporating handcrafted features; and compatibility with existing zero-shot reinforcement learning methods that can synthesize policies for linear combinations of features.

**Empirical Evaluation:**
Experiments were conducted using Google DeepMind's Gemma 1.1 2B model on the UltraFeedback dataset, and additionally on the PersonalLLM dataset for scenarios with real reward models as users.
1. **Synthetic Users:** To rigorously test the theoretical predictions, synthetic users were generated whose preferences were defined by a linear combination of 13 interpretable features (structural, syntactic, semantic). This allowed for precise control over the number of raters (`m`) and the homogeneity of their preferences (`p`).
2. **Performance Metrics:** The main metric was inter-user test accuracy, which measures the ability of the adapted model to predict the preferences of held-out users on a test set.
3. **Comparisons:** RFM was compared against:
 - A non-adaptive baseline (current practice).
 - A "linear baseline" (adapting only the last layer of a user-agnostic model).
 - Non-linear adaptive architectures (inspired by Park et al.).
 - In-context personalization methods using prominent LLMs (Gemini 1.5 Pro, GPT-4o).
 - Variational Preference Learning (VPL) by Poddar et al. for intra-user generalization.
4. **Best-of-N Evaluation:** RFM's ability to steer LLM behavior was assessed using a best-of-n selection task with responses from larger Gemma models.
5. **Real Reward Models as Users:** Experiments simulated real-world scenarios by using existing, publicly available reward models as the "users" to provide preference labels on UltraFeedback and PersonalLLM datasets, evaluating RFM's performance in a more realistic, albeit still simulated, setting.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yields several findings across its theoretical analysis and empirical evaluations, supporting the utility of adaptive reward models.

**Theoretical Insights:**
1. **Impact of Raters vs. Examples:** The PAC bounds (Proposition 1) demonstrate that the approximation error `ϵ` for a given classifier is `O(1/√m)` with respect to the number of raters `m`, but approaches a constant as the number of examples per rater `n` increases indefinitely for a fixed `m`. This suggests that increasing the diversity of raters is more critical for reducing inter-user generalization error than simply collecting more examples from a limited set of raters, especially when considering a fixed total data budget. The optimal strategy for a fixed `k=nm` examples is `n=1` and `m=k` (many raters, few examples each).
2. **Role of Preference Heterogeneity:** The theory indicates that adaptive reward models (`C`) offer significant advantages over user-agnostic models (`C_0`) when there is considerable disagreement among users. In such scenarios, `C` can achieve a lower optimal population loss `L_D(c*)`, even if it incurs a higher complexity cost in terms of covering numbers (`|C_α|`).

**Empirical Results:**

1. **Validation of Theoretical Predictions (Synthetic Users):**
 - **Rater Count (`m`):** Experiments with synthetic users confirmed that RFM's performance (inter-user test accuracy) consistently improves with an increasing number of training raters (`m`), while the non-adaptive baseline's performance remains largely unchanged (Figure 1a).
 - **Preference Homogeneity (`p`):** RFM demonstrated greater robustness to heterogeneous preferences (lower `p` values) compared to the baseline. While both models' performance improved with increasing homogeneity, RFM's degradation in highly heterogeneous settings was less pronounced, especially when trained with a sufficient number of features (d ≥ 32) (Figure 1b).
 - **Adaptation Examples (`ˆn`):** RFM exhibited effective adaptation to new users with a small number of examples. Performance increased with `ˆn`, showing a substantial improvement from `ˆn=1` to `ˆn=30` (Table 1, Figure 1c), and continued to improve slightly with more examples, achieving around 79% accuracy with `ˆn=90`. Even with a single adaptation example (`ˆn=1`), RFM's accuracy was above random chance (0.5481).
 - **Rater-User Distribution Mismatch:** When the distribution of training raters `p` differed from that of held-out users `p`, RFM's performance was less negatively affected than the baseline, indicating better generalization under distribution shift in user preferences (Figure 8).

2. **Modulating LLM Outputs (Best-of-N):**
 - When used in a "best-of-n" selection task to steer LLM outputs, RFM(32) consistently outperformed the non-adaptive baseline. The fraction of times RFM's selected response was preferred by the synthetic user increased with `n` (the number of candidate responses), highlighting its practical utility in improving personalized LLM behavior (Figure 2).

3. **Comparisons with Adaptive Counterparts:**
 - **Linear Baseline:** A "linear baseline" (adapting only the final layer of a user-agnostic model) performed similarly to the non-adaptive baseline. This suggests that features learned without distinguishing between users are insufficient to capture the variance in population preferences required for effective personalization.
 - **Non-linear Adaptive Models:** Non-linear architectures, even with more adaptation examples (`ˆn=100`), performed poorly compared to RFM. This was attributed to the high number of parameters needing to be trained with limited adaptation data.
 - **In-Context Personalization (LLMs):** Leading LLMs (Gemini 1.5 Pro, GPT-4o) using in-context adaptation for 10-shot learning performed close to chance when adapting to the paper's synthetic user preferences (Figure 3). While they performed better when adapting to LLM-generated features like helpfulness (Table 3), the benefit of 10-shot in-context prompting over zero-shot was negligible, suggesting limitations in how current LLMs leverage diverse preference signals in context for arbitrary criteria.
 - **Variational Preference Learning (VPL):** RFM's intra-user generalization performance (61.61%) was found to be comparable to VPL's (61.49%) under a similar evaluation protocol, despite RFM's simpler architecture (Figure 10).

4. **Modelling Groups of Real Users (Reward Models as Users):**
 - In experiments where publicly available reward models acted as "users," RFM's performance either matched or significantly surpassed that of non-adaptive and linear baselines on both UltraFeedback and PersonalLLM datasets (Figure 4, Figure 11). This indicates that RFM is beneficial even when adapting to aggregated preferences of reward models, suggesting its potential for real-world application with truly individual human feedback.

In summary, the results consistently demonstrate that RFM provides a robust and effective method for personalizing reward models, showing clear advantages over non-adaptive and several adaptive baselines, particularly in settings with diverse user preferences and limited adaptation data.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) and the broader development of adaptive AI systems, particularly Large Language Models (LLMs).

**Significance:**
1. **Theoretical Foundation for Personalization:** The paper introduces a novel theoretical framework by formalizing and analyzing the problem of learning adaptive reward models with non-i.i.d. human preference data. The derived PAC bounds provide a foundational understanding of how approximation error depends on the number of raters and examples per rater, guiding optimal data collection strategies. This formal rigor is a notable advancement in the theoretical understanding of personalized learning from human preferences.
2. **Principled Adaptive Architecture:** The proposed Reward-Feature Model (RFM) architecture offers a principled and practical approach to learning user-specific reward functions. By disentangling shared feature learning from individual preference weights, RFM enables fast and reliable adaptation to new, unseen users with minimal additional data. The reduction of the adaptation problem to convex logistic regression is a key practical advantage, ensuring stability and efficiency.
3. **Empirical Validation and Comparative Advantage:** The comprehensive empirical evaluation, using both synthetic and simulated real-world users, robustly supports the theoretical predictions. RFM consistently outperforms non-adaptive baselines and demonstrates competitive or superior performance against various adaptive counterparts, including complex non-linear models and state-of-the-art LLM-based in-context learning. This validation establishes RFM as a viable and effective method for personalization.
4. **Compatibility with Existing RL Methods:** RFM's design, which expresses user preferences as a linear combination of features, makes it directly compatible with "zero-shot" RL methods. These methods can synthesize a policy on-the-fly that performs well under a linear reward function. This seamless integration means that once an RFM is adapted to a user, a specialized LLM (or other agent) can be almost immediately deployed, providing a rapid personalization pipeline.

**Potential Impact:**
1. **Enhanced User Experience for LLMs:** The most direct impact is the potential to significantly improve user satisfaction with LLMs and other AI agents. By moving beyond "average" preferences, personalized models can tailor outputs to individual tastes, styles, and values, leading to more relevant and engaging interactions. This could transform LLMs from generic tools into highly customized assistants.
2. **Inclusion and Representation of Diverse Views:** Adaptive reward models can address the critical issue of minority opinions and under-represented preferences being overlooked by aggregated reward functions. By allowing individual users to personalize models, the technology can promote inclusivity and ensure that AI outputs reflect a broader spectrum of human viewpoints, potentially empowering diverse communities in digital spaces.
3. **Broad Applicability Across Modalities:** While the research focuses on LLMs, the RFM framework is generalizable to any problem that can be framed as an RLHF task. This includes personalization for agents interacting in other modalities like images, sound, or video, and in complex multi-step reinforcement learning environments.
4. **Mitigation of Undesirable Behaviors:** The paper acknowledges the potential for negative societal impacts, such as the reinforcement of biases, sycophantic behavior, or the creation of "echo chambers." However, it also outlines mitigation strategies. For instance, clearly defined rubrics for data collection can distinguish genuine subjective disagreement from factual denial or unethical preferences. Furthermore, the LLM itself can act as a safeguard, where the adaptive reward model primarily modulates subjective aspects of output, rather than factual correctness or ethical boundaries.
5. **Guidance for Data Collection:** The theoretical insights directly inform practical data collection strategies for preference learning. The emphasis on sampling a diverse set of raters rather than exhaustively collecting data from a few raters can optimize resource allocation and improve the cost-effectiveness of personalization efforts.

In conclusion, this research provides both a robust theoretical underpinning and a practical, empirically validated solution for personalizing AI systems with human feedback. Its significance lies in enabling AI to better serve the diverse needs and preferences of individual users, while also contributing to ongoing discussions about the responsible deployment of advanced AI technologies.
