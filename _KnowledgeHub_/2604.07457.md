# CMP: Robust Whole-Body Tracking for Loco-Manipulation via Competence Manifold Projection

> [!summary] Summary
> Competence Manifold Projection (CMP) enhances robust whole-body tracking for legged mobile manipulators by projecting arbitrary task-space inputs onto a learned safety manifold. This method improved survival rates in out-of-distribution scenarios by up to 10-fold in simulation and significantly in real-world tests, enabling 'best-effort' task execution instead of failures while maintaining in-distribution tracking performance.

## Problem

- Existing whole-body control policies for legged mobile manipulators are fragile to Out-of-Distribution (OOD) inputs, leading to unpredictable or physically unsafe behaviors.
- Real-time enforcement of infinite-horizon safety is computationally intractable due to the temporal-spatial complexity and fragmented nature of the safe command subspace.
- Traditional safety solutions often disrupt task continuity (e.g., emergency stops) or fail to accurately distinguish unmastered/unsafe behaviors from inputs that are simply outside the training distribution.

## Method

![[2604.07457-method.png]]
*CMP pipeline: target trajectories in the TCP frame are encoded by an Intent Encoder into a raw latent intention for the Low-level Policy, while a TD-trained Safety Estimator and Isomorphic Latent Space enable O(1) safety enforcement by truncating latents beyond the safety radius.*

- The **CMP** (Competence Manifold Projection) framework projects arbitrary task-space inputs onto a ==learned safety manifold== using a ==hierarchical latent space== approach for whole-body control.
- A ==Lower-Bounded Safety Estimator==, trained via ==Temporal Difference learning==, approximates the ==Maximum Probability of Perpetual Safety== to distinguish truly unmastered intentions from benign OOD inputs.
- An ==Isomorphic Latent Space (ILS)== aligns safety levels with the geometry of the latent space, enabling real-time, ==O(1) projection== of potentially unsafe commands onto the closest safe hypersphere.

## Results

- In simulation, CMP achieved up to a **10-fold** increase in survival rate for OOD-Geometry tasks (**46.9%** vs. **4.7%**) and nearly a **6-fold** increase for OOD-Sensor noise (**40.3%** vs. **6.9%**), while maintaining similar in-distribution tracking accuracy.
- Real-world experiments showed CMP achieved a **100%** survival rate for in-distribution tasks and significantly higher rates for moderate (**93.3%**) and extreme (**86.7%**) out-of-distribution scenarios compared to baselines.
- The framework operates with low latency (**2.99 ms**), comparable to an unshielded baseline, enabling "best-effort" task continuation by smoothly projecting unsafe commands to the closest feasible intention.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Infinite-horizon safety for dynamic robotic systems can be analytically reduced to a frame-wise, single-step latent inclusion problem using a hierarchical latent framework and a 'Maximum Probability of Perpetual Safety' metric.
> - Training a safety estimator with a lower-bounded target via Temporal Difference learning is crucial for accurately distinguishing between truly unsafe behaviors and inputs merely outside the training distribution, thereby improving robustness.
> - An isomorphic mapping between safety probability and latent space geometry enables efficient, O(1) online projection of unsafe commands to a 'best-effort' safe alternative, preserving task continuity.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Legged mobile manipulators are designed for operation in unstructured environments and execution of complex tasks. Recent advancements in whole-body control (WBC) paradigms, such as UMI-on-Legs, have enabled global end-effector tracking in task space, offering compatibility with cross-embodiment data collection, teleoperation interfaces, and high-level planning policies. This approach mobilizes whole-body coordination and provides spatial translation generalization for WBC policies.

However, a challenge in these unified coordination approaches is the fragility of learned policies to Out-of-Distribution (OOD) inputs. These inputs, which can arise from sensor noise, teleoperation latency, or infeasible user commands, often cause the robot to exhibit unpredictable or physically unsafe behaviors. Existing policies are typically trained on specific trajectories or limited spatial distributions, making them vulnerable when inputs exceed these boundaries.

Traditional safety solutions in robotics often fall short in this context. Inference-time policy steering methods, while effective, can be computationally intensive, rendering them unsuitable for the high-frequency control loops required by dynamic WBC. Standard OOD detectors may misidentify "out-of-training-distribution" with "out-of-safe-distribution," failing to recognize actions within the training data that were never safely mastered. Furthermore, reactive triggers or emergency stops disrupt task continuity and lack mechanisms for graceful performance degradation or "best-effort" execution in unsafe situations.

Decoupled control schemes, such as those employing Model Predictive Control (MPC) or hierarchical learning, offer robustness by separating locomotion and manipulation. However, this modularity can restrict whole-body synergy and compatibility with task-space paradigms.

This work addresses the limitations of current end-to-end whole-body control policies by introducing a method to improve robustness against OOD inputs without sacrificing task performance or continuity. It aims to provide an efficient, "best-effort" safety mechanism that operates in real-time and allows the robot to remain as close as possible to an infeasible target without entering unsafe states.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust whole-body tracking framework for legged mobile manipulators that can process arbitrary task-space inputs while maintaining safety and task continuity, particularly when faced with Out-of-Distribution (OOD) inputs. The authors identify three specific challenges that motivate their proposed solution:

1. **Temporal-Spatial Complexity**: Safety in dynamic robotic systems is inherently coupled with the infinite-dimensional temporal sequence of future commands. For a whole-body control policy that receives end-effector trajectories, determining if a current command will lead to a perpetually safe future trajectory is computationally intractable. The feasible subspace of commands is topologically fragmented and sparse, making direct, real-time (O(1)) constraint enforcement in the original command space unfeasible. For example, a minor modification to a trajectory coordinate might require a significant, system-level reorientation for proper tracking.

2. **Boundary Ambiguity**: The set of state-goal pairs observed during training ($D_{train}$) does not perfectly represent the true set of safe state-goal pairs ($D_{safe}$). Policies may encounter inputs that are within the training distribution but lead to unmastered, unsafe behaviors, or inputs that are outside the training distribution but still safe. Consequently, standard OOD detection methods that rely on identifying deviations from the training distribution are insufficient for ensuring physical safety. There is a need for a safety metric that can distinguish between intentions the policy has mastered and those it has not, independent of whether they appeared in the training data.

3. **Seamless Enforcement**: Abrupt safety mechanisms, such as emergency stops or reactive fall recoveries, disrupt task execution and can risk hardware damage. For continuous and dynamic tasks performed by legged manipulators, a mechanism is required that can gracefully degrade performance and adjust commands to the closest safe behavior online. This necessitates an efficient projection function ($\Phi: G \rightarrow G$) that maps an arbitrary, potentially unsafe target command ($g_t$) to a semantically similar, safe command ($g'_t$) while minimizing the semantic distance between them and ensuring the robot remains within its safe operating envelope. This "best-effort" behavior is critical for maintaining task continuity and overall system reliability.

To address these challenges, the authors propose the Competence Manifold Projection (CMP) framework, aiming to develop a system that maintains full performance for safe inputs and autonomously degrades to the closest feasible behavior for unsafe ones, enabling "best-effort" tracking along the robot's capability boundary.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Competence Manifold Projection (CMP) framework is designed for robust whole-body control by projecting arbitrary task-space inputs onto a learned safety manifold. The architecture comprises an Intent Encoder ($\phi$), a Low-level Policy ($\pi$), and a Safety Estimator ($\omega$), forming a hierarchical structure.

**A. Frame-Wise Safety Scheme**
This scheme addresses the temporal-spatial complexity of infinite-horizon safety.
1. **Hierarchical Latent Framework**: The control logic is divided into two levels:
 - The **Intent Encoder ($\phi$)** (an MLP with hidden layers) encodes the task goal ($g_t$) conditioned on the current state ($s_t$) into a latent distribution $N(\mu_\phi, \Sigma_\phi)$, from which a latent command ($z_t$) is sampled. This conditioning aims to make the latent space purely represent future trajectory intent relative to the current state, theoretically transforming fragmented safe regions into a continuous manifold.
 - The **Low-level Policy ($\pi$)** (an MLP with hidden layers) generates whole-body actions ($a_t$) based on the latent intention ($z_t$) and current state ($s_t$).
2. **Temporal Safety Reduction**: The concept of **Maximum Probability of Perpetual Safety**, $W_\pi(s_t, z_t)$, is introduced. This metric quantifies the long-term viability of executing $z_t$, representing the probability that the system remains within the safe state space ($S_{safe}$) indefinitely, assuming optimal future latent commands.
 - Using the Markov property, this infinite-horizon objective is decomposed into a Bellman recursive equation (Eq. 3).
 - The **Competence Manifold ($C_{\pi\delta}(s)$)** is defined as the set of latent commands from which the policy can maintain safety with a probability of at least $1-\delta$. This definition analytically links the infinite-horizon safety requirement to a verifiable single-step membership test: $z_t \in C_{\pi\delta}(s_t)$.

**B. Lower-Bounded Safety Estimator**
This component addresses the boundary ambiguity issue by distinguishing mastered from unmastered intentions.
1. **Approximation of $W_\pi$**: A **Safety Estimator ($\omega$)** (an MLP with hidden layers) is trained to approximate $W_\pi(s_t, z_t)$.
2. **Addressing Computational Hurdles**:
 - **Maximization over Latent Space**: The intractable maximization term $\max_{z'} W_\pi(s_{t+1}, z')$ in the Bellman equation is approximated by the safety value at the latent origin: $c_{W_\omega}(s_{t+1}, 0)$. This approximation assumes peak safety aligns with the latent origin, an assumption reinforced by the Isomorphic Latent Space (ILS) design.
 - **Temporal Difference Learning**: To balance estimation bias and signal propagation, Temporal Difference (TD($\lambda_{safe}$)) learning is employed, with $\lambda_{safe}=0.8$. This approach computes a training target ($W_{target,t}$) recursively backward through trajectories (Eq. 6). The TD(λ) mechanism ensures a conservative, lower-bound estimation of true safety, accelerating failure signal backpropagation without completely loosening the bound.
3. **Training**: The estimator is optimized via Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE) loss (Eq. 7) to track the safety target.

**C. Isomorphic Latent Space (ILS)**
ILS enables seamless, O(1) safety enforcement by aligning safety levels with latent space geometry.
1. **Mapping Construction**: A monotonically decreasing cubic inverse function ($R_t$) (Eq. 8) maps the safety probability ($W_{target,t}$) to a variance parameter. $R_t$ is clipped to prevent KL divergence explosion and adaptively adjusted using an $\epsilon$ term for stable gradient scaling. Lower safety probabilities are mapped to larger radii.
2. **Implicit Isomorphism**: The Intent Encoder ($\phi$) is trained to minimize a dynamic KL divergence loss ($L_{ILS}$) (Eq. 9). This loss encourages the latent distribution $N(\mu_\phi, \Sigma_\phi)$ to align with a spherical Gaussian $N(0, R_t^2 I)$. Consequently, the latent space is organized such that the norm of a latent vector (||z||) is proportional to $R_t$. This effectively reshapes irregular competence boundaries into regular hyperspheres where safety decreases radially from the origin.
3. **Runtime Competence Manifold Projection**: During inference, safety verification becomes an O(1) norm check: $||z_{raw_t}|| \le R_{safe}$, where $R_{safe}$ is a configurable safety radius. If an OOD command results in $||z_{raw_t}||$ exceeding $R_{safe}$, **Competence Manifold Projection (CMP)** is applied: the raw latent vector is truncated (Eq. 10) to the closest point on the hypersphere defined by $R_{safe}$. This operation ensures that the projected command $z_{safe_t}$ is within the competence manifold, providing the "best-effort" feasible intent without arbitrarily resetting the system.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents findings from both simulation and real-world experiments to evaluate the performance of CMP against baselines.

**A. Simulation Experiments**
Experiments were conducted in Isaac Gym using a Unitree Go2 quadruped with a Hexfellow Saber robotic arm and UMI gripper. Three scenarios were tested: In-Distribution (ID), OOD-Geometry (targets in the robot's rear workspace), and OOD-Sensor (ID trajectories with injected VIO noise). Metrics included Position Error ($e_p$), Orientation Error ($e_r$), and Survival Rate (SR).

- **Overall Performance Comparison (Table I)**:
 - **Baselines (UMI-on-Legs, Latent Shielding, Neural CBF)**: UMI-on-Legs exhibited good ID tracking precision but suffered catastrophic failures in OOD conditions (SR as low as 4.7% for OOD-Geometry). Neural CBF and Latent Shielding provided only marginal improvements in OOD SR, with Latent Shielding often interrupting tasks and Neural CBF struggling with complex legged dynamics.
 - **CMP (Ours)**: CMP demonstrated a substantial improvement in survival rates without significant degradation in ID tracking precision. At a balanced safety radius of $R_{safe} = 2.0$, CMP achieved a 10-fold SR improvement in OOD-Geometry (46.9% vs. 4.7% for UMI-on-Legs) and a nearly 6-fold increase under OOD-Sensor noise (40.3% vs. 6.9%). The ID position error for CMP was 4.5 cm, comparable to UMI-on-Legs' 4.9 cm. CMP's approach was characterized as a "best-effort" projection that smoothly bounds actions to competence boundaries, preserving intent semantics.

- **Ablation Studies (Table II, Fig. 4)**:
 - **CVAE (Frame-Wise Safety Scheme)**: Provided some baseline robustness by truncating latent commands but lacked a structured safety assessment.
 - **Safe-CVAE (SCVAE, with Safety Estimator)**: Improved over CVAE at larger safety radii by filtering unsafe data. However, at smaller radii, its performance degraded significantly because the latent origin was not optimized for safety or density, leading to ineffective truncation.
 - **CMP (with Isomorphic Latent Space)**: Achieved a superior trade-off curve between accuracy and safety. By aligning safety with latent geometry, CMP preserved a richer set of functional behaviors even at smaller radii, outperforming CVAE and SCVAE, which experienced rapid performance degradation.

- **Validation of Safety Estimator (Fig. 5, Table IV)**:
 - **Qualitative Analysis**: During a raw OOD sideways push command (without projection), the Safety Estimator's values ($W_{max}$, $W_{proj}$, $W_{raw}$) responded as expected. In a normal state, all values were high, with $W_{max} > W_{raw} \approx W_{proj}$. When the target became OOD, $W_{max} > W_{proj} > W_{raw}$ was observed, indicating correct penalization of risky raw inputs and a safer projected intent. During a near-fall state, all W values dropped significantly, confirming the estimator's ability to capture state-dependent risks.
 - **Quantitative Analysis**: A human validation study involving 500 OOD rollout states showed a strong correlation (approximately 97.7%) between the Safety Estimator's predictions ($W_{max}$) and human judgments of whether a state was "salvageable" or "unsalvageable." This suggested the estimator effectively captured the underlying safety semantics.

**B. Real-World Experiments**
Validation was performed on a physical Unitree Go2 robot with a Hexfellow Saber arm across 15 target trajectories categorized into ID, Moderate OOD, and Extreme OOD scenarios.

- **Robustness to OOD Commands (Table III, Fig. 6)**:
 - **ID Scenarios**: CMP was the only method to achieve a 100% survival rate across standard tasks, compared to approximately 80% for UMI-on-Legs and Neural CBF. Tracking errors were comparable to baselines, indicating no hindrance to nominal precision.
 - **Moderate OOD**: For tasks outside the training distribution (e.g., sideways tracking, diagonal jumping), UMI-on-Legs consistently failed (0% SR). Latent Shielding and Neural CBF showed partial resilience (33.3% and 60.0% SR, respectively). CMP achieved a 93.3% SR, demonstrating "best-effort" strategies (e.g., small, safe turns for sideways pushing) to accomplish originally unstable tasks.
 - **Extreme OOD**: In severe deviations from training (e.g., backward tracking), all baselines struggled (UMI-on-Legs 0.0%, Latent Shielding 20.0%, Neural CBF 40.0%). CMP achieved an 86.7% SR by truncating unsafe command components to prioritize stability, generating safe motions that semantically resembled the target intents.
 - **Latency**: CMP exhibited low latency (2.99 ms), comparable to the un-shielded baseline (2.97 ms) and significantly faster than Latent Shielding (3.89 ms) and Neural CBF (5.36 ms), which incurred computational overhead.

- **Robustness to Sensor Divergence (Fig. 7)**:
 - UMI-on-Legs often entered a positive feedback loop during rapid motion, where VIO drift led to unexpected inputs, aggressive corrective actions, increased body oscillation, and eventual destabilization.
 - CMP detected the low survival probability associated with anomalous VIO-induced goals and projected the latent command to a safe region. This dampened the response to sensor noise, blocking the hazardous feedback loop and maintaining system stability.

- **Observed Failures**: Out of 45 hardware trials with CMP, 3 failures occurred. These were attributed to either estimator inaccuracy (overprediction of safety leading to oscillatory motions) or imperfect latent mapping (projected commands remaining unsafe despite the statistical spherical boundary).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Competence Manifold Projection (CMP) framework represents an advancement in the robust control of legged mobile manipulators, particularly in scenarios involving Out-of-Distribution (OOD) inputs.

The work addresses a challenge in modern whole-body control paradigms where policies, while offering dynamic capabilities and compatibility with advanced interfaces, remain vulnerable to sensor noise or infeasible commands. By formulating safety as a frame-wise, single-step latent inclusion problem, CMP provides a computationally efficient approach to real-time safety enforcement. The introduction of the Lower-Bounded Safety Estimator allows for the distinction between mastered and unmastered behaviors, thereby moving beyond simple distribution matching for safety assessment. Furthermore, the Isomorphic Latent Space (ILS) aligns safety probability with geometric properties in the latent space, enabling an O(1) projection mechanism. This operational efficiency is relevant for high-frequency whole-body control loops.

The experimental results indicate that CMP can enhance the survival rate of legged manipulators in OOD scenarios, achieving up to a 10-fold improvement in simulation and demonstrating significant gains in real-world deployments, while maintaining comparable in-distribution tracking performance. This suggests an improvement in the reliability and resilience of these robotic systems.

A notable aspect of CMP is its ability to facilitate "best-effort" generalization behaviors. Instead of immediate failure or abrupt termination, the system projects unsafe commands onto the closest feasible intent within its competence boundaries. This allows the robot to continue task progress safely, albeit with adjusted performance, rather than disrupting operations. This behavior has implications for robotic systems operating in dynamic or uncertain environments, where graceful degradation is preferable to catastrophic failure.

The approach's demonstrated robustness against both geometrically OOD commands and sensor-induced divergence suggests its potential for improving the practical deployment of legged manipulators in diverse and unconstrained settings. By providing a method to bridge the gap between high-performance learning policies and real-world deployment reliability, CMP contributes to the development of more autonomous and dependable robotic systems.

Future research directions, such as scaling the framework to humanoid robots and developing adaptive mechanisms for tuning the safety radius, suggest further potential for expanding the applicability and sophistication of this approach.
