# Calf-Integrated Arms for Bimanual Quadruped Loco-Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at University College London developed calf-integrated manipulators for quadruped robots, enabling bimanual, ground-level grasping while maintaining a four-foot stance. A vision-language model-based planner autonomously sequences complex loco-manipulation tasks in simulation, demonstrating capabilities like cooperative lifting and object transfer.

## Problem

- Quadruped robots lack advanced manipulation capabilities while retaining their inherent agility and mobility.
- Existing manipulation designs either place arms too high for ground-level interaction or require sacrificing stable four-foot stances for bimanual tasks.
- There is a need for autonomous, long-horizon skill sequencing to enable quadruped robots to perform complex tasks based on environmental context and natural language instructions.

## Method

- A novel mechanical design integrates compact ==4-DoF manipulators== into each front calf of a Unitree Go2 robot, featuring a prismatic slider for ground reach and a yaw joint for bimanual cooperation.
- A two-layer control architecture combines a high-level vision-language model (VLM) for conditional skill sequencing with a low-level **PPO**-trained locomotion policy and an ==IK-based== manipulation module.
- Perception for grasping relies on a head-mounted RGB-D camera to detect fiducial markers, with 3D grasp points estimated via ==back-projection== and continuously tracked by a closed-loop controller.

## Results

- Successfully demonstrated three bimanual loco-manipulation tasks in simulation: a long-horizon cabinet task (carrying, opening, placing), cooperative two-handed lifting of a box, and a left-to-right object handover, all while maintaining a four-foot stance.
- The VLM-based planner autonomously selected and sequenced skills (e.g., grasp basket, open door, place in cabinet) for the long-horizon cabinet task, adapting to the task state.
- Grasp success rates remained between **70-74%** for depth noise levels up to **10 mm** standard deviation, indicating robustness of the perception and manipulation system to sensor noise.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating manipulators into the calves allows for bimanual, ground-level interaction without compromising the robot's four-foot stance stability or mobility, addressing a trade-off in prior designs.
> - Vision-language models can effectively act as high-level planners, dynamically sequencing predefined skills to execute complex, long-horizon loco-manipulation tasks in unstructured environments.
> - The closed-loop manipulation controller exhibits robustness to moderate depth sensor noise, maintaining high grasp success rates even with typical sensor limitations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research addresses a persistent challenge in robotics: endowing quadruped robots with advanced manipulation capabilities while maintaining their inherent agility and mobility. Quadruped robots are recognized for their ability to navigate complex, human-scale environments, yet their capacity to interact with objects (loco-manipulation) remains an active area of development.

Existing approaches to quadruped loco-manipulation generally fall into several categories:

- **Trunk-mounted arms:** This is a common strategy, where a dedicated manipulator arm is attached to the robot's torso. Examples include articulated arms on platforms like ANYmal and Spot. While these designs offer a large workspace and enable dynamic grasping, they typically place the arm high on the body, making it difficult to reach objects at ground level without complex maneuvers. Furthermore, most trunk-mounted systems carry only a single arm, precluding simultaneous two-handed tasks.
- **Reusing the legs as manipulators:** Some research explores using the robot's bare legs for manipulation, such as pushing or pressing buttons. This approach, often enabled by reinforcement learning or multi-contact optimization, avoids additional hardware. However, a bare foot lacks prehension, and crucially, using a leg for manipulation requires it to leave the support polygon, compromising the robot's stable stance.
- **Leg-mounted grippers:** An advancement in leg reuse involves attaching compact grippers directly to the legs. The LocoMan system, for instance, mounts grippers on the front calves, allowing for two-handed tasks by having the robot rear onto its hind legs. While this frees both front manipulators, it immobilizes the base and removes the front feet from the support polygon, limiting the robot's ability to walk or maintain a stable, four-foot stance during manipulation.
- **Reconfiguring the legs:** Other research focuses on morphological adaptation of the legs themselves, through designs such as embedded shape morphing, sprawling-angle adaptation, or modular/telescopic limbs. These designs primarily enhance locomotion versatility and terrain adaptability rather than providing prehensile manipulation capabilities.

Beyond hardware, the field has seen a growing trend towards incorporating high-level cognitive capabilities, particularly driven by large language models (LLMs) and vision-language models (VLMs). These models are being explored for sequencing locomotion and manipulation skills, enabling long-horizon tasks and autonomous planning based on environmental context and natural language instructions.

The work presented in this paper positions itself by addressing key limitations of these prior designs. Specifically, it aims to achieve ground-level grasping, bimanual manipulation, and the maintenance of a four-foot stance simultaneously—capabilities often traded off in other systems. By integrating manipulators into the front calves and pairing this with a VLM-based planner, the authors seek to combine these functionalities for autonomous, long-horizon loco-manipulation, thereby extending the utility of quadruped robots in complex scenarios.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the observation that existing quadruped loco-manipulation designs rarely combine three critical capabilities necessary for versatile interaction with human-scale environments: reaching objects at ground level, performing bimanual tasks, and sequencing skills autonomously over extended periods. While quadruped robots are adept at locomotion in cluttered spaces, their ability to act on objects within these spaces is often constrained by hardware design choices.

The key objectives of this work are articulated through two main contributions:

1. **To develop a novel mechanical design for quadruped manipulators that enables bimanual loco-manipulation and ground-level grasping while maintaining a stable four-foot stance.** The authors identified that trunk-mounted arms sit too high for ground-level interaction, and leg-mounted grippers often require the robot to rear onto its hind legs, fixing its base. The objective was to create a manipulator integrated into each front calf of a Unitree Go2 robot that could reach the ground and cooperate bimanually without compromising the robot's stability or mobility. This required careful consideration of joint configuration (prismatic slider for vertical reach, yaw joint for inter-arm cooperation) and compactness.
2. **To introduce a high-level control framework that leverages a vision-language model (VLM) for autonomous, long-horizon skill sequencing.** The motivation here is to move beyond fixed scripts, allowing the robot to select and execute appropriate skills from a predefined library based on real-time visual input and the current task state. This objective aims to enable autonomous behavior for complex tasks that involve multiple steps and interactions, such as opening a cabinet while carrying an object. The VLM is intended to provide conditional skill selection at each skill boundary, facilitating adaptive task execution.

In essence, the motivation is to bridge the gap between quadruped locomotion and advanced, coordinated manipulation, allowing robots to perform complex, multi-step tasks in unstructured environments where ground-level interaction and bimanual cooperation are essential, all while retaining the benefits of quadrupedal stability and mobility.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology involves a dual approach encompassing both hardware design and control architecture, primarily evaluated in simulation.

**A. Mechanical Design**

The core of the hardware approach is the integration of a manipulator arm into each front calf of a Unitree Go2 quadruped robot. This design ensures that each front leg can serve both for locomotion and manipulation.

- **Joint Configuration:** Each front leg is modified to include four additional degrees of freedom (DoF) for manipulation, supplementing the three original leg joints.
 - **q1 (Prismatic Slider):** A 0.105 m stroke slider is housed inside the rebuilt calf, contributing to a compact structure. Its primary function is to extend the gripper downwards, enabling reach to ground-level objects. It is driven by a non-backdrivable stepper motor, allowing it to hold a lifted load without continuous power.
 - **q2 (Revolute Pitch Joint):** This joint pitches the gripper, facilitating fine approach and orientation adjustments during grasping. It uses a servo for closed-loop position control.
 - **q3 (Revolute Yaw Joint):** This joint rotates about the vertical axis, sweeping the gripper towards the body centerline. This capability is critical for allowing the two front arms to cooperate on a single object. It also uses a servo for closed-loop position control.
 - **q4 (Parallel-Jaw Gripper):** This gripper has a 4 cm opening, suitable for grasping various objects. It is servo-controlled.
- **Workspace:** The integrated design allows the gripper's pinch point to reach from 0.01 m above the floor up to 0.34 m. The yaw joint (q3) ensures that the workspaces of the left and right grippers overlap at the robot's body centerline, enabling bimanual cooperation. This ground-level reach is achieved by placing the arm base low (0.18 m above the floor) compared to typical trunk-mounted arms (0.36 m).
- **Locomotion Trade-offs:** The authors acknowledge that integrating the manipulator increases the mass and swing inertia of the calf. The rebuilt calf also slightly restricts the knee's full fold by 8 degrees, though this is stated to only affect the deepest crouch and not normal walking step clearance.

**B. Control Architecture**

A two-layer control architecture is implemented, combining high-level planning with low-level execution.

- **High-level Skill Planner:** A vision-language model (VLM), specifically Kimi K2.6, is employed via a cloud API. The VLM is responsible for selecting discrete skills from a predefined library based on the head-camera image, the user task prompt, and a structured world state (binary progress flags, execution history). The VLM is queried only at skill boundaries, allowing each skill to run to completion before the next decision, maintaining a stable locomotion stance during VLM processing.
- **Skill Library:** A set of predefined skills (S) is established, categorized into single-arm and bimanual modes. Each skill is implemented as a finite state machine (FSM) composed of various phases. Common phases include `SEARCH` (yawing to locate a goal), `APPROACH` (walking to a standoff distance), `PRE-ALIGN` (body rotation), `EXTEND` (arm movement to reach pose), `SERVO` (closed-loop tracking of grasp target with IK), `CLOSE` (gripper actuation), `LIFT-TEST` (object lifting verification), `RECOVER` (error handling), and `DONE`. Bimanual tasks introduce specialized phases such as `SET-DOWN`, `RE-GRASP` for handovers, and `DUAL-ALIGN`, `DUAL-CLOSE`, `DUAL-LIFT` for cooperative lifts.
- **Locomotion Module:** A neural-network policy, trained in Isaac Lab (Isaac Sim 5.1.0) using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), governs the robot's locomotion. The policy operates at 50 Hz, taking 45-dimensional observations (base angular velocity, gravity, velocity command, joint positions/velocities, previous action) and outputting 12-dimensional position residuals for the leg joints. Domain randomization (friction, restitution, added base mass) is used during training to enhance robustness. The policy is designed to maintain a four-foot stance throughout, preserving the Go2's velocity-tracking interface.
- **Perception Module:** Object detection for grasping relies on small red fiducial markers placed at grasp points. RGB thresholding (R > 150, G < 80, B < 80) is used to segment the marker in the head-camera image. The 3D grasp point (pg) is estimated by back-projecting two opposite points on the marker's outline through the depth image into world coordinates and taking their midpoint. Partial detections are handled by averaging recent full detections.
- **Manipulation Module:** A 3-DoF Damped Least-Squares (DLS) inverse kinematics (IK) solver controls the manipulator joints (q1, q2, q3) to place the gripper's pinch point on the target pg. The gripper joint (q4) is directly commanded to open or close. The DLS solver uses a damping factor (λ) to balance tracking accuracy and stability near kinematic singularities. It operates in two modes: an open-loop search from multiple configurations for a global optimal reach, and a closed-loop tracking mode from the current configuration for continuous motion. An optional cap on the slider (q1) prioritizes prismatic extension over gripper folding.

**C. Simulation Setup**

All evaluations are conducted in simulation. Training is performed in Isaac Lab, leveraging 4096 parallel environments, and evaluation runs in MuJoCo. The robot receives natural-language instructions and must operate in scenes with unknown object poses, relying solely on its head-mounted RGB-D camera.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research demonstrates the capabilities of the calf-integrated manipulator design and the two-layer control architecture through a series of bimanual loco-manipulation tasks conducted entirely in simulation.

**A. Versatile Bimanual Loco-Manipulation Tasks**

The system successfully performs three distinct bimanual tasks, each executed while all four feet of the quadruped robot remain planted on the ground:

- **Long-horizon cabinet task:** In this task, the robot is instructed to "Place the basket in the cabinet." The robot first uses its left arm to carry a basket. It then walks to a cabinet, uses its right arm to open the cabinet door, and subsequently places the basket inside. This task chains locomotion, one-handed carrying, bimanual cooperation (implied by carrying while manipulating), and object placement.
- **Cooperative two-handed lift:** The robot is tasked with lifting a single box equipped with two side handles. Both front arms simultaneously grasp the box handles and raise it together. This demonstrates the effectiveness of the yaw joints (q3) in enabling the left and right grippers to overlap and cooperate on a shared object at the body's frontal centerline.
- **Left-to-right handover:** This task involves the robot grasping an object with its left arm, transferring it to its right arm at the robot's centerline without placing the object down, and then setting the object down with the right arm. This again highlights the utility of the overlapping workspace at the centerline for inter-arm cooperation.

**B. Comparison with Alternative Designs**

The authors emphasize that these demonstrations highlight the advantages of their planted-stance design over existing loco-manipulation paradigms:

- **Trunk-mounted arms:** Such designs typically feature a single arm and cannot perform tasks requiring simultaneous bimanual actions, such as carrying an object with one arm while manipulating with the other (e.g., the cabinet task).
- **LocoMan:** While LocoMan features leg-mounted grippers for bimanual tasks, it achieves this by rearing onto its hind legs. This action fixes the robot's base, preventing any locomotion or repositioning during manipulation, which is required for tasks like approaching the cabinet or performing a handover that involves some base movement. The proposed design maintains a four-foot stance throughout these operations.

**C. VLM Skill Selection and Reasoning**

The long-horizon cabinet task serves as a key demonstration of the VLM-based planner's autonomous reasoning. Given the instruction "Place the basket in the cabinet," the VLM dynamically sequences skills. The process involves:

- **World-state schema:** The planner initializes binary flags to track task progress (e.g., basket detected, basket held, door open, basket placed).
- **Iterative skill selection:** At each skill boundary, the VLM reads the head-camera image, updates the progress flags, and selects the next skill based on its reasoning. In the demonstrated run, the sequence was `grasp basket`, `open door`, `place in cabinet`, and `done`. This order is not pre-scripted but emerges from the VLM's interpretation of the scene and task state, allowing for adaptive behavior (e.g., reordering or repeating skills if necessary).
- **Performance:** The actual robot motion for the cabinet task was 18.6 seconds (7.1s to grasp, 9.1s to open door, 2.4s to place). The VLM planner latency, while the robot held a stable stance, was 16–40 seconds per query, demonstrating the computational cost of cloud-hosted VLM inference.

**D. Robustness to Depth-Sensor Noise**

The perception system's robustness to depth noise was evaluated by injecting zero-mean Gaussian noise into depth measurements during marker-based grasp perception.

- **Results:** Grasp success rates remained between 70–74% for noise levels up to 10 mm standard deviation, dropping to 54% at 30 mm standard deviation. This resilience is attributed to the closed-loop controller's continuous re-estimation of the grasp point (pg), which helps to mitigate zero-mean errors. Commodity RGB-D sensors are reported to operate with millimetre-scale noise, suggesting the system can tolerate typical real-world sensor limitations.

**E. Discussion on Limitations**

The authors identify several limitations of the current design:

- **Mechanical:** The lack of a roll joint in the manipulator restricts the gripper's ability to rotate objects about the approach axis, limiting the types of objects that can be grasped (e.g., objects requiring specific handle orientations).
- **Skill Library:** The VLM's capabilities are confined to selecting from a predefined library of skills. It cannot generate new skills, create novel states, or adapt strategies for complex scenarios like re-grasping at new contact points, rotating objects in hand, or obstacle avoidance while carrying.
- **Perception:** Grasping currently relies on fiducial markers. General object detection models like YOLO-World and SAM can localize objects as bounding boxes or masks, but these do not directly provide a precise grasp point suitable for the narrow 4 cm gripper aperture, necessitating future work on dedicated grasp-synthesis networks.
- **Simulation-only:** All results are obtained in simulation, which means the system's performance in real-world conditions (actual sensing, actuation, and contact dynamics) remains to be empirically verified.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of robotics by addressing several long-standing challenges in quadruped loco-manipulation, specifically by enabling capabilities that are often mutually exclusive in existing designs.

The **primary significance** lies in the demonstration of a quadruped robot capable of:
- **Ground-level grasping:** The calf-integrated manipulator design allows the robot to pick up objects directly from the floor, extending its operational range to common human environments.
- **Bimanual manipulation with a stable stance:** By keeping all four feet planted during manipulation, the system maintains stability and mobility, enabling tasks like cooperative lifting and handovers that are not possible with single-arm or rearing-based bimanual systems that immobilize the base.
- **Autonomous, long-horizon skill sequencing:** The integration of a vision-language model for high-level planning enables the robot to dynamically select and execute a sequence of skills based on environmental perception and task goals, moving beyond pre-scripted behaviors.

This combination of capabilities has the **potential to significantly impact** the applicability of quadruped robots in a broader range of complex, unstructured tasks. For example:
- **Logistics and industrial applications:** Robots could efficiently interact with diverse objects on the ground, load/unload items, or organize workspaces requiring two-handed coordination.
- **Search and rescue:** Enhanced manipulation could allow robots to clear debris, open doors, or retrieve objects in challenging environments.
- **Domestic assistance:** The ability to pick up items from the floor, open cabinets, and carry objects could pave the way for more versatile service robots in homes.

The research also lays a foundation for future advancements. The mechanical design, particularly the prismatic slider and pitch joint, hints at **further potential applications** such as using the arms as self-locking leg extensions for stilt-like locomotion, or enabling objects to be transferred onto the robot's trunk.

While the current work is limited to simulation, the successful demonstration of these integrated capabilities in a virtual environment represents a crucial step. The immediate next step of building a physical prototype and bridging the sim-to-real gap will be critical for validating the robustness of the marker-based perception, grasping mechanisms, and control under real-world conditions, thereby translating the simulated potential into practical robotic applications.
