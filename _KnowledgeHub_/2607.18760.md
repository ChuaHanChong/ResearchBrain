# Koopman DCM: Unstable Eigenfunctions as Data-driven Representations for Legged Balancing

> [!summary] Summary
> This work introduces a data-driven approach to balance control for legged robots by re-framing Divergent Components of Motion (DCMs) as unstable Koopman eigenfunctions. A neural network learns these Koopman DCMs directly from robot data, leading to a halved ZMP tracking error on a real wheeled biped robot and a 0% fall rate when used in Model Predictive Control for a simulated humanoid.

## Problem

- Balance control in legged robots has traditionally relied on reduced models (e.g., Linear Inverted Pendulum) and model-based DCMs, which limits their applicability to complex, nonlinear robot dynamics.
- Traditional DCM formulations derive their natural frequency from fixed kinematic parameters, often failing to capture the full, complex system behavior.
- Developing robust and adaptable balance controllers for legged robots without heavy reliance on precise analytical models is a standing challenge.

## Method

- Divergent Components of Motion (DCMs) are re-conceptualized as ==unstable eigenfunctions of the Koopman operator==, transforming their discovery into an eigenfunction search problem for general nonlinear systems.
- A data-driven pipeline is developed to learn these **Koopman DCMs** and their associated unstable eigenvalues directly from closed-loop measurement-action data, using a ==neural network (MLP)== as the eigenfunction encoder.
- A ==multi-step TD(λ) loss== function is employed during training, which proved essential for robustly identifying the unstable eigenvalue and eigenfunction from noisy, real-robot experimental data.

## Results

- On a real Cookie wheeled biped robot, the learned Koopman DCMs approximately **halved** the ZMP tracking Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) compared to a kinematic LIP-based baseline across various reference patterns.
- In PyBullet simulations with a Unitree G1 humanoid, MPC utilizing learned Koopman DCMs achieved a **0%** fall rate (**0** out of **90** trials) while also improving ZMP tracking, in contrast to a **33%** fall rate for a proportional DCM feedback controller.
- Initial validation on a simulated Linear Inverted Pendulum model confirmed the method's ability to accurately recover the ground-truth natural frequency with high precision.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The concept of DCMs, traditionally tied to reduced models, can be generalized to complex nonlinear systems by viewing them as unstable Koopman eigenfunctions.
> - Utilizing multi-step prediction losses (TD(λ)) is crucial for robustly learning the unstable natural frequency (eigenvalue) from real-robot data, where one-step losses often lead to trivial solutions.
> - Integrating the learned Koopman DCMs into Model Predictive Control (MPC) as hard viability constraints significantly enhances safety and prevents falls in complex robot behaviors.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

Balance control in legged locomotion has historically relied on reduced models, such as the Linear Inverted Pendulum (LIP). These models simplify complex robot dynamics into low-dimensional templates that allow for tractable control laws. A central concept within this framework is the Divergent Component of Motion (DCM), which isolates the unstable mode of the robot's dynamics, reducing balance control to the regulation of a single quantity. However, existing DCM formulations are typically model-based, derived from simplified dynamics (like the LIP), and their natural frequency is defined by fixed kinematic parameters (e.g., center-of-mass height). This limits their applicability to the full, complex, and nonlinear dynamics of real legged robots.

Concurrently, the Koopman operator framework has emerged as a method to analyze and control nonlinear systems by embedding them into a globally linear, albeit infinite-dimensional, representation. Prior applications of Koopman analysis in control often focus on identifying eigenvalues near zero, which correspond to conserved or slowly varying quantities. This research departs from that trend by deliberately targeting unstable eigenpairs with large eigenvalues, aligning with the necessity to control unstable modes in legged balance. The work integrates data-driven methods, such as those inspired by Dynamic Mode Decomposition (DMD) and Extended DMD (EDMD), which approximate the Koopman operator from data. By re-framing DCMs as Koopman eigenfunctions, this study aims to generalize the concept beyond reduced models and enable data-driven learning directly from robot interactions, addressing a long-standing challenge in robust legged robot control. It also intersects with Model Predictive Control (MPC) by providing a state representation suitable for incorporating viability constraints, a critical aspect of ensuring robot safety and stability.

### 2. Key objectives and motivation

The primary objective of this research is to advance the state of balance control for legged robots by developing a data-driven, model-agnostic approach to derive and utilize Divergent Components of Motion (DCMs). The authors identify two key limitations of traditional DCMs: their reliance on reduced models (like the Linear Inverted Pendulum, LIP) and their derivation from model-based parameters rather than direct system behavior.

To address these limitations, the study sets out to achieve four main contributions:

1. **Re-casting DCMs as Koopman Eigenfunctions:** The work proposes a novel formulation where DCMs are conceptualized as unstable eigenfunctions of the Koopman operator. This re-framing transforms the challenge of finding new, generalizable DCMs into an eigenfunction search problem within the Koopman framework, moving beyond the constraints of specific reduced-order models.
2. **Developing a Data-driven Training Pipeline:** A core motivation is to learn these Koopman DCMs directly from real-robot data. The authors develop a pipeline that uses closed-loop measurement-action data, making the approach adaptable to various robotic platforms without requiring precise analytical models. This objective aims to bypass the complexities and potential inaccuracies of kinematic or dynamic modeling.
3. **Improving Real-Robot Performance:** A practical objective is to demonstrate that DCMs learned through this data-driven approach can enhance the performance of real legged robots. Specifically, the research aims to show that deploying these Koopman DCMs in a proportional feedback control loop improves the tracking of reference walking patterns, thereby demonstrating their utility in practical robotic applications.
4. **Enabling Viability Constraints in Model Predictive Control (MPC):** Beyond feedback control, the study seeks to leverage the learned DCMs as intrinsic states for advanced control strategies. A specific objective is to show that these data-driven DCMs can be effectively integrated into Model Predictive Control as hard viability constraints, offering improved safety guarantees and robustness against falling, particularly for complex humanoid robots in simulated environments.

The overarching motivation is to create more robust, adaptable, and less model-dependent balance controllers for legged robots, ultimately leading to improved locomotion capabilities on complex, real-world terrain.

### 3. Methodology and approach

The methodology presented involves re-conceptualizing the Divergent Component of Motion (DCM) within the Koopman operator framework and then developing a data-driven pipeline to learn these Koopman DCMs from experimental robot data.

#### 3.1. From DCMs to Koopman Eigenfunctions

The work begins by reviewing the traditional DCM derived from the Linear Inverted Pendulum (LIP) model, where the horizontal Center of Mass (CoM) dynamics are described by $\ddot{c} = \omega^2(c - z)$. The DCM, $\xi = c + \dot{c}/\omega$, and Convergent Component of Motion (CCM), $\zeta = c - \dot{c}/\omega$, are introduced as components corresponding to the unstable ($+\omega$) and stable ($-\omega$) eigenvalues of the linearized LIP dynamics. Their controlled dynamics are given by $\dot{\xi} = \omega(\xi - z)$ and $\dot{\zeta} = -\omega(\zeta - z)$.

The Koopman operator framework is then introduced, where nonlinear dynamics $\dot{x} = f(x)$ are mapped to a linear operator $K$ acting on observable functions $g(x)$. An observable $\phi(x)$ is a Koopman eigenfunction if $L\phi = \dot{\phi}(x) = \lambda\phi(x)$, meaning it evolves linearly. For control-affine systems $\dot{x} = f(x) + B(x)u$, the eigenfunction dynamics become $\dot{\phi} = \lambda\phi + \nabla\phi(x) \cdot B(x)u$. The paper identifies that the LIP DCM, $\xi = [1 \ \omega^{-1}]x$ (where $x = [c \ \dot{c}]^\top$), is a Koopman eigenfunction with eigenvalue $\omega$, and its controlled dynamics match the Koopman eigenfunction-control equation.

#### 3.2. Learning Divergent Components of Motion

The core of the methodology is to learn this unstable Koopman eigenpair $(\omega, \xi_\theta)$ for a general nonlinear control-affine system. Unlike many Koopman studies focusing on stable or slowly varying modes, this work deliberately seeks the largest positive eigenvalue $\omega$ to capture the system's unstable mode.

- **Eigenfunction Search:** The eigenfunction $\xi_\theta$ is parameterized by a multilayer perceptron (MLP) with parameters $\theta$. The input to the MLP is a delay embedding of past measurements and actions $z_k = (y_{k-H}, u_{k-H}, \dots, y_k)$, which serves as a model-free representation of the state. The eigenvalue $\omega$ is also a learnable parameter, constrained to be positive ($\omega > 0$) by parameterizing it as $\omega = \exp(\tilde{\omega})$. An inductive bias is introduced by requiring the learned DCM to follow the linear differential equation $\dot{\xi}_\theta = \omega(\xi_\theta - u)$, which implies a state-independent control term.
- **DCM Dynamics Loss (`ℓ_DCM^(0)`):** Based on the linear differential equation for $\xi_\theta$, a discrete-time recursion for the DCM is derived: $\xi_{k+1} = e^{\omega\delta t}\xi_k + (1 - e^{\omega\delta t})u_k$. The DCM dynamics loss is an $\ell_2$-residual that quantifies how well the learned $\xi_\theta$ satisfies this one-step recursion: $E_{\text{data}} \left[ \left\| \xi_\theta(z_{k+1}) - e^{\omega\delta t}\xi_\theta(z_k) - (1 - e^{\omega\delta t})u_k \right\|^2 \right]$. A critical issue with this loss alone is a trivial minimum at $(\omega, \xi_\theta) \equiv (0,0)$.
- **CoM Prediction Loss (`ℓ_CoM^(0)`):** To mitigate the trivial minimum, a second loss term is introduced. This leverages the LIP property that the CoM position $c$ converges to the DCM $\xi$ according to $\dot{c} = \omega(\xi - c)$. A discrete-time CoM recursion is derived: $c_{k+1} = e^{-\omega\delta t}c_k + (1 - e^{-\omega\delta t})(\xi_k + \xi_{k+1})/2$. The CoM prediction loss is an $\ell_2$-residual measuring the difference between the actual CoM $c_{k+1}$ (estimated from $z_{k+1}$) and its prediction based on $c_k$ and the learned DCMs $\xi_k, \xi_{k+1}$. The combined loss $\ell^{(0)} = \ell^{(0)}_{\text{DCM}} + w_{\text{CoM}}\ell^{(0)}_{\text{CoM}}$ is optimized, with $w_{\text{CoM}}$ weighting the CoM prediction term.
- **Eligibility Traces (TD(λ) Losses):** To prevent $\omega$ from converging to overly low values on real-robot data, the one-step losses are extended to multi-step predictions, similar to eligibility traces in reinforcement learning. By iterating the discrete-time DCM and CoM recursions, $n$-step residuals are formed. These residuals are then combined geometrically with a discount factor $\lambda$ to create the final TD($\lambda$) losses, $\ell^{(\lambda)}_{\text{DCM}}$ and $\ell^{(\lambda)}_{\text{CoM}}$, which are then combined as $\ell^{(\lambda)} = \ell^{(\lambda)}_{\text{DCM}} + w_{\text{CoM}}\ell^{(\lambda)}_{\text{CoM}}$.

#### 3.3. Application to Legged Balancing

- **Data Collection:** The learning pipeline is deployed on a Cookie wheeled biped robot. Training data consists of closed-loop measurement-action episodes collected at 100 Hz over one hour on the real robot. A proportional DCM feedback controller, using a kinematic LIP estimate for the DCM, generates the initial control actions. Crucially, the CoM position and ZMP are estimated online using a quadratic programming (QP) state estimator from joint encoders, IMU, and rolling-contact tasks.
- **Encoder Architecture and Training:** The encoder $\xi_\theta$ is a two-hidden-layer MLP with 128 units and tanh activations. The delay-embedding horizon $H$ is a hyperparameter, empirically set to $H=10$. The optimization uses the Adam algorithm. The TD($\lambda$) loss (with $\lambda > 0$) is found to be essential for robustly identifying $\omega$ from real-robot data, unlike the one-step loss which collapses $\omega$ to near zero.
- **Evaluation:** The learned DCMs are evaluated in two main scenarios:
 - **Real-robot ZMP Tracking:** The learned DCMs are substituted into the proportional DCM feedback controller, and the ZMP tracking Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) is measured against sinusoidal reference patterns, comparing against the kinematic LIP-based DCM baseline.
 - **Model Predictive Control (MPC):** The learned DCM is used as the state variable in a finite-horizon linear-quadratic optimal control problem for a Unitree G1 humanoid in PyBullet simulation. The dynamics are the discrete-time DCM recursion, and the cost minimizes tracking errors for $\xi$ and $u$. Crucially, viability is enforced through hard inequality constraints on the DCM state ($|\xi_k| \le \xi_{\text{max}}$) and ZMP input ($|u_k| \le u_{\text{max}}$). This setup allows comparison of the fall rate and ZMP tracking performance between MPC and proportional DCM feedback.

### 4. Main findings and results

The research yielded several findings concerning the learning of Koopman DCMs and their application to legged balance control.

#### 4.1. Validation on Linear Inverted Pendulum (LIP) Model (Appendix A)

Initial validation on a simulated LIP model confirmed the methodological soundness:
- The learning pipeline accurately recovered the ground-truth natural frequency $\omega^\star = 5$ rad/s, achieving an estimated $\hat{\omega} = 4.9992$ rad/s with a relative error of $1.6 \times 10^{-4}$.
- The CoM prediction loss (with a weight $w_{\text{CoM}} = 1$) was essential to prevent the optimization from converging to the trivial minimum $(\omega, \xi_\theta) \equiv (0,0)$, demonstrating its regularization effect.
- For the ideal LIP model, the performance of DCM estimation was largely insensitive to the length of the delay-embedding horizon ($H$), indicating that the immediate observations ($c_k, \dot{c}_k$) already provide sufficient information.

#### 4.2. Real-Robot ZMP Tracking (Cookie Wheeled Biped)

Application of the learned Koopman DCMs to a real Cookie wheeled biped robot demonstrated tangible performance improvements in ZMP tracking:
- **Significant RMSE Reduction:** The H=10 encoder (optimal history length empirically determined for the real robot) approximately halved the ZMP tracking Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) compared to the kinematic LIP-based baseline across tested sinusoidal reference amplitudes (A $\in \{0.1, 0.2, 0.3\}$) and periods (T $\in \{0.8, 1.2, 1.6\}$ s).
- **Peak Performance:** The most substantial improvement was observed at an amplitude of A=0.2, where the learned DCM tracked 2.4 times better than the kinematic baseline (0.05 RMSE versus 0.12 RMSE).
- **Importance of TD(λ) Loss:** Unlike the LIP model, the one-step loss alone was insufficient for identifying a meaningful $\omega$ on real-robot data, often collapsing to $\hat{\omega} \approx 0.05$ rad/s. The introduction of the multi-step TD($\lambda$) loss with $\lambda > 0$ was crucial for achieving robust identification of a non-trivial $\omega$.
- **Effect of History Length:** On the real robot, the delay-embedding horizon H exhibited sensitivity, with H=10 providing the best overall performance compared to H=5 and H=20, suggesting that a sufficient history is needed to capture the robot's complex dynamics from partial observations.

#### 4.3. Model Predictive Control over Learned DCMs (Unitree G1 Humanoid Simulation)

The integration of learned DCMs into an MPC framework for a simulated Unitree G1 humanoid demonstrated enhanced safety and control:
- **Fall Rate Reduction:** Under challenging walking pattern references (varying amplitudes, offsets, and periods), the MPC controller using learned DCMs achieved a 0% fall rate (0 out of 90 trials). In contrast, a proportional DCM feedback controller (using the same learned DCMs) experienced a 33% fall rate (30 out of 90 trials). This highlights the effectiveness of incorporating viability constraints within MPC based on the learned DCM state.
- **Improved ZMP Tracking in MPC:** Beyond fall prevention, the MPC also exhibited superior ZMP tracking performance, with an average error of 0.59 compared to 0.73 for the proportional feedback controller. This indicates that the MPC approach not only enhances safety but also provides more precise control.

### 5. Significance and potential impact

This research contributes to the field of legged robotics and control theory by offering a novel, data-driven methodology for managing balance. Its significance stems from addressing critical limitations of conventional, model-based control strategies and by demonstrating practical applicability on real hardware.

The primary impact is the **generalization of Divergent Components of Motion (DCMs)**. By reformulating DCMs as Koopman eigenfunctions, the work moves beyond the simplified dynamics of reduced models (like the Linear Inverted Pendulum). This allows the concept of DCM to be applied to the full, complex, and nonlinear dynamics of diverse legged robots, potentially unlocking more sophisticated and robust balance control for a wider range of platforms and tasks.

A key implication is the **reduction of reliance on explicit dynamic modeling**. The data-driven training pipeline enables learning DCMs directly from closed-loop measurement-action data collected on a real robot. This approach inherently accounts for real-world dynamics, unmodeled complexities, and disturbances, circumventing the challenges and potential inaccuracies of analytical model derivation and the typical "sim-to-real" gap. This could accelerate the development and deployment of controllers for new and complex legged systems.

The demonstrated **improvement in real-robot performance** (halving ZMP tracking RMSE on a wheeled biped) provides empirical evidence for the practical utility of Koopman DCMs. This suggests that the learned representations capture essential dynamic characteristics relevant for robust control. This could lead to more agile, stable, and capable legged robots operating in unstructured environments.

Furthermore, the work highlights the utility of learned DCMs in **enhancing safety and robustness through Model Predictive Control (MPC)**. By providing a state variable suitable for hard viability constraints, the methodology enables MPC to proactively prevent falls, as evidenced by the 0% fall rate in simulations compared to 33% for proportional control. This is a crucial step towards developing highly reliable and safe autonomous legged robots.

The research also opens avenues for future work, as acknowledged in the limitations section. Investigating more general input couplings for the learned DCM dynamics (beyond the linear, state-independent form) and incorporating ZMP lag models could further enhance the fidelity and performance of these data-driven representations. This suggests a fertile ground for continued research in adaptive and learning-based control for complex robotic systems. In essence, this study provides a foundational step towards integrating advanced data-driven system identification techniques with established control principles to address fundamental challenges in robot locomotion.
