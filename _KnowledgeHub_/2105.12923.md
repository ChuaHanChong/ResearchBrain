# Robust Navigation for Racing Drones based on Imitation Learning and Modularization

> [!summary] Summary
> A modular navigation system for racing drones, developed by KAIST, combines deep learning perception with model-based control to enhance robustness in dynamic environments. The system, trained purely in simulation, demonstrates superior track completion rates over previous methods and successfully generalizes to unseen photo-realistic textures.

## Problem

- Autonomous drone racing requires robust navigation without perfect global state estimation or prior track knowledge, challenging traditional model-based methods.
- Purely end-to-end data-driven approaches often lack stability, generalization, and explainability for complex robotic tasks.
- Bridging the "reality gap" for simulation-trained systems operating in real-world environments remains a core difficulty for data-driven navigation.

## Method

- A modular navigation system is proposed, integrating an enhanced data-driven perception module with model-based planning and control components.
- The perception CNN incorporates both camera images and the drone's latest three estimated states as input, while explicitly predicting intermediate gate characteristics like 2D center and orientation.
- Data collection employs extensive randomization, varying visual scenes, track layouts, flight directions, and maximum velocities, combined with a modified DAgger strategy where the network is re-trained from scratch on all accumulated data after each augmentation.

## Results

- The proposed system exhibited superior navigation performance, achieving significantly higher completion rates on "hard static" and "hard dynamic" tracks compared to a state-of-the-art baseline.
- On "hard dynamic" tracks with moving gates, the method achieved nearly **100%** completion at **6.0** m/s, demonstrating strong generalization to complex, dynamic environments.
- The customized neural network supports switchable navigation behaviors (standard vs. direct-to-gate) from a single trained policy, offering operational flexibility.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Augmenting visual input with drone state information and explicitly predicting intermediate gate parameters (center, orientation, distance) enhances navigation robustness and explainability.
> - Training the imitation learning neural network from scratch on all accumulated data after each DAgger augmentation step significantly improves stability and prevents convergence to local minima.
> - Extensive randomization during simulation-based training, particularly for maximum velocity and illumination, is crucial for successful domain transfer to photo-realistic and dynamic real-world environments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Autonomous drone racing presents a complex challenge, requiring advancements across multiple robotics domains including state estimation, localization and mapping, path planning, dynamics modeling, and control. Traditional approaches often rely on precomputed global trajectories and highly accurate state estimation, which can be difficult to achieve reliably in real-world scenarios due to limitations in current sensor and estimation technologies.

In recent years, data-driven methods, particularly those leveraging deep learning, have emerged as a promising alternative for autonomous navigation. These approaches, including supervised learning (imitation learning) and reinforcement learning, offer advantages such as robustness against state estimation drift and the ability to learn from failures. However, purely end-to-end data-driven systems often face challenges related to stability, generalization to unseen environments, and the explainability or verifiability of their learned policies. Reinforcement learning, specifically, can suffer from prohibitively long training times in large state-action spaces.

A trend in this field involves the modularization of navigation systems, separating the process into distinct perception, planning, and control modules. This hybrid approach aims to combine the robust perceptual capabilities of data-driven methods, such as convolutional neural networks (CNNs), with the precision and theoretical guarantees of model-based planners and controllers. This modular architecture has shown potential for improved performance and interpretability compared to purely end-to-end learning. Prior work, particularly and, demonstrated the effectiveness of using CNNs for perception to identify local waypoints, which are then used by traditional planning and control modules. Reference further advanced this by incorporating domain randomization during data collection to improve simulation-to-reality transfer.

This current work situates itself within this modularized framework, specifically building upon and aiming to improve the approach presented in. It seeks to enhance the perception module's robustness and generalization capabilities through augmented input features, a customized neural network architecture, and an advanced data collection strategy, thereby contributing to the development of more reliable and versatile autonomous racing drone systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust vision-based modularized navigation system for racing drones that effectively combines the advantages of both data-based perception and model-based planning and control. The system aims to enable autonomous drones to navigate complex and dynamic racing tracks without prior knowledge of the track layout or reliance on highly accurate global state estimation.

The motivation behind the specific design choices and improvements presented in this paper stems from several identified limitations and opportunities:

- **Enhancing Perception Robustness:** Prior modular systems, such as, primarily relied on a single current camera image as input to the perception CNN. The authors hypothesize that a drone's state (e.g., velocity, angular rates) provides crucial contextual information that, when combined with visual data, can lead to more informed and robust navigation decisions. Therefore, a key objective is to augment the CNN input with the latest three estimated drone states to provide additional temporal and kinematic context.
- **Improving Explainability and Versatility:** Standard end-to-end networks often operate as "black boxes," making their decisions difficult to interpret. By designing a customized CNN architecture that explicitly predicts intermediate, gate-related information (e.g., 2D coordinates of the next gate's center, its relative orientation, and distance), the authors aim to make the perception module's decisions more explainable. Furthermore, this explicit prediction is intended to enable alternative, switchable navigation behaviors from a single trained network, offering flexibility in different racing scenarios.
- **Achieving Robust Simulation-to-Reality Transfer:** A significant challenge in data-driven robotics is bridging the "reality gap" – the discrepancy between simulated training environments and real-world deployment. Previous work showed the utility of visual scene randomization for domain transfer. This paper aims to further improve this by incorporating more extensive randomization factors during data collection, including track layout variability and drone aggressiveness (maximum velocity), in addition to visual scene elements. The goal is to train a system purely in simulation with synthetic textures that can successfully operate in environments with unseen photo-realistic textures without additional fine-tuning.
- **Optimizing Data Collection and Training Efficiency:** Imitation learning is susceptible to distribution mismatch between training and testing data, particularly when the expert policy only demonstrates ideal trajectories. The Dataset Aggregation (DAgger) policy is used to address this. The authors aim to refine the DAgger strategy by developing a "modified DAgger policy" that strategically uses a partially trained network for maneuvering but switches to the expert for recovery, while also exploring optimal training procedures (e.g., training from scratch vs. fine-tuning) to maximize performance and stability.
- **Outperforming State-of-the-Art:** Ultimately, the motivation is to develop a navigation system that demonstrates superior overall performance, particularly in challenging scenarios like difficult static and dynamic tracks, compared to existing state-of-the-art methods like.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed system adopts a modular architecture comprising a perception module, a planning module, and a control module, designed to exploit the strengths of both data-driven and model-based approaches.

#### 3.1. A. Modularization of the Navigation System

1. **Perception Module:** This module is built around a customized convolutional neural network (CNN).
 - **Inputs:** Unlike prior work that solely used the current camera image, this CNN takes two types of input: the current forward-facing camera image (300x200 resolution) and the latest three estimated drone states. The drone states comprise position change, Euler angles in the x-y-z world frame, and linear/angular velocities in the x-y-z body frame (33 dimensions in total, 11 values for each of the three latest states).
 - **Architecture:** The CNN architecture (Figure 3) utilizes a ResNet-8 backbone (excluding its final fully-connected layer) for image feature extraction. These features are then fed into a series of fully connected (Fc) layers. A distinctive feature is the explicit extraction of intermediate, gate-related predictions: 2D normalized coordinates of the next gate's center, its relative Euler angles, and its normalized distance. These intermediate predictions are concatenated with the latest three drone states before further processing through additional Fc layers.
 - **Outputs:** The final outputs of the perception module are the desired navigation direction $\tilde{x} \in [-1, 1]^2$ (in 2D normalized image coordinates) and a normalized navigation speed $v \in$.
 - **Loss Function:** The network is trained using a weighted mean square error (MSE) loss function that combines terms for all predicted outputs: navigation direction, normalized speed, 2D gate center coordinates, gate orientation, and normalized gate distance (Eq. 3). The coefficients $\gamma_1, \gamma_2, \gamma_3, \gamma_4$ weight these individual loss components.

2. **Planning Module:** This module translates the high-level navigation commands from the perception module into a local trajectory.
 - **3D Goal Point Generation:** The 2D navigation direction $\tilde{x}$ is back-projected to generate a camera projection ray. The 3D goal point is located on this ray at a distance $\ell_{\text{test}}$ (planning length at testing time), which is defined proportionally to the predicted normalized navigation speed $v$ (Eq. 4). This allows the planning horizon to dynamically adapt to the drone's speed.
 - **Trajectory Generation:** A minimum-jerk trajectory is then calculated to connect the drone's current state to this 3D goal point, with a desired speed $v_{\text{des}} = v_{\text{max}} \cdot v$ (where $v_{\text{max}}$ is a user-defined maximum speed, allowing behavior adjustment).
 - **Receding Horizon:** The local trajectory is planned and tracked in a receding horizon fashion, meaning it is re-planned whenever a new prediction from the perception module becomes available.

3. **Control Module:** This module is responsible for executing the planned local trajectory.
 - It uses a controller proposed in that specifically accounts for the rotor drag effect in quadrotor dynamics, which is relevant for accurate tracking of high-speed trajectories.

#### 3.2. B. Expert Policy

For imitation learning, ground truth labels for navigation direction and speed are automatically generated by an expert policy. This policy requires a pre-computed global trajectory and accurate drone/gate positions.
- **Global Trajectory:** A minimum-snap trajectory is first generated that passes through all gates of the track.
- **Prediction Horizon:** Given the drone's current position, a dynamic prediction horizon $d_{\text{train}}$ is calculated (Eq. 1), which adapts based on the distance to the previous and next gates, bounded by user-defined minimum and maximum values.
- **Ground Truth Labels:** A point $\tilde{p}_g'$ on the global trajectory, $d_{\text{train}}$ away from the drone in the forward direction, is identified. Its projection onto the camera image plane yields the ground truth navigation direction $\tilde{x}_g$. The ground truth normalized navigation speed $v_g$ is derived from the speed of the global trajectory at the point closest to the drone, normalized by the maximum speed along the trajectory.
- **Training Goal Point:** A 3D goal position $\tilde{p}_g$ is derived by back-projecting $\tilde{x}_g$ and finding a point on the ray at a training planning length $\ell_{\text{train}}$, which is also proportional to $v_g$ (Eq. 2).

#### 3.3. C. Data Collection Strategy

The data collection strategy is crucial for domain transfer and robust performance, incorporating extensive randomization and a modified DAgger policy.

1. **Domain Transfer through Randomization:** The system is trained purely with synthetic textures but aims to operate in environments with unseen photo-realistic textures. This is achieved through randomization during training data collection:
 - **Visual Scene Randomization (following):** Textures of walls and floors, shape and textures of gates, and illumination conditions are varied.
 - **Additional Randomization:** For every new data collection epoch, the following are randomized:
 - Gate positions (track layout).
 - Navigation direction (clockwise or counter-clockwise expert flight).
 - Maximum velocity for global and local trajectories ($v_{\text{max}}$ uniformly from 4.0 to 8.0 m/s).

2. **Modified Dataset Aggregation (DAgger) Policy:** This iterative approach addresses the distribution mismatch inherent in imitation learning.
 - **Initial Collection:** An initial dataset is collected by having the expert policy maneuver the drone for 150 trials in synthetic environments. The neural network is then trained for 100 epochs on this data.
 - **Iterative Augmentation:** The process then enters a loop for further data augmentation:
 - The partially trained network is used to maneuver the drone.
 - If the drone deviates beyond a specified margin $\epsilon$ from the global trajectory, the expert policy takes over to recover the drone.
 - Regardless of who is maneuvering, the expert policy always generates the ground truth decisions, which are recorded.
 - The margin $\epsilon$ is gradually increased (0.5m, 1.0m, 1.5m) in successive steps, allowing the collection of more diverse data as the network performance improves.
 - **Crucially, after each augmentation step, the neural network is trained *from scratch* on all accumulated data for 100 epochs, rather than fine-tuning from previous weights.**

3. **Dynamic Environment Generalization:** To enable navigation in dynamic environments (moving gates) at test time, the training procedure involves extensive randomization of *static* track layouts. This approach was found to be sufficient for generalizing to dynamic conditions.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were conducted in the RotorS Gazebo simulator, with both training and testing environments featuring randomized wall/floor textures. Tracks varied from "easy static" (circular, seven gates) to "difficult static" (perturbed gate positions) and "hard dynamic" (continuously moving gates).

#### 4.1. A. Neural Network Capacity and Loss Function Coefficients

- **Network Capacity:** Comparisons were conducted between networks of different capacities (depth) to balance performance and inference time for onboard processing units, leading to the selection of the customized network architecture shown in Figure 3.
- **Loss Function Coefficients:** An evaluation of various coefficient settings for the weighted MSE loss function (Table I) indicated that the policy's performance was not highly sensitive to these choices within a reasonable range. However, setting coefficients too high for one term (e.g., $\gamma_1 = 100$ for velocity) or too low for another (e.g., $\gamma_2 = 0.05$ for gate center position) could degrade performance. Specifically, the importance of $\gamma_2$ highlights that the gate-related intermediate outputs contribute positively to the trained policy's performance, supporting the customized network architecture over methods like that do not explicitly predict these. The optimal coefficients chosen were $\gamma_1 = 0.1, \gamma_2 = 1.0, \gamma_3 = 0.2, \gamma_4 = 0.2$.

#### 4.2. B. Data Collection and Training Procedure

- **Training from Scratch vs. Fine-tuning:** A key finding regarding the modified DAgger policy was that training the neural network *from scratch* on all accumulated data after each augmentation step yielded significantly more stable and better results compared to fine-tuning with previously trained weights (Figure 5). Training from scratch prevented the network from getting stuck in local minima, which could occur when continuing training with pre-trained weights on a newly augmented dataset.

#### 4.3. C. Performance Evaluation Compared to Previous Methods

- **Overall Performance:** The proposed method demonstrated superior navigation performance compared to the baseline state-of-the-art method across various track layouts (Figure 6).
 - On "easy static" tracks, both methods achieved high completion rates, but the proposed method generally showed a slight edge, especially at higher maximum velocities.
 - On "hard static" tracks (irregularly placed gates), the performance difference became more pronounced, with the proposed method maintaining higher completion rates across a range of velocities.
 - On "hard dynamic" tracks (moving gates), the proposed method significantly outperformed the baseline, achieving close to 100% completion at 6.0 m/s, whereas the baseline method struggled, often passing only one or two gates. This indicates a higher level of robustness and generalization to complex, dynamic environments.
- **Switchable Navigation Behaviors:** The customized neural network provides the capability for two distinct navigation behaviors from a single trained network (Figure 7).
 - The standard behavior uses the network's final predicted navigation direction.
 - An alternative behavior uses the intermediate prediction of the next gate's 2D center position as the navigation direction, causing the drone to fly straight towards the gate. This behavior is considered useful for high-speed flight or when gates are close, but carries a higher risk of collision if the drone's orientation significantly differs from the gate's.

#### 4.4. D. Analysis of Factors Contributing to Domain Transfer

- An analysis was conducted to identify which randomization factors were most critical for successful domain transfer by disabling each factor during data collection and observing the impact on test loss (RMSE) on photo-realistic testing data (Figure 8).
 - Randomization of gate positions and navigation direction were found to be indispensable for navigating difficult tracks in both directions; policies trained without these factors could not pass a single gate.
 - The randomization of **maximum velocity** and **illumination condition** were identified as the most significant factors, causing considerable performance drops if not randomized.
 - Randomization of **gate shape** had a less significant impact on performance.
 - Background (wall and floor) texture randomization, while not explicitly re-evaluated, is acknowledged as a critical factor for transfer, consistent with previous research.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of autonomous robotics, particularly in drone navigation and control, through several key advancements and demonstrations:

- **Enhanced Robustness and Performance:** The proposed modular navigation system demonstrates superior robustness and higher track completion rates, especially in challenging and dynamic racing environments, compared to existing state-of-the-art methods. This improvement is critical for practical applications requiring agile and reliable drone operation.
- **Effective Simulation-to-Reality Transfer:** The work successfully shows that a drone navigation system can be trained purely in simulation using synthetic textures and then effectively operate in unseen photo-realistic environments without further fine-tuning. This significantly reduces the cost and time associated with real-world data collection, which is often a bottleneck for data-driven methods.
- **Hybrid Architecture Benefits:** By combining a data-driven perception module with model-based planning and control, the system leverages the strengths of both paradigms. The CNN provides robust perceptual awareness, while the planner and controller offer precision and dynamic feasibility, resulting in a system that is both adaptable and controllable.
- **Improved Explainability and Flexibility:** The customized CNN architecture, which explicitly predicts gate-related information, contributes to the explainability of the perception module's decisions. Furthermore, the ability to switch between navigation behaviors using a single trained network offers operational flexibility, allowing the drone to adapt its flight strategy based on task requirements or environmental conditions.
- **Advanced Data Collection Strategies:** The research highlights the effectiveness of extensive randomization, including environmental, track layout, and dynamic parameters, in generating diverse training data that fosters strong generalization. The modified DAgger policy, particularly the finding that training from scratch after data augmentation improves stability and performance, offers valuable insights for future imitation learning approaches.
- **Broader Applicability:** The techniques developed for robust, agile navigation in drone racing environments have potential implications for other robotics applications that require high-speed, collision-free movement in complex and dynamic settings. This could include search and rescue operations, autonomous inspection of infrastructure, parcel delivery in cluttered urban environments, or autonomous exploration.
