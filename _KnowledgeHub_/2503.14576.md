# SocialJax: An Evaluation Suite for Multi-agent Reinforcement Learning in Sequential Social Dilemmas

> [!summary] Summary
> SocialJax introduces a JAX-based evaluation suite for multi-agent reinforcement learning in sequential social dilemmas, comprising nine diverse environments and reproducible MARL algorithms. The framework provides at least a 50x speed-up in training performance compared to CPU-based baselines and includes tools for nuanced analysis of social dynamics.

## Problem

- Existing sequential social dilemma (SSD) benchmarks are computationally inefficient, demanding extensive CPU resources and long training times, hindering rapid research iteration.
- Many prominent SSD frameworks lack publicly available baseline algorithm implementations, creating barriers to reproducibility and accessibility for researchers.
- Current JAX-based MARL frameworks and other benchmarks offer limited diversity in SSD environments and often prioritize purely cooperative tasks, neglecting complex mixed-motive interactions.

## Method

- Implemented **SocialJax**, a suite of nine diverse ==sequential social dilemma== environments, primarily derived from Melting Pot 2.0, utilizing JAX for high computational efficiency on GPUs/TPUs.
- Provided open-source, JAX-based implementations of several MARL algorithms, including IPPO (with individual and common rewards), MAPPO, VDN, Social Value Orientation (SVO), and IPPO with Reward Exchange (IPPO-RE), for a complete training pipeline.
- Integrated custom, environment-specific evaluation metrics and employed Schelling diagrams to quantify cooperative/competitive behaviors and rigorously validate the social dilemma properties of the environments.

## Results

- SocialJax achieved a minimum of **50x** faster real-time training compared to Melting Pot 2.0's RLlib baselines, with some environments showing 50-**400x** speed-ups, allowing 1e9 timesteps to be completed in approximately 3 hours.
- IPPO with common rewards (IPPO-CR) consistently yielded higher collective returns than IPPO with individual rewards (IPPO-IR), while MAPPO showed strong but less stable performance in some complex environments.
- Schelling diagrams confirmed that the implemented environments exhibit key social dilemma properties like 'fear' (e.g., Coins, Harvest) and 'greed' (e.g., Clean Up, Coop Mining), and custom metrics effectively quantified pro-social and anti-social agent behaviors.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Leveraging JAX for hardware acceleration significantly boosts computational throughput, enabling speed-ups of at least 50x to 400x over CPU-based MARL baselines in SSDs.
> - The choice of reward structure (individual vs. common) profoundly impacts agent behavior in SSDs, with common rewards generally fostering higher collective returns, while individual incentives often lead to selfish outcomes.
> - Mechanisms like Social Value Orientation and reward exchange can effectively promote cooperation, but their optimal application and precise impact on agent interactions vary across different social dilemma scenarios.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) research has predominantly concentrated on scenarios characterized by either fully cooperative or fully competitive interactions. However, a critical area of investigation involves sequential social dilemmas (SSDs), which necessitate environments that accurately model the inherent tension between individual and collective interests.

Existing benchmarks and environments for MARL, such as those within OpenSpiel (Lanctot et al., 2019) and PettingZoo (Terry et al., 2021), offer a limited selection of social dilemma scenarios. These environments typically rely on CPU-based parallelism, which can constrain simulation throughput and scalability. While frameworks like Sequential Social Dilemma (Vinitsky et al., 2019) and particularly Melting Pot (Leibo et al., 2021) and Melting Pot 2.0 (Agapiou et al., 2022) have provided dedicated environments and standardized evaluation protocols for SSDs, they present significant practical challenges. Training in these environments often requires substantial computational resources, including extensive CPU infrastructure and considerable processing time. Additionally, the implementations of baseline algorithms within Melting Pot, such as A3C, V-MPO, and OPRE, have not been publicly released, which can hinder reproducibility and subsequent research efforts.

The advent of JAX (Bradbury et al., 2018), a high-performance numerical computing library, has enabled the efficient execution of Python code on hardware accelerators like GPUs and TPUs. This has led to the development of JAX-based reinforcement learning frameworks. PureJaxRL (Lu et al., 2022) leveraged JAX for single-agent environments, significantly accelerating computation. JaxMARL (Rutherford et al., 2024) extended these efficiency gains to MARL, offering a suite of JAX-based environments and algorithms. While JaxMARL includes a few social dilemma-related environments (e.g., Coins and STORM), its primary focus remains on traditional cooperative tasks, and it lacks a comprehensive set of benchmarks specifically designed for the complexities of sequential social dilemmas.

SocialJax positions itself to address these combined limitations. It aims to bridge the gap by providing a diverse and computationally efficient suite of sequential social dilemma environments, coupled with reproducible JAX-based algorithm implementations. This initiative seeks to offer a robust and accessible platform for advancing research in MARL within mixed-motive settings.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for developing SocialJax stems from the computational and accessibility challenges inherent in evaluating multi-agent reinforcement learning algorithms within sequential social dilemmas. The paper identifies several key problems that current research tools struggle to adequately address:

1. **Computational Inefficiency of Existing Benchmarks:** Current state-of-the-art sequential social dilemma environments, notably Melting Pot, demand substantial computational resources. Training algorithms often requires numerous environment timesteps and extensive hardware specifications (e.g., over 1000 CPUs), leading to prohibitively long training times (e.g., hundreds or thousands of hours). This high cost impedes the rapid iteration and rigorous evaluation of new MARL algorithms.

2. **Limited Reproducibility and Accessibility of Baseline Implementations:** The lack of public availability for baseline algorithm implementations in certain prominent SSD frameworks, such as A3C, V-MPO, and OPRE in Melting Pot 2.0, creates barriers for researchers. This hinders the ability to reproduce results, verify claims, and build upon existing work, thereby slowing down the overall progress in the field.

3. **Insufficient Diversity and Standardization in SSD Environments:** While some social dilemma environments exist, they often offer a narrow range of scenarios or lack standardized evaluation protocols specifically tailored for sequential social dilemmas. The limited social dilemma environments in existing JAX-based MARL frameworks primarily focus on traditional cooperative tasks, neglecting the distinct challenges posed by mixed-motive interactions.

Against this backdrop, the authors establish the following key objectives for SocialJax:

1. **Implement a Diverse Suite of Sequential Social Dilemma Environments in JAX:** To provide a rich and varied set of scenarios that accurately capture the dynamics of mixed-incentive interactions, allowing for a comprehensive evaluation of agent behaviors. The implementation in JAX aims to achieve substantial improvements in simulation speed.

2. **Provide JAX-based Implementations of Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning Algorithms:** To offer a complete, efficient, and reproducible training pipeline by implementing several MARL algorithms, including those tailored for social dilemmas, within the JAX framework. This aims to significantly accelerate the training process and enhance research accessibility.

3. **Validate the Social Dilemma Properties of Environments:** To ensure that the developed environments genuinely exhibit the characteristics of social dilemmas, the authors aim to use tools like Schelling diagrams to analyze the payoffs of cooperative and defecting agents under varying conditions.

4. **Develop Specific Evaluation Metrics for Agent Behaviors:** Beyond conventional return metrics, the objective is to design tailored metrics for each environment that quantify the extent of cooperative and competitive behaviors, providing a more granular understanding of agent interactions.

5. **Establish a Performance Benchmark and Analysis Framework:** To systematically evaluate the simulation throughput and training speed of SocialJax environments and algorithms, providing a clear comparison with existing CPU-based alternatives.

By achieving these objectives, SocialJax aims to offer an efficient, diverse, and reproducible evaluation framework that can facilitate advancements in solving sequential social dilemmas within MARL.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The development of SocialJax involves the implementation of a suite of sequential social dilemma environments and multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) algorithms, leveraging the JAX numerical computing library to enhance computational efficiency.

#### 3.1. Environment Design and Implementation

The SocialJax suite comprises nine distinct multi-agent sequential social dilemma environments, primarily derived from Melting Pot 2.0. These environments are implemented at the grid level, providing agents with an 11x11 partially observable field of view, conceptually similar to MiniGrid environments. The environments are designed to model various mixed-incentive dynamics, including public goods dilemmas and common pool resource problems.
The environments include:
- **Coins:** Agents collect coins of their own color for individual reward; collecting another agent's coin penalizes the other.
- **Commons Harvest (Open, Closed, Partnership):** Agents collect apples, but their regrowth probability is dependent on local apple density, creating a sustainability dilemma.
 - *Closed:* Features defensible rooms with single entries.
 - *Partnership:* Features rooms requiring two players to defend two entry points.
- **Clean Up:** Agents earn rewards from apples, whose spawn rate depends on river cleanliness, requiring agents to clean polluted river tiles.
- **Coop Mining:** Agents can mine iron ore individually for a small reward or cooperate to mine gold ore for a larger, shared reward.
- **Mushrooms:** Agents consume different mushroom types, with varying individual and collective reward distributions (some benefit only the consumer, others benefit the group, and some only benefit others).
- **Gift Refinement:** Agents collect and can gift tokens to others, which refines them to a higher level, tripling their quantity and increasing potential rewards, emphasizing trust.
- **Prisoner’s Dilemma: Arena:** Agents collect "defect" or "cooperate" tokens and engage in pairwise interactions whose payoffs follow the classic Prisoner's Dilemma matrix.

#### 3.2. Algorithm Implementations

SocialJax includes JAX-based implementations of several MARL algorithms, facilitating a comprehensive training pipeline:
- **Independent PPO (IPPO):** A widely adopted algorithm, implemented in two variants:
 - **IPPO Individual Rewards (IPPO-IR):** Each agent optimizes its policy based on its private reward signal, often promoting selfish behaviors.
 - **IPPO Common Rewards (IPPO-CR):** All agents share a single, unified reward signal (typically the sum of individual rewards), aligning agents towards a common objective and promoting cooperation.
 - Both IPPO variants are evaluated with and without parameter sharing across agents.
- **Multi-Agent PPO (MAPPO):** A centralized learning approach that employs a centralized value function, enabling agents to leverage global information to potentially learn prosocial behaviors.
- **Value Decomposition Networks (VDN):** This algorithm decomposes the team's joint Q-value into a sum of individual agents' local Q-values, supporting centralized training with decentralized execution.
- **Social Value Orientation (SVO):** An intrinsic motivation framework that encourages cooperative behavior by incorporating an agent's preference for reward distribution. The utility function combines extrinsic rewards with a penalty based on the deviation from a target SVO angle (θ), which ranges from individualistic (θ = 0°) to altruistic (θ = 90°).
- **PPO with Reward Exchange (IPPO-RE):** A mechanism where agents exchange portions of their per-timestep rewards. Each agent retains a proportion `s` of its reward and distributes `1-s` equally among co-players. This method interpolates between individual and common reward structures to find a balance that induces collective outcomes while preserving individual incentives.

#### 3.3. Evaluation Metrics and Analysis Tools

Beyond traditional cumulative return, the authors designed environment-specific metrics to quantify cooperative and competitive behaviors:
- **Coins:** Number of own-color coins collected.
- **Commons Harvest:** Number of apples remaining on the map (indicative of resource preservation).
- **Clean Up:** Number of cleaned water tiles.
- **Coop Mining:** Amount of gold mined (as gold requires cooperation).
- **Mushrooms:** Number of blue mushrooms consumed (blue mushrooms benefit other agents).
- **Gift Refinement:** Number of received tokens (indicative of trust).
- **Prisoner’s Dilemma: Arena:** Quantity of cooperative resources collected.

To validate the social dilemma properties of the environments, **Schelling diagrams** are employed. These diagrams plot the average payoffs for cooperative and defecting agents as a function of the number of other cooperating players. Cooperative policies are derived from IPPO-CR training, while defector policies are derived from IPPO-IR training. This analysis helps verify the presence of "fear" (incentive to defect if others defect) or "greed" (incentive to defect if others cooperate) properties characteristic of social dilemmas.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

Experiments were conducted on a machine equipped with an NVIDIA A100 GPU and 14 CPU cores. Speed benchmarks involved running up to 4096 JAX environments in parallel. Training algorithms were run for up to 3 x 10^8 total timesteps. The paper also compares SocialJax's wall-clock training times against extrapolated estimates from open-source Melting Pot 2.0 implementations using Stable-Baselines3 and RLlib. Hyperparameters for all algorithms are detailed in the appendix.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of SocialJax focused on benchmarking its computational efficiency, analyzing the performance of various MARL algorithms across its environments, and validating the social dilemma properties of the environments using Schelling diagrams and custom metrics.

#### 4.1. Computational Efficiency Benchmarks

SocialJax demonstrates significant improvements in operational efficiency. Experiments showed that the JAX-based training pipeline achieves a speed-up of at least 50 times in real-time performance compared to the RLlib baselines in Melting Pot 2.0, when run on equivalent hardware (NVIDIA A100 GPU and 14 CPU cores). For simple environments like Coins, SocialJax was 50-400 times faster, capable of completing 1e9 timesteps within approximately 3 hours, whereas Melting Pot 2.0 with Stable-Baselines3 and RLlib would require an estimated 1,300 hours and 150 hours, respectively. For more complex environments such as Clean Up, SocialJax achieved a 50-140 times speed-up. The high-performance is attributed to JAX's GPU acceleration and parallel environment execution, with performance scaling significantly with the number of parallel environments (up to 4096 JAX environments tested). Increasing the number of agents from the default to 20 showed only a modest slowdown in frames per second (FPS), indicating that the parallel computation primarily affects GPU memory usage rather than significantly impacting speed.

#### 4.2. Algorithm Performance and Behavior Analysis

- **IPPO Common vs. Individual Rewards:** IPPO-CR generally yields higher collective returns across most environments compared to IPPO-IR. The common reward signal encourages agents to maximize total payoff, fostering cooperative behaviors. Conversely, individual rewards tend to incentivize selfish actions, often leading to lower overall returns due to behaviors like over-exploitation of shared resources.
- **MAPPO Performance:** As a centralized learning method, MAPPO exhibited strong performance in environments such as Clean Up and Mushrooms, where access to global information can be advantageous. However, MAPPO displayed less stable training dynamics in other environments, specifically underperforming IPPO-CR in Commons Harvest: Partnership and Gift Refinement. This is attributed to the increased learning difficulty associated with its centralized critic aggregating observations from all agents.
- **VDN Performance:** VDN surpassed IPPO-IR in several environments, including Coins, Commons Harvest variants, Mushrooms, and Prisoner’s Dilemma: Arena. However, its performance was suboptimal in more complex social dilemmas, limited by its linear decomposition of the joint Q-value, which restricts its capacity to model intricate inter-agent interactions.
- **Social Value Orientation (SVO):** Experiments with SVO demonstrated that collective returns generally increase with larger SVO preference angles (θ). Agents with an "Individualistic" orientation (θ = 0°) achieved the lowest returns, while those with an "Altruistic" orientation (θ = 90°) attained the highest returns (Table 2). An exception was observed in the Harvest environments, where highly altruistic SVO settings (high θ) led to agents intentionally avoiding apple collection to allow others access, resulting in a higher "apples remaining" metric but paradoxically lower collective returns.
- **IPPO with Reward Exchange (IPPO-RE):** Reward exchange proved effective in improving performance over IPPO-CR in Clean Up and Prisoner’s Dilemma, indicating that retaining some individual incentives can alleviate credit assignment problems and potentially enhance overall outcomes in these contexts. In Clean Up, IPPO-RE at a reward ratio of 0.5 notably outperformed the common reward setting. Conversely, IPPO-RE performed substantially worse than IPPO-CR in Gift Refinement, suggesting that individual incentives to free-ride are particularly strong in this environment, necessitating a greater degree of reward sharing. The study identified the minimum reward exchange ratio required to achieve at least 95% of the best-performing ratio's return for each environment (Table 4).

#### 4.3. Social Dilemma Properties and Custom Metrics Validation

Schelling diagrams were used to verify the social dilemma properties of the environments by analyzing the payoffs of cooperative (trained with IPPO-CR) and defecting (trained with IPPO-IR) policies.
- **Fear Property:** Environments such as Coins, Commons Harvest (Open, Closed, Partnership), Gift Refinement, and Mushrooms demonstrated the "fear" property, where agents prefer to defect if some co-players are defecting, even though mutual cooperation yields a better outcome. In Harvest: Open, a single defector had a disproportionately negative impact due to permanent resource depletion.
- **Greed Property:** Clean Up, Coop Mining, and Prisoner’s Dilemma: Arena exhibited the "greed" property, where agents are incentivized to defect if a sufficient number of co-players are cooperating. For instance, in Clean Up, defectors could benefit from cooperators cleaning pollution without contributing themselves.
The custom metrics designed for each environment were effective in quantifying prosocial or antisocial behaviors. Higher metric values generally correlated with increased cooperation and collective returns, although some nuances, like the Harvest environments' inverse relationship between apples remaining and overall returns under extreme altruism, were observed. As anticipated, selfish approaches (IPPO-IR, SVO-0°) resulted in significantly lower metric values compared to cooperative approaches (IPPO-CR, MAPPO, SVO-90°).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The SocialJax evaluation suite represents a notable contribution to the field of multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL), particularly concerning sequential social dilemmas. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Addressing Computational Bottlenecks:** By leveraging JAX for hardware acceleration (GPUs/TPUs), SocialJax achieves substantial speed-ups (at least 50x compared to existing CPU-based baselines like Melting Pot 2.0's RLlib). This significantly reduces the computational resources and wall-clock time required for experiments, thereby lowering the barrier to entry for researchers and enabling faster iteration of ideas and more extensive experimentation. This efficiency can accelerate the pace of discovery in MARL for social dilemmas.

2. **Enhancing Reproducibility and Accessibility:** SocialJax provides open-source implementations of both its diverse environments and a range of MARL algorithms. This addresses a critical challenge posed by prior benchmarks where baseline algorithm implementations were often proprietary or unavailable. The open and unified codebase fosters transparency, enables researchers to easily replicate results, and serves as a robust foundation for building and comparing new algorithms.

3. **Standardizing Evaluation for Sequential Social Dilemmas:** The suite offers a diverse collection of nine environments specifically designed to capture various facets of sequential social dilemmas (e.g., public goods, common pool resources, Prisoner's Dilemma variants). This provides a standardized and comprehensive testbed for evaluating agent behaviors in mixed-motive settings, which are often overlooked in MARL research dominated by purely cooperative or competitive tasks.

4. **Deepening Understanding of Social Dynamics:** The integration of custom, environment-specific metrics and the use of Schelling diagrams allow for a more nuanced and quantitative analysis of cooperative and competitive behaviors beyond simple cumulative returns. This methodology helps in verifying the underlying social dilemma properties of environments and provides insights into how different reward structures and intrinsic motivations (like SVO and reward exchange) influence agent decisions.

5. **Facilitating Algorithm Development:** SocialJax provides well-implemented JAX-based baselines for various MARL algorithms, including Independent PPO (with individual and common rewards), MAPPO, VDN, SVO, and IPPO with Reward Exchange. This comprehensive set of baselines offers a strong starting point for researchers developing novel algorithms specifically aimed at resolving or understanding social dilemmas. The ability to systematically test and compare algorithms under efficient and reproducible conditions can drive innovation in designing more robust and socially intelligent agents.

In summary, SocialJax is positioned to become a foundational tool for MARL researchers, democratizing access to computationally efficient and diverse environments for studying sequential social dilemmas. Its contributions are expected to accelerate research, foster greater understanding of complex multi-agent interactions, and ultimately aid in the development of more cooperative and effective artificial intelligence systems in socially complex settings.
