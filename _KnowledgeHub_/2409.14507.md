# A is for Absorption: Studying Feature Splitting and Absorption in Sparse Autoencoders

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from LASR Labs introduce and characterize "feature absorption" in Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs), a phenomenon where a general feature fails to activate in the presence of a more specific, token-aligned feature due to sparsity optimization. Their analysis, including a novel detection metric, empirically confirms this limitation in hundreds of LLM SAEs, demonstrating that absorption rates increase with higher sparsity and wider architectures.

## Problem

- Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) were designed to extract sparse, monosemantic features from LLM activations, but their reliability for capturing human-interpretable concepts had unexplained gaps, particularly concerning low recall.
- The underlying mechanisms by which SAEs represent and interact with hierarchical features within LLMs were not fully understood, leading to questions about their robust interpretability.
- Previous work observed SAE latents exhibiting lower-than-expected recall compared to known proxies, indicating an issue that needed a comprehensive mechanistic explanation.

## Method

- The paper formally defines and provides a theoretical proof for "==feature absorption==," demonstrating it as a consequence of SAEs' sparsity objective when handling hierarchical features.
- They use toy models to simulate hierarchical feature co-occurrence, showing how a general feature's latent can be 'absorbed' by a more specific child feature, preventing the parent from firing.
- A new, quantifiable metric is developed, combining causal ablation and integrated gradients, to empirically detect and measure feature absorption across diverse LLM SAEs.

## Results

- SAE latents consistently showed suboptimal F1 scores for first-letter identification compared to linear probes, with performance varying greatly based on L0 sparsity and across different letters.
- Empirical case studies, such as a general 'starts with S' latent failing to activate for '_short,' confirmed feature absorption, revealing that its causal effect was taken over by a more specific, token-aligned latent.
- The absorption metric demonstrated the prevalence of feature absorption across all tested LLM SAEs (Gemma Scope, Qwen2 **0.5B**, Llama 3.2 **1B**), with rates increasing in SAEs with higher sparsity (L0) and wider architectures.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Feature absorption is a fundamental, theoretically grounded limitation of current SAE designs, driven by sparsity optimization, rather than a superficial bug.
> - This phenomenon creates an "interpretability illusion" where seemingly monosemantic SAE latents can exhibit unreliable activation patterns and arbitrary false negatives, undermining their trustworthiness as concept classifiers.
> - While feature splitting (where general features diversify) is often beneficial, feature absorption (where specific features 'steal' causal credit) directly compromises the reliability and completeness of learned features.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated at the forefront of **mechanistic interpretability**, a field dedicated to reverse-engineering the internal algorithms and representations of neural networks, particularly large language models. The ultimate goal is to move beyond viewing LLMs as "black boxes" and gain a deeper, causal understanding of their behavior.

Central to this field are several key theoretical and practical concepts:
- **Linear Representation Hypothesis (LRH):** The theory that LLMs represent human-interpretable concepts as linear directions in their high-dimensional activation spaces.
- **Superposition:** A phenomenon where LLMs encode more concepts than they have neurons, causing individual neurons to be polysemantic (representing multiple concepts simultaneously). This makes direct neuron interpretation challenging.
- **Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs):** Introduced as a promising technique to address superposition. SAEs are neural networks trained to decompose dense, polysemantic LLM activations into a sparse set of "monosemantic" (single-concept) latent features. They achieve this through dictionary learning principles, aiming to recover the true underlying features from sparse activations.

Previous work with SAEs has identified a phenomenon called **feature splitting**, where a general feature in a smaller SAE might split into more specific features in a larger, overcomplete SAE (e.g., "starts with L" splitting into "starts with uppercase L" and "starts with lowercase l"). While feature splitting can increase granularity, it has generally been considered a potentially useful aspect, as the split features remain interpretable.

This paper critically intervenes in this landscape by introducing **feature absorption**, a *problematic* variant of feature splitting. It argues that while SAEs can indeed learn seemingly monosemantic features, these features are often unreliable. The authors demonstrate that the sparsity objective, fundamental to SAEs, can lead to hierarchical features (where one feature implies another, e.g., "short" implies "starts with S") being "absorbed." This means a more general parent feature might fail to activate when its child feature is present, with the child feature effectively "stealing" the parent's directional component.

By identifying and formally proving the existence of feature absorption, this work directly addresses a critical gap in the understanding of SAE robustness. Previous studies noted the "poor precision/recall of SAE latents compared to known proxies", but a comprehensive explanation has been lacking. This paper provides a compelling explanation for this observed low recall, challenging the naive assumption that SAE latents reliably track their apparent human-interpretable meanings across all contexts. It highlights a fundamental limitation of the sparse dictionary learning approach when underlying features form a hierarchy, regardless of other interpretability merits.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to thoroughly investigate and characterize a newly identified flaw in Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) that undermines their interpretability and reliability, particularly when dealing with hierarchical features.

The key objectives can be broken down into the following specific contributions:

1. **Identify and Define Feature Absorption:** To formally introduce and describe "feature absorption" as a problematic phenomenon where an SAE latent, despite appearing to track a human-interpretable concept, unexpectedly fails to activate in certain cases. In these instances, a more specific, usually token-aligned, latent "absorbs" the feature's direction, thereby "stealing credit" from the more general feature.
2. **Demonstrate the Causation:** To show that feature absorption is fundamentally caused by the sparsity optimization objective inherent in SAEs, especially when dealing with underlying features that form a hierarchy (e.g., "short" always implying "starts with S").
3. **Develop a Detection Metric:** To create a quantifiable metric that can reliably detect and measure the occurrence and extent of feature absorption within LLM SAEs.
4. **Empirical Validation:** To empirically validate the prevalence of feature absorption by testing it on a large number of diverse LLM SAEs, including hundreds of open-source models.

The motivation behind this investigation stems from several critical concerns regarding the practical application and trustworthiness of SAEs for LLM interpretability:

- **Reliability of SAE Latents:** If seemingly monosemantic latents exhibit "arbitrary false negatives" (i.e., failing to fire when they should), their perceived interpretability becomes an "illusion." This makes them inherently unreliable as classifiers for specific concepts.
- **High-Stakes Applications:** The unreliability introduced by absorption poses a significant obstacle for using SAEs in high-stakes scenarios, such as detecting and mitigating biases within LLMs or identifying deceptive behaviors, where confidence in a latent's activation pattern is paramount.
- **Circuit Discovery:** For efforts aimed at reverse-engineering LLM "circuits" by identifying sparse combinations of latents that perform specific computations, feature absorption complicates the task. It suggests that a much larger, less sparse set of latents might be needed to fully characterize a model's behavior, making circuit analysis more difficult.
- **Explaining Low Recall:** The paper aims to provide a mechanistic explanation for the "lower-than-expected recall" of SAE latents observed in previous work, which has remained poorly understood.
- **Foundational Understanding:** By analytically proving why absorption occurs and demonstrating it empirically, the authors seek to highlight a fundamental theoretical limitation of the current SAE paradigm, prompting future research into more robust dictionary learning methods.

In essence, the authors are motivated to move beyond surface-level interpretability (e.g., via max-activating examples) towards a more rigorous understanding of what SAE latents truly represent and how reliably they function.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors employ a multi-faceted methodology, combining theoretical analysis, toy model simulations, and extensive empirical validation on real LLM SAEs.

#### 3.1. Theoretical and Toy Model Analysis

The foundational argument for feature absorption begins with a **proof** (detailed in Appendix A.2) and **toy models** (Section 3, Appendix A.3).
- **Proof of Concept:** The authors analytically demonstrate that under conditions of hierarchical feature co-occurrence (e.g., feature f2 only fires if f1 also fires), an SAE's sparsity loss is minimized by "absorbing" f1's direction into f2 and preventing f1's dedicated latent from firing when f2 is present. This achieves perfect reconstruction with fewer active latents, making it an optimal strategy for an SAE optimizing for sparsity.
- **Initial Toy Model Setup:** A simple setting with four mutually orthogonal true features (50-dimensional, unit norm). An SAE with four latents is trained using L1 loss.
 - **Independent Features (Baseline):** When true features fire independently, the SAE perfectly recovers each feature with one latent.
 - **Hierarchical Co-occurrence:** Feature 1 is made to fire *only if* Feature 0 also fires. The SAE then exhibits absorption: Latent 0 (tracking Feature 0) learns to *not* fire when Feature 1 is active, while Latent 1 (tracking Feature 1) incorporates the direction of Feature 0 into its decoder, effectively representing "Feature 0 + Feature 1." This increases sparsity by requiring only one latent (Latent 1) to fire instead of two when both are present.
- **Extended Toy Models (Appendix A.3):**
 - **Magnitude Variance:** Showed that if parent feature magnitude varies, partial absorption occurs, where the parent latent still fires but very weakly when the child is present, sometimes turning off completely.
 - **Imperfect Co-occurrence:** Even when hierarchical co-occurrence is not perfect (e.g., Feature 1 co-occurs with Feature 0 95% of the time but can fire alone 5%), partial absorption still emerges.
 - **Batch TopK SAEs:** Demonstrated that absorption also occurs in batch TopK SAEs, indicating it is a consequence of sparsity optimization itself, not just L1 loss.

#### 3.2. LLM Experimental Setup

- **Models:** Primarily **Gemma-2-2B**, complemented by custom SAEs trained on **Qwen2 0.5B** and **Llama 3.2 1B**.
- **SAEs:** Utilized the comprehensive **Gemma Scope residual stream SAEs** (16k and 65k width) released by DeepMind, as well as custom-trained L1 loss and TopK SAEs.
- **Task:** **First-letter identification** from tokens. This task was chosen as a simple, well-defined, and causally traceable property of tokens.
- **Prompting:** In-context learning (ICL) prompts were used to elicit the model's knowledge (e.g., "tartan has the first letter: T\nmirth has the first letter: M\ndog has the first letter:").
- **Activations:** Residual stream activations at the target token index (e.g., `_dog`) were extracted for analysis and SAE application.
- **SAE Integration:** When applying SAEs, the SAE error term was included to prevent changes to the model's downstream output, ensuring causal interpretations focused on latent behavior.

#### 3.3. Metrics and Analysis

- **First-Letter Information Recovery:**
 - **Linear Probes (LR):** Trained on LLM activations to establish a baseline performance for the first-letter task.
 - **SAE Latent Identification:** SAE latents acting as "first-letter" classifiers were identified either by having the highest cosine similarity between their encoder direction and the LR probe direction, or via k=1 sparse probing (both methods yielded similar results, Appendix A.7).
 - **F1 Score, Precision, Recall:** Evaluated the classification performance of these identified SAE latents (Section 5.1, Appendix A.8).
- **Causal Intervention Studies:**
 - **Ablation Studies (Algorithm 1, Appendix A.4):** Used to determine the causal effect of individual SAE latents on the model's output for the first-letter task. This involved setting a latent's activation to zero and measuring the change in a defined metric.
 - **Metric:** `m = g[y] - (1/(|L|-1)) * sum(g[l] for l in L\y)`, where `g` are final logits, `y` is the correct letter, and `L` is the set of uppercase letters. This measures the model's confidence in the correct letter relative to incorrect ones (Appendix A.10). An alternative using `max` instead of `mean` was also explored with similar results.
 - **Probe Direction Projection:** To confirm that a latent's ablation effect was due to its component aligned with the probe direction, the probe direction was projected out of the latent before ablation.
- **Quantifying Feature Splitting (Section 5.3):**
 - **K-sparse Probing:** Used to measure if additional SAE latents provide meaningful signal for a feature. A "significant jump" in F1 score (defined as > 0.03, Appendix A.11) when increasing `k` was considered evidence of feature splitting.
- **Quantifying Feature Absorption (Section 5.3):**
 - **Feature Absorption Rate Metric:**
 1. Identify the `k` feature-split SAE latents for a given first-letter feature.
 2. Find "false-negative" tokens: cases where all `k` main latents fail to activate, but the LR probe correctly classifies the first letter.
 3. For these false-negative tokens, perform integrated-gradients ablation to find the most causally important SAE latents.
 4. An absorption event is declared if: (a) the latent with the largest negative ablation effect has a cosine similarity with the LR probe > 0.025, AND (b) its ablation effect is at least 1.0 larger than the second highest latent.
 5. `absorption_rate = num_absorptions / lr_probe_true_positives`.
 - **Limitations:** The metric relies on ablation studies, which become ineffective in later model layers (beyond ~Layer 17 in Gemma 2B) where attention has already moved the relevant information to the final token position (Appendix A.12). This means the metric provides a conservative estimate and cannot capture absorption across all layers.

#### 3.4. Tools

- **SAELens:** A library for training and analyzing SAEs.
- **Neuronpedia:** An online platform for hosting and exploring feature dashboards of SAE latents, used for inspecting max-activating examples.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yields several critical findings, reinforcing the paper's central argument about feature absorption.

#### 4.1. Performance of SAE Latents on First-Letter Identification

- **Suboptimal Performance:** Across hundreds of Gemma Scope SAEs, a wide variance in F1 score was observed for the first-letter identification task. Crucially, no SAE latent managed to match the performance of a simple linear probe (LR probe), suggesting that SAEs do not perfectly recover these features.
- **Sparsity's Influence on Precision/Recall:** The F1 score, and the balance between precision and recall, are highly dependent on the SAE's L0 sparsity coefficient. Low L0 values tend to produce high-precision, low-recall latents, while higher L0 values lead to low-precision, high-recall latents (Figure 5). This highlights a trade-off that cannot be resolved by simply adjusting sparsity.
- **Letter-Specific Variability:** The "optimal" L0 for achieving the best F1 score varies significantly across different letters, indicating that a single global hyperparameter setting might not be suitable for all features (Appendix A.8, Figure 20).

#### 4.2. Empirical Evidence for Feature Absorption (Case Study)

- **The "_short" Example:** A compelling case study in Gemma Scope layer 3, latent 6510, which appears to track the concept "starts with S" (0.81 F1 score). However, this latent surprisingly *fails* to activate on the token `_short`, despite `short` beginning with 'S'.
- **Absorbing Latent:** Instead, a different latent (1085), which is "token-aligned" with variations of the word "short," activates. While latent 1085 has a lower cosine similarity with the "starts with S" probe (0.12 vs. 0.52 for 6510), it activates with much higher magnitude for `_short`, contributing a similar amount of the "starts with S" direction to the residual stream (Figure 6).
- **Causal Confirmation:** Ablation studies on `_short` reveal that latent 1085 has a dramatically larger causal effect on the model's prediction of 'S' compared to other latents, including the main "starts with S" latent 6510 (Figure 7a). Further, removing the probe-aligned component from latent 1085 prior to ablation eliminates its significant causal effect (Figure 7b), confirming that its "starts with S" component is causally responsible.
- **Conclusion of Absorption:** This case study vividly demonstrates feature absorption: a seemingly interpretable "starts with S" latent fails to fire on a valid example, and its function is absorbed by a more specific, token-aligned latent.

#### 4.3. Quantification of Feature Splitting and Absorption

- **Feature Splitting is Widespread:** The authors observe strong evidence of feature splitting, such as the "L" feature splitting into "uppercase L" and "lowercase l" latents (Table 1, Figure 8a). This phenomenon is found to occur more frequently in SAEs with higher sparsity (L0) and wider architectures (Figure 8b). While common, feature splitting is not deemed inherently problematic if the split features remain clearly identifiable.
- **Feature Absorption is Prevalent:** Using their newly developed metric, the authors confirm that feature absorption occurs across a wide range of SAEs:
 - It is observed in **every LLM SAE tested**, including hundreds of Gemma Scope SAEs and custom-trained SAEs for Qwen2 0.5B and Llama 3.2 1B (Figure 9c).
 - **Absorption rate increases with higher sparsity (L0) and wider SAE architectures** (Figure 9b). This aligns with the theoretical argument that sparsity drives absorption as an optimization strategy.
- **Limitations of Absorption Metric:** The metric, relying on causal ablation, is limited to earlier layers (0-17 in Gemma 2B) where information is still localized at the source token. Beyond these layers, attention mechanisms move information, making causal tracing via source token ablation difficult (Appendix A.12). This suggests the reported absorption rates are likely conservative underestimates of the true extent of the phenomenon.

In summary, the results rigorously establish feature absorption as a pervasive and theoretically grounded limitation in current SAEs, directly impacting their reliability and interpretability.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The identification and comprehensive study of "feature absorption" by Chanin et al. carry significant implications for the field of mechanistic interpretability and the broader development of trustworthy AI.

#### 5.1. Undermining SAE Interpretability and Reliability

The most direct impact is on the perceived reliability of Sparse Autoencoders. The paper demonstrates that SAEs, by prioritizing sparsity, learn features that create an "interpretability illusion." A latent that appears monosemantic (e.g., "starts with S") might have arbitrary "false negatives" in its activation pattern, meaning it fails to fire in cases where its mainline interpretation suggests it should. This fundamental unreliability makes SAE latents less trustworthy as standalone classifiers for specific concepts.

#### 5.2. Obstacle to Practical Applications

Feature absorption presents a serious obstacle to several practical applications of SAEs:
- **High-Stakes Classification:** In domains requiring high confidence, such as detecting and mitigating biases in LLMs, ensuring compliance, or identifying instances of deceptive behavior, unreliable latents are unacceptable. If a "bias" latent fails to activate on certain biased examples due to absorption, it renders the interpretability tool ineffective and potentially misleading.
- **Causal Circuit Discovery:** Efforts to reverse-engineer LLM internal algorithms by identifying "sparse feature circuits" are complicated. If a feature's full causal role is distributed across multiple, more specific, "absorbing" latents instead of residing in a single, expected latent, then characterizing the true circuit requires analyzing a larger, less sparse set of latents. This increases complexity and reduces the clarity of the discovered circuits.
- **General Model Understanding:** The difficulty in confidently attributing behavior to specific SAE latents hinders the broader goal of truly understanding "black box" LLMs.

#### 5.3. Fundamental Limitation of Current SAE Design

The analytical proof and empirical validation across different SAE architectures (L1 loss, TopK, varying widths and sparsities) demonstrate that absorption is not a superficial bug but a fundamental consequence of the sparsity objective when hierarchical features exist. This suggests that simply tweaking hyperparameters or using slightly different SAE variants may be insufficient to fully solve the issue.

#### 5.4. Future Research Directions and Potential Solutions

The work naturally points towards critical avenues for future research:
- **Mitigation Strategies:** The primary goal is to find solutions to feature absorption. The authors suggest several promising directions:
 - **Meta-SAEs:** Training an SAE on the decoder of another SAE might help decompose absorbed latents into their constituent parts, potentially recovering the lost hierarchical structure.
 - **Attribution Dictionary Learning:** Exploring methods that explicitly incorporate causal attribution into the dictionary learning process might encourage more robust feature representations.
 - **Structured Sparsity:** Techniques like group lasso or hierarchical sparse coding could be adapted to explicitly model and enforce hierarchical relationships between features, preventing unwanted absorption.
- **Leveraging Absorption:** Instead of viewing absorption solely as a problem, the authors suggest it could be leveraged as a signal to "recover hierarchies between features in an LLM." The observed asymmetric patterns in the encoder and decoder of an SAE undergoing absorption might provide clues about underlying feature relationships.
- **Improved Metrics:** Developing alternative absorption metrics that can function reliably across all model layers, possibly by relying on probe alignment in reconstruction rather than causal ablation, could offer a more complete picture of the phenomenon.

In conclusion, "A is for Absorption" makes a crucial contribution to mechanistic interpretability by uncovering a widespread and theoretically grounded limitation of Sparse Autoencoders. It highlights that the pursuit of sparsity can inadvertently sacrifice the reliability and completeness of learned features, presenting a significant challenge that must be addressed before SAEs can be confidently used for robust, large-scale LLM interpretation. The paper serves as a vital call to action for the community to develop more sophisticated dictionary learning techniques that are robust to hierarchical feature interactions.
