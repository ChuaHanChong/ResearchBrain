# Morphologically Symmetric Reinforcement Learning for Ambidextrous Bimanual Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> The SYMDEX framework leverages morphological symmetry as an inductive bias within reinforcement learning to enable ambidextrous bimanual and multi-arm manipulation. The approach achieved over 80% success rates in six complex simulated bimanual tasks and demonstrated zero-shot sim-to-real transfer on physical robots, successfully scaling to four-arm systems with C4 symmetry.

## Problem

- Developing control policies for complex bimanual and multi-arm manipulation is challenging due to high-dimensional observation/action spaces and complex credit assignment.
- Achieving ambidextrous capabilities in bimanual robots, where either limb can interchangeably perform subtasks, is difficult for current reinforcement learning methods.
- Transferring learned policies from simulation to real-world environments robustly, while also ensuring safety such as preventing inter-arm collisions, remains a persistent challenge.

## Method

- The **SYMDEX** framework incorporates ==morphological symmetry== using ==G-equivariant neural networks== for policies and ==G-invariant networks== for value functions, formulated as a ==Multi-Task Multi-Agent POMDP==.
- Complex tasks are decomposed into per-hand subtasks with dedicated policies, which are then distilled into a unified, ambidextrous G-equivariant global policy.
- A two-component ==curriculum learning== strategy is employed, progressively introducing environmental randomization and safety penalties to facilitate robust zero-shot sim-to-real transfer.

## Results

- SYMDEX achieved over **80% success rates** across all six simulated bimanual manipulation tasks (e.g., box-lift, drawer-insert, threading), consistently outperforming baselines that lacked architectural symmetry or task decomposition.
- The framework demonstrated successful zero-shot sim-to-real transfer on physical robotic arms for box-lift and table-clean tasks, with policies generalizing to out-of-distribution objects.
- SYMDEX scaled effectively to a **four-arm** manipulation setup with **C4 symmetry**, enabling coordinated collaboration and generating symmetric behaviors consistent with the more intricate symmetry group.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Directly embedding morphological symmetry via G-equivariant network architectures serves as a strong inductive bias, enhancing sample efficiency and generalization for bimanual manipulation.
> - Decomposing complex bimanual tasks into per-hand subtasks with dedicated policies effectively addresses high-dimensionality and improves credit assignment, especially when arm roles diverge.
> - A structured curriculum learning approach, including gradual domain randomization and staged introduction of safety constraints, is crucial for achieving successful zero-shot sim-to-real transfer in dexterous manipulation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation, particularly bimanual and multi-arm systems, presents significant challenges due to high-dimensional observation and action spaces, complex credit assignment problems, and the difficulties associated with transferring learned policies from simulation to real-world environments. Reinforcement Learning (RL) has emerged as a paradigm for learning complex robot behaviors in simulation, offering robustness through training in randomized environments. However, its application to fully actuated bimanual or multi-robot systems performing dexterous, contact-rich tasks remains a subject of ongoing research. Previous RL approaches often simplify bimanual control by reducing degrees of freedom (DoF) or limiting task scope, thus not fully addressing the inherent complexity.

A separate but related area of research focuses on leveraging structural symmetries, referred to as morphological symmetries, as an inductive bias in robotic control. This approach has shown benefits in terms of improved control robustness and sample efficiency, predominantly in legged locomotion. Its application to manipulation, especially for bimanual or multi-robot systems with full DoF control, has been less explored. Existing work on symmetry in manipulation primarily addresses environmental symmetries (e.g., rotational symmetries in 3D objects) for tasks like grasping and pose estimation, often relying on task-space solvers and off-the-shelf inverse kinematics. This paper distinguishes itself by focusing on the morphological symmetries inherent in the robot's physical structure, operating entirely in joint space, and embedding these symmetries directly into the policy learning process for bimanual and multi-arm dexterous manipulation. The work aims to bridge the gap between exploiting morphological symmetry as an inductive bias and scaling RL to complex, ambidextrous manipulation tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a reinforcement learning framework that enables ambidextrous bimanual and multi-arm manipulation by explicitly leveraging the inherent bilateral symmetry of robotic systems as an inductive bias. The motivation stems from several observations and challenges:

First, humans exhibit bilateral symmetry in gross motor skills, allowing for mirroring actions between limbs. While fine dexterous tasks often lead to handedness, bimanual robots, which also possess bilateral symmetry, are not constrained by this. This presents an opportunity for robots to perform tasks ambidextrously, meaning they can interchangeably use either limb for a given subtask, making decisions based on efficiency rather than a fixed left/right preference. Achieving this ambidextrous capability is a core goal.

Second, learning control policies for complex bimanual manipulation using RL faces significant technical challenges. The high-dimensional observation and action spaces of fully actuated bimanual robots (e.g., two 6-DoF arms with 16-DoF hands each, totaling 44 DoF) lead to exponentially large exploration spaces, making policy learning difficult. The dual-arm setup and task complexity exacerbate the credit assignment problem, where individual arm contributions to a shared reward can be difficult to discern, especially when different arms perform distinct roles. Furthermore, when ambidexterity is desired, each arm may need to perform multiple distinct subtasks, which introduces elements of multi-task learning.

Third, ensuring robust deployment in the real world from policies trained in simulation (sim-to-real transfer) is a persistent challenge. This includes mitigating the sim-to-real gap and incorporating safety considerations, such as preventing inter-arm collisions, from the outset of policy learning.

To address these motivations and challenges, the paper introduces SYMDEX (SYMmetric DEXterity) with the following explicit contributions:
1. **Morphological Symmetry-Aware Policy Learning:** Develop an RL framework that leverages the inherent morphological symmetry in bimanual robotic systems to enable ambidextrous control and generalization across structurally symmetric task configurations.
2. **Scalable Framework for Multi-Arm Tasks:** Demonstrate the scalability of the approach to more complex multi-arm settings involving intricate symmetry groups without increasing task complexity, facilitating effective multi-arm collaboration and coordination.
3. **Full Learning Recipe for Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer:** Provide a comprehensive learning strategy, including a curriculum learning approach, to ensure robust real-world deployment without fine-tuning, thereby addressing sim-to-real transfer and safety concerns.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The SYMDEX framework addresses ambidextrous bimanual and multi-arm manipulation by embedding morphological symmetry as an inductive bias within its architectural and algorithmic design.

**A. MDP Formulation for Multi-Agent Multi-Task Manipulation:**
The approach formulates bimanual and multi-arm manipulation as a Multi-Task Multi-Agent Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (MTMA-POMDP). In this formulation:
- Each robot arm is considered an **agent**.
- A complex manipulation task is decomposed into **per-hand subtasks**.
- A dedicated policy is learned for each subtask. This decomposition aims to reduce the dimensionality of each agent's observation-action space and assign subtask-specific rewards, simplifying the credit assignment problem.

**B. Leveraging Morphological Symmetry via Equivariant Networks:**
The core of SYMDEX relies on the principles of group and representation theory to incorporate morphological symmetry.
- **Symmetry Group:** For bimanual systems, the symmetry group is typically the reflection group G = C2, representing bilateral symmetry. For multi-arm systems, more complex symmetry groups like C4 (90-degree rotations) are utilized.
- **G-Equivariance:** The dynamics of the MDP are assumed to be G-equivariant, meaning that the transformed state-action pair under a symmetry operation results in a symmetrically transformed subsequent state.
- **Policy and Value Function Constraints:** For a symmetric POMDP, the optimal policy is G-equivariant, and the optimal value function is G-invariant. To leverage this, the observation function must also be G-equivariant.
- **Equivariant Neural Networks:** Each subtask policy is parameterized as a G-equivariant Neural Network (NN), ensuring that transformations of the input observation lead to symmetrically transformed actions. This architectural choice enables inherent sharing of experience across symmetric limbs (e.g., training a policy for the left arm operating on an object automatically applies to the right arm operating on a symmetrically positioned object). Similarly, each subtask value function is parameterized as a G-invariant NN, meaning its output remains unchanged under symmetry transformations of the input observation.
- **Reinforcement Learning Algorithm:** The Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm is used to learn these G-equivariant policies and G-invariant value functions in parallel for each subtask.

**C. Global G-Equivariant Policy Distillation:**
To achieve a unified, ambidextrous policy that is independent of fixed hand-task assignments, SYMDEX employs a teacher-student distillation paradigm:
- **Teacher Policies:** The individual G-equivariant subtask policies learned in the previous step serve as "teacher" policies.
- **Data Generation:** These teacher policies are used to generate a dataset of state-action pairs. The global action space is formed by the tensor product of individual arm actions, ensuring that the collected data reflects the overall system's symmetric behavior.
- **Student Policy:** A unified "student" policy is trained to mimic the behavior of the teacher policies. This global policy is also designed as a G-equivariant neural network.
- **Ambidexterity:** The G-equivariance of the distilled global policy ensures that it can infer task-arm assignments dynamically from observations and perform identically across symmetric initial states. This enhances robustness and generalization to unseen configurations.
- **Joint Space Control:** The entire framework operates in joint space, with the policy outputting relative joint position actions. Joint commands are then smoothed using an exponential moving average.
- **Non-Privileged Observations:** The global policy is trained exclusively on non-privileged observations, making it suitable for zero-shot real-world deployment. The ESCNN library is utilized for implementing equivariant/invariant MLPs.

**D. Curriculum Learning for Sim-to-Real Transfer:**
To facilitate successful zero-shot transfer from simulation to the real world, a two-component curriculum learning strategy is implemented:
- **Randomization:** Training begins with scene-level symmetry randomization. As the agent's performance stabilizes (exceeds a success-rate threshold), environmental variations such as object poses and physical parameters (mass, friction, restitution) are gradually introduced.
- **Safety Penalty:** Collision and energy penalties are progressively introduced later in the curriculum. This avoids over-constraining the policy in early exploration phases, which could lead to overly conservative behaviors, while ensuring safety in later stages of learning and deployment. The curriculum progresses when the agent's success rate surpasses a predefined threshold.

**E. Real-World System:**
The real-world system consists of two 6-DoF xArm UF850 arms, each with a 16-DoF Allegro Hand V4 (44 total DoF). Control operates at 120Hz (low-level PD) and policy inference at 20Hz. Perception is managed by an egocentric ZED2i RGB-D camera combined with FoundationPose and SAM2 for robust multi-object 6D pose tracking. Pose estimates are smoothed with SLERP and EMA filtering.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of SYMDEX involved six simulated bimanual manipulation tasks and real-world deployment on two of them, along with a scalability demonstration on a four-arm system.

**A. Simulation Results:**
SYMDEX consistently achieved success rates exceeding 80% across all six challenging bimanual manipulation tasks: box-lift, table-clean, drawer-insert, threading, bowl-stir, and handover. This performance significantly surpassed all tested baselines.

- **Advantage of Task Decomposition:** Task decomposition proved beneficial, especially for tasks where the roles of the two arms diverge. Baselines like E-PPO, which use a single monolithic policy for the entire 44-DoF system, performed poorly on tasks requiring differentiated arm roles (e.g., drawer-insert, threading, bowl-stir, handover). While E-PPO succeeded on box-lift and partially on table-clean (where arms perform similar actions), its monolithic structure struggled to specialize. SYMDEX, by assigning a dedicated policy per subtask, avoided the challenges of multi-task learning within a single policy. Baselines like IPPO and E-IPPO, which also decomposed the action space but required each policy to handle multiple subtasks, also failed to generalize to more complex tasks.
- **Impact of G-Equivariance/Invariance Constraints:** Directly embedding symmetry priors into the network architecture (SYMDEX) consistently outperformed approaches that relied solely on on-policy symmetry-based data augmentation (SYMDEX-aug and E-IPPO vs. IPPO). This indicates that architectural constraints provide a stronger inductive bias for learning and generalization.
- **Impact of Centralized Learning:** The use of decentralized value functions in SYMDEX (a separate value function for each subtask) demonstrated superiority over centralized global value functions (E-PPO, SYMDEX-c). Centralized critics, which estimate total rewards across subtasks, suffered from poor credit assignment in complex, contact-rich settings. While task decomposition reduced action space dimensionality, combining it with a centralized critic (SYMDEX-c) still led to lower performance compared to E-PPO in some cases, suggesting a trade-off where decomposition can introduce uncertainty in joint optimization for centralized value functions.
- **Policy Distillation (Simulation):** Comparing different student policy architectures for distillation, both G-equivariant Gaussian and G-equivariant Diffusion policies outperformed a vanilla Gaussian policy. The equivariant Gaussian policy performed comparably to the diffusion variant. This suggests that incorporating equivariant constraints is important for robust distillation. The similar performance of the Gaussian policy was attributed to the teacher policies themselves being Gaussian, allowing for an effective fit to the generated dataset.

**B. Real-World Results:**
SYMDEX demonstrated successful zero-shot sim-to-real transfer on two representative tasks: box-lift and table-clean.

- **Curriculum Learning Importance:** Removing the curriculum learning strategy resulted in a significant drop in real-world performance, emphasizing the critical role of gradual domain randomization and safety constraint introduction for successful transfer.
- **Equivariant Gaussian vs. Diffusion in Real-World:** While the equivariant diffusion policy showed slightly better distillation results in simulation, the equivariant Gaussian policy proved more robust in real-world deployment. This was attributed to the diffusion model's sensitivity to out-of-distribution observations, particularly under imperfect state estimation from the real-world perception system, whereas the Gaussian policy's direct fit to the teacher's distribution made it more resilient to the sim-to-real gap.
- **Generalization to OOD Objects:** The policy successfully generalized to out-of-distribution objects (e.g., varying box sizes, an OOD toy dog) in real-world experiments, enabled by the curriculum learning strategy.

**C. Scalability to Multi-Robot Symmetry:**
The framework was successfully extended to a four-arm manipulation setup, featuring C4 symmetry (90-degree rotations). The task required two arms to hold a cardboard box while the other two picked and placed objects into it.
- **Coordinated Collaboration:** SYMDEX enabled effective multi-arm collaboration and coordination.
- **Symmetry-Aware Performance:** The system successfully completed the task from different initial orientations, with the G-equivariant policy generating symmetric behaviors consistent with the C4 group transformations. This demonstrates the scalability of SYMDEX to more intricate symmetry groups and multi-agent systems.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of robotic manipulation by introducing SYMDEX, an RL framework that effectively leverages morphological symmetries as an inductive bias for ambidextrous bimanual and multi-arm control. The significance and potential impact of this work are multi-faceted:

First, SYMDEX provides a principled approach to address several long-standing challenges in complex robotic manipulation, including the high-dimensionality of observation and action spaces, the difficulty of credit assignment in multi-agent settings, and the complexities of achieving reliable sim-to-real transfer. By decomposing tasks and employing equivariant neural networks, the framework inherently shares learning experience across symmetric limbs, leading to improved sample efficiency and robustness.

Second, the achieved ambidextrous manipulation capability allows robots to execute tasks with either hand based on efficiency rather than a fixed preference. This enhances the adaptability and flexibility of robotic systems, enabling them to handle diverse task configurations and dynamic environments more effectively. The demonstrated generalization to out-of-distribution objects further supports this adaptability.

Third, the scalability of SYMDEX to multi-arm systems with more complex symmetry groups (e.g., C4 for four arms) highlights its potential for orchestrating complex collaborative behaviors among multiple robotic agents. As robots with increasing numbers of limbs and greater morphological complexity (such as humanoid robots) become more prevalent, frameworks that can effectively leverage these inherent symmetries will be increasingly important.

Fourth, the integrated curriculum learning strategy for zero-shot sim-to-real transfer provides a robust recipe for deploying learned policies in physical environments without extensive real-world fine-tuning. This reduces development time and resources, accelerating the transition of advanced manipulation capabilities from simulation to practical applications.

The work acknowledges limitations, such as its reliance on the presence of morphological symmetry and its primary focus on kinematic-level symmetry rather than full dynamic symmetry. However, it argues that kinematic symmetry is sufficient for many manipulation tasks and applicable to a wide range of modern robotic platforms. Future work could explore the application of equivariant architectures to vision-based inputs to enhance robustness against perception system inaccuracies.

Overall, SYMDEX represents an advancement in the application of geometric deep learning principles to reinforcement learning for robotic manipulation. It offers a generalizable and scalable methodology that can contribute to the development of more capable, adaptable, and robust autonomous robotic systems for complex real-world tasks.
