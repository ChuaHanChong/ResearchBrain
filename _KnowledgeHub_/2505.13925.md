# Time Reversal Symmetry for Efficient Robotic Manipulations in Deep Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Shanghai Jiao Tong University and Duke Kunshan University developed the Time Reversal symmetry enhanced Deep Reinforcement Learning (TR-DRL) framework, which systematically leverages full and partial time reversal symmetries in robotic manipulation tasks. This framework significantly improves sample efficiency and final success rates across various Robosuite and Meta-World benchmarks.

## Problem

- Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) for robotics often suffers from low sample efficiency and insufficient robustness in complex tasks.
- Existing DRL methods leveraging symmetry primarily focus on spatial symmetries (e.g., reflection), largely neglecting the potential of temporal symmetries like time reversal.
- Previous attempts to incorporate time reversal symmetry were limited by restrictive assumptions, such as simple action negation, making them unsuitable for complex environments with contact dynamics or partial reversibility.

## Method

- The **TR-DRL** framework systematically exploits both full and a newly introduced concept of partial ==time reversal== (PTR) symmetry for robotic manipulation tasks, operating on pairs of reversible tasks.
- For tasks with full time reversal symmetry, it uses trajectory reversal augmentation: an inverse dynamics model learns reversed actions, and a forward dynamics model with a dynamics-aware filter validates and includes only physically feasible augmented transitions.
- For tasks exhibiting only partial time reversal symmetry, a time reversal guided reward shaping mechanism is employed, utilizing a potential function trained on successful trajectories of the reversed task to provide dense, informative feedback to the agent.

## Results

- TR-DRL achieved superior sample efficiency and final performance in Robosuite single-task settings, consistently reaching 1.00 success rates often earlier than baseline SAC (e.g., **0.33** IQM for TR-SAC vs. **0.11** for SAC at 50k transitions).
- The framework consistently enhanced both sample efficiency and final performance when integrated with multi-task baselines (Task-Cond SAC, Multi-Head SAC, Language-Conditioned SAC) on Robosuite and Meta-World MT50 benchmarks.
- Ablation studies confirmed that both trajectory reversal augmentation with dynamics-aware filtering and time reversal guided reward shaping individually contributed to performance gains, with their combination yielding the strongest results and validating design choices.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Temporal symmetries, particularly time reversal, are a largely underexplored yet powerful resource for enhancing DRL sample efficiency and performance in dynamic robotic tasks.
> - Many real-world robotic tasks exhibit only partial time reversal, where only specific object-related state components are reversible, requiring a novel approach like PTR symmetry to be effectively leveraged.
> - Accurate dynamics models are crucial for validating generated reversed transitions, preventing the inclusion of physically infeasible data that can degrade DRL agent performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) has demonstrated considerable success in solving complex tasks across various domains, including robotics, quantitative trading, and video games. However, a persistent challenge in DRL applications is its often low sample efficiency and, at times, inadequate agent robustness. To mitigate these issues, the concept of symmetry, a common property in many real-world scenarios, has been extensively leveraged in DRL research.

Existing work in DRL that exploits symmetry has predominantly focused on **spatial symmetries**. These include transformations such as reflection, rotation, and translation. Spatial symmetries are applied in various ways: through data augmentation to generate synthetic transitions from observed interactions, by embedding symmetry directly into neural network architectures to ensure inherent symmetry-respecting properties, or by enforcing symmetry as a regularization term during training. While these approaches have shown effectiveness in improving sample efficiency and agent performance, particularly in state-based and image-based control tasks, they primarily address static or geometric invariances.

In contrast, **temporal symmetries**, which relate to invariances over time, have remained comparatively underexplored in DRL. Time-reversal symmetry, specifically, implies that a system's dynamics are invariant under a reversal of the time direction, often accompanied by a reversal of actions. Intuitively, this means that if an agent can perform a task, it might be able to perform the reverse task by reversing its actions. Previous efforts exploring time reversal symmetry in DRL have been limited, sometimes assuming simple action negation for reversal or focusing solely on exploration from goal states. These approaches often encounter difficulties in complex environments involving contact dynamics or non-linear effects, where simple action negation may not yield physically valid reversed transitions. Furthermore, many real-world robotic tasks may exhibit only partial temporal reversibility, a scenario not adequately addressed by existing methods.

This paper addresses this gap by systematically investigating and exploiting time reversal symmetry in DRL for robotic manipulation tasks. It introduces a novel framework that not only accounts for full time reversal symmetry but also proposes a new concept of *partial time reversal symmetry*, thereby extending the applicability of temporal symmetry to a broader range of complex robotic interactions. By developing methods to identify and utilize both full and partial time reversal symmetries, this work aims to significantly enhance the sample efficiency and final performance of DRL algorithms, pushing beyond the conventional focus on spatial invariances.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to improve the sample efficiency and final performance of Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) algorithms when applied to robotic manipulation tasks by systematically leveraging time reversal symmetry. This overarching goal is driven by several key motivations and leads to specific aims:

1. **Exploiting Underexplored Temporal Symmetries:** The core motivation stems from the observation that while spatial symmetries (e.g., reflection, rotation) are widely used to enhance DRL, temporal symmetries, particularly time reversal symmetry, are largely neglected. The authors contend that time reversal symmetry is pervasive in robotics tasks, such as opening and closing a door or inserting and removing a peg, yet its potential for improving DRL sample efficiency remains untapped.

2. **Addressing Limitations of Existing Time Reversal Approaches:** Previous attempts to incorporate time reversal symmetry in DRL often suffer from restrictive assumptions. For instance, some methods rely on the direct negation of actions for reversed transitions, which is often infeasible in real-world robotics due to complex contact dynamics, friction, or non-linear effects. Other approaches might focus only on goal-directed exploration, missing the opportunity to leverage time reversal across entire trajectories. This paper is motivated to develop a more general and robust approach that can derive valid reversed actions and apply time reversal principles more broadly.

3. **Introducing and Utilizing Partial Time Reversal Symmetry:** A significant motivation is the recognition that many robotic tasks do not exhibit full time reversal symmetry in all their state-action components. For example, a robot can push a door closed but cannot "un-push" it open without first grasping the handle. However, the *object state* (e.g., the door's angle) might still be reversible. This observation motivates the introduction of the novel concept of "partial time reversal (PTR) symmetry," which aims to exploit temporal symmetry even when only specific components of the state (e.g., object-related information) are reversible.

4. **Developing a Comprehensive Framework (TR-DRL):** To achieve the above, the authors aim to propose a general framework, named Time Reversal symmetry enhanced Deep Reinforcement Learning (TR-DRL), that can effectively combine multiple techniques to leverage both full and partial time reversal symmetry. This involves creating a unified approach that integrates different mechanisms for data augmentation and reward shaping.

5. **Enhancing Sample Efficiency via Data Augmentation:** For tasks exhibiting full time reversal symmetry, a key objective is to generate valid reversed transitions from collected data to augment the replay buffer. This requires learning an inverse dynamics model to infer reversed actions and, crucially, a mechanism to filter out invalid reversed transitions that violate physical dynamics.

6. **Guiding Learning via Reward Shaping for Partial Reversibility:** For tasks with partial time reversal symmetry, where direct trajectory reversal augmentation might be less effective, the objective is to leverage the reversible components (e.g., object state changes) to guide the agent's learning. This is achieved through a novel time reversal guided reward shaping mechanism, which encourages the agent to follow state progressions that align with reversed successful trajectories.

By addressing these objectives, the research seeks to provide a more effective and broadly applicable methodology for integrating temporal symmetries into DRL, thereby enhancing the learning process for complex robotic manipulation tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework, **Time Reversal symmetry enhanced Deep Reinforcement Learning (TR-DRL)**, is designed to improve the sample efficiency of DRL algorithms by systematically exploiting time reversal symmetry. It operates on pairs of reversible tasks (e.g., door opening and closing) and comprises two primary techniques: trajectory reversal augmentation with dynamics-aware filtering, and time reversal symmetry guided reward shaping. The framework is integrated into a standard DRL algorithm, such as Soft Actor-Critic (SAC), and alternates training between the two symmetric tasks.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation: Full vs. Partial Time Reversal Symmetry

The paper distinguishes between two types of time reversal symmetry:

- **Full Time Reversal (FTR) Symmetry:** An MDP satisfies FTR symmetry if, for any transition (s_t, a_t, s_{t+1}), there exists a corresponding reversed transition (s_{t+1,rev}, a_{t,rev}, s_{t,rev}) such that the transition probability is equal (T(s_{t+1}|s_t, a_t) = T(s_{t,rev}|s_{t+1,rev}, a_{t,rev})). Unlike previous work that assumes a known involution for actions, this paper relaxes this assumption, necessitating learning the reversed action. An example is opening/closing a door outward where the agent consistently grasps the handle.

- **Partial Time Reversal (PTR) Symmetry:** This is a novel concept introduced by the authors. It applies when a state `s` can be decomposed into `(x, y)`, where `x` represents the object-related components (e.g., door angle, peg position) and `y` represents other components (e.g., robot joint angles). PTR symmetry holds if, for a pair of states `(s, s')`, there exist `(y_rev, y'_rev)` and `(a, a_rev)` such that `T(s'|s, a) = T(s_rev|s'_rev, a_rev)`, where `x_rev = f_X(x)` and `s_rev = (x_rev, y_rev)`. This acknowledges that some parts of the state (like object positions) might be reversible, even if the entire physical interaction (e.g., pushing vs. pulling without grasping) is not. An example is closing a door by pushing it versus opening it by pulling a handle, where the door's state (`x`) is reversible, but the robot's actions and internal state (`y`) might not be.

#### 3.2. Trajectory Reversal Augmentation with Dynamics-Aware Filtering (for FTR)

This technique focuses on generating valid reversed transitions for tasks exhibiting FTR symmetry, thereby augmenting the DRL agent's training data.

1. **Learning Inverse Dynamics Model (h):** To find the reversed action `a_rev` for a transition `(s, a, s')`, an inverse dynamics model `h(s, s')` is trained as a neural network to predict the original action `a`. This model learns the mapping `a = h(s, s')` by minimizing the mean squared error `L_h = E[(h(s, s') - a)^2]` over collected transitions. Since reversible tasks share underlying dynamics, a single inverse dynamics model is jointly trained using data from both tasks. When applied to `(s', s)`, it predicts `a_rev = h(s', s)`.

2. **Learning Forward Dynamics Model (g):** To ensure the validity of the generated reversed transitions, a forward dynamics model `g(s, a)` is also trained. This model predicts the next state `s'` given a current state `s` and action `a`. It minimizes `L_g = E[(g(s, a) - s')^2]`.

3. **Dynamics-Consistent Filtering:** For a potential reversed transition `(s', a_rev, s)` (where `a_rev = h(s', s)`), the forward dynamics model is used to predict the next state: `s_pred = g(s', a_rev)`. The reversed transition is deemed valid and added to the replay buffer for the reversed task only if the prediction error `||s - s_pred||` is below a predefined threshold `β`. This filter prevents the inclusion of physically infeasible augmented transitions.

#### 3.3. Time Reversal Symmetry Guided Reward Shaping (for PTR)

For scenarios where transitions are only partially reversible, the trajectory reversal augmentation might be ineffective due to frequent filtering. This technique leverages PTR symmetry through reward shaping.

1. **Potential Model (Φ):** A potential function `Φ(x)` is trained for the *reversed task*, mapping the object-related state `x` to a potential value. This model is trained to predict high potential values for object states closer to the successful end state of the reversed trajectory and low values for the start state.

2. **Training the Potential Model:** The potential model `Φ` is trained using high-reward (successful) trajectories `τ = (s_0..., s_n)` from the *original* task. For each state `s_t` in such a trajectory, its object component `x_t` is extracted. For the *reversed task*, these object states `x_t` are conceptually traversed in reverse (from `x_n` to `x_0`). Potential values are assigned linearly from 0 to 1 along this reversed sequence (e.g., `(n-t)/n` for `x_t`). The model `Φ` is trained to minimize `L_Φ = E[(Φ(x_t) - (n-t)/n)^2]`. The paper's ablation studies indicate that training two separate potential models (one for successful trajectories from its own task and one from its reversible counterpart) and averaging their rewards is effective, and a linear function performs well for potential value interpolation.

3. **Potential-Based Reward Shaping:** Following the principles of potential-based reward shaping, the reward `r_t` for each transition `(s_t, a_t, s_{t+1})` in the reversed task is modified as `r_t' = r_t + γΦ(x_{t+1}) - Φ(x_t)`. This shaped reward encourages the agent to navigate the object state space in a manner that increases the potential function, effectively guiding it along a path that resembles the reversed successful trajectories. This mechanism preserves policy optimality while providing dense, informative feedback.

#### 3.4. TR-DRL Algorithm Integration (Algorithm 1)

The TR-DRL algorithm integrates these components within an off-policy DRL framework (e.g., SAC). The training process involves:
- Alternating between collecting data from the two reversible tasks.
- Updating the forward and inverse dynamics models using all collected transitions.
- Updating the potential models using successful trajectories.
- Generating augmented transitions for the current task from its reversible counterpart, filtered by the dynamics-aware filter.
- Applying time reversal symmetry guided reward shaping to all transitions.
- Updating the DRL agent's actor and critic networks using both original and augmented, reshaped transitions.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research conducted extensive experiments on two established robotics control benchmarks, Robosuite and Meta-World, to evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed TR-DRL framework in both single-task and multi-task settings.

#### 4.1. Experimental Setup

- **Environments:** 60 environments were used in total, comprising 10 environments from Robosuite (5 pairs of reversible tasks like door open/close inward/outward, peg insert/remove, nut assembly/disassembly, block stack/unstack) and 50 environments from Meta-World (MT50), within which 12 pairs of reversible tasks were identified.
- **Baselines:** Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) for single-task settings; task-conditioned SAC, multi-headed SAC, and language-conditioned SAC (for Meta-World) for multi-task settings.
- **Reward Scheme:** Sparse rewards were used, a challenging setting for DRL. To provide initial guidance, agents' replay buffers were pre-filled with 10 expert demonstration trajectories.
- **Evaluation Metrics:** All results were averaged over five independent runs, reporting the mean and standard deviation. Performance was measured by success rate, evaluated every 20 training episodes, and aggregated using the Inter-Quartile Mean (IQM) as proposed by Agarwal et al..

#### 4.2. Main Results

- **Robosuite - Single-Task Setting (10 Environments):**
 - TR-DRL (referred to as Single-Task TR-SAC) demonstrated superior sample efficiency and final performance compared to the baseline Single-Task SAC and configurations utilizing only one of TR-DRL's components.
 - For example, at 50k environment transitions, Single-Task SAC achieved an IQM success rate of 0.11±0.07, while Single-Task TR-SAC achieved 0.33±0.14. By 150k transitions, Single-Task SAC reached 0.93±0.07, whereas Single-Task TR-SAC achieved a perfect 1.00±0.00 success rate, often significantly earlier.
 - Ablation results confirmed that both trajectory reversal augmentation and time reversal guided reward shaping individually contributed to performance gains, with their combination yielding the strongest results.

- **Robosuite - Multi-Task Setting (10 Environments):**
 - TR-DRL was integrated with existing multi-task baselines (Task-Cond SAC and Multi-Head SAC).
 - The integrated TR-DRL consistently enhanced both the sample efficiency and final performance of these multi-task methods. For instance, Multi-Head SAC achieved an IQM of 0.23±0.09 at 50k transitions, which improved to 0.54±0.17 when combined with TR-DRL (Multi-Head TR-SAC). At 200k transitions, all TR-DRL integrated multi-task variants achieved 1.00±0.00 success rates, outperforming their non-TR counterparts.

- **Meta-World (MT50) - Multi-Task Setting (12 Reversible Task Pairs):**
 - On the 12 identified reversible task pairs within MT50, TR-DRL again showed clear improvements. For example, Language-Conditioned SAC achieved an IQM of 0.63±0.05 at 50k transitions, which rose to 0.66±0.05 with TR-DRL integration (Lang-Cond TR-SAC).
 - Table 2, in the supplementary material, illustrates that TR-DRL significantly reduced the average number of training episodes required to achieve a 100% success rate across many of these tasks (e.g., "door unlock" reduced from 96±10 episodes for Lang-Cond SAC to 72±5 for Lang-Cond TR-SAC; "drawer close" from 8±1 to 8±1, indicating optimal performance already).

- **Meta-World (MT50) - All 50 Tasks:**
 - When applied across all 50 tasks in MT50, TR-DRL maintained its beneficial effect, demonstrating faster learning and better performance compared to the baselines. The average number of training episodes for 100% success across all 50 tasks for Language-Conditioned SAC was 216±28, which reduced to 196±28 for Language-Conditioned TR-SAC (Table 3).

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies

- **Dynamics-Aware Filtering:** Experiments confirmed that for partially reversible tasks (e.g., door opening/closing inward), incorporating trajectory reversal augmentation *without* filtering led to degraded performance due to the inclusion of invalid transitions. The dynamics-aware filter successfully removed these infeasible transitions, resulting in performance that surpassed the baseline. The hyperparameter `β` (error tolerance) was tuned, with `β = 0.01` selected for optimal performance.
- **Time Reversal Symmetry Guided Reward Shaping:**
 - The study on training strategies for potential models concluded that training two separate potential models—one using the task's own successful trajectories and another using the reversible task's successful trajectories—and averaging their derived rewards yielded the best performance.
 - Among various potential value labeling functions (linear, triangular, geometric), a linear function was found to be the most effective for interpolating potential values along successful trajectories.

#### 4.4. Conclusion of Results

The experimental results consistently demonstrate that TR-DRL, with its integrated approach to leveraging full and partial time reversal symmetry, significantly improves the sample efficiency and final success rates of DRL agents across a diverse set of robotic manipulation tasks in both single-task and multi-task learning environments. The ablation studies further validate the necessity and design choices of the individual components of the framework.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a notable advancement in the field of Deep Reinforcement Learning for robotics, particularly in addressing challenges related to sample efficiency and agent performance.

#### 5.1. Advancement in DRL for Robotics

The primary significance of this work lies in its systematic exploration and exploitation of temporal symmetries, specifically time reversal symmetry, in DRL. While spatial symmetries have been widely studied, this paper rigorously demonstrates the untapped potential of temporal symmetries, which are highly relevant to dynamic robotic manipulation tasks. By introducing the novel concept of "partial time reversal symmetry," the framework extends the applicability of temporal symmetry to a broader and more realistic spectrum of tasks where full physical reversibility may not hold due to complex interactions like contact and friction.

#### 5.2. Improved Sample Efficiency and Performance

The empirical results consistently show that TR-DRL leads to significant improvements in sample efficiency and final success rates across various Robosuite and Meta-World benchmarks. DRL agents augmented with TR-DRL learn faster and achieve higher performance ceilings. This reduction in the amount of data required for training is critical in robotics, where real-world data collection can be costly, time-consuming, and resource-intensive.

#### 5.3. Generalizability and Integration

The proposed TR-DRL framework is designed to be orthogonal to existing DRL algorithms (e.g., SAC) and multi-task learning frameworks (e.g., task-conditioned, multi-headed, language-conditioned). This modularity suggests that TR-DRL can be readily integrated into various DRL pipelines, enhancing their performance without requiring fundamental changes to the underlying learning algorithms. This broad applicability increases its potential for widespread adoption.

#### 5.4. Practical Implications and Potential for Real-World Deployment

By improving sample efficiency, TR-DRL contributes to making DRL more practical for real-world robotic applications. Faster training translates to reduced computational resource consumption, lower energy usage, and less hardware wear, aligning with sustainability goals. More robust and efficient DRL agents can accelerate the development and deployment of autonomous systems in manufacturing, healthcare, and service industries, potentially enhancing productivity, safety, and accessibility.

#### 5.5. Future Directions

The paper opens avenues for future research, notably the idea of automatically identifying reversible task pairs using prediction errors. Such an advancement could remove the need for manual identification of symmetric tasks, further streamlining the application of TR-DRL and making it even more autonomous and accessible.

#### 5.6. Limitations and Considerations

The authors acknowledge a key limitation: the absence of real-robot experiments. All evaluations were conducted in simulation environments, which, while enabling efficient prototyping, may not fully capture the complexities, noise, and physical constraints of real-world robotic systems. Future work would need to validate the method's robustness and generalizability on physical robots. Additionally, like other data augmentation methods, TR-DRL relies on prior knowledge about the task structure (specifically, time reversal symmetry), which might need to be provided or learned.

In summary, this research makes a substantial contribution by systematically addressing the underexplored area of temporal symmetries in DRL for robotics. The TR-DRL framework offers a robust and effective approach to boost learning efficiency, with significant implications for the development of more capable and deployable robotic systems.
