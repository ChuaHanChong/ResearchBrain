# OmniSpatial: Towards Comprehensive Spatial Reasoning Benchmark for Vision Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> OmniSpatial, a new benchmark based on cognitive psychology, evaluates Vision-Language Models on complex visual-spatial reasoning tasks including dynamic reasoning, spatial logic, interaction, and perspective-taking. It revealed that state-of-the-art models achieve over 56% accuracy, still more than 30 percentage points below human performance, with particular struggles in geometric reasoning and non-egocentric perspectives, while demonstrating improvements with explicit geometric cues and multi-view imagination.

## Problem

- Existing benchmarks for Vision-Language Models (VLMs) primarily assess basic spatial relations, with advanced models reaching over 90% accuracy, indicating saturation and insufficient challenge.
- Current VLM evaluations fail to capture the breadth and depth of human-like spatial cognition, which involves complex, dynamic, context-dependent, and 3D structural reasoning, as well as perspective-taking.
- The absence of a comprehensive and cognitively-informed benchmark hinders the identification of specific VLM limitations in advanced spatial understanding, thus impeding the development of capable embodied AI.

## Method

- Introduced **OmniSpatial**, a comprehensive visual-spatial reasoning benchmark organized by a taxonomy of four dimensions (dynamic reasoning, complex spatial logic, spatial interaction, perspective-taking) with 50 fine-grained subcategories.
- Constructed the **OmniSpatial** dataset with 8.4K manually curated question-answer pairs, sourcing images and video frames from diverse real-world contexts, public tests, and existing datasets to ensure variety and minimize bias.
- Developed two strategies to enhance VLM spatial reasoning: **PointGraph**, which integrates explicit ==scene graph== cues, and **SpatialCoT**, which augments visual inputs with ==3D novel views== to stimulate spatial imagination.

## Results

- Proprietary VLMs like ChatGPT o3 and Gemini-2.5-pro achieved the highest scores at over **56%** on OmniSpatial, but a significant gap of over **30 percentage points** remains compared to human accuracy (**92.6%**).
- Models performed stronger in Dynamic Reasoning and Spatial Interaction categories, but showed considerable weaknesses in Complex Spatial Logic (**30-40%** accuracy) and Perspective-Taking, particularly with allocentric or hypothetical viewpoints.
- PointGraph and SpatialCoT strategies consistently boosted VLM performance, especially in Dynamic Reasoning and Perspective-Taking; supervised fine-tuning on the OmniSpatial-train dataset yielded a **+7.82** average point gain for a baseline model, outperforming template-style data training.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - State-of-the-art VLMs, including proprietary models like ChatGPT o3 and Gemini-2.5-pro, achieve an average success rate of just over 56% on OmniSpatial, remaining significantly below human performance (92.6% accuracy).
> - VLMs demonstrate substantial limitations in complex spatial logic, such as pattern recognition and geometric reasoning (scoring 30-40%), and struggle with non-egocentric perspective-taking tasks.
> - Providing explicit structured object representations (PointGraph) and stimulating spatial imagination through multi-view inputs (SpatialCoT) consistently improves VLM accuracy in various spatial reasoning tasks, indicating the value of direct geometric cues and richer spatial context.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Spatial reasoning is a foundational cognitive ability essential for tasks that bridge visual observation with physical action, such as robotic manipulation, autonomous driving, and augmented/virtual reality (AR/VR). For artificial intelligence systems to operate effectively in the physical world, they must possess a robust understanding of spatial relationships to infer appropriate actions. Prior research has focused on integrating spatial information into Vision-Language Models (VLMs) and developing benchmarks to evaluate basic spatial understanding. These efforts typically target elementary spatial relations, including position (e.g., left/right, front/back), proximity (e.g., near/far), and object counting.

However, the authors observe that current advanced reasoning models, such as OpenAI's o3 and Google's Gemini-2.5-Pro, have achieved over 90% accuracy on these existing benchmarks (e.g., SpatialBot-Bench, EmbSpatial). This indicates that the field is approaching saturation in evaluating basic spatial understanding. The broader research landscape, informed by cognitive psychology, suggests that human spatial reasoning encompasses more complex capabilities, including interpreting ambiguous, dynamic, and context-dependent spatial relationships, understanding dynamic world-knowledge, engaging in interactive spatial behaviors, performing logical analysis of three-dimensional structures, and exercising perspective-taking abilities. These advanced forms of spatial reasoning remain a challenge for current VLMs.

OmniSpatial addresses this gap by introducing a benchmark designed to evaluate VLMs on a comprehensive spectrum of spatial reasoning tasks. By expanding the scope beyond basic relations, this work aims to provide a more rigorous assessment of VLM capabilities, aligning evaluation with the complexities of human spatial cognition and the demands of real-world embodied AI applications. The benchmark serves as a tool to identify current limitations in VLMs and to guide the development of future spatially- and physically-aware AI systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce OmniSpatial, a comprehensive and challenging benchmark for visual-spatial reasoning, grounded in principles from cognitive psychology. This benchmark is designed to overcome the limitations of existing evaluation methods, which primarily focus on basic spatial relations and have reached saturation with current VLM capabilities.

The key objectives and motivations are detailed as follows:

- **Address Saturation in Basic Spatial Reasoning Benchmarks:** Current benchmarks largely assess elementary spatial relationships (e.g., distinguishing left from right, near from far, object counting). The authors demonstrate that advanced VLMs already achieve high accuracy (over 90%) on these tasks, indicating that these basic evaluations are no longer sufficient to drive progress in complex spatial understanding. This motivates the need for a benchmark that challenges models with more sophisticated spatial problems.
- **Bridge the Gap with Human-like Spatial Cognition:** Human interaction with the physical world necessitates interpreting nuanced, dynamic, and context-dependent spatial relationships. Tasks such as route planning in an emergency, inserting a knife into a rack, or flattening a box require reasoning about motion, deformation, and complex spatial compatibility, far exceeding simple object placement. The authors are motivated by the understanding from cognitive psychology that complex spatial reasoning involves dynamic world-knowledge reasoning, interactive spatial behavior, logical analysis of 3D structures, and perspective-taking abilities. OmniSpatial aims to capture this breadth and depth of human-like spatial cognition.
- **Provide a Principled Foundation for Advanced AI Development:** By systematically categorizing spatial reasoning into four core dimensions—dynamic reasoning, complex spatial logic, spatial interaction, and perspective-taking—with 50 fine-grained subcategories, the research seeks to offer a structured framework. This framework is intended to provide a principled foundation for researchers developing next-generation spatially- and physically-aware AI systems, guiding them toward addressing specific cognitive faculties of spatial understanding.
- **Develop a Diverse and Challenging Dataset:** A critical objective is the construction of the OmniSpatial dataset, comprising over 8.4K manually annotated question-answer pairs. The motivation behind this is to provide a robust evaluation tool that features diverse scenes, resolutions, lighting conditions, and task complexities, avoiding the biases and limitations often associated with template-based or synthetically generated data.
- **Explore Strategies for Enhancing Spatial Reasoning:** Beyond benchmarking, the authors aim to investigate methods to bolster VLMs' spatial reasoning capabilities. This involves exploring strategies such as integrating explicit scene graph cues (PointGraph) and stimulating spatial imagination through novel-view chain-of-thought prompting (SpatialCoT). This objective is motivated by the desire to not only identify current limitations but also to propose actionable directions for improvement.

In essence, the motivation is to advance the field from evaluating what VLMs *can* do in basic spatial tasks to understanding what they *cannot* yet do in complex, human-relevant spatial reasoning, thereby fostering the development of more capable and intelligent AI systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research centers on the development of the OmniSpatial benchmark, encompassing a novel taxonomy of visual-spatial reasoning, a meticulous data collection and annotation process, and an evaluation framework for state-of-the-art VLMs. Additionally, the authors introduce two strategies to enhance VLM spatial reasoning.

#### 3.1. Taxonomy of Visual-Spatial Reasoning

The research defines visual-spatial reasoning as the capacity of an artificial system to infer, predict, and reason about spatial properties from visual observations. This definition excludes non-visual priors to attribute improvements directly to visual reasoning. The taxonomy is informed by cognitive psychology, which identifies faculties such as visualization, mental rotation, and perspective-taking, and by the need to move beyond basic spatial relations.
The authors partition visual-spatial reasoning into four complementary dimensions, each with fine-grained subcategories, totaling 50:

- **Dynamic Reasoning (27% of tasks):** Concerns inferring motion and temporal change from visual evidence. Subcategories include Manipulation (Operational Position Selection, Movement Direction Determination, Intent Recognition) and Motion Analysis (Uniform Motion, Variable Motion, Spatial Compatibility). This is crucial for adaptive decision-making in robotics and navigation.
- **Complex Spatial Logic (16% of tasks):** Involves higher-order reasoning about relations, transformations, and geometric structures. Subcategories include Pattern Recognition (Style, Quantity, Attributes, Location) and Geometric Reasoning (Polyhedron Unfolding, Sections and Projections, Mental Rotation, Assembly, Analytical Geometry). This underpins problem-solving in design, engineering, and manipulation.
- **Spatial Interaction (20% of tasks):** Emphasizes reasoning guided by environmental constraints and task goals. Subcategories include Traffic Analysis (Anomaly Detection, Sign Recognition, Action Recognition, Risk Detection, Behavior Guidance, Contextual Analysis), Localization (UI Interaction, Object Detection, Spatial Localization, Pose Estimation), and Geospatial Strategy (Spatial Recognition, Location Recognition, Region Recognition, Route Interpretation, Route Design, Route Selection, Navigation, Map/Scene Conversion, Legend Recognition, Terrain Identification). This covers skills like path planning and obstacle avoidance.
- **Perspective-Taking (37% of tasks):** Captures the ability to adopt alternative viewpoints. Subcategories include Egocentric (Count, Size, Direction, Order, Distance), Allocentric (Count, Size, Direction, Order, Distance), and Hypothetical Perspective Taking (Count, Size, Direction, Order, Distance). This enables understanding relations from diverse perspectives and flexible problem-solving.

#### 3.2. Benchmark Construction (OmniSpatial Dataset)

The OmniSpatial dataset comprises 8.4K manually curated question-answer (QA) pairs, emphasizing diversity, structure, and rigor, rather than sheer volume.

- **Data Collection:** Images and video frames are sourced from heterogeneous origins to ensure realism and complexity:
 - **Web Images:** Collected using task-specific search terms and filters to reduce synthetic content, covering diverse scenes, resolutions, lighting, and weather. All images are under MIT or CC-BY 4.0 license.
 - **Exam-Based Test Questions:** Public spatial cognition tests, including 3D transformations, rotations, and perspective shifts, are scraped and manually curated.
 - **Driving Test Questions:** Sourced from driving exam websites, standardized online tasks, and interactive U.S. driving test videos for dynamic reasoning in safety-critical scenarios.
 - **Existing Dataset Images:** Integration of MME (RGB-D data for depth-based inference and physics-based questions) and HOI4D (human-object interaction videos for motion prediction tasks) to introduce real-world motion and interaction complexities.
- **Question-Answer Annotation:** Multiple-choice questions (binary or four-option) are designed in a natural, conversational, and context-rich style. Six trained annotators (initially the authors) performed multi-round validation to ensure accuracy, consistency, and minimize ambiguity. Inter-annotator agreement (Krippendorff’s α) reached 0.84. The dataset is partitioned into a 1.5K test set (fully human-annotated) and a 6.9K training set (integrating samples from other datasets like SpatialViz, PhysBench, ViewSpatial, and DrivingVQA for diversity).

#### 3.3. Improving Visual Spatial Reasoning Abilities

Two strategies were explored to enhance VLM performance:

- **PointGraph:** This method constructs structured representations of object relationships. Open-vocabulary grounding models (e.g., Florence-2) localize objects, extract centers and bounding boxes. These detections are assembled into a JSON-style scene graph encoding object identities and relative positions. This structured spatial description is then concatenated with the original query to provide explicit geometric cues to VLMs.
- **SpatialCoT (Spatial Chain-of-Thought):** Inspired by human mental imagery, this approach augments visual inputs with 3D novel views. InstantMesh is used to synthesize six additional perspectives for each input image, which are then composed into a multi-view collage. This collage is fed into the VLM alongside the question as part of a chain-of-thought prompting pipeline, aiming to enrich spatial imagination and help disambiguate view-dependent reasoning.

#### 3.4. Evaluation Setup and Metrics

Models were evaluated on the OmniSpatial benchmark under a unified protocol:

- **Models Tested:** Four groups of state-of-the-art models were evaluated: Proprietary Models (GPT-4o, Claude, Gemini series), Reasoning Models (o1, o3, o4-mini, Gemini-2.5 "thinking" versions), Open-source Models (LLaVA, Gemma, InternVL, Qwen-VL), and Specialized Spatial Reasoning Models (SpaceMantis, SpatialBot, RoboPoint, SoFar).
- **Protocols:** Proprietary models were accessed via APIs in zero-shot settings with standardized system prompts. Open-source models were locally deployed.
- **Metrics:** Accuracy on multiple-choice questions. For standard models, four output protocols were tested: direct answer, regex parsing, JSON parsing, and LLM-as-a-Judge. For reasoning-oriented models with unstructured CoT outputs, GPT-4.1-mini was used as an automatic judge. Human performance was also benchmarked.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments on the OmniSpatial benchmark yielded several key findings regarding the spatial reasoning capabilities of Vision-Language Models (VLMs):

#### 4.1. Overall Model Performance

- **Significant Gap to Human Performance:** State-of-the-art proprietary reasoning models, such as ChatGPT o3 and Gemini-2.5-pro, achieved the highest overall performance, surpassing a 56% average success rate. However, this remains over 30 percentage points below human-level understanding (human average accuracy: 92.6%), indicating a substantial gap in comprehensive spatial reasoning.
- **Proprietary vs. Open-Source Models:** Proprietary models demonstrated stronger performance compared to open-source alternatives. Large-scale open-source models like InternVL3-78B and Qwen-VL2.5-72B showed competitive results, approaching the performance of smaller proprietary models such as GPT-4.1-mini and Gemini-2.0-flash-exp.
- **Limitations of Specialized Models:** Models explicitly designed for spatial reasoning (e.g., SpatialVLM, RoboPoint, SpatialBot) struggled to achieve substantial improvements on the comprehensive OmniSpatial benchmark. This suggests that their specialized training and architectural biases might limit generalization across the broad range of tasks presented.

#### 4.2. Category-wise Analysis

Performance varied notably across the four main categories of spatial reasoning:

- **Dynamic Reasoning and Spatial Interaction:** Proprietary models, leveraging extensive world knowledge and local understanding, demonstrated strong performance in these categories. This indicates proficiency in temporal understanding, spatial relationship analysis, and map-based comprehension.
- **Complex Spatial Logic (Pattern Geometric Reasoning):** Models exhibited significant limitations in tasks involving spatial imagination in planar geometry (e.g., pattern recognition, geometric reasoning). Even reasoning models designed for extended thinking achieved accuracies only around 30% to 40%, slightly above a random baseline. This highlights a persistent challenge in abstract spatial transformations.
- **Perspective-Taking:** Current models showed limited capabilities in perspective-taking. They predominantly analyzed scenarios from an egocentric viewpoint but struggled to infer or imagine perspectives from alternative or non-existent viewpoints (allocentric or hypothetical perspective-taking).

#### 4.3. Impact of PointGraph & Spatial CoT

- **PointGraph Effectiveness:** Integrating PointGraph, which provides explicit structured object representations (scene graphs with bounding boxes and locations), consistently boosted accuracy across models. This improvement was particularly evident in Dynamic Reasoning and Perspective-Taking tasks, validating the benefit of providing geometric cues. Traditional textual Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting did not yield comparable improvements.
- **Spatial CoT Effectiveness:** The proposed Spatial CoT, which augments visual inputs with 3D novel views generated by InstantMesh, also demonstrated performance improvements, especially within the Perspective-Taking track. This suggests that stimulating explicit spatial imagination through multi-view representations can enhance models' ability to reason about different viewpoints. When combined, SpatialCoT and PointGraph yielded additional synergistic gains.

#### 4.4. Training Exploration

- **Supervised Fine-Tuning on OmniSpatial-train:** Supervised fine-tuning of an open-source baseline model (Qwen-VL2.5-3B) on the 6.9K OmniSpatial-train dataset resulted in a substantial +7.82 average point gain over zero-shot performance. This improvement was consistent across dynamic, interaction, and perspective-taking oriented tracks, underscoring the value of diverse, manually curated spatial tasks. In contrast, training on a much larger (200K) template-style corpus yielded only a marginal +1.29 average gain, indicating that data quality and diversity are more impactful than sheer quantity of synthetically generated data.
- **Generalization to External Benchmarks:** Training with OmniSpatial also demonstrated positive generalization to the VSI-Bench, an external spatial reasoning benchmark. The overall score improved from 41.68 to 43.68. Notably, categories requiring ordering, counting, and metric/room-size reasoning experienced boosts, suggesting that OmniSpatial provides complementary supervision that transfers to external spatial tasks rather than overfitting to in-benchmark patterns.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The OmniSpatial benchmark and the accompanying research have several significant implications for the field of Vision-Language Models (VLMs) and broader AI development.

#### 5.1. Advancement in Spatial Reasoning Benchmarking

OmniSpatial addresses a critical void in current VLM evaluation by moving beyond basic spatial relations, which are largely saturated, to encompass a comprehensive range of complex spatial reasoning abilities. Its taxonomy, which distills spatial cognition into four primary categories—dynamic reasoning, complex logic, spatial interaction, and perspective-taking—and further subdivides into 50 fine-grained subtasks, provides a structured and principled framework for evaluation. The dataset's foundation in cognitive psychology and its meticulous manual curation of 8.4K question-answer pairs across diverse real-world scenarios contribute to a high-quality, non-templated benchmark that more faithfully assesses VLM capabilities. This will drive future research toward developing models with more sophisticated and human-like spatial intelligence.

#### 5.2. Identification of VLM Bottlenecks

The experiments clearly highlight the significant limitations of current state-of-the-art VLMs, both proprietary and open-source, in handling complex spatial tasks. The substantial performance gap compared to human accuracy (over 30 points) underscores that despite advancements, these models struggle particularly with geometric reasoning, non-egocentric perspective-taking, complex 3D scene understanding, and long-horizon temporal dynamics. This clear identification of bottlenecks is crucial for guiding future architectural and training innovations in VLMs, focusing research efforts on these challenging areas.

#### 5.3. Development of Enhancement Strategies

The introduction of PointGraph and SpatialCoT as plug-and-play strategies offers concrete directions for improving VLM spatial reasoning. PointGraph, by providing explicit scene graph cues, demonstrates the value of structured object representations in boosting accuracy for tasks involving distances, directions, and configurations. SpatialCoT, leveraging novel view synthesis, validates the effectiveness of stimulating spatial imagination for view-dependent reasoning and disambiguating occlusions. These methods serve as foundational steps toward developing more integrated, multi-modal reasoning capabilities within VLMs.

#### 5.4. Broader Societal and Industrial Impact

The advancements driven by OmniSpatial have potential positive impacts across various applications:
- **Autonomous Systems:** Improved reasoning about motion, collision risk, and traffic scenarios can lead to safer autonomous vehicles and more capable service robots that can foresee hazards and navigate complex, crowded environments responsibly.
- **Robotics and Manufacturing:** Enhanced geometric reasoning, including tasks like polyhedron unfolding and assembly, can streamline product design, packaging, and industrial manufacturing processes, potentially reducing material waste and energy consumption.
- **AR/VR and Human-Computer Interaction:** Better localization, UI interaction, and perspective-taking capabilities can foster more immersive and intuitive AR/VR experiences, facilitate accessibility tools for users with visual impairments, and enable more natural human-computer interfaces.

#### 5.5. Future Research Directions

The work also implicitly outlines future research avenues. The identified limitations suggest the need for deeper integration of 3D information, potentially leveraging 3D representations and perception techniques. Furthermore, the complexity of spatial reasoning tasks, akin to mathematical or coding challenges, points towards the development of more advanced reasoning models, potentially incorporating techniques like knowledge distillation and linking these cognitive abilities directly to robotic execution tasks in real-world environments.
