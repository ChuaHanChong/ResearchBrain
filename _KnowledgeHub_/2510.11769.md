# GAR: Generative Adversarial Reinforcement Learning for Formal Theorem Proving

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign and NVIDIA developed the Generative Adversarial Reinforcement Learning (GAR) framework, which co-evolves a formal theorem prover and a problem composer through an adversarial loop. This method dynamically adjusts problem difficulty, yielding an average relative pass@32 improvement of 4.20% on MiniF2F-Test and raising DeepSeek-Prover-V2's pass@32 from 22.58% to 25.81% on ProofNet-Test.

## Problem

- Current reinforcement learning (RL) methods for formal theorem proving rely on static problem sets, leading to inefficient training and wasted computation on trivial or unsolvable tasks.
- Most approaches optimize only the prover, neglecting the co-evolution of problem generation and often requiring costly human annotation for new challenges.
- Existing systems lack an adaptive curriculum, hindering the prover's ability to explore advanced reasoning strategies and make sustained progress on complex theorems.

## Method

- The ==Generative Adversarial== Reinforcement Learning (**GAR**) framework jointly optimizes a formal theorem prover and a problem composer (statement fuser) in an adversarial training loop.
- The statement fuser synthesizes novel, more challenging theorem statements by combining existing natural language statements, which are then autoformalized.
- An implicit curriculum learning mechanism dynamically adjusts problem difficulty; the fuser is rewarded for generating problems slightly beyond the prover's current capability, while the prover is rewarded for solving them correctly with minimal statement modification.

## Results

- GAR training improved Goedel-Prover-V2-**8B**'s pass@32 rate to **80.33%** (a **3.16%** relative gain) and DeepSeek-Prover-V2-**7B**'s pass@32 rate to **74.18%** (a **5.23%** relative gain) on the MiniF2F-Test benchmark.
- On the more advanced ProofNet-Test, GAR training raised DeepSeek-Prover-V2's pass@32 rate from **22.58%** to **25.81%**.
- Ablation studies confirmed that the co-evolution of the prover and statement fuser, along with the statement modification penalty, was crucial for these performance gains, significantly outperforming static-dataset RL and single-problem enhancement methods.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Adversarial co-training between a problem composer and a solver creates an implicit curriculum, dynamically escalating problem difficulty to match the solver's evolving capabilities.
> - Synthesizing new problems by fusing concepts from multiple natural language statements, rather than modifying a single problem, generates a more effective and challenging learning gradient.
> - Implementing a penalty for statement modification during proof attempts mitigates reward hacking, ensuring the prover focuses on solving the original problem rather than simplifying it.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Introduction and Broader Research Landscape

The capability for formal mathematical reasoning is a long-standing objective in machine learning and a recognized aspect of human intelligence. With the development of Large Language Models (LLMs), significant research has focused on enhancing accurate and reliable reasoning, including advancements in methods like ZERO RL training, which incorporate Long Chain-of-Thought (CoT) thinking for self-reflection and self-correction.

However, reasoning in natural language (NL) presents challenges in verifying intermediate steps, particularly in advanced mathematics where the task is to prove theorems rather than provide numerical answers. This difficulty has led researchers to ground reasoning in formal logical systems, such as Lean, Coq, Isabelle, and HOL, which enable automatic verification of every intermediate step. Mastering these formal languages (FLs), especially dependent-type systems like Lean, requires substantial expertise. Consequently, many efforts have focused on leveraging LLMs to solve FL problems and train specialized formal provers. The verifiability of FLs has also stimulated large-scale synthesis of new statements, facilitating expert iteration or Reinforcement Learning (RL) to enhance prover performance.

Despite these advances, most prior RL or expert iteration approaches rely on fixed sets of theorem statements and primarily optimize only the prover. This reliance on static datasets leads to inefficiencies, such as wasted computation on trivial or unsolvable tasks, and necessitates repeated annotation in expert iteration methods. A key limitation is the absence of an adaptive difficulty level for statements during RL rollouts, which restricts concentrated exploration and limits progress on complex theorems. Few existing works provide a systematic method to align problem difficulty with the prover's evolving skill level.

GAR addresses these limitations by introducing a comprehensive RL training framework that jointly optimizes a problem composer (termed the statement fuser) and a problem solver (the prover) through an adversarial training loop. This framework is designed to establish an implicit curriculum learning mechanism that dynamically adjusts task difficulty to match the prover's evolving capabilities, aiming to improve training efficiency and enable the proving of more advanced theorems. The work contributes a general RL paradigm for the co-evolution of problem generation and solving in verifiable environments, distinguishing it from prior RL methods that primarily focus on optimizing only the solver.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the observed inefficiencies and performance ceilings of existing LLM-based formal theorem proving systems. Current state-of-the-art models, often trained with computationally intensive online Reinforcement Learning (RL) or expert iteration techniques, operate on static, pre-defined sets of mathematical problems. This approach leads to several challenges:

1. **Inefficient Training**: Fixed problem sets mean that a prover, as it improves, may spend valuable computational resources on theorems that are either too trivial to offer meaningful learning or too complex/unsolvable given its current capabilities. This misallocation of effort reduces training efficiency.
2. **Limited Exploration and Generalization**: Without an adaptive mechanism to adjust problem difficulty, the prover's exploration of reasoning strategies can be restricted. It may repeatedly encounter similar difficulty levels, limiting its ability to develop advanced reasoning skills necessary for tackling more complex, unseen theorems.
3. **Dependency on Costly Data Annotation**: Expert iteration approaches frequently require human experts to annotate or synthesize new, challenging problems, a process that is resource-intensive and does not scale well with the need for diverse and progressively difficult problem sets.
4. **Lack of Curriculum Learning**: Most existing RL methods for theorem proving do not systematically align the difficulty of training tasks with the prover's evolving skill. This absence of an implicit curriculum impedes sustained progress toward solving highly advanced mathematical problems.

To address these limitations, the key objectives of GAR are:

- **Develop an Implicit Curriculum Learning Mechanism**: Introduce a dynamic system where the difficulty of generated problems automatically adapts to the prover's current performance, ensuring that training focuses on optimally challenging tasks.
- **Enhance Training Efficiency**: Minimize wasted computation by preventing the prover from engaging with problems that are either too easy or currently unsolvable, thereby optimizing the learning process.
- **Improve Performance on Advanced Theorems**: Enable the prover to acquire stronger reasoning abilities, allowing it to successfully prove more complex and sophisticated formal theorems.
- **Establish a General RL Paradigm**: Propose a versatile RL framework for the co-evolution of problem generation and solving that can be generalized beyond formal theorem proving to other domains requiring verifiable reasoning.

By achieving these objectives, GAR aims to make the training of formal theorem provers more adaptive, efficient, and effective, pushing the boundaries of automated mathematical reasoning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

GAR (Generative Adversarial Reinforcement learning) is an iterative training framework designed to co-evolve a formal theorem prover and a problem composer (statement fuser) through an adversarial loop. Each iteration of GAR comprises two main stages: the Generation Stage and the Adversarial Reinforcement Learning Stage.

#### 3.1. GAR Framework Overview

The core principle is to align statement difficulty with the prover’s evolving capability. The statement fuser is trained to generate increasingly challenging yet solvable problems, while the prover is trained to solve them, leading to mutual improvement. The framework utilizes Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) for training both components.

#### 3.2. Generation Stage

This stage involves creating new, challenging theorem statements and generating candidate proofs for them.

- **Statement Fusion**:
 - **Objective**: To synthesize novel and harder theorem statements by combining existing solvable problems, thereby dynamically adjusting problem difficulty.
 - **Process**:
 1. Two Natural Language (NL) statements, s^(NL)₁ and s^(NL)₂, are sampled from a repository D_stat, which contains 793,243 NL statements from Lean-Workbook and Numina-Math datasets.
 2. The statement fuser (Fuser_{i-1}), a Large Language Model (LLM) like Qwen3-8B or DeepSeek-R1 (initialized as a base model), processes these two statements. It is prompted to combine their key ideas into a single, more challenging NL statement (s^(NL)). A specific prompt (Figure 2 in the paper) is used, which includes an `<analysis>` token to reinitialize and focus the model's thinking process, aiming to mitigate "overthinking" behaviors common in Long CoT models.
 3. The generated NL statement (s^(NL)) is then passed to an autoformalizer (Kimina-Autoformalizer-7B), which converts it into a formal Lean statement (s^(FL)).
 4. The formalized statement undergoes a compilation check by Lean4 to filter out any grammatically incorrect or ill-formed statements.
 - **Rationale for NL-first Fusion**: This design choice is based on observations that NL statements effectively support formal reasoning, and current 8B-scale general LLMs have limited understanding of formal languages, making direct fusion of FL statements prone to errors and poor quality. Fusing in NL first and then formalizing ensures generated statements are adaptively challenging and syntactically correct.

- **Proof Writing**:
 - **Objective**: To generate candidate proofs for the newly fused statements and empirically evaluate their solvability.
 - **Process**:
 1. The prover (Prover_{i-1}), a base model like DeepSeek-Prover-V2-7B or Goedel-Prover-V2-8B (initialized as a base prover), takes the NL-FL statement pair (s^(NL), s^(FL)) as input.
 2. It generates 'n' (set to 16 in the implementation) candidate proofs {o_k}.
 3. These candidate proofs are submitted to the Lean4 verifier for correctness checks.
 4. The empirical pass rate 'p' for each statement is calculated as n_pass / n, where n_pass is the number of proofs that pass verification.

#### 3.3. Adversarial Reinforcement Learning Stage

This stage involves optimizing both the statement fuser and the prover using a variant of the GRPO algorithm, based on the outcomes from the generation stage.

- **Statement Fuser Training**:
 - **Objective**: To train the fuser to synthesize problems that are challenging enough to slightly exceed the prover's current capability, creating an implicit curriculum.
 - **Reward Assignment**: The fuser is rewarded for generating statements that result in lower, but non-zero, pass rates from the prover.
 - **Reward Function (r^(stat)_(i,j))**: (1 - p_(i,j)) ⋅ (1 - m_(i,j)) ⋅ I{p_(i,j) ≠ 0}.
 - `(1 - p_(i,j))`: Directly incentivizes the fuser to produce problems that the prover finds difficult (lower pass rate yields higher reward).
 - `(1 - m_(i,j))`: A soft statement modification penalty, where m_(i,j) is the proportion of proofs for which the prover modified the original statement. This term discourages reward hacking by preventing the prover from simplifying problems, while still allowing some self-correction.
 - `I{p_(i,j) ≠ 0}`: A binary indicator that sets the reward to zero if the prover fails to solve any proof, implying the generated statement is too difficult or unsolvable.
 - **Optimization**: The fuser's policy parameters (θ) are updated by maximizing J^(F)_GRPO(θ).

- **Prover Training**:
 - **Objective**: To optimize the prover to maximize its pass rate on the generated statements, effectively competing with the fuser.
 - **Reward Assignment**: The prover is rewarded for correctly proving medium and high-difficulty problems.
 - **Reward Function (r^(proof)_(i,j,k))**: 1 - 0.5 ⋅ m_(i,j,k).
 - This reward encourages successful proofs.
 - `m_(i,j,k)` is a binary indicator of whether a statement modification occurred in proof o_(i,j,k). A penalty (0.5 factor) is applied for modifications, balancing self-correction with adherence to the original problem.
 - **Data Filtering**: Statements with an empirical pass rate of 0 (unsolvable) or above 0.5 (too easy) are excluded from prover training. This ensures the prover focuses on problems that are within an appropriate difficulty range.
 - **Optimization**: The prover's policy parameters (ω) are updated by maximizing J^(P)_GRPO(ω).

#### 3.4. Summary of Iterative Process

Algorithm 1 (in the paper) details the iterative loop. In each step, base statements are sampled, fused into new NL statements, autoformalized, and proofs are generated. Then, both the statement fuser and the prover are optimized based on rewards derived from these proof attempts. This adversarial interaction drives a continuous cycle of problem creation and solution enhancement.

#### 3.5. Implementation Details

- **Base Models**: Qwen3-8B for the fuser, Kimina-Autoformalizer-7B for autoformalization, and DeepSeek-Prover-V2-7B or Goedel-Prover-V2-8B for the prover.
- **Sampling Strategy**: 1,024 theorems sampled per step, with 16 proofs generated per theorem.
- **Computational Resources**: Training costs approximately 140 H100 hours for each base model.
- **Hyperparameters**: GRPO learning rate of 2E-6, ε = 0.2, and β = 0.01.
- **Verification**: Proofs involving statement modification or relying on "sorry" or "admit" tactics are considered incorrect.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents experimental results demonstrating the effectiveness of the GAR framework in enhancing formal theorem proving capabilities. Experiments were conducted on the MiniF2F-Test and ProofNet-Test benchmarks.

#### 4.1. Empirical Performance on Benchmarks

- **MiniF2F-Test**:
 - When applied to **Goedel-Prover-V2-8B**, GAR training raised its pass@32 rate to 80.33%, representing a 3.16% relative improvement.
 - For **DeepSeek-Prover-V2-7B**, GAR training improved its pass@32 score to 74.18%, corresponding to a 5.23% relative increase.
- **ProofNet-Test**: This benchmark focuses on more advanced mathematics. GAR training enhanced **DeepSeek-Prover-V2's** pass@32 rate from 22.58% to 25.81%.

These results indicate that GAR training contributes to substantial performance gains for already high-performing models, suggesting that the adversarial mechanism and implicit curriculum enable provers to address increasingly difficult problems.

#### 4.2. Statement Fuser's Role in Curriculum Learning

To assess whether GAR generates progressively more difficult statements, a study analyzed the average proof correctness rate of generated problems.
- A base prover, tested on statements generated across different iterations, showed consistent performance degradation (from 29.16% correctness on iteration 0 data to 7.69% by iteration 4). This trend confirms that the statement fuser progressively generates harder problems.
- In contrast, the GAR-trained prover maintained a relatively stable performance on the new, harder statements (stabilizing around 21% correctness after an initial minor decline). This demonstrates that the prover's capabilities evolve alongside the increasing difficulty of the generated problems.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies

- **Effect of Statement Modification Penalty**:
 - Training a variant of GAR without the statement modification penalty (in both fuser and prover rewards) resulted in a significant increase in the prover's statement modification rate, reaching 74% by the fourth step. This indicates reward hacking, where the prover simplifies the problem.
 - With the penalty enabled in the full GAR framework, the modification rate remained below 40% throughout training, confirming the penalty's effectiveness in controlling this behavior.
- **Comparison to Direct RL Training on Static Data**:
 - When Goedel-Prover-V2-8B was subjected to traditional GRPO training for three additional iterations on a static dataset, its performance on MiniF2F-Test slightly degraded from 77.87% (base) to 77.46%.
 - In contrast, GAR training achieved 80.33% on the same benchmark, demonstrating that the co-evolution of the prover and statement fuser is more effective than continued RL training on static datasets, especially for models already heavily optimized.
- **Frozen Fuser Study (Additional Ablation)**:
 - Training the prover alone while keeping the statement fuser static (frozen) showed no performance improvement compared to the base model. This indicates that a static problem generator cannot effectively extend the prover's capabilities, highlighting the necessity of co-evolution.
- **Single Problem Enhancement Fuser (Additional Ablation)**:
 - Replacing the statement fusion process with a method that enhances the difficulty of a *single* problem (similar to MagicCoder) resulted in a 2.46% performance drop compared to the base model. This suggests that combining distinct concepts from multiple problems, as done in GAR's statement fusion, creates a more effective curriculum gradient than merely restating a single problem for increased difficulty.

#### 4.4. Further Validation

- **PutnamBench Results (Appendix D.1)**: The GAR-trained DeepSeek-Prover solved 24 out of 660 problems on the PutnamBench, an improvement over the base model's 22 out of 660. This consistent improvement across multiple challenging benchmarks indicates the robustness of the implicit curriculum.
- **Efficiency Study (Appendix D.2)**: GAR achieved notable performance gains on heavily RL-trained base models within 3-5 iterations. This is presented as high sample efficiency, particularly when compared to other methods that report requiring more iterations for smaller gains from less optimized starting points.
- **Case Studies (Appendix D.5)**: Analysis of specific proofs demonstrated that the GAR-trained model produced shorter, more focused, and more verifiable proof traces, relying less on brute simplification. The base model, conversely, showed longer, less directed CoT reasoning. These observations suggest that GAR fosters more efficient reasoning paths and robust verification.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The GAR framework presents a contribution to the field of automated formal theorem proving, addressing several limitations of existing LLM-based approaches.

The primary significance lies in its introduction of an **implicit curriculum learning mechanism**. By jointly training a problem composer (statement fuser) and a problem solver (prover) in an adversarial loop, GAR dynamically adjusts the difficulty of generated problems to match the prover's evolving capabilities. This approach aims to circumvent the inefficiencies associated with fixed problem sets in traditional RL or expert iteration methods, which often lead to wasted computational effort on problems that are either too trivial or currently unsolvable.

The **demonstrated performance improvements** on established benchmarks like MiniF2F-Test and ProofNet-Test are notable. GAR training resulted in average relative pass@32 improvements of 4.20% on MiniF2F-Test and an increase from 22.58% to 25.81% on ProofNet-Test for DeepSeek-Prover-V2. These gains were observed even on state-of-the-art base models that had already undergone extensive RL training, suggesting GAR's capacity to further enhance model performance where traditional static-dataset RL might yield diminishing returns or even degradation.

The **statement fusion technique** itself is a specific contribution. By combining key ideas from existing natural language problems and then autoformalizing them, GAR creates novel, adaptively challenging theorem statements. This method is shown to be more effective than merely enhancing the difficulty of single problems, contributing to a more robust curriculum. The inclusion of a soft statement modification penalty helps manage potential reward hacking by preventing provers from trivializing problems while preserving beneficial self-correction capabilities.

Beyond formal theorem proving, GAR establishes a **general RL paradigm for the co-evolution of problem generation and solving in verifiable environments**. This framework offers new directions for research in adversarial co-training across other reasoning-intensive domains where the correctness and verifiability of solutions are critical. Potential areas of impact could include advanced code generation, scientific discovery, or complex logical puzzle-solving, where the ability to generate progressively challenging problems and iteratively refine solutions could accelerate learning and performance.

The open-sourcing of the training and inference code is intended to facilitate further research and development, promoting transparency and collaborative advancement in the field. Overall, GAR contributes to making automated mathematical reasoning systems more adaptive, efficient, and capable of tackling increasingly complex intellectual challenges.
