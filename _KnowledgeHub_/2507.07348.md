# Zero-Shot Context Generalization in Reinforcement Learning from Few Training Contexts

> [!summary] Summary
> A new perturbative framework, featuring the Context-Enhanced Bellman Equation (CEBE) and Context Sample Enhancement (CSE), allows deep reinforcement learning agents to achieve zero-shot generalization to unseen contexts using limited training data. This approach theoretically provides first-order accurate Q-function approximations and empirically enhances policy performance in diverse control tasks, frequently outperforming or matching local domain randomization.

## Problem

- Deep reinforcement learning policies frequently exhibit poor zero-shot generalization to new environments whose context parameters differ from those seen during training, especially when training data is scarce.
- Current generalization methods, such as continual learning, meta-RL, and domain randomization, often involve high costs for online adaptation, require extensive labor for diverse environment creation, or have safety concerns.
- Architecture-based approaches often yield limited generalization benefits and necessitate problem-specific, complex designs that are challenging to scale.

## Method

- The research introduces a perturbative framework for ==Contextual Markov Decision== Processes (CMDPs) by defining the Context-Enhanced Bellman Equation (CEBE), which linearizes transition and reward functions around a base training context.
- It derives Context Sample Enhancement (CSE), an efficient data augmentation technique that generates synthetic training data for 'nearby' contexts by perturbing samples from a single base training context, leveraging first-order Taylor approximations.
- The methodology integrates structural knowledge about the general form of environment dynamics (e.g., physics equations with uncertain parameters) directly into the DRL training algorithm.

## Results

- Numerical experiments in a tabular Cliffwalking environment confirmed CEBE's first-order accuracy, showing an O(∣∣c−c0∣∣2)O(||c-c_0||^2)O(∣∣c−c0∣∣2) approximation error for the Q-function.
- CSE consistently improved zero-shot generalization capabilities across various continuous control DRL environments (SimpleDirection, Classic Control, and MuJoCo tasks) compared to a baseline trained on a single context.
- CSE performance often matched or surpassed Local Domain Randomization (LDR), an idealized benchmark requiring actual interaction with diverse contexts, demonstrating competitive or superior generalization in 3 out of 6 tested environments.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The Context-Enhanced Bellman Equation (CEBE) provides a first-order accurate approximation (O(∣∣c−c0∣∣2)O(||c-c_0||^2)O(∣∣c−c0∣∣2) error) of the true Q-function in a neighborhood of the training context, established through theoretical stability guarantees.
> - Q-functions in CMDPs exhibit stability under small perturbations to transition dynamics and rewards, which can be formally quantified using total variation norms and Wasserstein distances.
> - A policy optimized to be approximately optimal with respect to the CEBE will also be approximately optimal for the true Bellman equation, bridging the gap between theoretical value function approximation and practical policy performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Deep reinforcement learning (DRL) has demonstrated capabilities in domains such as competitive games and robotics. However, a persistent challenge in DRL is the generalization of trained policies to environments with different parameters or contexts than those encountered during training. This issue, often termed out-of-distribution generalization, becomes particularly salient in real-world applications where obtaining sufficient training data across a diverse range of contexts may be impractical or prohibitively expensive.

Current approaches to address generalization include:
1. **Continual Learning:** Policies adapt at deployment time in the testing environment. This can be problematic due to safety concerns or the high cost of online adaptation, preventing early or extensive agent deployment.
2. **Meta-RL and Domain Randomization:** These methods involve training with a multitude of diverse environments or variations of context parameters (e.g., friction coefficients). While effective, particularly in robotics for sim-to-real transfer, the creation and construction of such varied environments can be costly and labor-intensive.
3. **Architecture-based approaches:** Some works design neural network architectures to capture specific biases or structures in the underlying dynamics of the environment. While these can improve training performance, their generalization benefits have been noted to be limited, often requiring problem-specific, complex architectures that are challenging to scale.

This research addresses the problem of zero-shot generalization from *few* (ideally single) training contexts, specifically when the underlying dynamics of the environment exhibit *regularity* in context parameters. This contrasts with approaches that require extensive sampling from diverse contexts (like domain randomization) or on-the-fly adaptation. The paper aims to incorporate knowledge about the general form of environment dynamics (e.g., physics equations with uncertain parameters) directly into the training algorithm without requiring prior sampling from unseen contexts.

In relation to other areas, the work builds upon the concept of Contextual Markov Decision Processes (CMDPs), which extend MDPs by explicitly including context parameters. It differentiates itself from contextual multi-armed bandits by focusing on multi-step optimization and learning first-order approximations of dynamics rather than zeroth-order approaches for covering context space. While some related works use implicit linearity or gradient information, they often require access to multiple contexts during training, a constraint this paper seeks to relax. The approach also differs from exploration-focused methods by directly accounting for context variations during training, and from representation learning methods by incorporating structural knowledge directly into the Bellman equation rather than solely learning latent context representations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an approach that enhances zero-shot generalization capabilities of DRL agents to previously unobserved contexts, especially when training data is limited to a few, or even a single, context. This is motivated by the recognized challenge that DRL policies frequently exhibit poor generalization when evaluated in environments whose parameters differ from the training setup.

Specific motivations driving this work include:
- **Addressing Data Scarcity and Cost:** In numerous real-world applications, generating sufficient training data across a diverse spectrum of contexts is either infeasible, excessively time-consuming, or prohibitively expensive. This limitation prevents the broad application of DRL policies that rely on extensive domain randomization or meta-learning paradigms.
- **Leveraging Intrinsic Environment Regularity:** Many practical domains, particularly those involving physical systems (e.g., robotics, control problems), possess known underlying dynamics governed by differential equations with parameters (e.g., friction, mass, gravity) that can be modeled as context variables. These dynamics often exhibit smoothness or regularity with respect to these context parameters. The research aims to exploit this inherent structure to improve generalization.
- **Enabling Zero-Shot Transfer:** The goal is to allow policies to perform effectively in novel contexts without any additional training or adaptation upon deployment (zero-shot transfer). This is particularly important for safety-critical applications or scenarios where online adaptation is undesirable or costly.
- **Developing Generalizable Training Algorithms:** Rather than relying on specialized architectures for each problem, the motivation is to introduce a more general algorithmic modification that can be integrated into existing DRL frameworks to implicitly account for context variations.

To achieve these objectives, the paper outlines specific contributions:
1. **Theoretical Framework for Context Perturbations:** To formally analyze how context variations affect value functions, the research introduces the **context-enhanced Bellman equation (CEBE)**. This equation linearizes the transition and reward functions of a Contextual Markov Decision Process (CMDP) around a specific training context. A key objective is to theoretically demonstrate that CEBE provides a first-order accurate approximation to the true Q-function in nearby contexts.
2. **Efficient Data Augmentation:** Based on the CEBE, the paper aims to derive **context sample enhancement (CSE)**. This method is designed to efficiently generate synthetic training data from "nearby" contexts by augmenting samples from a single base training context, thereby effectively expanding the perceived training context distribution without actual environment interaction in new contexts. This is particularly focused on deterministic control environments for efficiency.
3. **Empirical Validation:** The final objective is to numerically validate the performance of both CEBE (in a tabular setting) and CSE (in continuous control DRL environments), showcasing their potential to improve zero-shot generalization in scenarios with limited training contexts.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

This research introduces a perturbative framework for Contextual Markov Decision Processes (CMDPs) to facilitate zero-shot generalization from limited training contexts. The methodology revolves around a novel Bellman equation approximation and a corresponding data augmentation technique.

#### 3.1. Contextual Markov Decision Processes (CMDPs)

The work formally defines a CMDP as $M = (C, S, A, M', \gamma)$, where $C$ is the context space, $S$ the state space, $A$ the action space, and $\gamma$ the discount factor. $M'$ maps a context $c \in C$ to a specific MDP $M'(c) = (S, A, T_c, R_c, \gamma)$, characterized by context-dependent transition ($T_c$) and reward ($R_c$) functions. Policies $\pi: C \times S \rightarrow \Delta(A)$ are considered. The objective is to find a policy that is $\epsilon$-optimal across a set of test contexts $U \subseteq C$, particularly in an out-of-distribution setting where training and test context distributions have disjoint supports. The focus is on generalizing from a single base training context $c_0$.

#### 3.2. Structural Assumptions

For generalization from a single context to be tractable, the CMDP is assumed to possess specific structures:
1. **Metric Spaces:** State ($S$) and action ($A$) spaces are metric spaces, and the context space ($C$) is an open convex subset of a Banach space.
2. **Deterministic Rewards:** The reward function $R_c: S \times A \times S \rightarrow R$ is deterministic.
3. **Smoothness:** Partial derivatives $\partial_c R_c$ and $\partial_c T_c$ exist and are Lipschitz continuous.
4. **Observable Context:** The context $c \in C$ is fully observable and constant throughout an episode.
5. **Observable Derivatives:** When a transition $(s, a) \rightarrow s'$ occurs, the agent observes $\partial_c R_c(s, a, s')$ and $\partial_c T_c(s'|s, a)$.
These assumptions are consistent with many physical environments where underlying dynamics are described by differential equations with context-dependent physical parameters.

#### 3.3. Context-Enhanced Bellman Equation (CEBE)

The core of the theoretical approach is the **Context-Enhanced Bellman Equation (CEBE)**. The standard Bellman equation for a CMDP is $Q_{BE}(s, a, c) = \mathbb{E}_{s' \sim T_c(s,a)} [R_c + \gamma \mathbb{E}_{a' \sim \pi(s',c)} Q_{BE}(s', a', c)]$. The CEBE (Equation 6) is defined as the Bellman equation of an *approximate* CMDP, using context-enhanced transition ($T_{CE}$) and reward ($R_{CE}$) functions that are first-order Taylor approximations around the base training context $c_0$. At $c=c_0$, CEBE coincides with the original Bellman equation.

Two specific cases for $T_{CE}$ and $R_{CE}$ are considered:
1. **Deterministic Transitions:** For $T_c(s,a) = \delta_{f_c(s,a)}$, where $f_c$ is a deterministic next-state function:
 - $T_{CE}(s,a) = \delta_{f_{c_0}(s,a) + \partial_c f_{c_0}(s,a)(c-c_0)}$ (Equation 7)
 - $R_{CE} = R_{c_0} + \partial_c R_{c_0} \cdot (c-c_0) + \partial_{s'} R_{c_0} \partial_c T_{c_0} \cdot (c-c_0)$ (Equation 8)
 This requires $R_c$ to be differentiable in the next state $s'$.
2. **Transitions with Differentiable Measure:** If $c \mapsto T_c(s,a)$ is a differentiable map:
 - $T_{CE}(s,a) = \mathcal{P}(T_{c_0}(s,a) + \partial_c T_{c_0}(s,a) \cdot (c-c_0))$ (Equation 9)
 - $R_{CE} = R_{c_0} + \partial_c R_{c_0} \cdot (c-c_0)$ (Equation 10)
 Here, $\mathcal{P}(\mu)$ projects the signed measure $\mu$ onto the probability simplex, necessary because a linear approximation can yield negative values.

#### 3.4. CEBE Approximation of the Bellman Equation (Theoretical Analysis)

The paper provides theoretical guarantees for the CEBE:
- **Theorem 1 (($T, R$)-stability of the Q-function):** This general theorem establishes that Q-functions are stable under small perturbations to transition dynamics and rewards. It quantifies the bound on the difference between two Q-functions based on the differences in their respective rewards (total variation norm) and transitions (Wasserstein distance), assuming Lipschitz policies and functions.
- **Theorem 2 (Deterministic CEBE is first-order accurate):** Applying Theorem 1 to deterministic transitions, this theorem proves that $Q_{CE}$ approximates $Q_{BE}$ with an error of $O(||c-c_0||^2)$, meaning CEBE provides a first-order accurate approximation, provided smoothness conditions on $f$ and $R$ and a suitable discount factor.
- **Theorem 3 (Stochastic CEBE is first-order accurate):** An analogous theorem for stochastic transitions with differentiable measures. It demonstrates $O(||c-c_0||^2)$ approximation error under similar smoothness assumptions, a bounded state space, and a condition on the magnitude of the context perturbation.
- **Theorem 4 (CEBE policy is approximately optimal):** This theorem shows that a policy optimized to be $\epsilon$-optimal with respect to the CEBE will also be $(2\delta + 2\epsilon)$-optimal with respect to the true Bellman equation, where $\delta$ is the uniform bound on the difference between $Q_{CE}$ and $Q_{BE}$. This links the theoretical approximation of value functions to the practical utility of policies.

#### 3.5. Context Sample Enhancement (CSE)

**Context Sample Enhancement (CSE)** is derived as a practical data augmentation method for estimating the CEBE from samples, specifically for the deterministic-transitions case. Given a sample $(s, a, r, s')$ from the CMDP in context $c_0$ and a context perturbation $\Delta c = c - c_0$, CSE generates a context-enhanced sample $(\bar{r}, \bar{s}')$:
- $\bar{r} = r + \partial_c R_{c_0}(s, a, s')\Delta c + \partial_{s'} R_{c_0}(s, a, s')\partial_c T_{c_0}\Delta c$
- $\bar{s}' = s' + \partial_c T_{c_0}\Delta c$ (Equation 11)
These $(\bar{r}, \bar{s}')$ are samples from the *approximate* CMDP in context $c$. CSE thus allows for sampling from perturbed contexts by augmenting data from a single base context, integrating structural information without requiring actual interaction in new contexts. Algorithm 1 details the off-policy RL algorithm with CSE, which involves collecting trajectories, sampling batches, generating perturbations, and then training on the updated, context-enhanced batches. The appendix also provides a regularization perspective, suggesting CSE implicitly adds $L_2$ regularization on the difference between the Jacobian of the trained model and the true function.

#### 3.6. Experimental Setup

The methodology is evaluated using several DRL environments:
- **DRL Algorithms:** Soft Actor Critic (SAC) for continuous control problems and Deep Q-Networks (DQN) for discrete action spaces (CartGoal).
- **Network Inputs:** State and context are appended (s, c) as input to the neural networks.
- **Baselines:**
 - **Vanilla SAC/DQN (Baseline):** Training only on the base context $c_0$.
 - **Local Domain Randomization (LDR):** Considered an idealized benchmark, LDR samples contexts as perturbations of the original training context and generates exact trajectories from these perturbed contexts.
- **Evaluation:** Policies are trained multiple times (10 policies), and performance is assessed by averaging returns over 64 trajectories, reporting the mean and 95% confidence interval.
- **Environments:**
 - **Tabular Cliffwalking:** A grid-world environment used for exact CEBE vs. BE comparisons. Context is slip probability $c$.
 - **SimpleDirection:** A 2D continuous control environment with linear transitions and rewards, designed for controlled testing.
 - **Classic Control:** Goal-based extensions of Gymnasium environments: PendulumGoal (context includes gravity, mass, length, goal torque), CartGoal (context includes cart mass, pole mass, pole length, gravity, goal state).
 - **MuJoCo Environments:** Goal-based variants from Lee and Chung (2021): CheetahVelocity (goal velocity context), AntDirection (goal direction context), AntGoal (goal position context).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper presents a series of numerical experiments to validate the theoretical claims and practical utility of CEBE and CSE across various simulation environments.

#### 4.1. Tabular CEBE Approximation

- **Finding:** In a tabular Cliffwalking environment, where exact solutions to the Bellman equation (BE) and CEBE can be computed via dynamic programming, the approximation error $||Q_c^{CE} - Q_c^{BE}||_\infty$ was numerically demonstrated to be $O(||c-c_0||^2)$.
- **Details:** Log-log plots of the error versus context perturbation size showed a best-fit line with a slope approximately equal to 2 for two different non-linear reward functions.
- **Significance:** This empirical result directly corroborates Theorem 3, confirming that CEBE provides a first-order accurate approximation of the true Q-function in a neighborhood of the training context.

#### 4.2. Simple Control Environments (SimpleDirection)

- **Finding:** On the SimpleDirection environment, which features linear transitions and rewards, policies trained with CSE performed similarly to Local Domain Randomization (LDR) and considerably better than the baseline (vanilla SAC).
- **Details:** When varying a context parameter, CSE maintained high returns, effectively matching the performance of LDR. The baseline agent's performance degraded notably as the context diverged from the training context.
- **Significance:** This demonstrates CSE's ability to significantly improve generalization in a controlled, well-behaved environment, suggesting its effectiveness in leveraging explicit gradient information.

#### 4.3. Classic Control Environments (PendulumGoal, CartGoal)

- **Finding:**
 - **PendulumGoal:** Both CSE and LDR consistently outperformed the baseline when evaluating across variations in gravitational acceleration and goal torque. In some contexts (e.g., goal torque greater than 0.6), CSE exhibited superior performance compared to LDR. When varying mass and length, all methods performed similarly, though CSE still generally outperformed LDR when baseline performance declined for higher masses.
 - **CartGoal:** Performance variations were observed. All methods performed similarly for changes in cart mass and pole mass. However, the baseline unexpectedly performed better than both CSE and LDR when varying gravitational acceleration, possibly due to the specific task and the small perturbation radius used during training relative to the evaluation range. LDR performed best for pole length variations, with CSE slightly outperforming the baseline. For goal state variations, CSE performed best when the goal was further from the origin.
- **Significance:** These results indicate that CSE can improve generalization in more complex classic control tasks, often matching or exceeding the performance of LDR, even when the context space includes task-defining parameters. Nuances in performance across different context parameters highlight the environment-specific challenges in generalization.

#### 4.4. MuJoCo Environments (CheetahVelocity, AntDirection, AntGoal)

- **Finding:**
 - **CheetahVelocity:** CSE performance was similar to LDR across most goal velocities, and both significantly outperformed the baseline, particularly for goal velocities between 0 and 2. For higher goal velocities (e.g., >2.6), the baseline sometimes achieved higher returns, but CSE still generally surpassed LDR.
 - **AntDirection:** CSE exhibited performance comparable to LDR in most contexts, although it showed slightly lower returns in a specific region of the goal direction parameter ([3.5, 5]). Both methods consistently outperformed the baseline.
 - **AntGoal:** CSE performed at least as well as LDR across all goal angles, and both methods consistently beat the baseline.
- **Significance:** These findings demonstrate the applicability and effectiveness of CSE in more complex, high-dimensional MuJoCo robotic control environments with context-dependent tasks. CSE consistently improves upon baseline performance and often matches or surpasses the idealized LDR benchmark, suggesting its practical relevance for real-world robotic applications where obtaining diverse training contexts is difficult.

#### 4.5. Additional Results (from Appendix)

- **PendulumGoal with Automatic Differentiation:** When analytical gradients were replaced with automatically differentiated ones, CSE performed similarly to LDR, sometimes outperforming it (e.g., for higher gravitational acceleration), indicating its robustness to the method of gradient computation.
- **Stochastic Transitions (SimpleDirection-Stochastic):** In an environment with additive Gaussian noise, CSE remained robust, performing similarly to LDR and outperforming the baseline, suggesting its potential applicability beyond strictly deterministic transitions.
- **Aggregate Statistics:** Across all six environments, CSE consistently outperformed the baseline. It performed better than LDR in three of the six environments (CheetahVelocity, SimpleDirection, and PendulumGoal), indicating competitive or superior generalization capabilities.

Overall, the empirical results suggest that CSE serves as a method for improving zero-shot generalization in DRL for smooth CMDPs, often achieving performance comparable to or better than local domain randomization, with the advantage of not requiring actual sampling from diverse contexts.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research makes several significant contributions to the field of deep reinforcement learning, primarily by addressing the critical challenge of zero-shot generalization from limited training contexts.

**Primary Significance:**
The paper introduces a novel perturbative framework for Contextual Markov Decision Processes (CMDPs) through the **Context-Enhanced Bellman Equation (CEBE)** and the **Context Sample Enhancement (CSE)** procedure. This framework provides a principled way to incorporate knowledge of underlying environment dynamics into the training algorithm, enabling DRL policies to generalize to unseen contexts without requiring extensive data collection from diverse environments. This is particularly relevant for real-world applications where generating sufficient training data for various context parameters is often infeasible or expensive.

**Theoretical Contributions:**
- The introduction of the CEBE offers a theoretical foundation for understanding how small perturbations in context parameters affect value functions in CMDPs.
- The rigorous analytical proofs (Theorems 1, 2, 3) demonstrating the first-order accuracy of CEBE's Q-function approximation (i.e., $O(||c-c_0||^2)$ error) provide strong theoretical backing.
- Theorem 4 further establishes that a policy optimized using CEBE is approximately optimal for the true Bellman equation, bridging the gap between value function approximation and practical policy performance. These theoretical results enhance the understanding of generalization in CMDPs under regularity assumptions.

**Practical Contributions and Potential Impact:**
- **Efficient Data Augmentation:** CSE is presented as an efficient, easy-to-implement data augmentation method. By generating synthetic samples for nearby contexts from a single base training context, it effectively broadens the training distribution. This can substantially reduce the computational and resource costs associated with collecting data from a multitude of varied environments, which is a major bottleneck for methods like domain randomization.
- **Improved Zero-Shot Generalization:** The empirical validation across diverse environments (tabular, simple control, classic control, and MuJoCo robotic tasks) demonstrates that CSE consistently improves generalization performance over baseline methods and often matches or exceeds the performance of local domain randomization. This suggests that policies trained with CSE are more robust to parameter shifts at test time.
- **Compatibility and Scalability:** CSE is designed to be compatible with existing model-free DRL algorithms (e.g., SAC, DQN) and can potentially be integrated with other generalization techniques. Its ability to leverage gradient information for nearby contexts may offer advantages in high-dimensional context spaces where domain randomization could suffer from the curse of dimensionality.
- **Future Avenues:** The work opens several promising directions for future research:
 - **Model-based RL:** CSE could potentially enhance the training of more generalizable world models in model-based settings.
 - **Offline RL:** Its data augmentation capabilities could be highly valuable for offline RL, where new samples cannot be collected.
 - **Prioritized Replay and Exploration:** Gradient information could be leveraged to design more principled prioritized replay buffers or sophisticated exploration strategies that focus on context-sensitive states.
 - **Robustness and Adversarial Contexts:** The framework provides tools to identify and potentially mitigate the effects of context parameters that are most sensitive to perturbations.

**Limitations and Future Considerations:**
The paper acknowledges several limitations:
- **Deterministic Transitions Focus:** While CSE is derived for deterministic transitions, and some empirical robustness to stochasticity is shown, a full theoretical treatment for stochastic transitions requiring differentiable transport maps between distributions is a complex future challenge.
- **Fully Observable Environments:** The current analysis and experiments assume fully observable state and context spaces. Real-world scenarios often involve noisy gradients or partial observability, necessitating future work to examine CSE's sensitivity and adaptability under these conditions.

**Broader Societal Impact:**
The authors note that the research focuses on foundational aspects of generalization in DRL, aiming to improve policy robustness to context perturbations. While no direct negative societal impacts are immediately foreseen from the research itself, the authors acknowledge that they do not control how these methods might be applied in practice. The increased robustness and generalization capabilities could contribute to the development of more reliable and adaptable AI systems, particularly in domains like robotics and autonomous control, potentially enhancing safety and efficiency in various industries.
