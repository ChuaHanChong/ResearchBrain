# How to Spend Your Robot Time: Bridging Kickstarting and Offline Reinforcement Learning for Vision-based Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> DeepMind researchers investigated strategies for data-efficient robotic manipulation, proposing algorithms that combine offline data from a suboptimal teacher with online student interaction and teacher action supervision. Their methods, particularly R-MPO and R-CRR, demonstrated high success rates, such as 81.5% on real-world stacking tasks, significantly reducing the need for extensive online robot time.

## Problem

- Reinforcement Learning for real-world robotics requires extensive and costly online interaction, especially for vision-based, long-horizon tasks with sparse rewards.
- Effectively utilizing suboptimal teacher policies and pre-existing data to accelerate learning for a new target task is challenging.
- Optimally allocating a fixed data budget between teacher-collected offline data and student-collected online data remains an open question.

## Method

- Extended existing off-policy RL algorithms (MPO and CRR) into R-MPO and R-CRR, which explicitly integrate teacher action distributions as a supervisory signal.
- The proposed algorithms train on a combined dataset of both offline data collected by the teacher and online data collected by the student policy.
- Conducted a systematic empirical evaluation of these hybrid algorithms against various baselines across different data budgets, teacher qualities, and five distinct vision-based robotic manipulation tasks.

## Results

- R-CRR generally achieved the highest performance in simulation, particularly at lower data budgets and when the teacher policy was less optimal.
- On real robots, R-MPO significantly outperformed the teacher and pure offline CRR on Triplet 1, reaching an **81.5%** success rate at 35,000 total episodes.
- Across real-world tasks, all three evaluated methods (CRR, R-MPO, R-CRR) consistently improved upon the Generalization teacher's performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A combined learning strategy, utilizing both offline teacher-collected data, online student interaction, and teacher action supervision, is more sample-efficient than relying on a single data source.
> - Purely offline RL methods, like CRR, can establish a strong baseline, often outperforming a suboptimal teacher even without online interaction if sufficient data is provided.
> - For methods using mixed data, an approximately 1:1 ratio of offline teacher-collected episodes to online student-collected episodes often leads to optimal performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The application of Reinforcement Learning (RL) to real-world robotics presents significant challenges, primarily due to the extensive and often expensive online interaction required for data collection. This is particularly evident in scenarios involving high-dimensional sensor inputs, such as images, and for long-horizon tasks with sparse rewards. To address this data inefficiency, researchers have explored various paradigms for leveraging existing knowledge or data.

This work situates itself within the "policy finetuning" setting, a framework where a learning agent (student) has access to both the environment for interaction and a suboptimal "teacher" policy that can provide guidance. This setting encompasses several established research areas:
- **Online RL:** Where the student learns solely from its own interactions. While capable of discovering novel solutions, it is data-intensive.
- **Offline RL:** Focuses on learning a policy from a fixed dataset of previously collected transitions without further environmental interaction. A primary challenge in this area is preventing policies from exploiting extrapolation errors in the Q-function, which can arise when optimizing for actions not well-represented in the dataset. Algorithms often employ pessimism biases or policy constraints to mitigate this.
- **Offline-to-Online RL:** Aims to accelerate online learning by first pre-training a policy on an offline dataset and then fine-tuning it with online interactions. This typically assumes a large pre-existing offline dataset.
- **Kickstarting / DAgger-style Learning:** Involves speeding up online RL by incorporating supervisory signals from a teacher policy, often in the form of action labels for states visited by the student.

The current research specifically investigates a policy finetuning scenario that integrates aspects from both offline RL and kickstarting. It explores how to optimally combine feedback from a teacher policy, data collected by that teacher, and data collected by the student within a limited interaction budget. This approach seeks to bridge the gap between purely offline methods (which might struggle with distribution shifts or insufficient data) and purely online methods (which are sample-inefficient), by proposing and evaluating algorithms that systematically leverage both sources of information. The work builds upon existing off-policy actor-critic methods like Maximum a Posteriori Optimization (MPO) and offline RL algorithms such as Critic-Regularized Regression (CRR) and Advantage-Weighted Actor-Critic (AWAC). It aims to provide empirical insights into the trade-offs and optimal strategies within this combined learning paradigm, particularly for complex, vision-based robotic manipulation tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and evaluate strategies for efficient policy learning in real-world robotic manipulation tasks by minimizing the requirement for extensive online interaction. This is motivated by the high cost and time investment associated with collecting large amounts of real robot data, particularly for challenging vision-based, long-horizon tasks with sparse rewards.

More specifically, the paper identifies and aims to address the following key questions within the policy finetuning setting:
- **Leveraging Suboptimal Teachers:** How can existing, suboptimal teacher policies – which might be derived from training on prior related tasks or in simulation – be most effectively utilized to accelerate the learning process of a student policy for a target task?
- **Optimal Data Allocation:** Given a fixed total data budget for interacting with the target environment, how should this budget be allocated between data collected by the teacher (for an offline dataset) and data collected by the student policy (through online interaction)? This includes determining the optimal proportion of offline versus online episodes.
- **Teacher Supervision Integration:** How should the teacher policy be used beyond just data collection? Specifically, how can its action distributions be incorporated as a supervisory signal during the student's training process to guide policy improvement?
- **Comparative Performance:** How do novel combinations of offline data utilization and online teacher supervision compare against established offline RL, online RL, offline-to-online RL, and kickstarting algorithms across varying data budgets, different teacher qualities (degrees of suboptimality), and diverse task requirements?

By systematically investigating these questions, the authors aim to provide empirical guidance and algorithmic solutions that enhance the data efficiency of robot learning, thereby making complex robotic tasks more amenable to RL in practical, resource-constrained environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research adopts a systematic empirical approach to investigate data-efficient policy learning in the policy finetuning setting. The core methodology involves extending existing online and offline Reinforcement Learning algorithms to explicitly integrate both teacher-collected data and teacher action supervision.

**A. Algorithmic Foundation and Extensions:**
The authors observe that several existing RL algorithms, including MPO (an online off-policy method) and CRR (an offline method), can be unified under a general objective function:
$ \hat{J}(\pi_\theta; \pi_{prior}) = E_{s \sim D, a \sim \pi_{prior}} [\exp (\frac{1}{\eta} Q_{\phi_k}(s, a) - Z) \log \pi_\theta(a|s)] $
This objective minimizes the KL divergence between the parametric student policy ($\pi_\theta$) and an improved policy derived from a prior policy ($\pi_{prior}$) and a learned Q-function ($Q_{\phi_k}$). The variations arise from the choice of the data distribution ($D$) and the prior policy ($\pi_{prior}$).

Building on this unified view, two new algorithms are proposed:
- **R-MPO (MPO with Relabeled Actions):** This variant adapts MPO by modifying its prior policy ($\pi_{prior}$) to be a mixture of the current student policy ($\pi_k$) and the teacher policy ($\pi_{teacher}$). Specifically, $\pi_{prior}(a|s) = (1 - \beta)\pi_k(a|s) + \beta\pi_{teacher}(a|s)$, where $\beta$ controls the weighting of the teacher's influence. For data ($D$), R-MPO uses a combination of offline data collected by the teacher ($D_{dataset}$) and online data collected by the student (replay buffer $B$).
- **R-CRR (CRR with Relabeled Actions):** This variant adapts CRR by modifying its prior policy ($\pi_{prior}$) to be a mixture of the behavior policy ($\pi_b$) that collected the data and the teacher policy ($\pi_{teacher}$). Specifically, $\pi_{prior}(a|s) = (1 - \beta)\pi_b(a|s) + \beta\pi_{teacher}(a|s)$. Similar to R-MPO, R-CRR also trains on combined data ($D = D_{dataset} \cup B$).

**B. Experimental Setup:**
- **Environment and Tasks:** The RGB-Stacking benchmark is used, which involves a robotic arm stacking specified objects from vision-based observations. The benchmark features 5 distinct object triplets (e.g., Triplet 1 requires precise grasping, Triplet 2 involves tool use, Triplet 3 requires balancing), each posing unique challenges for manipulation. Experiments are conducted on these 5 tasks individually.
- **Teacher Policies:** Two vision-based teacher policies with varying levels of suboptimality are used:
 - "Skill Mastery Teacher": Trained to master all 5 test triplets jointly, exhibiting high performance.
 - "Skill Generalization Teacher": Trained on a separate set of objects, leading to lower performance on the 5 test triplets.
 Both teachers are trained in simulation with domain randomization to ensure transferability to real robots.
- **Baseline Methods:** A comprehensive set of baselines are compared:
 - **Offline Methods:** Behavioral Cloning (BC), CRR (purely offline), CRR-mixed (CRR with mixed offline/online data), AWAC (offline-to-online RL). These methods primarily use $D_{dataset}$.
 - **Kickstarting Methods:** DAgger (online supervision), DAgger-mixed (DAgger with mixed data). These use $\pi_{teacher}$ for supervision.
 - **Online RL:** MPO (trained with shaped rewards as it does not use teacher supervision directly).
- **Data Budgets and Offline Data Proportion:** Experiments are conducted across various total data budgets (total number of episodes collected from the environment) in both simulation (5,000, 10,000, 15,000 episodes) and on real robots (15,000 to 40,000 episodes). For methods using mixed data, the proportion of offline episodes (collected by the teacher) within the total budget is systematically varied to identify optimal allocations.
- **Evaluation:** Performance is measured by average success rate. In simulation, results are averaged over 2 random seeds, with each policy evaluated over 1000 episodes. On real robots, 1-2 training runs are performed, with evaluations averaged over 200 episodes.
- **Hyperparameter Analysis:** The effect of the teacher mixing weight ($\beta$) in R-MPO and R-CRR, as well as the batch-size ratio for sampling from offline and online data, is investigated.

The methodology emphasizes a thorough empirical comparison of algorithmic choices and data allocation strategies across multiple tasks, teacher qualities, and deployment environments (simulation and real-world).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental study yielded several findings regarding the effective use of suboptimal teachers for data-efficient robotic manipulation.

**A. Simulation Results:**
1. **Overall Performance:** R-CRR generally achieved the highest performance, particularly at lower data budgets and when the teacher policy was less optimal (Generalization teacher). This suggests that combining teacher-collected data with online student data and teacher action supervision is effective for sample-efficient learning.
2. **Offline RL as a Strong Baseline:** Offline CRR, trained solely on teacher-collected data without online interaction, consistently outperformed the teacher in most cases, especially at higher data budgets (e.g., 15,000 episodes). This indicates that even without online interaction, a well-designed offline RL algorithm can significantly improve upon a suboptimal teacher policy if sufficient data is available.
3. **Benefit of Mixed Data Sources:** Methods that utilize both offline teacher-collected data and online student-collected data (R-CRR, R-MPO, DAgger-mixed, CRR-mixed) generally achieved better performance compared to those relying solely on one data source. This highlights the synergistic effect of diverse data streams.
4. **Optimal Offline Data Proportion:** For methods using mixed data, the most effective strategy often involved allocating approximately half of the total data budget to teacher-collected offline episodes and the other half to online student-collected episodes. This suggests a balanced approach to data acquisition is beneficial.
5. **R-MPO Performance:** R-MPO generally performed slightly worse than R-CRR and in some instances, even pure offline CRR in simulation. The study noted that online RL (MPO) struggled to solve long-horizon, sparse-reward tasks within limited budgets.
6. **Teacher Mixture Weight ($\beta$):** The hyperparameter $\beta$, controlling the teacher's influence in the prior policy mixture, exhibited different optimal values for R-MPO and R-CRR. For R-MPO, a low $\beta$ (e.g., 0.1) was found to be optimal, as higher weights could overly constrain the student policy. For R-CRR, higher $\beta$ values (e.g., 0.75) were beneficial.
7. **Batch-Size Ratio:** For R-CRR, the ratio of offline to online data within training mini-batches did not significantly impact final performance, provided that both data sources were represented in the batch. Training exclusively online (no offline data in the batch) resulted in lower asymptotic performance, which was attributed to potential overfitting to a small replay buffer early in training.

**B. Real-World Robot Results:**
1. **R-MPO Effectiveness:** On Triplet 1 with the Generalization teacher, R-MPO significantly outperformed the teacher and pure offline CRR. R-MPO demonstrated robust learning curves and reached high success rates (e.g., 81.5% at 35,000 total episodes), plateauing around 35,000 episodes regardless of the initial offline data proportion (within 15,000-25,000 offline episodes).
2. **CRR Limitations on Real Robots:** While CRR improved upon the teacher on real robots, its performance was generally lower than R-MPO. The authors hypothesize this might be due to the offline datasets containing episodes collected from a deterministic teacher policy, thus lacking sufficient action diversity for effective offline learning.
3. **Task-Specific Performance:** Across the five object triplets, no single method consistently outperformed the others. For example, R-MPO excelled on Triplet 1, while R-CRR achieved the highest success rate on Triplet 5 (94.0%) and performed comparably to R-MPO on Triplet 3. CRR also performed well on some triplets (e.g., Triplet 4, 94.5%). All three evaluated methods (CRR, R-MPO, R-CRR) consistently outperformed the Generalization teacher on real robots.

Overall, the findings indicate that a judicious combination of teacher-collected data and teacher action supervision, integrated into online-capable RL algorithms, is a robust strategy for achieving high performance with limited online interaction in complex robotic tasks. Purely offline methods serve as a strong baseline, particularly with adequate data, but active online learning with teacher guidance often leads to superior results, especially on real-world systems.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of robotic reinforcement learning by addressing a fundamental challenge: the high data requirements that impede real-world deployment. The findings offer practical implications and have the potential to impact future developments in several ways:

1. **Enhanced Data Efficiency for Robotics:** The study provides empirically supported strategies for significantly reducing the volume of online interactions necessary for training effective robotic policies. By demonstrating that combining teacher-collected data with online student data and teacher action supervision leads to improved performance, especially at lower data budgets, the work offers a pathway to making RL more feasible for real robot applications where data collection is expensive, time-consuming, and potentially damaging.
2. **Practical Guidance for Algorithm Selection and Data Allocation:** The comprehensive comparison of various algorithms (offline, online, kickstarting, and proposed hybrid methods) under different data budgets and teacher qualities provides concrete guidance for practitioners. The finding that a 1:1 ratio of offline teacher data to online student data is often optimal, and insights into teacher supervision strength (e.g., optimal $\beta$ values), can inform the design of future data collection and training pipelines.
3. **Algorithmic Advancements in Policy Finetuning:** The introduction of R-MPO and R-CRR as extensions of existing, high-performing RL algorithms demonstrates a systematic approach to integrating teacher knowledge within the policy finetuning paradigm. These algorithms serve as new baselines and tools for researchers exploring similar problems.
4. **Robustness Across Diverse Tasks and Environments:** The validation of results across five distinct robotic stacking tasks, two different teacher qualities, and both simulation and real-world environments enhances the generalizability and credibility of the findings. This suggests that the proposed strategies are not limited to a narrow set of conditions but can be broadly applicable to a range of manipulation challenges.
5. **Foundation for Future Research:** The thorough experimental analysis and the identification of strengths and weaknesses of different approaches (e.g., CRR's sensitivity to action diversity in deterministic datasets on real robots) open avenues for further research. Future work can investigate more sophisticated ways to handle diverse data sources, dynamic tuning of teacher influence, and the development of even more robust offline-to-online or kickstarting strategies.

In essence, this work moves beyond theoretical proposals to deliver an extensive empirical evaluation that provides actionable insights for spending "robot time" effectively. Its impact lies in accelerating the adoption of learning-based control in real-world robotics by making the training process more efficient and less resource-intensive.
