# Thinkless: LLM Learns When to Think

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the National University of Singapore developed "Thinkless," an RL-based framework that enables large language models to autonomously decide whether to employ concise or detailed reasoning for a given query. This adaptivity reduces token generation by 50-90% while maintaining performance across various mathematical benchmarks.

## Problem

- Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning in large language models (LLMs) is computationally inefficient, applying extensive reasoning steps to every query regardless of its complexity.
- Existing hybrid reasoning approaches, which attempt to switch between concise and detailed responses, rely on manually designed heuristics or separate router models that lack adaptability and generalizability.
- This 'overthinking' leads to redundant token generation, increased memory consumption, and substantially higher inference costs for LLMs.

## Method

- Introduced two special control tokens, `<think>` and `<no_think>`, generated as the first token of an LLM's output to signal whether to produce a concise or detailed reasoning response.
- Implemented a ==two-stage training process==: an initial distillation phase (Supervised Fine-Tuning) warms up the model to generate both short and long-form responses, followed by a reinforcement learning phase.
- Developed ==Decoupled Group Relative Policy Optimization (DeGRPO)==, a novel RL algorithm that independently normalizes and balances gradient signals for mode selection and content generation, mitigating 'mode collapse' during training.

## Results

- "Thinkless" reduced the usage of long-chain thinking by **5**0%** to 90%** across various benchmarks (e.g., Minerva Algebra, MATH-500, GSM8K), leading to significant token reduction and computational savings.
- The framework maintained or improved task performance, achieving efficiency gains with minimal to no degradation in accuracy (e.g., within **1%** on Minerva Algebra).
- It outperformed heuristic-based length-compressing techniques and router-based approaches by providing a truly adaptive and confidence-informed reasoning dispatch without manual tuning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - LLMs can learn to autonomously adapt their reasoning depth based on task complexity and their own capabilities, moving beyond reliance on manual heuristics.
> - Decoupling the reinforcement learning objective for mode selection and content generation is critical for stable training, effectively preventing 'mode collapse' that hinders learning in standard optimization methods.
> - The 'U-shaped learning curve' observed in Decoupled GRPO indicates a complex learning process where the model initially favors longer reasoning for accuracy, then optimizes by shifting simpler tasks to efficient short responses.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The rapid advancement of large language models has led to their remarkable performance on complex tasks, particularly those requiring multi-step logical inference, largely thanks to the "Chain-of-Thought" (CoT) prompting paradigm. CoT involves generating intermediate reasoning steps before arriving at a final answer, mimicking human-like problem-solving. While highly effective for challenging problems, this approach suffers from significant computational inefficiencies. Applying elaborate, step-by-step reasoning to every query, irrespective of its complexity, leads to redundant token generation, increased memory consumption, and substantially higher inference costs. This phenomenon is often termed "overthinking" in LLMs.

The broader research landscape in LLMs is thus actively seeking solutions to enhance inference efficiency without compromising accuracy or generalization. This work by Fang, Ma, and Wang directly addresses this critical challenge. Existing efforts to improve efficiency can be broadly categorized into:

- **Compression and Optimization of Reasoning Paths:** Techniques like reinforcement learning with length penalties, supervised fine-tuning with variable-length CoT data, prompt-based methods, and latent reasoning aim to generate more concise yet effective reasoning chains. Knowledge distillation has also been explored to transfer reasoning capabilities to smaller models.
- **Hybrid Reasoning:** A more direct approach involves dynamically adapting the inference behavior based on task complexity. This typically allows models to switch between "short-form" (non-reasoning, direct answer) and "long-form" (detailed CoT reasoning) responses. Hybrid reasoning can be implemented via:
 - **Multi-model frameworks:** Where a lightweight router model dispatches queries to either a smaller, faster model or a larger, reasoning-capable model.
 - **Single unified models:** Where the model is trained to support both reasoning modes and can switch between them, often controlled by explicit prompt signals (e.g., "reasoning on/off") or pre-defined computational budgets.

The key limitation of many existing hybrid reasoning approaches is their reliance on **manually designed heuristics**. These heuristics (fixed computational budgets, hard-coded prompt controls) often lack adaptability, fail to account for the LLM's real-time capabilities, and cannot generalize effectively across diverse tasks or varying complexities. This paper identifies this gap and poses the fundamental open question: "Can an LLM learn to decide when to think, guided by the complexity of the task and its own capability?" "Thinkless" positions itself as a pioneering answer to this question, moving beyond human-engineered heuristics to a fully learnable, data-driven adaptive reasoning strategy within a single LLM. By focusing on learning the decision-making process itself, it aims to achieve a more robust and efficient form of hybrid reasoning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind "Thinkless" stems from the aforementioned inefficiencies of applying exhaustive chain-of-thought reasoning universally. The authors highlight that while powerful for complex problems, this "always-on" reasoning approach incurs significant computational costs for straightforward queries, making LLMs impractical for many real-world applications where latency and cost are critical.

The core objective of this research is to empower an LLM to **autonomously and adaptively select the appropriate reasoning depth** – either a concise, short-form response or a detailed, long-form chain-of-thought – based on three key factors:
1. **Task complexity:** Simpler questions should elicit concise answers, while intricate ones require extended reasoning.
2. **Model capability:** A more capable model might solve problems concisely, whereas a less capable one might benefit from elaborate steps to maintain accuracy.
3. **User-defined trade-off:** The acceptable balance between efficiency (shorter responses) and accuracy.

To achieve this, the paper sets out to develop a **learnable framework**, "Thinkless," that leverages reinforcement learning (RL). The specific objectives are:
- To design a mechanism (control tokens) that allows the LLM to signal its intended inference style.
- To establish a robust training paradigm that enables the LLM to learn the "when to think" decision based on performance feedback.
- To overcome the training stability challenges inherent in applying standard RL techniques to hybrid reasoning, specifically addressing gradient imbalances between mode selection and content generation.
- To empirically demonstrate that "Thinkless" can significantly reduce the computational cost of reasoning models while preserving or enhancing task performance across various benchmarks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

"Thinkless" proposes a two-stage training framework for a hybrid reasoning model, employing special control tokens and a novel reinforcement learning algorithm.

#### 3.1. Control Tokens for Reasoning Modes

The framework introduces two special control tokens: `<short>` for concise responses and `<think>` for detailed, long-form reasoning. These tokens are generated as the *first* token of the model's output, acting as a signal for the intended inference style. The model then generates the subsequent response tokens conditioned on this initial control token.

#### 3.2. Two-Stage Training Process

**Stage 1: Distillation for Warm-up**
- **Purpose:** To equip a pre-trained base LLM (`π_θ`) with the fundamental ability to generate *both* short-form and long-form responses, conditioned on the respective control tokens. This serves as a robust initialization for the subsequent RL phase.
- **Data Generation:** A synthetic paired dataset (`D_distill`) is created. For each input query `x_i`, two expert models are used:
 - A "reasoning model" (`π_think`, e.g., DeepSeek-R1-671B) generates the long-form answer (`a_think_i`).
 - An "instruction-following model" (`π_short`, e.g., Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B-Instruct) generates the short-form answer (`a_short_i`).
 - Each response is then prefixed with its corresponding control token (`<think> a_think_i` or `<short> a_short_i`).
- **Training:** The base model `π_θ` is fine-tuned via Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) on this paired dataset. This distillation process ensures that the model learns a clear and balanced mapping between the control tokens and the desired response formats, making it capable of producing diverse output styles.

**Stage 2: Reinforcement Learning with Decoupled GRPO**
- **Purpose:** To enable the warmed-up model to *autonomously decide* which reasoning mode (`<short>` or `<think>`) is appropriate for a given input query `x`, while also improving the accuracy of the generated responses. This is framed as a reinforcement learning problem.
- **Policy Formulation:** The model learns a policy `π_θ(c, a | x) = π_θ(c | x) π_θ(a | x, c)`, where `c` is the control token and `a` is the subsequent response.
- **Reward Design:** A simple, minimally designed reward function `r(a, y*, c)` is used:
 - `+1.0` if `c = <short>` AND `Extract-Answer(a) = y*` (correct and efficient).
 - `1.0 - γ` if `c = <think>` AND `Extract-Answer(a) = y*` (correct but less efficient; `γ` is a small penalty, e.g., 0.1, encouraging shorter answers when possible).
 - `-1.0` if `Extract-Answer(a) ≠ y*` (incorrect, regardless of mode).
- **Addressing Vanilla GRPO Limitations:**
 - The authors initially consider Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), which trains on mini-batches of sampled trajectories.
 - **Problem with Vanilla GRPO:** When applied to hybrid reasoning, vanilla GRPO treats all tokens (control and response) uniformly within its objective. This leads to two critical imbalances:
 1. **Mode-Accuracy Imbalance:** A single control token `a_i,0` is responsible for mode selection, while hundreds to thousands of response tokens `a_i,1:T_i` contribute to accuracy improvement. The gradient signal for the control token becomes disproportionately weak.
 2. **Think-Short Imbalance:** Longer sequences (`T_think_i >> T_short_i`) further suppress the gradient contribution of the control token due to the `1/(T_i + 1)` normalization factor. This biases updates towards the `<short>` token, potentially leading to "mode collapse" where the model excessively favors one mode.
- **Decoupled Group Relative Policy Optimization (DeGRPO):**
 - **Solution:** DeGRPO addresses these imbalances by explicitly decoupling the learning objective into two independently normalized components:
 1. **Mode Selection:** Governed by `α * L_i,0(θ)`, focusing on the control token. `α` is a length-independent weighting coefficient (e.g., 1/1000) that can be adjusted to balance the influence of mode selection.
 2. **Accuracy Improvement:** Governed by `(1/T_i) * Σ_{t=1 to T_i} L_i,t(θ)`, focusing on the response tokens.
 - **Benefits:** This decoupled formulation ensures that the control token receives a consistent and adequate gradient signal regardless of response length, mitigating mode collapse and promoting stable optimization. It allows for fine-grained control over the balance between learning to select the mode and learning to generate accurate content within that mode. The choice of `α` is critical: a larger `α` accelerates mode learning, but too large can lead to premature policy shifts.

#### 3.3. Implementation Details

- **Base Model:** DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B.
- **RL Dataset:** DeepScaleR (approx. 40K labeled examples, primarily math).
- **Evaluation Datasets:** AIME, Minerva Algebra, MATH-500, GSM8K (all math-focused).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical evaluation of "Thinkless" yielded several significant findings, particularly concerning its efficiency, performance, and the dynamics of its RL training.

#### 4.1. Empirical Results on Hybrid Reasoning (Efficiency and Performance)

- **Significant Efficiency Gains:** "Thinkless" demonstrably reduces the usage of long-chain thinking by **50% to 90%** across various benchmarks (Minerva Algebra, MATH-500, GSM8K). This translates directly to reduced token generation and computational cost. For instance, on Minerva Algebra, it activates the reasoning mode for only 25.88% of samples, cutting token usage to one-third of the original while maintaining high accuracy.
- **Performance Maintenance:** Crucially, this efficiency is achieved with minimal or no performance degradation. On Minerva Algebra, performance is maintained within a 1% margin. On AIME (a more challenging dataset), Thinkless intelligently maintains a high proportion of long-form reasoning (100% in the reported table), resulting in a strong Pass@1 score, indicating its ability to adapt its strategy based on inherent task difficulty.
- **Superiority over Baselines:**
 - **Standard LLMs:** Reasoning models like DeepSeek-R1-1.5B generate 5-20 times more tokens than standard instruction-following models, highlighting the need for adaptive reasoning. Thinkless effectively bridges this gap.
 - **Length-Compressing Techniques (e.g., Merging, CoT-Valve):** These methods, while offering controllable reasoning length, struggle with generalizability. The optimal heuristic (e.g., merging coefficient or LoRA `α` parameter) for token reduction on one dataset often leads to significant performance degradation on others. "Thinkless" overcomes this by learning to adapt *per query*.
 - **Router-based Approaches:** While these employ a separate LLM to route queries, they often lack "comprehensive awareness of the target reasoning models" and cannot make confidence-informed decisions. Thinkless, by jointly considering input complexity and model capability through direct interaction, achieves superior, adaptive dispatching without manual tuning.

#### 4.2. Training Dynamics in Reinforcement Learning

- **Mode Collapse in Vanilla GRPO:** The experiments clearly show that standard GRPO suffers from severe "mode collapse." Due to the imbalanced gradient signals (control token's weak influence compared to numerous response tokens), the model quickly develops an excessive preference for one reasoning mode (e.g., favoring short responses). Within just 120 update steps, the number of generated long-chain responses can drop below 10, preventing the model from learning to differentiate between query difficulties effectively.
- **The "U-shaped Learning Curve" in Decoupled GRPO:** DeGRPO effectively mitigates mode collapse and exhibits a characteristic "U-shaped learning curve" for the proportion of long-chain outputs:
 - **Initial Increase in Long-Chain:** In early training, the model tends to prefer long-chain reasoning. This is because long chains initially yield higher accuracy, especially on more complex problems, providing stronger positive rewards.
 - **Subsequent Decrease and Rise in Short-Chain:** As training progresses, two phenomena occur:
 1. Reinforcement learning improves the accuracy of short-chain responses.
 2. The model learns to correctly assign simpler queries to the more efficient short-chain mode.
 This leads to short-chain responses receiving increasingly higher rewards, encouraging the model to explore and utilize this mode more frequently. Many short responses achieve perfect accuracy in later stages.
 - **Shift in Long-Chain Accuracy:** The apparent decline in the average accuracy of long-chain responses later in training is not due to degradation. Instead, it reflects a beneficial shift in task allocation: the most difficult queries (those that *cannot* be solved concisely) are now consistently assigned to the long-chain mode, naturally lowering its *average* accuracy as it handles the "hardest" subset of problems.
- **Influence of `α` (Control Token Weight):** The weighting coefficient `α` in DeGRPO, which balances mode selection and response generation, significantly impacts learning speed. A higher `α` accelerates the emergence of "all-correct short samples" by making the control token updates more aggressive. However, excessively fast policy updates (too large `α`) can be detrimental, leading to premature assignment of samples to a mode without fully exploring potential improvements in the other mode, effectively degenerating the algorithm into a simple binary classifier rather than a collaborative learning process.

#### 4.3. Details of Warm-up Distillation

- **Reasoning LLMs as Short Response Learners:** The warm-up phase confirms that reasoning LLMs (like DeepSeek-R1-1.5B) can indeed learn to generate effective short responses through knowledge distillation. Even with relatively small, domain-specific datasets (e.g., OpenR1-97K for math), the model successfully acquires this capability.
- **Impact of Distillation Data Scale:** While larger and more comprehensive distillation datasets (e.g., OpenThoughts-1M vs. OpenR1-97K) generally lead to improved performance in both long and short modes, the marginal gains diminish with increasing dataset size. This suggests that simple distillation is effective, and the primary challenge lies in the subsequent RL optimization.

#### 4.4. Case Study

- Analysis of the `P(<think>|x)` distribution on MATH-500 confirms that "Thinkless" makes smooth and hierarchical predictions based on query difficulty. Simple arithmetic problems (low `P(<think>|x)`) are routed to short reasoning, while complex problems involving multiple conditions and logical steps (high `P(<think>|x)`) are routed to long-form reasoning. This demonstrates a well-calibrated policy that intelligently adapts reasoning depth.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Thinkless" framework represents a significant step forward in developing more efficient and intelligent large language models, addressing a critical bottleneck in their practical deployment.

#### 5.1. Scientific Significance

- **Learnable Adaptive Reasoning:** This work moves beyond heuristic-based control of reasoning depth to a truly learnable, autonomous decision-making process within LLMs. It provides a foundational answer to the "Can LLMs learn when to think?" question, demonstrating that LLMs can internalize and adapt their reasoning strategies based on perceived task complexity and their own evolving capabilities.
- **Novel Reinforcement Learning Algorithm:** The introduction of Decoupled GRPO is a notable algorithmic contribution. By explicitly separating and balancing the learning objectives for mode selection and content generation, it overcomes critical instability issues (mode collapse) prevalent in applying standard RL to such hybrid generation tasks. This decoupled approach could have broader applicability in other LLM optimization problems requiring a balance between high-level strategic decisions and low-level content generation.
- **Deepening Understanding of LLM Behavior:** The analysis of the "U-shaped learning curve" and the influence of `α` provides valuable insights into the complex training dynamics of RL-optimized LLMs, particularly when learning to balance trade-offs.

#### 5.2. Practical Impact and Applications

- **Enhanced Computational Efficiency:** The most immediate and significant impact is the substantial reduction in inference costs. By avoiding unnecessary long-chain reasoning for simple queries, "Thinkless" can drastically cut down on token generation, memory footprint, and computational time. This makes powerful reasoning LLMs much more economical and faster for real-time applications.
- **Improved User Experience:** Users will experience faster response times for straightforward queries while still receiving detailed, accurate reasoning for complex problems. This adaptability leads to a more fluid and responsive interaction.
- **Resource Optimization in Deployment:** For organizations deploying LLMs, "Thinkless" offers a pathway to optimize compute resources, allowing for more efficient scaling and cost-effective operations. This is crucial for mass adoption of advanced LLM capabilities.
- **Foundation for Adaptive Systems:** The concept of an LLM learning its own inference strategy could be extended to other adaptive systems, such as intelligent agents that learn when to seek external tools, when to delegate tasks, or when to perform deep internal simulations.

#### 5.3. Limitations and Future Work

The authors acknowledge several limitations and outline future research directions:
- **Warm-up Optimization:** The current warm-up distillation relies on a simple SFT approach. Future work could explore more advanced strategies (e.g., parameter-efficient fine-tuning like LoRA, or model merging techniques) to better mitigate potential performance drops or catastrophic forgetting during this initial phase, aiming for an even stronger base model for RL.
- **Dataset Diversity:** The current validation is primarily on mathematical datasets. Expanding "Thinkless" to a broader range of domains (e.g., scientific reasoning, coding, general knowledge Q&A) with more diverse problem complexities would be crucial to ensure generalizability and practical utility. This would involve curating large-scale, multi-domain paired datasets for distillation and RL.

In conclusion, "Thinkless" makes a compelling case for a learned approach to adaptive reasoning in LLMs. By providing LLMs with the ability to "learn when to think," this research not only offers a powerful solution to the computational inefficiencies of reasoning models but also paves the way for more autonomous, context-aware, and resource-efficient AI systems.
