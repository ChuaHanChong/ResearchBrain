# Calibrated Partial Resets: Preventing Policy Collapse in Continual Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Calibrated Partial Resets (CPR) introduces an optimizer strategy to prevent policy collapse in continual reinforcement learning by smoothly reinitializing low-utility neurons. In the SlipperyAnt benchmark, CPR consistently avoided policy collapse across all 15 seeds over 400 million training steps, demonstrating improved adaptation and higher final returns compared to existing methods in diverse continual RL environments.

## Problem

- Deep neural networks experience plasticity loss and representation collapse when trained over extended periods with non-stationary data in continual reinforcement learning.
- Existing neuron reinitialization methods use abrupt, 'all-or-nothing' binary resets, which introduce instability and can lead to policy collapse.
- Uniform decay methods are smoother but lack selectivity, potentially negatively impacting high-utility neurons indiscriminately.

## Method

- Developed **Calibrated Partial Resets (CPR)**, an optimizer that periodically pulls low-utility neurons towards their initialization state.
- Implemented a ==utility-scaled reset coefficient==, where the reset strength is a continuous, monotonically decreasing function of a neuron's gradient-based utility, avoiding binary interventions.
- Used an ==exponential moving average== and ==layer-normalization== to estimate neuron utility, ensuring stable and comparable scores across layers and training time.

## Results

- **CPR** consistently prevented policy collapse over **400 million** training steps in the SlipperyAnt benchmark across all **15** random seeds, outperforming Adam and binary reset baselines.
- Achieved the highest average and final returns on SlipperyHumanoid, Continual MetaWorld, and Continual MinAtar, demonstrating superior adaptation and knowledge transfer in diverse continual RL settings.
- Maintained significantly lower dormant and linearized neuron ratios, and exhibited smaller fluctuations in actor gradient norms compared to other methods, indicating improved plasticity preservation and training stability.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Smooth, utility-calibrated partial resets effectively maintain network plasticity and prevent policy collapse without the instability caused by abrupt binary reinitialization.
> - The maximum reset magnitude (ρ) and the sharpness of the utility transformation function (κ) offer tunable control over the plasticity-stability trade-off, leading to robust performance.
> - Sustained high performance in continual RL requires maintaining low dormant-neuron and linearized-neuron ratios, coupled with constrained parameter norms and stable gradient dynamics.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Deep neural networks often encounter challenges in maintaining plasticity and expressivity when trained over extended periods with non-stationary data, a common characteristic of continual supervised learning and reinforcement learning (RL) environments. This phenomenon, known as plasticity loss, can manifest as an accumulation of dormant neurons, a decrease in feature rank, and a rise in parameter norms, potentially leading to representation collapse. In reinforcement learning, the problem is particularly pronounced because an agent's policy updates can alter its future data distribution, creating feedback loops where minor optimization instabilities can escalate into catastrophic policy degradation, including policy collapse.

Prior research has addressed plasticity loss through various methods, predominantly by adaptively reinitializing low-utility neuron units. These methods, such as Continual Backpropagation (CBP), ReDo, and ReGraMa, differ in how they estimate neuron utility (e.g., running averages of activations, batch-level activation statistics, or gradient magnitudes). Despite these differences, they commonly employ a binary intervention: units identified below a certain utility threshold are fully reinitialized, while others remain unchanged. This all-or-nothing approach, while selective in targeting specific neurons, introduces abrupt parameter changes that can destabilize training, especially in the long-horizon, non-stationary settings characteristic of continual RL. Conversely, methods like Shrink & Perturb apply smoother, uniform decay across all units, which is less disruptive but lacks the selectivity to target genuinely low-utility components effectively.

This research aims to address the identified gap between these two approaches. It proposes a mechanism that retains the advantages of selectively refreshing low-utility neurons while mitigating the destabilizing effects of abrupt, full reinitialization. By introducing a smooth, calibrated partial reset, the work attempts to prevent policy collapse and maintain stable, high performance in demanding continual RL scenarios. This contribution seeks to enhance the reliability of deep learning agents operating in dynamic, open-ended environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an optimization strategy that prevents policy collapse and maintains plasticity in deep neural networks trained in continual reinforcement learning settings. This overarching goal is motivated by specific challenges identified with existing methods for addressing plasticity loss.

The key motivations and objectives are detailed as follows:

1. **Addressing Instability from Binary Neuron Resets:** The paper identifies that current neuron-reset methods (e.g., CBP, ReDo, ReGraMa) use a binary intervention strategy: neurons are either fully reinitialized or left unchanged. This "all-or-nothing" reinitialization introduces sudden, large changes in network parameters. Such abrupt modifications are recognized as a source of instability in deep RL, potentially leading to gradient spikes, distribution shifts in subsequent layers (especially when outgoing weights are zeroed), and ultimately, catastrophic policy degradation or collapse. The objective is to design a method that avoids these sharp, destabilizing updates.

2. **Bridging the Gap Between Selective Resets and Smooth Decay:** Existing approaches present a trade-off. Binary reset methods are selective, targeting specific low-utility neurons, but they are discontinuous and can be brittle. Uniform decay methods, like Shrink & Perturb, introduce smoother changes but apply them indiscriminately across all units, potentially affecting high-utility neurons negatively. The motivation is to combine the advantages of both: a method that is *both selective and smooth*, allowing for targeted plasticity injection without destabilizing global network performance.

3. **Proposing Calibrated Partial Resets (CPR):** To achieve the above, the authors propose CPR. The core objective of CPR is to periodically pull low-utility neurons towards their initialization state, but with a pull strength that is continuously scaled by each neuron's utility, rather than a binary decision. This calibration aims to concentrate adjustment on units that require it most while minimally perturbing high-utility units.

4. **Maintaining Peak Performance Over Long Horizons:** In continual RL, agents must adapt to changing environments over extended training periods. Existing methods often exhibit a "plasticity-performance trade-off": some achieve high peak performance but eventually collapse (e.g., Adam), while others maintain trainability but struggle to achieve optimal policies due to unstable training dynamics (e.g., binary reset methods). A key objective for CPR is to enable agents to consistently achieve and maintain peak performance even after numerous task changes and over hundreds of millions of training steps, thereby overcoming this trade-off.

5. **Empirical Validation and Ablation Studies:** Beyond proposing CPR, the research aims to empirically demonstrate its effectiveness. This includes showing that CPR can prevent policy collapse, improve performance on diverse continual RL benchmarks (friction-shift control, off-policy control, visual control), and maintain beneficial plasticity-related statistics (dormant-neuron and linearized-neuron ratios). Furthermore, the research seeks to ablate key design choices within CPR, such as the maximum reset magnitude (ρ) and the shape of the utility transformation function (κ), to demonstrate how these parameters control the plasticity–stability tradeoff and to establish robustness to hyperparameter selection.

In summary, the motivation stems from the observed instability and performance limitations of current plasticity-maintaining techniques in continual RL, particularly the brittleness of binary resets and the indiscriminateness of uniform decay. The objective is to introduce a more robust and effective mechanism that can sustain learning and adaptation across long, non-stationary training horizons.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

This research introduces Calibrated Partial Resets (CPR), an optimizer designed to prevent policy collapse in continual reinforcement learning by selectively and smoothly reinitializing low-utility neurons. The methodology encompasses a specific utility scoring mechanism, a novel partial reset rule, and a comprehensive experimental evaluation across multiple continual RL benchmarks.

#### 3.1. Continual Reinforcement Learning Setting

The work focuses on the Continual RL setting, where an agent operates sequentially through a series of Markov Decision Processes (MDPs), M_1..., M_N. The environment dynamics (P_k, R_k) change periodically, requiring the agent to continually adapt its policy over a long training horizon (T). This introduces significant non-stationarity, challenging the agent's ability to maintain plasticity.

#### 3.2. Network Plasticity Metrics

To quantify plasticity loss and its mitigation, two primary metrics are utilized:
- **Dormant-neuron ratio:** Measures the proportion of neurons whose mean activation is small relative to the layer average (threshold τ = 0.1). High dormancy indicates under-used network capacity.
- **Linearized-neuron ratio:** Measures the percentage of neurons for which pre-activation values are consistently positive (threshold θ = 0.9). High linearization suggests a loss of gating diversity, which can reduce rank and gradient flow.

#### 3.3. Unit Utility Score Estimation

CPR employs a gradient-based utility criterion, following recent approaches that highlight its favorable scaling properties.
- **Raw Utility Score (S_i_l(t)):** Defined as the mean gradient magnitude of a neuron's incoming weights with respect to the loss function L, averaged over the current mini-batch.
- **Smoothed and Layer-Normalized Utility (u_i_l(t)):** The raw score is then smoothed using an exponential moving average (EMA) with a decay parameter β. This EMA is layer-normalized by dividing by the layer mean of scores, ensuring comparability across layers and training time.

#### 3.4. Calibrated Partial Resets (CPR) Mechanism

CPR is designed to be both partial (avoiding full reinitialization) and calibrated (utility-scaled).
- **Periodic Application:** Resets are applied every 'f' optimization steps.
- **Partial Reset Rule:** For each unit *i* in layer *l*, its weights (incoming W_l_i,: and outgoing W_l+1_,:,i) are pulled toward their initialization values (ξ_l_i,:, sampled from D_init) by a coefficient r_l_i.
 - W_l_i,: ← (1 - r_l_i) W_l_i,: + r_l_i ξ_l_i,:
 - W_l+1_,:,i ← (1 - r_l_i) W_l+1_,:,i
- **Calibrated Reset Coefficient (r_l_i):** This coefficient is determined by r_l_i = ρ * φ(u_l_i), where:
 - **ρ ∈ (0, 1]:** The maximum per-unit reset fraction, directly controlling the overall reset magnitude.
 - **φ(u_l_i):** A monotonically decreasing shape function of the neuron's utility (u_l_i). The primary function used is a scaled logistic sigmoid: φ(u_l_i) = min(2σ(-κ(u_l_i - 1)), 1).
 - **κ > 0:** The sharpness parameter, which determines how quickly the reset strength decreases for units with higher utility. A larger κ implies a sharper transition, approaching a step function where only units below layer-average utility are reset.
 - The function is normalized such that a unit with average utility (u_l_i = 1) receives a reset fraction of ρ. Units with below-average utility receive stronger resets, up to ρ, while those above average receive progressively weaker resets.
- **Utility Re-centering:** After a reset step, the running average of utility scores (u_l_i) for all neurons is reset to 1 (the layer mean), ensuring that future utility calculations reflect the post-reset state.

#### 3.5. Experimental Setup and Evaluation

The efficacy of CPR is evaluated on a suite of challenging continual RL benchmarks:
- **Friction-shift Control:** SlipperyAnt and SlipperyHumanoid (Brax-based environments, 400M training steps, friction changes every 20M steps).
- **Continual Off-policy Control:** Continual MetaWorld (MT10 taskset using SAC).
- **Continual Visual Control:** Continual MinAtar (sequence of Atari tasks using CNN architecture).

**Base Optimizers and Algorithms:** Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) with Adam is used for Slippery environments. Soft Actor Critic (SAC) with Muon is used for Continual MetaWorld, and discrete SAC with Adam for Continual MinAtar.

**Baselines:** CPR is compared against:
- Adam (standard optimizer, no reset mechanism).
- CBP, ReDo, ReGraMa (binary neuron reset methods).
- Shrink & Perturb (uniform decay method).

**Evaluation Metrics:**
- **Interquartile Mean (IQM) of episodic return:** A robust metric for aggregate performance across seeds.
- **Policy collapse:** Defined as a sustained drop of at least 8,000 in episodic return from a prior peak, lasting for at least 4M timesteps.
- **Plasticity-related statistics:** Dormant-neuron ratio, linearized-neuron ratio, actor gradient norms, and parameter norms.
- **Ablation studies:** To analyze the impact of ρ and κ, and the choice of shape function φ.

The implementation is built in JAX with Optax, treating reset methods as optimizer wrappers to ensure modularity and compatibility with existing training pipelines.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides empirical evidence supporting the effectiveness of Calibrated Partial Resets (CPR) in maintaining plasticity and preventing policy collapse in continual reinforcement learning.

#### 4.1. Prevention of Policy Collapse and Sustained Performance

- **SlipperyAnt:** CPR was the only method among those compared to consistently avoid policy collapse over 400 million training steps across all 15 random seeds. In contrast, Adam, without any explicit plasticity mechanism, eventually experienced collapse. Binary reset methods (ReDo, ReGraMa) showed initial peak performance but also exhibited a decline later in training, indicating a form of performance degradation or instability over very long horizons. CPR achieved the highest final return and maintained consistent peak performance after each friction change.
- **Policy Collapse Thresholds:** The definition of policy collapse was tested for robustness; CPR uniquely avoided collapse across a meaningful range of thresholds, demonstrating a qualitative shift in training reliability.

#### 4.2. Superiority in Continual Learning Benchmarks

- **SlipperyHumanoid:** CPR demonstrated substantial outperformance over baselines in mean episodic return throughout training, suggesting more effective knowledge transfer and adaptation across task changes in this complex environment.
- **Continual MetaWorld and Continual MinAtar:** CPR achieved the highest average and final returns on both benchmarks (Table 1 and Table 2), further supporting its generalizability and robustness in diverse continual RL settings involving different base optimizers and network architectures (e.g., CNNs for MinAtar).

#### 4.3. Improved Plasticity-Related Statistics

- **Dormant and Linearized Neurons:** CPR, alongside CBP, effectively maintained low dormant-neuron ratios, keeping them nearly flat over 400 million steps. This contrasts with Adam and other baselines, which showed steadily rising dormant ratios, correlating with their observed performance degradation. CPR also maintained stable linearized-neuron ratios, indicating preservation of representation capacity and gating diversity. These findings suggest that CPR successfully addresses the underlying causes of plasticity loss.
- **Gradient and Parameter Norms:** CPR exhibited smaller fluctuations in actor gradient norms over time compared to binary reset methods, which are prone to larger spikes (Figure 4). This reduced gradient instability is consistent with CPR's ability to prevent policy collapse. Additionally, CPR constrained parameter norms throughout training, preventing the excessive growth identified as a driver of plasticity loss in prior work (Figure 15).

#### 4.4. Tunable Stability–Plasticity Trade-off and Robustness

- **Maximum Per-Unit Reset Fraction (ρ):** The parameter ρ provides a direct control over the trade-off between plasticity and peak performance. While small ρ values might not inject sufficient plasticity, and large ρ values could over-reset useful structures, a robust operational range of ρ ∈ [0.01, 0.05] was identified where CPR consistently prevented policy collapse in SlipperyAnt and SlipperyHumanoid. This indicates that collapse prevention is not brittle to precise hyperparameter selection.
- **Sharpness Parameter (κ):** Ablation studies showed CPR's performance is robust across a wide range of κ ∈. Performance only degraded at very low κ values (≤1), where the utility-based targeting becomes nearly uniform, underscoring the importance of selective resets.
- **Shape Function (ϕ):** While different continuous transformation shapes for ϕ (e.g., Linear, Exponential, Softplus, Sigmoid) yielded measurable impacts, Sigmoid and Exponential functions generally performed best. This suggests that a non-linear decay of reset strength for neurons with moderately low gradients is beneficial.

#### 4.5. Additional Experiments

- **Standard RL (MetaWorld MT1):** CPR demonstrated no performance penalty in stationary single-task environments, matching or exceeding the base optimizer, suggesting its mechanisms do not introduce instability in simpler settings.
- **Continual Supervised Learning (Permuted MNIST):** CPR effectively maintained plasticity and adaptation efficiency, outperforming standard Adam and matching other reset-based baselines, even with 200 task transitions.
- **Rollout Size Impact:** Empirical results confirmed that larger on-policy rollout sizes can delay policy collapse and dormant neuron accumulation in baselines like Adam but do not fundamentally solve the underlying plasticity loss, reinforcing the necessity of dedicated plasticity maintenance methods.

#### 4.6. Computational Overhead

- CPR introduced a marginal computational overhead (approximately 6% increase in wall-clock training time compared to Adam in SlipperyHumanoid), suggesting its practicality for integration into existing training pipelines.

Overall, the findings indicate that CPR successfully addresses the identified limitations of existing plasticity-preserving methods by providing a stable, selective, and effective approach to continual learning across various challenging RL environments.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of Calibrated Partial Resets (CPR) represents a development in the field of continual reinforcement learning, addressing the critical challenge of plasticity loss and policy collapse in dynamic, long-horizon environments. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Enhanced Training Reliability in Continual RL:** CPR provides a method to mitigate policy collapse, a severe form of training instability that has been a major impediment to the deployment of deep RL agents in real-world, non-stationary settings. By preventing catastrophic performance degradation, CPR offers a qualitative shift in the reliability of long-horizon RL systems, where agents must continuously adapt without human intervention. This could enable more robust and autonomous intelligent systems.

2. **Addressing a Fundamental Design Flaw in Existing Plasticity Mechanisms:** The research identifies and addresses a key limitation of prior neuron reinitialization methods: the brittleness and instability caused by "all-or-nothing" binary resets. CPR's approach of continuous, utility-scaled partial resets fills a conceptual gap, demonstrating that selectivity and smoothness can be combined effectively. This insight could influence the design of future optimization algorithms for continual learning.

3. **Improved Performance Across Diverse Continual Learning Tasks:** The consistent outperformance of CPR on various benchmarks, including challenging friction-shift control tasks (SlipperyAnt, SlipperyHumanoid), off-policy control (Continual MetaWorld), and visual control (Continual MinAtar), suggests its broad applicability. This indicates that CPR is not specific to a niche problem but offers a general solution for improving adaptation and stability in diverse continual learning scenarios.

4. **Deeper Understanding of Plasticity Loss:** The diagnostic metrics used (dormant-neuron ratio, linearized-neuron ratio, gradient norms, parameter norms) provide convergent evidence for CPR's mechanism of action. By showing that CPR maintains beneficial plasticity-related statistics and constrains parameter growth while smoothing gradient dynamics, the research contributes to a more nuanced understanding of the factors underlying plasticity loss and how they can be effectively managed.

5. **Practical Implementability and Robustness:** CPR's robust performance across a range of hyperparameters (ρ and κ) and its marginal computational overhead make it a practical candidate for integration into existing deep learning frameworks. The modular Optax implementation further facilitates its adoption, requiring minimal modifications to standard training pipelines. This ease of use, combined with its effectiveness, enhances its potential for practical impact.

6. **Future Research Directions:** This work opens several avenues for future research. It highlights the importance of isolating and understanding the contributions of utility estimation, partial reinitialization, optimizer-state handling, and normalization in plasticity maintenance. Investigating CPR's effectiveness across an even wider range of non-stationary processes (e.g., reward non-stationarity, observation shifts, morphology changes, large-scale visual control) and architectural variations could further extend its impact.

In essence, CPR offers a more stable and effective approach to a fundamental problem in AI, enabling neural networks to learn and adapt over extended periods in dynamic environments without suffering from catastrophic forgetting or policy collapse. This contributes to the development of more reliable and intelligent agents capable of continuous, lifelong learning.
