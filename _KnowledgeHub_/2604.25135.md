# FAMA: Failure-Aware Meta-Agentic Framework for Open-Source LLMs in Interactive Tool Use Environments

> [!summary] Summary
> The FAMA (Failure-Aware Meta-Agentic Framework) is a two-stage approach designed to enhance the performance of open-source Large Language Models in interactive tool-use environments by identifying specific failure modes and dynamically activating a minimal subset of helper agents for mitigation. The framework achieved performance gains of up to 27% across various benchmarks like τ-bench, τ-trait, and ACEBench, while reducing token overhead and latency compared to indiscriminate multi-agent systems.

## Problem

- Open-source Large Language Models (LLMs) frequently fail in multi-turn, interactive tool-use scenarios due to accumulating decision-making errors, often exacerbated by their smaller parameter sizes and limited context windows.
- Existing improvement methods like supervised fine-tuning or reinforcement learning are often costly, require extensive data, and do not specifically target observed failure patterns.
- Static multi-agent frameworks frequently lead to unnecessary context injection, increased token usage, higher latency, and potentially degraded performance for resource-constrained open-source models.

## Method

- **FAMA** employs a ==Failure Analysis== stage where a baseline agent's failed trajectories are categorized into predefined error types via dedicated error-analysis agents and an orchestrator.
- In the ==Dynamic Mitigation== stage, a mitigation agent — conditioned on identified failure categories — selects an efficient, minimal subset of specialized helper agents from a predefined pool.
- The baseline agent is re-executed with its context dynamically constructed using only the recommended helper agents, providing targeted interventions for specific weaknesses.

## Results

- **FAMA** improved performance by up to **27%** across τ-bench, τ-trait, and ACEBench versus ReAct, Function Calling, and IRMA baselines.
- Reduced token overhead by **~30%** and lower latency vs. indiscriminate multi-agent systems (IRMA's **50-58%** overhead), with fewer context overflow errors.
- Strong generalizability across Airline, Retail, Telehealth, and Telecom domains without manual redesign.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Open-source LLMs consistently struggle with specific failure modes in tool-use tasks, such as Domain Policy Violation, Contextual Misinterpretation and Hallucination, and Incomplete Fulfillment.
> - Dynamically composing a minimal, task-relevant subset of specialized helper agents, based on identified failure modes, is more efficient and effective than static, indiscriminate multi-agent approaches for resource-constrained models.
> - The optimal memory size for an agent in interactive tool-use environments is domain-dependent rather than model-dependent, highlighting the need for adaptive context management strategies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of Large Language Models (LLMs) as autonomous agents capable of interacting with and effecting change in external environments is a significant area of current research. These agents are being explored for applications such as customer support, enterprise automation, scientific discovery, and software engineering, often requiring them to process long conversational histories and utilize external tools (APIs).

Despite advancements, a central challenge in multi-turn, interactive tool-use scenarios is the accumulation of decision-making errors. Benchmarks like τ-bench, τ-trait, and ACEBench highlight that even sophisticated LLM-based agents struggle with sustained reasoning and structured tool use, particularly under heterogeneous contexts that include complex tool outputs, domain rules, and evolving task constraints. This issue is reportedly exacerbated in open-source LLMs due to their smaller parameter sizes, limited context windows, and constrained inference budgets, leading to increased error accumulation.

Prior research has attempted to improve tool-using agents through supervised fine-tuning or reinforcement learning. However, these methods often face high costs for collecting high-quality data, require extensive compute, and may not specifically target observed failure patterns. Furthermore, the effectiveness of reinforcement learning can be limited by sparse and delayed rewards in long episodes. Many existing training-free techniques, such as structured prompting and modular agent orchestration, have predominantly been evaluated on large, proprietary LLMs with ample context windows, leaving the performance of smaller open-source models in tool-use settings relatively unexplored. Smaller models are crucial for real-world deployment due to cost, latency, and privacy considerations, and they also expose fundamental limitations in long-horizon decision-making more clearly.

This work addresses the gap in providing robust, training-free improvement techniques for open-source LLMs in multi-turn, interactive tool-use environments. It distinguishes itself by focusing on dynamically adapting agent behavior based on identified failure modes, rather than relying on static prompting, brute-force context injection, or computationally intensive training paradigms. It builds upon analyses of agent failures in multi-agent LLM systems and taxonomies of error causes, offering a practical, resource-constrained solution for enhancing agent reliability.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the reliability and efficiency of open-source LLMs when deployed as autonomous agents in interactive, multi-turn tool-use environments. The motivation stems from several critical observations and challenges:

- **Prevalence of Failures in Conversational Benchmarks:** LLM agents frequently fail in simulated customer-centric issue resolution scenarios due to cascading errors in decision-making. These failures often arise from incorrect tool invocation, misinterpretation of complex outputs, violations of domain policies, and difficulties in managing long conversational contexts.
- **Limitations of Open-Source LLMs:** Open-source LLMs, while offering advantages in cost, latency, and privacy for real-world deployment, are particularly susceptible to these failures. Their smaller parameter sizes, limited context windows, and constrained inference budgets contribute to a higher rate of error accumulation. Existing generic prompting strategies or static agentic scaffolding architectures are often insufficient to address their distinct and varied failure modes.
- **Inefficiencies of Current Improvement Paradigms:**
 - **Supervised Fine-tuning and Reinforcement Learning:** These methods are often prohibitively expensive for multi-turn tool-use tasks due to the cost of collecting high-quality, long-trajectory data and the computational overhead of training. They also typically optimize for correct behavior patterns rather than directly targeting specific failure modes.
 - **Static Multi-Agent Frameworks:** While multi-agent frameworks exist, their indiscriminate use of all available agents can lead to unnecessary context injection, increased token usage, higher latency, and sometimes degraded performance, especially for resource-constrained open-source models.
- **Need for Failure-Aware Adaptation:** There is a recognized need for training-free improvement techniques that can extract failure signals from agent trajectories and use them to modularly orchestrate agentic systems. Such techniques should be capable of dynamically adapting to observed weaknesses rather than relying on static or universal solutions.

To address these motivations, the authors propose the Failure-Aware Meta-Agentic (FAMA) framework, with the key objective of explicitly identifying dominant failure modes and dynamically tailoring the tool-use agent's internal context to mitigate these specific failures. This approach aims to provide targeted interventions, avoid excessive resource consumption, and ultimately build more dependable and resource-efficient conversational agents based on open-source LLMs.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Failure-Aware Meta-Agentic (FAMA) framework is a two-stage, dynamic approach designed to improve the performance of open-source LLM agents in interactive tool-use environments by systematically identifying and mitigating prevalent failure modes.

**Core Framework Stages:**

1. **Failure Analysis (Stage 1):**
 - **Baseline Execution:** A baseline tool-use agent, operating without multi-agent assistance, is initially executed on a set of domain tasks.
 - **Trajectory Analysis:** For tasks where the baseline agent fails, the complete interaction trajectories are collected and analyzed.
 - **Error Categorization:** Failures are categorized into a predefined set of general error types, identified through prior studies and empirical analysis of baseline trajectories. These categories include:
 - **Domain Policy Violation (DCV):** The agent performs an action explicitly forbidden or skips a required step by domain rules.
 - **Incorrect Retrieval from Complex Tool Outputs (WRCO):** The agent fails to correctly extract relevant information from verbose, nested, or weakly structured tool responses.
 - **Contextual Misinterpretation and Hallucination (CM):** The agent misinterprets user intent due to ambiguous language, over-literalizes phrasing, or hallucinates incorrect task formulations.
 - **Incomplete Fulfillment or Early Stopping (IFU):** The agent fails to complete all parts of a multi-part user request or prematurely terminates interaction.
 - **Independent Error Analysis Agents:** For each error category, a dedicated error analysis agent is deployed. These agents operate over predefined lists of failure causes (e.g., specific reasons for a policy violation) and produce a categorical decision along with a textual rationale.
 - **Orchestrator Agent:** The textual outputs and rationales from all independent error analysis agents, along with the full interaction trajectory of the failed task, are passed to an orchestrator agent. This orchestrator is responsible for identifying the primary root cause(s) of failure for a given task, considering the entire trajectory and potential recovery attempts.

2. **Dynamic Mitigation (Stage 2):**
 - **Agent Pool:** A predefined pool of specialized helper agents (A) and modules is assumed to be available. This pool includes agents adapted from prior work and extended for the benchmarks used, such as:
 - Domain Constraints Extractor Agent (DCE)
 - Tool Suggestion Agent (TSA)
 - Tool Output Reformulator Agent (TOR)
 - Planner Agent
 - Decision Verifier Agent
 - User Context Manager (Memory) module
 - **Mitigation Agent:** Conditioned on the error categories identified by the orchestrator agent and the functional definitions of agents in the pool A, a dedicated mitigation agent determines an "efficient subset" of helper agents (A\* ⊆ A). This subset is selected to specifically address the identified dominant failure modes for that task.
 - **Context Construction:** The baseline agent is then re-executed with its prior context dynamically constructed using only the helper agents recommended by the mitigation agent. This targeted context injection aims to mitigate specific weaknesses without introducing the overhead of unnecessary agents. The term "meta-agentic" highlights that FAMA reasons about agent behaviors and failures to compose optimal agent configurations, rather than directly acting in the environment.

**Experimental Setup:**

- **Models:** The study primarily focuses on open-source LLMs from the Qwen family, including Qwen3-4B-Instruct, Qwen3-14B, Qwen3-32B, and Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct. Reasoning-augmented variants were excluded due to their high token consumption and propensity for context window overflows in agentic settings.
- **User Agent Simulation:** Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct was identified as the best-performing open-source model to serve as the user agent simulator, closely approximating frontier models like GPT-4o in this role.
- **Benchmarks:** Experiments were conducted on three multi-turn, interactive tool-use benchmarks:
 - **τ-bench:** Covering Airline and Retail domains.
 - **τ-trait:** Extending to Telehealth and Telecom domains.
 - **ACEBench:** Focusing on multi-turn agent tasks in various everyday domains.
- **Baselines:** FAMA was compared against:
 - **Function Calling (FC):** A direct approach where the LLM directly calls tools.
 - **ReAct:** A framework synergizing reasoning and acting.
 - **Input-Reformulation Multi-Agent (IRMA):** A multi-agent framework that includes a collection of specialized agents without dynamic selection.
 - **Self-Reflection (SR):** An additional baseline implemented as part of the rebuttal, augmenting the base agent with an explicit reflection and correction phase.
- **Evaluation Metrics:**
 - **pass^k:** For τ-bench and τ-trait, measuring the probability that all k independently sampled outputs successfully complete the task.
 - **End-to-End Accuracy and Process Accuracy:** For ACEBench, measuring correspondence with ground truth and alignment with the ideal sequence of function calls, respectively.
- **Judgment Agents:** GPT-4o and GPT-4.1-mini were used as judgment agents within the FAMA framework to analyze failures and ensure consistency of decisions.
- **Ablation Studies:** Investigations included memory size optimization (k, number of recent user queries retained), alternative agent combinations, and the consistency of different judgment models.
- **Efficiency Analysis:** Token overhead and latency were measured to assess the computational cost of different methods.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted across various open-source LLMs and interactive tool-use benchmarks demonstrated consistent performance improvements for the FAMA framework compared to established baselines.

#### 4.1. Performance Gains

- **τ-bench:** FAMA consistently outperformed ReAct, FC, and IRMA. Specifically, it achieved gains of up to 25% (e.g., 4.63% over ReAct, 11.57% over FC, and 5.27% over IRMA in the Airline domain, and 5.30%, 8.96%, and 6.15% in the Retail domain, averaged over five runs).
- **ACEBench:** FAMA, even with the inclusion of only two auxiliary agents (Planner and Verifier), led to performance improvements in both End-to-End Accuracy and Process Accuracy when augmenting ReAct and the Base Agent. For example, with Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct, FAMA-Base achieved 50.0% End-to-End Accuracy compared to the Base agent's 23.3%.
- **τ-trait:** FAMA consistently outperformed IRMA across both Telehealth and Telecom domains on pass^k metrics (for k=1 to 5). While FC occasionally outperformed FAMA in isolated cases, FAMA demonstrated more consistent performance across domains and models, highlighting its robustness and generalizability.

#### 4.2. Generalizability and Adaptability

- FAMA demonstrated generalizability by adapting to agent failure behavior across multiple domains (Airline, Retail, Telehealth, Telecom) and benchmarks (τ-bench, τ-trait, ACEBench). This suggests that the framework's dynamic nature overcomes the limitations of static multi-agent frameworks, which often require extensive manual redesign for different models or domains.

#### 4.3. Error Analysis and Mitigation

- **Dominant Failure Modes:** A statistical comparison of error categories revealed that open-source LLMs consistently struggled with **Domain Policy Violation (DCV)** and **Contextual Misinterpretation and Hallucination (CM)** across τ-bench. In τ-trait, **Incomplete Fulfillment or Early Stopping (IFU)** emerged as the primary cause of task failures, in addition to DCV and CM. On ACEBench, CM was identified as a significant issue.
- **Targeted Recommendations:** The mitigation agent frequently recommended the **Memory module** and the **Domain Constraints Extractor (DCE) agent** for τ-bench and τ-trait, confirming memory-related issues and adherence to domain rules as critical bottlenecks for open-source agents. For ACEBench, **Planner** and **Verifier** agents were consistently recommended to address contextual misinterpretation and ensure task completion.
- **Memory Ablation Study:** An ablation study on memory size (k, number of recent user queries retained) showed that the optimal memory size is domain-dependent rather than model-dependent. The Retail domain, with more complex interactions, benefited from a larger memory (k=6), while the Airline domain achieved optimal results with a smaller memory (k=2). This indicates that an appropriately sized memory module, as dynamically determined by FAMA, contributes to improved performance.

#### 4.4. Efficiency and Resource Utilization

- **Token Overhead and Latency:** An analysis of efficiency showed that methods introducing additional reasoning or helper agents incurred higher runtime and token costs. IRMA, which indiscriminately uses all agents, exhibited substantial overhead (50-58%) and increased task completion latency (e.g., 149.8s for retail, 111.6s for airline with Qwen3-32B). FAMA, with its more efficient design through selective agent activation, incurred lower overhead (approximately 30%) and reduced latency compared to IRMA, resulting in fewer overflow-induced errors.
- **Thinking Variants Limitations:** Reasoning-enabled ("thinking") variants of Qwen3 models, while sometimes improving baseline methods, consumed substantial token budgets, leading to frequent context window overflows and performance degradation in multi-turn settings. FAMA achieved stronger and more stable performance without relying on these expensive internal reasoning steps.

#### 4.5. Comparison with Self-Reflection

- FAMA consistently outperformed the Self-Reflection (SR) baseline across multiple models in τ-bench. This suggests that FAMA's structured failure analysis and targeted mitigation strategy are more effective than generic reflection and revision mechanisms in the evaluated settings.

#### 4.6. Qualitative Example

- A qualitative example from ACEBench illustrated how FAMA, by incorporating Planner and Verifier agents, successfully navigated a complex message-sending task involving Wi-Fi issues, inbox capacity, and recipient name ambiguity, where a baseline agent repeatedly failed in a loop. The Verifier agent played a key role in identifying and correcting issues at each step.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The FAMA framework presents a significant contribution to the field of LLM-based autonomous agents, particularly concerning the practical deployment of open-source models in interactive tool-use environments.

**Enhanced Reliability and Efficiency for Open-Source LLMs:** The most direct significance of FAMA is its demonstrated ability to substantially improve the performance of open-source LLMs in complex, multi-turn conversational tasks requiring tool use. By achieving performance gains of up to 27% across various benchmarks, FAMA offers a pathway to making these more accessible and resource-efficient models viable for real-world applications where frontier models might be cost-prohibitive or raise privacy concerns. The framework addresses the inherent limitations of smaller models, such as limited context windows and inference budgets, by optimizing context injection.

**Novel Design Principle: Failure-Aware Agent Composition:** FAMA introduces "failure-aware agent composition" as a valuable design principle. Instead of applying static prompting or indiscriminately activating all available helper agents, FAMA dynamically identifies the dominant failure modes and activates only a minimal, task-relevant subset of specialized agents. This targeted error mitigation avoids the overhead and potential performance degradation associated with excessive context, leading to more dependable and efficient agents.

**Generalizable and Adaptive Framework:** The framework's ability to generalize across diverse domains (retail, airline, telehealth, telecom) and benchmarks (τ-bench, τ-trait, ACEBench) without extensive manual redesign is a key impact. This adaptability suggests that FAMA can be a robust solution for a variety of conversational agent deployments, as it dynamically tailors its approach based on the observed weaknesses of the underlying LLM agent and the task context.

**Addressing Systematic Errors:** FAMA explicitly targets systematic error patterns observed in practice, which often go unaddressed by methods focused solely on learning correct behavior. By categorizing errors and leveraging specialized agents for mitigation, FAMA provides a structured mechanism for improving agent robustness against common failure types like domain policy violations, contextual misinterpretations, and incomplete task fulfillment.

**Potential for Broader Applications:** While the current study focuses on structured conversational environments, the core principles of FAMA—diagnosing failures and dynamically composing corrective mechanisms—could potentially be extended to other complex agentic settings, including embodied agents, multi-modal systems, or open-ended environments. The modularity of FAMA also allows for the easy integration of new specialized agents as new failure patterns emerge.

**Future Directions:** The paper also identifies limitations that suggest future avenues for impact, such as automating the discovery or synthesis of new specialized agents beyond a predefined pool, and extending the framework to less well-defined failure taxonomies in more diverse interactive environments. Overcoming these limitations would further broaden FAMA's applicability and impact on developing truly autonomous and resilient AI agents.
