# TGRPO :Fine-tuning Vision-Language-Action Model via Trajectory-wise Group Relative Policy Optimization

> [!summary] Summary
> The TGRPO framework introduces a critic-free online reinforcement learning approach to fine-tune Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, utilizing Large Language Models for automated dense reward generation and a dual-level group relative policy optimization. This method achieved an 80.7% average success rate on the LIBERO benchmark, notably improving long-horizon task performance by 8.1% compared to standard supervised fine-tuning.

## Problem

- Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, typically fine-tuned with Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), often struggle with learning from failures and adapting to out-of-distribution scenarios due to an "action memorization" approach.
- Applying reinforcement learning (RL) to complex, long-horizon robotic tasks is challenged by sparse rewards, high variance and instability during optimization, and the computational burden of training separate value networks (critics).

## Method

- Leverages ==Large Language Models== (LLMs) to automatically decompose natural language task descriptions into sub-stages and construct fine-grained, ==multi-stage dense reward functions== using key object and end-effector pose information.
- Proposes **Trajectory-wise Group Relative Policy Optimization** (**TGRPO**), a ==critic-free== online RL framework that samples parallel trajectories and computes ==dual-level relative advantages== (step-level and trajectory-level) for robust policy updates.
- Fuses the step-level and trajectory-level advantages to provide a comprehensive optimization signal, updating the VLA model's ==LoRA== weights without requiring a separate value network.

## Results

- TGRPO achieved an average success rate of **80.7%** across the LIBERO benchmark, outperforming OpenVLA-SFT (**76.5%**) and other RL-based fine-tuning methods like DPO (**76.2%**) and GRAPE (**80.2%**).
- The framework demonstrated particularly strong performance on the challenging LIBERO-Long suite, securing a **59.2%** success rate, an **8.1%** improvement over SFT, highlighting its efficacy for complex, long-horizon tasks.
- Ablation studies confirmed that both trajectory-level and step-level relative advantages are essential for TGRPO's stability, with optimal performance achieved using specific advantage weightings (α1=0.3,α2=0.7\alpha_1=0.3, \alpha_2=0.7α1=0.3,α2=0.7) and a group size of **4**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Large Language Models can effectively automate the creation of dense, multi-stage reward functions for complex robotic tasks, mitigating the sparse reward problem without extensive manual engineering.
> - Combining relative advantages at both the trajectory and step levels provides a stable and comprehensive optimization signal, effectively balancing global task progress with local action contributions in long-horizon tasks.
> - Critic-free relative advantage estimation, derived from parallel trajectory sampling, offers a robust and efficient alternative to traditional value-based methods, reducing variance and simplifying the reinforcement learning pipeline.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research sits at the intersection of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, reinforcement learning (RL), and large language models (LLMs) applied to robotics. The broader research landscape is rapidly evolving towards creating general-purpose robotic policies capable of performing diverse tasks in varied environments.

**Evolution of VLA Models:** Initially, VLA models (e.g., PaLM-E, RT-2, OpenVLA, $\pi_0$) emerged as a promising direction for achieving general robotic policies. These models leverage large-scale pre-training on vast datasets of visual, linguistic, and action data to build robust representations and generalize across scenarios. They typically come in two main architectures: hierarchical models that decompose tasks into high-level plans and low-level actions, and end-to-end models that directly map observations and language instructions to actions. OpenVLA, the base model for this study, falls into the latter category, often using autoregressive LLMs as backbones. While these models have shown impressive cross-scenario generalization, their primary limitation stems from their training paradigm: they are largely trained via Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) on human-provided *successful* demonstrations. This "action memorization" approach makes them brittle when encountering out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios or requiring autonomous exploration and self-correction, as they lack the ability to learn from failures.

**Role of Reinforcement Learning in Robotics:** Reinforcement learning is a natural fit for addressing the limitations of SFT by providing a closed-loop optimization framework where agents learn through active trial-and-error. RL allows policies to adapt and optimize behavior directly through interaction with the environment, leading to more autonomous and robust capabilities. Policy gradient methods like PPO and SAC, and value-based approaches, have seen success in various robotic tasks. However, applying RL to complex, long-horizon robotic tasks is challenging due to:
1. **Sparse Rewards:** Real-world robotic tasks often have binary success/failure rewards, making it difficult for agents to receive sufficient feedback for learning.
2. **High Variance and Instability:** Long-horizon tasks involve multiple stages and diverse dynamics, leading to inconsistent reward scaling and amplified gradient variance, which can destabilize training and trap policies in suboptimal local minima.
3. **Credit Assignment:** Determining which specific actions contributed to a success or failure, especially over long sequences, is a hard problem.
4. **Need for Value Networks:** Many popular RL algorithms (e.g., PPO) require training a separate value function (critic) to estimate state values, adding complexity and potentially hindering efficiency.

**Recent Trends in Fine-tuning VLAs with RL:** Recognizing these challenges, recent research has started exploring RL-based fine-tuning for VLA models. Approaches like ConRFT combine offline behavior cloning, Q-learning, and consistency training, often requiring human intervention for exploration. iRe-VLA iterates between RL and supervised learning. GRAPE implicitly models rewards for successful/failed trials and uses large vision-language models for guidance in multi-stage tasks. TGRPO distinguishes itself from these by focusing on an *online*, *critic-free* RL framework that emphasizes *automatic dense reward construction* via LLMs and *novel group-based relative advantage estimation* to provide stable and efficient optimization, particularly for long-horizon tasks, with reduced reliance on excessive human intervention or large offline datasets. This work pushes the boundaries of how VLA models can acquire adaptive skills by learning directly from environmental interactions, bridging the gap between pre-trained general policies and robust task-specific performance in dynamic robotic scenarios.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations behind the TGRPO framework are rooted in addressing the inherent limitations of current Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models and the challenges associated with applying standard Reinforcement Learning (RL) techniques to complex robotic manipulation tasks.

1. **Overcome Limitations of Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) for VLA Models:** VLA models, despite their strong cross-scenario generalization after large-scale pre-training, are typically fine-tuned using successful human demonstrations. This SFT paradigm limits their ability to:
 - **Learn from failures:** They lack a mechanism to correct mistakes or adapt when faced with unexpected outcomes.
 - **Handle Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Scenarios:** When confronted with environments or tasks slightly different from the training data, their "action memorization" approach leads to poor performance.
 - **Exhibit Autonomous Exploration and Self-Correction:** They struggle to autonomously explore the environment or self-correct errors during execution. The motivation is to move beyond simple action memorization towards policies that can truly align actions with environmental context.

2. **Mitigate Challenges of Reinforcement Learning (RL) in Long-Horizon Robotic Tasks:** While RL offers a powerful framework for learning from interaction, its application to robotics, especially long-horizon multi-stage tasks, faces significant hurdles:
 - **Sparse Rewards:** Real-world robotic tasks often provide only binary success/failure signals, making effective credit assignment and exploration extremely difficult. Hand-crafting dense rewards is labor-intensive, requires domain expertise, and can introduce bias.
 - **High Variance and Unstable Optimization:** Long-horizon tasks involve complex sequential decisions and diverse subgoals. Inconsistent reward scaling across stages can amplify gradient variance, slow convergence, destabilize training, and lead to suboptimal policies (e.g., overfitting to intermediate subgoals).
 - **Need for Value Networks:** Many state-of-the-art policy gradient methods (like PPO) require training a critic (value network) alongside the policy, adding computational overhead and complexity to the learning process.

3. **Propose a Novel, Critic-Free Online RL Framework for VLA Fine-Tuning:** The overarching objective is to develop an online RL-based training framework that is adaptive, effective, and stable for post-training VLA models without relying on a value network. This includes:
 - **Automated Dense Reward Construction:** To solve the sparse reward problem, leverage large language models (LLMs) to automatically parse natural language task descriptions, decompose tasks into stages, and construct fine-grained, multi-stage dense reward functions based on environmental observations (key object positions, end-effector poses).
 - **Enhanced Credit Assignment and Variance Reduction:** Design a group-based policy optimization strategy that samples multiple trajectories in parallel and computes *relative advantages*. This strategy integrates both *trajectory-level* (global task performance) and *step-level* (local action contributions) advantage estimations to provide stable optimization signals. This dual-level approach aims to capture both global task progress and fine-grained corrective signals, addressing the challenge of balancing global and local optimization in long-horizon tasks.
 - **Improve Generalization and Robustness:** Enable VLA agents to actively explore, learn from failures, and adapt their policies to dynamic and OOD scenarios, ultimately leading to higher success rates and more robust performance on complex robotic manipulation tasks.

In essence, TGRPO aims to bridge the gap between the pre-training generalization of VLAs and the interactive adaptability of RL, specifically engineering solutions for reward sparsity and training instability in the robotic domain.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

TGRPO (Trajectory-wise Group Relative Policy Optimization) proposes an online reinforcement learning framework designed to fine-tune Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models without needing a separate value network (critic). The methodology is structured around three core components: a multi-stage reward design, a novel dual-level group-based relative policy optimization, and a practical simulation framework.

#### 3.1. Overall Framework

The TGRPO framework operates by taking natural language instructions and multimodal observations as input to an OpenVLA model. This VLA model generates action tokens to control the robot. The core idea is to sample trajectories in parallel across multiple environments, evaluate them using a specially designed multi-stage reward function, group these trajectories for robust advantage estimation at both step and trajectory levels, and then fuse these estimates to update the VLA policy effectively. The entire process is an online RL loop, iteratively improving the policy through interaction.

#### 3.2. Multi-Stage Reward Design

Recognizing the limitations of sparse, terminal rewards in long-horizon robotic tasks, TGRPO introduces a dense, multi-stage reward function.
- **LLM for Task Decomposition:** A key innovation is the use of a large language model (LLM), specifically Claude 3.7 Sonnet, to automatically decompose natural language task descriptions into sub-stages (e.g., "approaching," "grasping," "moving," "placing"). This eliminates the need for manual reward shaping, which is often tedious and prone to bias.
- **Spatial and Pose-based Rewards:** For each sub-stage, the LLM defines rewards using two types of information:
 - **Key Object Information ($f_1$):** Based on the spatial positions of task-relevant objects ($P_{object}(t)$) obtained from the simulation environment. This provides stage-wise progress feedback.
 - **End-Effector Pose Information ($f_2$):** Based on the Euclidean distance between the robot's current end-effector pose ($s_t$) and a set of reference key poses ($P_{pose}^k$) extracted from successful demonstrations. This "shaping signal" encourages alignment with expert behaviors and accelerates task completion.
- **Combined Reward:** The reward at each timestep $t$ is a sum of these two components: $R_t = f_1(P_{object}(t), P_{pose}^k) + f_2(P_{pose}^k, s_t)$. This fine-grained feedback helps in better credit assignment compared to binary rewards.

#### 3.3. Trajectory-wise Group Relative Policy Optimization (TGRPO Core)

The central algorithm, TGRPO, refines policy optimization by employing a novel group-based strategy that estimates advantages without a critic, combining both global and local optimization signals.
- **Parallel Trajectory Sampling:** $N$ trajectories of equal length $M$ are sampled in parallel from environments initialized with identical states. This parallel sampling is crucial for the group-based comparisons.
- **Critic-Free Advantage Estimation:** Instead of a value network, TGRPO uses *relative* advantages computed within a group of trajectories. This inherently reduces variance by comparing performance within a batch rather than against an absolute baseline.
- **Step-level Relative Advantage ($A_{i,t}$):** For each timestep $t$, the reward $R_{i,t}$ of trajectory $i$ is compared to the average reward of that same timestep across all $N$ parallel trajectories ($\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N R_{i,t}$). This difference is normalized by the standard deviation of rewards at timestep $t$ across the group. This captures local action effectiveness.
- **Trajectory-level Relative Advantage ($A_i$):** The total cumulative reward $R_i$ for an entire trajectory $i$ is compared to the average total cumulative reward across all $N$ trajectories ($\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N R_i$). This difference is also normalized. This captures global task success.
- **Fused Advantage ($Adv_{i,t}$):** The final advantage for a given step $t$ in trajectory $i$ is a weighted sum of the step-level and trajectory-level advantages: $Adv_{i,t} = \alpha_1 A_{i,t} + \alpha_2 A_i$. Empirically, $\alpha_1=0.3$ and $\alpha_2=0.7$ were found to be optimal, indicating a stronger emphasis on global trajectory performance for stability.
- **Policy Optimization Objective:** The policy $\pi_\theta$ is updated using a clipped surrogate objective, similar to PPO, but leveraging the proposed dual-level relative advantage ($Adv_{i,t}$). An importance sampling ratio $\rho_{i,t}$ compares the new policy's action probability to the old policy's. A KL divergence regularization term is also included to prevent the new policy from diverging too far from a reference policy, ensuring stable updates.

#### 3.4. VLA Post-training Framework in Simulation

The practical implementation involves:
1. **Parallel Environment Execution:** The VLA model controls robots in multiple (e.g., four) parallel simulation environments, all starting from identical states for the same task.
2. **Synchronized Trajectory Termination:** All trajectories are sampled step-by-step until either one environment completes the task or all reach a maximum step limit, ensuring all collected trajectories have the same length.
3. **Reward Computation:** The LLM-designed multi-stage reward function provides dense, step-wise rewards based on object positions and robot states from each environment.
4. **Group-based Advantage Calculation:** The collected trajectories' rewards and action log probabilities are used to compute step-level and trajectory-level relative advantages as described above.
5. **Policy Update:** The fused advantages drive the optimization objective, updating the VLA model's parameters (specifically, LoRA weights for OpenVLA) using an AdamW optimizer.

This integrated approach aims to provide robust, efficient, and stable online fine-tuning for VLA models, allowing them to learn from interactive trials and errors in complex, long-horizon robotic tasks.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted on the LIBERO benchmark demonstrate the efficacy and superior performance of the TGRPO framework in fine-tuning Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models for robotic manipulation tasks.

#### 4.1. Evaluation on LIBERO Benchmark

- **Benchmark Details:** TGRPO was evaluated on the LIBERO benchmark, which consists of four suites (Spatial, Object, Goal, Long) each with 10 tasks, designed to test generalization across various aspects (locations, object categories, goals, and long-horizon complexity).
- **Base Model and Training:** OpenVLA was used as the base VLA model, fine-tuned with LoRA. RL updates were performed using AdamW with a fixed learning rate.
- **Baselines:** The performance of TGRPO was compared against several strong baselines:
 - **OpenVLA-SFT:** The standard Supervised Fine-Tuning method, representing the baseline for VLA post-training.
 - **OpenVLA-DPO:** Another RL-based fine-tuning method (Direct Preference Optimization).
 - **GRAPE:** A recent RL-based VLA fine-tuning method that implicitly models rewards and uses LVM guidance.
 - **Octo:** An independent model not based on VLA post-training, included for broader comparison.

#### 4.2. Superior Overall Performance

- **Average Success Rate:** TGRPO achieved an average success rate of **80.7%** across all four LIBERO task suites. This significantly outperforms the standard SFT fine-tuning (76.5%) by 4.2%.
- **Outperforming RL Baselines:** TGRPO also surpassed other representative RL-based post-training methods, including DPO (76.2%) and GRAPE (80.2%), demonstrating its effectiveness in the online RL fine-tuning paradigm.
- **Consistent Leadership:** As shown in Table 1 and Fig. 3, TGRPO consistently ranked first or second in success rate across all individual LIBERO suites.

#### 4.3. Strong Performance on Long-Horizon Tasks

- **LIBERO-Long Suite:** A notable finding is TGRPO's exceptional performance on the LIBERO-Long suite, which involves complex, multi-stage, long-horizon tasks. Here, TGRPO achieved a success rate of **59.2%**, an impressive **8.1% higher than SFT** (51.1%). This result specifically highlights the strength of TGRPO's step-wise reward assignment and the integration of dual-level (trajectory and step) advantage signals in enabling VLA models to effectively explore and learn in challenging sequential decision-making scenarios.

#### 4.4. Ablation Study

An ablation study on the LIBERO-Object suite validated the necessity of TGRPO's dual-level advantage estimation:
- **TGRPO (Ours):** Achieved 92.2% success rate.
- **TGRPO w/o Trajectory-level Adv.:** Performance dropped significantly to 80.2%. This indicates that global signals from trajectory-level advantages are crucial for capturing overall task progress and stabilizing learning.
- **TGRPO w/o Step-level Adv.:** Performance decreased to 86.8%. This shows that fine-grained, step-wise feedback is critical for effective credit assignment and local optimization within long-horizon tasks.
- **Conclusion:** Both trajectory-level and step-level advantages are indispensable for the stability and effectiveness of TGRPO, demonstrating that their combined use provides a more comprehensive and robust optimization signal.

#### 4.5. Hyperparameter Analysis of Advantage Weights ($\alpha_1, \alpha_2$)

Experiments on the LIBERO-Goal suite examined the impact of weighting coefficients for the fused advantage ($Adv_{i,t} = \alpha_1 A_{i,t} + \alpha_2 A_i$).
- **Optimal Configuration:** The best performance (81.0% average success rate) was achieved with **$\alpha_1 = 0.3$ and $\alpha_2 = 0.7$**.
- **Interpretation:** This suggests that for long-horizon tasks, trajectory-level advantages (global signals) play a slightly more dominant role in stabilizing policy learning, while step-level advantages provide essential detailed guidance. An imbalance (e.g., too small $\alpha_1$ underutilizing step-level, or too large $\alpha_1$ overshadowing trajectory-level) leads to suboptimal performance.

#### 4.6. Impact of Group Size ($N$)

Analysis of group size (number of parallel trajectories) on LIBERO-Goal:
- **N=2:** Showed poor performance (76.2%), indicating that insufficient trajectories in a group lead to unstable relative comparisons.
- **N $\ge$ 4:** Performance differences became minor. **N=4** achieved the best balance of performance (81.0%) and efficiency, slightly outperforming larger group sizes while requiring fewer resources.
- **Conclusion:** A moderate group size of 4 is optimal, balancing the need for reliable relative comparisons with computational efficiency.

In summary, the results convincingly demonstrate that TGRPO's novel design, encompassing LLM-generated dense rewards and a dual-level group relative policy optimization, allows VLA models to learn more effectively from interaction, particularly in complex, long-horizon robotic manipulation tasks, surpassing existing fine-tuning and RL-based methods.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The TGRPO framework presents several significant contributions and holds substantial potential impact on the field of robotic learning, particularly in enhancing the capabilities of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models.

#### 5.1. Addressing Fundamental Limitations of VLA Models

- **Beyond "Action Memorization":** TGRPO directly tackles the core limitation of VLA models trained primarily through Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) – their inability to learn from failures and adapt to out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios. By enabling VLA agents to actively explore and optimize policies through online interaction, it moves robotic learning beyond mere "action memorization" towards true environmental alignment and self-correction. This is a crucial step for deploying robots in dynamic and unpredictable real-world settings.
- **Adaptive Policy Learning:** The framework fosters adaptive policy learning, allowing robots to refine their skills in response to real-time feedback. This is vital for complex tasks where perfect demonstrations are scarce or impossible, and where robustness to minor environmental variations is essential.

#### 5.2. Advancing Reinforcement Learning in Robotics

- **Automated Dense Reward Engineering:** One of the most significant challenges in applying RL to robotics is the sparse reward problem and the labor-intensive nature of hand-crafting dense reward functions. TGRPO's novel use of Large Language Models (LLMs) to automatically decompose tasks and construct multi-stage dense rewards is a game-changer. This substantially reduces the need for human domain expertise and manual engineering, making RL more accessible and scalable for complex robotic tasks.
- **Stable and Efficient Critic-Free Optimization:** The proposed dual-level (trajectory-level and step-level) group relative advantage estimation provides a robust and efficient mechanism for policy optimization. By eliminating the need for a separate value network (critic), TGRPO simplifies the training pipeline, potentially reducing computational overhead and improving training stability by avoiding issues related to value function estimation errors. The relative comparison within groups effectively reduces variance, a common pitfall in RL.
- **Improved Credit Assignment for Long-Horizon Tasks:** The integration of both global (trajectory-level) and local (step-level) optimization signals is particularly impactful for long-horizon, multi-stage robotic tasks. This allows the policy to simultaneously understand overall task progress and identify which specific actions contribute positively or negatively, leading to more effective credit assignment and faster learning in complex sequences.

#### 5.3. Practical Implications and Future Directions

- **Enhanced Performance on Complex Tasks:** The demonstrated state-of-the-art performance on the challenging LIBERO benchmark, especially for long-horizon tasks (LIBERO-Long), underscores TGRPO's practical utility. This indicates that the method can enable VLA-controlled robots to perform more intricate and sequential manipulation tasks with higher reliability.
- **Scalability for General Robot Learning:** By reducing reliance on human-engineered rewards and providing a stable online learning framework, TGRPO contributes to the development of more scalable solutions for general robot learning. This is crucial for the vision of foundation models in robotics, where pre-trained models can be quickly adapted to new tasks.
- **Path Towards Real-World Deployment:** While experiments are currently in simulation, the principles of TGRPO – learning from interaction, automated reward generation, and stable policy updates – are directly applicable to real-world robotics. The authors explicitly mention extending TGRPO to real-world and multi-task settings as future work, which would represent a significant step towards more autonomous and versatile robotic systems. The ability to fine-tune VLA models adaptively could accelerate robot deployment in various industries and home environments.

In conclusion, TGRPO offers a significant step forward in the fine-tuning of VLA models, effectively bridging the gap between impressive pre-trained generalization and robust, adaptive performance in interactive robotic tasks. Its innovations in automated reward design and critic-free, dual-level relative advantage estimation provide a powerful and efficient framework for developing the next generation of intelligent robotic agents.
