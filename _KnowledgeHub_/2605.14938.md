# Octopus: History-Free Gradient Orthogonalization for Continual Learning in Multimodal Large Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Octopus introduces a two-stage continual learning framework for Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs), employing History-Free Gradient Orthogonalization (HiFGO) to prevent catastrophic forgetting. This approach achieves state-of-the-art performance on the UCIT benchmark, improving average accuracy by 2.14% over prior methods, while operating entirely without historical data.

## Problem

- Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) suffer from catastrophic forgetting when continually learning new tasks.
- Existing regularization-based methods, particularly parameter orthogonality, are often insufficient to fully prevent inter-task interference.
- Prior gradient-based orthogonalization methods for continual learning typically require access to historical data, posing privacy and storage challenges.

## Method

- **History-Free Gradient Orthogonalization (HiFGO)** identifies inter-task interference by computing ==gradients of previous task parameters on the *current* task's data distribution== (GPWC).
- A ==two-stage finetuning strategy== first allows unconstrained adaptation to new tasks for plasticity, then applies constrained refinement with HiFGO to maintain stability.
- ==GPWC== is approximated using only the most recent task's parameters to maintain a constant computational cost, avoiding linear growth with the number of tasks.

## Results

- Octopus achieves state-of-the-art results on the UCIT benchmark, improving Average accuracy by **+2.14%** and Last accuracy by **+6.82%** over previous best methods.
- The framework also outperforms previous state-of-the-art on the CoIN benchmark by **+1.99%** in Average accuracy and **+2.29%** in Last accuracy.
- Ablation studies demonstrate that HiFGO provides superior resistance to forgetting, yielding a Backward Transfer (BWT) of **+0.41**, and the two-stage strategy boosts Immediate and Last metrics by over **8%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Gradient orthogonality between new task updates and previous task loss gradients is theoretically more critical for preventing catastrophic forgetting than parameter orthogonality.
> - Gradients of Previous parameters Within Current data distribution (GPWC) can effectively serve as a history-free proxy for past task gradients, capturing inter-task interference and high-curvature Hessian directions.
> - A two-stage fine-tuning process, separating unconstrained adaptation from constrained refinement, effectively balances model plasticity for new tasks and stability for retaining prior knowledge.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of artificial intelligence, particularly with the advent of large language models (LLMs) and multimodal large language models (MLLMs), faces the challenge of continually acquiring new knowledge without "catastrophic forgetting" of previously learned information. Continual learning (CL) addresses this problem, aiming to enable models to sequentially learn across multiple tasks while retaining robust performance on prior tasks.

Current continual learning approaches for MLLMs generally fall into three categories:
- **Architecture-based methods:** These approaches often introduce task-specific modules, such as LoRA modules, to store distinct task information. While offering some degree of task isolation, they can incur additional computational overhead during inference and may exhibit limitations in generalizing to unseen tasks.
- **Rehearsal-based methods:** These methods store historical data or activations in a memory buffer and replay them during training on new tasks to mitigate forgetting. However, they raise concerns regarding data privacy, require substantial storage, and may be impractical in scenarios where historical data access is restricted.
- **Regularization-based methods:** These strategies aim to constrain parameter updates to minimize interference with knowledge acquired from previous tasks, typically by adding penalty terms to the loss function. While appealing for their history-free nature, conventional regularization methods, particularly those enforcing parameter orthogonality, have been shown to be insufficient to fully prevent parameter interference. Prior work has suggested the importance of gradient orthogonality, but existing gradient-based methods typically rely on historical data.

This research positions itself within the regularization-based category, specifically addressing the limitations of existing methods by proposing a history-free approach to enforce gradient orthogonality. It seeks to overcome the reliance on historical data that has constrained previous gradient-based methods, while providing a more effective regularization strategy than parameter orthogonality. Furthermore, it aims to balance the trade-off between model plasticity (ability to learn new tasks) and stability (retention of old knowledge), a fundamental challenge in continual learning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to develop a robust and efficient continual learning framework for Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) that effectively mitigates catastrophic forgetting without relying on historical task data. The motivation stems from several identified limitations of existing continual learning strategies:

- **Insufficient Catastrophic Forgetting Mitigation in Regularization-based Methods:** The paper highlights that while regularization-based methods are desirable due to their independence from historical data, conventional approaches primarily focus on enforcing parameter orthogonality (e.g., OLoRA). The authors theoretically demonstrate that parameter orthogonality alone is insufficient to fully prevent parameter interference. Their analysis indicates that gradient orthogonality is a more critical condition for ensuring that learning a new task does not degrade performance on previous tasks. Specifically, the lossless condition for a new task not degrading a prior task simplifies to the inner product of the new task's parameter update and the previous task's gradient being zero (Equation 6). This theoretical insight motivates the shift from parameter-level to gradient-level orthogonalization.
- **Reliance on Historical Data for Gradient-based Orthogonalization:** Existing gradient-orthogonality methods (e.g., OGD) require access to historical task data to compute previous task gradients. This requirement conflicts with real-world constraints such as data privacy regulations and storage limitations, hindering their practical applicability. A key motivation is to develop a gradient-orthogonalization method that is entirely history-free.
- **Degradation of Fine-tuning Performance due to Regularization:** The authors observe that strong regularization constraints, while intended to prevent forgetting, can overly restrict the model's parameter search space, leading to suboptimal performance on the current task. This competition between regularization objectives and original task objectives, a known issue in multi-task learning, can compromise model plasticity and the overall efficacy of continual learning. The motivation here is to devise a strategy that allows the model to fully adapt to new tasks while still enforcing regularization effectively.
- **Computational and Generalization Overhead of Architecture-based and Rehearsal-based Methods:** Architecture-based methods, which allocate task-specific modules, tend to increase model complexity and may generalize poorly to unseen tasks. Rehearsal-based methods, while often effective, incur significant storage overhead and privacy concerns due to storing historical data. The research is motivated to provide an inference-efficient and rehearsal-free solution that surpasses these limitations.

In response to these motivations, the paper proposes "Octopus," aiming to achieve a principled balance between plasticity and stability through a novel history-free gradient orthogonalization approach and a two-stage finetuning strategy.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Octopus is presented as a two-stage continual learning framework built upon History-Free Gradient Orthogonalization (HiFGO) for MLLMs. The methodology addresses the core challenges of catastrophic forgetting and the plasticity-stability trade-off.

#### 3.1. Analysis on Parameter Interference

The theoretical foundation of Octopus rests on an analysis of parameter interference. Considering two sequential tasks, T1 and T2, the objective is to ensure that training on T2 does not degrade the performance achieved on T1. Let $W_0$ be the pretrained parameters, $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$ be the LoRA parameters for Task 1 and Task 2 respectively, and $L_{D_1}(\theta)$ be the loss function for Task 1 on its dataset $D_1$. By defining $\theta'_1 = W_0 + \theta_1$, the lossless condition for Task 1 is $L_{D_1}(\theta'_1) = L_{D_1}(\theta'_1 + \theta_2)$.
A Taylor expansion of $L_{D_1}(\theta'_1 + \theta_2)$ around $\theta'_1$ shows that, by neglecting higher-order terms (justified by LoRA weights being small), the lossless condition approximately reduces to:
$\langle \frac{\partial L_{D_1}(\theta'_1)}{\partial \theta'_1}, \theta_2 \rangle = 0$ (Equation 6).
This implies that the LoRA parameters for Task 2 ($\theta_2$) should be orthogonal to the gradient of Task 1's loss with respect to its combined parameters ($\theta'_1$). The paper argues this gradient orthogonality is more principled than parameter orthogonality (e.g., OLoRA) because $\frac{\partial L_{D_1}(\theta'_1)}{\partial \theta'_1}$ accurately captures the sensitivity of Task 1's performance, whereas $\theta_1$ represents a trajectory rather than an instantaneous gradient.

#### 3.2. History-Free Gradient Orthogonalization (HiFGO)

To overcome the reliance on historical data for gradient computation, HiFGO introduces the concept of **Gradients of Previous parameters Within Current data distribution (GPWC)**.
The key idea is to characterize inter-task interference by evaluating the gradients of previously learned parameters (which have converged to local optima for their respective tasks) on the *current* task's data distribution. This is motivated by the understanding that a model retaining previous knowledge will implicitly encode it in its latest parameters. GPWC thus reflects both reusable knowledge (shared representational subspace) and optimization conflicts (potential for current task updates to degrade previous tasks).

Specifically, when fine-tuning Task $i$, an orthogonality loss term $L_{orth}(\theta_i)$ is incorporated:
$L_{orth}(\theta_i) = \sum_{j=1}^{i-1} \left( \frac{\partial L_{D_i}(\theta'_j)}{\partial \theta'_j} \right)^T \theta_i$ (Equation 7),
where $\theta'_j = W_0 + \sum_{m=1}^{j} \theta_m$ represents the merged LoRA weights after Task $j$, and $L_{D_i}(\theta'_j)$ is the loss of parameters $\theta'_j$ on the *current* task's dataset $D_i$. This ensures the current task's parameter updates ($\theta_i$) are orthogonal to the gradients of previous task parameters when evaluated on the current task's data.

**Historical Task Proxy Approximation:** To mitigate the computational cost that grows linearly with the number of tasks in Equation 7, an approximation is introduced. Instead of summing over all previous tasks, the parameters $\theta'_{i-1}$ from the most recent task are used as a proxy for the entire task history, reducing the computational cost from $O(t)$ to $O(1)$:
$L'_{orth}(\theta_i) = \left( \frac{\partial L_{D_i}(\theta'_{i-1})}{\partial \theta'_{i-1}} \right)^T \theta_i$ (Equation 8).

A theoretical analysis in the supplementary material demonstrates that GPWC captures the projection of the adaptation direction towards a new task onto the high-curvature directions of the previous task's Hessian, which are the directions most sensitive to performance degradation.

#### 3.3. Two-stage Finetuning Strategy

The paper addresses the observed degradation in fine-tuning performance caused by strong regularization constraints, which can limit the effective parameter search space and trap optimization in suboptimal local minima. A two-stage finetuning strategy is proposed:

- **Stage 1 (Unconstrained Adaptation):** The model first adapts to the current task $D_i$ without any regularization constraints. This allows the model to freely explore the parameter space and approach a local optimum for the current task, maximizing plasticity. The loss function is a standard cross-entropy loss:
 $L_1 = \frac{1}{|D_i|} \sum_{(x_k,y_k) \in D_i} L_{ce}(f_{\theta'_{i,1}}(x_k), y_k)$ (Equation 9),
 where $\theta'_{i,1} = W_0 + \theta_{i,1}$, and $\theta_{i,1}$ is initialized from the previous task's converged parameters or from scratch for the first task.

- **Stage 2 (Constrained Refinement):** After Stage 1, the LoRA parameters $\theta_{i,2}$ are initialized from the converged $\theta_{i,1}$. In this stage, both the current task's fine-tuning objective and the regularization terms (HiFGO and an L2-norm regularization) are activated. This refinement stage ensures that parameter updates do not interfere with prior knowledge while staying close to the optimal solution manifold identified in Stage 1. The loss function is:
 $L_2 = \frac{1}{|D_i|} \sum_{(x_k,y_k) \in D_i} (L_{ce}(f_{\theta'_{i,2}}(x_k), y_k) + \lambda_1 L_{orth}(\theta_{i,2}) + \lambda_2 L_{norm}(\theta_{i,2}))$ (Equation 10).
 Here, $\theta'_{i,2} = W_0 + \sum_{m=1}^{i} \theta_{m,2}$, $L_{norm}(\theta_i)$ is an L2-regularization term, and $\lambda_1, \lambda_2$ are hyperparameters controlling the regularization strength. This stage aims to achieve stability while preserving the adaptation capacity.

The overall algorithmic procedure (Algorithm 1) outlines this sequential two-stage process for each task, ultimately merging the converged $\theta_{i,2}$ parameters with the pretrained weights.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper presents extensive experiments on two continual learning benchmarks for MLLMs: UCIT and CoIN, demonstrating the effectiveness of the proposed Octopus framework.

#### 4.1. Performance on UCIT Benchmark

On the UCIT benchmark, Octopus establishes state-of-the-art (SOTA) performance, surpassing prior SOTA methods by substantial margins.
- **Overall Performance:** Octopus outperforms the best previous methods by 2.14% in terms of Average accuracy (Avg) and 6.82% in terms of Last accuracy. The Avg metric reflects mean accuracy across all tasks throughout training, while Last measures average accuracy over all tasks after sequentially learning the entire task stream.
- **Comparison with Regularization-based Methods:** Octopus demonstrates significant improvements over existing regularization-based methods such as EWC, LwF, and OLoRA. Specifically, it achieves 6.54% and 12.65% higher performance than OLoRA in Avg and Last metrics, respectively. This outcome is attributed to HiFGO's more effective weight disentanglement compared to parameter-space orthogonalization.
- **Comparison with Architecture-based Methods:** Octopus also significantly outperforms MoE-based approaches like MoELoRA and HiDe-LLaVA. This indicates superior mitigation of inter-task parameter interference, alongside maintaining high inference efficiency, as MoE-based methods incur additional inference and storage costs.
- **Comparison with Rehearsal-based Methods:** Notably, Octopus even surpasses the vanilla rehearsal baseline, which explicitly stores and reuses past data. This indicates that Octopus achieves a more favorable plasticity–stability trade-off without the need for memory buffers, addressing privacy and storage concerns. The ArXivQA Last metric for Octopus exceeding that of Multi-task further suggests positive inter-task transfer.

#### 4.2. Performance on CoIN Benchmark (Supplementary Material)

Further experiments on the CoIN benchmark (detailed in the supplementary material) corroborate the strong performance of Octopus:
- **Overall Performance:** Octopus achieves improvements of 1.99% in Avg and 2.29% in Last accuracy over the previous SOTA on CoIN.
- **Robustness Across Diverse Tasks:** The consistent outperformance on CoIN, which covers eight distinct task categories with diverse data and task formats, demonstrates the robustness and broad applicability of HiFGO.

#### 4.3. Model Analysis (Ablation Studies)

Detailed ablation studies analyze the contributions of key components:

- **Effectiveness of History-Free Gradient Orthogonalization (HiFGO):**
 - HiFGO exhibits substantially stronger resistance to forgetting compared to parameter orthogonalization. It yields a Backward Transfer (BWT) of +0.41, compared to -2.51 for parameter orthogonalization. BWT measures the average performance degradation on previous tasks, with positive values indicating positive transfer. This suggests that HiFGO not only mitigates forgetting but can also facilitate performance improvement on previous tasks through new knowledge.
 - Jointly enforcing both parameter and GPWC orthogonality yields no additional gains, indicating that HiFGO is a more principled and effective constraint mechanism.
 - Comparison with history-based gradient orthogonalization reveals that the latter suffers significant forgetting (-8.95 BWT), possibly due to weak and oscillatory gradients at converged local optima of previous tasks. GPWC, by evaluating gradients on current data, provides a more faithful characterization of inter-task interference.

- **Effectiveness of the Two-stage Finetuning Strategy:**
 - The two-stage strategy significantly enhances the Immediate (Imd.) and Last metrics, which reflect performance on a given task immediately after fine-tuning and overall last task performance, respectively. It boosts the average Imd. and Last by 8.13% and 9.83% respectively.
 - Visualization of finetuning dynamics shows that Stage 1 (unconstrained) achieves faster convergence and better initial optima. Stage 2 (constrained) then refines these parameters, maintaining performance while enforcing regularization, without compromising the plasticity gained in Stage 1. In contrast, direct constrained finetuning from the start leads to noticeable performance degradation.

- **Effectiveness of Norm Regularization:**
 - Norm regularization, while mitigating higher-order term perturbations, moderately affects fine-tuning efficacy. Stronger norm regularization leads to superior performance in alleviating catastrophic forgetting (more positive BWT) but can cause a marginal drop in the Imd. metric.
 - An optimal balance between plasticity and stability is achieved with an appropriate norm regularization strength (e.g., $\lambda = 1e-2$), highlighting a trade-off that needs careful tuning.

- **Influence of Task Ordering:**
 - Experiments with varying task orderings on UCIT show that Octopus demonstrates remarkable insensitivity to task order. Performance remains highly consistent across different permutations or numbers of tasks, indicating strong robustness and order-invariant performance in the continual learning process.

- **Extra Number of Parameters for Inference:**
 - Compared to MoE-based approaches like HiDe-LLaVA, Octopus incurs a negligible number of additional parameters during inference, and this overhead does not scale with the number of tasks. This results in reduced storage costs and improved inference efficiency.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of Octopus carries several significant implications and potential impacts:

- **Advancement in History-Free Continual Learning:** Octopus directly addresses a critical limitation in continual learning by offering a gradient-orthogonalization method that does not require storing or accessing historical task data. This is particularly relevant in real-world scenarios where data privacy, storage costs, and regulatory constraints (e.g., GDPR) make rehearsal-based methods infeasible. The concept of History-Free Gradient Orthogonalization (HiFGO) provides a novel direction for regularization-based approaches.
- **Enhanced Catastrophic Forgetting Mitigation:** By theoretically justifying and empirically demonstrating the effectiveness of gradient orthogonality over parameter orthogonality, the research offers a more principled and robust mechanism for mitigating catastrophic forgetting. The observed positive backward transfer (BWT) further suggests that new knowledge acquisition can, in some cases, improve performance on previous tasks, rather than just preventing degradation.
- **Improved Plasticity-Stability Balance:** The proposed two-stage finetuning strategy provides a method to concurrently optimize for both new task adaptation (plasticity) and knowledge retention (stability). By decoupling unconstrained learning from constrained refinement, the framework allows models to achieve high performance on individual tasks while effectively enforcing regularization against forgetting.
- **Inference Efficiency for MLLMs:** Unlike architecture-based methods (e.g., MoE-based) that introduce additional task-specific modules and routing overhead during inference, Octopus maintains a single set of LoRA parameters that are merged with the base model, incurring minimal additional parameters. This makes it a highly efficient solution for deploying continually learning MLLMs in resource-constrained environments.
- **Robustness to Task Order:** The demonstrated insensitivity of Octopus to task ordering indicates its practical applicability in dynamic environments where the sequence of incoming tasks cannot be predetermined or controlled. This adds to the reliability and generalizability of the framework.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The work establishes a strong baseline and theoretical framework for gradient-level regularization in continual learning for large models. It opens avenues for further exploration into advanced history-free regularization techniques, more sophisticated two-stage or multi-stage optimization schedules, and methods to address the identified limitations regarding the upper bound on the number of tasks and performance degradation on highly analogous tasks.

**Limitations:** The paper acknowledges certain limitations of Octopus. It is constrained by an upper bound on the number of tasks, similar to other LoRA-based regularization methods. Additionally, performance degradation can occur on tasks that are highly analogous in domain but differ in problem formulation, suggesting challenges in disentangling very similar task representations. These areas are identified as requiring further investigation.
