# Interpretable Zero-Shot Learning with Locally-Aligned Vision-Language Model

> [!summary] Summary
> A training-free framework, LaZSL, developed by researchers from MBZUAI and collaborators, enhances interpretable zero-shot learning by employing Optimal Transport to align local visual regions with LLM-generated semantic attributes. This approach achieves state-of-the-art accuracy across diverse benchmarks, including 69.7% on average with a ViT-B/16 backbone, while providing human-understandable explanations for predictions.

## Problem

- Current Vision-Language Models (VLMs) like CLIP offer limited interpretability in Zero-Shot Learning (ZSL), providing global similarity scores without explaining specific visual cues for predictions.
- Global image-text alignment struggles with fine-grained classification and shows poor generalization across domain shifts, leading to suboptimal accuracy for challenging distinctions.
- Existing interpretable ZSL methods typically align entire images with attributes, failing to establish fine-grained local correspondences between visual regions and specific semantic attributes, particularly with frozen pre-trained VLM encoders.

## Method

- Employs Large Language Models (LLMs) to generate sets of ==discrete semantic attributes== for each class, providing fine-grained textual descriptions.
- Constructs a diverse ==region vision set== from multi-scale cropped image patches to capture local visual information.
- Leverages ==Optimal Transport (OT)== to establish fine-grained alignment between local visual features and semantic attributes, minimizing the cost of transforming visual distributions to semantic ones, enhanced by a vision selection mechanism and a region-global hybrid cost.

## Results

- LaZSL achieved state-of-the-art average accuracy on five diverse image classification benchmarks, demonstrating a **69.7%** average accuracy with a ViT-B/16 backbone and outperforming direct interpretable baselines like DCLIP by up to **3.8%**.
- The method showcased superior domain generalization capabilities on ImageNet variants (V2, R, Sketch, A), achieving an average accuracy of **60.9%** with ViT-B/16, surpassing both training-required and training-free baselines.
- Provides explicit, human-understandable interpretations for predictions by aligning specific local visual regions with discrete semantic attributes, illustrating *why* a particular class was chosen.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Establishing fine-grained, local visual-semantic alignment is essential for truly interpretable Zero-Shot Learning, moving beyond black-box global similarity scores.
> - Optimal Transport offers an effective training-free mechanism to achieve precise local alignment between visual regions and discrete semantic attributes derived from LLMs.
> - A balanced integration of local visual information, LLM-generated attributes, and global VLM context is key to boosting accuracy and domain generalization in ZSL while maintaining interpretability.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper operates at the intersection of Zero-Shot Learning (ZSL), Vision-Language Models (VLMs), and the growing demand for interpretable AI.

**Evolution of Zero-Shot Learning:**
ZSL has traditionally focused on recognizing objects from unseen categories by leveraging auxiliary information, typically human-annotated attributes or semantic embeddings. Early methods often involved mapping visual features to a semantic space or learning generative models to synthesize features for unseen classes. While these classical methods offered some interpretability through explicit attributes, they were labor-intensive due to the need for manual attribute annotation and struggled with large-scale datasets.

**Rise of Vision-Language Models (VLMs):**
The advent of large-scale VLMs like CLIP revolutionized ZSL. By training on vast datasets of image-text pairs, CLIP developed a powerful joint embedding space where visual and textual concepts are aligned. This enables zero-shot classification by simply comparing an image's embedding with the embeddings of class names (often enriched with prompt templates like "a photo of a *class_name*"). This paradigm significantly improved scalability and generalization compared to classical ZSL, eliminating the need for explicit attribute annotations.

**Challenges with Current VLMs:**
Despite their success, VLM-based ZSL methods face several challenges that this paper addresses:
1. **Limited Interpretability**: Standard CLIP-based classification relies on a global similarity score between the entire image and a class prompt. This black-box approach offers little insight into *why* a particular prediction was made, making it difficult to trust or debug.
2. **Sensitivity to Prompts and Domain Shift**: CLIP's performance can be highly sensitive to the specific text prompts used and may degrade on domain-specific datasets (e.g., fine-grained categories). Existing solutions, such as prompt learning and adapter learning, aim to mitigate these issues but often still operate at a global level and require additional training.
3. **Lack of Fine-Grained Alignment**: The global image-to-text similarity fails to capture fine-grained visual details and their corresponding semantic attributes. For instance, distinguishing between similar bird species requires recognizing specific features like "wing pattern" or "bill color," which global similarity might overlook.

**Positioning LaZSL:**
"LaZSL" emerges as a response to these VLM limitations, specifically targeting interpretability and fine-grained understanding without sacrificing the zero-shot capabilities. It builds upon a nascent trend of "interpretable VLM-based ZSL" that leverages Large Language Models (LLMs) to generate descriptive attributes for classes. However, even these methods typically align the *entire image* with these attributes, still lacking true local interpretability.

LaZSL differentiates itself by proposing a novel **local visual-semantic alignment** strategy. Instead of relying on global features, it attempts to match *local visual regions* with *discrete semantic attributes* using Optimal Transport (OT). This approach pushes the boundary of interpretable ZSL by offering:
- **True Local Interpretability**: Explaining predictions based on specific visual parts corresponding to specific attributes, akin to human reasoning.
- **Enhanced Fine-Grained Understanding**: By aligning local features, it can better discriminate between visually similar categories.
- **Training-Free Nature**: Crucially, LaZSL achieves these benefits without requiring additional training or fine-tuning of the pre-trained VLM, making it highly efficient and adaptable.

By integrating LLM-generated attributes with local visual feature alignment via OT, LaZSL represents a significant step towards more transparent, robust, and human-understandable AI systems for image classification, particularly in challenging zero-shot scenarios.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this research are twofold:
1. **Enhance the interpretability of Zero-Shot Learning (ZSL) predictions in Vision-Language Models (VLMs):** Current VLM-based ZSL methods, like CLIP, provide a global similarity score between an image and a class name, offering no insights into the specific visual cues that led to the prediction. The authors aim to enable models to explain their classifications based on relevant, discrete attributes, mirroring human perception.
2. **Improve the accuracy and domain generalization of VLM-based ZSL, especially for fine-grained categories, without additional training:** The authors seek to overcome the limitations of global image-text alignment, which often fails to capture fine-grained visual information crucial for distinguishing similar classes and can lead to poor generalization across different data distributions. They aim to achieve this by developing a method that can effectively align local visual features with corresponding semantic attributes.

The motivations behind these objectives stem from several critical observations and challenges:

- **Lack of Explainability in VLMs:** While powerful, VLMs often act as "black boxes." For critical applications, understanding *why* a model makes a certain prediction is essential for trust, debugging, and ethical deployment. The paper explicitly states that current methods compute similarity "between an entire query image and the embedded category words, making it difficult to explain their predictions."
- **Inadequacy of Global Alignment for Fine-Grained Recognition:** The global similarity approach of standard VLMs struggles with fine-grained classification tasks where subtle visual differences define categories. Humans recognize objects by identifying specific attributes (e.g., "long bill," "white belly"). The paper highlights that current interpretable methods, while using attributes, still align the *whole image* with attributes, "which cannot directly capture the relationships between fine-grained visual information and their corresponding attributes." This leads to "wrong visual-semantic alignments and limiting the generalization of VLMs."
- **The "New Challenge" of Local Alignment with Frozen VLMs:** The authors identify a core problem: "how to conduct effective alignment between local vision and attributes based on the pre-trained VLMs." Traditional ZSL methods could incorporate attention mechanisms for attribute localization, but pre-trained VLMs have frozen encoders, making network re-design and training difficult due to insufficient data for such fine-tuning.
- **Desire for Training-Free Solutions:** Many existing methods designed to improve CLIP (e.g., prompt learning, adapter learning) require additional training. A key motivation is to achieve improved performance and interpretability without this computational overhead, making the solution more practical and accessible.

To address these motivations, the authors propose "LaZSL," a novel approach that leverages the mathematical framework of Optimal Transport (OT) to establish fine-grained, local correspondences between image regions and semantic attributes, thereby enabling interpretable and accurate zero-shot classification.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

LaZSL, a locally-aligned vision-language model for interpretable Zero-Shot Learning, tackles the challenge of fine-grained visual-semantic alignment using Optimal Transport (OT). The methodology is structured into three main components: (1) Construction of Semantic and Vision Sets, (2) Local Visual-Semantic Alignment via OT, and (3) Zero-Shot Prediction.

#### 3.1. Construction of Semantic and Vision Sets

The first step is to prepare the visual and semantic inputs in a fine-grained manner.

- **Construction of Semantic Sets**: To introduce attribute-level semantic information, the method utilizes Large Language Models (LLMs), specifically GPT-3, to generate a set of discrete attributes for each class label. Given a class label `y`, a prompt (e.g., "What are useful visual features for distinguishing a {class name} in a photo?") is fed to the LLM `h(·)`, which then outputs a set of `M` semantic attributes `S_y = {s_y^i | i=1..., M}`. These attributes serve as the fine-grained semantic descriptions.
- **Construction of Vision Sets**: To capture local visual information, random multi-scale cropping is applied to the input image `x`. This process generates a "region vision set" `V_x^r = {v_x^i | i=1..., N}`, where `N` is the number of cropped regions. Each crop `v_x^i` is generated by `P_r(x, γ_i * min(W, H))`, where `γ_i` is a random scaling factor ensuring diverse region sizes. This set of cropped regions is intended to provide diverse local visual features that can correspond to the generated attributes.
These visual and semantic sets are then encoded into latent space representations using the vision (`σ_v`) and semantic (`σ_s`) encoders from a pre-trained VLM (like CLIP). The vision set is augmented with the global image feature `p_g` derived from the original image `x`, resulting in `P_x = {p_l} ∪ {p_g}`. The semantic set becomes `Q_y = σ_s(S_y)`.

#### 3.2. Local Visual-Semantic Alignment Via Optimal Transport (OT)

This is the core of LaZSL, where fine-grained visual regions are aligned with attributes.

- **Optimal Transport Formulation**: The task of aligning `N` local visual features with `M` semantic attributes is formulated as an Optimal Transport problem. OT seeks to find an optimal "transport plan" `T` that minimizes the cost of transforming one probability distribution (over visual regions) into another (over semantic attributes). The `T` matrix then represents the strength of alignment between each visual region and each attribute.
 - **Cost Matrix (C)**: The cost `C_{i,j}` between the `i`-th local visual feature `p_l^i` and the `j`-th attribute feature `q_y^j` is defined as `1 - cosine(p_l^i, q_y^j)`. Lower cosine similarity (higher dissimilarity) translates to higher cost.
 - **Probability Vectors (r, c)**: `r` and `c` are discrete probability vectors representing the distributions over the vision set and semantic set, respectively, initialized as uniform distributions.
- **Vision Selection**: To mitigate noise from random cropping, a "vision selection" mechanism is introduced. It filters out irrelevant local visual regions by comparing their similarity to the global image feature `p_g`. Regions with `cosine(p_l^i, p_g)` below an average similarity threshold `δ` are deemed irrelevant, and their corresponding probabilities in `r` are set to zero, effectively focusing OT on relevant visual patches.
- **Region-Global Hybrid Cost**: To prevent "knowledge forgetting" from CLIP's global context and improve robustness, a hybrid cost matrix `C*` is used. Each row `C*_i` (corresponding to a local visual feature) is a weighted combination of its similarity to the semantic attributes (`sim_i`) and the global image feature's similarity to the semantic attributes (`p_g^T Q_y`). This is defined as `C*_i = 1 - (θsim_i + (1 - θ)p_g^T Q_y)`, where `θ` is a hyper-parameter balancing local and global influence.
- **Compute OT Strategy**: With the refined probability vector `r*` and the hybrid cost matrix `C*`, the Optimal Transport plan `T` is computed using the Sinkhorn algorithm, which is an efficient iterative method for solving entropic-regularized OT problems.

#### 3.3. Zero-Shot Prediction

Finally, the learned OT plan and hybrid similarity are used to make predictions.
- **Hybrid Similarity Score**: For each category `y`, a hybrid similarity matrix `sim*` is computed, similar to `C*`, balancing local and global feature contributions.
- **Category Score**: The final category score `ψ_y` for a given image `x` and category `y` is calculated as the Frobenius inner product between the optimal transport plan `T` and the hybrid similarity matrix `sim*`: `ψ_y = <T, sim*>_F`. This score effectively combines the optimal alignment between local visual regions and attributes with their weighted similarities.
- **Prediction**: The class label `y*` with the highest category score `ψ_y` is chosen as the zero-shot prediction: `y* = arg max_y ψ_y`.

This meticulous methodology ensures that LaZSL not only leverages attribute-level semantics but also precisely aligns them with corresponding visual evidence, leading to interpretable and accurate predictions without the need for additional model training.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The extensive experimental evaluation of LaZSL across various datasets and backbones demonstrates its effectiveness in achieving interpretability, improved accuracy, and strong domain generalization.

#### 4.1. Quantitative Results

- **Cross-Dataset ZSL Performance (Table 1):**
 - LaZSL consistently achieves state-of-the-art average accuracy across five diverse image classification benchmarks (ImageNet, CUB, Oxford Pets, Food101, Place365) using different CLIP backbones (ViT-B/32, ViT-B/16, ViT-L/14). For example, it reaches 66.8%, 69.7%, and 74.0% average accuracy, respectively.
 - Compared to DCLIP, a direct interpretable ZSL baseline, LaZSL shows notable improvements, particularly on fine-grained datasets like CUB (e.g., +3.8% with ViT-B/32) and Place365 (e.g., +1.6% with ViT-B/32). This highlights the benefit of local alignment for challenging distinctions.
 - These results validate that LaZSL's mechanism of local visual-semantic alignment through Optimal Transport is effective in enhancing both the interpretability and accuracy of VLM-based ZSL.

- **Domain Generalization on Cross Domains (Table 2):**
 - LaZSL demonstrates superior domain generalization capabilities on ImageNet variants with natural distribution shifts (ImageNet-V2, ImageNet-R, ImageNet-Sketch, ImageNet-A), achieving an average accuracy of 60.9% with ViT-B/16.
 - It outperforms both standard VLM-based ZSL methods (e.g., CoOp, CoCoOp, TPT, MaPLe, ProGrad) which often require additional training on source data, and other interpretable ZSL methods like DCLIP (+1.7%) and CuPL (+0.9%).
 - Remarkably, LaZSL (which is training-free) even surpasses ArGue, an interpretable ZSL method that tunes prompts with additional training. This underscores the power of a well-designed training-free local alignment strategy for robustness to domain shifts.

#### 4.2. Ablation Studies (Table 3)

The ablation studies confirm the contribution of each proposed component:
- **Optimal Transport (OT):** Adding OT to the baseline (DCLIP) consistently improves performance (e.g., +1.2% on CUB). This validates the necessity and effectiveness of local alignment.
- **Vision Selection (VS):** Incorporating vision selection further boosts performance (e.g., from 59.0% to 60.0% on CUB with OT), indicating its role in filtering noisy visual regions.
- **Region-Global Hybrid Cost (Hybrid):** The hybrid cost also contributes to performance gains (e.g., from 59.0% to 59.3% on CUB with OT), confirming its ability to prevent knowledge forgetting and incorporate robust global context.
- **Full LaZSL:** The combination of all components (OT+VS+Hybrid) yields the best results across all datasets, validating the synergistic effect of the proposed modules.

#### 4.3. Qualitative Analysis (Figure 3 & 4)

- **Interpretable Class Prediction (Figure 3):** Visualizations clearly show that LaZSL provides interpretable classifications by explicitly listing the attributes and their corresponding scores that contributed to the final prediction (e.g., "Scissor Tailed Flycatcher" is predicted due to "black wings with white wingbars," "long, forked tail," etc.). This contrasts sharply with the black-box nature of standard CLIP.
- **Classification Comparison with DCLIP (Figure 4):** Examples demonstrate that LaZSL can accurately predict classes where DCLIP fails, particularly for fine-grained distinctions (e.g., correctly identifying "Brass Memorial Plaque" or "Library" versus DCLIP's misclassifications). This qualitative evidence strongly supports the superiority of LaZSL's local alignment in capturing subtle visual cues.

#### 4.4. Hyper-Parameter Analysis (Figure 5)

- **Cropping Scale (α):** LaZSL is relatively robust to the cropping scale, performing optimally around `α = 0.6`. Too large `α` can degrade performance as local alignment becomes less effective.
- **Hybrid Coefficient (θ):** Performance improves as local information is integrated, suggesting `θ` values around 0.8 are optimal. Very high `θ` (over-reliance on local features) can lead to performance degradation due to "knowledge forgetting" of the global VLM context.

#### 4.5. Cost Analysis (Table 4)

LaZSL introduces a minimal additional time (0.070s vs. ~0.015s for baselines) and memory cost (769.01MB vs. ~615MB for baselines) per sample compared to other attribute-based ZSL methods. This indicates that its performance gains come with reasonable computational overhead.

In summary, the findings robustly support LaZSL's claims of enhanced interpretability, improved accuracy, and strong domain generalization, achieved through its novel training-free local visual-semantic alignment via Optimal Transport.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of LaZSL represents a significant step forward in the field of Zero-Shot Learning (ZSL) and Vision-Language Models (VLMs), particularly in addressing the critical need for interpretability. Its potential impact spans several dimensions:

- **Enhanced Interpretability in AI Systems:** The most direct and profound impact of LaZSL is its ability to provide human-understandable explanations for VLM-based ZSL predictions. By explicitly aligning local visual regions with discrete semantic attributes, LaZSL moves beyond black-box classification. This interpretability is crucial for building trust in AI systems, enabling easier debugging, and facilitating deployment in sensitive applications such as medical diagnosis, autonomous driving, or security, where understanding *why* a decision was made is as important as the decision itself.
- **Improved Accuracy and Robustness for Fine-Grained Recognition:** The paper demonstrates that local visual-semantic alignment significantly boosts accuracy, especially for challenging fine-grained classification tasks (e.g., distinguishing bird species or specific objects). This implies that LaZSL can be highly valuable in domains requiring precise visual differentiation, where global similarity alone falls short. Its strong domain generalization capabilities also suggest greater robustness to unseen data distributions, a key challenge for real-world AI deployment.
- **Efficient and Training-Free Model Enhancement:** A major advantage of LaZSL is that it achieves its performance gains and interpretability *without requiring any additional training or fine-tuning* of the pre-trained VLM. This "training-free" nature is immensely significant:
 - **Reduced Computational Cost:** It avoids the substantial computational resources (GPUs, time) typically needed for fine-tuning large models.
 - **Accessibility:** It makes advanced, interpretable ZSL more accessible to researchers and practitioners who may not have access to large-scale training infrastructure.
 - **Flexibility:** It can be readily applied as a plug-and-play solution atop existing pre-trained VLMs, making it highly adaptable to various use cases.
- **Bridging the Gap between LLMs and VLMs:** LaZSL effectively harnesses the power of Large Language Models (LLMs) for generating rich, attribute-level semantic descriptions and integrates them seamlessly with VLMs for visual understanding. This approach showcases a promising direction for combining the strengths of different large foundation models to create more capable and versatile AI.
- **Inspiration for Future Research:** The paper opens several avenues for future research. The stated limitation regarding the quality of LLM-generated attributes points towards improving prompt engineering for LLMs or developing attribute filtering mechanisms. Furthermore, the success of Optimal Transport in this context could inspire its application to other fine-grained alignment problems in vision-language tasks, such as explainable object detection or dense captioning.

In conclusion, LaZSL contributes significantly to making powerful Vision-Language Models more transparent, accurate, and practically deployable. Its training-free, interpretable approach holds considerable promise for advancing the capabilities of AI in complex visual recognition tasks and fostering greater trust in AI-driven decision-making.
