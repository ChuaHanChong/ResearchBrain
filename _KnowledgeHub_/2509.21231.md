# SEEC: Stable End-Effector Control with Model-Enhanced Residual Learning for Humanoid Loco-Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> The SEEC framework enables stable and precise arm end-effector control for humanoid robots during dynamic loco-manipulation by integrating model-enhanced residual learning with a perturbation generation strategy. This approach reduces end-effector accelerations, demonstrating superior robustness compared to baselines, for instance achieving a mean linear acceleration of 2.82 m/s² on a real humanoid during walking, which is lower than the 3.57 m/s² of an IK baseline.

## Problem

- Achieving stable and precise arm end-effector control in humanoid robots during dynamic whole-body movements is challenging due to inherent bipedal instability.
- Model-based controllers struggle with real-world complexities like friction and unmodeled dynamics, while pure learning-based methods often overfit and lack generalization to diverse locomotion patterns.
- Existing locomotion-manipulation policies are often tightly coupled, requiring extensive retraining when locomotion patterns change or new disturbances occur.

## Method

![[2509.21231-method.png]]
*The training pipeline involves generating synthetic base perturbations and using a model-based torque signal to guide the Reinforcement Learning policy.*

- A model-enhanced residual learning framework (SEEC) is proposed, which distills analytical compensation signals from a simplified dynamics model into an RL policy for upper-body control.
- A perturbation generation strategy is introduced to expose the upper-body policy to diverse locomotion-induced base accelerations, such as impulses and rhythmic sway, during training.
- The RL policy learns to output joint targets for a low-level PD controller, guided by a reward function that penalizes the difference between measured torque and ideal model-based compensation torque.

## Results

- SEEC consistently achieved lower mean and maximum end-effector linear and angular accelerations in simulation across various locomotion tasks (e.g., 2.26 m/s² mean linear acceleration in 'Stepping' vs. 5.73 m/s² for IK baseline).
- The framework demonstrated superior robustness to unseen locomotion controllers, with an average degradation of 34.40% in mean linear acceleration, significantly outperforming co-trained policies which degraded by 57.45%.
- On the Booster T1 humanoid, SEEC yielded lower mean linear (2.82 m/s²) and angular (24.2 rad/s²) end-effector accelerations compared to an IK baseline, enabling stable execution of tasks like plate carrying without spillage.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Combining analytical model-based compensation with learning-based adaptability effectively addresses the limitations of both pure model-based and pure learning approaches for stable end-effector control.
> - Learning to compensate for a wide range of simulated base perturbations allows the upper-body controller to generalize robustly to unseen locomotion policies and dynamic real-world disturbances.
> - Using a torque-guided reward function based on operational-space compensation signals provides a more principled and efficient learning objective for end-effector stabilization than indirect reward penalties.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The integration of locomotion and manipulation capabilities is a fundamental requirement for humanoid robots to operate effectively in human environments. Achieving stable and precise control of the arm end-effector, particularly during dynamic whole-body movements, remains a significant challenge within humanoid robotics. The inherent instability of bipedal structures and the high degrees of freedom involved mean that even minor base movements can induce substantial accelerations at the end-effector, leading to tracking errors, unstable contact forces, and compromised task performance.

Historically, whole-body control for legged and mobile manipulators has largely relied on model-based methods. These approaches, utilizing techniques like numerical optimization, can achieve precise control by leveraging detailed dynamics models and online estimation. However, their practical application is often limited by the need for highly accurate models and precise parameter estimation, which struggle to capture complex real-world factors such as friction, backlash, and environmental uncertainties.

In contrast, learning-based methods, particularly those employing reinforcement learning (RL) and imitation learning (IL), have gained prominence for their ability to handle nonlinearities and uncertainty. End-to-end RL policies have demonstrated the capacity to generate expressive and robust behaviors for humanoid loco-manipulation. Nevertheless, these methods often face their own set of limitations. Many learning-based policies imitate joint or task reference trajectories without explicitly ensuring accurate end-effector stabilization, sometimes leading to uncontrolled hand motions as a byproduct of locomotion. While some works have attempted to stabilize end-effectors by penalizing acceleration in reward functions, such approaches have been observed to degenerate into static behaviors or lack generalization to diverse loco-manipulation scenarios. Furthermore, RL policies can overfit to training conditions, necessitating extensive retraining for new tasks or out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios, and struggle to adapt to unseen locomotion patterns or sudden disturbances.

Recent efforts have explored hybrid approaches, combining model-based and learning-based techniques. These include using model-based controllers to supervise learning, blending outputs from RL and model predictive control (MPC), or employing residual policy learning. While these hybrids have shown improvements in sample efficiency and stability, many have been evaluated on simplified tasks or flat terrain, often not directly addressing the unique problem of maintaining arm end-effector stability during dynamic loco-manipulation.

This paper addresses the identified limitations by proposing a model-enhanced residual learning framework. It aims to bridge the gap between the precision of model-based control and the adaptability of learning-based methods. Specifically, it distinguishes itself by:
1. **Principled Stabilization:** Instead of relying on naive penalization, it leverages analytical model-based compensation signals to guide the RL policy, providing a more direct and efficient way to learn stabilization.
2. **Modularity and Robustness:** Unlike tightly coupled or co-trained policies, the framework introduces a perturbation generation strategy. This allows the upper-body controller to be trained independently of specific locomotion policies, enabling robustness to a wide range of locomotion-induced disturbances and facilitating zero-shot transfer to unseen locomotion controllers.
3. **Real-World Validation:** The work demonstrates the deployment of such a hybrid framework on a full humanoid robot (Booster T1), providing empirical evidence of its effectiveness in both simulation and real-world dynamic tasks.

By integrating model-based expertise for analytical compensation and employing a robust perturbation generation strategy, this research contributes to advancing the state of the art in stable and adaptive end-effector control for humanoid loco-manipulation, addressing a critical bottleneck for their deployment in complex, dynamic environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a control framework that enables stable and precise arm end-effector control for humanoid robots performing loco-manipulation tasks, even amidst dynamic locomotion. This overarching goal is motivated by several identified challenges inherent in current approaches to humanoid control:

1. **Inherent Dynamic Instability in Loco-Manipulation:** Humanoid robots possess high degrees of freedom and an intrinsically unstable bipedal structure. Dynamic locomotion introduces significant disturbances that propagate to the arm end-effector. Even minor base movements can result in substantial accelerations at the arm, leading to tracking errors, destabilized contact forces, and compromised task execution, such as dropping objects or inaccurate interaction with the environment. This forms the fundamental problem the paper seeks to resolve.

2. **Limitations of Model-Based Controllers:** While model-based controllers are known for their precision, they critically depend on accurate dynamics modeling and real-time state estimation. Real-world environments introduce complexities like unmodeled friction, backlash in joints, and sensor noise, which are difficult to capture perfectly in analytical models. These inaccuracies can degrade the performance of model-based controllers in practical applications, preventing them from achieving desired stability and precision in complex scenarios. The motivation here is to retain the principled compensation abilities of model-based methods while mitigating their real-world vulnerabilities.

3. **Limitations of Learning-Based Controllers:** Pure reinforcement learning (RL) methods, while effective at capturing nonlinearities and handling uncertainties through exploration and domain randomization, have their own drawbacks. They often overfit to the specific conditions encountered during training, leading to a lack of generalization to unseen scenarios or different locomotion patterns. Furthermore, when aiming for end-effector stabilization, RL policies might struggle to "discover" an optimal compensation strategy efficiently, often relying on indirect reward penalties that can lead to suboptimal or overly static behaviors (e.g., rigid hand-holding). The motivation is to enhance the learning process with direct, principled guidance to ensure effective stabilization.

4. **Lack of Modularity and Robustness to Unseen Disturbances:** Many existing learning-based approaches for loco-manipulation involve jointly training locomotion and manipulation policies. This tight coupling limits modularity and robustness. When a different locomotion controller is deployed, or when the robot encounters dynamically changing real-world environments that induce out-of-distribution (OOD) disturbances, the manipulation policy may not adapt and can fail. A key motivation is to design a system where the upper-body policy can robustly compensate for a wide range of locomotion-induced disturbances, irrespective of the specific locomotion controller generating them, thus allowing for modular reuse and adaptation.

To address these motivations, the paper sets forth the following specific objectives:
- **Develop a Model-Enhanced Residual Learning Framework:** This aims to integrate the analytical precision of model-based compensation with the adaptability and robustness of learning-based control. The goal is to distill model-based acceleration compensation signals into an RL policy, providing a more principled approach to end-effector stabilization than naive penalization.
- **Introduce a Perturbation Generation Strategy:** This strategy is designed to expose the upper-body policy to a diverse spectrum of locomotion-induced disturbances during training. By learning to compensate for these perturbations, the objective is to enable the controller to achieve stable end-effector control that is independent of any specific locomotion policy and robustly transfers to unseen locomotion controllers and gaits without requiring joint re-training.
- **Demonstrate Real-World Deployment and Zero-Shot Transfer:** A crucial objective is to validate the effectiveness and practicality of the proposed hybrid framework by deploying it on a full-scale humanoid robot (Booster T1). This includes demonstrating zero-shot transfer from simulation to real hardware and evaluating its performance across various demanding loco-manipulation tasks.

Through these objectives, the research seeks to provide a control framework that not only achieves precise and robust end-effector stabilization but also offers enhanced modularity and generalization capabilities, thereby advancing the practical deployment of humanoids in complex, dynamic environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed Stable End-Effector Control (SEEC) framework addresses the challenges of humanoid loco-manipulation by coordinating two distinct controllers: a lower-body controller for locomotion and an upper-body controller for manipulation tasks. Both controllers are developed using a Markov Decision Process (MDP) formulation and trained within the IsaacLab simulation environment.

**Assumptions for Upper-Body Control:**
To simplify the complex dynamics and make the upper-body control problem tractable, two key assumptions are made:
1. **Negligible Arm-to-Base Back-Coupling:** The arms are considered dynamically lightweight relative to the lower body. This allows modeling base motion as an exogenous input when calculating compensation torques, effectively decoupling the arm’s influence on the base from the base’s influence on the arm.
2. **Robust Locomotion Controller:** The lower-body locomotion controller is assumed to be sufficiently robust to maintain balance and track desired trajectories, even under disturbances generated by arm movements and control torques. This simplifies the upper-body controller design by not requiring explicit torque constraints.

**A. Model-Enhanced Residual Learning (Upper-Body Controller)**

The upper-body stabilization is formulated as controlling an arm subjected to disturbances from a moving base. The methodology involves a three-stage pipeline for training an RL residual policy to counteract these disturbances:

1. **Simulated Base Acceleration (Perturbation Generation):**
 - **Challenge:** Directly applying spatial accelerations to a floating base in simulation can be numerically unstable due to the nonlinear inverse dynamics problem.
 - **Approach:** Base motion is emulated on a fixed-base model by injecting equivalent fictitious wrenches. These wrenches represent the inertial forces and torques (linear, Euler, centrifugal, Coriolis forces; angular-acceleration, gyroscopic torques) that would be induced by a generic base twist ($V_b$) and spatial acceleration ($A_b$). This method ensures stable application of disturbances.
 - **Disturbance Signal Construction:** Base acceleration signals are constructed from two characteristic sources of locomotion-induced perturbations:
 - **Impulse acceleration:** Simulating foot-ground reaction forces during contact.
 - **Rhythmic sway:** Representing the body's Center of Mass (CoM) shifting during steps.
 - **Composed Signal:** Equation (1) combines these sources: $A_b(t) = \sum_{k=1}^{N} [p_k g(t; T_k) + s_k \sin (2\pi t/T_k + \phi_k)]$, where $g(t; T_k)$ is a Gaussian impulse, $p_k$ is the impulse amplitude, $s_k$ is the oscillation amplitude, and $\phi_k$ is a phase offset.
 - **Parameter Sampling:** Disturbance parameters ($T_k$, $p_k$, $s_k$, $\phi_k$) are sampled from broad ranges to cover diverse, realistic signals. For instance, periods ($T_k$) are log-uniformly sampled from [0.64 s, 1.28 s], impulse amplitudes ($p_k$) from [−100 m/s², 100 m/s²]⁶, oscillation amplitudes ($s_k$) from [−10 m/s², 10 m/s²]⁶, and phase offsets ($\phi_k$) uniformly from [−π, π]. This extensive randomization exposes the policy to a rich set of disturbance profiles, promoting robust compensation strategies irrespective of specific walking styles or gait controllers.

2. **Compensation Torque Calculation:**
 - **Goal:** Compute a compensating torque ($\tau_{comp}$) that, when applied, results in a compensating acceleration ($a_{comp}$) to effectively cancel locomotion-induced end-effector accelerations ($a_{base,ee}$) and responsive accelerations ($a_{resp}$).
 - **Acceleration Components:** The global end-effector acceleration ($a_{glob,ee}$) is the sum of the base-induced acceleration ($a_{base,ee}$), the local acceleration due to arm joint motion ($a_{loc,ee}$), and the responsive acceleration ($a_{resp}$) caused by fictitious wrenches acting on the arm links (Equation 2).
 - **Operational-Space Formulation:** Under Assumption 2, the compensating torque ($\tau_{comp}$) is derived using the minimum-norm operational-space formulation (Equation 3): $\tau_{comp} = -J(q)^T \Lambda(q) [a_{base,ee} + a_{resp}]$, where $J(q)$ is the end-effector Jacobian and $\Lambda(q)$ is the operational-space inertia matrix.
 - **Integration with Task Control:** This compensating torque is then combined with a task-oriented controller signal ($\tau_{task}$), which typically handles local end-effector tracking (e.g., using a PD controller in operational space).

3. **Compensation Residual Policy Training:**
 - **Challenge:** Directly deploying the analytically computed $\tau_{comp}$ on hardware is problematic due to sensor noise, missing angular acceleration signals, and sim-to-real gaps (e.g., motor delays, friction).
 - **Approach:** An RL policy is trained to output joint targets ($q_{des}$) for a low-level PD controller. This PD controller generates a torque ($\tau_{PD}$) which, combined with $\tau_{task}$, is intended to match the ideal combined torque ($\tau_{comp} + \tau_{task}$). Target joint velocities are fixed at zero ($\dot{q}_{des}=0$).
 - **Observation Space:** The policy receives a history of end-effector commands and proprioceptive data, including IMU readings (base angular velocity, angular acceleration), upper-body joint states, and previous actions.
 - **Robustness Enhancement:** Observation noise and simulated friction are injected during training to improve sim-to-real transfer. Control effort and end-effector accelerations are regularized to prevent excessive actuation and jitter.
 - **Reward Function:** The primary reward component ($r_\tau$) penalizes the L2-norm difference between the measured torque ($\tau_{measured}$) and the desired ideal torque ($\tau_{comp} + \tau_{task}$) (Equation 4). Auxiliary rewards (Table I) are included to encourage position/orientation tracking (with tolerance), penalize global end-effector linear and angular accelerations, and promote action smoothness.
 - **RL Algorithm:** Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is used with recurrent actor-critic networks, each having hidden sizes.
 - **Target Frame:** The end-effector target is specified in the local frame, allowing the policy to focus on minimizing local tracking error while stabilizing against base perturbations. The paper notes that global target tracking is a direction for future work, requiring accurate world pose estimation.

**B. Locomotion Training (Lower-Body Controller)**

The lower-body locomotion policy is trained separately using conventional sim-to-real pipelines for robust locomotion, building upon existing frameworks like Booster Gym.
- **Observation Space:** Consists of clock signals (gait phase), proprioception (base angular velocity, joint states, previous actions), base velocity commands, and a 5-step observation history for short-term memory.
- **Action Space:** Controls 13 lower-body joints by providing target positions to low-level PD controllers.
- **Reward Functions:** Designed to promote balance stability, motion smoothness, and accurate velocity tracking.
- **Robustness:** Achieved through randomization of upper-body joint targets, end-effector mass, and other environment parameters across episodes.
- **RL Algorithm:** PPO is used, with actor and critic networks structured as MLPs with hidden sizes.

This modular approach allows the upper-body controller to learn generalizable compensation strategies, independent of the specific lower-body locomotion policy, enhancing overall system robustness and adaptability.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of the SEEC framework was conducted in both simulation (MuJoCo) and on real hardware (Booster T1 humanoid robot), focusing on end-effector stability and robustness to diverse locomotion scenarios.

**A. Simulation Results**

The simulation experiments addressed three main questions: (Q1) effectiveness of model-enhanced residual learning, (Q2) impact of the perturbation generation strategy, and (Q3) robustness to unseen locomotion controllers. End-effector linear and angular accelerations (mean and max) were used as primary metrics.

- **Q1 & Q2: End-Effector Stability and Perturbation Generation (Table II)**
 - **Performance Comparison:** Across various locomotion tasks (stepping, forward, lateral, rotational walking), the SEEC framework ("Ours") consistently demonstrated lower mean and maximum linear and angular end-effector accelerations compared to all baselines. For instance, in "Stepping" mode, "Ours" achieved mean linear acceleration of 2.26 m/s² and mean angular acceleration of 11.9 rad/s², significantly outperforming the IK baseline (5.73 m/s² linear, 18.1 rad/s² angular) and learning-based baselines.
 - **Ablation Study:**
 - Removing the operational space torque component ("Ours w/o $\tau_{task}$") led to a notable degradation in performance (e.g., 3.35 m/s² mean linear acc. in "Stepping"), indicating the importance of this precise tracking signal. This component accounted for the largest performance degradation among ablations (36.11% for mean linear acceleration, 26.39% for mean angular acceleration), suggesting it enables the RL policy to concentrate on learning only the residual compensation.
 - Removing the torque-guided reward ("Ours w/o $r_\tau$") also degraded performance (e.g., 2.42 m/s² mean linear acc. in "Stepping"), highlighting the value of distilling model-based compensation signals into the reward function.
 - Removing the simulated base acceleration generation ("Ours w/o Sim. Acc.") resulted in higher accelerations (e.g., 2.60 m/s² mean linear acc. in "Stepping"), confirming that the perturbation generation strategy is crucial for learning effective compensation.
 - **Conclusion:** The model-enhanced residual learning approach, particularly with the operational space torque and torque-guided reward, combined with the comprehensive perturbation generation, provides superior end-effector stability.

- **Q3: Robustness to Unseen Locomotion Controllers (Table III)**
 - **Experimental Setup:** The performance of different frameworks (Pre-Train, Co-Train, Ours) was evaluated when their trained manipulation policies were deployed with a *new*, previously unseen locomotion policy.
 - **Pre-Train Framework:** This method, where the manipulation policy is trained with a specific pretrained locomotion policy, consistently failed in all tasks when exposed to an unseen locomotion policy. This indicates a lack of generalization and highlights the limitations of tightly coupled state-space exploration.
 - **Co-Train Framework:** This method, where locomotion and manipulation policies are trained simultaneously, showed an average degradation of 57.45% in mean linear acceleration and 60.14% in mean angular acceleration when tested with an unseen locomotion policy. This suggests that co-training creates strong dependencies between the upper and lower body.
 - **SEEC Framework ("Ours"):** The proposed method exhibited a comparatively smaller average degradation of 34.40% in mean linear acceleration and 21.52% in mean angular acceleration when facing unseen locomotion policies. This demonstrates the superior robustness and generalization capabilities of SEEC, attributed to its modular design and perturbation generation strategy that decouples the upper-body learning from specific locomotion patterns.

**B. Hardware Results**

The SEEC framework was deployed on the Booster T1 humanoid robot and compared against an IK-based baseline.

- **1. End-Effector Acceleration Comparison (Table IV, Fig. 3)**
 - **Quantitative Results:** Motion capture data (120 Hz) was used to compute end-effector accelerations via numerical double differentiation. SEEC achieved lower mean and maximum linear (2.82 m/s² mean, 6.36 m/s² max) and angular (24.2 rad/s² mean, 78.6 rad/s² max) accelerations compared to the IK-based method (3.57 m/s² mean linear, 41.1 rad/s² mean angular).
 - **Qualitative Observation:** Figure 3 visually confirms that SEEC produced a smoother acceleration profile, indicative of greater stability, despite the challenges of real-world noise.

- **2. Solving Loco-Manipulation Tasks (Qualitative Demonstrations)**
 - **Chain Holding (Fig. 1 A-D):** The IK baseline induced large-amplitude oscillations in the chain, leading to it dropping from the robot's hand. SEEC effectively suppressed these oscillatory dynamics, maintaining the chain nearly vertical and stable during walking.
 - **Mobile Whiteboard Wiping (Fig. 1 E):** Using VR teleoperation, SEEC consistently maintained smooth trajectories and stable contact forces, leading to effective cleaning, while compensating for locomotion-induced disturbances.
 - **Plate Holding (Fig. 1 F, Fig. 4):** When carrying a plate of snacks, the IK baseline caused significant spillage due to end-effector oscillations. SEEC, in contrast, maintained stable motions, allowing the robot to carry the plate without spilling.
 - **Bottle Holding (Fig. 5):** The IK baseline caused violent shaking and splashing of liquid in a bottle. SEEC kept the bottle steady, minimizing liquid sloshing.

**Overall Conclusion from Results:**
The experimental results, both in simulation and on real hardware, consistently demonstrate that the SEEC framework provides more stable and precise end-effector control during dynamic humanoid locomotion compared to the baselines. The model-enhanced residual learning, coupled with the perturbation generation strategy, proves effective in compensating for locomotion-induced disturbances and robustly generalizes across different and unseen locomotion patterns.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The SEEC framework presents a significant advancement in humanoid robot control, particularly for tasks involving simultaneous locomotion and manipulation. Its primary impact stems from its ability to provide stable and precise end-effector control under dynamic whole-body movements, a capability that is crucial for the practical deployment of humanoids in complex, unstructured human environments.

The **model-enhanced residual learning framework** represents a notable contribution by effectively bridging the gap between traditional model-based control and modern learning-based approaches. By leveraging analytical compensation signals from a simplified dynamics model to guide reinforcement learning, the framework achieves the precision of model-based methods while gaining the adaptability and robustness to real-world complexities (e.g., friction, unmodeled dynamics) that learning provides. This hybrid approach overcomes the limitations of purely model-based systems (fragility to model inaccuracies) and purely learning-based systems (inefficient discovery of stabilization strategies, overfitting).

The introduction of a **perturbation generation strategy** during training enhances the **robustness and generalization capabilities** of the upper-body controller. By exposing the policy to a broad spectrum of locomotion-relevant disturbances, SEEC enables the arm control system to learn compensation behaviors that are independent of any specific locomotion policy. This modular design has several key impacts:
- **Reduced Retraining:** It minimizes the need for extensive retraining when locomotion patterns or controllers change, facilitating quicker deployment and adaptation to new tasks.
- **Enhanced Adaptability:** It allows the humanoid to maintain stable end-effector control even when encountering unforeseen locomotion-induced disturbances in real-world scenarios.
- **Improved Modularity:** It promotes a decoupled control architecture, allowing for independent development and optimization of locomotion and manipulation modules, which simplifies system design and integration.

The **successful deployment and validation on a full humanoid robot, Booster T1, via zero-shot transfer** from simulation, underscores the practical utility and robustness of the SEEC framework. The qualitative and quantitative results from demanding loco-manipulation tasks (e.g., chain holding, plate carrying, whiteboard wiping, bottle holding) demonstrate that SEEC can handle interactions with external objects and dynamic payloads without compromising stability. This real-world validation is critical for building confidence in the method's applicability outside of simulation, pushing humanoids closer to operating reliably in daily human-centric tasks.

The potential impact extends to various applications where stable manipulation during movement is essential, such as:
- **Logistics and Service Robotics:** Humanoids could transport delicate items or interact with the environment while navigating complex spaces.
- **Assistance Robotics:** Robots could assist humans with tasks requiring careful handling or precise tool use while moving.
- **Manufacturing and Assembly:** Humanoids might perform tasks requiring high precision on moving platforms or while repositioning themselves.

While the current work demonstrates significant progress, it also points to future research directions, such as integrating more advanced model-based controllers for constraint handling and utilizing richer state inputs with accurate world-pose estimation for global target tracking. These avenues suggest that SEEC serves not only as an effective solution but also as a foundational framework for further advancements in intelligent and agile humanoid loco-manipulation. The presented framework offers a robust and generalizable approach that could accelerate the development and deployment of humanoid robots in dynamic and interactive environments.
