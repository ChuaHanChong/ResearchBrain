# Energy-Based Transfer for Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Purdue University, AWS AI, and Carnegie Mellon University developed Energy-Based Transfer Learning (EBTL), a framework enabling teacher policies in reinforcement learning to selectively provide guidance to student policies. EBTL quantifies teacher familiarity with a state using energy scores linked to state visitation density, consistently improving sample efficiency and final policy returns by preventing negative transfer across various tasks.

## Problem

- Reinforcement Learning (RL) agents often exhibit poor sample efficiency, requiring vast amounts of experience to learn new tasks.
- Existing transfer learning methods in RL are susceptible to 'negative transfer,' where a pre-trained teacher policy provides counterproductive guidance in states unfamiliar to its training distribution.
- Prior transfer approaches lack a principled, introspective mechanism for teachers to assess their competence or familiarity with a student's current state before intervening.

## Method

- The **EBTL** (==Energy-Based Transfer Learning==) framework allows a teacher policy to provide guidance only in states deemed ==in-distribution== based on its training experience.
- Familiarity is quantified using ==energy scores==, which are the negative free energy of a state, shown to be proportional to the logarithm of the teacher's ==state-visitation density==.
- An ==energy-based regularization loss== is added to the teacher's training objective to improve discrimination between in-distribution and out-of-distribution states, further refining guidance selectivity.

## Results

- **EBTL** consistently achieved superior sample efficiency and higher final policy returns compared to all baseline methods across various single-task (Minigrid) and multi-task (Overcooked) environments.
- The teacher policy effectively distinguished between familiar and unfamiliar states using energy scores, resulting in a higher rate of beneficial guidance and a reduced incidence of detrimental advice in tasks involving covariate shift.
- Energy regularization improved EBTL's convergence speed and performance under strong covariate shifts, and the framework demonstrated robustness to varying covariate shift, environmental complexity, and hyperparameters, maintaining stable positive transfer.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The problem of selectively providing teacher guidance in RL can be formally addressed as an out-of-distribution detection task, where guidance is restricted to states within the teacher's data distribution.
> - Energy scores, calculated from a policy's neural network logits, serve as a theoretically grounded proxy for the logarithm of the state-visitation density, allowing objective quantification of teacher familiarity.
> - Incorporating an energy regularization term during teacher training significantly enhances the teacher's capacity to discriminate between familiar (in-distribution) and unfamiliar (out-of-distribution) states, improving transfer performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has emerged as a powerful framework for sequential decision-making. However, a persistent challenge in RL is its reliance on large amounts of experience, leading to poor sample efficiency. This limitation is particularly pronounced in multi-task or continual learning settings, where agents must acquire skills for numerous new tasks, often related to previously encountered ones.

To address the issue of sample inefficiency, transfer learning has been extensively explored in RL. A common paradigm involves a "teacher" policy, pre-trained on a source task, providing guidance to a "student" policy learning a new, related target task. This guidance can manifest in various forms, including direct action suggestions, reward shaping, or policy parameter initialization. While these methods can accelerate learning, a significant drawback arises when the target task sufficiently deviates from the teacher's training experience. In such scenarios, the teacher may provide sub-optimal or even detrimental guidance, biasing the student's exploration towards low-reward regions of the state-action space, thus hindering rather than helping learning. This phenomenon is often referred to as "negative transfer."

Existing behavior-based transfer methods, such as policy distillation and action advising, often rely on predefined heuristics, fixed hyperparameters, or brittle fine-tuning strategies to determine when to intervene. For instance, JumpStart RL restricts guidance to an initial segment of an episode, while introspective action advising uses deviations in the teacher's expected reward. Parameter-based methods, like fine-tuning, can overly bias the student towards the teacher's behaviors, limiting necessary exploration in novel tasks. These approaches may not generalize well, especially when there is significant covariate shift—a change in the distribution of states between the source and target tasks.

This work addresses the aforementioned gap by proposing a theoretically-grounded method for selective guidance that aims to prevent negative transfer. It integrates concepts from energy-based learning and out-of-distribution (OOD) detection into the RL transfer framework, allowing the teacher to assess its familiarity with the student's current state before intervening. This contrasts with prior methods that either provide guidance indiscriminately or rely on more superficial heuristics.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the sample efficiency of reinforcement learning algorithms, particularly in multi-task and continual learning environments. The authors aim to achieve this by developing a robust transfer learning mechanism that mitigates the risk of negative transfer.

The motivation stems from the observation that while knowledge transfer from a teacher policy can significantly accelerate student learning, it can also be counterproductive if the transferred knowledge is ill-suited for the target task. If a teacher policy, trained on a specific source task, provides guidance in states that are substantially different from its training distribution, this guidance can lead to sub-optimal exploration and degrade the student's performance. The authors contend that current transfer methods lack an effective, principled way for a teacher to discern when its knowledge is applicable and when it might be detrimental.

Specifically, the key objectives include:
- **Developing an introspective transfer learning method:** The authors seek to create a mechanism where the teacher policy can self-assess its competence or "familiarity" with the current state observed by the student. This introspection should enable the teacher to intervene only when its guidance is likely to be beneficial.
- **Leveraging out-of-distribution detection:** The core idea is to formalize the problem of "when to advise" as an out-of-distribution (OOD) detection task. The teacher should only provide guidance when the student is in a state that lies within the teacher's training data distribution.
- **Grounding the method theoretically:** The authors aim to provide a theoretical basis for their approach, demonstrating how the chosen OOD detection mechanism (energy scores) quantitatively reflects the teacher's state visitation density. This aims to establish a principled connection between familiarity and the decision to advise.
- **Empirically validating performance:** The method is intended to be evaluated across a range of transfer learning scenarios, including both single-task and multi-task settings, and under varying degrees of covariate shift. The empirical evaluation focuses on demonstrating improvements in sample efficiency and overall learning performance compared to existing transfer learning baselines.

By addressing these objectives, the research seeks to provide a more reliable and efficient approach to knowledge transfer in RL, allowing agents to learn new tasks more effectively by selectively integrating prior experience while avoiding the pitfalls of inappropriate guidance.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, Energy-Based Transfer Learning (EBTL), is designed to allow a teacher policy to selectively guide a student policy based on its familiarity with the current state. This familiarity is quantified using energy scores derived from the teacher's neural network.

**Core Mechanism:**
The central idea of EBTL is to restrict teacher intervention to states that are considered "in-distribution" (ID) with respect to the teacher's training experience. When the student observes a state $s_t$, the teacher computes an "energy score" for that state. If this score exceeds a predefined threshold $\tau$, the state is deemed ID, and the teacher may provide an action suggestion. Otherwise, if the state is considered "out-of-distribution" (OOD), the student policy acts independently. This action selection rule can be summarized as:
$a = \pi_T(\cdot | s)$ if $\phi(s; \pi_T) \geq \tau$, and $a = \pi_S(\cdot | s)$ if $\phi(s; \pi_T) < \tau$, where $\phi(s; \pi_T)$ is the energy score for state $s$ under teacher policy $\pi_T$.

**Energy Scores and State Visitation:**
The energy score $\phi(s; \pi_T)$ is defined as the negative free energy of a state $s$, calculated from the raw logits of the teacher's neural network. Specifically, for a network $f(x)$ with logits $f_1(x), \ldots, f_K(x)$, the free energy is $E(x; f) = -T \log \sum_{i=1}^K e^{f_i(x)/T}$, where $T$ is a temperature parameter. The energy score is then $\phi(s; \pi_T) = -E(s; \pi_T)$.
A key theoretical contribution is Proposition 4.1, which establishes that the logarithm of the state-visitation density $d_\pi(s)$ induced by a policy $\pi$ is proportional to its negative free energy (i.e., the energy score). This implies that states frequently visited by the teacher during its training will exhibit lower free energy (higher energy scores), thus providing a principled measure of the teacher's familiarity with a given state. The energy threshold $\tau$ is determined as the $q$-quantile of energy scores observed during the teacher's training states.

**Algorithm 1: Energy-Based Transfer for Reinforcement Learning:**
The overall algorithm operates in an episodic fashion. During each timestep $t$:
1. The student observes state $s_t$.
2. The teacher computes the energy score $\phi(s_t; \pi_T)$.
3. A random probability $p$ is sampled, and guidance is issued only if $\phi(s_t; \pi_T) \geq \tau$ AND $p < \delta(t)$, where $\delta(t)$ is a decaying probability schedule (e.g., linear decay) that gradually reduces the likelihood of guidance over time, promoting student autonomy.
4. If guidance is issued, the action $a_t$ is sampled from $\pi_T(\cdot | s_t)$; otherwise, it is sampled from $\pi_S(\cdot | s_t)$.
5. The chosen action is executed in the environment, yielding $r_t$ and $s_{t+1}$.
6. The collected transition $(s_t, a_t, s_{t+1}, r_t)$ is added to a batch.
7. After an episode or a fixed number of steps, the student policy $\pi_S$ is updated using the collected batch, typically with an on-policy RL algorithm like PPO.

**Energy Regularization:**
To enhance the teacher's ability to discriminate between ID and OOD states, an energy-based loss ($L_{energy}$) is incorporated into the teacher's training objective. This regularization term penalizes ID states that yield low energy scores (below a margin $m_{in}$) and OOD states that yield high energy scores (above a margin $m_{out}$). The OOD samples for this regularization are drawn from random policy rollouts in the target environment, while ID samples come from the teacher's own training experience. This explicit training on OOD examples aims to improve the separability of energy scores. The total teacher loss becomes $L_{total} = L_{RL} + \lambda \cdot L_{energy}$.

**Off-Policy Correction:**
Since actions sampled from the teacher policy are inherently off-policy with respect to the student, a correction mechanism is needed for on-policy RL algorithms like PPO. The authors augment the actor and critic losses with an importance sampling ratio $r_t$. This ratio accounts for the discrepancy between the behavior policy (either student or teacher) that generated the action and the current student policy being updated. The ratio is defined differently depending on whether the action at time $t$ was sampled from the student ($\alpha_t=0$) or the teacher ($\alpha_t=1$).

**Experimental Setup:**
The method is evaluated in two main settings:
- **Single-Task Setting (Minigrid):** A grid-world navigation environment with four interconnected rooms.
 - *Alternating-Goal Room:* Teacher trained with goal in Room 1. Student encounters goal in Room 1 or Room 3. Tests selective guidance in presence of partial overlap.
 - *Locked Room:* Teacher trained in open environment. Student encounters a locked door requiring a key. Tests guidance in presence of novel sub-tasks (key retrieval).
- **Multi-Task Setting (Overcooked):** A single-agent variant of a cooking environment where agents prepare different soups.
 - *Recipe Shift (2 $\Rightarrow$ 3 and 1 $\Rightarrow$ 3):* Teacher trained on a subset of recipes (e.g., 2 out of 3, or 1 out of 3), student needs to learn all 3. Tests guidance with unfamiliar recipe observations.
 - *Recipe + Layout Shift (2 $\Rightarrow$ 2):* Teacher trained on certain recipes in one layout; student learns different recipes in a new layout. Tests combined covariate shift.

**Baselines:**
EBTL is compared against:
1. **No Transfer:** Student trained from scratch.
2. **Action Advising (AA):** Teacher provides advice at every timestep, with a decaying rate.
3. **Fine-Tuning:** Student policy initialized from teacher, then trained on target task (convolutional layers frozen).
4. **Kickstarting RL (KSRL):** Policy distillation, adding a decaying cross-entropy loss to encourage student imitation.
5. **JumpStart RL (JSRL):** Time-based advising, teacher guides only during early steps of episodes.

All policies are trained using TorchRL's PPO implementation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrate that Energy-Based Transfer Learning (EBTL) provides consistent improvements in sample efficiency and performance across both single-task and multi-task reinforcement learning environments, especially under varying degrees of covariate shift.

**Overall Performance and Sample Efficiency:**
- EBTL consistently achieved higher sample efficiency and final policy returns compared to all baseline methods in both the Minigrid and Overcooked environments. This indicates that selective guidance based on familiarity leads to faster and more effective learning.

**Effectiveness of Selective Guidance:**
- **Minigrid Alternating-Goal Room:** EBTL significantly improved transfer performance when the energy threshold $q \geq 0.1$. The teacher successfully assigned higher energy scores to familiar states (goal in Room 1) and lower scores to unfamiliar states (goal in Room 3), enabling it to intervene only when its knowledge was relevant. This led to a higher rate of "correct guidance" (guidance in ID states) and a lower rate of "incorrect guidance" (guidance in OOD states) compared to baselines.
- **Minigrid Locked Room:** Despite the introduction of novel elements (key, locked door) creating a stronger covariate shift, the teacher was still able to meaningfully differentiate between pre-key (unfamiliar) and post-key (familiar) states, assigning consistently lower energy scores to the former. This allowed EBTL to maintain positive transfer.

**Impact of Covariate Shift on OOD Detection:**
- In the **Alternating-Goal Room**, where covariate shift was relatively low, the energy distributions for in-distribution and out-of-distribution states were well-separated (Figure 3c).
- In the **Locked Room**, the higher covariate shift made the separation less distinct, indicating a more challenging OOD detection problem (Figure 4c).
- In **Overcooked Recipe Shift** scenarios with shared layouts, the covariate shift primarily due to recipe encoding resulted in clearly bimodal energy distributions, simplifying OOD detection (Figure 6, Figure 7 top rows).
- However, in **Overcooked Recipe + Layout Shift**, where both recipes and layouts changed, there was a systematic decrease in ID energy scores, blurring the ID/OOD boundary. Even states associated with familiar recipes appeared slightly OOD due to the layout change. Despite this, EBTL still maintained positive transfer.

**Optimal Energy Threshold:**
- The performance curves (e.g., Figure 3a, Figure 4a) exhibited a "mountain-shaped" trend, suggesting the existence of an optimal energy threshold $q$. Initially, increasing $q$ improved performance by filtering harmful advice. However, if $q$ became too high, the teacher became overly conservative, withholding guidance even in helpful situations, leading to a decline in performance. Optimal performance was often observed in a range (e.g., $q \in [0.1, 0.7]$ for Minigrid).

**Role of Energy Regularization:**
- Energy regularization, by incorporating an energy-based loss during teacher training, significantly improved EBTL's convergence speed and overall performance (Figure 5a). This effect was particularly noticeable in scenarios with higher covariate shift, such as the Locked Room environment, where explicit training to separate ID from OOD states was more crucial. In contrast, other baselines showed no significant performance difference with or without energy regularization, underscoring its specific benefit to the EBTL framework.

**Robustness to Layout Complexity (Overcooked):**
- EBTL demonstrated stable positive transfer across both Simple Room and Ring Room layouts in Overcooked, achieving high returns and low variance. In contrast, baseline methods that lacked selective guidance, such as Action Advising (AA), experienced performance degradation and instability as layout complexity increased, suggesting that their unselective advice became increasingly detrimental.

**Sensitivity of Energy-Based Separation (Appendix D):**
- Analysis showed that the teacher's ability to distinguish between false and true OOD states remained robust across various settings of the energy regularization margins ($m_{in}$, $m_{out}$). Statistical tests (ANOVA and Kruskal-Wallis) yielded high p-values for various divergence metrics, indicating that the choice of these hyperparameters does not significantly impact the fundamental separation capability of the energy scores.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents Energy-Based Transfer Learning (EBTL) as a significant step towards more reliable and efficient knowledge transfer in reinforcement learning.

**Addressing the Negative Transfer Problem:** EBTL directly confronts the critical issue of negative transfer, where well-intentioned guidance from a teacher policy can hinder student learning if the underlying tasks diverge. By providing a principled mechanism for introspective decision-making, the method allows the teacher to discern when its experience is applicable, thereby preventing the student from being led astray by irrelevant or counterproductive advice. This capability is particularly important in complex, dynamic environments where task variations are common.

**Methodological Advance in Transfer Learning:** The integration of energy-based models and out-of-distribution detection into the RL transfer framework represents a novel methodological contribution. Unlike prior heuristic-based or parameter-copying approaches, EBTL offers a theoretically-grounded criterion (state visitation density linked to energy scores) for selective guidance. This shift from ad-hoc rules to a more formal foundation could lead to more robust and generalizable transfer learning solutions.

**Improved Sample Efficiency and Performance:** The empirical results consistently demonstrate that EBTL enhances sample efficiency and yields higher final returns across diverse tasks (navigation, multi-task cooking) and varying degrees of covariate shift. This implies that agents can learn new tasks faster and achieve better performance by selectively leveraging prior knowledge, which is crucial for real-world applications where data collection is expensive or time-consuming.

**Robustness and Generalizability:** The method's effectiveness across both single-task and multi-task settings, as well as its resilience to increasing covariate shift and environmental complexity (e.g., Minigrid Locked Room, Overcooked Ring Room), indicates its potential for broad applicability. The demonstrated robustness against varying energy regularization hyperparameters further supports its practical utility.

**Foundation for Future Research:** The paper identifies limitations, such as the need for a specified energy threshold and its primary focus on covariate shift rather than label shift. These limitations suggest promising avenues for future research, including the development of adaptive thresholding mechanisms and methods capable of handling broader types of task differences. Furthermore, the theoretical connection between energy scores and state visitation density could inspire new ways to characterize and leverage policy knowledge in RL.

In summary, EBTL offers a principled and effective approach to selective knowledge transfer, making reinforcement learning more efficient and robust in multi-task and continual learning scenarios. Its theoretical grounding and strong empirical performance underscore its potential to impact the development of more capable and adaptable AI agents.
