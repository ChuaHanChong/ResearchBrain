# A Snapshot of Influence: A Local Data Attribution Framework for Online Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign and Stanford University developed a local data attribution framework for online Reinforcement Learning (RL), specifically PPO, to understand and optimize its dynamic training process. Their Iterative Influence-Based Filtering (IIF) algorithm, derived from this framework, achieved 20-67% sample efficiency gains and reduced training runtime by 29-69% across RL benchmarks, while also demonstrating significant improvements in RLHF for large language models.

## Problem

- Online Reinforcement Learning (RL) frequently suffers from sample inefficiency and training instability, requiring extensive data and exhibiting inconsistent performance.
- A lack of interpretability in RL agents hinders understanding of their decision-making processes and the specific experiences that shape their behavior.
- Existing data attribution methods are fundamentally designed for static datasets and fixed objectives, making them unsuitable for the dynamic, continuously evolving nature of online RL.

## Method

- The paper introduced a local data attribution framework for online RL, focusing on ==Proximal Policy Optimization== (PPO), to analyze how individual experiences in a rollout buffer influence policy updates within a single training round.
- It adapted the gradient-based TracIn method to calculate influence scores for two key target functions: agent action and cumulative return, addressing the unique challenges of online RL's policy-dependent data distribution.
- An Iterative Influence-Based Filtering (IIF) algorithm was proposed, which iteratively identifies and removes records with the most negative influence on cumulative return before training the PPO agent.

## Results

- The IIF algorithm demonstrated substantial sample efficiency gains, reducing the number of training rounds by 20-**67%** and total runtime by 29-**69%** to reach peak performance compared to standard PPO on various RL benchmarks.
- IIF consistently achieved higher final returns than standard PPO and significantly outperformed baselines using random or heuristic-based data filtering.
- Applied to RLHF for large language models, IIF enabled approximately a **4x** reduction in total runtime, leading to higher training rewards and lower test toxicity in mitigation tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Records identified as negatively influential on cumulative return consistently exhibit inaccurate advantage estimates, indicating that misleading reward signals are a primary bottleneck in online RL training.
> - The formation of agent behaviors during training undergoes a three-stage phase transition: initial action-advantage association, followed by semantic clustering of states, and eventual influence saturation.
> - Removing identified detrimental training data within a single round directly improves policy performance, validating the framework's ability to purify the learning signal.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has achieved groundbreaking successes in complex decision-making tasks, from game playing to robotic control and the alignment of large language models (LLMs). Online RL, in particular, where agents continuously interact with and adapt to environments, is crucial for real-time and safety-critical applications like autonomous driving. However, online RL is plagued by significant challenges: sample inefficiency (requiring vast amounts of data), training instability (high variance and inconsistent performance), and a notorious lack of interpretability. The latter is especially problematic in high-stakes domains, where understanding *why* an agent makes certain decisions is as important as the decision itself.

The broader research landscape for RL interpretability has explored various avenues. Early efforts focused on "feature-level explanations" like saliency maps to highlight relevant parts of observations. Later work moved to "policy-level explanations," approximating learned policies with interpretable rules or designing transparent architectures. More recently, researchers have investigated identifying "critical states" or analyzing entire training trajectories to understand behavior formation. While these methods offer useful insights, many are either coarse-grained, computationally intensive, or limited to specific settings (e.g., requiring a sufficiently developed policy or offline data).

Data attribution has emerged as a powerful, complementary paradigm in machine learning interpretability, tracing model behavior back to individual training samples. Techniques like influence functions and TracIn have found applications in data selection, bias mitigation, and fact tracing. However, applying these methods directly to online RL is non-trivial. Existing data attribution methods are fundamentally designed for static datasets and fixed objectives, assuming that the training data distribution does not change. This assumption is explicitly violated in online RL, where the agent's evolving policy continuously generates new data, creating a circular dependency between the model and its training experiences. Each experience not only updates the policy but also *shapes* future data collection.

This paper addresses this critical methodological gap. It is the **first study to initiate the rigorous investigation of data attribution for online RL**, thereby introducing a principled framework to understand and improve these dynamic learning systems. By adapting established attribution techniques to account for the unique data-model feedback loop in online RL, it aims to unlock a new dimension of interpretability and efficiency, pushing the boundaries of what is possible in understanding and optimizing adaptive AI agents.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core motivation behind this research stems from the persistent challenges faced by modern online Reinforcement Learning (RL) systems:
1. **Sample Inefficiency:** Online RL often requires millions of interactions with the environment to converge, making it impractical for many real-world applications where data collection is costly or time-consuming.
2. **Training Instability and High Variance:** Performance can be inconsistent across different training runs or susceptible to minor environmental fluctuations, hindering reliable deployment.
3. **Limited Interpretability:** Understanding *why* an RL agent behaves in a particular way or *which specific experiences* contributed to a policy's undesirable actions remains largely opaque, especially in complex, deep RL settings. This lack of transparency is a major barrier to trust and deployment in safety-critical domains.

To address these issues, the paper sets out three key objectives:

- **Develop a Principled Data Attribution Framework for Online RL:** The primary objective is to overcome the fundamental incompatibility between existing data attribution methods (which assume static datasets) and the dynamic nature of online RL. This involves designing a framework that can account for the continuous interplay between policy updates and data generation, specifically focusing on the widely used Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm.
- **Gain Fresh Insights into Online RL Learning Dynamics:** Leverage the proposed framework to provide fine-grained explanations and diagnostic capabilities. This includes understanding what characterizes "helpful" vs. "harmful" training experiences, how agent behaviors form over time, and identifying bottlenecks in the learning process.
- **Improve the Efficiency and Effectiveness of Online RL Training:** Translate the interpretability insights into practical algorithmic improvements. Specifically, the paper aims to demonstrate that by identifying and selectively managing influential training data, online RL agents can achieve faster learning, improved sample efficiency, and superior final performance.

In essence, the paper is motivated by the belief that a deeper understanding of the influence of individual experiences on an RL agent's learning process is not merely an academic exercise in interpretability but a crucial step towards building more robust, efficient, and trustworthy adaptive AI systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The paper introduces a novel **local data attribution framework** specifically tailored for online Reinforcement Learning, focusing on the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm. This framework addresses the unique challenge of online RL: the circular dependency where the agent's policy influences data collection, and collected data influences policy updates.

The core approach involves three key components:

1. **Local Attribution Framework:** Instead of attempting to trace influence across the entire, complex training history, the framework focuses on a "local" scope. In each training round *k*, it examines how records within the current rollout buffer, $B^{(k)}$, contribute to the policy update from $\theta^{(k)}$ to $\theta^{(k+1)}$. This design choice circumvents the non-differentiable and cascading dependencies across multiple rounds, making the problem tractable.

2. **Entity of Attribution:** The atomic unit of attribution is an individual training record $z_i = (s_i, a_i, r_i, \log \pi_i, v_i, \hat{A}_i)$ within the rollout buffer. These are the fundamental pieces of experience that PPO uses for its updates.

3. **Target Functions:** To measure influence, the framework defines two target functions that capture crucial aspects of an RL agent's behavior:
 - **Agent Action ($f_{action}(\theta)$):** Defined as $\log \pi_{\theta}(a | s)$, this function quantifies how individual records influence the agent's propensity to take a specific action *a* in a given state *s*. It is primarily used for diagnostic purposes.
 - **Cumulative Return ($f_{return}(\theta)$):** Designed to measure the influence on the agent's ability to maximize long-term returns, this function is defined as $E_{\tau \sim \pi_{ref},(s,a)\sim\tau} [\log \pi_{\theta}(a | s) \hat{A}_{ref}(s, a)]$.
 - **Key Design Choices:** To overcome challenges like policy-dependent data distributions and high variance of raw returns, it uses a *reference policy* $\pi_{ref}$ (set to the policy at the beginning of the current round, $\pi_{\theta^{(k)}}$) for fixed evaluation, and *advantage estimates* $\hat{A}_{ref}$ (also from $\pi_{\theta^{(k)}}$) to reduce variance. This target function is structurally similar to the REINFORCE objective with a baseline, making it tractable and relevant for online RL.

4. **Method of Attribution:** The framework adapts **TracIn** [Pruthi et al., 2020], a gradient-based attribution method. For each record $z_i$ in round *k*, its influence score $I_i$ is computed by summing the inner product of the target function's gradient and the PPO loss's per-sample gradient over all optimization steps *j* within that round:
 $I_i := \sum_{j:z_i \in B^{(k)}_j} \langle \nabla_{\theta} f(\theta^{(k)}_j), \nabla_{\theta} L_{PPO}(\theta^{(k)}_j, z_i) \rangle$
 - **Computational Efficiency:** To reduce computational and storage costs associated with vanilla TracIn (which sums over all intermediate checkpoints), the paper makes two practical adaptations:
 - It computes influence scores using only the initial parameter $\theta^{(k)}$ for the entire rollout buffer, avoiding a full training pass.
 - It employs an efficient "ghost dot product" implementation for gradient calculations.

Building on this framework, the paper proposes an algorithm called **Iterative Influence-Based Filtering (IIF)**. In each training round, IIF:
1. Collects experiences into the rollout buffer.
2. Computes influence scores for all records in the buffer using the local attribution framework.
3. Filters out a percentage *p* of records identified as having the most negative influence (bottom records) on the cumulative return target function.
4. Trains the PPO agent on this *filtered* dataset.
This iterative process refines the policy by learning from a cleaner, more constructive set of experiences, and the improved policy then samples new data, closing the loop for continuous enhancement.

Finally, for **RLHF with Large Language Models**, the framework is adapted to a sequence-level objective $f_{seq}(\theta)$, which considers prompt-generation pairs as the attribution entity and emphasizes reward model feedback at the last token, reflecting the trajectory-centric nature of LLM generation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research demonstrates the power and utility of its local data attribution framework through several applications and a novel algorithm.

#### 4.1. Diagnosis of Learning: Characterizing Bottom Records

- **Finding:** The records identified as having the most negative influence on cumulative return ($f_{return}$) consistently feature **inaccurate advantage estimates**. These inaccuracies manifest in two ways:
 1. **Sign Mismatch:** A significant proportion of harmful records show opposite signs for the estimated advantage ($\hat{A}$) and the true Monte Carlo advantage ($\bar{A}$).
 2. **Large Magnitude Errors:** These records also exhibit substantial differences in magnitude between $\hat{A}$ and $\bar{A}$.
- **Implication:** This suggests that misleading advantage signals during training (rewarding bad actions, penalizing good ones) are a primary bottleneck for online RL, causing harmful gradient steps. The framework effectively pinpoints these problematic experiences.

#### 4.2. Temporal Analysis of Behavior Formation: Phase Transition

- **Finding:** Tracking the evolution of top records (most positive influence on `f_action` for a specific behavior) reveals an intriguing **three-stage phase transition** in how agent behaviors are shaped:
 1. **Initial Association:** Early in training, top records are characterized by simple action-advantage associations (target action with positive $\hat{A}$, alternative actions with negative $\hat{A}$), largely ignoring state context.
 2. **Semantic Clustering:** As the agent learns more nuanced representations, top records show action-advantage associations within states that are *semantically similar* to the target state. This indicates the agent's ability to generalize.
 3. **Influence Saturation:** Near convergence, influence scores stabilize and become noisy, and the structure of top records becomes less clear, although the basic action-advantage association persists.
- **Quantification:** This phase transition is quantitatively supported by analyzing the "roughness" of a semantic similarity graph of records, which significantly drops during the semantic clustering phase.

#### 4.3. Targeted Interventions During Training: Amplifying Policy Gain

- **Finding:** A simple intervention—removing records with negative influence on `f_return` within a single training round and retraining—consistently **improves policy performance** across various environments.
- **Implication:** This "sanity check" validates the framework's ability to accurately identify detrimental data and demonstrates that purging such data can purify the learning signal, leading to direct performance benefits.

#### 4.4. Iterative Influence-Based Filtering (IIF) for Online RL Training

- **Overall Performance:** IIF, the proposed algorithm that iteratively filters negative-influence records, significantly enhances online RL training across a diverse suite of standard RL benchmarks (classic control, navigation, locomotion) and in RLHF for LLMs.
- **Sample Efficiency:** IIF achieves **substantial sample efficiency gains**, demonstrating a 20-67% reduction in the number of training rounds required to match or surpass the performance of standard PPO.
- **Computational Cost:** The overhead for influence calculation in IIF is **negligible**. Combined with the reduced number of optimization steps (due to fewer records after filtering), IIF often leads to a **significant reduction in total runtime** (29-69% faster to reach peak performance).
- **Final Performance:** IIF consistently **achieves higher final returns** compared to standard PPO, indicating that filtering harmful experiences leads to more robust and effective policies.
- **RLHF for LLMs:** Applied to toxicity mitigation in LLMs via RLHF, IIF shows remarkable improvements. It reduces the number of training rounds and optimization time, leading to an **approximate 4x reduction in total runtime** while achieving higher training rewards and lower test toxicity.
- **Comparison to Baselines:** Random filtering performs significantly worse than standard training, confirming that IIF's gains stem from principled data attribution, not just data reduction. Heuristic-based filtering (e.g., based on advantage estimates or TD errors) works in very simple environments but fails in more complex ones, highlighting the robustness and nuance of IIF's gradient-based influence scores.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work marks a significant advancement in the field of reinforcement learning by **pioneering the study of data attribution for online RL**. Its contributions extend across interpretability, efficiency, and robustness, with far-reaching potential impacts:

- **Enhanced Interpretability of Online RL:** The proposed local data attribution framework provides an unprecedented fine-grained lens into the internal mechanics of online RL agents. By identifying which specific experiences contribute positively or negatively to policy updates and behavior formation, it moves beyond superficial explanations to offer actionable insights. This increased transparency is crucial for building trust and enabling responsible deployment of RL systems in safety-critical domains where understanding agent decisions is paramount.
- **Improved Efficiency and Effectiveness of RL Training:** The Iterative Influence-Based Filtering (IIF) algorithm demonstrates a practical and principled method to directly address long-standing challenges of sample inefficiency and training instability in online RL. By systematically pruning detrimental experiences, IIF accelerates learning, reduces computational costs, and often leads to higher-performing final policies. This can significantly lower the barriers to entry for complex RL applications and make cutting-edge RL techniques more accessible and scalable.
- **Bridging Fundamental Gaps in ML Interpretability:** The paper successfully adapts advanced data attribution techniques to a challenging, dynamic setting where existing methods falter. This extends the applicability of influence analysis to a whole new class of adaptive learning systems, stimulating future research at the intersection of interpretability and sequential decision-making.
- **Direct Impact on RLHF for LLMs:** The successful application of IIF to Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback for large language models highlights its immediate relevance to one of the most impactful and rapidly evolving areas of AI. By improving the efficiency and robustness of RLHF, this work can accelerate the development of safer, more aligned, and more capable LLMs.
- **Opening New Research Directions:** The paper identifies several promising avenues for future work, including tailoring attribution methods for adaptive optimizers (like Adam), extending the framework to a broader range of online RL algorithms (e.g., GRPO for LLMs), and tackling the complex problem of counterfactual interpretation in dynamic RL settings. These directions suggest that the framework laid out here is a foundational step for a new wave of research in interpretable and efficient adaptive AI.

In summary, "A Snapshot of Influence" provides both foundational theoretical advancements and immediate practical utility, positioning itself as a key contribution towards making online reinforcement learning more understandable, robust, and effective for real-world challenges.
