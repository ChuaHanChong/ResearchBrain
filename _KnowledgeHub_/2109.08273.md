# ThriftyDAgger: Budget-Aware Novelty and Risk Gating for Interactive Imitation Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at UC Berkeley's AUTOLAB developed ThriftyDAgger, an interactive imitation learning algorithm that intelligently balances robot task performance with human supervisor burden. It achieved superior task success rates across simulated and physical robotics tasks, while substantially reducing the human workload, particularly in multi-robot supervision scenarios.

## Problem

- Traditional imitation learning suffers from a distribution mismatch problem, leading to brittle robot policies when encountering unfamiliar states.
- Interactive imitation learning, while improving robustness, places a significant burden on human supervisors through continuous monitoring and frequent interventions.
- Existing robot-gated interactive learning methods often use incomplete intervention criteria (e.g., novelty alone) and require tedious manual tuning of multiple parameters.

## Method

- **ThriftyDAgger** introduces a novel ==robot-gated switching policy== that combines ==state novelty== and a new learned ==risk metric== (probability of task failure) to determine when to solicit human interventions.
- It employs ==budget-aware automatic parameter tuning==, allowing supervisors to specify a desired context switching rate, which the system uses to set intervention thresholds.
- The algorithm utilizes ==asymmetric switching criteria==, requiring stricter conditions for the robot to regain autonomous control, promoting longer and more informative human interventions.

## Results

- ThriftyDAgger achieved the highest autonomous success rate (**73%** in peg insertion simulation, **12/15** in physical cable routing) compared to prior methods, indicating improved policy robustness.
- In a user study involving a fleet of three robots, it yielded higher task throughput (**9.2** vs. **3.0-5.1** for others) and allowed human supervisors to perform better on a secondary task, reflecting reduced burden.
- Ablation studies demonstrated that both the novelty and risk components are crucial for the algorithm's performance, as removing either significantly decreased autonomous success rates (to **49%** in simulation).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Combining state novelty (unfamiliarity) with a learned risk measure (likelihood of task failure) provides a more comprehensive and effective criterion for identifying critical states where human intervention is truly beneficial.
> - Allowing human supervisors to specify a desired context switching rate, rather than tuning individual thresholds, simplifies deployment and enables the system to adapt to varying human availability.
> - Employing asymmetric control switching, with stricter conditions for the robot to cede control back to itself, reduces the frequency of costly context switches and encourages more thorough, data-rich human interventions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper situates itself within the evolving landscape of **Imitation Learning (IL)** for robotics, a field that aims to enable robots to learn complex behaviors by observing human demonstrations. Traditionally, IL has relied on offline datasets of expert demonstrations (Behavior Cloning), which often suffer from a critical limitation: the **distribution mismatch problem**. When the robot deviates from the demonstrated trajectories into unfamiliar states, its learned policy can become brittle, leading to compounded errors and task failure in real-world environments.

**Interactive Imitation Learning (IIL)** emerged as a promising paradigm to address this brittleness. In IIL, a human supervisor provides online feedback and corrective interventions as the robot operates, guiding it through novel or challenging situations. This human-in-the-loop approach allows the robot to collect data in the state distribution it actually experiences, leading to more robust policies. However, the efficacy of IIL is often bottlenecked by the **burden imposed on the human supervisor**. Continuous monitoring and frequent interventions demand significant human time, attention, and cognitive load, making it difficult to scale to complex tasks or large fleets of robots.

Prior work in IIL can be broadly categorized into:
- **Human-gated algorithms (e.g., HG-DAgger):** These require the human supervisor to continuously monitor the robot and decide when and for how long to intervene. While allowing expert judgment, this approach imposes maximum burden and does not scale well, especially for multi-robot supervision.
- **Robot-gated algorithms (e.g., SafeDAgger, EnsembleDAgger, LazyDAgger):** These algorithms empower the robot to actively query for interventions based on predefined criteria, thereby reducing the need for constant human vigilance. However, existing robot-gated methods often struggle with designing effective intervention criteria that balance learning efficiency with minimal human burden. Some rely on state novelty (uncertainty in action prediction) or predicted action discrepancy between robot and human policies. The authors argue that novelty alone is insufficient as it doesn't account for the precision required in "bottleneck" states, while action discrepancy can be misleading if high discrepancy is permissible in non-critical states. Furthermore, these algorithms often require careful, task-specific tuning of multiple parameters, which can be a tedious and expert-dependent process.

**ThriftyDAgger** directly addresses these limitations by introducing a novel robot-gated IIL algorithm that:
1. **Combines two distinct intervention criteria:** state novelty and a new risk metric, which measures the probability of task failure. This provides a more comprehensive understanding of when human help is truly needed.
2. **Offers budget-aware, automatic parameter tuning:** It allows the supervisor to specify a desired context switching rate ($\alpha_h$) rather than manually tuning individual thresholds, simplifying deployment and ensuring human availability is respected.
3. **Employs asymmetric switching criteria:** Building on previous work like LazyDAgger, it encourages lengthier, more informative interventions while reducing the frequency of costly context switches.

By tackling the dual challenges of policy robustness and human burden with a principled, budget-aware approach, ThriftyDAgger aims to push the boundaries of scalable and practical interactive robot learning, particularly for scenarios involving human supervision of multiple robotic agents.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to develop an interactive imitation learning algorithm that can **effectively balance task performance and the burden imposed on a human supervisor**, particularly when working within a predefined budget of human interventions. The authors formalize human burden to include the cost of context switching (latency) and the time spent providing actual interventions.

The specific motivations driving ThriftyDAgger's development are:
- **Addressing the limitations of existing robot-gated IIL algorithms:** Previous methods often rely on intervention criteria (e.g., state novelty, action discrepancy) that are either incomplete or can lead to suboptimal decisions about when to solicit help. The paper argues for a more nuanced understanding of when human intervention is truly valuable.
- **Introducing a comprehensive intervention criterion:** The motivation is to identify states where the robot truly needs help, not just because it's in an unfamiliar state, but also because it's in a familiar state that is prone to failure (a "bottleneck" state requiring high precision). This led to the development of combined novelty and risk measures.
- **Enabling budget-aware operation:** Recognizing that human time is a finite and costly resource, the algorithm aims to allow supervisors to specify their availability (a desired context switching rate) and have the system automatically adapt its intervention requests to stay within this budget, eliminating the need for tedious manual parameter tuning.
- **Scaling human supervision to robot fleets:** The problem becomes even more critical when a single human supervises multiple robots. Minimizing burden and ensuring timely, informative interventions are crucial for efficient multi-robot operation.
- **Enhancing real-world applicability:** The design principles of ThriftyDAgger are motivated by the need for robot learning systems that can be deployed in practical, human-centric scenarios, such as warehouses (fleet learning) or surgical environments (high-precision manipulation), where reliable performance and manageable human workload are paramount.

In essence, ThriftyDAgger seeks to make interactive robot learning more efficient, less burdensome for humans, and more adaptable to real-world constraints by intelligently deciding *when* and *for how long* to ask for help.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

ThriftyDAgger operates within an iterative **DAgger-like framework**, where the robot policy is continuously refined by aggregating data from human interventions. Its core innovation lies in its novel **switching policy**, which determines whether the robot operates autonomously or cedes control to a human supervisor based on two estimated properties of the current state: **novelty** and **risk**.

The algorithm proceeds through the following key stages:

1. **Initialization:**
 - An initial robot policy ($\pi_r$) is trained via Behavior Cloning on a small offline dataset of human demonstrations ($D_h$).
 - An initial offline dataset ($D_r$) is collected by rolling out $\pi_r$.
 - A Q-function ($\hat{Q}_{\pi_r,G}$) for risk estimation is initialized by optimizing Equation (5) on $D_r \cup D_h$.
 - Key parameters ($\beta_h, \beta_r, \delta_h, \delta_r$) governing switching thresholds are automatically initialized based on a user-specified desired context switching rate ($\alpha_h$).

2. **Novelty Estimation (Section 4.1):**
 - To quantify how "unfamiliar" a state $s$ is to the robot policy, ThriftyDAgger trains an **ensemble of policies** using bootstrapped samples from collected supervisor trajectories.
 - The **Novelty(s)** metric is then calculated as the variance of the policy outputs (action vectors) at state $s$ across the ensemble members. High variance indicates a state far from the distribution of previously encountered states, suggesting the robot policy cannot be trusted.

3. **Risk Estimation (Section 4.2):**
 - To quantify the likelihood of task failure, the paper introduces a novel **Risk(s, a)** metric. This is derived from a learned Q-function, $\hat{Q}_{\pi_r,G}(s, a)$, which estimates the discounted probability of successfully converging to the goal set $G$ from state $s$ by taking action $a$ and following $\pi_r$ thereafter (Equation 4).
 - **Risk$_{\pi_r}(s,a) = 1 - \hat{Q}_{\pi_r,G}(s,a)$**. A high risk value means a low probability of task success.
 - The Q-function is trained on collected transitions ($s_t, a_t, s_{t+1}$) from both offline data and online interventions by minimizing an MSE loss (Equation 6), inspired by standard Q-learning. The goal is for the Q-function to distinguish risky from non-risky states, not necessarily to be perfectly accurate for RL.

4. **Regulating Switches in Control Modes (Section 4.3):**
 - **Autonomous to Supervisor (Intervention criteria):** The robot initiates a switch to supervisor mode if the current state $s_t$ is either **sufficiently novel** (Novelty($s_t$) > $\delta_h$) OR **sufficiently risky** (Risk$_{\pi_r}(s_t, \pi_r(s_t))$ > $\beta_h$). Crucially, risk is only considered for non-novel states, as the learned risk measure is more reliable within familiar state distributions.
 - **Supervisor to Autonomous (Ceding criteria):** The robot switches back to autonomous mode if **both** the action discrepancy between the robot and human policies ($||\pi_r(s_t) - \pi_h(s_t)||_2^2$) is below a threshold $\delta_r$ AND the robot's task failure risk (Risk$_{\pi_r}(s_t, \pi_r(s_t))$) is below a threshold $\beta_r$. The risk metric ensures task completion probability, while action discrepancy verifies familiarity. An asymmetric switching criterion is used, with stricter conditions for ceding control ($\beta_r < \beta_h$), to encourage longer, more informative interventions and reduce frequent context switches.

5. **Computing Risk and Novelty Thresholds from Data (Section 4.4):**
 - To address the challenge of parameter tuning, ThriftyDAgger allows the user to specify a desired **context switching rate ($\alpha_h$)**.
 - Based on $\alpha_h$, the intervention thresholds ($\beta_h$, $\delta_h$) are **automatically set** to be the $(1-\alpha_h)$-quantiles of the observed Risk and Novelty values across previously visited states by $\pi_r$. This ensures that interventions are solicited approximately $\alpha_h$ percent of the time.
 - The ceding thresholds ($\delta_r, \beta_r$) are set to the mean action discrepancy and median risk, respectively, for supervisor-visited states (with $\beta_r$ adjustable for desired intervention length).

6. **Iterative Training Loop (Section 4.5):**
 - For N episodes, the robot executes actions, switching between autonomous and supervisor control based on the above criteria.
 - Data collected in autonomous mode is added to $D_r$; data from supervisor mode (human interventions) is added to $D_h$.
 - After each episode, the robot policy $\pi_r$ is updated using supervised learning on $D_h$, and the Q-function $\hat{Q}_{\pi_r,G}$ is updated on $D_r \cup D_h$.

This integrated approach allows ThriftyDAgger to intelligently solicit interventions only when truly necessary, manage the human workload, and learn robust policies in challenging robotic environments.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The effectiveness of ThriftyDAgger is evaluated through a series of experiments across diverse domains: a simulated peg insertion task, a human user study involving a fleet of three simulated robots, and a physical cable routing task with a surgical robot.

#### 4.1. Peg Insertion in Simulation

- **Autonomous Success Rate:** ThriftyDAgger achieves the highest autonomous success rate (73/100) among all compared algorithms, significantly outperforming prior robot-gated methods (SafeDAgger: 24/100, LazyDAgger: 48/100) and even surpassing human-gated DAgger (HG-DAgger: 57/100). Behavior Cloning performs poorly (24/100).
- **Intervention-Aided Success Rate:** When interventions are allowed at execution time, ThriftyDAgger achieves a 100% success rate (20/20), indicating its ability to identify critical states for human assistance.
- **Human Burden:** While ThriftyDAgger requests a similar number of *interventions* as other robot-gated algorithms, it requires more *human actions* per intervention. This is attributed to its conservative exit criterion (stricter $\beta_r < \beta_h$), which encourages lengthier, more thorough interventions. This trade-off is beneficial when context switching costs (latency) are high.
- **Ablation Studies:** Both ablations (ThriftyDAgger (-Novelty) and ThriftyDAgger (-Risk)) show significantly lower autonomous success rates (49/100 for both), demonstrating that both the novelty and risk measures are crucial for the algorithm's performance.

#### 4.2. User Study: Controlling A Fleet of Three Robots in Simulation

- **Throughput & Efficiency:** ThriftyDAgger significantly outperforms all prior algorithms in total task successes (throughput: 9.2 $\pm$ 2.0 vs. HG-DAgger: 5.1 $\pm$ 1.9, SafeDAgger: 3.0 $\pm$ 2.4, LazyDAgger: 5.1 $\pm$ 1.7). It achieves this with fewer interventions and fewer total human actions.
- **Human Performance & Burden:** ThriftyDAgger enables higher performance on an independent human concentration task (33.0 $\pm$ 8.5 pairs vs. SafeDAgger: 17.7 $\pm$ 8.2, LazyDAgger: 20.9 $\pm$ 7.9) and reduces the mean idle time for the robots in the fleet. Qualitative NASA-TLX survey results indicate that users experience lower mental load and frustration with ThriftyDAgger and LazyDAgger compared to HG-DAgger (due to constant monitoring) and SafeDAgger (due to frequent, short interventions).
- **Wall Clock Time:** ThriftyDAgger required the lowest amount of human wall clock time for active teleoperation, allowing humans more time for other tasks.

#### 4.3. Physical Experiment: Visuomotor Cable Routing

- **Autonomous Success Rate:** ThriftyDAgger achieves a high autonomous success rate (12/15), comparable to HG-DAgger (10/15) and significantly better than Behavior Cloning (0/15).
- **Intervention-Aided Success Rate:** It achieves a 100% intervention-aided success rate (15/15) with minimal supervision at execution time, reinforcing its ability to precisely identify intervention points.
- **Human Burden:** ThriftyDAgger requires fewer interventions and a similar number of total human actions during training compared to HG-DAgger, demonstrating its efficiency in a real-world, image-based manipulation task.

In summary, the results consistently show that ThriftyDAgger strikes a superior balance between learning robust policies and minimizing human supervisor burden. Its novel combination of novelty and risk criteria, coupled with budget-aware parameter tuning, leads to higher task success rates and improved human-robot collaboration, particularly in multi-robot scenarios.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

ThriftyDAgger represents a significant step forward in the field of interactive imitation learning, offering a principled and practical solution to the long-standing challenge of balancing robot learning efficiency with human supervisor workload. Its significance and potential impact can be highlighted in several areas:

1. **Reduced Human Burden and Enhanced Scalability:** The most immediate impact of ThriftyDAgger is its ability to substantially reduce the cognitive load and active engagement required from human supervisors. By autonomously and intelligently determining *when* to ask for help based on a user-defined budget, it frees humans to perform other tasks or supervise a larger fleet of robots. This is crucial for deploying robotic systems in real-world settings like warehouses, self-driving car operations, or even remote surgery, where human operators oversee many agents or have other primary responsibilities. The user study emphatically demonstrates this benefit, showing improved human performance on a distractor task and lower mental load.

2. **Improved Policy Robustness:** The novel combination of novelty and risk as intervention criteria addresses a critical shortcoming of prior methods. By not only detecting unfamiliar states but also identifying "bottleneck" or high-precision states prone to failure (even if somewhat familiar), ThriftyDAgger ensures that interventions are solicited at the most informative moments. This leads to the learning of more robust and reliable robot policies, as evidenced by its consistently higher autonomous and intervention-aided success rates across diverse tasks and environments. The 100% intervention-aided success rate in both simulation and physical tasks is particularly impactful, suggesting that the algorithm effectively identifies critical states for recovery.

3. **Practical Deployability via Budget-Aware Tuning:** The ability for a user to specify a desired context switching rate ($\alpha_h$) and have the algorithm automatically tune its intervention thresholds is a major practical advancement. It removes the need for expert knowledge and tedious hyperparameter tuning, making ThriftyDAgger more accessible and deployable by non-experts. This "budget-aware" nature allows the system to adapt to varying human availability (e.g., lower intervention rates at night, higher during training vs. deployment).

4. **Foundation for Future Interactive Learning:** The methodology presented in ThriftyDAgger, particularly the learned risk metric based on Q-functions and the combination of heterogeneous uncertainty measures, could inspire future research in interactive learning. The authors explicitly suggest applying these ideas to interactive reinforcement learning, where human feedback could guide exploration and accelerate learning in complex environments without solely relying on reward functions.

5. **Addressing "Long Tail" Problems:** By effectively managing interventions, ThriftyDAgger helps tackle the "long tail" of robotics problems – rare but critical situations where autonomous systems struggle. Human interventions in these specific, high-risk, or novel states provide invaluable data to make the robot more robust over time.

In conclusion, ThriftyDAgger offers a robust, user-friendly, and scalable approach to interactive imitation learning. Its contributions are poised to accelerate the deployment of intelligent robotic systems in complex, dynamic environments, fundamentally altering the nature of human-robot collaboration by making human supervision more efficient and less taxing. The future work suggested by the authors, exploring adaptive $\alpha_h$ and scaling to larger physical fleets, underscores the significant potential for this research to evolve and address even broader challenges in advanced robotics.
