# What Matters in Orchestrating Robot Policies: A Systematic Study of Hierarchical VLA Agents

> [!summary] Summary
> Google DeepMind researchers systematically studied hierarchical Vision-Language-Action (Hi-VLA) robot systems, revealing that specific design choices for VLM reasoning, VLA policies, termination conditions, observation representations, and memory are critical. Their optimized hierarchy achieved substantial performance gains on complex robot manipulation tasks in both simulation and real-world settings.

## Problem

- Monolithic Vision-Language-Action (VLA) systems struggle with complex tasks requiring long-horizon planning, compositional skills, or abstract reasoning.
- Existing hierarchical VLA (Hi-VLA) systems lack unified design principles, making it challenging to identify critical components and their interactions.
- It is difficult to systematically evaluate the impact of individual design choices for components like the high-level VLM, low-level VLA, switching mechanisms, and memory.

## Method

- Unified various Hi-VLA agents under a common control framework, conceptualizing the VLM as a high-level planner and the VLA as a low-level controller.
- Systematically evaluated different implementations for high-level VLM policies, low-level VLA policies, termination conditions, observation representations, and memory mechanisms.
- Benchmarked design choices across short-horizon, long-horizon, and reasoning-intensive manipulation tasks in simulation, with real-robot validation.

## Results

- An 'Optimized Hierarchy', combining the best design choices, achieved significantly higher success rates on long-horizon and reasoning-intensive tasks compared to 'Naive Hierarchy' and 'Flat VLA' baselines.
- The hierarchical approach, along with its orchestration components, remained critical for performance even with a highly capable scripted low-level VLA, preventing a drastic performance degradation on challenging tasks.
- Real-robot experiments on an ALOHA platform corroborated simulation findings, demonstrating the practical transferability and performance advantages of the optimized hierarchical system in physical environments.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The VLM's reasoning capability (e.g., 'thinking' passes) is more critical for high-level planning, especially in long-horizon tasks, than its raw model size.
> - Effective hierarchical orchestration requires steerable low-level VLA policies; fine-tuning that compromises instruction following can be detrimental to overall system performance.
> - Rich textual observation representations (e.g., derived from bounding boxes or privileged contact information) and cross-episodic memory summarization are high-leverage interface mechanisms connecting VLM planning and VLA control.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics has seen advancements through the development of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. These models are designed to map natural-language commands directly to robot actions, exhibiting capabilities in generalization across various tasks. Examples include models such as RT-2, Pi, OpenVLA, and Gemini Robotics. While these monolithic VLA systems offer steerability and can execute open-ended prompts, they encounter limitations when addressing tasks that involve long-horizon planning, compositional skills, or abstract reasoning. These limitations are attributed to training data primarily consisting of short trajectory segments and the potential for large Vision-Language Models (VLMs) to experience catastrophic forgetting of their reasoning capabilities when fine-tuned on action data.

Hierarchical VLA (Hi-VLA) systems have emerged as a method to address these challenges. Inspired by biological cognitive processes, Hi-VLA architectures typically involve a high-level VLM acting as a planner that decomposes complex tasks into language subgoals, which are then executed by a low-level VLA controller. This division of labor aims to integrate the semantic understanding and compositional strengths of VLMs with the physical grounding abilities of VLAs. Prior Hi-VLA systems, including G0, Humanoid-VLA, RoboOS, Hi-Robot, Pi-0.5, Gemini Robotics 1.5, HAMSTER, and Helix, have demonstrated success in multi-step tasks, cross-embodiment adaptation, and semantic reasoning.

Despite the empirical progress in Hi-VLA systems, there has been a lack of systematic investigation into their underlying design principles. Existing systems implement various choices for VLM planners, VLA controllers, switching mechanisms between the two, and the representation of observations and memory for the planner. This diversity in design has made it challenging to ascertain which components are critical, how they interact, and their specific relevance to different types of robotic tasks. This paper positions itself within this landscape by conducting a systematic study to provide a principled understanding of Hi-VLA design, aiming to identify effective design choices and their impact on system performance.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to conduct a systematic study of Hierarchical VLA (Hi-VLA) design for robot manipulation. This study aims to unify representative Hi-VLA agents under a common control framework and benchmark core design choices across a range of task complexities.

The motivation for this work arises from several factors:
- **Lack of Unified Design Principles:** Despite the emergence and empirical progress of Hi-VLA systems, there is an absence of established guidelines or unified design principles. Existing Hi-VLA agents often differ significantly in their architectural choices and component implementations.
- **Complexity of Hi-VLA Systems:** A Hi-VLA agent is a complex integration of multiple components. Critical design decisions include:
 - The specific VLM model used for high-level planning.
 - The VLA policy for low-level control.
 - The method for representing observations to the VLM.
 - The criteria for switching control between the VLA and VLM (termination conditions).
 - The mechanism for memory and historical information processing.
- **Difficulty in Component Evaluation:** The varied implementations across different Hi-VLA systems make it difficult to isolate the impact of individual components, understand their interactions, or determine which design choices are most beneficial for particular task regimes (e.g., short-horizon, long-horizon, or reasoning-intensive tasks).
- **Bridging the Gap between VLM and VLA Strengths:** While VLMs possess strong reasoning and compositional capabilities and VLAs offer physical grounding, effectively combining these strengths in a hierarchical system requires careful orchestration. Unoptimized hierarchical designs may not fully leverage the potential of either component.
- **Improving System Capabilities and Robustness:** A deeper understanding of Hi-VLA design is motivated by the potential to construct more capable, robust, and principled hierarchical agents, enabling them to tackle more complex and open-ended robotic manipulation challenges effectively.

By systematically evaluating design choices, the authors intend to distill practical principles that can inform the development of future Hi-VLA systems, moving beyond ad-hoc design to a more principled approach.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a systematic empirical study to evaluate various design choices within hierarchical VLA systems. The methodology is structured around a unified control framework and comprehensive benchmarking across diverse task categories.

#### 3.1. Unified Control Framework

The authors first unify disparate hierarchical VLA systems under a shared control loop inspired by the options framework. This framework conceptualizes the low-level VLA as an intra-option policy (π_VLA), which takes a language instruction (subgoal) and an observation to produce robot actions. The high-level VLM is viewed as an option selection policy (π_VLM), responsible for generating language instructions based on observations and the overall task instruction.
The unified control loop (Algorithm 1 in the paper) includes:
- **High-level VLM Execution Loop:** The VLM observes the environment, processes the image using an observation representation module (ϕ), updates a memory module (mem) with historical interactions, and generates a language instruction (l).
- **Low-level VLA Execution Loop:** The VLA executes the generated language instruction for multiple steps until a termination condition (β) is met, at which point control returns to the VLM.
This framework allows for isolating and evaluating major design choices systematically.

#### 3.2. Components Under Study

The study systematically evaluates the impact of different implementations for each component within this unified framework:
- **High-level VLM Policy:** Investigates the effect of VLM model size and reasoning capabilities.
 - **Models:** Gemini 2.5 series (Lite, Flash, Pro).
 - **Reasoning Capability:** Comparing inference with and without "thinking" capability enabled (multiple internal passes for refinement).
- **Low-level VLA Policy:** Examines the influence of VLA model size and training data.
 - **Models:** Gemini Robotics On-Device (GROD) Model in different configurations: GROD (large, real data), GROD (small, real data), and GROD (small, fine-tuned with in-domain simulation data).
- **Termination Conditions:** Evaluates mechanisms for switching control from VLA to VLM.
 - **Methods:** Fixed Frequency (preset duration), Success Detection (VLM-based check of subgoal completion), and VLM Termination (VLM predicts execution time).
 - **Parametric Study:** For Fixed Frequency, the effect of varying execution horizon (e.g., 2, 4, 8, 14, 20 seconds) is explored.
 - **Robustness Study:** For Success Detection, the impact of false positive and false negative error rates (10%, 30%, 50%) is assessed.
- **Observation Representations:** Explores how observations are presented to the high-level VLM.
 - **Methods:** Raw Image (no additional text), Naive Summarization (VLM describes image), Summarize with Privileged Info (VLM describes image with contact information from simulator), and Summarize with Bounding Box (VLM generates bounding boxes, then describes the scene using this info).
- **Memory:** Investigates the impact of historical interaction context provided to the VLM.
 - **Memory Length:** Accumulating VLM history for 1, 3, 5 steps, or the entire episode.
 - **Memory Summarization:** No summary, summary of the last step, summary of the current episode, or summary of previous episodes (extracting affordances).

#### 3.3. Evaluation Setup

- **Environment:** Experiments are conducted in the MuJoCo ALOHA suite, a simulated table-top manipulation benchmark. Selected experiments are also validated on a real ALOHA robot to confirm sim-to-real transferability.
- **Task Categories:** Tasks are categorized to disentangle different capabilities:
 - **Short-horizon:** Tasks resembling typical VLA training trajectories (e.g., single object pick and place).
 - **Long-horizon:** Tasks requiring composition of multiple short-horizon skills, significantly longer than VLA training trajectories.
 - **Reasoning:** Tasks requiring interpretation of indirect or semantic instructions.
- **Metrics:** Performance is measured by average success rate for each task category, averaged over 5 tasks and 200 independent trials per task. Standard errors are reported.
- **Default Configuration:** For consistency, when one component is being evaluated, the others are set to a standard configuration: Gemini 2.5 Flash with thinking (VLM), GROD 3B (VLA), fixed frequency termination (8 seconds), scene description with contact information (observation representation), and a 3-step memory window without summarization.

#### 3.4. Aggregation of Discoveries

Finally, the best-performing design choices from individual component evaluations are aggregated to form an "Optimized Hierarchy." This is then compared against a "Naive Hierarchy" (using simple choices for each component) and a "Flat VLA" (no hierarchical VLM planner) on both simulation and real robot tasks. An additional experiment investigates the impact of improved VLA capabilities on the necessity of hierarchical orchestration using a scripted low-level policy.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The systematic study yielded several findings regarding the orchestration of robot policies within hierarchical VLA agents:

#### 4.1. Effect of High-level VLM Policy

- **Reasoning Capability:** Enabling the "thinking" capability in VLMs consistently led to improved performance across all task categories, with long-horizon tasks benefiting more significantly. This suggests that the VLM's internal reasoning processes are critical for effectively utilizing available information in complex tasks.
- **Model Size:** The study indicated that the VLM model size (Lite, Flash, Pro within the Gemini 2.5 series) did not show a substantial impact on overall performance when the thinking capability was active. Smaller, reasoning-enabled models performed comparably to larger ones, suggesting that current robotics benchmarks may not fully leverage the additional knowledge or instruction-following abilities of larger VLMs.

#### 4.2. Effect of Low-Level VLA Policy

- **VLA Size:** A larger VLA model (GROD-3B) demonstrated higher performance than a smaller model (GROD-1B) across all task types. This was attributed to improved instruction following and generalization capabilities of the larger model, which are beneficial for VLM orchestration.
- **Fine-tuning Impact:** Fine-tuning a smaller GROD model with in-domain simulation data resulted in the lowest performance, particularly for long-horizon tasks. This outcome suggests that such fine-tuning can compromise the VLA's instruction-following ability (steerability), which is critical for effective integration within a hierarchical system.

#### 4.3. Termination Conditions

- **Success Detection:** Using a success detector as a termination condition generally achieved good performance, indicating its utility in improving hierarchical system effectiveness by enabling accurate switching between VLM and VLA.
- **VLM Termination:** Having the VLM predict the expected execution time for a command performed worst. This is likely due to the inherent stochasticity of low-level VLA execution, making accurate temporal predictions difficult for the VLM.
- **Fixed Frequency:** Short-horizon tasks were less sensitive to the choice of termination condition. For fixed-frequency termination, a moderate VLA execution horizon (e.g., 4-8 seconds) was found to balance computational cost (less frequent VLM queries) with performance, avoiding significant drops seen with overly long horizons that could cause timeouts in multi-step tasks.
- **Robustness to Error:** The success detector demonstrated robustness to moderate detection errors (e.g., 10% error rate showed no performance degradation and a slight boost in some cases). However, increasing false positive errors substantially reduced system performance, as premature termination led the VLM to progress to subsequent, unachievable subgoals. False negatives had a more moderate impact, partly due to the assumption of independent errors across timesteps in the simulation.

#### 4.4. Observation Representations

- **Textual Augmentation:** Augmenting raw image observations with structured text descriptions significantly improved performance. Descriptions derived from VLM-generated bounding box information or privileged contact information yielded notable boosts over raw images or naive summarization.
- **Privileged Information:** Incorporating privileged contact information from the simulator when generating text descriptions consistently led to the highest performance. This finding suggests that VLMs might sometimes overlook crucial visual details in raw image inputs, particularly as task complexity increases, making explicit textual cues beneficial.

#### 4.5. Memory

- **Raw History Length:** Varying the length of raw in-episode history provided to the VLM (1, 3, 5 steps, or full episode) did not substantially affect performance. This implies that the current VLM, in its default configuration, may not effectively extract useful information from unprocessed historical interaction logs within a single episode.
- **Memory Summarization:** In-episode summarization (of the last step or current episode) generally had a neutral to negative effect on performance. However, summarizing experiences across *previous* episodes, particularly into learned affordances, showed a positive impact. This indicates that leveraging cross-episodic knowledge, especially from successful past interactions, is more beneficial than relying on immediate in-episode failure signals for VLM corrections.

#### 4.6. Aggregation of Discoveries

- **Hierarchy Benefit:** Even a "Naive Hierarchy" (using basic design choices) generally outperformed a "Flat VLA" (no hierarchical planner), particularly on long-horizon and reasoning tasks, confirming the general utility of a hierarchical structure.
- **Optimized Hierarchy Performance:** An "Optimized Hierarchy," built by combining the best-performing design choices from each component study (cross-episodic memory, thinking VLM, contact-based observation description, success-based termination), yielded substantial performance gains. It achieved significantly higher success rates across all task categories compared to both the Naive Hierarchy and Flat VLA, with the largest improvements observed in long-horizon and reasoning-intensive tasks.
- **Real Robot Validation:** Experiments on a real ALOHA robot corroborated these findings, demonstrating that the optimized hierarchical system translated its performance advantages from simulation to the physical world, outperforming both naive hierarchy and flat VLA control.
- **Durability of Hierarchy:** An additional experiment with a scripted, near-perfect low-level VLA showed that even with a highly capable VLA, the hierarchical design and its orchestration components remained critical. Ablating hierarchical elements significantly degraded performance from approximately 95% down to nearly 0% on challenging long-horizon tasks, indicating that hierarchical design is not obviated by improved low-level policies.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This systematic study provides a foundation for understanding and developing hierarchical Vision-Language-Action (Hi-VLA) systems for robot manipulation. Its significance lies in several areas:

- **Principled Design Guidance:** The research distills practical design principles by systematically evaluating individual components and their interactions within a unified framework. This moves beyond ad-hoc design choices towards a more evidence-based approach for constructing Hi-VLA agents. Researchers and practitioners now have specific insights into which architectural and interface mechanisms are impactful for different task complexities.
- **Improved System Performance:** By demonstrating that an optimized hierarchical system significantly outperforms both naive hierarchical implementations and flat VLA control, particularly for long-horizon and reasoning-intensive tasks, the work contributes to the development of more capable and robust robot manipulation systems. The validation on a real ALOHA robot underscores the practical relevance of these findings.
- **Understanding Component Contributions:** The study clarifies the specific roles and importance of various components:
 - **VLM Reasoning:** Emphasizes that VLM's reasoning capability (e.g., "thinking" passes) is more critical than raw model size for high-level planning.
 - **VLA Steerability:** Highlights the necessity of steerable, high-fidelity low-level VLA policies, showing that compromised instruction following (e.g., from certain fine-tuning) can be detrimental.
 - **Interface Mechanisms:** Identifies that effective termination conditions (like success detection), rich observation representations (e.g., text descriptions with contact or bounding box information), and cross-episodic memory summarization are high-leverage mechanisms for connecting planning with control.
- **Future Research Directions:** The paper also identifies limitations and suggests avenues for future work, including investigating latency-sensitive and dynamic environments, exploring advanced reinforcement learning or supervised fine-tuning techniques for VLMs to better leverage cross-episodic knowledge, and integrating hierarchical architectures to guide low-level continual policy improvement. These suggestions can stimulate further advancements in modular embodied AI systems.
- **Enduring Relevance of Hierarchy:** The experiment with a highly capable scripted VLA indicates that hierarchical design and careful orchestration are likely to remain important even as low-level VLA capabilities improve. This suggests that the principles identified in this study will have lasting relevance in robotics.

Overall, the research provides a foundational empirical analysis that can inform the architectural decisions for current and future hierarchical robot learning systems, potentially accelerating the development of more autonomous and adaptable robotic agents.
