# Mind the Context: Continual Learning of Socially Appropriate Robot Actions via Environmental-Social Disentanglement

> [!summary] Summary
> EDD (Explicit Disentanglement Dual-Branch) lets a social robot continually learn how appropriate its actions are as it moves through new indoor domains. Grounded-SAM panoptic masks split each scene into an environment-only and an agents-only view, fed to two MobileNetV2 encoders whose fused features regress nine appropriateness scores, with replay rehearsal against forgetting. EDD beat CL and VLM baselines on RMSE, PCC, CCC, and forgetting.

## Problem

- Prior continual learning for socially appropriate robot actions studied single-domain or task-incremental settings, never domain-incremental shifts across genuinely different indoor environments.
- Earlier appropriateness models consume full-scene images, implicitly entangling environmental layout with social configuration and inviting spurious correlations between the two cue types.
- Classical domain adaptation seeks domain-invariant features, discarding environmental context that is itself part of the normative signal determining whether an action is appropriate.

## Method

- ==Semantic context decomposition==: ==Grounded-SAM== panoptic masks build an environment view (agent boxes plus ten sampled decoy boxes greyed out) and a social view (agent silhouettes kept, background greyed).
- **EDD** ==dual-branch network==: separate ImageNet-pretrained ==MobileNetV2== environmental and social encoders yield two 1280-dim vectors, concatenated to 2560-dim and passed to a ==LayerNorm/ReLU/Dropout== regression head emitting nine scores.
- ==Domain-incremental experience replay==: a fixed-capacity buffer stores equally-balanced samples from every past domain, mixed with current-domain data and trained under ==MSE loss==, downsampled after each domain.

## Results

- EDD achieved the best scores against FedLGR, DUCA, DARE++ and zero-shot VLMs: RMSE **0.783**, PCC **0.552**, CCC **0.375**, with backward transfer of **0.02** indicating little forgetting.
- Ablations put dual-branch replay close to the joint-training upper bound (RMSE **0.76**, PCC **0.57**, CCC **0.39**); single-branch and replay-free fine-tuning both degraded markedly.
- Bounding-box decomposition led the four strategies (RMSE **0.78**, PCC **0.55**, CCC **0.38**), beating silhouette, robot close-up and no-decomposition; domain ordering changed metrics only modestly.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Structured input-level separation of environmental and social cues can outperform generic domain-incremental learners, so perceptual preprocessing remains a competitive alternative to bigger implicit models.
> - Zero-shot vision-language models reach passable error but correlate poorly with human appropriateness judgments, so low error alone is a misleading metric for social alignment.
> - Coarse bounding-box masking beat precise silhouettes, suggesting that removing an agent's location cue matters more than preserving its exact shape.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses challenges at the intersection of social robotics and continual learning (CL). Social robots are increasingly expected to operate in dynamic, human-centric environments such as homes and offices. In these settings, the appropriateness of a robot's actions is highly context-dependent, influenced by both the physical environment and the social configurations of people and other agents. Traditional robot programming or single-shot training methods are insufficient for these complex and evolving scenarios, as it is impractical to anticipate all possible environmental settings and social norms. This necessitates approaches where robots can continually adapt to new experiences and environments without forgetting previously acquired knowledge, a problem known as catastrophic forgetting in machine learning.

Prior research in continual learning for robotics has explored applications such as learning user routines, contextual understanding, and aspects of social appropriateness. However, existing work on learning socially appropriate robot actions has largely focused on single-domain or task-incremental CL settings. These approaches typically involve learning new tasks within a consistent environment or adding new actions. The present work identifies a gap in domain-incremental CL for social appropriateness, where the robot incrementally encounters entirely new environments (e.g., transitioning from a living room to various office spaces) that inherently alter the normative context.

Furthermore, previous methods for learning social appropriateness often rely on full-scene images, which implicitly combine environmental and social cues. The authors argue that an explicit separation of these cues could enhance domain-incremental CL by reducing spurious correlations and facilitating knowledge transfer across diverse environments. This approach diverges from classical domain adaptation techniques that typically aim for domain-invariant representations, as the environmental context itself can be a critical component of the normative signal in social appropriateness. Therefore, this research positions itself to advance the field by proposing a structured perception mechanism within a continual learning framework for domain-incremental social appropriateness in robotics.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable social robots to continually learn and adapt their understanding of socially appropriate actions across a sequence of diverse indoor environments while mitigating catastrophic forgetting. This overarching goal is driven by several motivations and is broken down into three specific research questions:

1. **Context-Dependency of Social Norms:** Social appropriateness is not universal; identical robot actions can be acceptable in one crowded space (e.g., a home gathering) but disruptive in another (e.g., an office meeting). Handcrafting rules for all possible contexts is infeasible, and training on an exhaustive dataset of all environments and norm variations is unrealistic. Robots require mechanisms to learn and adapt to these context-dependent norms dynamically.
2. **Mitigating Catastrophic Forgetting:** When robots continually learn in new environments, they risk forgetting knowledge acquired from previous experiences. Continual Learning (CL) techniques are necessary to allow robots to adapt to new domains (data distribution shifts) while preserving previously learned social appropriateness knowledge.
3. **Limitations of Implicit Cue Integration:** Prior approaches for learning social appropriateness often process full-scene images, which implicitly mix environmental cues (e.g., room layout, furniture) and social cues (e.g., people's positions, group formations). The researchers hypothesize that explicitly separating these complementary information sources can lead to more robust and transferable learning. Such disentanglement could reduce the likelihood of the model learning spurious correlations between combined cues and improve its ability to generalize across different environmental and social contexts.

To investigate these motivations, the study poses three interconnected research questions (RQs):

- **RQ1: Does explicit environmental–social disentanglement improve domain-incremental CL for socially appropriate robot actions?** This question probes the core hypothesis regarding the benefit of separating environmental and social information for continual learning in dynamic social settings.
- **RQ2: How should these cues be represented and separated in visual observations?** This aims to evaluate different strategies for decomposing visual scenes into distinct environmental and social contexts, comparing semantic and spatial disentanglement methods.
- **RQ3: How does the order of encountered domains affect a robot’s CL of socially appropriate actions?** This question investigates the impact of the sequence in which new environments are presented to the robot, considering factors like complexity and contrast between domains, on the robot's ability to continually learn and retain social appropriateness knowledge.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research proposes the Explicit Disentanglement Dual-Branch (EDD) framework to address domain-incremental continual learning of socially appropriate robot actions.

**A. Problem Formulation:**
The problem is formulated as a multi-output regression task. For a given scene image, the robot predicts a K-dimensional vector of appropriateness scores, where K represents the number of possible robot actions (e.g., cleaning, serving, conversing). Human annotations serve as ground-truth scores. The learning occurs in a domain-incremental continual learning (DIL) setting, where the robot encounters a sequence of distinct indoor locations (domains D1..., DT). At each time step `t`, the robot trains only on data from the current domain `Dt`, with the objective of minimizing the cumulative loss across all encountered domains (D1 to Dt) without direct access to past domain data.

**B. Domain-Incremental Learning with Rehearsal:**
To approximate the ideal objective of learning across all domains simultaneously while adhering to the DIL constraint, EDD employs an Experience Replay strategy. A fixed-capacity memory buffer (`B_t`) stores a subset of training examples from previously encountered domains. During training for domain `Dt`, the model utilizes a mixed training set comprising data from the current domain `Dt` and sampled replay examples from `B_{t-1}`. The replay samples are evenly distributed among all past domains stored in the buffer. The model is trained by minimizing a Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss on this mixed dataset. After training on `Dt`, a subset of its training examples is added to the buffer, and the buffer is downsampled to maintain capacity and equal representation of all stored domains, thereby mitigating catastrophic forgetting.

**C. Semantic Context Decomposition:**
The core of EDD's approach is the explicit decomposition of visual input into environmental and social contexts, motivated by the hypothesis that social appropriateness depends on both (`p(y | x) ≈ p(y | x_E, x_S)`). This is achieved through deterministic preprocessing transformations `T_E` and `T_S`:

- **Panoptic Segmentation:** A pre-trained panoptic segmentation model (Grounded-SAM) is used to analyze each input scene `x`. This model detects social agents (e.g., humans) and provides binary silhouette masks (`M_A_s`) and bounding box masks (`M_B_s`) for each detected agent `s`.
- **Environmental View (`x_E`):** To create `x_E`, regions occupied by current social agents are masked out using their bounding box masks. Additionally, `m=10` random bounding box masks are sampled from a domain-specific pool (`P_t`), which contains all detected agent bounding boxes from the current domain. These sampled "decoy" masks are combined with the current scene's agent masks. The masked pixels are replaced with a fixed RGB value (`μ`). This process aims to obscure agent appearance and precise location, focusing the view on the static environmental layout and persistent domain characteristics.
- **Social View (`x_S`):** To create `x_S`, only the social agents are visible. This is achieved by combining individual agent silhouette masks (`M_A_s`) into a single `M_A(x)`. The scene is then multiplied by `M_A(x)`, and the background (unmasked pixels) is replaced with the fixed RGB value (`μ`). This view emphasizes the configuration and presence of social entities.

**D. Dual-Branch Network:**
The decomposed environmental (`x_E`) and social (`x_S`) views are processed by a dual-branch perception architecture:

- **Encoders:** Two separate ImageNet-pretrained MobileNetV2 backbones serve as the Environmental Encoder (`ϕ_E`) and Social Encoder (`ϕ_S`). Each encoder maps its respective input image to a 1280-dimensional feature vector (`h_E`, `h_S`).
- **Fusion:** The learned representations from both branches are concatenated (`[h_S || h_E]`), forming a 2560-dimensional fused representation.
- **Regression Head:** This fused representation is then passed to a fully connected regression head (`g`). The head consists of three linear transformations with LayerNorm, ReLU, and Dropout layers, ultimately outputting 9 appropriateness scores for the robot actions.

The dual-branch design allows each branch to specialize in processing distinct cues, with the final fusion integrating this specialized knowledge to predict action appropriateness.

**E. Experimental Details:**
- **Dataset:** The merged OfficeDB and MannersDB+ datasets were used, comprising synthetic images of a Pepper robot in six indoor environments (Home, BigOffice-2, BigOffice-3, Hallway, MeetingRoom, SmallOffice). The dataset includes human annotations for the social appropriateness of nine robot actions on a 1-5 scale.
- **Baselines:** EDD was compared against state-of-the-art baselines including FedLGR (robotics CL), DUCA and DARE++ (domain-incremental computer vision methods), and large Vision-Language Models (VLMs) like Qwen, LLaVA, and DeepSeek in a zero-shot setting. Ablations for single-branch architectures and different training regimes (fine-tuning, joint training) were also performed.
- **Disentanglement Strategies (RQ2):** Four strategies were evaluated: Bounding-box decomposition (ours), Silhouette decomposition, Robot close-up (spatial decomposition), and a No decomposition control (single full image).
- **Domain Orderings (RQ3):** Five domain sequences were tested: Default, Complexity Increasing, Complexity Decreasing, Contrast A, and Contrast B, designed to explore the impact of curriculum-like progression and abrupt domain shifts.
- **Metrics:** Performance was evaluated using Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE, lower is better), Pearson’s Correlation Coefficient (PCC, higher is better), Concordance Correlation Coefficient (CCC, higher is better), and Backwards Transfer (BWT, lower indicates less forgetting).
- **Implementation:** PyTorch, single NVIDIA RTX 2060 GPU, Adam optimizer, linear learning rate scheduler, 5-fold cross-validation. Panoptic segmentation was identified as the main latency bottleneck, not the EDD architecture itself.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

**A. RQ1: Environmental and Social Context Disentanglement**
- **Superior Performance of EDD:** The Explicit Disentanglement Dual-Branch (EDD) framework demonstrated the lowest RMSE (0.783), highest PCC (0.552), and highest CCC (0.375) among all compared baselines (Table V). These results indicate EDD's predictions are more accurate and show a stronger correlation with human-provided appropriateness scores.
- **Improved Continual Learning:** Compared to other continual learning baselines, EDD exhibited lower Backwards Transfer (BWT) of 0.02 (Table VI), suggesting better knowledge retention and reduced catastrophic forgetting across sequential domains.
- **Ablation Study Insights:** The dual-branch architecture with replay-based continual learning (EDD) achieved performance closer to the theoretical upper bound of joint training (0.76 RMSE, 0.57 PCC, 0.39 CCC) than a single-branch architecture trained under the same CL conditions. Fine-tuning without replay significantly degraded performance for both dual-branch and single-branch models.
- **VLM Performance:** Vision-Language Models (VLMs) showed moderately competitive RMSE values but exhibited substantially weaker correlations (PCC and CCC) with human judgments compared to EDD. This suggests that while VLMs can perform some level of zero-shot prediction, they do not align as closely with human perception of social appropriateness in these specific embodied contexts.

**B. RQ2: Impact of Disentanglement Strategies**
- **Bounding Box Superiority:** Among the tested decomposition strategies, the bounding box-based method (used by EDD) generally yielded the most favorable results (Table VII). It achieved the lowest overall RMSE (0.78) and the highest PCC (0.55) and CCC (0.38) across all domains. This indicates that a cleaner separation of social agents from the environmental context, as achieved by bounding box masking, leads to a better match with human-provided judgments.
- **Comparison with other strategies:** The "Robot close-up" and "No decomposition" strategies showed similar RMSE values, both generally lower than the "Silhouette" method. However, "No decomposition" consistently produced higher correlations (PCC/CCC) than "Robot close-up" and "Silhouette."
- **Forgetting Across Strategies:** Bbox and Robot close-up strategies achieved the lowest BWT, indicating better retention of previously learned knowledge compared to Silhouette and No decomposition. This suggests that explicit disentanglement, even spatial (close-up), can aid in mitigating forgetting.

**C. RQ3: Sensitivity to the Order of Domain Sequences**
- **Modest Effect of Domain Order:** The study found that the order in which domains are encountered had a modest impact on overall performance metrics (RMSE, PCC, CCC) and minimal effect on forgetting (BWT) (Table VIII). All tested schedules yielded performance within a narrow range.
- **Complexity-Based Schedules:** The "increasing complexity" schedule (C↑), where Home was presented last, resulted in the strongest performance specifically for the Home domain (lowest RMSE, highest PCC/CCC for Home). This suggests a possible curriculum-style effect, where exposure to more complex environments before simpler ones might benefit learning.
- **Decreasing Complexity Performance:** The "decreasing complexity" schedule (C↓) generally performed worst for the pooled office environments in terms of correlations (PCC and CCC).
- **Forgetting Robustness:** BWT values remained low and exhibited minimal variability across all domain orders, indicating that EDD, with its replay-based mechanism, is relatively robust to catastrophic forgetting regardless of the sequence of encountered environments in this experimental setup.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the fields of social robotics and continual learning by demonstrating a method for robots to continually adapt their understanding of socially appropriate actions in dynamic, multi-domain environments.

**Significance of Findings:**
- **Validation of Explicit Disentanglement:** The primary significance lies in the empirical validation that explicitly separating environmental and social cues at the input level, followed by dual-branch processing, is beneficial for domain-incremental continual learning of social appropriateness. This approach outperforms established continual learning baselines and general-purpose Vision-Language Models, suggesting that a structured perceptual pipeline can be more effective for this specific task than generic models or implicit feature learning.
- **Improved Alignment with Human Judgments:** The EDD framework consistently produces appropriateness scores that align more closely with human judgments (higher PCC and CCC) while maintaining low prediction error (RMSE). This indicates a more nuanced and human-centric understanding of social norms learned by the robot.
- **Effective Catastrophic Forgetting Mitigation:** The consistent low BWT across EDD and its variants confirms the efficacy of replay-based rehearsal combined with the disentanglement strategy in mitigating catastrophic forgetting, a critical challenge for long-term robot deployments.
- **Insights into Decomposition Strategies:** The comparative analysis of disentanglement strategies highlights that "cleaner" semantic separation (e.g., bounding box masks) can lead to more accurate and correlated predictions, emphasizing the importance of how visual context is structured for learning social intelligence.
- **Relative Robustness to Domain Order:** While some subtle effects were observed, the overall robustness of EDD to different domain ordering sequences is significant. This suggests that the framework is not overly sensitive to the curriculum design, which can simplify deployment considerations in real-world scenarios where domain encounter order may be unpredictable.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Enhanced Robot Autonomy and Adaptability:** The EDD framework could enable social robots to operate more effectively and appropriately in diverse real-world settings (e.g., homes, hospitals, offices, public spaces) over extended periods. This adaptability is crucial for building trust and acceptance in human-robot interaction.
- **Foundation for Future Social AI:** By demonstrating the utility of structured perception for learning social norms, this work lays a foundation for developing more sophisticated and context-aware social AI systems.
- **Guidance for Robotics System Design:** The findings provide practical guidance for robot perception system designers, suggesting that explicit environmental-social disentanglement should be considered for tasks requiring context-dependent social intelligence.
- **New Research Directions:** The work opens several avenues for future research, including:
 - **Multi-robot Embodiments:** Extending EDD to accommodate different robot types and their unique affordances.
 - **Real-World Generalizability:** Testing EDD's robustness in actual physical environments, addressing synthetic-to-real domain shifts and imperfections in panoptic segmentation.
 - **Explainability:** Leveraging the dual-branch structure to analyze what each branch learns and how environmental versus social cues contribute to specific action appropriateness predictions, enhancing transparency and interpretability of robot behavior.
 - **Adaptive Fusion:** Developing mechanisms for the model to dynamically adjust the weighting or interaction between environmental and social cues based on the specific robot action or interaction context.
