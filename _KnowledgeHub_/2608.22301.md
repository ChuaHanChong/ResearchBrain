# The Imitator Game: Benchmarking Robot Imitative Ability Beyond Action Prediction

> [!summary] Summary
> The Imitator Game is a four-level benchmark (L0-L3) that progressively widens the mismatch between a human demonstration video and the robot's own scene, paired with IG-10K, a 20,000+ episode environment-aligned human-robot dataset, and Imitator Arena for blind A/B human evaluation. Across nine state-of-the-art models performance stays flat from L0 to L2 then collapses at L3, isolating functional substitution as the decisive barrier.

## Problem

- Learning from human video stays trajectory-level: hand-keypoint or optical-flow retargeting replays motion only when the demonstration and execution scenes remain closely matched.
- Video-conditioned and cross-embodiment skill policies are evaluated in-distribution, with only limited and ungraded mismatch between the demonstrator scene and the robot scene.
- Existing generalization benchmarks perturb training-time scenes or score language-conditioned tasks, never testing whether a policy infers demonstrated intent absent any explicit task specification.

## Method

- The **Imitator Game** defines a four-level ==L0-L3 hierarchy== over a scene decomposition into appearance, geometry, semantic category and spatial configuration, each level transition induced by one interpretable mismatch.
- **IG-10K**, an environment-aligned paired dataset of 11.7K real teleoperated plus 10K simulated paired episodes, 50+ tasks over six domains, annotated with ==MANO hand pose==, masks, depth, language.
- **Imitator Arena** with predicate-based ==SR/Sub-SR scoring== plus blind A/B human rubric judging, and adapters giving VLAs a fixed dense captioner while freezing all backbones so only action heads train.

## Results

- ACT/DINOv2 leads seen-task simulation at SR **0.81** and Sub-SR **0.93**, while XSkill leads real-world human-judged imitation with SR **0.63** and a **0.89** win rate.
- Zero-shot transfer to unseen tasks sits near floor with best SR **0.13**; pretrain-then-finetune on **10** demonstrations beats from-scratch on **12 of 15** variants and improves with pretraining scale.
- Real-world fine-tuned success is stable near **0.42** across L0-L2 but drops to **0.29** at L3, with XSkill falling from **0.57** to **0.29** and imitation score **7.20** to **5.63**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Functional substitution, not layout shuffling or instance variation, is where imitation breaks; benchmarks perturbing only scenes miss the rung that separates methods.
> - Keeping the demonstration as raw video beats transcribing it into language: video-conditioned policies consistently outperform caption-conditioned ones under mismatch and transfer.
> - Paired human-robot pretraining buys few-shot adaptability, not zero-shot ability; scaling the corpus or the action head alone will not yield intent-level imitation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

Current research in robot manipulation frequently focuses on learning observation-to-action mappings from visual inputs or language instructions. Many approaches that learn from human video demonstrations rely on trajectory remapping, where visual cues such as hand keypoints or optical flow are extracted and retargeted to robot actions in scenes that are largely similar to the demonstration environment. While these methods are effective for bootstrapping robot behaviors in closely matched settings, they often exhibit limitations when faced with variations in object placement, geometry, or available tools. These methods typically assume that the robot execution scene is congruent with the demonstration and are often evaluated in-distribution, meaning there is limited mismatch between the demonstration and execution environments.

This paper addresses a gap in the existing research by investigating robot imitative ability beyond mere trajectory reproduction. It specifically evaluates whether a robot can infer the *intent* of a human demonstration and reproduce that intent in a novel scene, even when significant differences exist in objects, their properties, or functional affordances. This contrasts with previous generalization benchmarks that primarily assess robustness to perturbations of training scenes, or benchmarks for long-horizon and language-conditioned tasks. The work is positioned as a step toward enabling robots to leverage the vast amount of human video data available online as a practical source of supervision, by moving beyond literal action replay to understanding the underlying purpose of an action. It builds upon concepts from cultural learning and goal-directed imitation, which emphasize reproducing the purpose rather than the exact motion of an action.

### 2. Key objectives and motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a comprehensive benchmark for evaluating a robot's ability to imitate human actions at the level of intent, rather than merely replaying observed trajectories. This objective is motivated by the observation that human imitation involves inferring a goal and adapting its execution based on available tools and environmental layouts, a capability largely absent in current robot learning policies.

To address this, the authors establish several specific objectives:

- **Define a hierarchy of imitative capabilities:** The core motivation is to quantify intent-level imitation by progressively increasing the mismatch between a human demonstration scene ($S_{demo}$) and a robot's execution scene ($S_{imit}$). This is formalized into a four-level hierarchy (L0–L3) where each level isolates a distinct challenge to imitative behavior.
 - **L0 (Scene-identical execution):** The robot scene matches the demonstration, requiring direct reproduction of the demonstrated trajectory.
 - **L1 (Spatial adaptation):** Object identities are preserved, but their spatial configuration differs, necessitating a different trajectory to achieve the same object-level outcome.
 - **L2 (Visual-physical generalization):** Task-relevant objects differ in appearance or geometry while maintaining their semantic role, requiring generalization across visually or physically distinct instances of the same object category.
 - **L3 (Intent-level transfer / Functional substitution):** The demonstrated object is replaced by one with different semantics or function, requiring the robot to infer the underlying intent and realize it through a different affordance structure (e.g., using a stone instead of a hammer to drive a nail). This level is designed to assess functional substitution, where the robot must achieve the same goal through functionally different means.

- **Construct a large-scale, environment-aligned human-robot paired dataset (IG-10K):** Existing datasets are noted as insufficient for evaluating intent-level imitation due to limitations in scene mismatch or human-robot pairing. The motivation is to provide a robust data foundation for training and evaluating policies across the defined L0–L3 hierarchy, spanning both real and simulated environments.

- **Develop an open evaluation platform (Imitator Arena):** The motivation is to provide a standardized and unbiased evaluation mechanism, combining both automated metrics for simulation and structured human preference judgments for real-world and simulated rollouts. This platform aims to facilitate fair comparison of different robot learning approaches by enabling blind A/B testing.

- **Systematically benchmark state-of-the-art models:** The authors aim to evaluate the performance of various prominent robot manipulation policy architectures (Vision-Language-Action models, skill-based methods, and video-conditioned visuomotor policies) against the introduced benchmark. This objective seeks to identify current limitations and challenges these models face in achieving intent-level imitation and to understand the impact of data scale and pre-training on their transfer capabilities. The intent is to determine where current approaches succeed and where they collapse within the L0-L3 hierarchy, thereby highlighting areas for future research.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers around "The Imitator Game," comprising a four-level imitation hierarchy, a large-scale dataset, an evaluation platform, and a systematic benchmarking of state-of-the-art models.

### 4. The Imitator Capability (Four Levels of Imitation)

The core of the methodology is the definition of four progressively challenging levels of imitation, based on the degree of mismatch between the human demonstration scene ($S_{demo}$) and the robot's imitation scene ($S_{imit}$). A scene is represented by a collection of objects (each with appearance $A$, geometry $G$, and semantic category $S$) and their spatial configuration $P$. The hierarchy is structured as follows:

- **L0: Trajectory imitation.** $P$, $A$, $G$, and $S$ are all preserved. The robot is expected to reproduce the demonstrated trajectory.
- **L1: Object end-state imitation.** $P$ is changed (objects are rearranged), but $A$, $G$, and $S$ are preserved. The robot must achieve the same final object states via a different trajectory.
- **L2: Semantic-task imitation.** $A$ or $G$ (or both) of task-relevant objects are changed, but $S$ is preserved. $P$ is not applicable as the object set has changed. The robot must complete the same semantic task, even if final object states differ. This includes sub-cases where only appearance changes or where both appearance and geometry change.
- **L3: Affordance-adapted imitation.** $S$ of at least one task-relevant object is changed (implying changes in $G$ and $A$). $P$ is not applicable. The robot must infer the underlying intent of the demonstration and fulfill it using objects with different affordances.

### 5. The Imitator Benchmark

- **IG-10K Dataset:**
 - **Paired-episode design:** The dataset's atomic unit is a paired episode, consisting of a human demonstration video ($V$) in $S_{demo}$ and one or more robot episodes in $S_{imit}$ that instantiate one of the L0-L3 relationships.
 - **Scale and Diversity:** IG-10K contains over 20,000 paired episodes (11.7K real human-robot, 10K simulated), covering more than 50 base tasks across 6 domains (Household, Supermarket, Restaurant, Logistics, Hospital, Laboratory). This yields over 200 task variants, with each task typically having an instantiation for all four levels.
 - **Annotation:** Each episode is richly annotated with multi-view RGB-D images, 3D MANO hand poses, semantic segmentation masks (from Grounded-SAM-2), and multi-abstraction language descriptions (per-arm sub-task sequence, semantic task, underlying intent).
 - **Data Collection:** Real-world data were collected using VR teleoperation on a dual-arm Realman platform. Simulated data were generated using a dual-arm Franka in ManiSkill3 with OMPL-planned trajectories.

- **Imitator Arena:**
 - **Unified Interface:** Policies receive the demonstration video ($V$) and robot observations ($o_t$). Language-conditioned policies receive a fixed, deterministic caption ($T(V)$) generated by a Qwen3-VL-augmented human annotation process, ensuring fair comparison. The imitation level is not revealed to the policy.
 - **Human Evaluation:** Human evaluators perform blind A/B comparisons of two anonymized model rollouts against the demonstrator video. They judge per-rollout task completion (SUCCESS/PARTIAL/FAIL), assign an imitation quality score (0-10 rubric), and express pairwise overall preference. Evaluation is structured to ensure comparability across models and tasks.
 - **Automated Metrics (Simulation):** In simulation, where scene state is fully observable, automated metrics include final success rate (SR) and sub-goal success rate (Sub-SR). These are level-agnostic indicators of task completion.

- **Baseline Adapters:**
 - Nine state-of-the-art models, grouped into three families, were adapted and evaluated:
 - **VLA Models:** GR00T-N1.6, RDT-1B, π0.5, OpenVLA (language-conditioned; use $T(V)$ as prompt).
 - **Skill-based Models:** XSkill, UniSkill (learn shared human-robot skill representations).
 - **Video-conditioned Visuomotor Models:** ACT, Diffusion Policy, VQ-BeT (conditioned by a task embedding from frozen DINOv2, SigLIP2, or VideoMAE encoders).
 - **Training Protocol:** For all models, the video encoder or vision-language backbone was frozen, and only the action-generation module was trained or fine-tuned on IG-10K. This approach focuses on evaluating the action-generation interface and ensures a comparable adaptation budget.

### 6. Experimental Setup

Experiments evaluated performance across ten manipulation tasks (five seen, five unseen) over the L0–L3 hierarchy. Pre-training was conducted on nested corpora of 15, 30, or 45 tasks. Evaluation settings included zero-shot transfer (ZS), from-scratch few-shot learning (Scr.), and pre-train and fine-tune (P+FT). Simulation evaluations used SR and Sub-SR, while real-world evaluations (on four representative models) used human judgments from Imitator Arena.

### 7. Main findings and results

The study yielded several key findings regarding robot imitative ability across different model architectures, data scales, and imitation levels.

### 8. Imitation Interface Strength (Q1)

- **Video-based policies generally outperform language-conditioned ones for seen-task imitation.** In simulation, ACT/DINOv2 achieved the highest automated success rate (SR = 0.81, Sub-SR = 0.93) on seen tasks, followed closely by XSkill (SR = 0.79, Sub-SR = 0.91). The strongest language-conditioned VLA, π0.5, was lower at SR = 0.73.
- **Skill-based methods (XSkill) demonstrated robust performance.** In real-world evaluations, XSkill showed the strongest human-judged imitation across all settings (Seen SR = 0.63, ZS SR = 0.29, Scr. SR = 0.28, P+FT SR = 0.49), consistently outperforming language-based models in zero-shot transfer.
- **Video representation choice is significant.** Within video-conditioned visuomotor policies (Video-VA), DINOv2 and SigLIP2 encoders consistently yielded stronger results than VideoMAE, indicating the importance of the visual feature extractor.

### 9. Impact of Scale on Zero-shot Transfer and Few-shot Adaptation (Q2)

- **Paired pretraining primarily supports few-shot adaptation rather than direct zero-shot transfer.** Zero-shot performance on unseen tasks remained low across all paradigms in simulation (best SR = 0.13). This suggests current frameworks struggle to generalize to unseen tasks without task-specific robot data.
- **Pre-train and fine-tune (P+FT) significantly improved few-shot learning.** For most models, P+FT led to substantial gains over training from scratch on 10 demonstrations, and these benefits increased with larger pre-training scales (from 15 to 45 tasks). Specifically, 14 out of 15 simulation variants and all 4 real-world representative models showed improved P+FT success rates with increasing pre-training data.
- **Zero-shot transfer shows different scaling behavior between video- and language-conditioned models.** On real hardware, zero-shot success for video-conditioned models (XSkill, ACT/DINOv2, DP/DINOv2) monotonically improved with increased corpus size. In contrast, the language-conditioned π0.5 ’s zero-shot performance remained at a low baseline regardless of pre-training scale, suggesting that providing more paired human-robot video improves zero-shot capability for policies that directly process video, but not necessarily for those relying on an intermediate language caption.

### 10. Hierarchy Difficulty (Q3)

- **L3 (functional substitution) represents a decisive barrier to intent-level imitation.** In real-world P+FT settings, where policies had task-specific adaptation and were judged by humans, the average success rate (SR) was stable from L0 to L2 (around 0.4) but dropped to 0.29 at L3. The human imitation quality score (Q) showed a similar pattern, falling from approximately 6.2 on L0-L2 to 5.62 on L3.
- This indicates that current policies can cope with rearranged layouts (L1) and object replacements within the same semantic category (L2), but struggle when the task requires functionally different objects to achieve the same underlying intent. Simulation results showed a non-monotonic trend with an L2 dip, attributed to specific simulation construction choices (arm swapping, rule-based motions) that do not reflect real-world physical adaptation challenges.

### 11. Significance and potential impact

The "Imitator Game" benchmark offers several significant contributions and potential impacts on the field of robot learning:

- **Quantifying Intent-Level Imitation:** By introducing a four-level hierarchy (L0-L3) that systematically increases the mismatch between demonstration and execution scenes, the research provides a framework to precisely measure and distinguish between trajectory-level imitation and true intent-level understanding. This shifts the focus from merely reproducing observed motions to inferring goals and adapting to novel situations, a hallmark of advanced cognitive abilities.
- **Comprehensive Data Resource (IG-10K):** The release of IG-10K, the largest environment-aligned human-robot paired dataset to date, covering diverse tasks and instantiated across all four imitation levels in both real and simulated settings, provides an invaluable resource for the research community. This dataset is crucial for training and evaluating future robot learning models that aim for greater generalization and intent understanding from human demonstrations. The rich multi-modal annotations further enhance its utility.
- **Standardized Evaluation Platform (Imitator Arena):** The Imitator Arena, with its support for automated simulation metrics and structured blind human evaluation, offers a robust and fair platform for benchmarking. This standardization facilitates direct comparison of different robotic policies and accelerates progress by providing clear and consistent performance indicators, fostering reproducibility and transparency in research.
- **Identification of Key Challenges:** The systematic evaluation of nine state-of-the-art models revealed that functional substitution (L3) is a major unresolved challenge for current systems. Performance drops significantly at L3, indicating that existing approaches struggle with inferring underlying intent when it requires adapting to objects with different affordances. This finding highlights the need for fundamental advancements beyond current scaling paradigms or simple action-head improvements.
- **Guidance for Future Research:** The results suggest that intent-level imitation may not emerge as a byproduct of simply scaling data or improving action generation alone. It points towards the necessity of developing new interfaces that maintain the "purpose" of a demonstration, such as affordance-aware object grounding, explicit goal inference mechanisms, or intermediate representations that are robust to object substitution. This redirection of research effort towards more cognitive approaches could lead to robots that are more adaptable and capable of genuine understanding.
- **Catalyst for Generalization:** By providing a clear benchmark and dataset, this work is expected to catalyze research into more generalizable robot manipulation policies. It encourages the development of models that can leverage human videos to acquire new skills and adapt them to varied environments, ultimately reducing the reliance on extensive robot-specific data collection for every new task.

The limitations, such as the finite and manually designed nature of IG-10K, the single substitution pattern per task-level pair, and the potential biases in simulation, also outline clear directions for subsequent benchmark development and research.
