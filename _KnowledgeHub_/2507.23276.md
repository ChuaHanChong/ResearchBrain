# How Far Are AI Scientists from Changing the World?

> [!summary] Summary
> This survey critically assesses the capabilities of large language model-powered AI Scientist systems, introducing a unique framework to map current achievements and identify gaps in scientific research automation. An empirical evaluation of 28 AI-generated papers revealed severe limitations, including "Experimental Weakness" in 100% of the assessed works, underscoring the distance from autonomous scientific discovery.

## Problem

- Traditional human-centric scientific research is constrained by limited time and cognitive capacity, leading to inefficiencies in discovery and hypothesis generation.
- The rapidly evolving field of LLM-powered AI Scientist systems lacked a structured, prospect-driven review and a clear roadmap for their development.
- There was an absence of rigorous identification of key bottlenecks and missing components required for AI Scientists to achieve truly autonomous research capabilities.

## Method

- A novel =="capability-level framework"== (knowledge acquisition, idea generation, verification and falsification, and evolution) was introduced to systematically analyze AI Scientist system development.
- Existing literature on AI Scientist systems was thoroughly reviewed and synthesized within this framework, categorizing methods and evaluation benchmarks.
- An empirical assessment utilized an advanced AI reviewer model, ==DeepReviewer-14B==, to quantitatively evaluate ==28 AI-generated research papers== for quality and prevalent defects.

## Results

- An evaluation of 28 AI-generated research papers found low quality scores (highest average **4.63/10**), with "Experimental Weakness" present in **100%** of the assessed papers and "Methodological Unclarity/Flaws" in **96.4%**.
- Underlying LLMs showed low accuracy on practical implementation benchmarks, such as OpenAI's "o1-preview" achieving only **16.90%** success on MLE-Bench tasks.
- Identified research capability gaps include insufficient precision in information retrieval, difficulty generating truly novel hypotheses, weak multi-agent collaboration, and underdeveloped dynamic planning mechanisms.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Despite advancements in LLM technology, AI Scientist systems face fundamental limitations such as hallucination, high costs for knowledge updating, and catastrophic forgetting, hindering scientific accuracy.
> - Current AI Scientists excel in foundational knowledge acquisition and idea generation but demonstrate severe deficiencies in rigorous verification, experimental design, and autonomous evolution.
> - The empirical evaluation confirmed that AI-generated research papers frequently fail to meet established scientific standards, particularly regarding experimental soundness and methodological clarity.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper is a crucial **survey** positioned at the forefront of the rapidly evolving field of AI Scientist systems. The authors explicitly state that "The emergence of large language models (LLMs) is propelling automated scientific discovery to the next level," with LLM-based AI Scientist systems now "taking the lead in scientific research." This highlights the contemporary and impactful context of the work. The acknowledgment of "influential works" already in existence and the acceptance of "AI-generated research papers at the ICLR 2025 workshop" establish the urgency and relevance of a comprehensive assessment.

The survey differentiates itself from existing literature by providing a "prospect-driven review" that specifically focuses on the central question: "How far are AI scientists from changing the world and reshaping the scientific research paradigm?" As highlighted in their own comparison (Table 6), this survey uniquely offers a **"Capability Roadmap"** for the development of mature AI Scientist systems and provides a **"Critical Gap Assessment"**—aspects largely absent in other contemporary surveys on LLM-based scientific agents, tools, or research support systems.

Historically, efforts in automating scientific discovery have ranged from leveraging deep learning for knowledge acquisition (pre-LLM era) to developing domain-specific models. The advent of LLMs, with their remarkable comprehension and generation abilities, has fundamentally shifted this landscape, enabling systems to generate novel hypotheses and evaluate them using automated tools. This survey synthesizes these advancements, clearly delineating the progression from "scientific tools" to true "AI Scientist systems" capable of leading discovery. By introducing a structured "capability-level framework" (knowledge acquisition, idea generation, verification and falsification, and evolution), the authors aim to provide a clearer understanding of current limitations and define the ultimate goals for scientific AI, thus serving as a guiding document for future research and development in this burgeoning field.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this survey is to answer the pivotal question: "How far are AI scientists from changing the world and reshaping the scientific research paradigm?" To achieve this, the authors are motivated by several factors and aim to fulfill distinct goals:

- **Addressing Human Limitations in Scientific Research:** The introduction highlights that traditional human-centric research is "inherently constrained by human limitations such as limited time and cognitive capacity," leading to inefficiencies in literature review, hypothesis generation, and experiment execution. The promise of AI Scientist systems is to overcome these constraints, making "ground-breaking discoveries that solve grand challenges" possible.
- **Providing a Prospect-Driven Review:** The paper aims to offer a comprehensive analysis of the current achievements of AI Scientist systems, moving beyond a mere cataloging of existing work to provide a forward-looking perspective.
- **Identifying Key Bottlenecks and Missing Components:** A core motivation is to rigorously evaluate the current state of AI Scientist capabilities, pinpointing specific areas where present systems fall short. This involves understanding "what is missing" for AI Scientists to evolve into truly autonomous agents.
- **Establishing a Capability-Level Framework:** The authors introduce and define a hierarchical framework comprising four progressive levels (knowledge acquisition, idea generation, verification and falsification, and evolution). This framework serves as a structured roadmap to systematically analyze the development stages of an AI Scientist, from foundational knowledge to continuous improvement.
- **Guiding Future Research:** Ultimately, the survey hopes to "contribute to a clearer understanding of limitations of current AI Scientist systems, showing where we are, what is missing, and what the ultimate goals for scientific AI should be." By clarifying the state-of-the-art and identifying critical gaps, the authors intend to set a reasonable roadmap for current and future research directions towards achieving mature scientific AI.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

As a comprehensive survey paper, the methodology primarily involves a systematic review and synthesis of existing literature on AI Scientist systems, structured around a novel conceptual framework developed by the authors.

The core of their approach is the **"capability-level framework"** for AI Scientist development, illustrated in Figure 1 and elaborated throughout the paper. This framework systematically breaks down the complex abilities of a mature AI Scientist into four progressive levels:

1. **Knowledge Acquisition:** The foundational ability to retrieve, review, and comprehend scientific knowledge from existing literature to build a knowledge base.
2. **Idea Generation:** The capacity to autonomously formulate novel and feasible scientific hypotheses based on acquired knowledge.
3. **Verification and Falsification:** The ability to systematically design, implement, and analyze experiments to test (verify) or disprove (falsify) AI-generated hypotheses.
4. **Evolution:** The capacity for continuous advancement of overall research abilities through internal reflection and external feedback, encompassing dynamic planning and autonomous learning.

For each capability level, the authors meticulously review "existing achievements," categorize various methods (e.g., pre-LLM era vs. LLM era; direct prompting, RAG-based, multi-agent methods), and discuss relevant "evaluation benchmarks" to assess progress and limitations.

Beyond literature review, a critical component of their methodology is the **empirical assessment of current AI Scientist systems** (Section 5.3). This is a unique and significant contribution for a survey paper. The authors utilize an advanced AI reviewer model, DeepReviewer-14B (from their own prior work), to rigorously evaluate 28 publicly available research papers generated by 5 leading AI Scientist systems. This direct, quantitative assessment provides concrete evidence to support their claims about the limitations of current systems. They analyze these papers based on criteria like soundness, presentation, and contribution, and identify prevalent defect categories.

Finally, the survey includes a dedicated **discussion section** (Section 7) that synthesizes the identified limitations, distinguishing between "Fundamental Limitations of Base Models" (e.g., hallucination, knowledge updating challenges) and "Limitations of Research Capabilities of AI Scientist Systems" (mapped back to the four-level framework). It also addresses crucial **ethical challenges** associated with the rise of AI Scientists and proposes "future directions" (Section 8) to bridge current gaps, including developing communication protocols and outlining pathways for personalized and societal AI Scientist systems. This holistic approach ensures a balanced, critical, and forward-looking analysis.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The survey presents a critical assessment of AI Scientist systems, revealing significant progress alongside persistent and fundamental limitations across its proposed capability framework.

**A. Capability Landscape of AI Scientist Systems (Figure 2):**
- **Knowledge Acquisition:** LLMs have enabled automated retrieval, review, and summarization of scientific literature, with RAG and multi-agent approaches enhancing context-aware discovery. However, systems still struggle with accurate integration and synthesis from vast, diverse literature.
- **Idea Generation:** LLM-based AI Scientists can autonomously generate hypotheses across various fields, with some even rated as more novel than human-expert ideas. Yet, objective evaluation of impact and feasibility assessment remain challenging, often lacking genuine novelty and being repetitive.
- **Verification and Falsification:** Progress has been made in automated experimental design, code generation, and result analysis, especially in computer science. LLM-based agents can propose, implement, and partially validate experiments. Nevertheless, they struggle with designing and executing rigorous experiments, particularly in collaborative workflows, and translating conceptual plans into verifiably correct, operational code.
- **Evolution:** Elementary mechanisms for iterative improvement like self-reflection, reinforcement learning, and externally guided revision (human-in-the-loop, multi-agent communities) have emerged. However, AI Scientists lack dynamic adaptation and autonomous learning from feedback for independent innovation and long-term research planning.

**B. Empirical Evidence of Limitations:**
The paper provides compelling empirical evidence to support its claims:
- **Low Accuracy on Implementation Benchmarks (Table 2):** Even state-of-the-art LLMs show low accuracy on benchmarks requiring practical implementation. For instance, OpenAI's "o1-preview" achieved only 16.90% success on MLE-Bench (AI training tasks), and "o1-high" managed just 26.00% on PaperBench (replicating ICML papers). Claude-Sonnet-3.7 achieved 39.00% on SciReplicate-Bench (code generation from algorithm descriptions). This highlights a "fundamental limitation in their verification capabilities."
- **Deficiencies in AI-Generated Papers (Tables 4 and 5):** An original evaluation using DeepReviewer-14B on 28 publicly available papers from 5 leading AI Scientist systems revealed profound shortcomings. The highest-rated system scored only 4.63 out of 10 on average, with most systems performing considerably worse across metrics like soundness, presentation, and contribution. A stark finding was "Experimental Weakness" in 100% of the assessed papers (Table 5), pointing to severe limitations in experimental design, execution, and analysis. Other pervasive defects included "Methodological Unclarity/Flaws" (96.4%), "Writing & Presentation Issues" (92.9%), "Novelty Concerns" (89.3%), and "Theoretical Weakness" (85.7%).
- **Conclusion:** "Current AI scientist systems, despite their technological sophistication, cannot independently produce scientific artifacts that meet established standards for high-quality scientific communication."

**C. Fundamental Limitations of Underlying Models (LLMs):**
The survey identifies intrinsic limitations of the foundational LLMs that hinder AI Scientist systems:
- **Hallucination:** LLMs are prone to generating plausible but false content, fabricating findings, unreliable citations, and logical inconsistencies, which compromises scientific accuracy and credibility.
- **High Cost and Inefficiency of Knowledge Updating:** The rapid pace of scientific discovery makes it challenging and computationally expensive to continuously update LLMs with the latest information, leading to outdated knowledge and slow integration.
- **Catastrophic Forgetting:** Attempts to integrate new scientific knowledge can inadvertently erase or degrade existing understanding, undermining the system's ability to maintain a comprehensive and coherent knowledge base.

**D. Limitations of Research Capabilities:**
Specific gaps in research capabilities, aligned with the framework, include:
- **Knowledge Acquisition:** Insufficient precision in information retrieval, and struggles with multi-document summarization, synthesis, and factual accuracy.
- **Idea Generation:** Difficulty generating truly novel and high-quality hypotheses (often repetitive), and challenges in objective, scalable evaluation of hypothesis quality.
- **Verification and Falsification:** Weaknesses in multi-agent collaboration (robustness, adaptability, fault tolerance, error handling), lack of scientific rigor and practicality in experimental designs, and poor performance in translating plans into executable code.
- **Evolution:** Underdeveloped dynamic planning (e.g., inefficient tree-search strategies), and "looping errors" in self-reflection without robust self-criticism. A lack of standardized "scientist-to-scientist" communication protocols hinders effective multi-agent collaboration.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The significance and potential impact of this survey paper are multi-faceted, ranging from its contribution to academic understanding to outlining the transformative (and challenging) future of AI in science.

**A. Academic Significance of the Survey Itself:**
This survey serves as a critical meta-analysis for the burgeoning field of AI Scientist systems. By introducing a novel, structured "capability-level framework" and conducting a unique empirical evaluation of AI-generated papers, the authors provide a clear, evidence-based roadmap. This is crucial for:
- **Clarifying the State-of-the-Art:** It precisely maps current achievements to specific capabilities, avoiding exaggerated claims.
- **Guiding Future Research:** By systematically identifying bottlenecks ("what is missing"), it provides a robust agenda for researchers to focus their efforts on the most impactful challenges.
- **Promoting Rigor:** The quantitative assessment of AI-generated research (Tables 4, 5) sets a benchmark for the quality expected from such systems, emphasizing the need for scientific rigor and implementation.

**B. Potential Transformative Impact of Mature AI Scientist Systems:**
If the identified gaps are addressed, the impact of AI Scientist systems could be revolutionary:
- **Accelerating Scientific Discovery:** By overcoming human limitations (cognitive bias, time, capacity), AI Scientists could drastically speed up literature review, hypothesis generation, experimentation, and data analysis, leading to faster breakthroughs.
- **Solving Grand Challenges:** The authors envision AI Scientists making "ground-breaking discoveries that solve grand challenges across medicine, energy, and the environment," fundamentally changing human civilization.
- **Reshaping the Research Paradigm:** AI could transition from being a mere "tool" to a "principal actor" in scientific research, driving discovery autonomously and collaboratively with humans.
- **Personalized Research Assistance:** AI Scientists could become tailored partners for individual researchers, actively learning preferences, identifying knowledge gaps, curating literature, and offering mutual guidance for co-evolution in research.
- **Societal Benefit:** Beyond individual researchers, AI Scientists could serve society by adapting to scientific trends, enabling cross-domain collaboration, conducting predictive research, reducing resource disparities in global research, and accelerating solutions for critical global issues (e.g., renewable energy, disease treatment).

**C. Ethical Considerations and Call to Action:**
A highly significant aspect of this survey is its dedicated section on ethical challenges. The authors warn of potential negative impacts:
- **Overwhelming Peer Review:** AI-generated content could flood scientific publishing, degrading quality and fairness.
- **Acceleration of Harmful Technologies:** Without oversight, AI could autonomously pursue dangerous research domains.
- **Erosion of Human Research Skills:** Over-reliance on AI could diminish critical thinking and hands-on skills in human scientists.
- **Research Biases and Vulnerabilities:** AI could perpetuate biases, waste resources, and introduce security flaws.

To mitigate these risks, the authors propose a comprehensive framework for **generation management, ethical oversight, and quality evaluation**. This includes centralized platforms for AI-generated output, clear boundaries between human and AI research, and global conventions for ethical principles and risk management. This proactive engagement with the ethical implications underscores the paper's broader societal relevance, advocating for responsible development alongside scientific advancement.

In conclusion, this survey is not just a review of existing technology but a strategic blueprint for the future of AI in science, critically evaluating its current standing and charting a responsible course toward its world-changing potential.
