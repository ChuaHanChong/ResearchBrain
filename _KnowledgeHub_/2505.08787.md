# UniSkill: Imitating Human Videos via Cross-Embodiment Skill Representations

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Yonsei University developed UniSkill, a framework enabling robots to acquire skills from arbitrary, unlabeled human and robot videos by learning universal, embodiment-agnostic motion representations. The system achieved higher success rates on real-world and simulated manipulation tasks, demonstrating robust generalization to novel compositional tasks and unseen environments.

## Problem

- Robots struggle to acquire skills from readily available human videos due to the significant "embodiment gap" in visual appearance, kinematics, and physical capabilities.
- Existing cross-embodiment imitation methods often rely on labor-intensive paired human-robot datasets, explicit semantic labels, or specialized data collection setups.
- The inability to leverage diverse, unaligned human video data limits the scalability and generalizability of robot skill acquisition.

## Method

- **UniSkill** proposes a three-stage framework comprising ==Universal Skill Representation Learning==, Universal Skill-Conditioned Policy Training, and Cross-Embodiment Imitation.
- It employs Inverse and Forward Skill Dynamics (ISD/FSD) models, trained on large-scale, unlabeled, multi-embodiment video data, to learn representations focused on motion dynamics between frames.
- The ISD model integrates pre-trained monocular depth estimation, and FSD is formulated as an image-editing task, guiding skill representations to capture dynamics rather than embodiment-specific visual details.

## Results

- UniSkill consistently achieved superior success rates compared to baseline methods (GCBC, XSkill) across real-world tabletop, kitchen, and simulated LIBERO benchmarks, particularly when prompted by human videos.
- The framework demonstrated strong generalization, enabling robots to perform novel compositional tasks and operate effectively in unseen, cluttered environments (Scene B) when guided by human video prompts.
- Ablation studies showed incorporating large-scale human video datasets (Something-Something V2, H2O) boosted performance by an additional **15%**, while t-SNE visualizations confirmed embeddings cluster by skill/task rather than demonstrator embodiment.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Reusable motion patterns exist across different embodiments and can be extracted as universal skill representations that are independent of the demonstrator's body or specific visual context.
> - The combined use of depth prediction and an image-editing objective is crucial for forcing skill representations to encode dynamic changes and avoid embedding static, embodiment-specific visual features.
> - Leveraging large-scale, diverse, and unlabeled human and robot video datasets is essential for achieving scalable and generalizable cross-embodiment imitation learning, removing common data collection constraints.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in "UniSkill" addresses a fundamental challenge in contemporary robot learning: enabling robots to acquire new skills by observing human demonstrations. This paradigm, known as learning from human videos, is critical for overcoming the notorious scarcity of robot-specific training data. Human videos are ubiquitous and diverse, offering a potentially vast and inexpensive source of knowledge for robot skill acquisition. However, a major hurdle remains the "embodiment gap" – the inherent differences between human and robot bodies in terms of their visual appearance, kinematics, and physical capabilities.

Historically, approaches to bridge this gap have largely fallen into a few categories:
1. **Paired Human-Robot Datasets**: Methods like those in rely on collecting demonstrations where humans and robots perform the exact same task in the same scene, often with explicit correspondence. This is extremely labor-intensive and difficult to scale.
2. **Predefined Semantic Skill Labels**: Other works attempt to transfer skills using pre-assigned semantic labels, which again requires significant human annotation and limits the discovery of novel skills.
3. **Explicit Action Representations**: Approaches using 2D/3D trajectories or flow fields extract more granular action information. However, these often demand specialized setups like multi-view calibrated cameras, pose tracking, or wearable sensors, restricting their applicability to "in-the-wild" human videos.
4. **Latent Action Models**: Recent advancements in learning latent actions from videos have shown promise, but these are often tailored to specific domains like games or use latent actions merely as pseudo-labels for pre-training, not as direct, transferable skill representations for robot policies.

A particularly relevant piece of prior work is XSkill, which also aims to learn cross-embodiment skill representations without relying on manually designed representations or explicit supervision. XSkill aligns skills from human and robot videos through Sinkhorn-Knopp clustering, enforcing shared, embodiment-agnostic prototypes. However, a crucial limitation of XSkill is its implicit assumption of *some degree of alignment* between human and robot videos, meaning human demonstrations still ideally need to cover the target robot task and be captured in similar environments for effective skill transfer.

UniSkill distinguishes itself by aiming to **completely remove these data collection constraints**. It proposes a novel approach that learns predictive representations by focusing on future frame forecasting rather than clustering. This fundamental shift allows UniSkill to leverage *arbitrary, embodiment-agnostic video datasets* for training, including "in-the-wild" human videos that are entirely unrelated to the robot's target tasks or environment. By learning from diverse, large-scale, and *unlabeled* video data, UniSkill directly tackles the scalability issue, pushing the boundaries of what is possible in learning robotic skills from broadly available human visual data. This positions UniSkill as a significant advancement towards genuinely scalable and generalizable cross-embodiment imitation learning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of UniSkill is to develop a **scalable framework for cross-embodiment imitation learning**, enabling a robot to learn new tasks by observing human demonstrations presented in video format. This overarching goal is driven by several key motivations:

1. **Bridging the Embodiment Gap without Alignment Constraints**: The most significant motivation is to overcome the inherent differences between human and robot embodiments (visual appearance and physical capabilities) without the need for labor-intensive paired human-robot datasets, explicit semantic labels, or strict scene/task alignment between human and robot demonstration videos. This aims to unlock the vast potential of "in-the-wild" human video data.
2. **Learning Universal, Embodiment-Agnostic Skill Representations**: The authors hypothesize that reusable motion patterns exist across different embodiments. Their objective is to extract these "universal skill representations" that are independent of the demonstrator (human or specific robot type) and the specific visual context. This representation should encode *dynamics changes* rather than static visual features.
3. **Enabling Direct Transfer to Robot Policies**: The learned skill representations must be directly interpretable and actionable by a robot's control policy. The goal is for skills extracted from an unseen human video prompt to effectively condition a robot policy, allowing it to execute the demonstrated behavior.
4. **Enhancing Generalization and Robustness**: The framework aims to generalize beyond seen demonstrations, enabling robots to perform compositional tasks, operate in unseen environments with novel objects, and be robust to variations in video prompts.
5. **Leveraging Large-Scale Unlabeled Video Data**: Motivated by the abundance of diverse human videos, a key objective is to design a system that can effectively learn from these large-scale, unlabeled sources, thereby moving beyond the limitations of smaller, curated datasets.

In essence, UniSkill seeks to answer the central question: "Can robots acquire cross-embodiment skill representations by watching large-scale human demonstrations?" by proposing a novel, constraint-free approach to skill representation learning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

UniSkill proposes a novel framework built upon three interconnected stages: Universal Skill Representation Learning, Universal Skill-Conditioned Policy Training, and Cross-Embodiment Imitation during inference.

#### 3.1. Universal Skill Representation Learning

This stage focuses on learning `embodiment-agnostic skill representations` (`z_t`) from large-scale, unlabeled, multi-embodiment video datasets (`D_u`). The core idea is that `z_t` should effectively compress the *dynamics* (motion patterns) between two temporally distant video frames, `I_t` and `I_{t+k}`, irrespective of the embodiment.

- **Inverse Skill Dynamics (ISD) Model**: This model takes a pair of frames `(I_t, I_{t+k})` and outputs the universal skill representation `z_t = ISD(I_t, I_{t+k})`. The visual encoder for ISD is based on ResNet-18, augmented with Spatial-Temporal Transformer blocks to capture dependencies.
- **Forward Skill Dynamics (FSD) Model**: This model takes the current frame `I_t` and the skill representation `z_t` and predicts the future frame `I_{t+k} = FSD(I_t, z_t)`.
- **Embodiment-Agnostic Design Choices**:
 - **Depth Integration**: To prevent `z_t` from encoding embodiment-specific details (like demonstrator appearance or static scene context), the ISD model internally employs a pre-trained monocular depth estimator (DepthAnythingV2). The predicted depth maps are used as intermediate representations, concatenated with visual features, guiding the model to focus on object interactions and motion rather than surface appearance.
 - **Image-Editing Formulation**: FSD is formulated as an image-editing task, adapting the diffusion-based InstructPix2Pix model. Here, `z_t` replaces the language instruction, enabling FSD to modify *only the dynamic components* of `I_t` to generate `I_{t+k}`, while preserving static background. This implicitly forces `z_t` to encapsulate motion patterns.
 - **Training Data Scale and Diversity**: The models are jointly trained on a vast array of video datasets, including human videos (Something-Something V2, H2O) and robot videos (DROID, BridgeV2, LIBERO). This wide diversity, coupled with the absence of labels or alignment constraints, is crucial for learning truly universal motion patterns.

#### 3.2. Universal Skill-Conditioned Policy Training

Once ISD and FSD are pre-trained and frozen, a robot policy `π_ϕ(a_{t:t+h} | o_t, z_t)` is trained.

- **Policy Architecture**: A diffusion policy is employed, conditioned on the current robot observation `o_t` and the skill representation `z_t`. It predicts a sequence of actions `a_{t:t+h}` over an action horizon `h`.
- **Training Data**: The policy is trained using behavioral cloning on a smaller, action-labeled robot demonstration dataset (`D_a`). During this training, the skill representations `z_t` are extracted by the *frozen* ISD using pairs of robot observations `(o_t, o_{t+k})` from `D_a`.
- **Mitigating Train-Test Discrepancy**: To enhance the policy's robustness when encountering `z_t` from different embodiments during inference (e.g., human videos), image augmentation (e.g., colorjittering) is applied to `o_t` and `o_{t+k}` during policy training. This simulates the domain gap and improves generalization.

#### 3.3. Cross-Embodiment Imitation with Universal Skill Representations (Inference)

During inference, UniSkill enables a robot to imitate behaviors from a given video prompt `V_p` (which can be from any embodiment, e.g., a human).

- **Skill Extraction**: The *frozen ISD* is used to extract a sequence of skill representations `{z_i}` from the prompt video `V_p` using a sliding window approach with a fixed temporal distance `k` between frames.
- **Policy Execution**: The *frozen skill-conditioned policy* `π_ϕ` then sequentially receives the robot's current observation `o_t` and the extracted skill `z_i` as conditioning signals to predict and execute the corresponding actions. The embodiment-agnostic nature of `z_i` allows `π_ϕ` to effectively translate human behaviors into robot actions, without any additional inputs like language instructions or goal images.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of UniSkill demonstrates its effectiveness in cross-embodiment imitation across diverse settings, significantly outperforming baselines and validating its core design principles.

- **Superior Cross-Embodiment Imitation**:
 - **Real-World Benchmarks (Tabletop, Kitchen)**: UniSkill consistently achieves higher success rates compared to goal-conditioned behavioral cloning (GCBC) and XSkill, especially when using human or unseen robot (Anubis) prompts. XSkill, despite requiring scene-aligned human data, performs poorly, indicating its inability to capture effective cross-embodiment dynamics.
 - **Simulation Benchmark (LIBERO)**: UniSkill again surpasses baselines, demonstrating strong performance even when human prompts involve novel objects and are from environments visually dissimilar to the simulation.
 - This highlights UniSkill's ability to generalize across different morphologies and unseen environments, a direct consequence of its embodiment-agnostic skill representations.

- **Robustness and Generalization of Skill Representations**:
 - **Compositional Task Solving**: UniSkill shows remarkable performance on novel compositional tasks (e.g., combining four distinct sub-tasks) even when prompted by human videos. This suggests it learns low-level, combinatorial skills rather than overfitting to specific tasks, indicating scalability to a wider range of complex behaviors.
 - **Unseen Scene Generalization**: UniSkill maintains comparable performance in visually altered (Scene A) and highly cluttered (Scene B) environments, confirming its resilience to background and distractor variations.
 - **Spatial Sensitivity**: While generally robust, UniSkill's performance can degrade if the target object's initial position in the test environment significantly deviates (e.g., >8cm) from its position in the prompt video, indicating its reliance on motion patterns rather than purely abstract task understanding.

- **Impact of Data Scale and Diversity**:
 - Ablation studies confirm that increasing the diversity and scale of training data is crucial. Adding more robot datasets improved performance by 20%, but crucially, incorporating large-scale *human* video datasets (Something-Something V2, H2O) further boosted performance by an additional 15%. This empirically validates the benefit of leveraging diverse human videos for learning transferable skills.

- **Effective Capture of Dynamics Information**:
 - Qualitative results from the Forward Skill Dynamics (FSD) model demonstrate that it successfully predicts future frames based on the extracted `z_t`, even when the current image is identical but `z_t` encodes different motions. This confirms that `z_t` truly captures meaningful motion dynamics.
 - Comparison with LAPA shows UniSkill's FSD produces sharp and accurate future frame predictions, unlike LAPA which yields blurry outputs, further validating the effectiveness of UniSkill's image-editing based objective for dynamic information encoding.

- **Embodiment-Agnostic Properties**:
 - **Preservation of Embodiment**: FSD predicts future frames that retain the input image's original embodiment, even when the conditioning `z_t` comes from a different embodiment. This property is exploited to improve GCBC (creating GCBC-U), where FSD-predicted frames (matching robot embodiment) as sub-goals significantly boost performance (by 15%) compared to raw human sub-goal images.
 - **Clustering by Skill**: t-SNE visualizations of UniSkill embeddings show distinct clusters corresponding to different *skills/tasks* rather than different *embodiments* (human vs. robot), directly confirming that the learned representations are indeed embodiment-agnostic.

- **Importance of Design Choices (Ablations)**:
 - **Depth Prediction**: Removing the internal depth prediction module drastically reduces performance and K-means clustering accuracy, underscoring its role in preventing `z_t` from encoding embodiment-specific visual details.
 - **Augmentation**: Augmenting ISD inputs during policy learning significantly improves robustness to scene color and object appearance changes, vital for generalization.
 - **Skill Interval (k)**: The chosen skill interval (k=20 for policy learning, 20-40 for representation learning) is optimal; too short or too long intervals lead to performance degradation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

UniSkill represents a significant step forward in the field of robot learning and cross-embodiment imitation, with profound implications for the scalability and accessibility of robotic skill acquisition.

- **Groundbreaking Scalability**: The most impactful contribution of UniSkill is its ability to learn transferable skill representations from **large-scale, unlabeled, and unaligned video data** across diverse embodiments. By effectively removing the need for labor-intensive paired demonstrations, semantic labels, or strict environment/task alignments, UniSkill unlocks the vast potential of "in-the-wild" human videos for robot learning. This fundamentally changes the data bottleneck challenge in robot learning.
- **Embodiment-Agnostic Learning**: The novel formulation using Inverse and Forward Skill Dynamics, coupled with intelligent use of depth information and an image-editing pipeline, successfully extracts representations that capture generic motion patterns rather than embodiment-specific visual details. This is a crucial technical advancement for true cross-embodiment transfer.
- **Enhanced Generalization**: The demonstrated ability to perform compositional tasks, operate in unseen environments with novel objects, and generalize across different robot morphologies (Franka, Anubis) significantly boosts the practical applicability of robot learning from human demonstrations. This move towards more robust and versatile robots is critical for real-world deployment.
- **Reduced Development Costs**: By leveraging readily available video data, UniSkill lowers the barrier to entry for developing new robot skills. This could democratize access to advanced robotics capabilities, allowing researchers and practitioners to rapidly prototype and deploy robots for new tasks without extensive custom data collection.
- **Interpretability of Skills**: The qualitative results showing FSD's accurate prediction of future frames based on skill representations indicate that UniSkill learns semantically meaningful motion dynamics. This interpretability can be valuable for understanding learned behaviors and debugging.
- **Future Research Catalyst**: UniSkill's limitations, such as handling variable execution speeds and abrupt viewpoint changes, open clear avenues for future research. The suggestion of integrating UniSkill with Vision-Language-Action (VLA) frameworks for higher-level semantic reasoning is particularly exciting, promising a powerful combination of fine-grained motion control and abstract task understanding.

In conclusion, UniSkill moves the field closer to the long-held vision of robots learning directly from human observation in a scalable and generalizable manner. Its elegant solution to the embodiment gap, by focusing on universal motion dynamics, is a significant technical achievement with the potential to accelerate the adoption of intelligent robots in diverse applications.
