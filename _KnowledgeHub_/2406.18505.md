# Mental Modeling of Reinforcement Learning Agents by Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> An empirical study investigates the capability of Large Language Models (LLMs) to construct "mental models" of reinforcement learning (RL) agents by inferring agent policies and environmental dynamics from interaction history. Findings indicate LLMs can partially model simpler tasks, such as predicting agent actions in MountainCar with 86.18% accuracy, but performance degrades with increasing task complexity and requires careful context provisioning, with explicit dynamics principles significantly improving understanding.

## Problem

- While Large Language Models (LLMs) demonstrate advanced linguistic reasoning, their inherent capacity to interpret and model decision-making agents operating in dynamic, physical or simulated environments remains underexplored.
- Reinforcement Learning (RL) agents often operate as black boxes, making their behavior difficult for humans to understand, trust, and debug, posing a significant challenge for Explainable Reinforcement Learning (XRL).
- There is a lack of empirical investigation into whether off-the-shelf LLMs can inherently construct "mental models" of an agent's policy and environmental dynamics solely from interaction history.

## Method

- The ==LLM-Xavier== evaluation framework is introduced, assessing LLMs' ==agent mental modelling== capabilities within ==Markov Decision Process (MDP)== contexts using in-context learning.
- Prompts are constructed with a system-level description of the RL task, a subset of the agent's interaction history (state-action-reward tuples), and specific evaluation questions, employing ==Chain-of-Thought (CoT)== for step-by-step reasoning.
- Evaluation metrics are defined for both "Actions Understanding" (agent's policy) and "Dynamics Understanding" (environment's transition function), covering prediction and deduction tasks for discrete and continuous spaces.

## Results

- LLMs demonstrated the ability to predict agent behaviors in simpler tasks with accuracies significantly above baseline, such as Llama3-70b achieving **86.18%** for next action prediction in MountainCar.
- Agent mental modeling capabilities consistently declined with increasing task complexity, particularly for environments with larger state and action spaces like Acrobot and Fetch tasks.
- The optimal interaction history length for in-context learning exhibited a saturation point, with excessively long histories sometimes degrading performance, especially for dynamics predictions.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Large Language Models can establish partial mental models for simpler reinforcement learning tasks by leveraging common-sense knowledge and inferring from interaction history.
> - The processing of interaction history for in-context learning is non-linear, with benefits saturating and sometimes degrading with excessive length, and LLMs surprisingly predict absolute continuous actions better than binned categories.
> - Providing explicit "dynamics principles" (e.g., equations) in the system prompt significantly enhances LLMs' ability to understand both agent behavior and environmental transitions, indicating their capacity to integrate structured physical knowledge.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research sits at the intersection of Large Language Models (LLMs), Reinforcement Learning (RL), and Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI), specifically eXplainable Reinforcement Learning (XRL). The broader landscape is characterized by the rapid advancements in LLMs, which have demonstrated surprising emergent reasoning abilities across various domains, including math, symbolic, and spatial reasoning, largely learned from vast text corpora. Simultaneously, RL agents are achieving superhuman performance in complex tasks, but their black-box nature often hinders human understanding and trust.

**Current State of LLMs:** While LLMs excel at processing and generating human-like text, their ability to interpret and model agents interacting with physical or simulated environments has remained relatively underexplored. Existing research has shown LLMs aiding in planning for embodied tasks (Li et al., 2022a; Huang et al., 2023) or being grounded/fine-tuned for enhanced embodied experiences (Liu et al., 2022; Xiang et al., 2023). However, these efforts often involve either limited physical world understanding or explicit fine-tuning. This paper differentiates itself by focusing on the *off-the-shelf* capabilities of general-purpose LLMs, examining their *inherent* capacity to build mental models of agents without specific fine-tuning for physics or dynamics.

**In-Context Learning and World Models:** The paper leverages the concept of in-context learning, a key emergent property of transformers, where models learn from input-output pairs provided in the prompt (Brown et et al., 2020; Garg et al., 2022). This is distinct from "in-context reinforcement learning," which typically involves fine-tuning transformers to predict actions given interaction history (Laskin et al., 2022; Lee et al., 2023). Instead, Lu et al. explore whether LLMs can *understand* agent behavior and environmental dynamics from historical state-action-reward tuples, thereby approximating an agent's "mental model" (Johnson-Laird, 1983; Bansal et al., 2019). This connects to the idea of "internal world models" (Lake et al., 2017; Amos et al., 2018), which allow agents to plan, predict, and reason. The authors investigate if LLMs inherently possess such world models relevant to physical interactions.

**Explainable Reinforcement Learning (XRL):** A major challenge in RL is explaining agent behavior to humans (Milani et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2024). LLMs' ability to provide human-readable explanations makes them a tempting candidate for XRL. This work directly addresses this by exploring whether LLMs can comprehend agent actions and their consequences, thereby potentially serving as "communication mediators" between black-box RL agents and human stakeholders.

In summary, this paper contributes to the cutting edge of AI research by empirically testing the fundamental question of whether LLMs, trained primarily on text, can develop cognitive maps or "mental models" of decision-making agents operating in dynamic environments. It aims to bridge the gap between abstract linguistic reasoning and grounded physical understanding, a crucial step for developing more robust and interpretable AI systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to empirically investigate, for the first time, whether Large Language Models (LLMs) can effectively construct a "mental model" of reinforcement learning (RL) agents. This capability, termed "agent mental modelling," involves reasoning about an agent's behavior and the resulting effects on environmental states solely from its interaction history.

The motivations driving this study are multifaceted:

1. **Assessing LLM Capabilities:** Modern LLMs exhibit impressive reasoning abilities and can theoretically express any probable token distribution. However, it's largely underexplored how the vast "world knowledge" acquired during pre-training can be applied to comprehend an agent's sequential behavior in the physical or simulated world. The study aims to shed light on the inherent capabilities and limitations of off-the-shelf LLMs in this specific, grounded reasoning task.
2. **Advancing Explainable Reinforcement Learning (XRL):** A significant motivation is to leverage LLMs for elucidating the opaque behavior of RL agents. If LLMs can build accurate mental models, they could translate complex agent policies and environmental dynamics into human-understandable explanations, addressing a key challenge in XRL and fostering greater trust and understanding of autonomous systems.
3. **Informing Agent-Oriented LLM Development:** Understanding LLMs' ability to interpret agent behavior is crucial for guiding the development of future "agent-oriented" LLMs. Such models could be designed not just to process language but also to plan and generate sequences of embodied actions more effectively, potentially leading to more sophisticated and autonomous agents.
4. **Enabling LLMs as Communication Mediators:** The authors envision LLMs acting as intermediaries between complex black-box agents and various stakeholders (e.g., human users, designers, regulators). By understanding agent behavior, LLMs could facilitate smoother communication and interaction.
5. **Formalizing Agent Mental Modelling:** The study formalizes "agent mental modelling" by requiring LLMs to comprehend both the actions taken by an agent and the subsequent state changes in a Markov Decision Process (MDP) context. This goes beyond traditional reasoning tasks, which often involve simpler symbolic manipulations.

In summary, the core objective is to quantify and qualitatively analyze LLMs' nascent ability to comprehend and predict both agent actions and environmental dynamics from interaction data, with a strong motivation to unlock their potential for explainable AI and more intelligent, embodied agents.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors propose an evaluation framework called **LLM-Xavier** (inspired by Xavier from X-Men for its mind-reading connotation) to empirically assess LLMs' agent mental modelling capabilities. The framework focuses on evaluating LLMs' understanding within the context of Markov Decision Processes (MDPs), comprising policies (π) and transition functions (T).

#### 3.1. In-Context Prompting Strategy

The evaluation relies on "in-context learning," where LLMs are provided with relevant information within the prompt itself, rather than undergoing fine-tuning. Each prompt is meticulously constructed with three main components:
- **System-level Prompt:** Provides a comprehensive description of the RL task's MDP components, including state space, action space, reward space, transition dynamics, initial state, and termination conditions. This gives the LLM foundational knowledge of the environment.
- **Interaction History (E_t,H):** A subset of the agent's actual interaction history (state-action-reward tuples: (s_i, a_i, r_i)) leading up to the current time step (t). This history serves as the "in-context" data from which the LLM is expected to infer agent behavior and dynamics. The length of this history (H) is a variable studied in ablations.
- **Evaluation Question Prompts:** Specific queries designed to probe different aspects of agent mental modelling, tailored based on whether the RL setting involves discrete or continuous state/action spaces.

All LLMs are prompted using the **Chain-of-Thought (CoT)** strategy, explicitly encouraging them to provide step-by-step reasoning and explanations before generating a final answer. This facilitates qualitative analysis of their thought processes and error types.

#### 3.2. Evaluation Metrics

To systematically assess LLMs' mental models, the authors define two primary categories of understanding, each with specific evaluation questions:

- **Actions Understanding (Agent's Policy):**
 - **Predicting Next Action:** Given the current state (s_t+1), predict the action (a_t+1) the agent would take.
 - **Deducing Last Action:** Given the current and next states (s_t+1, s_t+2), infer the action (a_t+1) that must have been taken.
- **Dynamics Understanding (Environment's Transition Function):**
 - **Predicting Next State:** Given the current state (s_t+1) and the action taken (a_t+1), predict the subsequent state (s_t+2).
 - **Deducing Last State:** Given the action taken (a_t+1) and the resulting state (s_t+2), infer the preceding state (s_t+1).

#### 3.3. Data Post-processing and Evaluation

- LLM predictions are extracted using regular expressions.
- For discrete actions, accuracy is a direct matching rate with ground truth.
- For continuous actions, two approaches are tested:
 - **Predicting bins:** The action range is divided into 10 bins, and accuracy is the matching rate of the predicted bin.
 - **Predicting absolute numbers:** LLMs predict exact values, which are then quantized into 10 bins for comparison with ground truth.
- For continuous state prediction, LLMs predict *relative changes* (increase, decrease, unchange) in state components, and accuracy is measured as a classification score.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

- **LLMs Tested:** GPT-3.5-turbo, Llama3-8B-Instruct, and Llama3-70B-Instruct.
- **Offline RL Datasets:** Seven diverse tasks from OpenAI Gym (Brockman et al., 2016) and Fetch environments (Plappert et al., 2018), covering varying state spaces (continuous, up to 25 dimensions), action spaces (discrete and continuous), and reward structures. These include MountainCar, Acrobot, LunarLander, Pendulum, InvertedDoublePendulum, FetchPickAndPlace, FetchPush, and FetchSlide. Approximately 2000 query samples (s, a, r tuples) were collected.
- **Ablation Studies:** Investigate the impact of:
 - History size (H).
 - Inclusion of sequential indices in history.
 - Inclusion of dynamics principles in the system prompt.
 - Inclusion of task descriptions in the system prompt.
- **Qualitative Error Analysis:** Manual review of 50 CoT responses for the MountainCar task to categorize error types (Task Understanding, Logic, History Understanding, Physical Understanding, Mathematical Understanding, Missing Information) and compare human judgment against automatic evaluation.

This comprehensive methodology allows for both quantitative performance measurement and qualitative insight into LLMs' reasoning processes and failure modes when attempting agent mental modelling.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical evaluation of LLMs' agent mental modelling capabilities yielded several key findings, highlighting both emergent abilities and significant limitations:

1. **Partial Mental Modelling from History:** LLMs can establish agent mental models to some extent, particularly for simpler tasks. For instance, in MountainCar, LLMs (e.g., Llama3-70b) can predict agent behaviors with accuracy significantly above a random baseline (e.g., 86.18% for next action prediction), utilizing common-sense knowledge related to cars and hills.
2. **Impact of Task Complexity:** Performance in both action and dynamics understanding consistently declines with increasing task complexity, especially for tasks with larger state and action spaces (e.g., Acrobot, Fetch tasks). This suggests that current LLMs struggle with more intricate environmental dynamics and agent policies.
3. **History Size for In-Context Learning:** Providing a longer interaction history generally improves LLMs' understanding of agent behaviors initially. However, this benefit saturates and can even degrade with excessively long histories, particularly for dynamics predictions. This "overload" effect indicates that current LLMs, despite large context windows, struggle to effectively process and extract relevant information from redundant or complex sequential data, contrary to typical learning curves.
4. **Surprising Performance in Continuous Action Prediction:** Counter-intuitively, LLMs performed better at predicting *absolute continuous action values* than at categorizing them into predefined *bins*. For example, Llama3-8b achieved 47.73% accuracy for absolute values versus 10.87% for bins in the Pendulum task. This suggests that predicting bins might require additional mathematical reasoning for categorization, which LLMs find more challenging than direct regression.
5. **Dynamics Understanding is Challenging but Shows Potential:**
 - LLMs' understanding of state changes diminishes with increasing task complexity, especially in high-dimensional state spaces. For example, in Acrobot, dynamics prediction accuracy was close to 0%.
 - There's a noticeable difference in predicting individual state elements. For MountainCar, LLMs found it easier to predict car position (element 0) than velocity (element 1), possibly due to the more linear dynamics of position change. In contrast, Acrobot showed uniform low accuracy across all elements due to highly non-linear dynamics.
 - Crucially, explicitly providing "dynamics principles" (equations) in the prompt significantly improved both behavior and dynamics prediction, indicating that LLMs can leverage structured physical knowledge when available.
 - Smaller models (e.g., Llama3-8b) sometimes performed comparably to larger models (e.g., GPT-3.5) in predicting individual state elements, suggesting that state complexity might be a more influential factor than model size in certain dynamics understanding tasks.
6. **Impact of Prompting Format and Information:**
 - **Indexed History:** Excluding sequential indices from the history context negatively impacted performance, indicating that explicit indexing helps LLMs process raw sequential data.
 - **Task Description:** Providing a "task description" (even if not directly numerical) was essential for a better understanding of both agent behavior and dynamics. Removing it significantly degraded performance, highlighting LLMs' ability to integrate high-level contextual information beyond mere numerical regression.
7. **Qualitative Analysis of Error Types:** A manual review of Chain-of-Thought (CoT) responses for MountainCar revealed distinct error patterns:
 - **Task Understanding Errors:** Common across models, such as misunderstanding the goal (e.g., being concerned about "overshooting" a positive target position in MountainCar where it's not a penalty).
 - **Logical Errors:** Paradoxical reasoning or incorrect causal links between actions and outcomes.
 - **History Understanding Errors:** Misinterpreting or misremembering past actions/states.
 - **Physical/Mathematical Errors:** Incorrect application of physical principles or numerical misinterpretations (e.g., sign errors).
 - GPT-3.5 showed superior comprehension of task strategies (e.g., needing to oscillate for momentum) compared to Llama3 models, although Llama3-70b sometimes excelled in maintaining coherence.

In essence, while LLMs exhibit a foundational ability to model simple agent behaviors from history, their performance is highly sensitive to task complexity, the quality of provided context, and the structure of the prompt. Significant challenges remain, especially in complex dynamics understanding and robust reasoning.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research marks a significant step towards understanding the cognitive capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs) beyond pure linguistic tasks, particularly concerning their ability to comprehend and model decision-making agents in dynamic environments. Its significance and potential impact span several critical areas:

1. **Foundational Understanding of LLM Capabilities:** By conducting the first empirical study on "agent mental modelling," the paper provides novel insights into whether LLMs can synthesize a coherent understanding of an agent's policy and environmental dynamics from interaction data. The findings demonstrate that LLMs possess some emergent capabilities in this domain, yet also highlight critical limitations, especially with increasing task complexity and continuous action/state spaces. This helps to better delineate what LLMs can and cannot currently do, informing future research directions in AI.

2. **Advancing Explainable Reinforcement Learning (XRL):** The most direct impact of this work lies in XRL. The ability of LLMs to generate human-readable explanations, combined with their capacity for agent mental modelling, holds immense promise for making black-box RL agents more transparent. If LLMs can accurately infer an agent's intentions, strategies, and the consequences of its actions, they could serve as powerful tools for generating post-hoc explanations, helping humans understand, trust, and debug complex autonomous systems in critical domains like robotics or healthcare. This could bridge the "understanding gap" between highly performant, yet opaque, AI agents and human users.

3. **Enabling LLMs as Communication Mediators:** The vision of LLMs acting as intelligent communication mediators between AI agents and various human stakeholders (e.g., developers, end-users, regulators) is compelling. By translating agent behaviors and environmental nuances into natural language, LLMs could facilitate safer human-AI collaboration and more effective oversight of AI systems.

4. **Guiding the Development of Agent-Oriented LLMs:** The insights gained regarding LLMs' strengths and weaknesses in understanding physical interactions and agent policies can directly inform the design of future "agent-oriented" or "embodied" LLMs. Future architectures or training regimes could be optimized to explicitly learn world models, physical common sense, and better integrate sequential decision-making data, leading to LLMs that are not just language processors but also capable planners and actors in the physical world. The finding that providing explicit dynamics principles improves performance suggests avenues for incorporating more structured knowledge into LLM training or prompting.

5. **Benchmarking and Evaluation Methodology:** The proposed LLM-Xavier evaluation framework, along with its specific evaluation metrics for action and dynamics understanding (including handling continuous spaces), provides a valuable testbed for systematically assessing LLMs in this novel domain. This standardized approach can be adopted by future research to track progress and compare different LLM architectures or prompting strategies.

6. **Highlighting Future Research Directions:** The paper identifies clear avenues for future work, such as scaling up the amount of demonstration data provided to LLMs, exploring fine-tuning with specific domain demonstrations, and extending the evaluation to multi-modal RL tasks. It also raises crucial ethical considerations regarding the potential for LLM "hallucinations" and the need for robust reliability checks before deploying LLMs for explaining agent behavior to humans.

In conclusion, this research is a pivotal step towards integrating the powerful reasoning capabilities of LLMs with the dynamic, sequential decision-making of RL agents. While current LLMs demonstrate nascent abilities, the work rigorously quantifies their limitations and provides a clear roadmap for developing more robust, interpretable, and truly intelligent AI systems that can seamlessly operate and explain themselves within complex environments.
