# DiffTune: Auto-Tuning through Auto-Differentiation

> [!summary] Summary
> DiffTune, developed by researchers at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign, offers a framework for efficiently auto-tuning robot controllers by computing gradients directly from physical system data using sensitivity propagation. It integrates L1 adaptive control to handle real-world uncertainties, achieving faster convergence and superior tracking accuracy in both simulations and hardware experiments on a quadrotor compared to current auto-tuning techniques.

## Problem

- Manual tuning of low-level robotic controllers is inefficient, time-consuming, and requires significant expert knowledge for complex, nonlinear systems.
- Existing model-based auto-tuning methods struggle with model imperfections and unmodeled dynamics in physical systems, leading to a "sim-to-real gap."
- Model-free auto-tuning techniques are often computationally inefficient for high-dimensional parameter spaces and lack strong stability guarantees, despite being compatible with real-world data.

## Method

- **DiffTune** formulates controller tuning as a ==parameter optimization== problem, minimizing a differentiable loss function using ==projected gradient descent==.
- Introduces ==sensitivity propagation== to compute gradients directly from real-world sensor data, bypassing the need for a full computational graph of system dynamics.
- Integrates ==L1 adaptive control (L1AC)== to compensate for unmodeled dynamics and disturbances in physical systems, ensuring ==unbiased gradient computation==.

## Results

- DiffTune demonstrated superior efficiency in simulations, achieving faster convergence and lower Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) for Dubin's car and quadrotor tasks compared to AutoTune, SafeOpt, and GIBO.
- In real-world quadrotor experiments, DiffTune reduced tracking RMSE by up to **3.5 times** for aggressive trajectories within a budget of only **10 trials**, consistently outperforming manually tuned parameters.
- Ablation studies in both simulation and physical experiments confirmed that combining DiffTune's parameter optimization with L1 Adaptive Control's uncertainty compensation yielded the most robust and accurate tracking performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Efficient gradient-based auto-tuning can be applied to physical systems by propagating sensitivity states forward in time, enabling direct use of real-world sensor data.
> - The combination of parameter optimization (DiffTune) and uncertainty compensation (L1 adaptive control) provides synergistic benefits for both tuning efficiency and robustness in dynamic, uncertain environments.
> - Leveraging first-order gradient information leads to significantly faster convergence and performance improvement compared to model-free, probabilistic auto-tuning methods, especially with limited trials.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The performance of robotic systems in complex tasks is significantly dependent on the meticulous tuning of their low-level controllers. Traditionally, this process involves manual trial-and-error by experienced experts or the application of domain-specific methods (e.g., Ziegler-Nichols for PID controllers). Such manual approaches are often inefficient, particularly for systems with long operational cycles or high-dimensional parameter spaces.

To address these limitations, automatic tuning (auto-tuning) methods have been developed. These methods generally fall into two categories:
- **Model-based auto-tuning:** These approaches utilize knowledge of the system model to infer optimal parameters, often employing gradient descent on a performance criterion. They can offer stability guarantees. However, their primary drawback is sensitivity to model imperfections and real-world uncertainties, leading to a "sim-to-real gap" when parameters tuned in simulation are deployed on physical systems. Examples include LQR tuning using Simultaneous Perturbation Stochastic Approximation and methods differentiating through the model and feedback loop for PID controllers.
- **Model-free auto-tuning:** These methods typically approximate gradients or use surrogate models, often data-driven, without strong assumptions about the system model. This characteristic makes them compatible with data from physical systems. Representative techniques include Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC), deep neural networks (DNN), and Bayesian optimization (BO), such as SafeOpt. While compatible with real-world data, model-free methods often struggle to provide stability guarantees and can be computationally inefficient, especially in high-dimensional parameter spaces.

The current research by Cheng et al. introduces DiffTune, which aims to integrate the strengths of both model-based and model-free approaches while mitigating their respective weaknesses. DiffTune seeks to achieve stability, compatibility with physical system data, and efficiency simultaneously for general controllers. It leverages the "end-to-end" concept from machine learning by formulating tuning as a parameter optimization problem amenable to gradient-based methods, but adapts the gradient computation for real-world application. Furthermore, it incorporates L1 adaptive control to explicitly address the inherent uncertainties in physical systems, which is a common challenge for model-based methods. This positions DiffTune as a method designed to bridge the gap between theoretical model-based efficiency and practical real-world robustness.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the recognized challenges in optimizing the performance of robotic systems. Achieving optimal performance often necessitates fine-tuning the parameters of lower-level controllers. These controllers, governing complex, intrinsically nonlinear dynamics, make manual tuning an arduous, time-consuming task requiring substantial expertise. Existing automatic tuning methods present significant trade-offs: model-based methods often falter in the presence of unmodeled dynamics and uncertainties inherent in physical systems (the "sim-to-real gap"), while model-free, data-driven methods, though compatible with physical system data, frequently lack efficiency in high-dimensional parameter spaces and struggle to provide strong stability guarantees.

Against this backdrop, the key objectives of the DiffTune framework are detailed as follows:
- **Formulate controller tuning as a parameter optimization problem:** The goal is to mathematically define the tuning task as minimizing a differentiable loss function (representing performance) with respect to controller parameters, constrained by the system's dynamics and the chosen controller structure.
- **Develop a gradient-based automatic tuning framework:** This framework, termed DiffTune, aims to systematically update controller parameters through gradient descent, leveraging first-order information for efficient optimization.
- **Ensure broad applicability to general nonlinear systems and controllers:** DiffTune is intended to be applicable as long as the system dynamics, the controller, and the loss function are differentiable. This covers a wide range of algebraically computed controllers (e.g., PID) and those derived from optimization problems (e.g., MPC, LQR) via the Implicit Function Theorem.
- **Enable compatibility with physical systems' data:** A crucial objective is to overcome the limitation of standard auto-differentiation (AD) methods, which require a complete computational graph. DiffTune aims to compute gradients directly from real-world sensor data, addressing the sim-to-real gap.
- **Achieve efficiency for online deployment:** The method should facilitate rapid parameter convergence to minimize the number of costly real-world trials and enable potential real-time adaptation.
- **Address uncertainties and disturbances in physical systems:** Unmodeled dynamics can bias gradient computation, leading to inefficient or suboptimal tuning. An objective is to integrate a mechanism, such as L1 adaptive control (L1AC), to compensate for these uncertainties, ensuring that the gradient used for tuning remains accurate and effective.
- **Demonstrate superior performance compared to state-of-the-art auto-tuning methods:** Through both simulations and physical experiments, DiffTune is intended to exhibit better efficiency and achieve lower tracking errors than existing model-free probabilistic auto-tuning techniques.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The DiffTune framework formulates controller tuning as a parameter optimization problem (P), seeking to minimize a differentiable loss function, $L(\theta)$, where $\theta$ represents the controller parameters. This minimization is subject to the discrete-time system dynamics $x_{k+1} = f(x_k, u_k)$ and a feedback controller $u_k = h(x_k, \bar{x}_k, \theta)$, where $x_k$ are states and $\bar{x}_k$ are desired states. The solution approach involves projected gradient descent, iteratively updating $\theta$ using the formula $\theta \leftarrow P_{\Theta}(\theta - \alpha \nabla_{\theta} L)$, where $P_{\Theta}$ projects parameters into a feasible set $\Theta$ (ensuring stability), and $\alpha$ is the learning rate.

The core challenge lies in computing the gradient $\nabla_{\theta} L$.
- **Auto-Differentiation (AD) for Simulations:** When the entire system (dynamics and controller) is simulated, it can be unrolled into a complete computational graph. In this scenario, standard AD tools like PyTorch, TensorFlow, JAX, or CasADi can directly compute $\nabla_{\theta} L$ by differentiating through this graph.
- **Sensitivity Propagation for Physical Systems:** A significant innovation of DiffTune is its compatibility with data collected from physical systems. For physical systems, the computational graph is "broken" because states are obtained from sensor measurements rather than explicit evaluation of the dynamics function. To overcome this, DiffTune employs **sensitivity propagation**.
 - The gradient $\nabla_{\theta} L$ is broken down using the chain rule into terms involving $\partial x_k / \partial \theta$ and $\partial u_k / \partial \theta$ (termed "sensitivity states").
 - Iterative formulas for these sensitivity states are derived by taking partial derivatives of the system dynamics and controller equations with respect to $\theta$:
 $\frac{\partial x_{k+1}}{\partial \theta} = (\nabla_{x_k} f + \nabla_{u_k} f \nabla_{x_k} h) \frac{\partial x_k}{\partial \theta} + \nabla_{u_k} f \nabla_{\theta} h$
 $\frac{\partial u_k}{\partial \theta} = \nabla_{x_k} h \frac{\partial x_k}{\partial \theta} + \nabla_{\theta} h$
 with $\frac{\partial x_0}{\partial \theta} = 0$.
 - These equations propagate the sensitivity states forward in time, in parallel with the system's actual evolution. The Jacobians (e.g., $\nabla_x f$, $\nabla_u f$, $\nabla_x h$, $\nabla_{\theta} h$) are evaluated at the measured states $x_k$ and computed control $u_k$. While the full graph is broken, AD tools can still be used to compute these individual Jacobians.
 - The total gradient $\nabla_{\theta} L$ is then computed as a weighted sum of these sensitivity states. This forward-in-time propagation enables online tuning and flexible adjustment of the evaluation horizon.
- **Uncertainty Handling with L1 Adaptive Control (L1AC):** Physical systems inevitably contain model uncertainties and disturbances. If not accounted for, these can bias the computed gradient, leading to inefficient tuning. DiffTune integrates L1AC to compensate for these uncertainties.
 - L1AC (e.g.,) employs a state predictor and an adaptation law to estimate matched and unmatched uncertainties ($\sigma_m$, $\sigma_{um}$).
 - An adaptive control term ($u_{ad}$) is generated to cancel out the matched uncertainties, effectively making the uncertain physical system behave similarly to its nominal (uncertainty-free) model.
 - By ensuring the closed-loop system behaves like the nominal system, the sensitivity propagation can proceed based on the nominal dynamics, preserving the gradient from being biased by unmodeled uncertainties. This results in more efficient and robust tuning for real-world systems.
- **Open-Source Toolset:** The authors provide DiffTuneOpenSource, an open-source toolset that automates the generation of required partial derivatives, simplifying the application of DiffTune for users with custom systems and controllers.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research validated DiffTune through extensive simulations on a Dubin's car and a quadrotor, as well as physical experiments on a quadrotor.

#### 4.1. Simulation Results

- **Dubin's Car:**
 - **Comparison with Baselines:** DiffTune demonstrated superior efficiency in the initial phase of tuning (first 20 trials) compared to AutoTune, SafeOpt, and GIBO, achieving the fastest reduction in Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE). This was attributed to its effective use of first-order system information. While GIBO eventually achieved a slightly lower minimum error after 100 trials, DiffTune showed more rapid convergence. AutoTune and SafeOpt exhibited slower error reduction and higher final errors.
 - **Generalization:** Controller parameters tuned on a batch of nine diverse trajectories generalized effectively to four unseen testing trajectories (lemon-, twist-, peanut-, and spiral-shaped). The tuned parameters consistently resulted in significantly lower tracking losses compared to untuned parameters, demonstrating robustness to previously unencountered paths (e.g., RMSE reduction from 974.57 to 2.05 on a 'twist' trajectory).
 - **Uncertainty Handling (Ablation Study):** With additive force and moment uncertainties injected into the system, an ablation study revealed that both DiffTune and L1AC independently improved performance. The combination of DiffTune and L1AC achieved the best overall performance, indicating that L1AC effectively compensated for uncertainties while DiffTune optimized parameters, leading to a synergistic improvement across a range of uncertainty intensities.

- **Quadrotor:**
 - **Comparison with Baselines:** For a geometric controller with a 12-dimensional parameter space, DiffTune consistently achieved the minimum tracking RMSE with superior efficiency across 2D circle, 3D circle, and 3D figure 8 trajectories compared to AutoTune, SafeOpt-PSO, and GIBO. The baseline methods, being model-free and probabilistic, were less efficient and required tuning more hyperparameters. DiffTune only required tuning the learning rate.
 - **Uncertainty Handling (Ablation Study with MoI perturbation):** When the quadrotor's moment of inertia (MoI) was perturbed (scaling factor $\beta$ from 0.5 to 4), an ablation study confirmed that both tuning via DiffTune and compensation via L1AC individually reduced tracking RMSE. The joint application of DiffTune and L1AC yielded the lowest tracking RMSE across all tested perturbation levels, confirming the benefits of combining parameter optimization with robust uncertainty handling.

#### 4.2. Experiment Results (Quadrotor)

- **Performance Improvement under Limited Budget:** DiffTune was applied to tune a nonlinear quadrotor controller (12 parameters) for tracking aggressive circular trajectories (1, 2, and 3 m/s) with a budget of only 10 trials.
 - The tracking RMSE was reduced by 1.5x, 2.5x, and 3.5x for the 1, 2, and 3 m/s trajectories, respectively.
 - All DiffTune-tuned parameters consistently achieved lower RMSEs than manually tuned parameters.
 - The evolution of parameters, particularly the angular velocity derivative gains ($k_{\Omega x}, k_{\Omega y}$), showed significant changes and complex trajectories in the parameter space, which are challenging to identify manually.
- **Generalization to Unseen Trajectories:** Parameters tuned for specific circular speeds (P1, P2, P3) were tested on unseen 3D figure 8 and vertical figure 8 trajectories, which involved wider ranges of speed and acceleration.
 - Tuned parameters performed best on the speeds they were trained for, akin to an "overfitting" phenomenon in machine learning.
 - However, parameters tuned for more aggressive maneuvers (P2, P3) generalized well to the complex figure 8 trajectories, outperforming hand-tuned parameters, demonstrating improved agility. P3, for instance, showed the best performance on DF(2) and VF(2), though it induced minor oscillations at lower speeds.
- **Ablation Study (DiffTune vs. L1AC):** An ablation study in real flight demonstrated that DiffTune alone improved performance (reducing RMSE from 61.8 cm to 17.7 cm for a 3 m/s trajectory with L1AC off). L1AC alone also improved performance by compensating for inherent system uncertainties (reducing RMSE from 61.8 cm to 46.6 cm for a 3 m/s trajectory with DiffTune off). The combination of DiffTune and L1AC yielded the best tracking performance across all speeds, illustrating their synergistic roles in mitigating uncertainties and optimizing parameters.
- **Sim-to-Real Comparison:** Parameters auto-tuned solely in simulation often performed poorly or led to crashes when deployed on the real quadrotor, particularly with more simulation-tuning trials (e.g., Sim_30, Sim_50 crashed on C(2) and C(3)). This highlighted the limitations of simulation-based tuning due to the sim-to-real gap. In contrast, parameters tuned with DiffTune using experimental data (ExpT) provided the best real-flight performance, confirming the benefit of tuning directly on physical system data. Simulation-tuned parameters from early trials (e.g., Sim_10) could, however, serve as effective warm-starts for experiment-based tuning.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

DiffTune represents a significant contribution to the field of robotic control and auto-tuning by offering a framework that combines the efficiency of gradient-based optimization with compatibility for physical systems.

The core significance of this work lies in:
- **Bridging the Sim-to-Real Gap:** By enabling direct gradient computation from physical system data through sensitivity propagation, DiffTune offers a robust solution for tuning controllers on actual hardware, addressing a long-standing challenge in robotics where simulation-tuned parameters often fail to transfer effectively.
- **Enhanced Efficiency and Performance:** The method leverages first-order information, allowing for rapid performance improvement with a limited number of trials compared to probabilistic model-free methods that require extensive data collection. This is crucial for real-world applications where trial execution can be costly, time-consuming, or lead to mechanical wear.
- **Robustness to Uncertainties:** The integration of L1 adaptive control provides a principled way to compensate for inherent model uncertainties and disturbances in physical systems. This ensures that the computed gradients remain unbiased, leading to more accurate and effective parameter updates, ultimately enhancing the reliability and robustness of the tuned controllers.
- **Broad Applicability:** DiffTune is designed for general nonlinear dynamical systems and controllers, requiring only differentiability. This broad scope means it can be applied to a wide array of robotic platforms and control strategies, from classic PID to modern MPC and safe controllers.
- **Reduced Reliance on Human Expertise:** By automating the tuning process, DiffTune can reduce the need for seasoned experts to manually tune complex multi-parameter controllers. This can democratize access to high-performance control and facilitate the rapid deployment of robotic systems.
- **Facilitating Research and Development:** The provision of an open-source toolset (DiffTuneOpenSource) encourages adoption and further research, allowing other practitioners to implement and extend the method for their specific systems and applications.

The potential impact of DiffTune extends to various domains within robotics, including autonomous navigation, agile manipulation, and aerial robotics, where precise and high-performance control is paramount. This framework could enable robotic systems to adapt more efficiently to new tasks, changing environments, or hardware modifications, leading to more intelligent and autonomous operation. Future work may explore its application to non-differentiable systems using subgradients and methods to ensure convergence to a global minimum, further expanding its utility.
