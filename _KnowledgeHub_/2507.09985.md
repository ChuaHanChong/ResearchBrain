# Demonstrating the Octopi-1.5 Visual-Tactile-Language Model

> [!summary] Summary
> The Octopi-1.5, a Visual-Tactile-Language Model from the National University of Singapore, integrates touch, vision, and language through an enhanced tactile encoder and a Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) module. The model demonstrated high accuracy in object identification (up to 95.12% for unseen objects with RAG) and the ability to sort items by tactile properties using a portable interface.

## Problem

- Robots lack comprehensive tactile perception, limiting their ability to understand crucial latent object properties like softness and texture for dexterous manipulation.
- Effectively integrating tactile signals with visual and linguistic reasoning in multimodal AI models remains a challenge.
- Current Visual-Tactile-Language Models (VTLMs) often struggle with adapting to new objects and generalizing tactile property inference without extensive retraining.

## Method

- Developed **Octopi-1.5**, a VTLM leveraging ==Qwen2-VL 7B== as its backbone and an improved CLIP-based tactile encoder, trained through a two-stage process on expanded datasets using both ==regression and contrastive losses==.
- Integrated a ==Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)== module to enable on-the-fly learning for new objects and enhance tactile descriptions.
- Showcased capabilities via a portable Tactile Manipulation Interface (TMI), allowing for accessible real-time interaction and demonstration.

## Results

- Achieved up to **95.12%** accuracy in tactile-only "Guessing Game" for unseen objects when RAG's "teaching" mode was enabled, significantly outperforming prior models.
- Demonstrated high accuracy in sorting objects by hardness, achieving **100%** for seen balls and **93.18%** for seen fruits.
- Validated the effectiveness of a portable Tactile Manipulation Interface (TMI) for real-time demonstrations, enabling accessible interaction with the VTLM.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating a Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) module significantly enhances VTLM performance, especially for unseen objects, by enabling on-the-fly knowledge acquisition.
> - Using a powerful VLM backbone (Qwen2-VL 7B) combined with an improved tactile encoder trained on expanded datasets leads to superior object identification and property description.
> - Designing VTLMs to process tactile signals from multiple distinct parts of an object allows for a more granular and comprehensive understanding of complex items.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of Octopi-1.5 is situated at the intersection of several rapidly advancing fields: robotic tactile perception, multimodal large language models (MLLMs), and embodiment for AI systems.

Historically, touch has been recognized as a critical sense for humans and robots alike, enabling dexterous manipulation, material identification, and navigation in visually occluded environments. While vision has dominated robotic perception research, tactile sensing has seen significant advancements in recent years, particularly with the emergence of high-resolution optical tactile sensors like GelSight and Digit, as well as piezoresistive sensors like TAC-02. Concurrently, the field of artificial intelligence has witnessed an explosion in the capabilities of large language models (LLMs) and vision-language models (VLMs), enabling sophisticated reasoning and natural language interaction.

This paper directly addresses a crucial gap: the effective integration of tactile signals into the rich representational and reasoning capabilities of VLMs, leading to the nascent field of Visual-Tactile-Language Models (VTLMs). Previous pioneering works have laid the groundwork by collecting large-scale multimodal datasets (e.g., TVL Dataset, ObjectFolder, PhysiCLeAR) and developing methods to align tactile, visual, and language representations. These efforts often involve training tactile encoders (e.g., using Visual Transformers) and integrating them with pre-trained VLMs.

Octopi-1.5 builds upon this foundation by specifically advancing the capabilities of VTLMs in several key ways. First, it scales up the training data by expanding existing datasets, contributing to more robust tactile understanding. Second, it leverages a more powerful and open-source VLM backbone (Qwen2-VL 7B instead of Llama used in its predecessor), enhancing its general interaction and commonsense knowledge capabilities. Most notably, Octopi-1.5 introduces a simple yet effective Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) module. This is a significant step, as RAG allows the model to retrieve and utilize information from a database of similar objects, not only boosting performance on known tasks but also offering a pathway for "on-the-fly" learning of new tactile-object pairings. This moves VTLMs closer to adaptive and knowledge-aware robotic systems.

Furthermore, the paper emphasizes the practical aspect of interaction through the Tactile Manipulation Interface (TMI). The TMI, a modified Universal Manipulation Interface (UMI), provides a portable and accessible platform for real-time demonstration without requiring a complex robotic setup. This focus on demonstrable, interactive systems helps bridge the gap between theoretical model development and real-world robotic deployment, fostering broader community engagement and feedback on current VTLM capabilities and limitations. In essence, Octopi-1.5 pushes the boundaries of VTLMs by enhancing their learning, reasoning, and interactive capabilities through improved data, model architecture, and a novel RAG component, all while presenting it in a user-friendly demonstration setup.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind the Octopi-1.5 project stems from the recognized importance of touch for robotic capabilities, especially in scenarios where vision is insufficient or contact-rich interaction is required. Robots often need to discern latent object properties like softness, texture, or deformability for tasks such as dexterous manipulation, material identification, or handling objects in visually occluded environments. Integrating this crucial tactile information with the high-level reasoning and natural language understanding offered by modern large language models is seen as a vital step towards more capable and intuitive robots.

The key objectives of the Octopi-1.5 project, as detailed in this paper, are:

- **Advance Visual-Tactile-Language Models (VTLMs):** To develop an improved VTLM that can effectively process and integrate visual, tactile, and language modalities. This involves enhancing tactile encoding and leveraging a more powerful VLM backbone.
- **Handle Multi-Part Objects:** To enable the model to process tactile signals not just from single objects, but also from different distinct parts of an object (e.g., a hairbrush handle versus its bristles), allowing for more nuanced property descriptions.
- **Improve Performance and Learnability:** To enhance the model's ability to perform tactile inference tasks and, crucially, to explore methods for enabling it to "learn new objects on-the-fly" through a Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) module. This aims to overcome the limitations of static, pre-trained knowledge.
- **Demonstrate Real-time Capabilities:** To showcase the practical utility and interactive nature of the Octopi-1.5 system in real-time through a live demonstration. This includes scenarios like identifying grasped objects (Guessing Game) and sorting items based on tactile properties.
- **Facilitate Accessible Interaction:** To provide a convenient and accessible platform for users to interact with the VTLM without requiring a complex robotic setup. The handheld Tactile Manipulation Interface (TMI) serves this purpose, making the technology approachable and portable for conference demonstrations.
- **Highlight Progress and Limitations:** To openly present both the advancements achieved by Octopi-1.5 and the remaining challenges and limitations of current VTLMs, thereby fostering discussion and inspiring further research in the field of tactile robotics and multimodal AI.

In essence, the motivation is to make robots more "sensory-aware" beyond just vision, enabling them to leverage touch for richer understanding and more sophisticated interactions, with the ultimate goal of developing more generally intelligent and adaptable robotic systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Octopi-1.5 is a sophisticated Visual-Tactile-Language Model (VTLM) designed to interpret multimodal inputs and respond to natural language queries. Its methodology can be broken down into three main components: the model architecture, its training strategy, and the accompanying hardware for interaction.

#### 3.1. Octopi-1.5 Model Architecture

The core of Octopi-1.5 is built upon the **Qwen2-VL 7B** open-source vision-language model (VLM). This marks an upgrade from the previous Octopi version, which used Llama, aiming for enhanced interaction and commonsense knowledge capabilities. The model's overall structure comprises:
- **Encoders:** These modules transform raw inputs into tokens digestible by the VLM.
 - **Tactile Encoder:** A key improvement, this component translates tactile frames from a GelSight Mini sensor into tokens. It consists of a fine-tuned **CLIP (L/14)** module augmented with a projection layer. To manage computational load, only "salient" tactile frames (top 10 frames with largest differences from preceding ones) are processed using a heuristic.
 - **Vision Encoder:** This handles visual inputs.
 - **Word Encoder:** This processes natural language queries.
- **Decoder/Transformer (Qwen2-VL):** This is the large language model backbone that processes the encoded tokens from all modalities to generate textual outputs.

#### 3.2. Training Methodology

Octopi-1.5's training proceeded in two distinct stages to ensure stability and optimal performance:
- **Stage 1: Tactile Encoder Pre-training:** The CLIP module within the tactile encoder was pre-trained using a combination of **regression and contrastive losses**.
 - **Datasets:** Training utilized an expanded **PhysiCLeAR dataset** (including both marker and markerless GelSight pads), alongside the **Hardness** and **ObjectFolder** datasets. Table I details the dataset statistics.
 - **Regression Loss:** The network was trained to predict human-annotated hardness and roughness scores based on GelSight inputs. Annotators followed specific guidance (Table II) to ensure consistent labeling.
 - **Contrastive Loss:** This loss function trained the network to distinguish between tactile inputs from the same object/part (positive pairs) and those from different objects/parts (negative pairs), ensuring consistency by sampling within the same GelSight pad type.
 - This stage involved 30 epochs, with the best encoder selected based on validation loss on a set of six unseen objects.
- **Stage 2: End-to-End VTLM Fine-tuning:** After the CLIP module was frozen, its projection layers were trained alongside the Qwen2-VL decoder.
 - **Visual Prompt Tuning (VPT):** This technique was employed for training on specific tasks.
 - **Tasks:** The model was trained on description and ranking tasks (Fig. 3). A notable enhancement in Octopi-1.5 is its explicit design to handle objects with **multiple distinct parts**, allowing for more granular tactile descriptions (e.g., a hairbrush handle vs. its bristles).

#### 3.3. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)

A key experimental feature is the integration of a simple RAG module to enhance tactile descriptions. This module acts as a "memory" of previously seen items, allowing for more informative outputs (Fig. 4). The process involves:
1. **Embedding Computation:** For a new set of salient tactile images, their average embedding is computed using the tactile encoder.
2. **Similarity Search:** A cosine-similarity search is performed over an existing database of tactile embeddings to identify the top-5 most similar objects.
3. **Augmentation:** The labels and tactile descriptions of these similar items are aggregated, ranked by the number of retrieved samples, and then used to augment the new object's description.
This RAG scheme, while straightforward, demonstrably improved Octopi-1.5's performance in demonstration tasks.

#### 3.4. Tactile Manipulation Interface (TMI)

For the live demonstration, the authors developed the TMI, a modified Universal Manipulation Interface (UMI). This handheld gripper is designed for portability and ease of use, eliminating the need for a full robot setup.
- **Sensors:** The TMI fingers are equipped with two types of tactile sensors:
 - **GelSight Mini Sensor:** Provides high-resolution tactile imaging, used as the primary input for Octopi-1.5 due to extensive training data.
 - **TAC-02 Piezoresistive Sensor:** Offers direct pressure readings with a higher sampling rate and smaller form factor, providing a proof-of-concept for integrating heterogeneous tactile modalities, though not primarily used for model inference in this demonstration.
- **Setup:** The TMI connects to a laptop, which interfaces with Octopi-1.5 running on a remote workstation/server. A GoPro camera can provide visual input when needed. This portable setup can be assembled in under 15 minutes (Fig. 6).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper presents preliminary experimental results to validate the feasibility and performance of Octopi-1.5, particularly in the context of the planned interactive demonstrations. The key findings are:

- **Improved Performance over Predecessor:** Octopi-1.5 consistently shows higher overall accuracy compared to the older Octopi-1 model across different tasks and object categories. This validates the improvements in tactile encoder training (expanded datasets) and the use of the Qwen2-VL 7B backbone.

- **Effectiveness of RAG Module:** The integration of the simple RAG scheme significantly boosts Octopi-1.5's performance.
 - **Guessing Game:** In the "Guessing Game" (identifying objects based solely on tactile input), Octopi-1.5 with RAG enabled achieved remarkable accuracy:
 - **Seen Objects (Balls, Fruits):** 96.00% for balls and 100.00% for fruits. This is a substantial improvement over Octopi-1 (44-48% for balls, 34-42% for fruits) and even better than the Encoder-1.5 baseline (80-100% on seen objects, which relies on cosine similarity in the training set).
 - **Unseen Objects:** 73.17% accuracy for entirely unseen objects. When "teaching" was enabled (adding new item samples to the database on the fly), accuracy for unseen objects further rose to 95.12%, demonstrating the RAG module's potential for immediate adaptation. This highlights a critical advantage over encoder-only baselines which cannot generalize to unseen items without database updates.

- **Tactile Property Sorting Capabilities:** Octopi-1.5 demonstrates a strong ability to sort objects based on their tactile properties, specifically hardness.
 - **Seen Objects:** It achieved 100% accuracy for sorting balls and 93.18% for sorting fruits by hardness.
 - **Unseen Objects:** However, performance significantly dropped for unseen objects, achieving only 43.33% accuracy in sorting tasks. This indicates a limitation in generalizing tactile property rankings to entirely novel items, suggesting challenges in annotation or the relative nature of language-described properties.

- **Multi-Part Object Recognition:** While not explicitly quantified in the results tables, the paper states that Octopi-1.5 is "explicitly designed to handle objects comprising multiple parts" and the demonstration plans to "show Octopi-1.5’s ability to recognize multiple parts of the same object." This is a qualitative improvement over previous models, allowing for more detailed and localized tactile understanding.

- **Feasibility of Portable Demonstration:** The successful preliminary tests confirm the viability of showcasing Octopi-1.5's capabilities using the portable Tactile Manipulation Interface (TMI) and a remote workstation setup, making it accessible for live interaction without requiring a full robotic system.

In summary, the main findings highlight that Octopi-1.5 represents a significant step forward in VTLMs, particularly due to its improved tactile encoder and the effective integration of RAG, leading to superior performance on object identification and property description tasks for both seen and (to a lesser extent) unseen objects. The limitations, particularly in sorting unseen objects and generalizing roughness, are also openly acknowledged, providing avenues for future research.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Octopi-1.5 project holds significant implications for the fields of robotics, artificial intelligence, and human-robot interaction. Its potential impact spans several crucial areas:

- **Advancing Multimodal AI for Robotics:** Octopi-1.5 directly contributes to bridging the long-standing gap between high-level AI reasoning (enabled by LLMs) and low-level physical interaction (enabled by tactile sensing). By effectively integrating touch with vision and language, it moves towards a more comprehensive and embodied understanding for AI systems. This is crucial for robots that need to operate in unstructured, real-world environments where objects have diverse physical properties and visual information alone might be insufficient or ambiguous (e.g., in occluded spaces).

- **Enhanced Robotic Manipulation and Dexterity:** The ability of Octopi-1.5 to infer object properties like hardness and texture, and to respond to natural language queries about handling, is vital for developing more dexterous and adaptable robots. This could lead to robots that can safely grasp delicate items, sort objects by material properties, or perform assembly tasks requiring fine-grained tactile feedback, moving beyond pre-programmed motions to truly intelligent manipulation.

- **Foundation for Adaptive and On-the-Fly Learning Systems:** The integration of the RAG module is a particularly impactful contribution. It demonstrates a pathway for VTLMs to learn and adapt to new objects and scenarios without extensive retraining. This "on-the-fly" learning capability is critical for robots operating in dynamic environments where they encounter novel items. It also suggests a more memory-efficient approach to knowledge acquisition compared to purely increasing model size.

- **Improved Human-Robot Collaboration:** The natural language interface and the ability to describe tactile sensations make human-robot interaction more intuitive. Users can verbally query the robot about an object's properties or instruct it based on tactile feedback, fostering more effective collaboration. This moves towards robots that can not only "see" and "hear" but also "feel" and communicate their tactile experiences, making them more natural partners.

- **Accessible Research and Development:** The design of the Tactile Manipulation Interface (TMI) as a portable, accessible demonstration platform is highly significant. By allowing researchers and enthusiasts to interact with a VTLM without a complex robot setup, it lowers the barrier to entry for experimentation and data collection. The open-sourcing of code and design files (GitHub link provided) further amplifies this impact, encouraging wider adoption, experimentation, and collaborative development within the research community. This can accelerate progress in tactile sensing, data synthesis, and VTLM architectures.

- **Inspiration for Future Embodied AI:** By openly discussing both the successes and current limitations (e.g., generalization for unseen object sorting, limitations of text-only RAG, lack of direct manipulation outputs), the paper serves as a valuable roadmap for future research. It explicitly points towards the development of Vision-Tactile-Language-Action (VTLA) models that can directly output robot actions, representing the ultimate goal of truly intelligent, embodied agents.

In essence, Octopi-1.5 is more than just an improved model; it is a significant step towards enabling robots to understand and interact with the physical world through touch, much like humans do. Its contributions pave the way for more capable, adaptable, and intuitive robotic systems in a wide array of applications.
