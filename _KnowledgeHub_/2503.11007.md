# From Abstraction to Reality: DARPA's Vision for Robust Sim-to-Real Autonomy

> [!summary] Summary
> DARPA's TIAMAT program outlines a vision for achieving "adaptive expertise" in autonomous systems, focusing on abstract-to-real transfer by leveraging diverse low-fidelity simulations and semantic anchors to enable rapid and robust deployment in unpredictable real-world environments.

## Problem

- Autonomous systems consistently face a "sim-to-real gap," where performance trained in simulations does not reliably transfer to complex, dynamic, and unforeseen real-world conditions.
- Current transfer methods often assume a small sim-to-real gap or require prohibitively long training times with high-fidelity simulations, limiting broad adaptation and real-time deployment.
- The sim-to-real gap impacts all core components of an autonomy stack—perception, planning, and control—necessitating a holistic approach rather than isolated improvements.

## Method

- The program proposes "abstract-to-real transfer," utilizing knowledge from a variety of low-fidelity simulations and abstract models to perform real-world tasks without extensive prior physical testing.
- It introduces "semantic anchors" (e.g., logic, natural language, scene graphs) as shared conceptual understandings across diverse environments to ground representations and enable robust transfer despite visual or physical differences.
- An iterative refinement cycle feeds insights and data from real-world experiences back into abstract models and simulations, continuously enhancing system adaptability and transfer effectiveness.

## Results

- The TIAMAT program includes two challenge competitions: the Abstract Planning and Semantic Understanding (APSU) Challenge for quadruped robots (sim-to-sim and sim-to-real phases) and the Indy Autonomous Challenge for high-speed autonomous driving.
- Participating teams are exploring a range of advanced methods, including neuro-symbolic transfer, large multi-modal models, self-supervised reasoning, and robust reinforcement learning, to achieve adaptation across perception, dynamics, and mission categories.
- Evaluation metrics for challenges include semantic similarity, runtime, adaptation time, inference time, semantic accuracy, semantic localization, and localization accuracy, emphasizing rapid and accurate transfer.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Broader training diversity from varied low-fidelity simulations, connected by shared semantics, cultivates more flexible and robust autonomous systems than reliance on high-fidelity simulations alone.
> - Semantic anchors provide a powerful mechanism to simplify complex autonomy tasks into manageable reference points, reducing data requirements for learning and enabling effective generalization across large sim-to-real gaps.
> - An effective feedback loop that refines abstract models and simulations based on real-world agent experience is critical for continuous improvement and maintaining robustness in dynamic environments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work described in this paper is positioned within the broader field of autonomous systems, specifically addressing the persistent challenge known as the "sim-to-real gap." This gap refers to the disparity between the performance of autonomous systems in simulated environments and their performance in real-world conditions. While simulations are critical for training and testing, they often fail to fully replicate the dynamic, complex, and unpredictable nature of real-world environments, including factors such as environmental variability, sensory noise, and intricate physical dynamics.

Current research efforts to bridge this gap primarily focus on two approaches: developing increasingly sophisticated, high-fidelity simulations or creating algorithms that can learn effectively from lower-fidelity simulations. Existing algorithmic methods, such as domain randomization, domain adaptation, imitation learning, meta-learning, policy distillation, and dynamic optimization, often operate under the assumption of a relatively small sim-to-real gap. However, the paper argues that even advanced simulations do not always support this assumption, and the time required for learning autonomy in high-fidelity simulations can be prohibitive for time-sensitive applications.

The TIAMAT program deviates from approaches that rely solely on high-fidelity simulations. Instead, it proposes a strategy that emphasizes leveraging a diversity of lower-fidelity simulations and abstract models to achieve robust and rapid transfer of autonomy to the real world. This contrasts with efforts primarily focused on narrowing the sim-to-real gap through more realistic simulations, by aiming to develop systems capable of adapting to a potentially large initial gap. The program acknowledges that the sim-to-real gap affects all core components of an autonomy stack: perception (interpreting sensory data), planning (determining actions), and control (executing actions), and posits that addressing these interdependencies requires a holistic approach.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The fundamental objective of the DARPA TIAMAT program is to enable autonomous systems to develop "adaptive expertise." This concept is analogous to human cognitive abilities, such as a firefighter's capacity to apply learned knowledge and strategies from controlled training to unforeseen, complex, and dynamic real-world emergency scenarios. Specifically, TIAMAT aims for autonomous systems to quickly formulate and execute effective plans based on minimal prior knowledge and to generalize strategies learned in simpler or more controlled environments to unpredictable real-world situations with limited adaptation time.

The motivation for this program stems from the limitations of current sim-to-real transfer methods. While existing techniques have demonstrated performance in specific tasks, they often struggle with broad adaptation in unforeseen, complex, and dynamic real-world scenarios due to the inherent simplifications and approximations in simulations. The paper highlights that improving simulation fidelity alone is insufficient to address this challenge, as even advanced simulations cannot capture the entire spectrum of real-world conditions. Furthermore, the time required to develop autonomy using high-fidelity simulations is often impractical for time-sensitive operational contexts.

The TIAMAT program seeks to address these issues by focusing on rapid and robust transfer of autonomy technologies across diverse and complex environments, goals, and platforms. The term "rapid" implies the ability of autonomous systems to operate effectively in new environments with limited time for adaptation to novel dynamics, semantics, and contexts. The program’s goal is to ensure consistent operational capability even as the transfer gap between simulated and real-world environments widens, thus minimizing the delay between technological development and real-world deployment. This vision is predicated on the idea that autonomous systems should be resilient to operational differences and capable of broad generalization.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The TIAMAT program proposes a distinct methodology to address the sim-to-real gap, diverging from conventional approaches that prioritize increasingly high-fidelity simulations. Its core technical hypothesis posits that broader training diversity and experience lead to more flexible and robust autonomous systems. This is achieved by learning from a variety of low-fidelity simulations, in conjunction with limited high-fidelity simulations, all connected by shared semantics. This approach draws inspiration from the success of foundational models and large language models, which leverage diverse training data to achieve broad generalization.

**Abstract-to-Real Transfer:** The central concept is "abstract-to-real transfer," defined as the process where an agent utilizes knowledge, concepts, or models acquired from abstract simulations to perform tasks in the real world without requiring explicit prior real-world testing. This method is particularly relevant when the sim-to-real gap is substantial. The program assumes that if the underlying abstraction is sufficiently broad, the system will be capable of rapid adaptation to novel, unseen environments, platforms, and tasks.

**Semantic Anchors:** A key insight driving TIAMAT is the recognition that diverse environments can share underlying semantics despite visual or physical differences. "Semantic anchors" are abstractions that capture these shared meanings across different environments, providing a common conceptual understanding. Examples include logic, natural language, traffic laws, 3D scene graphs, and deterministic finite automata. These anchors can be user-defined, inferred from agent observations, or generated based on learned models. By grounding representations in these semantic anchors, the program aims to create robust and transferable autonomous systems that can operate effectively even with significant sim-to-real gaps. This approach is expected to reduce the data required for learning and transfer by simplifying complex autonomy tasks into manageable reference points within anchored representations.

**Refinement Process:** The methodology includes an iterative refinement cycle. This involves taking insights and data gained from real-world experiences and applying them back to enhance the abstract models or simulations. This bidirectional feedback loop ensures that theoretical models evolve in response to actual performance and real-world conditions, contributing to the continuous improvement and adaptability of the systems.

**Technical Challenges:** The program outlines two primary technical challenges:
1. **Autonomy transfer under restricted time budgets:** Ensuring robust transfer despite sim-to-sim and sim-to-real gaps in agent observations, actions, transitions, and goals, particularly when limited time is available for adapting to new situations and environments.
2. **Refining models and simulations based on agent experience:** Establishing an effective feedback loop between autonomy transfer mechanisms and abstraction refinement to enhance the robustness and effectiveness of the transfer process.

To address these challenges, the program encourages diverse technical approaches, including neuro-symbolic transfer with semantic anchors, robust reinforcement learning, and physics-informed neural networks.

**Current Approaches to Rapid and Robust Autonomy:** The paper also contextualizes TIAMAT's approach within broader trends in autonomy, noting the integration of symbolic reasoning (rule-based), data-driven strategies (machine learning), and generative methods (e.g., Large Language Models (LLMs) and Vision-Language Models (VLMs)). These methodologies are applied across the entire autonomy stack, encompassing perception (e.g., CLIP for object interpretation), planning (e.g., symbolic logic for strategic decisions), and control (e.g., adaptive and robust reinforcement learning).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

This paper describes a program vision rather than presenting empirical findings from executed research. Consequently, this section outlines the program's structure for fostering research, its technical challenges, and the diverse approaches being pursued by participating research teams.

The TIAMAT program is structured around two challenge competitions designed to evaluate the effectiveness of proposed solutions in addressing the abstract-to-real problem:

1. **Abstract Planning and Semantic Understanding (APSU) Challenge:** This competition focuses on semantic understanding and the transfer of agents from simple abstractions and indoor simulation environments to outdoor simulations and eventually to physical hardware. It assesses a robot's ability to understand and act within complex, previously unseen real-world settings, adapting to changes in scene appearance, terrain variability, control dynamics, and reasoning about the future impacts of planning actions.
 - **Phase 1 (Sim-to-Sim):** Participants will be tested in various basic simulators (MiniGrid, Mujoco, Habitat, IsaacLab). They will be given unseen indoor and outdoor environments and one hour to adapt their systems before receiving a new prompt for grading.
 - **Phase 2 (Sim-to-Real):** This phase focuses on real-world performance using a Unitree Go2 Quadruped robot. Teams will have eight hours of practice time in the real terrain before the final challenge, with metrics evaluating the performance difference between simulated abstractions and the real-world system.

2. **Indy Autonomous Challenge:** This component involves autonomous racing, inspired by Formula 1, to test and refine advanced autonomous driving technologies in high-speed, dynamic, and high-stakes scenarios, focusing on precise control and planning transfer.

Both competitions incorporate an adaptation period, allowing teams to refine their prototypes within a fixed timeframe before scoring. This emphasizes the development of flexible systems capable of refining performance through limited experience, rather than switching between multiple pre-configured solutions.

The required adaptation capabilities are categorized into three areas:
- **Perception and Semantics:** Transfer across different environmental semantics (e.g., training in a desert and deploying in a forest) and visual/linguistic representations.
- **Dynamics and Terrain:** Adaptation to various terrains and physical interactions (e.g., grass, sand, mud for legged robots) or unexpected system dynamics (e.g., tire friction coefficients for racing).
- **Missions and Tasks:** Reasoning from high-level mission descriptions, extracting crucial details, and executing appropriate actions (e.g., searching a disaster scene for specific survivors).

**Evaluation & Metrics:** For the APSU challenge, planned metrics include:
- **Semantic Similarity:** Measuring the semantic distance between objects, regions, or concepts.
- **Runtime:** Total execution time of a submission.
- **Adaptation Time:** Time utilized by a team to fine-tune its prototype for the challenge environment.
- **Inference Time:** Time taken by a prototype to compute a single output action.
- **Semantic Accuracy:** Evaluating the match between expected and identified objects/concepts based on semantic similarity.
- **Semantic Localization:** Assessing the accuracy of abstract location inference.
- **Localization Accuracy:** Measuring the physical distance between reported and actual object locations.

**Performer Approaches:** The program involves 14 teams from universities and industry partners, exploring two complementary directions:
- **Autonomy Transfer Methods:** Enhancing systems' ability to learn, adapt, and generalize knowledge across diverse tasks and environments.
- **Abstraction Refinement Methods:** Improving the efficiency of learning and problem-solving by refining and adapting internal model representations.

Performers are leveraging a diverse array of advanced methods, including neuro-symbolic transfer, large multi-modal models, self-supervised reasoning frameworks, generative bisimulations, game-theoretic embeddings, probabilistic programming toolkits, distributionally robust optimization, meta-learning, scene graphs, low-rank adaptation fine-tuning, geometric and topological perspectives, compositional neuro-symbolic learning, Bayesian inference, and chance-constrained signal temporal logic. Examples include Florida International University's neuro-symbolic approach with diffusion models and probabilistic hybrid automata, the University at Buffalo's self-supervised neural-symbolic reasoning with causal graphs, Oregon State University's bisimulation approach, and GE Research's framework based on foundation models and geodesic simulation continua. Most teams are addressing all three adaptation categories (perception and semantics, missions and tasks, dynamics and terrain), with some exhibiting a stronger focus on specific areas.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The DARPA TIAMAT program holds potential significance for the advancement of autonomous systems and artificial intelligence. By emphasizing abstract-to-real transfer and the utilization of diverse low-fidelity simulations connected by semantic anchors, the program seeks to address a fundamental limitation of current autonomy development: the difficulty of transferring capabilities from simulated environments to unpredictable real-world scenarios in a robust and timely manner.

The potential impact includes:
- **Enhanced Autonomy Robustness and Adaptability:** The program's success could lead to autonomous systems that are more resilient to unforeseen environmental variations, sensor noise, and complex dynamics, enabling them to operate reliably in a wider range of real-world contexts than currently possible.
- **Accelerated Development-to-Deployment Cycle:** By aiming for rapid adaptation with limited real-world experience, TIAMAT could significantly reduce the time and resources required to field autonomous technologies, making them more practical for time-sensitive applications across various domains.
- **Advancements in Generalization and Adaptive Expertise:** The focus on enabling autonomous systems to develop "adaptive expertise" could contribute to a deeper understanding of how AI can generalize knowledge and strategies effectively from abstract or simpler contexts to novel, complex situations, thereby pushing the boundaries of AI capabilities.
- **Reduced Reliance on High-Fidelity Simulations:** By demonstrating that broad generalization can be achieved through diverse low-fidelity simulations and semantic abstractions, the program could offer a more efficient and scalable alternative to the costly and time-consuming development of high-fidelity simulators.
- **Broadening Application Domains:** Improved sim-to-real transfer capabilities could expand the practical applicability of autonomous systems in critical areas such as defense, disaster response, exploration, logistics, and highly dynamic environments like autonomous racing, where rapid decision-making and adaptation are paramount.
- **Integration of Neuro-Symbolic AI:** The program's encouragement of neuro-symbolic approaches, combining data-driven learning with symbolic reasoning and semantic anchors, could foster novel architectures that offer both the flexibility of neural networks and the interpretability and robustness of symbolic systems.

Overall, TIAMAT aims to cultivate a new generation of autonomous systems capable of rapid, flexible, and robust operation in real-world conditions, fundamentally altering the paradigm for their development and deployment.
