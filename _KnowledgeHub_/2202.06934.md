# Slicing Aided Hyper Inference and Fine-tuning for Small Object Detection

> [!summary] Summary
> The SAHI (Slicing Aided Hyper Inference) framework, developed by researchers at OBSS AI and Middle East Technical University, improves small object detection by processing high-resolution images in overlapping slices at inference time and optionally during fine-tuning. This generic, detector-agnostic method boosted Mean Average Precision (AP) by up to 14.5% on challenging datasets like VisDrone and xView.

## Problem

- Conventional deep learning object detectors struggle with 'small objects' (objects with few pixels) in high-resolution images due to a lack of distinguishable features.
- High-resolution imaging devices (e.g., 4K cameras, drones) exacerbate the small object problem and lead to significant memory demands for processing.
- Existing solutions are often detector-specific, requiring costly architectural modifications or extensive retraining, limiting their broad applicability.

## Method

- **SAHI** introduces ==Slicing Aided Hyper Inference== where high-resolution input images are divided into ==overlapping patches==, resized, and processed independently by any base object detector, with bounding boxes merged via ==Non-Maximum Suppression==.
- An optional ==Slicing Aided Fine-tuning (SF)== strategy augments the training data by extracting and resizing overlapping patches from original images, training the detector on both patches and full images.
- The framework is designed to be generic and ==detector-agnostic==, acting as a plug-and-play wrapper for existing object detection models (e.g., FCOS, VFNet, TOOD, YOLOv5) without architectural modifications.

## Results

- Applying SAHI (inference only) increased overall AP on the VisDrone dataset by **6.8%** for FCOS, **5.1%** for VFNet, and **5.3%** for TOOD, demonstrating immediate utility.
- Combining SAHI with Slicing Aided Fine-tuning (SF) resulted in cumulative AP increases up to **14.5%** (for TOOD on VisDrone), highlighting the synergy of the combined approach.
- For the extremely challenging xView dataset, FCOS with SF+SAHI+PO achieved an **AP50 of 17.1%**, a substantial improvement over the **2.2%** baseline, making models viable for such tiny objects.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Dividing large images into smaller, overlapping slices makes small objects appear relatively larger to the detector, providing more pixel information and significantly improving detection performance without altering the base model architecture.
> - Combining inference-time slicing with a fine-tuning approach that also uses sliced and resized training data yields the most substantial performance gains for small object detection.
> - Including full-image inference alongside sliced patch inference is crucial for robustly detecting larger objects that might span multiple small patches or be better represented in their original context.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of object detection has seen transformative advancements with the advent of deep learning, yielding highly accurate models such as Faster R-CNN, RetinaNet, YOLO, and their numerous variants. These detectors have achieved remarkable success on well-established datasets like ImageNet, Pascal VOC, and MS COCO, which primarily feature images with relatively large objects covering a substantial portion of the image area (e.g., 60% of image height on average).

However, the proliferation of high-resolution imaging devices, such as 4K cameras and drones, has exposed a significant limitation of these conventional detectors: their poor performance in detecting "small objects" or "objects far away in the scene." These objects, often represented by only a few pixels, lack sufficient visual detail for standard deep learning models to reliably identify. This challenge is particularly acute in critical applications like video surveillance, drone-based monitoring, and satellite imagery analysis, where detecting minute details (e.g., a person in a vast aerial shot) is paramount. The DORI (Detection, Observation, Recognition and Identification) criteria explicitly define minimum pixel heights for objects at different recognition levels, underscoring the practical importance of this problem.

Existing approaches to small object detection generally fall into several categories:
- **Architectural Modifications:** Some methods propose entirely new network architectures or heavy modifications to existing ones (e.g., using particle swarm optimization, integrating super-resolution, or employing generative adversarial networks). While potentially effective, these often require extensive pretraining from scratch, are costly, and are not easily adaptable to different base detectors.
- **Data Augmentation:** Techniques like oversampling images with small objects or augmenting them by copying small objects aim to provide more training data. However, these can be computationally intensive and might require additional annotations (e.g., segmentation masks) not typically available in standard object detection datasets.
- **Contextual or Attention Mechanisms:** Other methods try to learn richer features for small objects by enlarging specific areas or using early visual attention to focus on promising regions. These can introduce computational burdens for area selection.
- **Prior Slicing Techniques:** Some prior work has explored slicing-based techniques. However, the paper notes that these implementations were often not generic, applicable only to specific object detectors, thereby limiting their broad utility.

The work presented by Akyon, Altinuc, and Temizel directly addresses these limitations by introducing SAHI. It distinguishes itself by offering a *generic*, *detector-agnostic* solution that can be applied as a wrapper on *any existing object detector*. This "plug-and-play" capability, which requires no architectural modifications or retraining from scratch for the base detector, significantly lowers the barrier to improving small object detection performance. By focusing on both inference-time and fine-tuning strategies that leverage image slicing, SAHI offers a practical, scalable, and highly adaptable framework, carving a unique and valuable niche in the crowded object detection research landscape.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind this research stems from the critical need to accurately detect small objects in high-resolution imagery, a persistent challenge in applications such as surveillance, drone monitoring, and remote sensing. Conventional deep learning-based object detectors, while powerful, inherently struggle with these "tiny" objects because they are represented by a minimal number of pixels, leading to a severe lack of distinguishable features. Additionally, processing high-resolution images with these detectors often incurs significant memory demands, further complicating deployment.

Given these challenges, the key objectives of the paper are:

1. **Enhance Small Object Detection Accuracy:** To develop a method that significantly improves the detection performance (specifically, Mean Average Precision, AP) for small objects, which are often overlooked or poorly identified by standard detectors.
2. **Ensure Generality and Detector Agnosticism:** To propose a solution that is generic and compatible with *any* existing object detection model, regardless of its architecture (e.g., single-stage, two-stage, anchor-based, anchor-free). This objective aims to avoid the need for costly and time-consuming modifications to detector architectures or retraining from scratch.
3. **Provide Flexible Deployment Options:** To offer both an inference-only method (requiring no retraining of the base model) for immediate deployment and a fine-tuning pipeline for further performance gains when training data is available.
4. **Optimize Resource Utilization:** To handle high-resolution images effectively while maintaining reasonable memory footprints, allowing for practical deployment on various hardware platforms.
5. **Facilitate Community Adoption:** To release the proposed framework as open-source, thereby promoting wider adoption, reproducibility, and further research and development within the computer vision community.

In essence, the authors aim to bridge the gap between high-performance general object detectors and the specialized requirements of small object detection, making these advanced capabilities accessible and practical for a broader range of real-world applications.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the proposed solution, Slicing Aided Hyper Inference (SAHI), is a generic framework that leverages image slicing during both the fine-tuning (optional) and inference stages. The fundamental idea is to divide a high-resolution input image into multiple overlapping smaller patches. This process effectively makes small objects within these patches appear *relatively larger* to the object detector, providing more pixels and contextual information for improved detection.

The methodology consists of two main components:

1. **Slicing Aided Fine-tuning (SF):**
 - **Augmentation Strategy:** This component addresses the issue that models pretrained on datasets like MS COCO (which contain mostly large objects) perform poorly on small objects. To overcome this, the fine-tuning dataset is augmented by extracting overlapping patches from the original images.
 - **Patch Generation and Resizing:** Each image is sliced into patches of predefined (and hyper-parameterized) dimensions. These patches are then resized while preserving their aspect ratio, such that their width (or height) falls within a specified range (e.g., 800 to 1333 pixels). This resizing makes the objects within the patches appear larger in terms of pixel count relative to the patch size, and thus, more amenable to detection by the underlying network.
 - **Combined Training:** The fine-tuning process utilizes both these augmented patches *and* the original full images. Including original images is crucial to ensure that the detector does not lose its ability to detect large objects, which might be too big to fit entirely within a single small patch or might be less optimally detected from partial views.
 - **Detector Agnostic:** Importantly, the SF method modifies the training *data* rather than the *detector architecture*, allowing it to be used with any existing object detector.

2. **Slicing Aided Hyper Inference (SAHI):**
 - **Patch Generation at Inference:** During inference, the input image is divided into a configurable number of overlapping patches. The patch size and overlap percentage are hyperparameters.
 - **Independent Inference on Patches:** Each generated patch is resized (maintaining aspect ratio) and then fed independently into the object detection model. This is where the "hyper inference" comes in – multiple inferences are performed on different parts of the image.
 - **Optional Full Inference (FI):** To ensure comprehensive detection, particularly for large objects that might span multiple patches or be better detected in their original context, an optional step involves running the detector on the full, original image as well.
 - **Result Merging and Post-processing:** The bounding box predictions from all individual patches (and optionally from the full image) are converted back to the original image's coordinate system. A crucial step then involves Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS) to merge overlapping detections and remove redundant or low-confidence predictions. NMS uses both Intersection over Union (IoU) and detection probability thresholds to refine the final set of bounding boxes.
 - **Memory Efficiency:** By processing smaller patches, the method keeps the memory requirements for each forward pass fixed, regardless of the original image's resolution, although the total computation time increases linearly with the number of patches.

**Implementation Details:**
The authors integrated SAHI with prominent object detection frameworks, namely Detectron2, MMDetection, and YOLOv5, demonstrating its versatility and ease of integration. This confirms its generic nature and practicality for researchers and practitioners alike. The code and benchmarks are publicly available on GitHub.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The authors conducted extensive experimental evaluations using various state-of-the-art object detectors and challenging small object detection datasets to demonstrate the effectiveness of SAHI.

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Base Detectors:** FCOS (Fully Convolutional One-Stage Object Detection), VarifocalNet (VFNet), and TOOD (Task-aligned One-stage Object Detection) were chosen for their strong performance and representation of modern anchor-free detection methods. These were implemented within the MMDetection framework.
- **Datasets:**
 - **VisDrone2019-Detection:** A dataset comprising 8599 drone-captured images, characterized by small, densely distributed, and often occluded objects (pedestrians, cars, etc.). It features diverse conditions including illumination and perspective changes.
 - **xView:** One of the largest publicly available datasets for object detection from satellite imagery, featuring over 1 million instances across 60 classes. Objects in xView are typically very small, presenting a significant detection challenge.
- **Evaluation Metric:** Mean Average Precision at an IoU threshold of 0.5 (AP50) was used, along with AP50 for small (AP50s), medium (AP50m), and large (AP50l) objects, following the MS COCO evaluation protocol.
- **Training Details:** Standard SGD optimizer with learning rate scheduling and linear warmup was employed. Patch sizes for fine-tuning were selected within specific ranges for each dataset, and inference patches were fixed.

**Key Findings:**

1. **Significant AP Improvement with SAHI (Inference Only):**
 - Even without any fine-tuning of the base detectors, applying only the Slicing Aided Hyper Inference (SAHI) scheme yielded substantial improvements in AP. For VisDrone, SAHI increased object detection AP by **6.8%** for FCOS, **5.1%** for VFNet, and **5.3%** for TOOD. This demonstrates SAHI's immediate utility as a plug-and-play inference enhancement.

2. **Further Gains with Slicing Aided Fine-tuning (SF):**
 - When SAHI was combined with Slicing Aided Fine-tuning (SF), the performance gains were even more pronounced. On VisDrone, the cumulative AP increase reached **12.7%** for FCOS, **13.4%** for VFNet, and a remarkable **14.5%** for TOOD. This highlights the synergy between augmenting the training data with sliced patches and applying sliced inference.

3. **Impact of Patch Overlap (PO) and Full Image Inference (FI):**
 - Applying a 25% overlap between patches during sliced inference (PO) consistently improved overall AP and specifically small/medium object AP. While it sometimes slightly decreased large object AP (attributed to potential false positives from slices matching large ground truth boxes), the net effect was positive.
 - Integrating Full Image (FI) inference (processing the original image alongside patches) was found to be crucial for robust detection of larger objects. The best overall detection AP often included FI, balancing the specialized detection of small objects from patches with the general detection of larger objects from the full image.

4. **Exceptional Performance on Extremely Small Object Datasets (xView):**
 - For the xView dataset, where targets are typically extremely small, standard training (FI baseline) resulted in very poor detection performance (e.g., FCOS AP50 of 2.2%). SF and SAHI drastically improved these results. For instance, FCOS with SF+SAHI+PO achieved an AP50 of **17.1%**, a substantial increase from the baseline. This demonstrates SF's critical role in making models viable for such challenging datasets.

5. **Error Analysis:**
 - Error analysis curves (for TOOD on VisDrone and xView) indicated that while SAHI+SF improved performance, there remained significant room for improvement, particularly in reducing false positives and false negatives. Localization errors and supercategory/category confusions were less dominant error sources, suggesting the primary challenge shifted to correctly identifying object presence rather than precise localization or classification given a detection.

6. **Computational Considerations:**
 - The proposed approach increases computational time linearly with the number of slices but maintains fixed memory requirements per slice. This allows for a trade-off between speed and memory based on the target platform and application needs.

In summary, the results conclusively demonstrate that SAHI provides a highly effective and versatile solution for boosting small object detection performance across a range of modern detectors and challenging datasets, offering both an immediate inference-time boost and further gains through targeted fine-tuning.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presented by Akyon, Altinuc, and Temizel, through the SAHI framework, offers a highly significant contribution to the field of computer vision, particularly in addressing the persistent challenge of small object detection. Its impact can be assessed across several dimensions:

1. **Broad Applicability and Generality:** The most significant aspect of SAHI is its *generality*. Unlike many specialized solutions that require architectural modifications or custom training pipelines, SAHI is a detector-agnostic framework. It can be seamlessly integrated as a "plug-and-play" module on top of *any* existing object detection model (demonstrated with FCOS, VFNet, TOOD, and integrated with Detectron2, MMDetection, YOLOv5). This significantly reduces the barrier to entry for practitioners and researchers looking to improve small object detection, allowing them to leverage their preferred or pre-existing models without extensive re-engineering.

2. **Substantial Performance Gains:** The empirical results showcase remarkable improvements in Mean Average Precision (AP), with up to 6.8% AP increase from inference alone and a cumulative 14.5% AP increase with slicing-aided fine-tuning. These gains are particularly impactful for small objects, where baseline performance is often critically low. Such performance boosts are crucial for the reliability and effectiveness of real-world applications.

3. **Addressing Critical Application Needs:** The problem of small object detection is paramount in numerous high-stakes domains:
 - **Surveillance and Security:** Detecting distant individuals, vehicles, or anomalous objects in high-resolution camera feeds.
 - **Drone and Aerial Imagery:** Identifying targets in vast landscapes captured by drones, which are inherently small in pixel representation.
 - **Satellite Imagery Analysis:** Pinpointing specific structures, vehicles, or environmental features from orbital platforms.
 - **Autonomous Systems:** Enhancing perception for self-driving cars or robots, especially for far-away obstacles or pedestrians.
 - **Medical Imaging:** Potentially aiding in the detection of small anomalies or cells in high-resolution scans.
 By providing an effective solution, SAHI directly contributes to advancing these critical areas.

4. **Resource Efficiency and Flexibility:** The method's approach of processing images in patches effectively manages memory constraints inherent in high-resolution imagery. While computation scales linearly with the number of patches, the fixed memory per slice allows for flexible trade-offs, enabling deployment on diverse hardware platforms. This makes high-resolution object detection more feasible for systems with limited computational resources.

5. **Open-Source Contribution and Community Empowerment:** The decision to release SAHI as an open-source framework is a major driver of its potential impact. Public availability on GitHub encourages widespread adoption, facilitates benchmarking, fosters community contributions, and accelerates further research and development in small object detection. This commitment to reproducibility and collaboration elevates the work beyond a mere research paper into a practical tool for the entire computer vision ecosystem.

6. **Foundation for Future Research:** The paper also lays groundwork for future work, indicating plans to benchmark instance segmentation models and evaluate different post-processing techniques using the slicing approach. This positions SAHI not just as a final solution, but as a robust platform for ongoing innovation in fine-grained visual recognition tasks.

In conclusion, SAHI represents a significant advancement by offering a highly effective, generic, and open-source solution to a pervasive challenge in object detection. Its practical utility, coupled with its substantial performance improvements and broad applicability, positions it to have a lasting and widespread impact across numerous computer vision applications and research endeavors.
