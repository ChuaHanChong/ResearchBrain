# Bi-Level Motion Imitation for Humanoid Robots

> [!summary] Summary
> Bi-Level Motion Imitation (BMI) introduces a framework for humanoid robots to learn agile human-like movements from motion capture data by dynamically adapting reference motions for physical consistency. It employs a Self-Consistent Auto-Encoder (SCAE) to generate robust motion representations, leading to improved robot policy performance on challenging tasks like kicking and jumping with enhanced stability.

## Problem

- Human motion capture (MoCap) data is often physically infeasible for direct imitation by humanoid robots due to morphological differences, joint limits, and force constraints.
- Existing imitation learning methods either suffer from unstable learning and sample inefficiency (GAIL) or are sensitive to physically inconsistent reference trajectories.
- Traditional physics-based motion retargeting is computationally complex, requires explicit robot dynamics models, and struggles with generalizability.

## Method

- A Bi-Level Motion Imitation (BMI) framework that alternately optimizes both the robot policy and the motion generation model.
- A Self-Consistent Auto-Encoder (SCAE) is developed to learn robust, sparse, and structured latent dynamics from kinematically retargeted MoCap data, utilizing a novel latent reconstruction regularization.
- An iterative fine-tuning process adjusts the SCAE decoder to generate physically consistent motions for the robot, which the policy then imitates, while maintaining fidelity to original motion patterns through latent space regularization.

## Results

- The SCAE model achieved significantly lower latent reconstruction error and learned sparser, more structured latent representations compared to the FLD baseline.
- BMI fine-tuning resulted in improved performance on challenging tasks, achieving longer kicking times and more stable standing in the kick motion, and enabling successful jumps.
- The framework successfully acquired complex motions like kick and jump where baselines struggled, demonstrating the ability to modify reference motions for physical consistency while preserving desired patterns.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Introducing a self-consistent regularization in the latent dynamics model (SCAE) yields more robust and structured motion representations that are less prone to noise.
> - Alternating optimization of the robot policy and the reference motion generator (bi-level fine-tuning) allows for effective adaptation of human MoCap data to robot's physical capabilities without an explicit dynamics model.
> - Maintaining latent space fidelity during motion adaptation ensures that modified reference motions retain the essential characteristics of the original human movements.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of sophisticated humanoid robot controllers often benefits from imitation learning approaches, particularly those that leverage human motion capture (MoCap) data. MoCap data offers rich, expert-generated reference trajectories that can guide robot policy learning, potentially reducing the extensive reward engineering typically required in reinforcement learning.

Current methodologies in motion imitation broadly fall into two categories. The first involves generative adversarial imitation learning (GAIL) methods, which aim to learn motion styles in a generative adversarial manner. While GAIL-based approaches, such as those used in animation, can facilitate tracking various human motions, they have been observed to suffer from unstable learning and sample inefficiency. Moreover, their reported successes often rely on simulated robots with high degrees of freedom, unlimited force capabilities, or external assistance, raising questions about their direct transferability to more realistic, underactuated humanoid robots with limited force and joint ranges.

The second category comprises methods that directly learn to track provided motion trajectories. A significant challenge in this approach is that human MoCap data, even after kinematic retargeting, can be noisy and physically infeasible for realistic humanoid robots. These inconsistencies arise from fundamental morphological differences, such as varying degrees of joint freedom, force limits, and overall embodiment. Integrating physically infeasible data into training datasets can lead to suboptimal robot behaviors or outright failure in imitation tasks.

The issue of physically inconsistent reference motions has also been addressed in the field of motion retargeting. Physics-based retargeting typically formulates the problem as a trajectory optimization task, incorporating known robot dynamics and constraints derived from reference trajectories. Methods like differential optimal control use bi-level optimization to optimize retargeting parameters and robot trajectories. However, these approaches are often computationally complex, require explicit modeling of robot dynamics, and necessitate specific domain knowledge, limiting their generalizability across different robot platforms.

This paper addresses the gap in learning-based methods that can guarantee physical consistency for humanoid robots without relying on an explicit dynamics model or human-specified constraints. It proposes a data-driven approach that integrates motion generation with policy learning, aiming to modify reference motions to be physically consistent while preserving the original human motion patterns. This positions the work as an advancement in making imitation learning from MoCap data more robust and applicable to realistic humanoid robots by actively adapting the reference data itself.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust imitation learning framework for humanoid robots that overcomes the limitations posed by physically infeasible human motion capture (MoCap) data. This objective is driven by several key motivations:

1. **Addressing Physical Infeasibility of MoCap Data:** Human MoCap data, while valuable for providing diverse and complex behaviors, is often not directly executable by humanoid robots. Differences in morphology, such as varying joint limits, motor strengths, and overall dynamics, mean that many human motions are physically impossible for a robot to replicate exactly. When such infeasible data is used directly as training references, it can hinder the robot's policy learning, leading to suboptimal performance, unstable behaviors, or outright failures. The authors explicitly state that "incorporating physically infeasible MoCap data in training datasets can adversely affect the performance of the robot policy." This necessitates a mechanism to adapt or modify the reference motions to be physically consistent with the robot's capabilities.

2. **Improving Learning Stability and Efficiency:** Existing imitation learning methods, particularly those based on Generative Adversarial Imitation Learning (GAIL), often suffer from unstable training processes and require large amounts of data (sample inefficiency). While direct trajectory tracking methods avoid some of these issues, they are susceptible to the problem of physically infeasible references. The motivation is to create a more stable and effective learning paradigm that leverages the richness of MoCap data without inheriting its inconsistencies or the instability of adversarial approaches.

3. **Developing a Learning-Based, Model-Agnostic Solution:** Traditional approaches to achieve physically consistent motions, such as physics-based motion retargeting, typically rely on complex trajectory optimization techniques that require an explicit model of the robot's dynamics and manually defined constraints. Such methods are often difficult to implement, require specialized domain knowledge, and struggle to generalize across different robot platforms. The authors identify an "emergent need for a learning-based method that does not rely on an explicit dynamics model while guaranteeing physical consistency at the same time." This work aims to fulfill this need by proposing a purely data-driven solution.

4. **Preserving Essential Motion Patterns:** As reference motions are modified for physical consistency, there is a risk of deviating significantly from the original human intent or characteristic motion patterns. A crucial motivation is to ensure that the adapted motions retain the essential stylistic and behavioral qualities of the original MoCap data. This requires a regularization mechanism that keeps the modified motions "close to the desired motion patterns" embedded in the original human data.

To achieve these objectives, the research proposes a bi-level optimization-based imitation learning framework. The core idea is to *alternately optimize both the robot policy and the target MoCap data* itself. This involves:
- Developing a novel generative latent dynamics model, the Self-Consistent Auto-Encoder (SCAE), to learn sparse and structured motion representations that capture essential patterns and are robust to noise. This model is intended to provide a stable basis for motion generation and regularization.
- Utilizing this latent dynamics model to generate and dynamically update reference motions, while employing latent space regularization to ensure these updated motions are physically consistent for the robot and maintain fidelity to the original human motion characteristics.
- Enabling the robot policy to continuously improve by imitating these adaptively generated, physically consistent reference motions.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed Bi-Level Motion Imitation (BMI) framework employs a three-stage training procedure to address the challenges of physically inconsistent reference motions in humanoid robot imitation learning.

#### 3.1. Stage 1: Self-Consistent Latent Dynamics Model Learning

This initial stage focuses on learning a robust generative latent dynamics model from kinematically retargeted human MoCap data. The model, termed Self-Consistent Auto-Encoder (SCAE), builds upon the Fourier Latent Dynamics (FLD) model but introduces a novel regularization technique.

- **Foundation in Fourier Latent Dynamics (FLD):** SCAE is inspired by FLD, which uses an auto-encoder to learn a structured motion representation. The encoder maps a trajectory segment ($ \tau_t $) into a latent space representation ($ z_t $). A differentiable Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) layer is then applied to $ z_t $ to extract frequency ($ f_t $), amplitude ($ a_t $), and offset ($ b_t $) parameters, while an additional fully connected (FC) layer determines the phase ($ \phi_t $). These latent parameters are used to reconstruct the latent embedding ($ \hat{z}_t $) via a sinusoidal function, which is then decoded into a reconstructed trajectory segment ($ \hat{\tau}_t $). FLD also employs a multi-step forward prediction mechanism, where subsequent latent phases are approximated to predict future trajectory segments, and a reconstruction loss ($ L_{N}^{FLD} $) is minimized over multiple steps.

- **SCAE Innovation: Latent Reconstruction Regularization:** The authors observe that in FLD, accurate motion reconstruction in the state space does not guarantee closeness in the latent space. This can lead to overfitting to current data, making the latent representation less robust to noise. SCAE addresses this by introducing a "self-consistent" regularization. After decoding the latent embedding into a reconstructed trajectory ($ \hat{\tau}_t $), this *reconstructed trajectory* is fed back into the *same encoder* to obtain a "re-encoded" latent representation ($ \hat{\bar{z}}_t $). The SCAE training loss ($ L_{N}^{SCAE} $) then includes an additional term: a latent reconstruction error ($ \beta|\hat{\bar{z}}'_{t+i} - \hat{z}'_{t+i}|^2 $). This term penalizes discrepancies between the original latent embedding and the latent embedding derived from the reconstructed trajectory. This regularization enforces consistency between the learned latent representation and its decoded motion, encouraging the autoencoder to capture general motion patterns rather than specific nuances or noise that might be altered during subsequent fine-tuning for physical consistency.

#### 3.2. Stage 2: Policy Pre-Training

In this stage, a humanoid robot policy is trained to imitate reference motions generated by the learned SCAE model. This serves as an initial learning phase before the bi-level fine-tuning.

- **Reference Motion Generation:** Instead of directly using raw MoCap data, the policy is trained using motions sampled from the latent space of the pre-trained SCAE. For each episode, a set of latent variables ($ z_t $) is sampled from a buffer collected during SCAE training. These latent variables are transformed into Fourier parameters ($ \phi_t, f_t, a_t, b_t $). While the frequency, amplitude, and offset are maintained, an initial phase ($ \phi_0 $) is uniformly sampled, and subsequent phases are updated according to a learned latent dynamics model. These evolving latent variables are then decoded by SCAE's decoder into a target motion trajectory ($ \hat{\tau}_t $). The most recent state in this trajectory serves as the target state for the robot at the current timestep.

- **Policy Learning:** The robot policy is trained using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), a reinforcement learning algorithm. This process benefits from the SCAE-generated motions in two ways: (i) the latent space allows for interpolation and generation of new motion transitions, expanding the training data; and (ii) the learned latent representation from SCAE is concise, focusing on essential motion patterns rather than potentially noisy nuances, which facilitates policy learning.

#### 3.3. Stage 3: Bi-Level Fine-Tuning

This final stage is the core of the BMI framework, where the robot policy and the motion generation model (specifically, the decoder) are iteratively optimized to ensure physical consistency and further policy improvement.

- **Alternating Optimization:** The framework alternately optimizes the robot policy ($ \pi $) and the SCAE decoder ($ dec $). During this phase, the convolutional encoder ($ enc $) and initial FC/BN layers of SCAE are frozen, meaning the fundamental latent representation learning is fixed.

- **Decoder Update Objective:** The decoder is updated to generate motions that are physically consistent with the robot's capabilities. Its loss function consists of two main components:
 1. **Robot-Trajectory Matching:** A Mean Squared Error (MSE) term measures the difference between the *robot's actual trajectory* (which is physically consistent by its nature) and the *decoded trajectory* ($ |\hat{s}_t - s_t|^2 $). This term encourages the decoder to produce motions that the robot can successfully track, effectively adapting the reference motions to be physically consistent.
 2. **Latent Regularization:** A latent reconstruction error term ($ \beta|\hat{\bar{z}}_t - \hat{z}_t|^2 $) is reintroduced, similar to the SCAE training. This term ensures that the motions generated by the updated decoder remain close to the original motion patterns in the latent space. This prevents the decoder from degenerating into trivial motions (e.g., merely copying the robot's current state) that deviate too far from the desired human-like behaviors.

- **Policy Update Objective:** Concurrently, the robot policy is optimized to minimize the tracking error between its own trajectory and the updated, physically consistent reference motions generated by the fine-tuned decoder. This iterative process allows the policy to improve by imitating references that are increasingly tailored to its physical capabilities, leading to more robust and versatile skills.

#### 3.4. Implementation Details

The BMI framework is evaluated on a realistic model of the MIT Humanoid Robot within the Isaac Gym simulation environment. The dataset, originally from DeepMimic and used by FLD, is extended to include 13 different motions, encompassing challenging acrobatic behaviors like jump, kick, spin-kick, and cross-over. PPO is used for policy learning with a detailed reward function comprising tracking rewards (for linear/angular velocity, gravity, joint positions) and regularization rewards (for action rate, joint acceleration, joint torque). Domain randomization is applied during policy training.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents findings regarding both the learned latent dynamics model and the performance of the robot policy, comparing the proposed Self-Consistent Auto-Encoder (SCAE) and Bi-Level Motion Imitation (BMI) framework against the Fourier Latent Dynamics (FLD) baseline.

#### 4.1. Latent Dynamics Model Learning

- **Motion and Latent Reconstruction Accuracy:** The SCAE model demonstrates comparable accuracy to FLD in reconstructing original motion states (motion reconstruction error), as shown in Figure 3b. However, SCAE achieves a significantly lower latent reconstruction error (Figure 3a). This indicates that the explicit self-consistency constraint effectively improves the consistency between the learned latent representation and its decoded motion without compromising the fidelity of state-space motion reconstruction. An ablation study confirms that SCAE is robust across a range of values for the latent reconstruction coefficient $ \beta $.

- **Learned Latent Representations (Sparsity and Structure):**
 - **Sparsity:** Visualizations of learned latent amplitudes and phases (Figure 4, Figure 9) indicate that SCAE learns a sparser representation compared to FLD. SCAE utilizes fewer frequency components and lower amplitudes to reconstruct the same motions, with many channels having amplitudes near zero. This suggests that SCAE focuses on more essential motion patterns, reducing redundancy.
 - **Structure:** The latent manifolds induced by SCAE across 13 different motions exhibit more consistent and well-shaped structures than those from FLD (Figure 5). These consistent circular patterns, representing the primary period of individual motions, suggest that SCAE successfully captures the high-level, constant features of motions (e.g., velocity, frequency), providing a more robust and organized latent space.

#### 4.2. Performance of Policy Learning

The robot policy performance is assessed through quantitative metrics on challenging motions and qualitative observations across various tasks.

- **Improved Performance on Challenging Motions:**
 - **Kick Task:** BMI fine-tuning achieves the longest kicking time and the most stable standing during the kick motion (Table 1, Figure 6b). BMI also results in a slightly higher kicking foot height compared to FLD and SCAE (Figure 6a).
 - **Jump Task:** Surprisingly, the SCAE pre-training stage (without further BMI fine-tuning) achieves the longest jumping time (Table 1). This is attributed to the sparser and more concise latent parameterizations provided by SCAE, which simplify policy learning by focusing on essential motion patterns rather than noisy nuances.
 - **Overall Improvement:** The experiments confirm that the proposed latent dynamics model (SCAE) improves policy learning during pre-training, and the bi-level fine-tuning framework (BMI) further enhances the robot policy by adapting reference motions to be physically consistent.

- **Qualitative Results and Motion Changes:**
 - **Diverse Motions:** Qualitative results presented in supplementary videos (accessible via the project website) illustrate BMI's improvement across diverse motions, including cross-over, stride, and step.
 - **Physical Consistency Manifestation:** Visualization of motion changes after bi-level fine-tuning reveals modifications that enhance physical consistency. For instance, the fine-tuned stride motion exhibits an increased arm swing, which is suggested to be more consistent with the robot's dynamics.
 - **General Learning:** BMI successfully acquires complex motions like kick and jump (Figure 12) where FLD struggled, demonstrating the ability to naturally lift the kicking foot while maintaining stability and achieving successful jumps with both feet leaving the ground.

- **Limitations in Highly Dynamic Motions:** Despite overall improvements, the policy still encounters difficulties with highly dynamic and balance-critical motions such as spin-kick and cross-over (Figure 13). In these cases, the robot prioritizes maintaining balance, which may result in not fully replicating the intricate motion patterns (e.g., incomplete leg crossing or limited kicking foot lift).

- **Additional Experimental Insights:**
 - **Robustness to Perturbations:** Zero-shot sim-to-sim experiments show that the learned policy maintains good performance even when the robot's mass is increased by 5kg, indicating robustness.
 - **Generative Capabilities:** The learned latent dynamics model can function as a generative model. By interpolating latent amplitude and frequency parameters, new kinematically consistent motions can be synthesized.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of humanoid robot control and imitation learning by proposing a novel framework that addresses a fundamental challenge: the physical inconsistency of human motion capture (MoCap) data for robotic execution.

**Primary Significance:**

- **Addressing Physical Infeasibility:** The most significant contribution is the development of a data-driven method that automatically converts physically infeasible human MoCap data into physically consistent reference motions for humanoid robots. This is achieved without requiring an explicit dynamics model of the robot or human-specified constraints, simplifying the process compared to traditional physics-based retargeting.
- **Enhanced Robot Policy Learning:** By providing physically consistent and high-fidelity reference motions, the Bi-Level Motion Imitation (BMI) framework facilitates more effective and stable policy learning for robots. This leads to improved performance on challenging tasks and enables the robot to learn a wider repertoire of agile and natural-looking skills.
- **Novel Latent Dynamics Model (SCAE):** The introduction of the Self-Consistent Auto-Encoder (SCAE) is a significant methodological advancement. It learns sparse, structured, and robust latent representations of motion patterns. This robust representation serves as a reliable anchor during the bi-level optimization, ensuring that adapted motions retain the essence of the original human movements.
- **Scalability and Generalizability:** As a purely data-driven approach, BMI holds the potential for better scalability to large-scale human MoCap datasets (e.g., AMASS) and improved generalizability across different humanoid robot platforms, as it does not rely on robot-specific dynamics models that are tedious to formulate and transfer.

**Potential Impact:**

- **Accelerated Robot Skill Acquisition:** By making imitation learning from human demonstrations more practical and robust, BMI could significantly accelerate the process of teaching complex, human-like skills to humanoid robots. This could reduce development time and the need for extensive manual reward engineering in reinforcement learning.
- **Development of More Capable Humanoids:** Robots that can accurately and stably imitate a broader range of human movements are likely to be more versatile and capable in real-world applications, from industrial tasks to assistance in human environments.
- **Advancements in Robotics and AI:** The proposed bi-level optimization strategy and the SCAE model offer novel ways to integrate generative models with reinforcement learning, potentially inspiring new directions in both robotics and broader AI research concerning learning from demonstrations with inherent domain mismatches.
- **Future Research Directions:** The work lays groundwork for several future research avenues, including scaling to even larger and more diverse MoCap datasets, developing improved metrics for robot tracking performance when references are not optimal, and, importantly, bridging the sim-to-real gap to deploy these learned policies on actual physical humanoid robots.

In conclusion, this research presents a method for training humanoid robots to perform human-like motions by intelligently adapting the reference data itself, representing a notable step towards more robust and efficient imitation learning for complex robotic systems.
