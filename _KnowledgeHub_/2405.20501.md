# ShelfHelp: Empowering Humans to Perform Vision-Independent Manipulation Tasks with a Socially Assistive Robotic Cane

> [!summary] Summary
> ShelfHelp augments a navigation smart cane with grocery-shelf manipulation guidance: a two-stage vision pipeline (region proposal plus autoencoder feature matching) locates a target product, and a discrete-command MDP planner, trained on human hand-movement demonstrations, verbally guides the user's hand to grasp it, matching human-caller performance in a pilot study.

## Problem

- Prior manipulation guidance systems direct users toward a general region but lack fine-grain guidance needed for densely packed shelves with poor tactile differentiability.
- Existing product identification relies on fixed-class classifiers or barcode scanning, which do not scale to tens of thousands of grocery products and require possession of the item first.
- Human-powered assistance services suffer from scalability limits, unavailability in developing regions, dependence on active data connections, and unavoidable privacy concerns.

## Method

- Two-stage product detector: a ==YOLOv5== network trained on SKU-110K proposes shelf region boxes, then a frozen MS-COCO-trained ==autoencoder=='s encoder extracts features matched to the target image via ==cosine similarity==, needing no retraining.
- ==Data-association== and ==spatial-scoring== modules fuse T265 ==SLAM== pose with D455 depth to track product instances as ==3D Gaussians== over time and pick the best instance, preferring top-shelf items to avoid toppling.
- A discrete verbal planner formulated as an ==MDP== over relative hand-target position, with transition probabilities fit from a 1250-command human-demonstration dataset and a reward penalizing axis interleaving, solved offline via ==value iteration==.

## Results

- Product detector achieved **0.91** precision and **0.77** recall on SKU-110K cross-validation; first-scan localization succeeded in **129/150 (86%)** of trials during the pilot study.
- Both continuous and discrete planners guided users to the product in **150/150** trials; Tukey's HSD showed the discrete mode used significantly fewer commands and less time than continuous, matching the human baseline (**75/75** success).
- TOST equivalence tests confirmed no significant difference between discrete-mode and human-caller command count and guidance time, while net hand movement did not differ significantly between planners but was highest for the human caller.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Discretizing continuous guidance into a fixed verbal command set solvable as an MDP can match human-level efficiency, suggesting structured command vocabularies outperform free-form continuous cueing.
> - Feature-matching against a frozen, generically pretrained encoder avoids costly retraining and generalizes to open-ended product catalogs, a pattern applicable beyond grocery retrieval.
> - Blindfolded-sighted pilot results are only a proxy; the paper itself cautions against treating them as reliable indicators of readiness for the actual visually-impaired target population.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in "ShelfHelp" addresses the challenges faced by people with visual impairments (PVI) in performing daily tasks independently, specifically focusing on grocery shopping. Maintaining independence in activities such as shopping is identified as a factor in quality of life. Current assistance mechanisms for PVI in grocery stores often involve reliance on sighted humans or store staff, which can introduce issues related to privacy and scalability. Furthermore, some environments, such as densely packed grocery shelves or kitchen counters with hazardous items, pose difficulties for tactile exploration alone.

Prior research in assistive technology for PVI has explored several related areas:
- **Navigation Assistance:** A substantial body of work has focused on developing robotic smart canes and other devices for locomotor guidance in diverse environments. These systems often utilize sensors, haptic feedback, and audio cues to help users navigate. ShelfHelp builds upon and extends the hardware capabilities of such existing smart canes.
- **Manipulation Guidance:** The robotics community has investigated manipulation guidance for over a decade, with some approaches using saliency maps or teleoperated systems to direct users' hands to regions of interest. However, these often lacked a global frame of reference or focused on general direction rather than fine-grain manipulation, which is crucial for distinguishing items in dense settings like grocery shelves. Human-powered services, such as Be My Eyes, offer a solution but face limitations in terms of human availability, internet connectivity requirements, and privacy.
- **Product Identification:** Existing computer vision solutions for product identification in grocery stores often struggle with the vast and constantly changing inventory of products. Techniques relying on a fixed number of output classes require extensive retraining and labeled data, making them impractical. Barcode scanning is reliable but typically requires the product to be in hand, which is inefficient for initial search. The challenge lies in creating scalable and real-time product identification without constant retraining.
- **Grocery Assistant Systems:** Much of the previous work in this domain has concentrated on navigation within the store (locomotor space) rather than specific product retrieval (haptic space). Some solutions involve environmental augmentations like RFID tags, which limit their applicability in uninstrumented settings. Text-based product selection and wayfinding for "the last few meters" have also been explored.

ShelfHelp positions itself by addressing the gap in autonomous, fine-grain manipulation guidance for product retrieval within the user's "haptic space"—the immediate area accessible without significant locomotion. It seeks to provide a self-contained, offline solution for product identification and precise verbal guidance, thereby enhancing independence and mitigating privacy concerns that arise from human-dependent assistance.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for the ShelfHelp project stems from the recognized need to enhance the independence and quality of life for people with visual impairments, particularly in the context of grocery shopping. Grocery stores present unique challenges due to the sheer volume and variety of products, with approximately 30,000 new products introduced annually in the US market. This dynamic environment can overwhelm even advanced computer vision systems and makes tactile exploration inefficient and potentially unsafe. Current reliance on sighted assistance compromises privacy and scalability. Specifically, the authors identify the problem of *product retrieval* within the "haptic space" (the immediate reachable area around the user) as an underserved area, where high product density makes exhaustive tactile search burdensome.

Against this background, the key objectives of the ShelfHelp research are:

1. **Develop a Socially Assistive Robotic Cane System:** To create a proof-of-concept system, named ShelfHelp, that extends the capabilities of instrumented canes beyond navigation to include assistance for manipulation tasks. This involves re-purposing existing hardware with new software functionalities.
2. **Propose a Novel Visual Product Locator Algorithm:** To design a scalable and practical product detection system capable of reliably locating desired items on grocery shelves. This algorithm is intended to work offline, without requiring continuous internet connectivity or frequent retraining as new products enter the market.
3. **Create a Novel Fine-Grain Manipulation Guidance Planner:** To develop an autonomous planner that issues verbal commands to guide users precisely towards retrieving a desired product. The guidance is designed to be "fine-grain," bringing the user close enough to grasp the object and minimizing exhaustive haptic search.
4. **Validate System Effectiveness and Efficiency:** To conduct a human-subjects study to assess the system's success in locating products and providing effective manipulation guidance. This includes comparing two proposed verbal guidance modes (continuous and discrete) against each other and a human assistance baseline.
5. **Investigate User Preferences:** To gather insights into user preferences regarding desirable properties of manipulation planners, such as command quantity, guidance time, and perceived system attributes (e.g., competence, intelligence, ease of use).

These objectives aim to contribute a self-contained, autonomous solution that addresses the practical and social challenges of independent shopping for PVI.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The ShelfHelp system integrates hardware and software components to enable vision-independent manipulation. The overall system follows a serial architecture: perception, planning, and guidance.

#### 3.1. Hardware System

ShelfHelp is designed to extend an existing robotic cane. The hardware setup includes:
- **RealSense D455:** Mounted on the cane for RGB-Depth sensing.
- **RealSense T265:** Mounted on the cane for odometry, providing pose information and IMU data.
- **Dell G15 Laptop:** Equipped with an RTX 3060 GPU, carried in a backpack, processing sensor data and executing software components.
- **Speaker:** Integrated into the cane for verbal guidance output.

The system is used with a standard cane grip for navigation and a different grip (behind sensors) for manipulation assistance.

#### 3.2. Software System

The software architecture encompasses alignment, product detection, data association, spatial scoring, planning, and verbal guidance.

- **Alignment:** Before manipulation guidance, the system verbally guides the user to align the cane (and thus the camera sensors) straight and upright towards the shelf. This is achieved by utilizing the T265 camera's IMU to ensure upright posture and the Hough transform to detect the largest horizontal line, guiding the user to turn until the cane is perpendicular to the shelf.

- **Product Detection:** A two-stage pipeline is employed for real-time product localization (Algorithm 1):
 1. **Region Proposal:** A YoloV5 network, pre-trained on the SKU-110K dataset, is used to detect regions likely to contain any product, generating bounding box proposals. This stage has a reported precision of 0.91 and recall of 0.77.
 2. **Feature Matching:** An autoencoder, trained on MS-COCO color images, serves as a feature extractor. The encoder portion generates a latent space representation for both the target product image (provided by the user) and each proposed region. Cosine similarity is then used to match features, identifying the best possible match based on a similarity score threshold (empirically set at 0.6). This approach does not require retraining for new products; only their feature vectors are saved.
 3. **3D Localization:** Pose information from the T265 (SLAM-based) and depth data from the D455 are fused to determine the 3D location of detected products in a global frame. A Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) refines depth information to distinguish foreground products from background pixels within bounding boxes.

- **Data Association:** To maintain a consistent map of product locations across successive camera frames, data association techniques are used. Each product instance is modeled as a multivariate Gaussian defined by its 3D global location (x, y, z) and its width (w) and height (h). The T265's IMU data contributes to global pose tracking, creating a persistent map. Instance information is updated using a rolling mean, and a lazy deletion strategy removes sparse or old detections.

- **Spatial Scoring:** ShelfHelp assigns a score to each detected instance of the target product. This score considers the rolling similarity with the target image and spatial information. This allows the system to prioritize accessible instances, for example, selecting an item from the top of a stack to minimize toppling risk.

- **Planning:** Two distinct verbal guidance mechanisms were developed:
 1. **Continuous Planner:** This mode calculates the relative position of the target to the device and provides continuous directional cues ("keep on going {direction}"). It issues a "stop" command when the error on the current guidance axis falls below a threshold, responding to user hand deceleration.
 2. **Discrete Planner:**
 - **Human Demonstration Dataset:** A dataset was collected from 25 blindfolded volunteers, who performed 50 movement commands each while their hand movements were tracked by an OptiTrack system. This mapped 36 discrete verbal commands to participants' net hand movements, which were characterized as Gaussian distributions.
 - **Markov Decision Process (MDP):** The problem of sequential decision-making for discrete guidance is modeled as an MDP (S, A, T, R).
 - **States (S):** A tuple (Δx, Δy, Δz, axis) representing the difference in distance to the target and the previous axis of motion. States are discretized at 5 cm resolution within an 0.8m operational cuboid.
 - **Actions (A):** The set of 36 discrete verbal commands (e.g., "Move 6 inches to the left").
 - **Transition Function (T):** Stochastic, calculated from the human demonstration dataset (X_c), representing the probability of state transitions given an action.
 - **Reward Function (R):** Designed to encourage reaching the target, penalize excessive commands (living penalty), and discourage illegible axis changes (interleaving penalty). It also rewards adherence to a prescribed axis order (e.g., vertical to horizontal) and necessary axis transitions when the current axis error is minimized.
 - **Policy Learning:** The MDP is solved offline using value iteration to generate a general reaching policy, which is then queried online.

- **Guidance:** Verbal guidance consists of two parts:
 1. **Plan Overview:** Provides initial context by stating the general direction to the target (e.g., "at about {clock-based direction}") to set user expectations.
 2. **Hand Movement Instructions:** Commands are either generated online (continuous mode) or retrieved from the learned policy (discrete mode) based on the target's relative position. New commands are issued only when the user's hand deceleration indicates readiness. Users are instructed to grasp the target with their non-occupied hand.

#### 3.3. Pilot Study

An IRB-approved pilot study was conducted with 15 novice, blindfolded sighted students. The use of blindfolded sighted participants is acknowledged as a preliminary validation step before engaging with the PVI community.

- **Hypotheses:**
 - H1: Discrete guidance requires fewer commands than continuous.
 - H2: Discrete guidance leads to faster product retrieval time than continuous.
 - H3: Discrete guidance results in less net hand movement than continuous.
- **Experiment Design:** Participants performed product retrieval tasks under three conditions: Continuous planner, Discrete planner, and a Human caller baseline (providing freeform verbal guidance via video call). Each condition involved retrieving 5 different products from a lab-based setup approximating a grocery shelf, with product configurations randomized and conditions counterbalanced. Users were instructed to minimize locomotion.
- **Metrics:** System success rate, number of commands, guidance time, net hand movement, and subjective evaluation (using scales for human-like, interactive, competent, intelligent, ease of use, confidence, mental demand, temporal demand, frustration).
- **Statistical Analysis:** Post-hoc comparisons using Tukey's HSD test and Two One-Sided Tests (TOST) were employed.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The pilot study provided quantitative and qualitative data on ShelfHelp's performance and user experience.

#### 4.1. System Success Rate

- **Human Condition:** Achieved a 100% success rate (75/75 retrievals).
- **Product Locator System:** The product detection failed to locate the desired item on the first scan in 21 out of 150 trials. This was primarily due to the desired product not being in the camera frame, occlusion, or incorrect identification of visually similar products when the target was not optimally presented. The system demonstrated robustness to blurring and overexposure in some instances.
- **Manipulation Planners:** Both the continuous and discrete planners successfully guided users to the desired product in all 150 trials (100% success rate). In the continuous mode, participants picked up an immediately adjacent item in 8 out of 75 trials, and in the discrete mode, this occurred in 6 out of 75 trials. These were considered successful high-level guidance, minimizing exhaustive search.

#### 4.2. Hypotheses Testing

- **H1 (Fewer Commands):** Confirmed. Post-hoc analysis showed that participants retrieved products with significantly fewer commands in the discrete mode compared to the continuous mode. The number of commands for the discrete mode was comparable to the human caller baseline.
- **H2 (Faster Retrieval Time):** Confirmed. Participants retrieved products significantly faster in the discrete mode than in the continuous mode. The guidance time for the discrete planner was also comparable to the human caller. A TOST confirmed distributional equivalence for both number of commands and guidance time between the discrete planner and human caller. However, this equivalence did not extend to total time, which included alignment and plan reporting for the automated systems.
- **H3 (Less Net Hand Movement):** Not confirmed. No statistically significant difference was found in net hand movement between the continuous and discrete planners. Interestingly, the human caller condition resulted in significantly *higher* net hand movement, which the authors attributed to the human having to re-gauge hand position when it moved out of the video frame.

#### 4.3. Time Breakdown

Both continuous and discrete modes incurred similar durations for system alignment and reporting the plan overview. The guidance time component, however, was lower for the discrete mode, aligning with the performance of the human caller.

#### 4.4. Subjective Evaluation

Participants rated both the continuous and discrete planners highly on metrics such as competence, intelligence, and ease of use. These ratings were not statistically different from the human baseline for competence and intelligence. Both planners were rated as less human-like than the human baseline. The discrete planner received a slightly lower rating on interactiveness, which was hypothesized to be due to its lack of explicit affirmations compared to the continuous mode or human guide.

The plan overview feature was rated as helpful, with an average score of 4.2 out of 5, although a large standard deviation indicated some participants found it confusing. User feedback also revealed individual preferences: some liked the continuous mode for its "Stop" affirmations, while others preferred the discrete mode for its precise movement instructions. Participants generally rated ShelfHelp favorably across metrics including confidence, mental demand, temporal demand, and frustration.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The ShelfHelp system represents a development in socially assistive robotics, specifically targeting vision-independent manipulation for people with visual impairments. The work demonstrates a proof-of-concept for enhancing existing robotic canes with advanced capabilities beyond navigation.

The research's primary contributions include:
- **Extended Robotic Cane Functionality:** ShelfHelp integrates computer vision and planning capabilities into an instrumented cane, allowing it to perform manipulation assistance in addition to traditional navigation tasks. This approach leverages existing assistive hardware.
- **Novel Product Locator System:** The two-stage, modular product detection pipeline offers a scalable and practical solution for grocery environments. Its ability to work offline and avoid frequent retraining for new products addresses significant limitations of prior methods, making it adaptable to dynamic retail inventories.
- **Novel Fine-Grain Manipulation Guidance Planner:** The introduction of a fine-grain verbal guidance system, particularly the MDP-based discrete planner, provides a method for precise user instruction in the haptic space. This is intended to minimize exhaustive tactile searching, which is inefficient and potentially unsafe in dense or hazardous environments.

The potential impact of ShelfHelp is substantial, aiming to foster greater independence for PVI in daily activities like grocery shopping. By providing autonomous assistance, the system could alleviate reliance on sighted individuals, thereby enhancing user privacy and scaling accessibility beyond human availability or geographical constraints. The objective validation against a human baseline, showing comparable performance for the discrete planner in terms of command count and guidance time, suggests a viable alternative to human assistance. The positive subjective feedback regarding ease of use, competence, and intelligence indicates a promising user experience.

While the pilot study used blindfolded sighted participants—a recognized limitation for drawing definitive conclusions about the target PVI population—the preliminary results are reported as encouraging. Future work is suggested to enhance the discrete planner with affirmations for improved interactiveness and to integrate semantic information into the product locator for more robust identification. Subsequent validation with the PVI community would be a critical next step to fully assess its real-world impact and readiness.
