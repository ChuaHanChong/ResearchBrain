# Improving Robotic Imitation Learning via Trajectory Standardization

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers developed Information-Standardized Trajectory Resampling (ISR), an offline preprocessing method that enforces consistent kinematic-dynamic information density across demonstration trajectories. This approach led to an average increase of 24.0 percentage points in task success rate for robotic manipulation tasks compared to time-uniform downsampling, while often reducing dataset size and improving robustness to varied human operator styles.

## Problem

- Human-generated demonstration trajectories in imitation learning often exhibit temporal non-uniformity and spatial redundancy, leading to non-Markovian contradictions and inefficient learning.
- Variations in operator speed, intermittent pauses, and inconsistent action density across demonstrations introduce skewed data distributions and dominate training gradients with low-value motions.
- Existing preprocessing methods, such as time-uniform downsampling or arc-length parameterization, fail to account for critical kinematic-dynamic information, potentially discarding dynamic intent or oversimplifying task-critical segments.

## Method

- The Information-Standardized Trajectory Resampling (**ISR**) method defines an ==information-intensity field==, L(t), as a weighted sum of velocity and acceleration norms (λ_vel ||v(t)|| + λ_acc ||a(t)||).
- This field warps the raw trajectory into a 1D Riemannian manifold where geodesic distance represents kinematic-dynamic information content.
- ISR uses a bottom-up dynamic programming approach to extract a resampled trajectory, ensuring consecutive points are separated by approximately equal information distance, thereby standardizing the data's kinematic-dynamic properties.

## Results

- ISR consistently increased average task success rates: by **24.0** percentage points for π0.5 (from **47.8%** to **71.8%**) and **17.5** percentage points for VO-DP (from **62.7%** to **80.2%**) compared to time-uniform 3× downsampling.
- Achieved these performance gains while retaining comparable or fewer action points (**28%** to **40%** of original data) than 3× downsampling, demonstrating improved data quality rather than increased volume.
- ISR exhibited improved robustness to cross-operator variability, achieving **45.8%** success with mixed operator data on Place&Stack, more than doubling the **22.2%** achieved by time-uniform downsampling.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Standardizing demonstration trajectories by enforcing consistent kinematic-dynamic information density across points significantly improves the performance and robustness of imitation learning policies.
> - A decoupled information distance formulation, combining secant distance for spatial uniformity (velocity term) and integrated acceleration for dynamic intent preservation (acceleration term), effectively addresses both spatial redundancy and dynamic fidelity.
> - The acceleration term is particularly critical for contact-rich or force-sensitive manipulation tasks, as it preserves subtle dynamic cues (e.g., deceleration phases) that are essential for precise execution and often overlooked by purely spatial methods.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work addresses a data-centric challenge within the field of imitation learning (IL) for robotic manipulation. Imitation learning enables robots to acquire skills by observing human demonstrations, and its effectiveness is significantly influenced by the quality and consistency of the input data.

The broader landscape of IL currently faces several issues related to human-generated demonstration trajectories. These trajectories are often characterized by temporal non-uniformity and spatial redundancy. This includes variations in operator speed, intermittent pauses, and inconsistent action density. These characteristics can introduce artifacts such as non-Markovian contradictions, leading to less efficient learning and degraded policy performance. While recent advancements in generative policies, such as diffusion-based and flow-matching approaches, have improved the modeling of multimodal action distributions, they remain sensitive to these underlying data quality issues.

Current common preprocessing strategies often involve time-uniform downsampling (e.g., 3x), which aims to reduce sequence length but does not account for the kinematic and dynamic content of the trajectory. This method treats all time intervals as equally important, failing to differentiate between unintentional pauses and deliberate decelerations critical for fine manipulation, or to adapt sampling density to segments with rapid directional changes. Other approaches in trajectory processing, such as those based on Euclidean length or geometric simplification algorithms like Douglas–Peucker, can improve spatial uniformity but generally lack sensitivity to acceleration-level features that encode dynamic intent.

Related research has explored methods to mitigate challenges posed by dense action sequences, primarily through:
- **Waypoint- and Keyframe-Based Learning:** These methods introduce sparse representations at the policy level, modifying the policy architecture or prediction target. Examples include Automatic Waypoint Extraction (AWE) for next-waypoint prediction, HYDRA for hybrid action spaces, and VIEW for visual waypoint extraction from human videos. While these approaches address sparsity at the policy level, they do not standardize the underlying training data itself.
- **Trajectory Simplification and Resampling:** These techniques operate at the data level. Beyond time-uniform downsampling, general trajectory compression methods from fields like transportation and spatiotemporal data mining exist (e.g., Douglas–Peucker, MDL-based methods). Arc-length-based warping re-parameterizes trajectories by Euclidean arc length, aiming for spatial uniformity, but it only considers first-order kinematic quantities and is insensitive to acceleration.

This research positions itself by identifying "trajectory standardization as an underexplored yet practically important preprocessing problem" in imitation learning. It seeks to fill a gap by proposing a data-level resampling method that moves beyond purely temporal or spatial criteria to incorporate both kinematic and dynamic information, producing standardized datasets compatible with various downstream policies without requiring architectural changes.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to improve the effectiveness of robotic imitation learning by addressing the critical issue of demonstration trajectory data quality. The authors observe that human-generated teleoperated trajectories frequently exhibit temporal non-uniformity and spatial redundancy, which degrade the efficiency and success rates of learning policies.

The detailed motivations for this work stem from several practical challenges in using raw human demonstration data for imitation learning:

- **Temporal Non-uniformity and Spatial Redundancy:** Human operators introduce variable speeds, intermittent pauses, and inconsistent action densities during teleoperation. This leads to:
 - **Within-episode issues:** At typical recording frequencies (30–60 Hz), unintentional pauses and slow movements generate many near-duplicate action points. This creates non-Markovian contradictions when "stay" and "move" commands are associated with identical observations. Policies trained on such sequences may tend to replay recent actions rather than inferring task-relevant states.
 - **Across-episode issues:** Differences in operator pacing, style, and skill level lead to skewed data distributions. Frequent low-value motions can dominate training gradients, potentially underrepresenting crucial task-critical actions. This redundancy also increases memory and latency costs, particularly for transformer-based policies with long contexts.
- **Limitations of Existing Preprocessing:** The most common preprocessing method, time-uniform downsampling (e.g., 3x), simplifies trajectories by reducing their length. However, it fails to account for the actual information content. It treats every time interval equally, meaning it cannot effectively:
 - Remove speed-induced non-uniformity or redundant pauses.
 - Distinguish between an unintentional pause and a deliberate deceleration for fine manipulation.
 - Adapt sampling density to segments where the end-effector undergoes rapid directional changes (high curvature).
 - Alternative methods, such as Euclidean arc-length parameterization, improve spatial uniformity but are insensitive to second-order kinematic features (acceleration) that encode dynamic intent or force-sensitive actions.

Given these challenges, the key objectives are:
1. **Develop a resampling method** that enforces trajectory-level *information consistency* across demonstrations. This means ensuring that adjacent resampled points carry comparable *kinematic–dynamic information*.
2. **Produce compact yet high-fidelity datasets** that are significantly smaller than raw or time-uniformly downsampled data, while retaining critical manipulation intent.
3. **Improve both training efficiency and policy performance** of mainstream imitation learning policies by feeding them with these standardized datasets.
4. **Demonstrate the method's ability to denoise demonstrations** by reducing non-Markovian artifacts and to standardize data distribution across different operators and styles.

The research aims to create a "plug-and-play" offline preprocessing module that can be integrated into standard imitation learning pipelines, providing a data-centric solution to improve the foundational quality of demonstration data.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors propose Information-Standardized Trajectory Resampling (ISR), an offline preprocessing method designed to resample demonstration trajectories. The core idea is to enforce approximately equal kinematic and dynamic information distance between consecutive points in the resampled trajectory.

The methodology proceeds in three main steps:
1. **Defining Information Distance via an Information-Intensity Manifold:**
 - Raw demonstration trajectories are represented as sequences of end-effector positions `P_raw = {(p_i, t_i)}`.
 - First-order kinematic states (velocities, `v_i`) and second-order kinematic states (accelerations, `a_i`) are computed using finite differences from these positions and timestamps. Velocity captures instantaneous displacement rate, while acceleration serves as a kinematic proxy for force-sensitive intent (e.g., decelerations before contact, centripetal acceleration during turns).
 - To unify kinematic displacement and dynamic velocity variation, an **information-intensity field `L(t)`** is defined:
 `L(t) = λ_vel ||v(t)|| + λ_acc ||a(t)||`
 Here, `λ_vel` and `λ_acc` are non-negative weights that balance the contribution of velocity and acceleration norms.
 - This `L(t)` is interpreted as an information density that warps the original trajectory into a one-dimensional **Riemannian manifold `M`**. In this manifold, regions of high activity (high `L(t)`) are stretched, while low-activity regions are compressed.
 - The **geodesic information distance** between two points at times `t_i` and `t_j` on this manifold is defined as the integral of `L(t)` over that interval: `Δs_M(i, j) = ∫ L(t) dt`. Consequently, equal geodesic intervals correspond to equal kinematic-dynamic information content.

2. **Geodesic-Equidistant Optimization:**
 - The objective is to extract a resampled subsequence `K = {k_0, k_1..., k_M-1}` from the raw trajectory such that adjacent resampled points `(p_k_m, p_k_{m+1})` are separated by approximately equal information distance, targeted at a predefined `D_target`.
 - The continuous geodesic distance `Δs_M` is approximated in discrete form for two consecutive resampled points `k_m` and `k_{m+1}`:
 `Δs_M(k_m, k_{m+1}) ≈ λ_vel ||p_k_m - p_k_{m+1}|| + λ_acc Σ ||a(t)||` (summing `||a(t)||` from `t=k_m` to `t=k_{m+1}-1`).
 - A key aspect of this discrete approximation is the **decoupled design** of the two terms:
 - The **velocity term** uses the *secant distance* `||p_k_m - p_k_{m+1}||` (Euclidean distance between endpoints) rather than the integrated arc length. This is crucial for enforcing spatial uniformity; it penalizes large spatial gaps between retained points regardless of the path taken, effectively correcting spatial non-uniformity caused by oscillations or curvature that would inflate arc length.
 - The **acceleration term** retains its integral (summation) form `Σ ||a(t)||`. This captures the *kinematic-dynamic complexity* within the segment, preserving high-curvature features and dynamic intent (like deceleration-to-contact) that might not be evident from endpoint displacement alone.
 - A **local segment cost function** penalizes the squared deviation of each segment's approximated information distance from the target distance `D_target`:
 `Cost(k_m, k_{m+1}) = [Δs_M(k_m, k_{m+1}) - D_target]^2`.
 - The **global objective `J(K)`** is to minimize the sum of these local costs over all segments in the resampled trajectory `K`.
 - This optimization problem is solved offline using a **bottom-up dynamic programming (DP)** approach, which has `O(T^2)` time complexity for a trajectory of length `T`. Prefix sums are used to make each segment-cost evaluation `O(1)`. The algorithm ensures that the start and end points of the original trajectory are always preserved.

3. **Effects of the Cost Terms:**
 - **Velocity term (`λ_vel ||v(t)||`):** This term drives the optimizer to achieve spatial uniformity between consecutive resampled points. It effectively acts as a denoising mechanism, merging segments with near-zero inter-point distances (e.g., random pauses, hesitations, slow movements) into larger, spatially uniform steps. This results in more uniform end-effector velocity across the resampled episode and eliminates redundant low-displacement segments.
 - **Acceleration term (`λ_acc ||a(t)||`):** This term preserves trajectory curvature and dynamic intent. It contributes in two ways:
 - **High-curvature regions:** During arc-like movements or obstacle avoidance, directional changes induce centripetal acceleration, which locally increases `L(t)`. This forces denser sampling, preventing oversimplification of arcs.
 - **Dynamic intent in contact-rich tasks:** Operators often decelerate precisely before fine manipulation phases (e.g., aligning, grasping). Although spatial displacement is small during such decelerations, the tangential acceleration is significant. The acceleration term amplifies the geodesic distance in these critical windows, preserving the "deceleration–preparation–manipulation" temporal sequence that a purely spatial criterion might discard as redundant static frames.

The result is a compact, standardized trajectory that aims to retain the operator’s manipulation intent with significantly fewer action points, leading to more consistent kinematic-dynamic characteristics across datasets.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates ISR on three real-world robotic manipulation tasks using two mainstream imitation learning policies: π$_{0.5}$ and VO-DP. The experimental setup involved an AgileX Piper robot arm with a two-finger gripper, dual Intel RealSense D435 cameras (third-person and wrist-mounted views), and teleoperated demonstrations recorded at 30 Hz. The three tasks were Place&Cover (100 episodes), Place&Stack (150 episodes), and Push-T (150 episodes), each with systematic object randomization for varied initial conditions.

The main findings are categorized as follows:

1. **Baseline Comparison against Time-Uniform Downsampling:**
 - ISR was compared against a standard time-uniform 3× downsampling baseline.
 - **Improved Task Success Rates:** For the π$_{0.5}$ policy, ISR consistently increased the average task success rate from 47.8% (with 3× downsampling) to 71.8%, an absolute improvement of 24.0 percentage points across the three tasks (Place&Cover: 45.8% to 72.2%; Place&Stack: 48.6% to 72.2%; Push-T: 48.9% to 71.1%).
 - Similar improvements were observed with the VO-DP policy, where the average success rate increased from 62.7% (with 3× downsampling) to 80.2% (Place&Cover: 56.9% to 77.8%; Place&Stack: 66.7% to 91.6%; Push-T: 64.4% to 71.1%).
 - **Comparable or Reduced Dataset Size:** These performance gains were achieved while retaining a comparable or even fewer number of action points. ISR's resampling ratios ranged from 28% to 40% of the original data, which is similar to or less than the 33.3% retained by 3× downsampling. This indicates that the performance improvements stem from the enhanced information distribution and quality of the resampled sequences, not merely from retaining more data.

2. **Ablation Study on Acceleration Weight (λ_acc):**
 - The study investigated the impact of the acceleration weight `λ_acc` on task success and resampling ratio, keeping `λ_vel = 1.0` and `D_target = 0.05` fixed.
 - **Task-Dependent Sensitivity:** Reducing `λ_acc` from 0.01 to 0.005 led to a more aggressive compression (sampling rate dropped from ~28% to ~20%). The impact on task performance was task-dependent:
 - For **Place&Cover**, the success rate marginally decreased (from 72.2% to 69.4%), suggesting this task is less sensitive to fine-grained acceleration control.
 - For **Place&Stack**, the success rate sharply dropped (from 72.2% to 45.8%), returning to approximately the time-uniform baseline level. This indicates that stacking, a contact-rich manipulation task, critically relies on preserving the "decelerate–align–place" phase, which is under-sampled when `λ_acc` is too low.
 - **Confirmation of Acceleration Term Importance:** These results confirm that the acceleration term is a crucial component for tasks requiring force-sensitive or precise manipulation. Setting `λ_acc` appropriately is necessary to balance compression efficiency with the fidelity of dynamic intent.

3. **Cross-Operator Robustness:**
 - ISR's robustness to variations in operator styles was evaluated on the Place&Stack task, using a dataset from a single operator versus a mixed dataset combining demonstrations from three different operators (each contributing 50 episodes).
 - **Single Operator:** With a single operator, ISR improved success from 48.6% (3× downsampling) to 72.2%.
 - **Mixed Operators:** When demonstrations from different operators were combined, the time-uniform 3× downsampling method suffered a significant performance degradation, with success rates dropping from 48.6% (single operator) to 22.2%. This performance collapse was attributed to conflicting training signals introduced by preserved operator-specific speed, pause patterns, and stylistic differences.
 - In contrast, ISR achieved 45.8% success with the mixed-operator dataset, more than doubling the 3× downsampling result (22.2%) and approaching the performance of the single-operator 3× baseline (48.6%). This indicates that ISR effectively absorbs and standardizes inter-operator variability through its kinematic-dynamic information-based approach, making it a more practical solution for collecting demonstrations from multiple sources.

In summary, the main findings demonstrate that ISR consistently outperforms time-uniform downsampling by providing demonstrations with a more consistent and task-relevant information distribution, leading to higher policy success rates with comparable or reduced data volume. The acceleration term is essential for dynamic-sensitive tasks, and the method exhibits robustness to varying operator styles, enhancing its practicality.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research on Information-Standardized Trajectory Resampling (ISR) carries several significances and potential impacts within the field of robotic imitation learning and broader robotics applications.

1. **Improved Data Quality and Policy Performance:** The core significance lies in demonstrating that a sophisticated data-centric preprocessing step can yield substantial improvements in the performance of imitation learning policies. By standardizing demonstration trajectories based on kinematic-dynamic information, ISR enhances the learning signal, leading to higher task success rates (e.g., 24.0 percentage points average improvement for π$_{0.5}$) and more robust policies. This suggests that focusing on the *information content* rather than just temporal or spatial properties of demonstration data is critical for effective imitation learning.

2. **Enhanced Training Efficiency and Resource Management:** ISR achieves these performance gains while either reducing or maintaining a comparable number of action points in the training datasets compared to traditional time-uniform downsampling. This means that learning algorithms can be trained on more compact, yet higher-fidelity, datasets. The reduction in dataset size and training costs has direct implications for:
 - **Memory Footprint:** Less storage required for demonstration datasets.
 - **Computational Cost:** Faster training times and reduced computational resources, which is particularly beneficial for large-scale models or resource-constrained environments.
 - **Reduced Latency:** For transformer-based policies with long contexts, compact trajectories can alleviate latency issues.

3. **Robustness to Operator Variability and Scalability of Data Collection:** The finding that ISR significantly mitigates the performance degradation caused by mixing demonstrations from multiple operators is a critical contribution. Human demonstrations naturally vary in style, speed, and pacing. ISR's ability to absorb this inter-operator variability by standardizing the kinematic-dynamic features makes demonstration collection more scalable and practical. Instead of requiring a single, highly consistent operator, roboticists can leverage contributions from diverse individuals, potentially accelerating the creation of large, varied datasets necessary for general-purpose robot autonomy.

4. **A "Plug-and-Play" Preprocessing Module:** ISR is designed as an offline preprocessing module, meaning it can be easily integrated into existing imitation learning pipelines without requiring modifications to the policy architecture or training procedures. This modularity facilitates its adoption by the research community and practitioners.

5. **Deeper Understanding of Information Content in Trajectories:** The proposed information-intensity field, with its decoupled velocity and acceleration terms, offers a principled way to quantify and preserve different types of information within a trajectory. It highlights the importance of second-order kinematic cues (acceleration) as proxies for dynamic intent, especially in force-sensitive or contact-rich manipulation tasks, which are often overlooked by purely spatial methods.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Accelerated Robot Skill Acquisition:** By providing cleaner, more informative training data, ISR can accelerate the development and deployment of robot manipulation skills across various domains.
- **More Generalizable Policies:** With datasets standardized across operator styles, learned policies may exhibit better generalization to new scenarios and variations in execution.
- **Foundation for Future Data-Centric AI in Robotics:** This work underscores the importance of data quality in AI for robotics. It could inspire further research into intelligent data curation and preprocessing methods that optimize for information content rather than just raw volume.

**Limitations and Future Work (as identified by the authors):**
- **Scope of Trajectory Representation:** ISR currently operates only on end-effector position trajectories. Extending the information-intensity field to incorporate joint-space representations could broaden its applicability to a wider range of robot control scenarios.
- **Manual Tuning of Hyperparameters:** The acceleration weight `λ_acc` currently requires task-specific manual tuning. Different manipulation tasks exhibit varying sensitivities to acceleration-level features. Developing an adaptive mechanism that can infer or automatically tune this weight from trajectory statistics would significantly improve the generality and ease of use of ISR.

Despite these limitations, ISR presents a tangible advancement in addressing a fundamental data quality problem in imitation learning, offering a practical and effective solution for enhancing robot skill learning from human demonstrations.
