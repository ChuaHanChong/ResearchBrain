# Rapid Learning of Dexterous In-Hand Pen Writing through Real-Time Jacobian Estimation

> [!summary] Summary
> An online task-Jacobian estimation approach for in-hand pen writing with anthropomorphic hands. A recursive least-squares estimator learns the command-to-pen-tip map live on hardware, bootstrapped by an 18-second grip-pose excitation sweep, while a damped pseudo-inverse controller writes and a nullspace posture term holds the grasp. Runs on a laptop CPU, achieving 0.64 mm mean in-plane error across 38 runs without simulation, models, or demonstrations.

## Problem

- RL for in-hand manipulation needs heavy domain randomization because simulators cannot replicate contact richness, and imitation learning stalls on demonstrations that occlusion makes impossible to record fully.
- Prior uncalibrated visual servoing targeted serial arms; Jacobian-field extensions needed hours of multi-view RGBD data and showed only limited tool manipulation, never in-hand articulation.
- Inverse-Jacobian estimators skip matrix inversion but expose no nullspace, limiting redundant hands; earlier pen-writing hardware studies evaluated single letters, free-space traces, or reported errors only in simulation.

## Method

- A command-space ==task Jacobian== estimated by ==recursive least squares== with a forgetting factor and diagonal covariance, regressed on commanded joint increments rather than noisy measured velocities, gated on active joint motion.
- An 18-second ==excitation phase== sweeps six manually recorded grip poses along a Catmull-Rom spline, injecting continuous joint motion so the estimator observes well-conditioned joint and pen-tip pairs before tracking.
- A damped ==Tikhonov pseudo-inverse== control law tracks a PID-plus-feedforward task command over Bezier glyph paths, with a ==nullspace posture-pullback== term pulling joints toward the initial grip pose.

## Results

- Pooled over 38 fully configured runs the in-plane error averaged **0.64 mm**, **0.62 mm** in air and **0.67 mm** on paper, with 95th percentile near **1.4 mm**.
- Freezing the Jacobian right after excitation diverged in two of three runs; freezing after 30 seconds of tracking held baseline at **0.62 mm** and generalized to unseen letters.
- Removing grip regularization broke four of ten runs, worst means above **12 mm**; a 31-minute alphabet run stayed stationary at **0.59-0.69 mm**; simulated Shadow Hand reached **0.17 mm**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A locally valid approximate Jacobian, kept fresh by continuous updating, can replace an analytic contact model; precision needs a well-conditioned map, not a correct one.
> - Excitation design matters as much as the estimator in closed kinematic chains: arbitrary joint babbling perturbs the grasp instead of producing informative task-space motion.
> - The formulation assumes continuous, unchanging contact and slow speeds, so regrasping, finger gaiting, and human-speed writing stay out of reach.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position within the Broader Research Landscape

Dexterous in-hand manipulation refers to repositioning or articulating an object using the fingers while maintaining a grasp. It is a central challenge in robotics because it combines high-dimensional actuation, intermittent and changing contacts, friction, compliance, object motion, and potentially redundant hand configurations. Although anthropomorphic robotic hands have many of the mechanical degrees of freedom required for human-like manipulation, controlling those degrees of freedom reliably remains difficult.

Recent research has primarily followed two directions. The first uses **reinforcement learning (RL)**, generally in simulation, to learn policies for object manipulation. These approaches can generate complex behaviors, but their performance depends on accurate simulation, extensive domain randomization, and substantial computational resources. The contact dynamics between fingers and objects are difficult to reproduce in simulation, creating a persistent sim-to-real problem. The second direction uses **imitation learning (IL)**, which avoids some requirements for explicit contact modeling but requires large and informative demonstration datasets. In-hand manipulation is especially difficult to demonstrate visually because the hand and object occlude the fingers and contact regions. Human demonstrations must also be retargeted to robotic hands with different kinematic structures.

The present work belongs to a third line of research: **online, model-free or model-light visual servoing based on Jacobian estimation**. A task Jacobian describes the local relationship between actuator motion and task-space motion. Earlier visual-servoing methods estimated this relationship online for robotic arms, often using recursive least squares or related filtering approaches. More recent work has extended Jacobian estimation to dense image features and generalized robot systems. Other studies have estimated inverse Jacobians directly for manipulation and writing tasks.

The paper adapts forward task-Jacobian estimation to a substantially more difficult setting: a redundant, tendon-driven anthropomorphic hand manipulating a grasped pen. Pen writing is used as a benchmark because it requires coordinated finger motion, stable grasping, and accurate continuous trajectory following. Unlike demonstrations that move a tool through free space, writing on paper provides a visible physical outcome. The authors distinguish their approach from previous writing systems that use arm motion, open-loop transitions, synergy spaces, tactile RL policies, or evaluations limited to particular letters or simulated environments.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

The main objective is to determine whether a robotic hand can learn the local mapping from finger commands to pen-tip motion directly on the physical system, without relying on:

- An analytic hand–object kinematic or contact model.
- Training in simulation.
- Precollected demonstrations.
- High-performance computing.
- Hand-specific Jacobian calibration.

The authors are motivated by the observation that the relationship between finger actuation and object motion is nonlinear, dependent on the grasp configuration, and affected by changing contact conditions. An analytic model would need to represent tendon transmission, joint geometry, finger–pen contacts, compliance, friction, and potentially slippage. Such a model could be difficult to obtain and may become inaccurate as the grasp changes.

Instead, the paper estimates a local **command-space task Jacobian** from the robot’s commanded joint changes and observed pen-tip motion. The controller then uses the estimated Jacobian to calculate finger commands that should move the pen along a target trajectory. Because the hand has more actuated joints than the two-dimensional writing task requires, the authors also seek to use the Jacobian nullspace to stabilize the grasp while tracking the path.

A further objective is to assess whether the same estimator and controller can transfer across different hand embodiments. The physical experiments use the ORCA hand, while simulated experiments use the Shadow Hand and Wuji Hand 2. This tests whether the formulation depends on a particular hand’s kinematics or can instead operate as an embodiment-independent control architecture.

The authors also examine the method’s limitations through ablation studies. These tests evaluate the contributions of continuous Jacobian updating, the initial excitation phase, nullspace grip stabilization, and writing speed.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Robotic Hand and Grasp

The physical platform is the **ORCA hand**, a 17-degree-of-freedom tendon-driven anthropomorphic hand. The pen is held in a fixed power–precision grasp using a custom soft thermoplastic polyurethane sleeve. The sleeve increases the pen’s effective diameter by approximately four times and introduces compliance at the hand–pen interface, helping maintain the grasp.

Ten joints are actively controlled: four thumb joints and three joints each in the index and middle fingers. The wrist, ring finger, and pinky, comprising seven joints, remain fixed during each writing motion. The arm only repositions the hand between letters in demonstrations such as “hello”; it does not assist with the individual writing strokes.

#### 3.2. Visual Sensing

A webcam observes an ArUco marker attached to the pen and three markers placed on the desk. The desk markers define the paper coordinate frame. The pen-tip location is reconstructed from the marker pose using a fixed marker-to-tip offset and is transformed into the paper frame.

The measured trajectory is processed with a constant-velocity Kalman filter. Innovation-based outlier rejection reduces the influence of erroneous detections. Camera timestamps provide a common time basis for perception, estimation, and control. The complete feedback loop operates at approximately 15 Hz, limited by the perception pipeline, although the camera has a nominal 30 Hz frame rate.

#### 3.3. Online Jacobian Estimation

Let \(q\) represent the ten commanded finger-joint positions and \(x\) represent the two-dimensional pen-tip position in the paper plane. Locally, the task relationship is expressed as:

\[
\dot{x} = J\dot{q},
\]

where \(J\) is an unknown \(2 \times 10\) task Jacobian.

The Jacobian is estimated recursively using a diagonal-covariance recursive least-squares or Kalman-filter-style update. The estimator uses the previous incremental joint command as the joint-motion regressor rather than measured joint velocity. This choice reflects the operational mapping from controller commands to observed motion and avoids the greater noise associated with the robot’s measured velocities.

A forgetting factor prevents the estimate from becoming permanently fixed, allowing it to adapt to slowly changing contact and transmission conditions. Updates occur only when the hand is actively moving, which prevents observation noise from modifying the estimate while the hand is nearly stationary. Covariance values are bounded by a floor and an initialization limit to maintain numerical stability.

#### 3.4. Excitation Phase

The Jacobian is initially set to zero. Before writing, the hand performs an approximately 18-second excitation sequence. The fingers move through six manually selected grip poses along a Catmull–Rom spline. The motion lasts approximately 15 seconds, followed by three seconds of settling.

The excitation trajectory is designed to generate informative pen-tip motion in both in-plane directions while keeping the grasp secure. Smooth spline interpolation is used to avoid the velocity discontinuities associated with piecewise-linear paths and the repeated velocity reductions associated with certain smoothstep trajectories. The excitation phase provides the estimator with an initial approximation of how joint combinations influence pen motion.

#### 3.5. Path Generation and Control

The target writing trajectories are planar curves traversed at constant tangential speed. Letters A–Z are represented as cubic Bézier glyphs. The system also supports circles, hearts, flowers, stars, and arbitrary single-stroke SVG outlines.

A task-space PID controller with feedforward produces a desired pen-tip velocity. The feedforward term is the target path’s tangent velocity, while proportional, integral, and derivative terms compensate for tracking error. Integral windup is limited by an anti-windup clamp.

The estimated Jacobian is inverted using a damped right pseudoinverse:

\[
J^+ = J^T(JJ^T+\epsilon I)^{-1}.
\]

The resulting task-space command is combined with a nullspace posture term. This term pulls the joints toward their initial grip pose while projecting the motion into the approximate Jacobian nullspace, thereby helping maintain the pen grasp without directly opposing the writing motion. Joint velocity limits and low-pass filtering are applied before the commands are integrated into absolute joint-position commands.

The physical hand controls only the \(x\) and \(y\) coordinates. Out-of-plane \(z\) motion is not controlled. For on-paper experiments, the paper is folded or mounted so that its center bulges upward by approximately one centimeter and absorbs the observed two- to three-millimeter vertical drift.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Tracking Accuracy

The fully configured system completed closed shapes, arbitrary SVG trajectories, alphabet letters, and writing on paper. Across 22 post-excitation in-air runs, the mean in-plane tracking error was approximately **0.62 ± 0.12 mm**, with run-to-run values ranging from approximately 0.39 to 0.86 mm. The mean 95th-percentile error was approximately 1.4 mm.

Across 16 on-paper runs, the mean in-plane error was approximately **0.67 ± 0.08 mm**, ranging from 0.57 to 0.82 mm, with a similar 95th-percentile error of approximately 1.4 mm. The combined mean across 38 runs was approximately **0.64 ± 0.10 mm**. Paper contact did not substantially worsen in-plane performance, largely because the compliant paper arrangement absorbed vertical drift.

In a continuous approximately 31-minute run, the hand wrote all 26 letters. The mean error across six time windows remained between 0.59 and 0.69 mm, with no upward trend. A transient during the letter “K” produced an approximately 20 mm peak error, but the controller recovered without re-excitation or manual intervention.

#### 4.2. Ablation Results

Continuous Jacobian updating was important for robustness. When the estimate was frozen immediately after the excitation phase, two of three runs diverged within two minutes, and the remaining run operated at approximately 1.0 mm mean error. Freezing the estimate after only 12 seconds of writing produced similar degradation. Freezing after approximately 30 seconds of tracking performed better, with all four runs completing at approximately 0.62 mm mean error. However, continuous updating remained advantageous because it could respond to disturbances and grip changes not present during the initial learning period.

Nullspace grip stabilization was also important. Without the posture regularization term, four of ten runs failed outright, two additional runs degraded substantially, and only four remained near baseline accuracy. Some failed runs reached mean errors above 12 mm, with 95th-percentile errors as high as approximately 140 mm. These failures were associated with loss of a stable pen grasp.

The system operated most reliably at a deliberately slow target speed of \(8 \times 10^{-4}\) meters per second. Increasing speed produced a nonlinear tradeoff between throughput and accuracy. At two times the baseline speed, one trial achieved approximately 0.8 mm error while another degraded to 2.3 mm. At three times the speed, error increased to approximately 2.1 mm. Progressive speed increases caused large transients or divergence.

#### 4.3. Additional Simulated Platforms

The same controller was tested in MuJoCo on the Shadow Hand and Wuji Hand 2. These simulations controlled all three pen-tip coordinates rather than only the planar coordinates. The Shadow Hand achieved a reported RMSE of approximately **0.17 mm**, while the Wuji Hand 2 achieved approximately **1.48 mm**. The simulations provide evidence that the formulation can be applied to different hand kinematics, although they benefit from noise-free ground-truth state information and therefore are not directly equivalent to the physical experiments.

### 5. Significance, Potential Impact, and Limitations

The paper demonstrates that online estimation of a local task Jacobian can support contact-rich in-hand manipulation without a detailed hand–object model, simulation training, or demonstrations. Its main significance is methodological: it presents an alternative to data-intensive RL and IL for tasks involving continuous object motion under a stable grasp. The combination of initial excitation, ongoing adaptation, damped inversion, and nullspace grip stabilization addresses several practical problems specific to anthropomorphic hands.

The approach may be relevant to robotic systems with limited onboard computation, including compact manipulators, prosthetic devices, and systems operating where simulation or large datasets are unavailable. Its embodiment-independent formulation could also simplify deployment across hands with different joint layouts, although the experiments do not establish universal transfer without tuning.

Several limitations constrain the current results. The physical controller regulates only planar pen motion and leaves vertical motion uncontrolled. Consequently, the system relies on a compliant or uneven writing surface and cannot yet write reliably on a rigid flat surface. The arm is required to reposition the hand between characters, and the system is not designed for multi-stroke writing or coordinated hand–arm motion. It also assumes continuous contact and a fixed grasp; it cannot address regrasping, finger gaiting, or contact-breaking actions. Writing speed remains substantially below human levels. Finally, accuracy is measured through the same vision pipeline used for control, without independent measurement of deposited ink, so the reported errors inherit marker-detection, calibration, and camera-jitter limitations.

Overall, the work supports the feasibility of rapid embodied adaptation for a specific class of continuous in-hand manipulation tasks. Further progress would require three-dimensional hand control, faster and more robust perception, explicit handling of contact transitions, independent physical evaluation of written output, and integration of fine finger control with gross arm positioning.
