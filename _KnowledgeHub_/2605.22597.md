# MoSA: Motion-constrained Stress Adaptation for Mitigating Real-to-Sim Gap in Continuum Dynamics via Learning Residual Anisotropy

> [!summary] Summary
> The MoSA framework improves real-to-sim dynamics learning for deformable objects by augmenting isotropic physics models with learned residual anisotropy and heterogeneity, guided by motion-constrained optimization. It achieved superior accuracy on synthetic data and a mean PSNR of 31.35 on real-world generalization, enabling higher success rates in robot manipulation tasks.

## Problem

- Classical physics-based system identification struggles with real-world objects exhibiting mild anisotropy and heterogeneity, leading to a "real-to-sim" gap due to simplified isotropic material models.
- End-to-end neural network models for dynamics often discard strong physical priors, resulting in poor data efficiency, unstable training, and overfitting when learning complex dynamics from scratch.
- Learning dynamics from visual observations typically relies on indirect and ill-conditioned image reconstruction losses, which provide ambiguous supervision and hinder stable optimization.

## Method

- A structured, physics-informed neural network progressively adapts an isotropic constitutive prior by learning bounded residual stress corrections, including pre-linear, microplane-based redistribution, and post-linear terms, to capture mild anisotropy.
- Heterogeneity is modeled by modulating a global material parameter with a continuous implicit field, η(x)η(x), providing local spatial variations in material properties.
- A motion-constrained optimization strategy uses higher-order supervision derived from dynamic 3D Gaussian Splatting reconstructions, incorporating derivative-based regularization (flow, scale, and rotation losses) in addition to image/mask rendering losses.

## Results

- MoSA achieved the lowest Chamfer Distance (**13.5**) and Earth Mover Distance (**8.8**) on the synthetic PAC-NeRF dataset for 3D trajectory prediction, demonstrating superior accuracy over baselines.
- On a real-world dataset, MoSA consistently outperformed all baselines in generalization, achieving a mean PSNR of **31.35** and SSIM of **92.3**, compared to GIC's **30.02** PSNR and **91.5** SSIM.
- The learned dynamics from MoSA enabled robot policies to achieve substantially higher real-world success rates in manipulation tasks (e.g., **68%** for elastic-rabbit placement vs. **42%** for baselines), demonstrating practical utility.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Targeted learning of residual continuum effects (anisotropy and heterogeneity) on top of a physics prior is more effective for bridging the real-to-sim gap than purely physics-based or purely data-driven approaches.
> - Higher-order motion constraints derived from dynamic 3D reconstructions provide more direct and stable supervision for dynamics learning from videos compared to indirect image-level reconstruction losses.
> - Decomposing anisotropic stress adaptation into structured, progressive corrections (pre-linear, microplane-based redistribution, post-linear) improves learning stability, efficiency, and offers physical interpretability for the learned residual dynamics.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Learning real-world dynamics from visual observations is a research area with applications in fields such as computer graphics, robotics, and embodied agents. Existing approaches generally fall into two categories:

1. **Classical Physics-Based System Identification**: This paradigm involves calibrating simulators by estimating physical parameters (e.g., Young’s modulus, Poisson’s ratio) for predefined physical models. These models often assume homogeneous and isotropic materials for simplicity and stability in differentiable simulation. While effective for dominant behaviors, this approach faces limitations when real-world objects exhibit mild anisotropy and heterogeneity. These "residual effects," which can arise from internal structure, fabrication, or wear, are not fully captured by simplified isotropic models and can become a significant bottleneck in closing the "real-to-sim" gap.
2. **End-to-End Neural Network Modeling**: This alternative uses neural networks to directly fit dynamics from observations. While capable of modeling complex or missing physics, these black-box models often discard strong physical priors. This can lead to poor data efficiency, unstable training, and overfitting, as the network must simultaneously learn the dominant physical backbone and capture subtle residual effects without explicit separation.

A common challenge across both paradigms, especially when learning from visual data, is that supervision from image reconstruction alone can be indirect and ill-conditioned. Different 3D deformation trajectories and material responses may produce similar visual appearances, leading to ambiguity during optimization.

MoSA positions itself to address these limitations by adopting a targeted modeling approach. Instead of learning full dynamics from scratch or being limited by fixed isotropic models, it maintains a calibrated isotropic constitutive law as a physics prior. It then specifically learns the residual effects—mild anisotropy and heterogeneity—that deviate from this prior. Furthermore, it introduces motion-aware constraints to provide more direct supervision than image-level reconstruction, thereby enhancing data efficiency and reducing overfitting. The work aims to bridge the gap between purely physics-based and purely data-driven approaches by combining strong physical inductive biases with the flexibility of neural networks for fine-grained adaptations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to improve the accuracy and generalization of real-to-sim dynamics learning for deformable objects, particularly by addressing the residual gap left by conventional isotropic physical models.

The motivation for this work is multi-faceted:

- **Limitations of Isotropic Physical Models**: While standard homogeneous and isotropic material models provide a simplified and often effective approximation for many real-world objects, they are rarely exact. Real objects frequently exhibit mild anisotropy (directional dependence of mechanical properties) and heterogeneity (spatial variation of properties). These residual effects, though subtle, can become the primary source of real-to-sim mismatch once the dominant, near-isotropic behavior is well-calibrated. Traditional parameter estimation within isotropic models cannot account for these missing physical details.
- **Inefficiencies of End-to-End Neural Dynamics**: Purely neural, black-box approaches to dynamics learning, while capable of modeling complex phenomena, tend to be data-inefficient and prone to overfitting. This is because they must re-learn fundamental physical principles (the isotropic backbone) alongside the subtle residual effects, without leveraging established physical knowledge. This redundancy increases data requirements and training instability.
- **Challenges of Indirect Visual Supervision**: Learning dynamics from visual observations often relies on image reconstruction losses (e.g., pixel-level differences), which offer indirect and coarse constraints on the underlying 3D deformation field. The ill-conditioned nature of this problem means that multiple 3D deformations can produce similar 2D image projections, leading to ambiguity and hindering stable optimization. More direct supervision on the deformation itself is needed to improve data efficiency and reduce the risk of overfitting.

To address these motivations, the specific objectives of MoSA are:
1. **Develop a physics-informed residual stress adaptation module**: This module aims to augment an isotropic constitutive prior by learning bounded residual operators that capture mild anisotropy and heterogeneity. The design seeks to progressively correct prior stress responses in a structured manner, preserving physical inductive bias while providing interpretability for the learned residual dynamics.
2. **Introduce a motion-constrained optimization strategy**: This strategy leverages detailed motion cues derived from dynamic 3D reconstructions from videos. It aims to provide higher-order supervision by constraining the temporal and spatial derivatives of the deformation field, thereby offering more direct and effective guidance for dynamics learning than pixel reconstruction alone.
3. **Validate the framework's performance**: Experimentally demonstrate superior accuracy, generalization, and robustness on both synthetic and real-world datasets, confirm the physical meaningfulness of the learned residual anisotropy, and evaluate its practical impact in downstream applications like robot manipulation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The MoSA framework aims to learn real-to-sim dynamics from multi-view videos by augmenting an isotropic constitutive prior with learned residual stress correction operators. The overall pipeline involves two main components: a structured stress adaptation module and a motion-constrained optimization strategy. Object geometry is represented as particles and simulated using the Material Point Method (MPM).

#### 3.1. Dynamic Gaussian Splatting for Scene Reconstruction

The method begins by performing a two-stage dynamic 3D reconstruction from multi-view video sequences.
1. **Static Geometry Initialization**: A static 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) model is first reconstructed at the initial timestamp. Gaussian primitives are initialized as isotropic (equal scales, identity rotations) to provide a stable initial particle representation.
2. **Dynamic Deformation Learning**: With the initial static particle scaffold fixed, a dynamic 3DGS model is trained. This model allows Gaussian primitives to deform and rotate over time, capturing the dynamic motion via a deformation network $F_\theta$. This decoupling ensures that the dynamic model focuses on deformation rather than absorbing static geometry errors.

#### 3.2. Constitutive Model as Physics Prior

The Material Point Method (MPM) is utilized for simulation, requiring a constitutive model to define how materials deform under stress. MoSA uses a classical isotropic constitutive model (e.g., linear elasticity, Neo-Hookean, Fixed Corotated, St. Venant-Kirchhoff, or plasticity models like Drucker-Prager, Von Mises, Herschel-Bulkley) as a physics prior. This prior defines the initial stress-strain relationship, $\sigma = \sigma(\epsilon)$, which is then subject to neural adaptation.

#### 3.3. Structured, Progressive Stress Adaptation for Anisotropic Residual Effect

The core of MoSA's dynamics learning is a physics-informed neural network that progressively corrects the prior isotropic stress response to capture mild anisotropy and heterogeneity. Instead of directly modeling a complex anisotropic constitutive law, MoSA learns residual corrections.

The corrected stress $\hat{\sigma}$ is formulated as:
$\hat{\sigma}_{ij} = T_{\sigma}^{-1} \sum_{kl} (\text{I} + C_{\Psi_{ijkl}}) T_{\sigma} \cdot (\sigma(\epsilon)_{kl} + L_{\Phi,pre_{kl}}) + L_{\Theta,post_{ij}}$

This formulation applies corrections in a structured manner:

- **Pre-linear term ($L_{\Phi,pre}$)**: An isotropic complementary matrix, predicted by an MLP ($\Phi$), that makes initial adjustments to the prior isotropic stress $\sigma_p$. This accounts for minor deficiencies even within the isotropic model itself. It uses the Right Polar Tensor $U$ of the deformation gradient $F$ and prior stress $\sigma_p$ as input.
- **Redistribution term ($C_{\Psi}$)**: This is a fourth-order tensor that plays a central role in modeling anisotropy. To improve learning stability and efficiency, and provide physical grounding, $C_{\Psi}$ is decomposed into four second-order tensors ($C_x, C_y, C_z, C_{xyz}$) with microplane-based stress redistribution constraints.
 - **Microplane-based Redistribution**: Stress components are first rescaled and redistributed within their respective microplanes (x, y, z planes). For example, for the x-plane, $\tilde{\sigma}_x = (\text{I} + C_x) \sigma_x$. This means each corrected stress component is a linear combination of all stress components on its corresponding microplane.
 - **Shear Symmetrization**: After per-plane redistribution, the mutual equivalence of shear stresses is applied (e.g., $\hat{\sigma}_{xy} = 0.5(\hat{\sigma}_{xy} + \hat{\sigma}_{yx})$).
 - **Cross-plane Redistribution**: A fourth tensor, $C_{xyz}$, further redistributes the updated normal stress components ($\hat{\sigma}_{xx}, \hat{\sigma}_{yy}, \hat{\sigma}_{zz}$) across the three principal directions. This step specifically refines anisotropic effects along principal axes.
 - All $C$ matrices are predicted by cascaded MLPs ($\Psi_1, \Psi_2$) based on the material state ($U$, prior/intermediate stresses). Empirical coefficients $\alpha_1, \alpha_2$ control the deviation from prior stress. The $C$ matrices are designed to be invertible by constructing them from upper and lower triangular matrices.
- **Post-linear term ($L_{\Theta,post}$)**: Another linear adjustment predicted by an MLP ($\Theta$), applied after the $C_{\Psi}$ contraction to further refine the stress.
- **Symmetrization**: Both $L_{pre}$ and $L_{post}$ are symmetrized as $L = \frac{1}{2}(L+L^T)$ to maintain physical consistency.
- **Plastic Yield Projection**: The framework extends this adaptation logic to correct the plastic yield projection, modeling anisotropic plasticity criteria.

#### 3.4. Heterogeneity Modeling

To capture spatial variations in material properties, MoSA estimates a single global material parameter for the object, then modulates it with a continuous implicit field, $\eta(x)$, that provides local variations at each spatial location. The local parameter $P(x)$ is defined as $P(x) = P_{global} \cdot (1 + 0.2 \cdot \tanh(\eta(x)))$. The $\tanh$ activation and a scaling factor of 0.2 constrain local variations, ensuring a smooth and physically consistent representation. The network output $\eta(x)$ is encouraged to have zero mean and minimal variance through a loss function.

#### 3.5. Motion-constrained Optimization Strategy

To overcome the limitations of indirect image-based supervision, MoSA incorporates higher-order motion constraints derived from dynamic 3D reconstructions.

- **Loss from Dynamic 3DGS Reconstruction**: The two-stage dynamic 3DGS reconstruction provides crucial cues about how the object moves and deforms. The method uses an image/mask rendering loss ($L_{img/mask}$) to align rendered outputs with input videos:
 $L_{img/mask} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_t L_1(R_{img/mask}(P_t, F_t), I_t/M_t)$
 where $P_t, F_t$ are simulation particle states, and $I_t, M_t$ are image and mask ground truths.
- **Derivative-based Regularization**:
 - **Flow Loss ($L_{flow}$)**: Supervises particle velocities by comparing Gaussian Flow maps rendered from simulated Gaussian splats with flow maps obtained from input videos using Gaussian Flow. This provides implicit supervision for velocity and sharpens silhouettes.
 - **Scale Regularization ($L_s$)**: Constrains the relative scaling components of the deformation gradient $F$ using information from the dynamic 3DGS. Specifically, it relates the change in Gaussian covariance matrix ($A_t$) to the deformation gradient $F_t$ ($A_t = F_t A_0 F_t^T$) and regularizes the magnitude of scaling $(\Lambda - I)$ to match the relative scale change $(\delta S / S_0)$ predicted by the dynamic reconstruction.
 - **Rotation Regularization ($L_r$)**: Constrains the rotational components of the deformation gradient $F$ by comparing the simulated rotation with the rotation derived from the dynamic 3DGS output.
- **Heterogeneity Loss ($L_{het}$)**: Regularizes the learned implicit field $\eta(x)$ to have zero mean and minimal variance, ensuring that the global parameter dominates and local variations are subtle.

The total loss function is a weighted sum of these components: $L_{total} = L_{flow} + \beta_1(L_{img} + L_{msk}) + \beta_2(L_r + L_s) + \beta_3 L_{het}$. The parameters of the stress adaptation module ($\Psi, \Theta, \Phi$), the global physical parameters ($P$), and the heterogeneity field ($\eta$) are jointly optimized.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates MoSA on both synthetic and real-world datasets, focusing on object dynamics grounding and real-world generalization.

#### 4.1. Dataset and Metrics

- **Synthetic Dataset**: PAC-NeRF dataset, which provides multi-view RGB sequences and 3D ground-truth geometry trajectories.
- **Real-world Dataset**: A newly collected dataset of 7 objects (elastic, elastoplastic, plastic) captured by a light-field system with synchronized industrial cameras during free-fall scenarios.
- **Metrics**: Chamfer Distance (CD) and Earth Mover Distance (EMD) for 3D geometry accuracy; PSNR and SSIM for rendering quality (cropped around the object).

#### 4.2. Object Dynamics Grounding (Synthetic Data)

On the PAC-NeRF dataset, MoSA demonstrates superior overall performance in capturing object dynamics from multi-view video supervision. Quantitative results show that MoSA achieves the lowest CD and EMD values across various object types (Table 1), indicating more accurate 3D trajectory prediction compared to baselines like PAC-NeRF, DEL, and GIC. For instance, MoSA achieves a mean CD of 13.5 and EMD of 8.8, outperforming the next best, GIC (CD 13.8, EMD 9.1).

#### 4.3. Real-world Generalization

MoSA consistently outperforms all baselines on the real-world dataset in generalizing to unseen initial orientations and drop heights. Qualitative comparisons (Fig. 3) and quantitative results (Table 2) show MoSA achieving higher PSNR and SSIM across all tested objects (Chick1, Gorilla, Mandarin, Chick2, Peanut, Rabbit, Rainbowball). MoSA's mean PSNR is 31.35 and SSIM is 92.3, compared to GIC's 30.02 PSNR and 91.5 SSIM. Fully neural approaches (DEL, NeuMA) exhibit worse performance, attributed to overfitting with limited real data or domain shift from pretraining. System identification methods like GIC perform comparatively better, but MoSA's residual stress adaptation demonstrates a higher performance ceiling.

#### 4.4. Additional Experiments and Analysis

- **Ablation Studies (Table 3)**:
 - **Redistribution Term (C)**: Removing $C_{\Psi}$ resulted in the largest performance drop, confirming the importance of anisotropic stress redistribution.
 - **Linear Correction Terms ($L_{pre}, L_{post}$)**: Both terms contribute to consistent performance gains.
 - **Heterogeneity Modeling**: Removing the heterogeneity module (no Het) led to consistent performance drops (e.g., PSNR on Rabbit decreased from 30.35 to 29.67), indicating its role in capturing spatial variations.
 - **Motion Constraints ($L_{flow}, L_{scale}, L_{rot}$)**: All derivative-level supervision terms contribute to improved accuracy and generalization, validating their importance for stable learning from videos.
- **Predictions of Long-term Dynamics (Fig. 4a)**: MoSA maintains better stability and a slower increase in loss over extended simulation times compared to baselines.
- **Robustness to Prior Physical Models (Fig. 4b)**: MoSA exhibits robustness to the choice of the prior physical law. Unlike parameter-calibration baselines that are sensitive to model misspecification, MoSA, by refining the constitutive prior, maintains stable performance even when starting from simpler models.
- **Application on Downstream Manipulation Tasks (Fig. 5)**: MoSA-learned dynamics enabled robot policies to achieve substantially higher real-world success rates in manipulation tasks (e.g., 68% vs 42% for elastic-rabbit placement, 82% vs 55% for tower-hanging), demonstrating the practical utility of more accurate real-to-sim dynamics.
- **Sensitivity to Dynamic Reconstruction Quality (Table 4)**: Motion constraints provide consistent performance gains even when using reconstructions from early optimization stages, indicating benefits beyond perfect reconstruction.
- **Analysis of Learned Anisotropy (Fig. 6, Appendix A)**: On isotropic synthetic data, the directional Jacobian response $w(\theta)$ remains near zero, indicating minimal deviation from isotropy. On real data (Mandarin), $w(\theta)$ exhibits pronounced directionality aligned with the object axis, suggesting that MoSA adaptively captures missing anisotropic effects. Comparison of effective directional modulus further confirms the accuracy of learned anisotropic properties.
- **Spatial Variation of the Learned Heterogeneity (Fig. 7)**: Visualizations of the learned $\eta(x)$ field show smooth, object-dependent patterns with physically meaningful variations in local stiffness, confirming it learns structured heterogeneity.
- **Complexity Analysis (Appendix H)**: MoSA demonstrates faster training (3.5 hours) and inference (0.76 seconds per inference) compared to other learning-based methods like NeuMA and DEL.
- **System Identification (Appendix I)**: MoSA achieves superior performance in global parameter estimation, outperforming baselines in 21 out of 23 cases, attributed to the stress correction module simplifying the learning objective and motion-constrained optimization guiding towards physically plausible solutions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The MoSA framework introduces a method for reducing the real-to-sim gap in continuum dynamics by focusing on mild, systematic continuum residual effects, such as anisotropy and heterogeneity, which are often overlooked by standard isotropic simulators. By explicitly learning targeted residual stress corrections and integrating stronger supervision through motion constraints derived from dynamic 3D reconstruction, the approach demonstrates improved stability and generalization in dynamics learning.

The significance of this work includes:

- **Enhanced Simulation Fidelity**: MoSA improves the accuracy of dynamics models, particularly for real-world objects that are rarely perfectly isotropic or homogeneous. This allows for more realistic simulations that better reflect the behavior of physical materials.
- **Data Efficiency**: By leveraging a strong physical backbone and focusing neural capacity on residual effects, the method mitigates the data inefficiency and overfitting issues common in purely black-box neural dynamics models.
- **Practical Applicability**: The demonstrated improvements in downstream robot manipulation tasks indicate that better real-to-sim dynamics directly translates to more reliable sim-to-real transfer for robotic policies, which is a key challenge in robotics.
- **Interpretability**: The structured residual stress adaptation module, particularly the microplane-based decomposition, offers a degree of physical interpretability regarding the nature of the learned anisotropic and heterogeneous effects.
- **Foundation for Future Work**: The findings suggest that future dynamics learning methods can benefit from combining strong physical priors with targeted residual modeling, rather than solely relying on either fully physics-based or fully data-driven approaches.

The potential impact of MoSA extends across various domains:

- **Graphics and Digital Twins**: It can facilitate the creation of higher-fidelity physics-consistent digital twins and more realistic deformable object simulations in computer graphics.
- **Robotics and Embodied AI**: It enables more accurate dynamics identification and simulation for deformable objects, which can lead to more robust and reliable autonomous systems that interact with the physical world.
- **Engineering Design**: Improved simulation reliability may support safer design, testing, and deployment of systems that depend on predicting material behavior.

The authors acknowledge that, like many advances in physical modeling, improved simulation capabilities could be used in various ways, including potentially harmful applications in autonomous systems. They encourage responsible use and evaluation in safety-critical contexts, while emphasizing that their work focuses on foundational algorithmic improvements for dynamics learning without involving sensitive personal data or human subjects.
