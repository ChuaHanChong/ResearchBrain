# Training-Free Refinement of Flow Matching with Divergence-based Sampling

> [!summary] Summary
> The Flow Divergence Sampler (FDS) from KAIST refines generative trajectories of pre-trained Flow Matching models at inference time, spatially steering samples away from ambiguous velocity regions by minimizing flow divergence. This training-free method consistently improves image generation fidelity, achieving lower FID scores on datasets like ImageNet 256x256 and enhancing detail in various synthesis and inverse problems.

## Problem

- Flow Matching models learn a marginal velocity field as an average of conflicting 'sample-wise' velocities at intermediate states.
- This averaging can misguide generative trajectories towards low-density regions, leading to degraded or blurry generated samples.
- The resulting discrepancy is an irreducible error, unobservable during inference, that cannot be resolved by training alone.

## Method

- **FDS** (Flow Divergence Sampler) introduces a ==training-free==, plug-and-play ==inference-time framework== that spatially refines intermediate states during the generative process.
- It leverages a theoretical finding that the unobservable velocity discrepancy can be reliably estimated using the ==local divergence== of the pre-trained model's velocity field.
- At each solver step, **FDS** generates a set of nearby candidate states, evaluates their divergence, and selects the candidate with the ==minimum divergence== for the next integration step.

## Results

- **FDS** consistently improved Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) and LPIPS across CIFAR-10 and ImageNet 256x256, reducing FID on ImageNet 256x256 with JiT-L/16 (Heun, 99 NFEs) from **2.713** to **2.496**.
- The method outperformed compute-matched baselines (those with increased NFEs) and training-based solutions (e.g., HRF, VRFM), indicating it addresses a distinct type of generative error.
- FDS demonstrated improved qualitative output, enhancing fine-grained details in images for class-conditional generation, text-to-image synthesis, and inverse problems (deblurring, super-resolution).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The divergence of the marginal velocity field serves as a data-free, inference-time surrogate for the unobservable conflict between marginal and sample-wise velocities, effectively quantifying local ambiguity.
> - Spatial refinement of intermediate states, guided by minimizing this divergence, is a practical and effective strategy to mitigate a fundamental limitation of Flow Matching models without requiring retraining.
> - Significant performance improvements can be achieved with minimal computational overhead (e.g., one refinement iteration with one candidate), especially when interventions are focused on the early stages of the generative process.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Flow Matching (FM) has emerged as a significant paradigm in generative modeling, capable of synthesizing high-fidelity data across various modalities, including images, audio, and video. These models operate by learning a continuous-time marginal velocity field that transports samples from a simple prior distribution to a complex target data distribution.

A fundamental limitation of traditional Flow Matching models arises from the nature of this marginal velocity field. While each paired source-target sample induces its own "sample-wise" velocity, the learned marginal velocity at any given intermediate state is an average of all such sample-wise velocities passing through that state. When these underlying sample-wise velocities locally conflict (i.e., point in opposing directions), the averaged marginal velocity can misguide generative trajectories towards low-density regions between modes. This can result in degraded generation quality, often manifesting as blurry or inaccurate outputs. This discrepancy represents an irreducible error, persisting even with a perfectly optimized flow model, as it stems from the intrinsic multi-modality of the sample-wise velocities at certain points in the latent space.

Previous research efforts to address this issue can be broadly categorized. One line of work focuses on redesigning the training formulation to explicitly model the multi-modal distribution of conflicting velocities, rather than just their average. Examples include Hierarchical Rectified Flow (HRF) and Variational Rectified Flow Matching (VRFM), or acceleration-based approaches like CAF. While effective, these methods necessitate costly retraining of the model, often with significant architectural or objective modifications, thereby precluding the direct application of powerful, pre-trained, off-the-shelf flow models.

Another category of related work aims to enhance generation quality at inference time. This includes developing advanced ODE solvers (e.g., higher-order numerical approximations, UniPC, or dynamic stiffness evaluation) to reduce temporal integration errors. These approaches primarily focus on improving the accuracy of following a *fixed* integration path in time. Separately, advanced guidance mechanisms (e.g.,) exist that perform spatial updates to the state `x_t` at inference time, often by incorporating external signals like reward models or classifier gradients.

The work presented in this paper, Flow Divergence Sampler (FDS), occupies a distinct position within this landscape. It directly addresses the issue of conflicting sample-wise velocities by introducing a *training-free, plug-and-play inference-time framework* that performs *spatial refinement* of intermediate states. Crucially, FDS does not modify the underlying pre-trained model or require external signals, instead leveraging an intrinsic property of the marginal velocity field (its divergence) to steer trajectories away from ambiguous regions. This approach is novel in its ability to mitigate a fundamental architectural limitation of Flow Matching models without necessitating resource-intensive retraining, thus enabling the enhancement of existing strong generative models.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this work stems from the inherent limitation of Flow Matching (FM) models where the marginal velocity field, learned as a conditional expectation, can represent an average of conflicting sample-wise velocities at certain intermediate states. This averaging can lead to the marginal velocity pointing towards low-density regions, rather than valid data modes, resulting in the generation of degraded or blurry samples. The authors quantify the severity of this issue using the conditional mean-squared discrepancy between the marginal velocity and the sample-wise velocities, denoted as `L*CFM(x_t, t)`. This discrepancy term is irreducible by training alone and is not directly observable during inference, as it depends on knowledge of the true sample-wise velocities from the training data.

Given this challenge, the central question posed by the authors is whether this problem can be mitigated effectively at inference time without requiring the costly retraining or modification of the underlying generative model. This question directly motivates the development of a training-free solution that can leverage existing, powerful pre-trained flow models.

The key objectives of this research are therefore:
1. **Identify a Data-Free Surrogate for Discrepancy**: To establish a theoretical foundation that allows the estimation of the unobservable discrepancy `L*CFM(x_t, t)` using only information computable from a pre-trained flow model at inference time. This surrogate should serve as a reliable indicator of local ambiguity in the marginal velocity field.
2. **Develop a Plug-and-Play Inference-Time Refinement Method**: To design a novel sampling framework, termed the Flow Divergence Sampler (FDS), that can intervene during the generative process. This framework should spatially refine intermediate states by steering them towards regions where the pre-trained model's velocity field is more consistent and reliable, thereby reducing the risk of generating degraded outputs. The method is designed to be plug-and-play, compatible with various off-the-shelf flow backbones and standard ODE solvers.
3. **Demonstrate Robust and Consistent Performance**: To experimentally validate that the proposed FDS framework consistently improves generation fidelity across diverse generation tasks, model architectures, and numerical solvers, even under matched computational budgets, and without the need for model retraining or external guidance signals.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Flow Divergence Sampler (FDS) introduces a training-free, inference-time methodology to address the limitations of Flow Matching models by spatially refining intermediate states during sampling. The approach is built upon a theoretical finding that links the local ambiguity of the marginal velocity field to a computable property of the field itself.

**Theoretical Foundation: Flow Divergence as a Discrepancy Surrogate**
The core of the methodology is a theoretical result (Theorem 1 in the paper) which establishes that the intractable, data-dependent discrepancy term, `L*CFM(x_t, t)` (Eq. 6), which quantifies the conflict between marginal and sample-wise velocities, can be expressed in closed form. This expression depends solely on the divergence of the marginal velocity field, `∇_x_t · u_t(x_t)`, and known schedule-dependent coefficients (`α_t`, `β_t`). Since the coefficients are constant for a fixed timestep `t`, minimizing `L*CFM(x_t, t)` over nearby states `x_t` is equivalent to minimizing the spatial divergence `∇_x_t · u_t(x_t)`.

In practice, the true marginal velocity field `u_t` is replaced by the pre-trained neural network `u_θ(x, t)`. This allows the definition of an inference-time, data-free surrogate for local reliability: `ˆδ_t(x) = ∇_x · u_θ(x, t)`. A lower value of this divergence surrogate is interpreted as an indication of lower discrepancy and, consequently, reduced risk of degradation in generated samples. To efficiently compute this divergence in high-dimensional spaces, the authors employ Hutchinson's trace estimator, which approximates the trace of the Jacobian matrix (and thus the divergence) using a single noise vector.

**Flow Divergence Sampler (FDS) Mechanism**
Building on this divergence surrogate, FDS operates by performing targeted spatial refinement of the intermediate state `x_t` before each step of the ODE solver. The objective is to move `x_t` to a nearby state `˜x_t` where the divergence `ˆδ_t(x)` is minimized.

1. **Zero-Order Local Refinement**: Directly optimizing `ˆδ_t(x)` via gradient descent would be computationally expensive, requiring second-order derivatives of the flow model. To circumvent this, FDS adopts a zero-order approach. At a given solver state `x_t` and timestep `t`, it generates a set of `M` nearby candidate states. These candidates are created by adding small random perturbations to `x_t`: `x^(m) = x_t + σ_t ξ^(m)`, where `ξ^(m) ~ N(0, I)` is a random noise vector and `σ_t` is a decaying perturbation scale schedule. The authors use a cosine schedule for `σ_t`, which gradually decreases from a maximum value (`σ_max`) to zero, prioritizing larger perturbations in early stages.

2. **Candidate Selection**: For each of the `M` candidates (including the original `x_t`), its divergence `ˆδ_t(x)` is evaluated. The candidate `x^(m*)` that exhibits the minimum divergence is selected as the refined state `˜x_t`.

3. **Iterative Refinement (Optional)**: This refinement procedure can be repeated for `N` iterations. In each iteration, the newly selected `˜x_t` serves as the base state for generating new perturbations. Empirically, the authors found that `N=1` is often sufficient for significant improvements.

4. **Integration with Solver**: The refined state `˜x_t` is then passed to a standard ODE solver (e.g., Euler, Heun's method) to compute the next state `x_{t+1}`. This means FDS acts as a plug-in module, modifying the input to the solver rather than the solver itself. The overall sampling step thus becomes: `˜x_{t_k} = Refine(x_{t_k}, t_k; u_θ)`, followed by `x_{t_{k+1}} = SolverStep(˜x_{t_k}, t_k, t_{k+1}; u_θ)`.

**Computational Efficiency Considerations**:
FDS introduces additional computation proportional to `N × M` divergence evaluations per solver step. However, the authors optimize for efficiency by:
- Setting `N=1` and `M=1` in many practical scenarios, demonstrating that even minimal refinement can yield substantial benefits.
- Restricting FDS application to the early generative regime (`t < T_trunc`, typically `T_trunc = 0.5`). This is based on the empirical observation that severe trajectory conflicts predominantly occur during the initial denoising stages.

These design choices aim to establish FDS as a low-overhead, practical inference-time solution that enhances generation fidelity without incurring excessive computational burden.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings that collectively support the effectiveness and versatility of the Flow Divergence Sampler (FDS).

#### 4.1. Synthetic 2D Experiments

On a 2D synthetic checkerboard distribution, FDS demonstrated improved generation quality compared to standard Flow Matching (FM), achieving a lower Wasserstein Distance (WD) to the true path. Visualizations confirmed that FDS effectively steered samples from high-divergence regions towards low-divergence regions, thereby reducing the number of stray samples and aligning them more accurately with the target distribution. Critically, the inference-time divergence surrogate (ˆδ_t(x)) derived from the pre-trained model showed strong structural alignment with the ground-truth discrepancy map (L*CFM(x_t, t)) computed using training data. This provided empirical validation for the theoretical claim that divergence serves as a reliable proxy for local ambiguity.

#### 4.2. Main Experiments on Image Generation

FDS was evaluated on class-conditional and unconditional image generation tasks using CIFAR-10 and ImageNet 256×256 datasets, with established flow-based models like EDM and JiT (JiT-B/16, JiT-L/16) as backbones.
- **Consistent FID Improvement**: FDS consistently improved Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) across all tested datasets, models, and ODE solvers (Euler and Heun). For instance, on ImageNet 256×256 with JiT-L/16 and a Heun solver (99 NFEs), FDS reduced FID from 2.713 to 2.496.
- **Outperformance of Compute-Matched Baselines**: When compared against baselines with an equivalent computational budget (i.e., increased Number of Function Evaluations (NFEs) to match FDS's wall-clock time), FDS demonstrated superior performance. While simply increasing NFEs offered diminishing returns, especially with advanced solvers, allocating the same budget to FDS yielded more substantial fidelity enhancements. This suggests that FDS's spatial refinement addresses a distinct type of error (trajectory misguidance) that temporal integration methods alone cannot fully resolve.
- **Qualitative Enhancements**: Qualitative results on ImageNet 256×256 showed that FDS produced images with finer details (e.g., specific textures and features) that were less apparent or absent in samples generated by compute-matched baselines.
- **Robustness Across Denoising Steps and Architectures**: Additional experiments (Appendix D) confirmed FDS's consistent outperformance even with 100 denoising steps. Furthermore, FDS was effective when applied to the state-of-the-art latent diffusion backbone SiT-XL, showing improvements in FID, although the nature of the corrections appeared more structural in latent diffusion models compared to pixel-space models.

#### 4.3. FDS on Various Tasks

The plug-and-play capability of FDS was further validated across diverse tasks:
- **Text-to-Image Synthesis**: When integrated with SD3-Medium for text-to-image generation on the DrawBench dataset, FDS generally improved metrics such as ImageReward (IR), HPSv2, Aesthetic Predictor, and CLIP Score across varying classifier-free guidance (CFG) scales. Qualitatively, FDS enhanced the rendering of fine-grained details in generated images.
- **Inverse Problems**: For image deblurring and super-resolution (×4) tasks (using TFG as a baseline on the Cat dataset), FDS consistently improved quantitative metrics (FID, LPIPS) and produced visually more detailed and realistic images, preserving intricate features like fur textures and eye pupils compared to the baseline.

#### 4.4. Analysis and Ablation Studies

- **Comparison with Training-Based Frameworks**: FDS, when combined with a strong pre-trained model like EDM, significantly outperformed training-based methods designed to resolve crossings (e.g., HRF, VRFM) on CIFAR-10, even with comparable parameter counts (e.g., EDM+FDS FID 2.44 vs HRF FID 4.96). This suggests that training to explicitly model multi-modal velocities might impose a capacity bottleneck that FDS avoids by operating on an already powerful marginal velocity field at inference time.
- **Early-Stage Interventions**: Ablation studies confirmed that FDS yielded the most substantial benefits when applied during the early stages of flow integration (t < T_trunc ≈ 0.5), where trajectory conflicts are more pronounced. A cosine schedule for the perturbation scale `σ_t`, which allocates larger weights to earlier timesteps, was found to be more effective than linear or concave schedules.
- **Refinement Iterations (N) and Candidates (M)**: Increasing the number of iterative updates (N) or perturbation candidates (M) generally improved performance, but the gains quickly plateaued. The study indicated that setting `N=M=1` offered a practical balance, providing competitive generation quality with minimal computational overhead.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Flow Divergence Sampler (FDS) presents several significant contributions and has substantial potential impact on the field of generative modeling, particularly concerning flow-based models:

1. **Addresses a Fundamental Limitation of Flow Matching**: FDS directly targets and mitigates the inherent issue of ambiguous marginal velocity fields in Flow Matching models, where conflicting sample-wise velocities can lead to degraded outputs. By demonstrating that this ambiguity can be quantified and acted upon during inference, the work offers a novel solution to a core problem.

2. **Training-Free and Plug-and-Play Paradigm**: A key strength of FDS is its nature as a training-free and plug-and-play inference framework. This allows it to enhance the performance of *any existing, pre-trained flow backbone* (e.g., EDM, JiT, SD3-Medium, SiT) and integrate seamlessly with *standard ODE solvers* (e.g., Euler, Heun) without requiring any architectural modifications, re-training, or fine-tuning of the underlying model. This significantly lowers the barrier to adoption and maximizes the utility of already powerful generative models.

3. **Consistent and Robust Performance Improvements**: The extensive experimental validation demonstrates FDS's robust applicability and consistent generation quality improvements across a diverse range of settings. This includes various generation tasks (class-conditional image generation, text-to-image synthesis, inverse problems), datasets (CIFAR-10, ImageNet, DrawBench, Cat), and model scales. The observed improvements in metrics like FID and LPIPS, along with qualitative enhancements, indicate a reliable mechanism for improving output fidelity.

4. **Novel Approach to Spatial Trajectory Refinement**: Unlike prior inference-time methods that primarily focus on reducing temporal integration errors or rely on external guidance signals, FDS introduces a distinct paradigm of *spatial trajectory refinement*. It actively steers the intermediate state away from unreliable regions using an intrinsic signal (divergence of the marginal velocity), opening a new avenue for enhancing generative processes.

5. **Computational Efficiency**: Despite introducing additional computation, FDS is designed to be efficient. The finding that minimal iterations (N=1) and candidates (M=1) combined with early-stage intervention are often sufficient to yield significant improvements ensures that the framework remains practical for real-world applications, even outperforming compute-matched baselines.

6. **Potential for Broader Impact**: By providing a general, easy-to-implement method for improving the fidelity of flow-based generative models without additional training costs, FDS could lead to higher quality outputs across numerous applications. This includes, but is not limited to, higher-fidelity image and video synthesis, improved performance in inverse problems, and more reliable content generation. The conceptual simplicity of using divergence as a reliability signal might also inspire similar inference-time refinement strategies in other continuous-time generative paradigms, such as Score-based Diffusion Models. This work establishes a foundation for future research exploring intrinsic properties of generative models for on-the-fly quality enhancements.
