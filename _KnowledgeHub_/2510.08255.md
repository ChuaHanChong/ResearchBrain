# Opponent Shaping in LLM Agents

> [!summary] Summary
> This research investigates opponent shaping in LLM agents, proposing ShapeLLM, a model-free opponent shaping algorithm adapted for transformer architectures. The study demonstrates that LLM agents can strategically influence co-players' learning dynamics in both competitive and cooperative game-theoretic environments, for example, achieving an average reward of 3.96 in the Iterated Prisoner's Dilemma compared to 0.10 for the shaped opponent, while baseline naive agents averaged 1 each.

## Problem

- Understanding the strategic behavior of Large Language Model (LLM) agents in multi-agent systems is essential for their effective and safe deployment.
- Existing multi-agent LLM research often treats LLM agents as static entities, overlooking dynamic strategic interactions where agents adapt to one another.
- Traditional opponent shaping (OS) algorithms from Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) are not directly applicable to transformer-based LLMs due to architectural differences and requirements for higher-order derivatives or specific memory structures.

## Method

- Introduces ShapeLLM, a novel model-free ==opponent shaping== algorithm designed for transformer architectures that integrates both intra-episode history and inter-episode context into structured natural language prompts.
- LLM agents, based on `gemma-2-2b-it`, are fine-tuned using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) in repeated 2x2 matrix game environments.
- The shaper agent's policy parameters are updated less frequently (only after completing an entire trial, spanning multiple episodes of opponent learning) to account for and influence the opponent's learning dynamics.

## Results

- In competitive games like the Iterated Prisoner's Dilemma, shapers successfully exploited naive opponents, achieving an average reward of 3.96 compared to the opponent's **0.10** (baseline was 1 for each naive agent).
- In cooperative settings such as the Cooperative Iterated Prisoner's Dilemma and Iterated Stag Hunt, shapers consistently guided interactions towards mutual cooperation and Pareto-optimal equilibria, leading to improved collective welfare.
- ShapeLLM demonstrated robustness against opponents initialized with varying propensities for specific actions and to different prompt formulations, with ablation studies confirming that observing opponent learning dynamics across episodes is key to shaping.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - LLM agents, when augmented with ShapeLLM, possess the capability to actively influence the learning trajectories and behaviors of other LLM agents solely through interaction.
> - Opponent shaping in LLMs can be leveraged for both exploiting naive opponents in competitive scenarios and fostering coordination and prosocial behavior in cooperative games.
> - Encoding opponent learning dynamics across episodes as contextual information in natural language prompts is crucial for effective shaping, distinguishing it from simply observing within-episode history.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The rapid development and increasing deployment of Large Language Models (LLMs) as autonomous agents in real-world environments necessitates an understanding of their strategic behavior in multi-agent contexts. While LLM-based agents are being adopted for complex tasks such as web navigation and code generation, existing research on multi-agent LLM systems often treats these models as static entities, overlooking the strategic dynamics that emerge when agents continuously adapt to one another.

This work draws upon and extends concepts from Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL), a field concerned with the interaction of multiple learning agents in shared environments. A core challenge in MARL is that agents may treat co-players as static environmental components, which can lead to suboptimal collective outcomes (e.g., mutual defection in the Iterated Prisoner's Dilemma). Opponent shaping (OS) is a MARL paradigm designed to address this by developing agents that actively anticipate and influence co-players' learning dynamics, steering behavior towards more favorable equilibria.

However, existing OS algorithms, such as Learning with Opponent-Learning Awareness (LOLA) and Model-Free Opponent Shaping (M-FOS), are not directly applicable to transformer-based LLMs. LOLA relies on higher-order derivatives and assumes knowledge of the opponent's learning rule. M-FOS, while model-free, utilizes a dual-agent architecture that can present scalability challenges. SHAPER, a simplification of M-FOS, is inherently tied to recurrent neural network (RNN) architectures, leveraging distinct memory streams that are not directly available in transformers.

This paper addresses this gap by presenting the first investigation of opponent shaping with LLM agents. It proposes an adaptation of model-free OS methods specifically designed for transformer architectures, thereby bridging MARL's opponent shaping capabilities with the distinct characteristics and natural language processing abilities of LLMs. The work extends the understanding of LLMs beyond static decision-makers to dynamic strategic entities capable of influencing and being influenced by other learning agents.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the increasing deployment of Large Language Models (LLMs) as autonomous agents in real-world environments. As these deployments scale, interactions among multiple LLM agents become inevitable. Understanding the strategic behaviors that emerge in such multi-agent systems is crucial for both their effective and safe deployment. A central open question that motivates this work is whether LLM agents, similar to reinforcement learning agents, possess the capacity to shape the learning dynamics and influence the behavior of other agents solely through interaction.

Existing opponent shaping (OS) algorithms, effective in traditional reinforcement learning settings, are not directly transferable to LLMs due to fundamental differences in their architecture and operational mechanisms. Specifically, these algorithms often require access to higher-order derivatives, face scalability issues with complex models, or depend on architectural components (like distinct memory streams) that are absent in transformer-based LLMs. This incompatibility represents a significant gap in the current understanding of multi-agent LLM systems, as it limits the ability to explore and leverage strategic influence.

The authors highlight the dual implications of investigating opponent shaping in LLM agents. On one hand, understanding this capability is critical for identifying potential vulnerabilities: LLM agents might be susceptible to exploitation by adversaries who strategically manipulate their learning dynamics for unilateral gain. On the other hand, opponent shaping could serve as a beneficial tool for fostering prosocial behavior and enhancing coordination in multi-agent LLM systems, guiding interactions towards mutually advantageous outcomes.

In response to these motivations, the key objectives of this research are:

1. To conduct the first investigation of opponent shaping specifically within the context of LLM-based agents.
2. To propose and develop ShapeLLM, a novel model-free opponent shaping algorithm adapted for transformer architectures, which leverages structured natural language prompts to encode both historical and contextual information.
3. To empirically examine whether LLM agents, utilizing ShapeLLM, can effectively influence the learning dynamics of their co-players across a variety of game-theoretic environments.
4. To demonstrate ShapeLLM's efficacy in two distinct scenarios:
 - **Exploitative settings:** Showing that LLM agents can guide opponents toward exploitable equilibria in competitive games such as the Iterated Prisoner’s Dilemma (IPD), Matching Pennies (IMP), and Chicken Game (ICG), maximizing their individual payoffs.
 - **Cooperative settings:** Demonstrating that shaping can promote coordination and improve collective welfare in cooperative games like the Iterated Stag Hunt (ISH) and a modified cooperative version of the Prisoner’s Dilemma.

By addressing these objectives, the research aims to establish opponent shaping as a significant dimension of multi-agent LLM research, providing insights into both the risks and opportunities associated with deploying these sophisticated agents in interactive environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research centers on introducing and evaluating ShapeLLM, a model-free opponent shaping algorithm specifically designed for transformer-based LLM agents. The approach involves fine-tuning LLMs using reinforcement learning in repeated game-theoretic environments.

#### 3.1. LLM Agent and Environment Formalization

An LLM agent is defined as a system that uses an LLM for decision-making. Actions are token sequences sampled from the model's policy `ρ_θ(w_1:L | c)`. For simplicity, the authors set `L=1`, meaning agents select a single token as an action. Environments are formalized as repeated normal-form games (e.g., 2x2 matrix games) played over `T` time steps. Player actions `a_t_i` are sampled from policies `ρ_θ_i(w | f(h_t))`, where `f(h_t)` is a function of the joint action history.
To manage the mismatch between the LLM's large vocabulary and the game's small action space, a mapping `ϕ: V → A'` is used. Actions are restricted to specific tokens (e.g., "C" or "D"), with any other generation considered an `a_null` action, incurring a penalty `r_null`. Transitions involving `a_null` are excluded from game history.

#### 3.2. ShapeLLM Algorithm

ShapeLLM adapts model-free opponent shaping to transformer architectures by consolidating both "history" (intra-episode information for conditional strategies) and "context" (inter-episode information about opponent learning dynamics) into structured natural language prompts.
- **Trial Structure:** Interactions are organized into trials, each comprising `n_games` parallel environments. Within each environment, agents play `E` episodes, each consisting of `T` rounds of a matrix game.
- **Shaper's Observation:** At each round `τ`, the shaper receives an observation `c_τ_j` that concatenates the most recent joint action (`a_τ-1`) and a natural language summary of all prior joint actions within the current trial (represented as cumulative state visitation counts, e.g., "CC: 1, CD: 1, DC: 2, DD: 3"). This representation of context persists across episodes within a trial.
- **Opponent Learning:** Opponent LLM agents, referred to as "naive learners" (NL), update their policy parameters `θ_i` between episodes using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), based on the experience collected in the preceding episode. They treat the shaper as a static part of the environment.
- **Shaper Learning:** The shaper's parameters `θ_j` are updated only at the completion of an entire trial, aiming to maximize the cumulative trial return `J = Σ r_τ_j`. This longer update horizon allows the shaper to account for and influence multiple opponent updates.
The shaping task is formalized as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP) where the state includes players' parameters and conditioning prompts, and observations are functions of past joint actions.

#### 3.3. Implementation Details

- **Base Model:** `gemma-2-2b-it`, a small, instruction-tuned, open-source language model, was used for computational efficiency. The model's context window served as its memory, without additional reasoning scaffolds like Chain-of-Thought prompting.
- **Fine-tuning:** QLoRA (Dettmers et al., 2023) was employed, quantizing the base model to 4-bit precision. LoRA adapters with rank `r=2` were applied to query/value projections and the value head, managed by the PEFT library.
- **Reinforcement Learning:** A custom PPO implementation, inheriting from the TRL package, was used. A Kullback-Leibler (KL) penalty was included in the reward signal, standard in RLHF, to prevent divergence from the pre-trained model.
- **Training Parameters:** Training typically involved 200-300 epochs, with `n_games=5` parallel environments, `E=5` episodes, and `T=20` rounds per episode.
- **Hardware:** All training was performed on a single A100 GPU with 40GB of VRAM.
- **Hyperparameters:** Specific hyperparameters (e.g., learning rate, value function coefficient, clipping range) were tuned for naive learners and shapers, with some variations across games and opponent types to ensure stability (details in Appendix A.3).

#### 3.4. Experimental Settings

- **Game Environments:** Iterated versions of four canonical 2x2 games were used: Iterated Prisoner’s Dilemma (IPD), Iterated Matching Pennies (IMP), Iterated Chicken Game (ICG), and Iterated Stag Hunt (ISH). A cooperative variant of IPD (C-IPD) was also introduced where the shaper's highest payoff was mutual cooperation.
- **Baseline Configuration:** Two LLM-based naive learners (NL) were trained independently using PPO. Their conditioning prompts contained the game description and the most recent joint action.
- **Shaper vs. Naive Learner Configuration:** An LLM-based shaper interacted with an LLM naive learner (of the same configuration as the baseline). The shaper updated its parameters only at trial completion.
- **Evaluation:** Performance was evaluated by having trained agent pairs play 100 games of `T=20` rounds.
- **Robustness Experiments:**
 - **Ablation Study:** Explored whether shaping effects could be attributed solely to the shaper's enriched observation space by giving one NL intra-episode state counts.
 - **Prompt Variations:** Tested sensitivity to different prompt formulations (table-format payoff matrix, reversed action order) and the need for entropy regularization.
 - **Opponent Initialization:** Evaluated shapers against naive learners initialized with different probabilities of playing a specific action (0.75, 0.5, 0.25) to assess robustness to opponent policies.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides empirical evidence that LLM agents can engage in opponent shaping across various game-theoretic environments.

#### 4.1. Shaping in Exploitative Settings (IPD, IMP, ICG)

- **Iterated Prisoner’s Dilemma (IPD):** In baseline experiments, two naive learners converged to mutual defection, yielding an average payoff of 1 for each. In contrast, the shaper achieved an average reward of 3.96, while its naive opponent obtained 0.10. This outcome indicates successful exploitation by the shaper, surpassing typical results of strategies like Tit-for-Tat. The training dynamics showed the shaper strategically reducing its cooperation, plateauing to maintain opponent cooperation, and then gradually decreasing cooperation for maximal exploitation.
- **Iterated Matching Pennies (IMP):** The baseline players oscillated around the mixed Nash equilibrium, resulting in near-zero average payoffs (-0.03 and 0.03). With a shaper, the shaper consistently achieved a reward of 0.99, while the opponent received -0.99, demonstrating clear exploitation. State visitation converged to the two states favoring the shaper (H, H) and (T, T).
- **Iterated Chicken Game (ICG):** Baseline players averaged 2 each, with runs converging to one of the two pure Nash equilibria (Swerve, Go Straight or Go Straight, Swerve). The shaper, however, consistently achieved 2.98, limiting its opponent to 1.01. The shaper's training dynamics involved an early aggressive strategy, quickly reducing its swerving probability to force convergence to its preferred equilibrium.
- **Summary:** Across all three competitive games, the shaper consistently outperformed its opponent, demonstrating its ability to influence opponent learning dynamics toward exploitative equilibria.

#### 4.2. Robustness Against Different Opponents

Shapers maintained their exploitative capabilities against opponents initialized with varying propensities (0.75, 0.5, 0.25) to play a specific action (cooperation in IPD, heads in IMP, swerving in ICG).
- **IPD and ICG:** Opponents with initially more cooperative policies converged to less favorable outcomes, suggesting that shapers strategically adapted their behavior based on the opponent's initial policy, providing longer cooperation incentives to initially defective agents before exploitation.
- **IMP:** The shaping outcome showed no significant sensitivity to opponent initializations, with shapers achieving near-optimal rewards across all initial policy types.

#### 4.3. Ablation Study: Naive Learners with Enriched Observations

An ablation study found that providing one naive learner with enriched intra-episode observation (state counts) did not lead to shaping effects in IPD and IMP, mirroring baseline outcomes. While the ICG initially showed a skewed advantage, a control experiment with different action labels demonstrated that this was due to favorable initialization, not the enriched observations themselves. This indicated that observing opponent learning dynamics *across* episodes (as the shaper does) is crucial, rather than just within-episode history.

#### 4.4. Robustness to Prompt Variations

Experiments with different prompt formulations (table-format payoff matrix and switched-order action labels) generally confirmed ShapeLLM's robust shaping capabilities. While initial agent behaviors varied significantly with prompt changes, and entropy regularization was sometimes necessary to encourage exploration, successful shaping outcomes consistent with the main findings were observed.

#### 4.5. Shaping in Cooperative Settings (C-IPD, ISH)

- **Cooperative IPD (C-IPD):** In the baseline, both naive agents converged to mutual defection (1 each). With a shaper, the system consistently achieved mutual cooperation, yielding rewards of 5.88 for the shaper and 2.86 for the naive learner. This suggests shaping can achieve globally beneficial outcomes in environments with mixed incentives.
- **Iterated Stag Hunt (ISH):** Baseline runs primarily converged to the Pareto-inferior equilibrium (mutual Hare, average reward 1.30). When a shaper was present, runs consistently converged to the Pareto-optimal equilibrium (mutual Stag), with both agents achieving rewards of approximately 3.96. This demonstrates shaping's ability to resolve coordination failures and guide interactions toward mutually beneficial outcomes.

#### 4.6. Conclusion on Findings

The findings establish that LLM agents can both actively shape and be shaped by the learning dynamics of their interaction partners. This capability extends to competitive scenarios for unilateral gain and cooperative scenarios for collective welfare improvement.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research marks the first investigation into opponent shaping within Large Language Model (LLM) agents, demonstrating a novel capability for these systems. The findings carry significant implications for the understanding, deployment, and future development of autonomous LLM agents in multi-agent environments.

The **primary significance** is the empirical demonstration that LLM agents can strategically influence the learning dynamics of other agents through interaction alone. This extends the concept of opponent shaping, previously established in Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL), to the domain of transformer-based language models. The development of ShapeLLM provides a practical, model-free algorithm for enabling this capability, tailored to the architectural characteristics of LLMs.

**Potential impacts** are multifaceted:

1. **Vulnerability and Security:** The research highlights a potential vulnerability in future LLM agent deployments. As LLM agents increasingly operate and learn from real-world interactions, they could be susceptible to strategic exploitation by adversarial agents. An attacker might strategically influence an LLM agent's learning trajectory to induce behaviors that serve the attacker's unilateral advantage, without direct control over the target LLM. This raises concerns for the security and robustness of continually learning LLM systems and necessitates the development of defensive mechanisms.

2. **Facilitating Prosocial Behavior and Coordination:** Conversely, the same shaping mechanisms could be leveraged beneficially. In complex multi-agent LLM systems, shaping could be a tool to promote coordination, resolve conflicts, and guide interactions towards mutually beneficial or socially optimal outcomes. For instance, an LLM agent designed with prosocial objectives could shape other agents to foster cooperation, even in environments with mixed incentives or coordination challenges. This could lead to more robust and effective multi-agent LLM systems for collaborative tasks.

3. **Advancing Multi-Agent LLM Research:** This work establishes opponent shaping as a key dimension for future research in multi-agent LLM systems. It moves beyond treating LLMs as static decision-makers to considering them as dynamic entities whose learning and behavior are influenced by and exert influence on their peers. This opens new avenues for studying emergent strategic behaviors, learning dynamics, and the design of more sophisticated and adaptive LLM collectives.

4. **Directions for Future Research:** The paper identifies several promising areas for future work:
 - **Scaling and Model Capabilities:** Investigating how shaping capabilities manifest in larger LLMs and how model scale correlates with vulnerability or shaping efficacy.
 - **Natural Language Interaction:** Exploring shaping dynamics when LLM agents can communicate through natural language, allowing for more nuanced signaling, negotiation, or deception beyond fixed action tokens.
 - **Complex Environments:** Extending the study to environments with richer payoff structures, multiple objectives, or more nuanced interdependencies to better understand generalization to real-world complexities.
 - **Countermeasures:** Developing methods and architectures to make LLM agents more robust against adversarial shaping.

In conclusion, this research provides foundational evidence for opponent shaping in LLM agents, underscoring both the opportunities for designing cooperative and robust multi-agent systems and the inherent risks of exploitation that must be addressed for responsible deployment.
