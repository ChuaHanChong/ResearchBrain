# RILe: Reinforced Imitation Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> RILe (Reinforced Imitation Learning) is a novel trainer-student framework that concurrently learns an adaptive reward function and an imitation policy, overcoming the static feedback and computational bottlenecks of prior imitation learning methods. It achieves superior performance and robustness across various high-dimensional robotic control tasks, often outperforming state-of-the-art baselines under noisy conditions.

## Problem

- Traditional Reinforcement Learning often requires extensive, manual, and error-prone engineering of reward functions for complex behaviors.
- Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL) is computationally expensive due to its iterative process of policy training, behavior assessment, and reward function refinement.
- Existing Imitation Learning (IL) and Adversarial Imitation Learning (AIL) methods provide insufficient, coarse-grained feedback in high-dimensional environments, limiting effective exploration and nuanced guidance.

## Method

- **RILe** introduces a cooperative trainer-student system that jointly learns a reward function and an imitation policy within a single, integrated process.
- A "trainer" agent learns to dynamically generate state-action dependent reward signals for the "student" agent, using its own policy, guided by a discriminator's assessment of expert-likeness.
- The framework employs specific training strategies, such as freezing the trainer and using a smaller replay buffer for trainer updates, to stabilize learning and ensure rapid adaptation of the reward function.

## Results

- RILe's reward function demonstrated dynamic adaptation, providing evolving guidance to the student, unlike the static reward landscapes observed in GAIL and AIRL.
- The framework exhibited superior robustness to noise and covariate shift in expert demonstrations, outperforming GAIL by up to **470%** in tasks like Humanoid-v2 with **0.5** action noise (RILe: 5154 vs GAIL: 902).
- RILe achieved competitive or superior performance across various MuJoCo and LocoMujoco tasks, scoring 5928 on Humanoid (outperforming GAIL: 5709, AIRL: 5623) and closely approaching expert scores in challenging robotic locomotion scenarios when integrated with DRAIL (e.g., Walk UnitreeH1: **995.8** vs expert: 1000).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Dynamically generated and adaptive reward functions can provide a more effective and continuously evolving learning curriculum for agents, leading to better guidance in complex tasks.
> - Concurrently learning the reward function and policy through a reinforcement learning-based trainer-student dynamic offers computational efficiency advantages over iterative IRL methods.
> - Aligning the trainer's reward with a discriminator's output allows for context-sensitive and fine-grained reward customization, addressing the limitations of static or coarse feedback in existing imitation learning paradigms.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The acquisition of complex behaviors is a central challenge in artificial intelligence, particularly in high-dimensional environments where the search space for optimal policies is extensive. Traditional Reinforcement Learning (RL) often necessitates significant manual effort in designing reward functions, which can be time-consuming and prone to error.

Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL) emerged as an alternative, inferring reward functions from expert demonstrations. While reducing the burden of manual reward engineering, IRL typically involves an iterative process: a policy is trained, its behavior is assessed against expert demonstrations, and the reward function is subsequently refined. This iterative retraining can be computationally expensive, especially in high-dimensional settings where both the reward and policy functions explore large state-action spaces.

Imitation Learning (IL) offers a more direct approach by comparing an agent’s actions to expert demonstrations. Early IL methods, such as Behavioral Cloning (BC), directly match actions, often requiring substantial expert data. Adversarial Imitation Learning (AIL) methods, exemplified by GAIL, enhance data efficiency by employing a discriminator to evaluate the expert-likeness of learned behaviors. However, both traditional IL and AIL frequently provide insufficient feedback in high-dimensional environments. Their comparison mechanisms, whether a direct distance measure or a binary classifier, often lack the granularity to signal specific subgoals, partial improvements, or incentives for exploration. Adversarial Inverse Reinforcement Learning (AIRL) integrates a learned reward function within a discriminator to decouple it from environmental dynamics but remains tightly coupled to the discriminator's output, thereby inheriting AIL's limitations in providing fine-grained guidance.

This research introduces Reinforced Imitation Learning (RILe) to address these limitations. RILe is positioned to combine the adaptive reward learning capabilities of IRL with the computational efficiency typically associated with AIL. It aims to overcome the computational expense of IRL's iterative policy retraining and the insufficient, static feedback provided by AIL's discriminators. By proposing a dynamic, cooperative trainer-student framework, RILe seeks to provide nuanced and adaptive reward signals that evolve with the student's learning progress, particularly beneficial for complex, high-dimensional tasks where existing methods often struggle.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations behind the development of RILe are articulated in response to persistent challenges in teaching artificially intelligent agents complex behaviors:

1. **Mitigate the Burden of Manual Reward Function Engineering:** A fundamental challenge in traditional Reinforcement Learning is the necessity for extensive manual design of reward functions. This process is resource-intensive and susceptible to human error, particularly for complex tasks where defining an effective reward signal is non-trivial. RILe aims to automate or significantly reduce this manual effort by enabling the system to learn an appropriate reward function.

2. **Enhance Computational Efficiency Compared to Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL):** While IRL provides a method to infer reward functions from expert demonstrations, its iterative nature presents a computational bottleneck. IRL typically involves repeatedly training a policy, observing its behavior, and then updating the reward function. This cycle of policy retraining can be prohibitively expensive, especially in environments characterized by high dimensionality and large state-action spaces. RILe seeks to develop a more computationally efficient approach by learning the reward function and the policy concurrently within a single, integrated process, thereby avoiding the costly iterative retraining loop.

3. **Provide Fine-Grained and Adaptive Feedback in High-Dimensional Environments:** Existing Imitation Learning (IL) and Adversarial Imitation Learning (AIL) methods often fall short in high-dimensional tasks due to their reliance on coarse-grained feedback mechanisms. Traditional IL, like Behavioral Cloning, directly matches actions, demanding substantial expert data. AIL, using discriminators, primarily distinguishes expert-like from non-expert behavior. These methods typically lack the capacity to provide nuanced guidance on specific subgoals, offer incremental rewards for partial improvements, or actively encourage beneficial exploration. In high-dimensional settings, agents require more granular and adaptive feedback to effectively navigate complex learning landscapes. RILe is motivated to provide context-sensitive guidance that dynamically adjusts to the student agent's current learning stage.

4. **Decouple Reward Learning from Discriminator Limitations:** Adversarial Inverse Reinforcement Learning (AIRL) attempts to learn a reward function that is decoupled from environmental dynamics by embedding it within the discriminator's structure. However, this still results in a tight coupling of the reward function to the discriminator's output, which inherits the aforementioned limitations of AIL in providing adaptive, fine-grained feedback. RILe seeks to establish a more independent reward learning mechanism through its trainer agent, which can explore and shape rewards more strategically.

5. **Establish a Cooperative Teacher-Student Dynamic:** The motivation for RILe is also drawn from real-world learning paradigms, such as human pedagogy or animal training, where a teacher dynamically refines their teaching approach based on the student's progress. This creates a cooperative, positive-sum relationship where lessons learned from suboptimal behaviors can lead to better teaching strategies. Existing AIL and IRL methods often lack this synergistic co-evolution; AIL's discriminator acts as a competitive judge, while IRL refines rewards only after policy convergence. RILe aims to implement such a cooperative synergy where a "trainer" agent continuously learns to provide tailored reward functions as the "student" agent evolves.

By addressing these points, RILe aims to develop a framework capable of producing high-performing policies in complex, high-dimensional tasks where existing imitation learning paradigms face significant hurdles.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

RILe (Reinforced Imitation Learning) operates as a novel trainer–student system designed to jointly learn a reward function and a policy that imitates expert behavior within a unified learning process. The framework comprises three principal components: a student agent, a trainer agent, and a discriminator.

**Student Agent:**
The student agent is tasked with learning an action policy, denoted as $\pi_S$, through interaction with an environment in a standard Reinforcement Learning (RL) setting. For each action $a_S$ it takes, the environment returns a new state $s_S$. Unconventionally, the student’s reward, $r_S$, is not derived from a pre-defined function but is generated by the trainer agent's policy, $\pi_T$. Specifically, the trainer's action, $a_T$, serves as the student’s reward: $r_S = \pi_T((s_S, a_S))$. The student agent's objective is to maximize its discounted cumulative reward, as formulated by:
$\pi^*_S = \arg \max_{\pi_S} E_{(s_S,a_S)\sim\pi_S} [\sum_{t=0}^\infty \gamma^t [\pi_T((s^S_t, a^S_t))]]$

**Discriminator:**
The discriminator, parameterized by $\phi$, is a feed-forward deep neural network responsible for distinguishing between state-action pairs originating from expert demonstrations ($\tau_E$) and those generated by the student agent's policy ($\pi_S$). Its objective is to maximize its classification accuracy, defined by the following binary cross-entropy loss:
$\max_\phi E_{(s,a)\sim\tau_E}[\log(D_\phi(s,a))] + E_{(s,a)\sim\pi_S}[\log(1 - D_\phi(s,a))]$
This discriminator functions as a feedback mechanism, providing an indication of how expert-like the student’s behavior is.

**Trainer Agent:**
The trainer agent plays a central role by learning a reward function (its policy, $\pi_T$) that effectively guides the student toward expert behavior. The trainer’s environment is modeled as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP$_T$), as it observes only the student's state-action pair, $s_T = (s_S, a_S)$, but not the student's underlying policy $\pi_S$. The trainer’s action space, $A_T$, consists of scalar values, representing the reward given to the student, typically within the range [−1, 1].

A critical aspect of RILe is the trainer's own reward function, $R_T$, which evaluates the effectiveness of its guidance. This reward is designed to ensure the trainer learns to align its actions with the discriminator's assessment of expert-likeness. It is defined as:
$R_T = e^{-|\upsilon(D_\phi(s_T))-a_T|}$
where $\upsilon(x) = 2x - 1$ scales the discriminator’s output $D_\phi(s_T)$ to the range [−1, 1]. This formulation incentivizes the trainer’s action ($a_T$) to closely match the scaled discriminator output.

The trainer's objective is to maximize its own discounted cumulative reward:
$\pi^*_T = \arg \max_{\pi_T} E_{s_T\sim\pi_S, a_T\sim\pi_T} [\sum_{t=0}^\infty \gamma^t [e^{-|\upsilon(D_\phi(s^T_t))-a^T_t|}]]$.
Through this mechanism, the trainer learns to provide dynamically tailored feedback, exploring various reward designs and considering their long-term effects, which differentiates it from static or iteratively updated reward functions in other methods.

**Joint Training Process:**
RILe integrates these three components through a continuous co-evolutionary process. Both the student and trainer agents are trained simultaneously using off-policy RL methods. In each iteration:
1. The student agent generates trajectories by interacting with the environment, guided by the trainer's current reward function. These trajectories are stored in separate replay buffers ($B_D, B_S, B_T$).
2. A batch of student transitions is sampled from $B_S$, and the student's policy $\pi_S$ is updated using the rewards provided by the trainer agent's policy $\pi_T$.
3. A batch of transitions from $B_D$ (containing both expert and student data) is used to train the discriminator $D$ to differentiate between expert and student behaviors.
4. A batch of student transitions is sampled from $B_T$, and the trainer agent's policy $\pi_T$ is updated. The trainer's reward $R_T$ for these updates is derived from the discriminator's output and the trainer's own actions.

**Training Strategies (Appendix C):**
To manage the instabilities inherent in a dynamically evolving reward function, RILe employs specific training strategies:
1. **Freezing the Trainer Agent Midway:** To prevent the trainer from overfitting to minor fluctuations in student behavior and issuing inappropriate negative rewards in later stages, the trainer agent is frozen once its critic network converges. This stabilizes learning by halting further adaptation.
2. **Utilizing a Smaller Buffer for the Trainer Agent:** The trainer agent uses a smaller replay buffer compared to the student. This ensures the trainer primarily learns from recent student interactions, allowing its reward function to adapt more rapidly to the evolving student policy, thereby providing timely and relevant feedback.
3. **Increasing the Student Agent’s Exploration:** The student agent's exploration rate (e.g., via an epsilon-greedy strategy) is augmented relative to standard AIL methods. This broader exploration exposes the trainer to a more diverse set of state-action pairs, which is crucial for the trainer to learn a robust and effective reward function that captures the effects of various actions.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of RILe encompassed a series of ablation studies and performance comparisons across various learning-from-demonstration benchmarks.

**Ablation Studies:**

1. **Reward Function Evaluation (Maze Environment - Figure 3):**
 RILe demonstrated a dynamic reward function that adapted to the student's progress. Early in training (25% completion), the trainer encouraged exploration in initially suboptimal areas (e.g., bottom-right of the maze). As the student progressed, the high-reward regions shifted to guide the student incrementally towards the expert trajectory (e.g., top-left, then near the goal), effectively creating a learning curriculum. In contrast, GAIL and AIRL exhibited comparatively static reward landscapes throughout training, showing limited adaptation to the student's evolving policy.

2. **Reward Function Dynamics (High-dimensional Robotic Control - Figure 4):**
- **Reward Function Distribution Change (RFDC):** RILe exhibited the highest adaptability in its reward function distribution over consecutive 10,000-step training intervals, indicating continuous adjustment. DRAIL-RILe also showed high adaptability, while GAIL was less adaptive.
- **Fixed-State Reward Function Distribution Change (FS-RFDC):** For a fixed set of expert states, RILe’s reward function displayed the most significant changes over time, suggesting that the underlying reward function itself adapts, not solely due to shifts in the student's policy.
- **Correlation between Performance and Reward (CPR):** DRAIL-RILe achieved the highest positive Pearson correlation between changes in the learned reward function and student performance improvements. RILe ranked second, indicating that its trainer agent effectively guides the student. GAIL's correlation was initially positive but became negative, suggesting its discriminator-based reward signal saturates and becomes less informative as training progresses.

3. **Trainer-Discriminator Relation (Figure 5):**
 An investigation of six different trainer reward functions revealed that while the "Exponential Naive" reward function led to the fastest convergence, the "Exponential Difference" reward function (default in RILe) yielded the highest overall performance in terms of final mean test reward.

4. **Robustness to Noise and Covariate Shift (MuJoCo Humanoid-v2):**
- **Noise in Expert Demonstrations (Table 1):** RILe consistently outperformed baseline methods (GAIL, AIRL, RIL-Co, IC-GAIL, IQ-Learn) under varying levels of Gaussian noise in expert actions and states. For instance, with $\Sigma=0.5$ noise in expert actions, RILe achieved an average score of 5154, significantly higher than GAIL (902) and AIRL (4589).
- **Covariate Shift (Table 2):** When reward functions were learned in a clean environment and then frozen to train new student agents in noisy environments (covariate shift), RILe's learned reward function demonstrated superior robustness. Under high action noise ($\Sigma=0.5$), RILe's frozen reward function resulted in a student performance of 5196, compared to AIRL's 4967.

5. **Explicit Usage of Expert Data inside RILe (MuJoCo Humanoid - Figure 6):**
 Increasing the proportion of expert data in the replay buffers accelerated RILe's convergence. However, excessive expert data (e.g., 100% expert data) led to a marked reduction in the final performance, indicating that it can hinder the trainer's real-time adaptation and context-sensitive reward customization. Despite this, RILe still outperformed baselines like IQ-Learn and BC, which rely heavily on expert data.

**Performance on Benchmark Tasks:**

1. **Learning from Expert Demonstrations (MuJoCo Tasks - Table 3):**
 RILe achieved competitive or superior performance across four standard MuJoCo continuous control tasks. Notably, in the high-dimensional Humanoid task, RILe scored 5928, outperforming GAIL (5709), AIRL (5623), and IQ (327).

2. **Robotic Continuous Control from Motion Capture Data (LocoMujoco Tasks - Table 4):**
 In challenging, high-dimensional robotic locomotion tasks (imitation from noisy state-only motion capture data), RILe consistently outperformed AIL/IL/IRL baselines. For example, in "Walk Atlas", RILe achieved a score of 870.6, surpassing GAIL (792.7) and AIRL (300.5). With the integration of DRAIL enhancements (DRAIL-RILe), performance levels often approached expert scores, such as 995.8 for "Walk UnitreeH1" (expert: 1000) and 896.6 for "Walk Talos" (expert: 1000). The results generally indicated that RILe's adaptive reward function is effective for mastering complex, high-dimensional behaviors.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of RILe (Reinforced Imitation Learning) presents a notable advancement in the field of imitation learning, particularly in its approach to learning complex behaviors in high-dimensional settings. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Addressing Core Limitations in Imitation Learning:** RILe directly addresses critical shortcomings of existing methods. It mitigates the computational expense associated with iterative policy retraining in Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL) and overcomes the issue of insufficient, coarse-grained feedback prevalent in traditional Imitation Learning (IL) and Adversarial Imitation Learning (AIL). By integrating an adaptive reward function that evolves with the student's progress, RILe provides a more effective and efficient learning mechanism.

2. **Introduction of an Adaptive and Dynamic Reward Function:** The core innovation of RILe lies in its trainer-student framework, where a trainer agent learns to dynamically generate a reward function. This adaptive reward function acts as a curriculum, providing context-sensitive guidance that encourages beneficial exploration and gradual improvement, rather than solely punishing deviations from expert behavior. This is particularly impactful for tasks requiring intricate motor skills or long-horizon planning, where static or simple distance-based rewards are often inadequate.

3. **Demonstrated Superior Performance and Robustness:** RILe consistently exhibited competitive or superior performance compared to state-of-the-art baselines across a variety of imitation learning benchmarks, including challenging high-dimensional robotic locomotion tasks and standard MuJoCo environments. The framework also demonstrated enhanced robustness to noisy expert demonstrations and environmental covariate shifts, indicating its capacity for reliable deployment in less controlled, real-world scenarios.

4. **Enhanced Efficiency in Reward Learning:** By training the reward function concurrently with the policy through reinforcement learning, RILe avoids the computationally intensive, iterative cycles of policy optimization and reward refinement that characterize traditional IRL methods. This simultaneous learning process contributes to a more efficient overall training pipeline.

5. **Paving the Way for More Complex Behavior Acquisition:** The ability of RILe to learn nuanced and dynamic reward signals holds the potential for agents to acquire a broader range of complex and sophisticated behaviors. This could lead to breakthroughs in areas such as realistic character animation, advanced robotics for intricate manipulation, and autonomous systems operating in dynamic environments.

6. **Future Research Directions:** The paper acknowledges existing challenges, such as maintaining policy stability with rapidly evolving rewards and preventing discriminator overfitting. These challenges point to promising avenues for future research, including exploring cooperative multi-agent reinforcement learning for continuous adaptation and developing discriminator-less formulations for reward learning, which could further improve the stability and generalization capabilities of such frameworks.

In conclusion, RILe proposes a paradigm shift towards a more cooperative and dynamically adaptive approach to imitation learning. By fostering a synergistic relationship between reward learning and policy optimization, it offers a promising direction for developing intelligent agents capable of learning intricate behaviors with greater efficiency and robustness.
