# One-Step Flow Q-Learning: Addressing the Diffusion Policy Bottleneck in Offline Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at KAIST developed One-Step Flow Q-Learning (OFQL), an offline reinforcement learning framework that replaces multi-step diffusion policies with an efficient one-step average velocity field. OFQL delivers superior performance on D4RL benchmarks, reducing training time by up to 87% and increasing inference speed by over 250% compared to Diffusion Q-Learning, without compromising policy expressivity.

## Problem

- Diffusion Q-Learning (DQL), a leading offline RL method, relies on multi-step denoising for action generation, resulting in high computational demands and slow inference speeds.
- DQL's policy updates involve backpropagation through time (BPTT) across the entire denoising chain, which can lead to numerical instability and hinder convergence to optimal solutions.
- Existing approaches to accelerate DQL often introduce additional architectural complexity, multi-phase training, or trade-offs in performance or scalability.

## Method

- **OFQL** reformulates the policy learning component of DQL by adopting a ==Flow Matching== (FM) paradigm, which learns a continuous-time velocity field to transport noise to data.
- To enable accurate one-step action generation, OFQL models an *average velocity field* instead of the instantaneous velocity, addressing the practical curvature issues of conventional FM trajectories for single-step inference.
- The one-step average-velocity policy is integrated into a behavior-regularized actor-critic framework, allowing for direct, differentiable action sampling during both training and inference without multi-step denoising or BPTT.

## Results

- OFQL achieved superior average normalized scores on D4RL benchmarks (e.g., 92.5 on MuJoCo, 84.6 on AntMaze, 67.0 on Kitchen), outperforming DQL and other diffusion-based baselines.
- Training time was drastically reduced, with OFQL completing training in 6.3 hours compared to 11.7 hours for 5-step DQL and 49.5 hours for 50-step DQL on MuJoCo tasks.
- Inference speed (decision frequency) increased significantly to **846.5 Hz**, which is over **250%** faster than 5-step DQL (**238.7 Hz**) and substantially faster than 50-step DQL (**35.5 Hz**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Learning an *average velocity field* from noise to data allows for highly accurate one-step action generation, overcoming the limitations of instantaneous velocity fields in Flow Matching for single-step inference.
> - Eliminating multi-step denoising and backpropagation through time (BPTT) significantly boosts computational efficiency, leading to faster training and substantially higher inference speeds for expressive policies.
> - It is possible to achieve competitive or superior offline RL performance with a simpler, direct one-step policy formulation, avoiding the need for auxiliary models, policy distillation, or multi-stage training procedures.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Offline Reinforcement Learning (Offline RL) focuses on learning policies from pre-collected, static datasets without further interaction with the environment. Early approaches in Offline RL often employed unimodal Gaussian policies, which struggled to capture complex, multi-modal action distributions prevalent in diverse datasets. The integration of generative models, particularly diffusion models, has advanced Offline RL by enabling policies to model these complex distributions more effectively.

Diffusion Q-Learning (DQL) emerged as a prominent method, replacing conventional Gaussian policies with Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPMs) within a behavior-regularized actor-critic framework. DQL demonstrated substantial performance improvements over prior methods and has remained a competitive baseline, often outperforming more recent diffusion-based planning and policy optimization techniques.

Despite its performance, DQL presented practical limitations. Its reliance on multi-step denoising for action generation led to high computational demands during both training and inference. Additionally, the training process for DQL involved backpropagation through time (BPTT) across the entire denoising chain, which could amplify computational load and contribute to optimization fragility, potentially hindering convergence to optimal solutions.

Prior research attempted to mitigate these issues through various strategies:
- **Efficient Denoising Solvers:** Approaches like Kang et al. (2023) focused on reducing the number of denoising steps. However, direct application for one-step generation often led to significant performance degradation.
- **Implicit Q-Learning (IQL)-based Methods:** Hansen-Estruch et al. (2023) bypassed DQL's BPTT by training a diffusion policy to clone behavior, reweighted by a separately learned IQL-based value function. These methods typically offered less effectiveness compared to actor-critic learning frameworks.
- **Auxiliary Policies and Policy Distillation:** Methods by Park et al. (2025) and Chen et al. (2023; 2024) involved training an auxiliary policy or distilling a multi-step diffusion/flow model into a one-step policy. While achieving one-step inference, these solutions introduced additional complexity, multi-phase training procedures, or trade-offs in scalability and policy quality.

The presented work, One-Step Flow Q-Learning (OFQL), aims to address these limitations directly by designing a truly one-step policy for action generation that operates efficiently during both training and inference. It seeks to achieve this without the need for auxiliary models, policy distillation, or multi-stage training, thereby offering a simpler and more robust solution that also maintains or improves policy expressivity and performance. This positions OFQL as an attempt to overcome the diffusion policy bottleneck in offline RL by fundamentally rethinking the policy parameterization using a specific form of Flow Matching.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a novel offline reinforcement learning framework, One-Step Flow Q-Learning (OFQL), that enables effective and efficient one-step action generation. This objective extends to both the training and inference phases of the policy learning process.

The motivation for this work stems from the identified practical limitations of Diffusion Q-Learning (DQL), which include:
- **High Computational Demands:** DQL's core policy, based on Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPMs), requires multiple denoising steps (K steps) to generate a single action. This iterative process makes both inference and training computationally intensive. During inference, each action requires K denoising steps. During training, the critic loss requires sampling target actions (each needing K steps), and the actor loss also requires sampling current actions (again, K steps).
- **Slow Inference Speed:** The multi-step nature of DDPMs inherently limits the decision frequency of DQL, making it less suitable for applications requiring real-time, high-frequency control.
- **Optimization Fragility and Suboptimal Convergence:** The policy update in DQL utilizes the reparameterization trick to backpropagate gradients through the entire K-step denoising chain. This Backpropagation Through Time (BPTT) through a long stochastic computation graph can lead to numerical instability and potentially impede convergence to optimal solutions.
- **Complexity and Trade-offs of Existing Solutions:** While prior attempts have tried to accelerate DQL (e.g., efficient solvers, IQL-based methods, policy distillation), these often introduce additional architectural components, multi-phase training schedules, or result in compromises in terms of simplicity, scalability, or overall policy performance. The paper highlights that it remains unclear whether a one-step policy could be trained directly without such trade-offs.

Therefore, the key motivation is to devise a direct and principled approach that eliminates DQL's computational bottlenecks and training instabilities by fundamentally enabling single-step action generation. This involves moving away from the iterative denoising process of DDPMs and the associated recursive gradient propagation, aiming for a simpler, faster, and more robust training and inference pipeline while preserving or enhancing policy expressivity.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

OFQL reformulates the policy learning component of DQL within the Flow Matching (FM) paradigm, but with a specific modification to enable one-step action generation. The methodology addresses the limitations of multi-step diffusion policies and the inherent curvature of standard Flow Matching trajectories.

#### 3.1. Reformulating Policy with Flow Matching

Conventional DQL policies rely on DDPMs, which generate actions through a reverse diffusion process involving multiple iterative denoising steps. OFQL shifts this to a Flow Matching approach, which aims to learn a continuous-time velocity field that deterministically transports noise to data.
- **Linear Flow Paths:** Given a clean action `a` (from the dataset) and Gaussian noise `ϵ`, Flow Matching defines a linear path `a_t = (1-t)a + tϵ`, where `t ∈`. The instantaneous conditional velocity along this path is `v_t = ϵ - a`.
- **Conditional Flow Matching (CFM) Loss:** Standard FM learns a marginal velocity field `v_θ(a_t, t; s)` parameterized by a neural network, using the loss `L_CFM(θ) = E[∥v_θ(a_t, t; s) - v_t(a_t | a; s)∥^2]`. Once trained, action sampling requires solving an ODE `da_t/dt = v(a_t, t; s)` iteratively, starting from noise `a_1 = ϵ`.

#### 3.2. Addressing One-Step Limitation of Conventional Flow Matching

The paper notes that conventional Flow Matching, despite its "straight-through" design intention, often results in curved trajectories in practice, particularly for complex distributions. This curvature limits the accuracy of one-step inference, as a single large step (∆t=1) with the instantaneous marginal velocity field `v(a_t, t; s)` cannot reliably connect the noise to the clean action.

#### 3.3. Learning an Average Velocity Field

To enable accurate one-step generation, OFQL departs from modeling the instantaneous (marginal) velocity and instead proposes to model an *average velocity field*.
- **Definition of Average Velocity:** The average velocity `u(a_t, r, t; s)` over an interval `[r, t]` (where `0 ≤ r ≤ t ≤ 1`) is defined as the integral of the instantaneous velocity `v(a_τ, τ; s)` over that interval, divided by the duration `(t-r)` (Eq. 10). This represents the total displacement across the interval.
- **One-Step Action Generation:** By learning `u_θ`, actions can be generated in a single step using an approximate endpoint map: `a = T_θ(ϵ, s) = ϵ - u_θ(ϵ, r=0, t=1; s)`, where `ϵ ∼ N(0, I)` (Eq. 12). This design eliminates iterative ODE integration and the associated discretization errors and computational overhead.

#### 3.4. Practical Loss Formulation via MeanFlow Identity

Directly optimizing `u` from its integral definition (Eq. 10) is computationally intractable. OFQL adopts the MeanFlow Identity (Geng et al., 2025) which reformulates the average velocity as:
`u(a_t, r, t; s) = v(a_t, t; s) - (t-r) * d/dt u(a_t, r, t; s)` (Eq. 13).
This identity links the average velocity `u` to the instantaneous velocity `v` and the total time derivative of `u`. The total derivative `d/dt u` is computed efficiently using Jacobian-vector products.
- **Average-Velocity Matching Loss:** The neural network `u_θ` is trained to approximate this `u_tgt` using the `L_FBC(θ)` loss (Eq. 14): `E[∥u_θ(a_t, r, t; s) - sg(u_tgt)∥^2]`, where `sg(·)` denotes the stop-gradient operator. The `v_t` in the `u_tgt` calculation is approximated by `ϵ - a`, consistent with CFM.
- **Flow Ratio:** During training, a "flow ratio" hyperparameter determines the probability that `t=r`. This mechanism biases training toward learning the instantaneous velocity when `t=r`, while still allowing for regression to the average velocity when `t≠r`, which is stated to improve bootstrapping and stability.

#### 3.5. One-Step Flow Q-Learning (OFQL) Algorithm

OFQL integrates this one-step, average-velocity policy `u_θ` into the behavior-regularized actor-critic framework.
- **Policy Sampling:** Action sampling `a ∼ π_θ(· | s)` is a single differentiable operation: `a = ϵ - u_θ(ϵ, r=0, t=1; s)`.
- **Critic Loss:** The Q-network `Q_ϕ` is trained using the standard Bellman error (Eq. 6), but the target actions `a'` are now generated by the one-step policy `π_θ'`.
- **Actor Loss:** The actor `u_θ` is trained by minimizing `L(θ) = L_FBC(θ) - α E[Q_ϕ(s, a)]` (Eq. 17). The `L_FBC(θ)` term provides behavior regularization by encouraging the learned one-step policy to stay close to the data distribution, while the `Q_ϕ` term guides policy towards higher returns. The coefficient `α` balances these two objectives and is adapted based on the Q-value scale.

This methodology eliminates the need for multi-step denoising in both training and inference and removes the computationally expensive and potentially unstable backpropagation through time, leading to a faster and more robust learning process.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted on the D4RL benchmark suite demonstrate that One-Step Flow Q-Learning (OFQL) achieves enhanced performance and efficiency compared to existing offline RL methods, including multi-step diffusion policies and other one-step approaches.

#### 4.1. Performance on D4RL Benchmarks

OFQL consistently achieved competitive or superior normalized scores across various tasks in the MuJoCo, AntMaze, and Kitchen domains.
- **MuJoCo Locomotion Tasks:** OFQL improved the average performance from 87.9 (DQL) to 92.5, marking a notable gain, particularly on "medium" and "medium-replay" tasks which contain suboptimal and noisy data. This improvement is attributed to OFQL's expressive policy modeling for complex action distributions and more stable value estimation due to the avoidance of Backpropagation Through Time (BPTT).
- **AntMaze Navigation Tasks:** OFQL substantially outperformed DQL (64.6 to 84.6) and other diffusion-based baselines, as well as the one-step FQL. This domain benefits significantly from accurate and stable Q-value guidance, suggesting that OFQL's avoidance of BPTT in Q-learning contributes to its strong performance.
- **Kitchen Manipulation Tasks:** OFQL surpassed DQL (61.6 to 67.0), achieving the strongest performance among all methods. This result indicates that OFQL's one-step formulation preserves sufficient policy expressivity without degradation.
- **High-Dimensional Action Spaces (Adroit, Appendix J):** OFQL consistently outperformed Behavior Cloning (BC) and DQL in high-dimensional dexterous manipulation tasks (e.g., `adroit-pen-human`, `adroit-pen-cloned`), demonstrating robustness in complex control settings.

#### 4.2. Efficiency Comparison

- **Training Time:** OFQL exhibited substantially reduced training time. For 1 million steps on MuJoCo tasks, OFQL completed training in 6.3 hours, whereas 5-step DQL required 11.7 hours, and 50-step DQL took 49.5 hours. OFQL also showed shorter training times than FQL, despite FQL achieving comparable inference speed, because FQL still relies on multi-step computations for distillation targets.
- **Inference Speed (Decision Frequency):** OFQL achieved a decision frequency of 846.5 Hz, which is significantly faster than 5-step DQL (238.7 Hz) and 50-step DQL (35.5 Hz). This demonstrates a substantial improvement in real-time action generation capability.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies

- **Strategies Toward One-Step Prediction:** A comparison of different strategies for one-step action generation on MuJoCo tasks (Table 2) showed:
 - DQL (5 steps): 87.9
 - DQL+DDIM (1 step inference): 11.6 (severe degradation)
 - FBRAC (Flow policy, 1 step inference): 67.1 (lags behind DQL)
 - FQL (Distilled one-step policy): 79.2 (narrows the gap)
 - OFQL (1 step training & inference): 92.6 (outperforms DQL)
 This indicates that direct training for one-step generation, as implemented in OFQL, is superior to applying efficient samplers post-hoc or using distillation-based methods.
- **Flow Ratio:** Varying the flow ratio (probability `t=r`) on HalfCheetah datasets (Table 3) showed that a ratio of 0.5 yielded optimal performance across different data regimes (e.g., 95.2 on Medium Expert), suggesting a beneficial balance between learning instantaneous and average velocities. Extreme values (0 or 1) resulted in performance degradation.
- **Marginal vs. Average Velocity Parameterization (Toy Datasets):** Experiments on synthetic datasets (Crescent, Spiral, Checkerboard) demonstrated that the average velocity parameterization (u-param) in OFQL achieved strong mode coverage and alignment with target distributions in a single step (Figures 4, 6, 7). In contrast, marginal velocity parameterization (v-param) in Flow Matching and DDPMs required multiple steps for comparable quality, and v-param often produced collapsed samples with fewer steps. This provides evidence for the expressiveness of OFQL's one-step formulation.
- **Generalization to Out-of-Distribution (OOD) States (Appendix L):** OFQL consistently achieved lower Out-of-Distribution Mean Squared Error (OOD-MSE) than DQL on HalfCheetah datasets, suggesting improved generalization capabilities to unseen states.
- **Feasibility on Visual Observation (Appendix K):** While OFQL was functional in visual manipulation tasks (OGBench), its performance lagged behind stronger visual baselines. This indicates that while the core mechanism works, specific architectural and algorithmic considerations (e.g., robust encoders, structured feature fusion) are necessary for competitive results in high-dimensional pixel-based domains.

Overall, the findings indicate that OFQL successfully addresses the identified bottlenecks of diffusion policies in offline RL, delivering substantial improvements in both computational efficiency and policy performance, without introducing additional system complexity.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of One-Step Flow Q-Learning (OFQL) represents a significant advancement in offline reinforcement learning, primarily by addressing a fundamental bottleneck in diffusion-based policies. The work demonstrates that it is feasible to achieve high-performance and efficient policy learning without compromising on simplicity or expressivity.

The significance and potential impact of OFQL include:

- **Resolution of the Diffusion Policy Bottleneck:** By enabling truly one-step action generation during both training and inference, OFQL directly resolves the computational inefficiency and training instability associated with multi-step denoising and recursive gradient propagation (BPTT) in Diffusion Q-Learning (DQL). This makes diffusion-like expressive policies more practical for real-world applications.
- **Enhanced Practicality and Scalability:** The substantial reduction in training time and significant increase in inference speed make OFQL a more practical solution for deploying offline RL agents. This efficiency lowers the computational barrier, potentially enabling the application of reinforcement learning to larger datasets and more complex tasks that were previously limited by computational resources.
- **State-of-the-Art Performance without Compromise:** OFQL demonstrates that efficiency gains do not necessitate a trade-off in performance. By achieving state-of-the-art results across various D4RL benchmarks while simultaneously reducing computational overhead, OFQL presents a superior balance of effectiveness and efficiency.
- **Foundation for Real-time Control and Latency-Sensitive Domains:** The capability for high-frequency decision-making (over 800 Hz) positions OFQL as a promising candidate for real-time control applications. This has direct implications for fields such as robotics, autonomous driving, and other latency-sensitive systems where rapid and accurate decision-making is critical.
- **Simpler and More Robust Learning Pipeline:** OFQL's design eliminates the need for auxiliary modules, policy distillation, or multi-stage training procedures often found in other acceleration efforts. This simplified and more robust learning pipeline contributes to easier implementation, debugging, and broader applicability.
- **New Avenues for Research:** OFQL provides a general foundation for fast and expressive policy learning. The work opens promising directions for future research, including:
 - **Online Reinforcement Learning:** The one-step formulation naturally extends to online settings, where real-time interaction demands high decision frequencies.
 - **High-Dimensional Observation Spaces:** Adapting OFQL for visual or point-cloud-based control, which involves high-dimensional observations, presents a significant challenge but also a high-impact area for future work.
 - **Goal-Conditioned and Multi-Task RL:** Extending the concept of learning conditional average velocity fields to support diverse goal-directed behaviors and multiple tasks could lead to more flexible and generalizable agents.

In summary, OFQL contributes a significant step towards making advanced, expressive policy learning accessible and efficient for a wider range of challenging real-world offline reinforcement learning problems.
