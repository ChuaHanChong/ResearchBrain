# From Perception to Action: An Interactive Benchmark for Vision Reasoning

> [!summary] Summary
> The Causal Hierarchy of Actions and Interactions (CHAIN) benchmark introduces an interactive 3D, physics-driven testbed for evaluating AI's physical reasoning capabilities, revealing that state-of-the-art vision-language models achieve an overall success rate of only 22.9% and often struggle with constraint-aware, multi-step problem-solving. It further demonstrates that current world models systematically fail to maintain physical consistency during structured interactions.

## Problem

- Existing Vision-Language Model (VLM) evaluations primarily rely on static, single-turn setups, failing to assess dynamic, multi-step physical reasoning about geometry, contact, and support relations.
- Current evaluations for diffusion-based models are largely confined to simplified 2D environments, neglecting the complexities of 3D geometry and multi-body physical constraints in problem-solving.
- A lack of interactive benchmarks to rigorously test AI's capacity to understand, plan, and execute structured action sequences inherently grounded in 3D physical constraints.

## Method

- Introducing **CHAIN**, an interactive ==3D physics-driven benchmark== comprising **109 distinct levels** across two task families: ==interlocking mechanical puzzles== and ==3D stacking/packing==.
- Utilizing a ==closed-loop evaluation protocol== where an agent iteratively observes, selects actions from a predefined space, and receives environmental feedback until task completion or a step budget is reached.
- Implementing environments using ==Unity== for complex puzzles and a lightweight 3D Python engine for stacking tasks, with a uniform, color-hinted interaction interface and ==multi-view observations==.

## Results

- The best-performing VLM, **GPT-5.2**, achieved an overall Pass@1 of **22.9%**, with significantly lower success on Puzzle tasks (**0.0-3.1%**) compared to Stacking tasks (**10.4-31.2%**).
- Performance on Stacking tasks degraded with increasing difficulty (e.g., GPT-5.2 achieved **100%** for easy but **6.3%** for hard), while Puzzle tasks remained challenging even at "easy" levels (peak **10.0%**).
- Stronger models achieved higher success rates but often showed increased plan inefficiency (more steps, higher Dist2Opt) and consequently higher token and cost consumption per solved task.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Current state-of-the-art VLMs consistently struggle with interactive 3D physical reasoning, particularly with interlocking mechanical puzzles, demonstrating a limited capacity to infer and utilize hidden structural constraints.
> - Interaction and iterative feedback are crucial for improving performance, with the interactive setting yielding substantially higher accuracy than one-shot planning, indicating models leverage feedback to adapt plans.
> - Contemporary world models (video generation models) exhibit catastrophic failures in structured physical interactions, violating fundamental physics, suffering from representational collapse, and lacking object permanence.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper positions itself at the intersection of Vision-Language Models (VLMs), embodied AI, and physical reasoning, addressing critical limitations in existing evaluation paradigms.

**Limitations of Current VLM Evaluations:**
The prevailing method for evaluating VLMs, such as Visual Question Answering (VQA), primarily relies on **static, single-turn setups**. These benchmarks assess a model's ability to passively observe an image and provide a textual answer, often focusing on factual grounding and recognition (Agrawal et al., 2016; Lu et al., 2022). However, they **fall short in evaluating agents' capacity to reason about dynamic environments**, where geometry, contact, and support relations jointly constrain possible actions over multiple steps. This static approach fails to test sequential planning, adaptation to evolving constraints, and the crucial aspect of how early actions impact subsequent possibilities, thereby underestimating the complexity of real-world physical reasoning (Cheng et al., 2025).

**Challenges with Diffusion-Based Models:**
While diffusion models have emerged as powerful tools for generative tasks, their evaluation in the context of reasoning and planning has largely been confined to **simplified 2D environments**. This avoids the inherent complexities of 3D geometry, intricate contact constraints, and multi-body support relations that are pervasive in real-world physical interactions (Taufeeque et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025; Luo et al., 2025). Consequently, their true capabilities in complex 3D physical problem-solving remain largely unassessed.

**Embodied AI and Physical Reasoning Benchmarks:**
The broader field of embodied AI and robotics heavily relies on an agent's ability to interact with and understand its physical environment. Current benchmarks in this space often emphasize specific manipulation skills (Gu et al., 2023) or commonsense physical reasoning, but still frequently operate under static observations or simplified dynamics (Riochet et al., 2018; Rajani et al., 2020; Yi et al., 2019). While some progress has been made in multi-stage supervision and reinforcement learning, these are frequently validated on static benchmarks, not truly interactive scenarios (Xu et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025; Kang et al., 2025).

**3D Structure Perception:**
Foundational work in 3D structure perception has enabled models to infer depth and relative positioning from multi-view inputs or point clouds (Chen et al., 2024; Lyu et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024a). However, these efforts predominantly focus on static scene reconstruction or snapshot reasoning (Zhao et al., 2025; Lu et al., 2025; Ma et al., 2026). They do not adequately assess how spatial configurations dynamically evolve through interaction or how actions induce causal changes over time.

**The Contribution of CHAIN:**
The "Causal Hierarchy of Actions and Interactions" (CHAIN) benchmark directly addresses these gaps by shifting the evaluation paradigm from passive perception to **active, closed-loop problem-solving in dynamic, physics-driven 3D environments.** It provides a crucial testbed for assessing how well models can integrate visual understanding, plan multi-step actions, and adapt to evolving physical constraints, bridging the gap between "seeing" and "acting" in physically meaningful ways. This benchmark pushes the boundaries of VLM and world model evaluation towards more realistic and complex interactive intelligence, critical for advancements in areas like embodied agents, interactive design, and long-horizon robotic manipulation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation behind introducing the CHAIN benchmark stems from the limitations of current AI evaluation methodologies in assessing truly interactive and physically grounded reasoning capabilities. The authors highlight that prevailing Vision-Language Model (VLM) evaluations are predominantly confined to static, single-turn tasks like Visual Question Answering (VQA). Such setups, while useful for diagnostic purposes, fail to capture the dynamic, constraint-driven nature of real-world physical problem-solving. In interactive scenarios, an agent must not only perceive the environment but also reason about the interplay of geometry, contact, and support relations to determine feasible actions, plan sequential steps, and adapt to visual feedback as the environment evolves. Existing evaluations, including those for diffusion-based models, largely sidestep the complexities introduced by 3D geometry and physical constraints, often relying on simplified 2D environments. This leaves a significant gap in understanding whether state-of-the-art models can genuinely solve structured 3D physical problems.

To address this critical deficiency, the **key objectives** of the CHAIN benchmark are:

1. **To introduce an interactive 3D, physics-driven testbed:** CHAIN is designed to rigorously evaluate whether AI models can understand, plan, and execute structured action sequences that are inherently grounded in physical constraints. This moves beyond passive observation to active, multi-step problem-solving.
2. **To assess constraint-aware reasoning:** The benchmark specifically targets the evaluation of how models reason about geometric constraints, contact-induced dependencies, and multi-body support relations that dictate the feasibility of actions in a dynamic environment.
3. **To span diverse physical reasoning tasks:** CHAIN includes two distinct task families—interlocking mechanical puzzles and 3D stacking/packing—each designed to stress complementary aspects of structured physical reasoning, from fine-grained manipulation with hidden constraints to long-horizon spatial planning.
4. **To establish reproducible baselines and identify limitations:** By conducting a comprehensive empirical study of state-of-the-art VLMs and diffusion-based models under unified interactive settings, the authors aim to highlight persistent limitations in their ability to internalize physical structure, maintain coherent multi-step strategies, and translate perceived structure into effective actions.
5. **To catalyze progress towards physically grounded interactive agents:** Ultimately, CHAIN serves as a challenging, open-source benchmark to drive future research towards developing more robust, structure-aware, and interactive AI agents capable of operating effectively in the physical world.

In essence, the authors are motivated to create a more realistic and demanding evaluation standard that can truly push the boundaries of AI's physical reasoning capabilities, moving beyond simple recognition to deep, interactive understanding and manipulation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The CHAIN benchmark introduces a novel, interactive, and physics-driven approach to evaluating AI models, moving beyond static perception tasks. Its methodology encompasses careful task design, a robust construction pipeline, and comprehensive evaluation metrics.

**Benchmark Design: CHAIN (Causal Hierarchy of Actions and Interactions)**
CHAIN is an interactive 3D benchmark built upon a physics-engine-driven environment, allowing models to iteratively observe, select feasible actions, and revise plans. It comprises 109 distinct interactive levels across two task families:

1. **Puzzle (Interlocking Mechanical Structures):** This family includes 32 task instances (10 easy, 12 medium, 10 hard), drawing inspiration from classic designs like Kongming and Lu Ban locks. The core challenge lies in reasoning about mechanical constraints (kinematic feasibility, collision avoidance, force directions, component dependencies) to assemble or disassemble multi-piece structures. Tasks are inherently long-horizon and causally structured, requiring precise structural understanding and sequential manipulation (e.g., pick, rotate, insert). Difficulty is stratified based on the number of pieces, complexity of interlocks, and the length of the required disassembly sequence.

2. **Stacking (3D Spatial Packing):** Consisting of 77 task instances (10 easy, 20 medium, 47 hard), this family focuses on spatial packing problems where agents must place diverse geometric blocks into a fixed container. It primarily evaluates three-dimensional spatial reasoning, requiring joint consideration of shape compatibility, orientation constraints, and how early placements restrict remaining free space. Difficulty is programmatically determined by container size, number of objects, and block shape complexity, allowing for scalable testing of long-horizon spatial planning and constraint satisfaction.

**Benchmark Construction Pipeline:**
The benchmark is constructed through a three-step pipeline designed to ensure multi-step causal dependencies, controllable environments, and accurate evaluation:

1. **Finding Suitable Puzzles:** Candidate puzzles are sourced (e.g., from Puzzlemaster) and filtered based on three criteria:
 - **Chain Dependency:** Puzzles must require sequential reasoning where early actions causally influence later ones.
 - **Feasibility:** Puzzles must be reliably modelable with controllable action spaces and stable state transitions, and their difficulty must be simulatable and informative.
 - **Human Difficulty Check:** Human experts play each candidate puzzle, and their completion times are used to assign difficulty labels (easy: <5 min, medium: 5-15 min, hard: >15 min). Stacking tasks are programmatically generated, but their difficulty tiers are also validated by human solve times.

2. **Building Physical Environments:** The environments are implemented using two complementary toolchains:
 - **Unity:** Used for complex interlocking puzzles, offering precise control over kinematic constraints and contact interactions.
 - **Lightweight 3D Python Engine:** Used for stacking tasks due to simpler physical dynamics, enabling greater development efficiency and programmatic generation (see Appendix A.5 for details on polycube stacking generation, including exact cover solvability via DLX, physical feasibility checks like "no isolated piece," and linear assembly sequence extraction).
 - **Uniform Interaction Interface:** A color-hinted control scheme is adopted, where objects are identified by distinct colors and metadata. This avoids confounding the evaluation with additional action controllers. Multi-view observations are provided to mitigate occlusion.

3. **Closed-Loop Evaluation Protocol:** Each task instance is evaluated interactively:
 - An agent receives a task instruction, a compact interaction history (most recent five turns), and current multi-view visual observations.
 - It selects an action from a predefined space (e.g., pick, rotate, insert for puzzles; select, rotate, place for stacking).
 - The simulator executes the action, updates the environment, and returns new observations.
 - This perception-action loop continues until the task is solved or a predefined step budget (30-60 steps) is reached. Evaluation metrics are computed from the full trajectory.

**Evaluation Metrics:**
The performance is assessed from three perspectives:

1. **Task Success:**
 - **Pass@1:** The fraction of tasks successfully solved in a single attempt.

2. **Plan Efficiency (conditioned on successful tasks):**
 - **AvgSteps:** The mean number of actions taken for successfully solved tasks.
 - **Distance-to-Optimal (Dist2Opt):** The number of extra steps beyond the minimal plan length for solved tasks.
 - **Normalized Distance (NormDist):** Dist2Opt normalized by the optimal plan length, providing a scale-free measure of inefficiency.

3. **Token and Cost Efficiency:**
 - **Solved/1M Tokens:** The number of tasks solved per million tokens consumed (input + output), reflecting token usage and verbosity.
 - **Solved/USD:** The number of tasks solved per USD, calculated using provider prices for input and output tokens, quantifying deployment cost-effectiveness.

**Models Evaluated:**
A diverse set of state-of-the-art models were tested, including closed-source VLMs (GPT-5.2, OpenAI-o3, Claude-Opus/Sonnet-4.5, Gemini-3-Pro/Flash, Seed-1.6/Flash) and open-source VLMs (Qwen3-VL variants, GLM-4.6V, Kimi-k2.5). Additionally, state-of-the-art video generation models (SORA 2, WAN 2.6, VEO 3.1, KLING 2.6, HUNYUANVIDEO 1.5) were evaluated for their "world model" capabilities in a puzzle disassembly subtask. All models used identical generation hyperparameters and operated via a system proxy for atomic actions to isolate decision-making.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The comprehensive empirical study on CHAIN revealed significant insights into the current capabilities and persistent limitations of state-of-the-art VLMs and diffusion-based world models in interactive physical reasoning.

**Overall VLM Performance and Task Difficulty:**
- **Challenging Benchmark:** CHAIN proved to be a highly challenging benchmark. The best-performing model, GPT-5.2, achieved an overall Pass@1 of only 22.9% (solving 25 out of 109 tasks), indicating that current top-tier models still struggle with these tasks. Kimi-k2.5 was the strongest open-source model with 13.8% Pass@1.
- **Puzzle vs. Stacking Disparity:** A striking performance gap was observed between the two task families. Models consistently performed much worse on the **Puzzle tasks** (most achieving only 0.0-3.1% success) compared to **Stacking tasks** (10.4-31.2%). This highlights that interlocking mechanical puzzles, which demand deep structural inference and constrained sequential manipulation, remain a major bottleneck. Failures often occurred early, suggesting difficulty in identifying valid intermediate constraints or critical moves.
- **Difficulty Stratification:**
 - For **Stacking**, performance gradually degraded from easy (e.g., GPT5.2 at 100% success) to medium (55.0%) and hard (6.3%), demonstrating that success is increasingly dependent on long-horizon planning as complexity grows.
 - For **Puzzles**, even "easy" levels yielded low success rates (peak 10.0%), with medium and hard levels remaining at 0.0% for most models. This indicates that the core challenge for puzzles is not just scaling complexity but the fundamental difficulty of 3D interlocking reasoning itself, requiring inference of hidden blocking constraints and multi-step disentanglement from partial observations.

**Plan, Token, and Cost Efficiency:**
- **Efficiency Trade-offs:** While stronger models like GPT-5.2 achieved higher success rates, they often did so with more backtracking and plan revisions, leading to higher `AvgSteps`, `Dist2Opt`, and `NormDist`. This increased interaction length also translated to higher `Solved/Tokens` and `Solved/USD` costs (e.g., GPT-5.2 was the most expensive per solved task at $1.3/level).
- **Cost vs. Quality:** The analysis showed that smaller, "flash" models were cheaper but achieved significantly lower success. There was no single model that excelled in both success rate and cost efficiency across all tasks, highlighting a complex trade-off for deployment.

**Qualitative Failure Analysis:**
- **Puzzles:** Models struggled with **weak perception and utilization of physical structure**. Even with revealed internal structure, they often couldn't reliably identify the crucial first move to unlock a puzzle, resorting to extensive trial-and-error and random exploration rather than constraint-guided progress.
- **Stacking:** Failures were dominated by **object-set complexity, coupling, and global spatial planning issues**. Models often made greedy local placements, leading to fragmented free space or irreversible configurations that prevented fitting remaining pieces. Stronger models exhibited more deliberate lookahead to avoid such dead-ends.

**Catastrophic Failure of World Models:**
- State-of-the-art video generation models (SORA 2, WAN 2.6, VEO 3.1, KLING 2.6, HUNYUANVIDEO 1.5) were tested on puzzle disassembly and exhibited **systematic, catastrophic failures**, even for simple configurations.
- **Physics Violations:** Some models showed superficial instruction following but violated physical constraints (e.g., translating pieces through interlocked components).
- **Representational Collapse and Hallucinations:** Other models suffered severe representational collapse, generating distorted geometries, spurious components, or entirely altering the puzzle structure (adding, removing, or merging beams). These failures worsened with increased structural complexity, indicating a breakdown in object permanence and constraint consistency.
- **Implication:** This demonstrates that current world models, despite generating visually plausible motion, are fundamentally unreliable for structured, constraint-driven interaction and lack the object-centric reasoning needed for multi-step physical manipulation.

**Impact of Interaction (One-shot vs. Interactive):**
- **Significant Benefit of Interaction:** The interactive setting consistently yielded substantially higher accuracy than the one-shot setting (where models received only an initial image and had to output a complete plan). The `∆ (Interactive - One-shot)` was significant (e.g., GPT5.2 dropped from 31.2% to 9.1% on Stacking, and Puzzle accuracy collapsed to 0.0% in one-shot).
- **Conclusion:** This validates that CHAIN genuinely evaluates closed-loop physical-structure reasoning, where models leverage iterative feedback to discover constraints and adapt plans, rather than relying on pre-computed reasoning from a single static view.

**Reward Models vs. Verifier-based Checking:**
- Experiments with Kimi-k2.5 showed that `Pass@1` and `Avg@4` (multi-sample selection) were nearly identical, implying limited sampling variance. While multi-sample selection helped, it saturated quickly.
- Reward Model (RM)-based reranking yielded only marginal improvements (+0.6%), and even a stronger VLM pairwise judge provided limited gains (+1.3%), still trailing simple `Pass@2`.
- **Conclusion:** This suggests that current vision RMs offer limited leverage for robust long-horizon interactive planning. More reliable, environment-grounded verifier-style signals are crucial for consistent performance gains in such complex tasks.

In summary, the results demonstrate a clear and persistent gap between the perceptual capabilities of current AI models and their ability to perform robust, physically-grounded interactive reasoning, especially when faced with tight structural and causal constraints.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of the CHAIN benchmark marks a significant stride in the rigorous evaluation of AI models, particularly Vision-Language Models (VLMs) and nascent world models, in the domain of physical reasoning. Its significance and potential impact span several critical areas of AI research and application:

**1. Advancing AI Evaluation Paradigms:**
CHAIN directly addresses a critical deficiency in current AI evaluation, moving beyond static, single-turn tasks (like VQA) to a dynamic, interactive, and physics-driven paradigm. This shift from "passive perception to active problem-solving" is crucial for building AI that can truly understand and interact with the physical world. By focusing on multi-step, closed-loop interaction, CHAIN offers a more realistic assessment of an agent's ability to reason about evolving constraints, adapt plans, and learn from iterative feedback.

**2. Highlighting Persistent Gaps in State-of-the-Art Models:**
The comprehensive evaluation of leading VLMs and video generation models on CHAIN exposes fundamental limitations. The stark performance drop, especially on interlocking mechanical puzzles and complex stacking tasks, clearly demonstrates that current models struggle to:
 - **Internalize Physical Structure:** They fail to deeply understand how geometry, contact, and support relations jointly constrain actions.
 - **Maintain Coherent Long-Horizon Plans:** Models often struggle with multi-step strategies, frequently making locally plausible but globally suboptimal or irreversible choices.
 - **Translate Perception to Effective Action:** There's a persistent gap between "seeing" a scene and "acting" effectively within it, indicating a lack of robust physical intuition and causal reasoning.
The "catastrophic failures" of world models in generating physically valid disassembly sequences are particularly impactful, revealing that despite visual plausibility, these models lack foundational object permanence and constraint consistency.

**3. Catalyzing Research in Physically Grounded AI:**
By clearly delineating areas of struggle, CHAIN provides a powerful impetus for future research. It encourages the development of:
 - **More Structure-Aware Models:** Models that can infer and exploit hidden geometric and mechanical constraints.
 - **Improved Planning and Reinforcement Learning Algorithms:** Methods capable of robust long-horizon planning, multi-step causal reasoning, and effective exploration in complex, physics-driven environments.
 - **Robust World Models:** Generative models that can accurately simulate physical dynamics, object interactions, and constraint propagation over time, moving beyond superficial visual coherence.
 - **Effective Interaction Strategies:** Techniques for leveraging iterative feedback to refine plans and adapt to evolving circumstances, as demonstrated by the performance gap between one-shot and interactive solving.

**4. Impact on Embodied AI and Robotics:**
The benchmark is highly relevant for embodied AI and robotics. Agents operating in the real world (e.g., household robots, industrial manipulators, virtual assistants) must possess strong physical reasoning capabilities to manipulate objects, navigate complex spaces, and interact safely and effectively with their environment. CHAIN provides a stepping stone towards developing AI systems that can perform complex tasks requiring fine-grained manipulation, assembly, and rearrangement.

**5. Open-Source Contribution and Reproducibility:**
The open-sourcing of CHAIN, along with reproducible baselines, fosters collaborative research and allows the wider AI community to build upon this work. This is crucial for accelerating progress and ensuring fair comparisons between new models and methods.

In conclusion, CHAIN is more than just another benchmark; it's a recalibration of what constitutes "intelligent" physical reasoning in AI. By pushing models beyond passive observation into active interaction with a physically simulated world, it illuminates fundamental challenges and points towards critical directions for building truly capable, physically-grounded, and interactive artificial intelligence systems. The findings underscore the vast potential for advancements in areas like robotics, interactive design, and any application where AI must reason about and manipulate the tangible world.
