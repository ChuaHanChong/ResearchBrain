# Practical Efficiency of Muon for Pretraining

> [!summary] Summary
> Essential AI demonstrates that Muon, a second-order optimizer, offers superior practical efficiency over AdamW for large language model pretraining, expanding the compute-time Pareto frontier and requiring 10-15% fewer tokens to reach target losses. They also introduce a "telescoping algorithm" for efficient hyperparameter tuning with Muon, validating its compatibility with Maximal Update Parameterization (muP) to achieve substantial compute savings for hyperparameter search.

## Problem

- AdamW has been the de facto optimizer for LLMs, but second-order methods theoretically offer better convergence, yet were deemed impractical due to high computational and memory costs.
- Hyperparameter optimization (HPO) for massive LLMs is prohibitively expensive, and the compatibility of principled scaling methods like muP with novel optimizers like Muon was unconfirmed.
- Traditional optimizer comparisons often fail to capture the complex interplay between compute resources, training time, and data efficiency, especially at large batch sizes common in distributed training.

## Method

- The paper proposes ==Muon== as a simple, memory-efficient ==second-order optimizer== and validates its practical advantages over AdamW for LLM pretraining.
- It introduces the =='compute-time Pareto frontier'== and 'token ratio' as comprehensive metrics for evaluating optimizer performance across varying compute configurations and batch sizes.
- A =='telescoping algorithm'== is developed for efficient hyperparameter tuning with Muon, leveraging ==Maximal Update Parameterization (muP)== to transfer hyperparameters from smaller to larger models while controlling errors.

## Results

- Muon consistently expanded the compute-time Pareto frontier compared to AdamW, allowing for a target loss to be achieved either faster or with fewer compute resources.
- Muon required **10-15%** fewer tokens than AdamW to reach an identical target loss, with the absolute token savings largely agnostic to model size (e.g., **~2 billion** fewer tokens to reach **3.2 nats**).
- The 'telescoping algorithm' for hyperparameter tuning provided over **50%** compute savings compared to brute-force search, successfully tracking optimal hyperparameters and reducing errors.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Muon, a second-order optimizer, practically outperforms AdamW by offering a more favorable compute-time tradeoff for large language model pretraining.
> - Muon maintains superior relative data efficiency even at very large batch sizes, where AdamW typically shows diminishing returns.
> - The Maximal Update Parameterization (muP) framework is compatible with Muon, and the proposed 'telescoping algorithm' efficiently enables principled hyperparameter transfer across model scales.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper addresses critical challenges in the landscape of large language model pretraining, specifically focusing on optimization algorithms and hyperparameter tuning at scale.

**Optimization Algorithms:** For years, Adam (Kingma and Ba, 2014) and its variant AdamW (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2017) have been the de facto standard optimizers for neural networks, owing to their empirical effectiveness and ease of use. While second-order optimizers theoretically offer advantages in convergence speed and data efficiency by exploiting curvature information, they have historically suffered from high computational and memory costs, making them impractical for large-scale deep learning.

Recent research has begun to challenge AdamW's dominance by introducing more scalable second-order methods, such as Shampoo (Gupta et al., 2018; Anil et al., 2020), Soap (Vyas et al., 2024), and Muon (Jordan et al., 2024). These methods promise improved data efficiency without compromising overall computational advantage. Crucially, the paper positions Muon as the "simplest instantiation of a second-order optimizer," making it a prime candidate for practical adoption. This work builds directly on prior findings (Liu et al., 2025) that showed Muon to be more FLOP-efficient than AdamW, making a stronger claim about its overall practical advantage.

**Compute-Time Tradeoff and Batch Size:** Traditional comparisons of optimizers often rely on fixed metrics like wall-clock time or FLOPs, which fail to capture the complex interplay between compute resources (e.g., number of devices) and training time, especially for distributed large-scale pretraining. This paper explicitly adopts and promotes the "compute-time Pareto frontier" concept (previously used for batch size dynamics by McCandlish et al., 2018) as a more comprehensive metric for comparing optimizers. This shift in evaluation methodology is significant, as it provides a holistic view of an optimizer's practicality in a resource-constrained environment.

Furthermore, the paper delves into the impact of batch size, a crucial element in the compute-time tradeoff. While a large body of work discusses the "critical batch size" (Balles et al., 2016; Goyal et al., 2017; McCandlish et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2019), beyond which data efficiency diminishes, this paper introduces a novel "token ratio" metric to continuously measure the relative data efficiency of optimizers in the *post-critical regime*. This offers a more granular understanding of optimizer behavior at very large batch sizes, which are common in extreme-scale pretraining.

**Hyperparameter Optimization (HPO):** The computational cost of hyperparameter tuning for massive LLMs is prohibitive, often requiring brute-force grid searches that consume a significant fraction of the total compute budget. The Maximal Update Parameterization (muP) (Yang et al., 2022; Yaida, 2022) is a principled theoretical framework that enables "zero-shot hyperparameter transfer" by ensuring that optimal hyperparameters found on a small model remain effective for a larger one, thus drastically reducing HPO costs. However, an open question remained regarding muP's compatibility with novel second-order optimizers like Muon (Moonshot, 2025; Liu et al., 2025). This paper directly addresses this gap and further refines muP's practical application by proposing a "telescoping algorithm" to account for finite-width estimation errors. This work positions itself as a critical bridge between theoretical advancements in scaling laws and their practical application in industrial-scale LLM development.

In essence, this research aims to provide a comprehensive, practical solution for efficient LLM pretraining by integrating advancements in second-order optimization and principled hyperparameter scaling, directly challenging the status quo and offering a new "recipe" for practitioners.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to demonstrate that Muon, a second-order optimizer, is a practically superior alternative to AdamW for pretraining large language models and to provide a robust method for its efficient hyperparameter tuning.

The key motivations and specific objectives can be summarized as follows:

- **To Dethrone AdamW with Practical Second-Order Optimization:** The primary motivation is to overcome the empirical dominance of AdamW by proving that Muon offers a tangible, practical advantage. While second-order methods theoretically offer better convergence, their historical computational and memory overheads have limited their adoption. This paper aims to demonstrate that Muon overcomes these practical barriers.
- **To Characterize the Fundamental Compute-Time Tradeoff:** The authors argue that traditional optimizer comparisons (wall-clock time, FLOPs) are insufficient. A crucial motivation is to provide a more meaningful comparison by explicitly plotting the "compute-time Pareto frontier," illustrating how optimizers allow practitioners to trade off compute resources (number of devices) against total training time to reach a target loss. The goal is to show that Muon "expands" this frontier, offering more favorable resource allocation options.
- **To Analyze Data Efficiency at Large Batch Sizes:** A key element of the compute-time tradeoff is the batch size. The motivation here is to understand why Muon might be superior: by being persistently data-efficient even at very large batch sizes, far beyond the "critical batch size" where AdamW typically sees diminishing returns. The paper introduces the "token ratio" metric to quantify this comparative data efficiency.
- **To Validate Maximal Update Parameterization (muP) for Muon:** Hyperparameter search for large models is astronomically expensive. muP offers a principled way to transfer hyperparameters from small to large models. An open question existed regarding muP's compatibility with Muon. A core objective is to empirically confirm that muP's scaling rules remain valid and effective when using Muon.
- **To Develop a Robust and Efficient Hyperparameter Tuning Algorithm:** Recognizing that even muP has practical limitations (finite-width bias, sampling errors from discrete grids), a key motivation is to devise an algorithm that systematically controls and suppresses these errors with minimal overhead. This leads to the development of the "telescoping algorithm," which aims to reduce the computational burden of hyperparameter search while guaranteeing near-optimal results at scale.

In essence, the paper aims to provide a "practitioner's guide on optimization for pretraining," delivering a "unified recipe" that integrates Muon as a superior optimizer, muP for principled hyperparameter scaling, and the telescoping algorithm for efficient tuning. This comprehensive approach is motivated by the desire to make large-scale LLM pretraining more economical, flexible, and accessible.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a rigorous experimental methodology combined with theoretical analysis to achieve its objectives.

**Optimizer Comparison (Muon vs. AdamW):**
- **Optimizer Choice:** The paper focuses on Muon (Jordan et al., 2024), chosen for its simplicity, light memory footprint (only maintaining the first moment), diminishing FLOP overhead with increasing batch size (Θ(m/B)), and compatibility with AdamW's learning rate schedules (due to a 0.2√n update normalization). It is also argued to be the "minimal version" of a class of second-order optimizers that includes Shampoo and Soap, implying its findings generalize.
- **Evaluation Metric:** Instead of simple wall-clock time or FLOPs, the authors introduce a comparison on the **compute-time plane**. They measure the total training time required to reach a specific "target loss" as a function of the number of devices (which correlates with batch size in distributed training). This generates "iso-loss frontiers," allowing for a direct Pareto frontier comparison between optimizers.
- **Data Efficiency Metric:** To quantify Muon's advantage at large batch sizes, they propose the **token ratio** `R_L(B) = T_L,A(B) / T_L,M(B)`, where `T` is the number of tokens consumed to reach a target loss `L` at batch size `B` for AdamW (A) and Muon (M). A ratio above 1 indicates AdamW requires more tokens.
- **Experimental Setup:**
 - **Models:** Decoder-only Transformer models based on Gemma 3 (with global attention, ablating with Gemma 2).
 - **Model Sizes:** Extensive experiments across 100M, 500M, 1B, 2B, and 4B parameters.
 - **Data:** High-quality general web text (DCLM) and Python code (Stack V2), chunked to 8192 tokens.
 - **Token Budget:** Training runs were designed to meet or exceed Chinchilla-optimal token budgets (e.g., 100M on 10B tokens, 4B on 50B tokens), ensuring convergence.
 - **Distributed Training:** Simulated by varying batch sizes (128K to 16M tokens) across a corresponding range of TPU v5p devices (8 to 128 devices) to maintain sufficient on-device memory and achieve high Model FLOP Utilization (MFU ~50%).
 - **Implementation:** Standard Optax AdamW was used. An in-house Jax implementation of Muon (provided in Appendix G) was used for all layers except embedding and normalization layers, which used Optax Adam with shared hyperparameters for consistency.
 - **Hyperparameter Tuning (Initial):** Initial sweeps were done at smaller scales (100M, 500M, 1B) to find optimal learning rate schedules (linear warmup, cosine decay) and weight decay, which were then carried over to larger models.

**Hyperparameter Optimization (muP & Telescoping Algorithm):**
- **Problem Formulation:** The challenge is to efficiently select optimal hyperparameters for large models, given that brute-force searches are intractable and direct transfer from small models via muP has finite-width errors.
- **Theoretical Analysis of Errors:**
 - **Finite-width bias:** Explains that the optimal hyperparameter `x*(n)` for a network of width `n` deviates from the infinite-width optimum `x*` by an `O(1/n)` term, leading to an unavoidable bias unless the proxy model is very large (Eq. 4).
 - **Sampling error:** Acknowledges that discrete grid searches introduce `ε` error reflecting the resolution of the sweep (Eq. 5).
- **"Telescoping" Algorithm (Algorithm 1):** A hierarchical hyperparameter transfer protocol designed to control these errors:
 - **Initialization:** Start with a broad hyperparameter grid sweep at a base model width `N0`.
 - **Iterative Refinement:** In a loop, double the model width (`n ← 2n`) and geometrically reduce the number of sweep points per hyperparameter (by a factor of `4^(-1/k)` for `k` hyperparameters). This simultaneously adjusts the mesh resolution.
 - **Rationale:** The `O(1/n)` shift in optimal hyperparameters means that as `n` doubles, halving the mesh spacing is sufficient to track the drift. By reducing the number of grid points, the cost of each successive stage remains approximately constant, preventing over-refinement while ensuring the true minimum is captured.
- **Experimental Setup (for muP/Telescoping):**
 - **Models:** A family of Transformer models aimed at a final size of ~3.7B parameters, with fixed depth (34 layers), sequence length (8192), batch size (8M tokens), and training steps (20,000 steps / 160B tokens for the largest model).
 - **Scaling Dimension:** Only the "width" dimensions (query heads, KV heads, embedding dimension, MLP dimension) were varied, consistent with muP's core focus. Head dimension was fixed.
 - **Data:** 50/50 mix of DCLM web data and Python code.
 - **Hyperparameters Tuned:** Learning rate and coupled weight decay.
 - **Evaluation:** Visualize the loss landscape at different widths (Figure 6) to show the narrowing of the search space and the tracking of the minimum. Quantify the distribution of loss values and fit to scaling laws (Figure 14) to demonstrate precision and accuracy. Analyze compute savings (Figure 13).

This comprehensive approach allows the authors to rigorously compare optimizers and develop an efficient hyperparameter tuning strategy, backing their claims with extensive empirical evidence across varied model sizes, data types, and resource configurations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper presents compelling evidence for Muon's superiority over AdamW and the effectiveness of the proposed telescoping algorithm for hyperparameter tuning.

#### 4.1. Muon Improves the Compute-Time Tradeoff over AdamW

- **Pareto Frontier Expansion:** The most significant finding is that Muon explicitly expands the Pareto frontier on the compute-time tradeoff compared to AdamW (Figure 1 and 2). This means that for any given target loss, Muon can achieve it either with fewer compute resources (fewer devices) or in less total time for the same number of devices. This advantage holds consistently across various loss thresholds and different data distributions (Python and DCLM).
- **Practical Efficiency:** The plots demonstrate that Muon offers novel economically feasible options for practitioners, enabling faster training or requiring fewer devices to reach a desired performance level.

#### 4.2. Muon's Superior Relative Data Efficiency at Large Batch Sizes

- **Persistent Data Efficiency:** Muon proves to be more effective than AdamW in retaining data efficiency even at very large batch sizes, extending "far beyond the so-called critical batch size" where AdamW typically degrades.
- **Token Ratio Advantage:** The proposed "token ratio" (AdamW tokens / Muon tokens) `R_L(B)` consistently remains above 1 and is generally non-decreasing (often increasing) as the batch size grows (Figure 3, top row). This signifies that AdamW always requires more tokens than Muon to reach the same target loss, and this token overhead often becomes more pronounced at larger batch sizes.
- **Quantified Savings:** Muon requires 10-15% fewer tokens than AdamW to achieve an identical loss.
- **Model Size Agnostic Token Savings:** Surprisingly, the *absolute number* of additional tokens required by AdamW over Muon to reach a target loss is largely agnostic to the model size (Figure 3, bottom row). For instance, reaching a target loss of 3.2 nats consistently required around 2 billion more tokens with AdamW than with Muon across 1B, 2B, and 4B parameter models. This robust benefit translates directly into compute savings or faster training.

#### 4.3. Successful Hyperparameter Transfer with Muon using muP

- **MuP Compatibility Confirmed:** The paper provides the first empirical demonstration that the Maximal Update Parameterization (muP) remains valid and effective when combined with Muon. This addresses a significant open question in the field.
- **Consistent Scaling Rules:** It was found that the same muP scaling rules (for weight initialization, learning rate scaling, and coupled weight decay) that work for AdamW also work well for Muon, simplifying practical adoption. Experiments validate successful transfer for models up to 3.7 billion parameters at a sequence length of 8192.

#### 4.4. Effectiveness of the Telescoping Algorithm for Hyperparameter Tuning

- **Controlled Error Reduction:** The "telescoping" algorithm effectively controls both finite-width bias (`O(1/n)` drift) and sampling error in hyperparameter transfer. As the model width doubles and the search space is geometrically reduced, the distribution of loss values tightens around its mean, demonstrating increased precision in locating the optimal hyperparameters (Figure 14).
- **Tracking the Optimum:** Figure 6 visually confirms that the minima of the loss surfaces, representing optimal hyperparameters, smoothly shift with model width in a manner consistent with the `O(1/n)` prediction, and the telescoping algorithm successfully tracks this shift.
- **Computational Efficiency:** The algorithm introduces only a modest additional compute cost, roughly `O(C log(N))`, where `C` is the cost of training the final model and `N` is its width. Compared to a brute-force grid search on the full model, the telescoping approach results in substantial compute savings (typically >50%), while ensuring that a significant fraction of the total compute budget (more than 20%) is still dedicated to the final full-scale training run, thus balancing exploration and exploitation (Figure 13).
- **Scaling Law Consistency:** The final 3.7B parameter model, trained with hyperparameters tuned using this method, achieved a loss of 1.61 nats and showed excellent agreement with a shifted power law for parameter scaling (`R^2 ≈ 1`), with an exponent of 0.31, close to the Chinchilla exponent of 0.34.

These findings collectively establish Muon as a compelling alternative to AdamW and provide a robust methodology for efficient hyperparameter tuning at the scale required for modern LLM pretraining.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The findings presented in this paper carry significant implications for the field of large language model pretraining and the broader machine learning community.

**1. A New Paradigm for LLM Optimization:** The most immediate and profound impact is the direct challenge to AdamW's long-standing dominance. By demonstrating that Muon consistently expands the compute-time Pareto frontier, the paper strongly advocates for Muon as a "drop-in successor" and a "robust, drop-in replacement for AdamW." This shifts the conversation from merely finding *different* optimizers to identifying *strictly superior* ones for large-scale training, potentially marking a pivotal moment in the practical application of second-order optimization.

**2. More Economical and Flexible LLM Pretraining:** The core finding that Muon enables more economical training by maintaining data efficiency at very large batch sizes is critical. This means that practitioners can achieve a target model quality either faster or with fewer computational resources. For resource-intensive tasks like LLM pretraining, where budgets are often in the millions of dollars, a 10-15% reduction in token consumption translates directly into substantial cost savings and accelerated development cycles. This newfound flexibility in trading off compute and time resources empowers organizations to optimize their training runs more effectively for various constraints.

**3. Practical Realization of Second-Order Methods:** Historically, second-order optimizers have been considered too computationally expensive for deep learning. This work, along with recent related efforts, bridges the gap between theoretical promise and practical viability. By highlighting Muon's minimal memory footprint, diminishing FLOP overhead, and demonstrated efficiency, the paper transforms second-order optimization from an academic curiosity into a "practical default for industry-scale language model pretraining." This could encourage further research and development into more advanced and even more efficient second-order techniques.

**4. Streamlined Hyperparameter Optimization at Scale:** The validation of muP with Muon and the introduction of the "telescoping algorithm" fundamentally changes the landscape of hyperparameter tuning for LLMs. The current brute-force approaches are prohibitively expensive and time-consuming. The new recipe offers a principled, efficient, and robust way to find near-optimal hyperparameters with a modest `O(C log N)` overhead. This will significantly reduce the cost and time spent on hyperparameter search, allowing researchers and engineers to iterate faster on new model architectures, datasets, and training objectives. This is particularly impactful for companies training foundation models, where every optimization translates to immense savings.

**5. A Unified Recipe for Efficient Pretraining:** The paper doesn't just propose individual improvements; it synthesizes them into a "unified recipe: Muon optimization, muP scaling, and telescoping hyperparameter transfer." This comprehensive approach offers a clear, actionable guide for practitioners seeking to optimize their LLM pretraining pipelines for superior data efficiency, shorter training times, and reduced tuning costs. This integrated solution provides a significant competitive advantage for those developing next-generation AI models.

**6. Driving Further Research and Development:** By establishing new benchmarks and practical methodologies, the paper is likely to spur further innovation. Researchers may now explore deeper into the theoretical underpinnings of Muon's data efficiency, investigate its performance across different modalities and tasks, and refine the telescoping algorithm for even greater efficiency or broader applicability (e.g., to other scaling dimensions beyond width). The release of experimental artifacts on Hugging Face further supports community engagement and accelerates follow-up research.

In conclusion, this research marks a significant step forward in making large-scale AI model training more efficient, economical, and accessible. It offers a clear path for moving beyond the current standard of AdamW, empowering practitioners to train more capable models faster and at lower cost.
