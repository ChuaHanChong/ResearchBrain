# Rethink Before You Execute: Adaptive Execution for World Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> TempoWAM is a plug-and-play execution layer for World Action Models that replaces fixed-horizon chunk execution with progress-driven replanning. A lightweight 2.27M-parameter Recurrent Progress Monitor estimates task progress from observation, instruction, candidate actions, and history; an Adaptive Execution Protocol compares observed versus required progress rate under a two-timescale calibration factor. On real robots it cuts WAM inference calls 26.9% on easy tasks and raises success 13.3 points on hard ones.

## Problem

- WAMs execute a fixed prefix of each predicted action chunk, but chunk reliability varies by task stage, so no single execution horizon fits every stage.
- Prior adaptive-execution signals use proxies like action entropy, denoising variance, or self-attention, which measure plausibility rather than whether the task is actually advancing.
- Those proxies are costly for WAMs: sampling multiplies expensive inference calls, imagination-verification needs future rollouts efficient WAMs omit, and RL horizons need online interaction.

## Method

- ==Recurrent Progress Monitor==: ==GRU== core fusing frozen-WAM visual features, recurrent action-prefix encoding, prefix-length embedding, and the previous progress estimate into a sigmoid progress value.
- ==Adaptive Execution Protocol== compares current progress rate against required rate normalized by average episode length, forming a scale-free ratio; below one discards remaining actions and triggers replanning.
- ==Two-timescale calibration==: offline per-task least-squares factor, intra-episode ==EMA== over raw ratios, and cross-episode success-gated scaling toward a target success rate.

## Results

- On RoboTwin 50 tasks, success rose **91.68%** to **92.86%** clean while long-horizon tasks improved **86.93%** to **88.47%**; open_microwave jumped **53%** to **83%**.
- On LIBERO, success held at **97.25%** while average calls dropped **14.88** to **12.46**; baselines Auto-Horizon and AAC needed more calls with lower success.
- Attached to Motus without retraining, move_can_pot success rose **35%** to **94%**; ablations show smoothness and history-consistency regularizers each worth about **0.6** points.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Execution timing should be gated on measured task advancement, not step counts or uncertainty proxies; plausible-looking action sequences can make no progress at all.
> - Comparing progress rates instead of absolute progress increments makes the decision rule scale-free across tasks of very different episode lengths.
> - Time-index progress labels break on periodic or symmetric tasks where observations alias; such tasks need phase decomposition or semantic progress supervision.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated within the field of robot manipulation, specifically focusing on the execution strategies for World Action Models (WAMs). WAMs represent a paradigm for robot control where future actions and environmental evolution are jointly predicted by a single model. This approach moves beyond traditional methods that might separate planning and execution or rely solely on action prediction. Existing WAMs typically operate by generating a chunk of future actions, from which the robot executes a fixed prefix before triggering a replanning step.

The paper identifies a limitation in this standard WAM execution: the fixed execution horizon. Prior research has indicated that a static horizon is often suboptimal across diverse task stages, where the reliability of predicted action chunks can fluctuate significantly. For instance, in easy task segments, a chunk might remain accurate for many steps, minimizing the need for frequent replanning. Conversely, during complex or sensitive stages, errors can accumulate rapidly, necessitating earlier intervention and replanning.

The broader landscape of adaptive execution in robotics has explored various signals to determine when to replan. These include proxy metrics such as action entropy, which quantifies uncertainty in sampled actions; prediction consistency; attention weights within the model; verification of imagined future states against observations; or learning dynamic horizons through online reinforcement learning. However, the authors note that these existing methods often face limitations when applied to WAMs. Sampling-based approaches can multiply the computational cost of WAM calls, which are already deployment-intensive. Attention-based methods might disregard the explicit environment state modeling inherent in WAMs. Verification techniques may require test-time future imagination, a component that some efficient WAMs intentionally omit. Reinforcement learning-based strategies typically demand costly online interaction.

This work contributes to the literature by proposing an adaptive execution scheme, TempoWAM, that departs from these proxy-based methods. Instead of relying on indirect uncertainty metrics, TempoWAM directly incorporates an explicit measure of "task progress" to inform replanning decisions. It aims to address the identified mismatch between fixed execution horizons and dynamic task reliability in a lightweight, plug-and-play manner, without requiring modifications or retraining of the underlying WAM architecture. This approach distinguishes it from other adaptive execution methods by grounding decisions in observable task advancement rather than internal model uncertainties or computationally expensive simulations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and evaluate an adaptive execution scheme for World Action Models (WAMs) that can dynamically adjust the execution horizon based on the observed progress of a task. The motivation stems from several identified limitations of current WAM execution paradigms:

1. **Mismatch of Fixed Execution Horizon to Task Dynamics:** Standard WAM execution involves generating a chunk of actions and then executing a predetermined, fixed prefix of these actions before initiating a replanning step. The authors argue that this fixed execution horizon is suboptimal because the reliability and applicability of an action chunk vary significantly across different stages of a task. During simpler, less error-prone stages, a chunk may remain accurate and effective for an extended duration, rendering frequent replanning unnecessary and inefficient. Conversely, in more complex or sensitive stages, errors can rapidly accumulate, making the initial chunk unreliable after only a few steps and necessitating prompt replanning to avoid failure. This variability implies that the optimal timing for replanning should be contingent on the current execution state and the results of accumulated actions, rather than a constant number of steps.

2. **Limitations of Existing Adaptive Execution Proxies:** While some research has begun to explore adaptive execution, many existing methods rely on indirect proxy metrics such as action entropy, prediction consistency, or attention weights within the model. The authors contend that these metrics do not directly assess what is most critical for successful task execution: whether the robot is genuinely advancing the task towards its goal. An action sequence, for example, might appear smooth or exhibit low entropy, yet fail to make meaningful progress. Conversely, a high-entropy sequence could still be effectively moving the task forward. For WAMs, which model both actions and environmental evolution, an execution decision mechanism grounded in task advancement is considered more appropriate and robust than one based solely on the plausibility or uncertainty of the action sequence itself.

3. **Need for a Plug-and-Play, Efficient Solution:** The motivation also includes developing a solution that is practical for deployment. This implies that the adaptive execution scheme should be lightweight, adding minimal computational overhead compared to a full WAM inference call, which is typically expensive. Furthermore, it should be "plug-and-play," meaning it can be integrated with existing WAM architectures without requiring modification or retraining of the backbone WAM itself. This design goal ensures broader applicability and easier adoption of the proposed method across various WAM implementations.

In summary, the key objective is to enhance the efficiency and success rate of robot manipulation by introducing TempoWAM, a progress-aware adaptive execution system that determines replanning needs directly from task advancement, offering a lightweight and modular solution to a fundamental challenge in WAM deployment.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

TempoWAM (Timing Execution by Monitoring Progress Online) is presented as a lightweight, plug-and-play adaptive execution scheme designed for World Action Models (WAMs). It operates at the execution layer without requiring modifications or retraining of the underlying WAM. The system comprises two main components: a Recurrent Progress Monitor (RPM) and an Adaptive Execution Protocol (AEP).

#### 3.1. Overall Execution Flow

When a WAM generates a new action chunk `c` of horizon `H`, TempoWAM first executes a short, fixed prefix of length `n_p` unconditionally to ensure environment interaction. Following this initial execution, TempoWAM enters a decision loop. At each decision step `t`, it considers the next `n_t = min(n_p, |c_rem_t|)` actions from the remaining chunk `c_rem_t` as a candidate prefix. The RPM then estimates the task progress that would be achieved by executing these `n_t` candidate actions. Based on this estimate, the AEP decides whether to continue executing the current chunk or to discard the remaining actions and trigger a fresh WAM inference call for replanning. If execution continues, the robot executes the `n_t` actions, and the process repeats with the updated `c_rem_t`. If replanning is triggered, the WAM generates a new chunk, and the cycle restarts.

#### 3.2. Recurrent Progress Monitor (RPM) Module

The RPM module is responsible for estimating task progress, `v_t`, within a range of, where 0 indicates the task's beginning and 1 indicates near completion. At each decision step `t`, the RPM takes the following inputs:
- Current observation (`o_t`)
- Remaining action candidate prefix (`c_rem_t`) of length `n_t`
- Previous progress estimate (`v_{t-1}`)
- Current decision step (`t`)
- Task instruction (`ℓ`)

The internal workings of the RPM are detailed as follows:
- **Observation Encoding:** The current observation `o_t` is processed by the frozen visual backbone `E_obs` of the base WAM. The resulting features are then projected into the monitor's space using a lightweight MLP (`f_obs`) to produce `z_o_t`.
- **Action-Prefix Encoding:** The first `n_t` actions of the candidate prefix `c_rem_t` are encoded by a recurrent encoder (`f_act`). An embedding corresponding to `n_t` is added to allow the monitor to distinguish candidate prefixes of different lengths, yielding `z_a_t`.
- **History Recurrence:** The previous progress estimate `v_{t-1}` is incorporated through a learned projection (`f_v`) to `z_v_t`. For the first decision step of an episode, a learned start token `e_0` is used. This mechanism ensures that the decision is informed by the historical progression of the task.
- **Aggregation and Prediction:** The encoded observation (`z_o_t`), action-prefix (`z_a_t`), history (`z_v_t`), and a temporal position embedding (`p_t`) are fused. This aggregated information forms the input to a GRU (Gated Recurrent Unit) core, which updates an internal recurrent state `h_t`. Finally, a small prediction head (MLP followed by a sigmoid activation function) uses `z_o_t`, `h_t`, and the task instruction embedding `z_g` (from the base WAM) to output the current progress estimate `v_t`.

#### 3.3. Adaptive Execution Protocol (AEP) Module

The AEP uses the RPM's progress estimate `v_t` to make execution decisions. It converts absolute progress into progress rates to account for varying task lengths:
- **Observed Progress Rate (`ρ_cur_t`):** Calculated as `(v_t - v_{t-1}) / n_t`, representing the progress achieved per executed step.
- **Required Progress Rate (`ρ_need_t`):** Defined as `max((1 - v_{t-1}) / max(T_ep_bar - u_t, n_t), 0.2 / T_ep_bar)`. This metric estimates the average progress per remaining step needed to complete the task within the average episode length (`T_ep_bar`) observed during demonstrations, with a minimum required rate. `u_t` is the number of steps executed so far.

The core decision is based on a raw ratio `tilde{r}_t = ρ_cur_t / ρ_need_t`. Values below 1 suggest the current actions are advancing too slowly. To make this ratio robust and adaptable in real-world scenarios, TempoWAM employs a task-dependent calibration factor `κ` with online adaptation:
- **Offline Calibration:** For each task with demonstration data, an episode-level factor `κ_ep` is fitted by least squares, minimizing the difference between the monitor's scaled progress estimates and the ground-truth normalized time `u/T_ep_bar`. The median of these episode-level factors provides a robust task-specific initialization `κ_task`.
- **Online Adaptation (Intra-episode):** At deployment, `κ` is initialized with `κ_task` and then continuously updated within an episode using an Exponential Moving Average (EMA) of the observed raw ratios: `κ ← λ κ + (1 − λ) tilde{r}_t`. This adaptation compensates for any training-execution gap and tracks scale shifts specific to the current episode.
- **Online Adaptation (Inter-episode):** A global scaling factor `δ'` is adjusted across episodes to steer the overall system towards a target success rate `s*`. If the recent average success rate `hat{s}` falls below `s*`, `δ'` increases (making replanning more frequent); if `hat{s}` exceeds `s*`, `δ'` decreases (promoting chunk reuse). The final calibration factor `κ_final = κ · δ'` is used to compute the decision ratio `r_t = tilde{r}_t / κ_final`. Execution continues if `r_t ≥ 1`; otherwise, replanning is triggered. For tasks without demonstrations, `κ` is initialized to 1, relying solely on online adaptation.

#### 3.4. Training Objective

The RPM is trained using the same demonstration trajectories as the backbone WAM. The supervision target for each step `u` in a trajectory of length `T_ep` is `y_u = u / T_ep`, representing normalized time as a proxy for progress. The training objective consists of three components:
- **Progress Prediction Loss (`L_BCE`):** A binary cross-entropy loss applied to the soft progress labels `y_u` and predicted progress `v_u`.
- **Smoothness Regularization (`L_smooth`):** A term that encourages the predicted progress `v_t` to vary smoothly over time, reducing abrupt oscillations that could destabilize decision-making. It penalizes discrepancies between the predicted and ground-truth progress differences over consecutive steps.
- **History Consistency Regularization (`L_hcr`):** To make the monitor less susceptible to transient visual noise, this regularizer randomly masks subsets of observations in the input window during training. It penalizes the discrepancy between progress predictions made with original and masked observations, thereby encouraging the monitor to rely more on the execution history.
The full objective function is a weighted sum of these three losses: `L = L_BCE + λ_smooth L_smooth + λ_hcr L_hcr`. Probabilistic teacher forcing (`p_tf=0.5`) is used during training to mitigate the exposure bias between training (ground-truth history) and deployment (model's own estimates).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates TempoWAM across various benchmarks and real-world scenarios, demonstrating its effectiveness in improving the efficiency-success trade-off for WAM execution.

#### 4.1. Simulation Experiments

Experiments were conducted on RoboTwin 2.0 (50 tasks, 100 trials each) and LIBERO (4 suites, 50 trials each) benchmarks, using FastWAM as the primary backbone WAM. Baselines included fixed-horizon execution (replanning every 24 or 12 steps) and other adaptive methods (Auto-Horizon, AAC). Metrics reported were success rate (SR), WAM inference calls (computational cost), and executed steps.

- **RoboTwin Results (Table 1, 2):** TempoWAM consistently improved the efficiency-success trade-off.
 - On "short-horizon" tasks (those with average calls < 10 for the baseline), TempoWAM maintained or slightly improved success rates while reducing WAM calls. For example, on `click_bell`, it preserved 100% success while cutting calls from 3.00 to 2.04.
 - On "long-horizon" tasks, TempoWAM exhibited earlier replanning when chunks became unreliable, leading to improved success rates. For `open_microwave`, success increased from 53% to 83% in the clean setting and from 41% to 56% under randomization, alongside a reduction in executed steps.
 - Simply reducing the fixed execution horizon (e.g., from 24 to 12 steps) did not yield similar benefits; it often increased calls without improving or even degrading success, indicating that the timing of replanning is critical, not just its frequency.
 - Other adaptive baselines (Auto-Horizon, AAC) performed less effectively, often leading to excessive replanning without achieving comparable success rates or even degrading performance significantly.

- **LIBERO Results (Table 3):** TempoWAM preserved high success rates while reducing WAM calls across all four suites. On average, calls decreased from 14.88 to 12.46. Specifically, on LIBERO-Long tasks, TempoWAM slightly improved success while reducing calls from 24.82 to 20.73. Auto-Horizon and AAC again demonstrated more calls than the fixed baseline without improving success.

- **Cross-Backbone Generalization (Table 4):** To assess its plug-and-play capability, TempoWAM was attached to Motus, another WAM backbone, without retraining. It significantly improved success rates (e.g., on `move_can_pot`, SR increased from 35% to 94% in the clean setting and from 75% to 87% with randomization). This indicates that the progress-driven adaptive execution mechanism is generalizable across different WAM architectures.

#### 4.2. Real-World Experiments and Efficiency

TempoWAM was deployed on a dual-arm robot platform for three tasks: `take_drinks` (easy), `pick_and_place` (medium), and `pack_hand_cream` (hard).

- **Real-Robot Results (Table 5):** The real-world results mirrored simulation findings.
 - On `take_drinks`, TempoWAM maintained success (90%) while reducing WAM calls by 26.9% (from 32.7 to 23.9).
 - On `pick_and_place`, success improved from 93.3% to 96.7% with a modest increase in calls.
 - On `pack_hand_cream`, the most difficult task, success improved from 50.0% to 63.3%.
 This confirms TempoWAM's ability to aggressively reuse reliable chunks on easy tasks and replan earlier on difficult ones.

- **Deployment Cost (Table 6):** WAM inference calls are the dominant computational cost. While optimized WAM calls still take tens of milliseconds (e.g., 42.3 ms on Thor), the RPM decision cost is negligible. It adds only 3.54% of one WAM call per decision when requiring visual encoding, and a mere 0.34% if shared visual features from the WAM can be reused. This demonstrates that the monitor's overhead is marginal relative to the computational savings or success improvements achieved by adaptive replanning.

#### 4.3. Analysis and Ablations

- **Online Calibration Behavior (Fig. 5, Table 7):** Intra-episode online EMA of `κ` showed that the monitor's raw estimates became more optimistic in harder task stages, and the EMA successfully compensated for this bias, leading to earlier replanning. The inter-episode success-gated adaptation of `δ'` effectively adjusted the protocol: on easy tasks surpassing the target success rate, `δ'` relaxed to prioritize efficiency; on difficult tasks below the target, `δ'` tightened to recover success. This mechanism allowed the system to self-tune without per-task parameter adjustments.
- **Adaptive Execution Visualization (Fig. 4):** Visual examples illustrated TempoWAM's behavior: it discarded unreliable action chunks early in challenging stages (e.g., `open_microwave`) to enable successful replanning, and fully reused reliable chunks in straightforward stages (e.g., `press_stapler`) to reduce WAM calls.
- **Training Loss Ablations (Table 8):** Smoothness regularization and history consistency regularization each contributed approximately 0.6 percentage points to the success rate. The use of probabilistic teacher forcing (`p_tf=0.5`) was optimal, with `p_tf=0` destabilizing training and `p_tf=1` suffering from train-test mismatch. The GRU core was found to be slightly more effective and efficient than an LSTM.
- **Validity of Time-Based Progress Labels (Fig. 6):** An analysis on a contrived "palindromic" task (task completion followed by visual undoing) revealed that time-based labels (`u/T_ep`) are effective only for progress-monotonic, non-aliasing observation sequences. When observations could map to multiple progress values (e.g., the same scene appearing at both `τ` and `1-τ`), the monitor failed to learn any meaningful progress estimation, indicating its reliance on visually grounded states rather than mere step counting.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents TempoWAM, an adaptive execution scheme that has several significant implications for the field of robot manipulation and the deployment of World Action Models (WAMs).

Firstly, TempoWAM directly addresses a fundamental limitation in the current deployment of WAMs: the reliance on fixed execution horizons. By demonstrating that chunk reliability is highly stage-dependent, the work highlights the inefficiency of uniform replanning schedules. TempoWAM's ability to dynamically adjust the execution horizon based on estimated task progress leads to a consistent improvement in the efficiency-success trade-off. This translates to practical benefits such as reducing unnecessary WAM inference calls (computational cost) on easy tasks while maintaining high success rates, and crucially, improving success rates on difficult tasks by enabling timely replanning when initial action chunks become unreliable.

Secondly, a key impact of TempoWAM is its plug-and-play design. The system operates as a lightweight module that integrates with existing WAM architectures without requiring modifications or retraining of the complex backbone model. This generalizability is demonstrated by its successful deployment across different WAMs (FastWAM, Motus) and various task benchmarks (LIBERO, RoboTwin, and real-world tasks). This modularity significantly lowers the barrier to adoption, making it easier for researchers and practitioners to enhance the performance of their existing WAM-based robotic systems.

Thirdly, the methodology introduces "task progress" as a direct and explicit criterion for adaptive execution. This approach moves beyond the use of indirect proxy metrics—such as action entropy or attention weights—which may not always correlate directly with actual task advancement. By grounding execution decisions in whether the task is observably progressing, TempoWAM offers a more intuitive and potentially robust decision-making framework for robot policies.

Furthermore, the sophisticated two-timescale online calibration protocol (intra-episode EMA and inter-episode success-gated adaptation) contributes to the system's robustness and autonomy. This mechanism allows TempoWAM to adapt its replanning strategy to specific task instances and overall performance targets, effectively bridging the training-deployment gap and enabling self-tuning without requiring manual, task-specific parameter adjustments. This capability is vital for real-world robotic applications where conditions can vary.

Finally, the computational efficiency of the Recurrent Progress Monitor is notable. Being orders of magnitude less expensive than a full WAM inference call, the monitor's overhead is marginal, ensuring that the benefits of adaptive execution are not negated by increased computational burden.

The research also opens avenues for future work, particularly in exploring progress supervision methods beyond simple time-based labels, which could enhance the system's applicability to tasks with repetitive actions or non-monotonic progress. Overall, TempoWAM represents a significant step towards more intelligent, efficient, and robust robot manipulation, with broad potential for impact in both research and practical robotic deployments.
