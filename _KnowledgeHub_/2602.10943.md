# Towards Learning a Generalizable 3D Scene Representation from 2D Observations

> [!summary] Summary
> A generalizable Neural Radiance Field predicts 3D workspace occupancy directly from egocentric robot camera views. A convolutional 2D feature extractor, a world-frame cost volume built by projecting voxels into source views, a 3D U-Net, and an MLP are trained end-to-end by novel-view image reconstruction. Trained on 40 real scenes with a humanoid robot, it reaches 26mm depth error and infers occluded geometry without per-scene finetuning.

## Problem

- Vision pipelines for manipulation stay on 2D backbones and 2D image features even when grasping, collision checking, and interaction reasoning demand explicit 3D geometry.
- Standard neural radiance fields overfit one scene and need retraining for every new environment, so they cannot serve unseen object arrangements at deployment time.
- Generalizable radiance fields build cost volumes in camera-centric frustums and target view synthesis quality, not workspace-frame geometry a manipulator can plan against.

## Method

- ==World-frame cost volume==: a 9-layer convolutional extractor gives 32-channel pixel-aligned features, voxels at 1cm spacing project into each source view, and per-channel variance aggregates across views.
- A ==3D U-Net== with three strided downsampling stages plus upsampling turns the cost volume into an 8-channel feature volume for geometry reconstruction and occlusion reasoning, feeding a 128-width ==MLP== for density and color.
- Training pairs ==MSE== rendering loss with a ==Plenoxels beta loss== weighted 0.1 to binarize alpha; empty-scene background compositing forces density onto changing elements, and occupancy is read as alpha equals one minus exp of negative density.

## Results

- Three head-angle source views with **40** training scenes give **0.02638m** mean absolute depth error and **23.64** PSNR, the best configuration reported.
- Viewpoint diversity helps monotonically: single left-eye view scores **0.03754m** error, stereo left-plus-right **0.03195m**, three head poses **0.02638m** under identical training data.
- Scaling training scenes from **5** to **10**, **20**, then **40** cuts depth error from **0.04562m** to **0.03800m**, **0.03513m**, and **0.02638m**, with PSNR climbing **20.81** to **23.64**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Anchoring the cost volume in a global workspace frame rather than a camera frustum makes learned radiance geometry directly consumable by manipulation planners.
> - Photometric novel-view supervision alone suffices to hallucinate occluded geometry, so stereo depth is recovered as a two-view special case of a multi-view model.
> - Rendering quality tracks geometric accuracy here, so PSNR can proxy for reconstruction error when 3D ground truth is scarce, though depth sensors remain the honest check.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robotic manipulation tasks necessitate a comprehensive understanding of 3D scene geometry to facilitate planning, collision avoidance, and reasoning about object interactions. Traditional computer vision pipelines frequently rely on 2D image processing, which presents limitations when the target application inherently requires explicit 3D spatial understanding. This work addresses the disparity between 2D visual input and the demand for 3D scene representations in robotics.

The concept of "Neural Fields," which map coordinates to target signals, has gained prominence, particularly with the introduction of Neural Radiance Fields (NeRFs) by Mildenhall et al. (2022). NeRFs are capable of learning continuous density fields that are consistent with observed 2D images and camera poses, enabling novel view synthesis and 3D scene representation. However, conventional NeRF models typically overfit to individual scenes, requiring full retraining for new environments. This limitation restricts their applicability in dynamic robotic scenarios where environments frequently change.

To overcome this, "Generalizable NeRFs" (GNeRFs) have emerged, designed to learn across multiple scenes and infer directly on unseen scenes without finetuning. The present work builds upon this lineage, specifically referencing MVSNeRF (Chen et al., 2021), which constructs plane-swept cost volumes by aggregating features across views within a single camera frustum. However, MVSNeRF primarily focuses on novel view synthesis, and its representations are often camera-centric.

Prior research has also explored occupancy prediction using NeRFs. Density Fields (Wimbauer et al., 2023) estimates 3D density from a single view but requires proximal views during training for color retrieval. OccNeRF (Zhang et al., 2024) addresses 3D occupancy prediction for autonomous driving, integrating photometric and semantic consistency across frames. This current paper distinguishes itself by specifically tailoring the approach to a robot workspace and focusing on learning 3D occupancy from photometric consistency alone, while constructing representations in a global workspace coordinate frame, rather than a camera-centric one. This design choice directly supports robotic manipulation tasks, where a global understanding of the environment is crucial for planning and execution. The work therefore contributes to the development of more adaptable and robust 3D scene understanding methods for embodied agents.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a Generalizable Neural Radiance Field approach capable of predicting 3D workspace occupancy from egocentric robot observations. This objective is motivated by the inherent limitations of 2D vision in addressing the 3D reasoning requirements of robotic manipulation tasks such as grasp planning, collision prediction, and object interaction.

The authors advocate for a "3D-first" paradigm for embedded agents, which involves two main steps:
1. **Self-supervised 3D scene representation:** Building 3D scene models from the robot's own egocentric visual observations without requiring external ground-truth geometry.
2. **Downstream application:** Utilizing these 3D representations for subsequent perception and control tasks.
The rationale behind this paradigm is that making the 2D-to-3D lifting process explicit allows for localized geometric error identification and provides a unified world model that can serve various robotic tasks.

Neural Radiance Fields (NeRFs) are identified as a promising technology for learning these 3D representations due to their ability to learn continuous density fields that are consistent with observed 2D images. However, standard NeRFs overfit to specific scenes, necessitating retraining for new environments. This constraint motivated the development of a *generalizable* approach that can function in unseen object arrangements without requiring scene-specific finetuning.

The work aims to address limitations of traditional stereo vision methods by inferring complete geometry, including occluded regions, from sparse egocentric viewpoints. The key contributions delineated by the authors are:
- The introduction of a generalizable, workspace-centric, and geometrically consistent 3D occupancy predictor.
- The successful implementation and demonstration of this approach on a humanoid robot platform.
- A quantitative evaluation of the predicted 3D geometry using depth sensors, providing empirical validation for the method's accuracy.

In summary, the motivation stems from the need for robust, generalizable 3D scene understanding for robotic manipulation, and the objective is to fulfill this need by leveraging and extending Generalizable NeRF principles to create a workspace-centric 3D occupancy predictor.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method introduces a 3D occupancy predictor that can estimate scene geometry from one or multiple egocentric source views. The system is based on an end-to-end trained neural architecture comprising three main modules, supervised by an image reconstruction loss between a target view and its neural rendering.

#### 3.1. 3D Occupancy Predictor Architecture

The architecture, depicted in Figure 1 of the paper, consists of the following modules:

1. **2D Feature Extractor:**
 - This module processes each incoming source view.
 - It employs a lightweight 9-layer convolutional backbone.
 - The backbone includes two downsampling stages.
 - The output consists of pixel-aligned 32-channel feature maps, downsampled to H/4 × W/4 resolution.

2. **2D→3D Feature Lifting (Cost Volume Construction):**
 - A cost volume is constructed in a global world coordinate system, with a voxel spacing of 1 cm.
 - For each 3D point within this volume, it is projected into each available source view using calibrated camera intrinsics and extrinsics.
 - Corresponding RGB values and pixel-aligned features are retrieved from the 2D feature maps.
 - Features across different views are aggregated by computing the per-channel variance, resulting in a cost volume voxel grid.

3. **3D Reasoning (Geometry Reconstruction and Occlusion Reasoning):**
 - A 3D U-Net is utilized for geometry reconstruction and occlusion reasoning.
 - This U-Net progressively downsamples the input cost volume through three strided convolution stages.
 - It then upsamples the features back to the original resolution, producing an 8-channel neural feature volume.
 - Finally, a 6-channel, 128-width Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) takes inputs including bilinearly interpolated 3D volume features, relative positions within the volume, and view directions.
 - The MLP regresses density (σ) and RGB values for each sampled point.
 - Standard volumetric rendering is then used to calculate target pixel colors.
 - Pixels with incomplete opacity accumulation during rendering receive background values from empty scene images, which encourages the model to allocate density specifically to dynamic scene elements.
 - Opacity values (α = 1 − exp(−σ), with α ∈) are derived from the predicted densities and treated as probabilistic occupancy values for the 3D scene.

#### 3.2. Humanoid Robot Setup and Dataset

To validate the approach, a humanoid robot named NICOL was employed.
- **Robot Hardware:** NICOL is equipped with a 2-Degrees of Freedom (DoF) 3D-printed head structure. This head houses two 4k RGB cameras and a RealSense D435if depth camera.
- **Workspace Cameras:** Additionally, three static RealSense cameras are positioned to capture the workspace from multiple perspectives, supplementing the robot's egocentric views.
- **Calibration:** All cameras were intrinsically and extrinsically calibrated with respect to a common world coordinate system, yielding a reprojection error of approximately 2 pixels.
- **Dataset Collection:**
 - A total of 60 unique scenes were recorded.
 - Each scene contained a spatial arrangement of five objects selected from the YCB dataset. Object arrangements were varied between scenes.
 - The robot performed a scanning motion by moving its head through 15 distinct positions, from right to left, capturing 15 frames per head camera at each position.
 - Concurrently, one frame was captured from each static workspace camera.
 - The RealSense cameras provided both RGB and aligned depth data.
 - A subset of 20 scenes was designated as a consistent evaluation set, which included instances of object occlusion from the head cameras.

#### 3.3. Training Setup

- **Target View Selection:** During each training step, one RealSense camera was randomly selected as the target view. If a head RealSense camera was chosen, one of its 15 viewpoints was randomly sampled.
- **Loss Function:** The model was trained using a combination of two loss components:
 - Mean Squared Error (L_MSE) on RGB values for image reconstruction.
 - Beta loss (L_β) from Plenoxels (Fridovich-Keil et al., 2022), which is designed to encourage binary alpha values, thereby promoting distinct occupancy regions.
 - The combined loss function was defined as L = L_MSE + 0.1 × L_β.
- **Training Parameters:** The model was trained for 400 epochs with a learning rate of 5 × 10⁻⁴.

#### 3.4. Evaluation Metrics

The evaluation of the predicted 3D occupancy utilized RealSense depth measurements as ground truth.
- **Depth Map Prediction:** For each evaluation scene, depth maps were predicted from four distinct viewpoints: all static workspace cameras and the central pose of the head RealSense camera.
- **Predicted Depth Calculation:** The predicted depth (D) for a ray was computed as the minimum depth (z_i) where the accumulated opacity (sum(α_j)) along the ray reached or exceeded 0.5.
- **Error Measurement:**
 - Measurements were filtered to include only points within the defined workspace, and a validity mask was applied to both ground truth and predicted depth maps.
 - The primary metric for 3D geometry reconstruction accuracy was the Mean Absolute Error (MAE_depth).
 - Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR) was also reported to quantify the quality of the neural image rendering.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments systematically evaluated the proposed Generalizable NeRF model across various source view configurations and training dataset sizes. The main findings are summarized as follows:

#### 4.1. Geometry Prediction Error and PSNR (Table 1)

The quantitative results presented in Table 1 demonstrate the model's performance under different conditions:

- **Effect of Viewpoint Diversity:**
 - Using a single source view (Left Eye Mid, trained on 40 scenes) resulted in a Mean Absolute Depth Error (MAE_depth) of 0.03754 meters and a PSNR of 22.85 dB.
 - Employing stereo vision (Left + Right Eye Mid, trained on 40 scenes) improved performance, yielding an MAE_depth of 0.03195 meters and a PSNR of 23.49 dB.
 - Further increasing viewpoint diversity with three distinct head angles (Left Eye Left+Mid+Right, trained on 40 scenes) led to the lowest MAE_depth of 0.02638 meters and the highest PSNR of 23.64 dB.
 - **Observation:** These results indicate that increasing the diversity of input viewpoints consistently enhances both geometric reconstruction accuracy (lower MAE_depth) and image rendering quality (higher PSNR). This suggests that the model effectively integrates additional spatial information from varied camera poses.

- **Effect of Training Scene Count:**
 - With three source views (Left Eye Left+Mid+Right):
 - Training on 40 scenes resulted in an MAE_depth of 0.02638 meters and PSNR of 23.64 dB.
 - Reducing the training set to 20 scenes increased the MAE_depth to 0.03513 meters and lowered PSNR to 22.34 dB.
 - Further reductions to 10 scenes led to an MAE_depth of 0.03800 meters and PSNR of 21.48 dB.
 - Training on only 5 scenes resulted in the highest MAE_depth of 0.04562 meters and lowest PSNR of 20.81 dB.
 - **Observation:** These data illustrate that the model's performance, in terms of both geometric accuracy and rendering quality, scales positively with the number of training scenes. This indicates that the approach benefits from greater scene diversity during the training phase, which is consistent with the principles of generalizable neural networks.

#### 4.2. Correlation between PSNR and Geometric Accuracy

A strong correlation between PSNR and geometric accuracy was observed. Higher PSNR values were consistently associated with lower depth errors, suggesting that the quality of novel view synthesis is a reliable indicator of the underlying 3D geometry reconstruction fidelity.

#### 4.3. Qualitative Reconstruction of Occluded Regions

Figures 3 and 4 in the paper provide a qualitative demonstration of the model's capabilities. Specifically, Figure 3 illustrates that the model can correctly reconstruct the geometry of occluded object parts (e.g., the lower wing of an object) even when these regions are not directly visible in the input source views. This finding supports the claim that the approach is capable of inferring complete 3D occupancy, extending beyond the capabilities of traditional stereo vision methods that are limited to visible surfaces. The overall reconstruction error of 26mm (2.6 cm) achieved with 40 training scenes and three views includes these occluded regions.

In summary, the model demonstrated a mean reconstruction error of 26mm on real scenes, showed improved performance with increased source view diversity and larger training datasets, and exhibited the ability to infer complete 3D geometry, including occluded areas.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research introduces a Generalizable Neural Radiance Field (GNeRF) model that learns geometrically accurate 3D workspace representations from 2D observations via novel view synthesis. This work holds several significant implications and potential impacts, particularly for the field of robotics.

Firstly, the development of a **workspace-centric 3D occupancy predictor** is a notable contribution. Unlike many prior GNeRF approaches that operate in camera-centric coordinate frames, this method constructs 3D representations within a global workspace coordinate system. This design choice is directly relevant and applicable to robotic manipulation tasks, where a consistent, global understanding of the environment is essential for planning paths, grasping objects, and avoiding collisions. By providing a fixed reference frame, the model simplifies the integration of perception outputs into robot control systems.

Secondly, the model's **generalization capability** is a key advantage. The ability to generalize to unseen object arrangements without requiring scene-specific finetuning significantly reduces the deployment burden for robots operating in dynamic or novel environments. This contrasts with traditional NeRFs that require extensive retraining for each new scene, making them impractical for many real-world robotic applications. This generalizability contributes to the development of more autonomous and adaptable robotic systems.

Thirdly, the method's capacity to **infer complete 3D geometry, including occluded regions**, from sparse egocentric viewpoints surpasses the limitations of traditional stereo vision techniques. Conventional stereo methods are typically restricted to reconstructing visible surfaces. The ability to predict the geometry of hidden parts of objects or scenes is crucial for robust manipulation, as robots often need to reason about object stability or potential collisions involving unobserved sections. This comprehensive understanding enhances the robot's perception and decision-making capabilities.

The successful implementation and demonstration of the approach on a **humanoid robot platform** validates its practical applicability and hardware compatibility. Quantitative evaluation against 3D sensor ground truth provides empirical evidence for the model's accuracy, demonstrating a mean reconstruction error of 26mm, which includes occluded regions. This level of accuracy is a foundational requirement for precise robotic interaction.

Looking forward, the research lays a groundwork for several potential future directions:
- **Scalability and transfer learning:** Plans to scale the dataset to include a greater diversity of object categories and scene complexities could lead to models with broader applicability. Investigating knowledge transfer across object classes would further enhance generalization.
- **Semantic understanding:** Extending the model to predict multi-channel semantic features, effectively lifting 2D semantic information into the 3D representation, represents a significant step. This would enable task-relevant scene understanding directly in 3D space, moving beyond mere occupancy to identify and categorize objects, surfaces, or regions relevant to specific tasks. Such an advancement could empower robots with more sophisticated reasoning for complex manipulation scenarios.

In summary, this work provides a framework for embodied agents to acquire robust, generalizable 3D scene representations from visual input, directly addressing a core challenge in robotics and paving the way for more intelligent and adaptable autonomous systems.
