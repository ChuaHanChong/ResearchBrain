# Language Models Can Learn from Verbal Feedback Without Scalar Rewards

> [!summary] Summary
> This research introduces the Feedback-Conditional Policy (FCP) framework, enabling Large Language Models to learn directly from verbal feedback without converting it into scalar rewards. The FCP, particularly when enhanced with an online bootstrapping stage, achieves performance comparable to or exceeding established scalar-based reinforcement learning methods while preserving the richness of feedback.

## Problem

- Reliance on scalar rewards in Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) causes information loss from rich, nuanced verbal feedback.
- Mapping complex, mixed, or ambiguous human and AI feedback to precise scalar values is inherently ambiguous and arbitrary.
- Maintaining consistent reward scales across diverse tasks (e.g., math, coding) is difficult, leading to imbalanced learning signals and potential biases.

## Method

- A ==Feedback-Conditional Policy (FCP)== is proposed, treating verbal feedback as a direct conditioning signal for LLMs, similar to captions in text-to-image generation.
- An ==offline training stage== initializes the FCP by maximizing the likelihood of observed response-feedback pairs, utilizing diverse feedback types.
- An ==online bootstrapping stage== iteratively refines the policy by generating responses conditioned on desired positive feedback and updating with fresh, real-world verbal feedback from an environment.

## Results

- FCP+Bootstrap achieved an average accuracy of **38.7%** on mathematical reasoning and **47.8%** on general reasoning benchmarks, matching or surpassing leading scalar-based baselines like GRPO and RFT.
- The model demonstrated strong controllability, significantly increasing accuracy when conditioned on 'fully_positive' feedback (**68.5%** on MATH500) and adapting to stylistic requests like 'has_code' (**74.3%** of responses containing code).
- FCP exhibited robustness against reward hacking, maintaining high accuracy with lower assigned scalar rewards compared to GRPO, suggesting it learns a more direct alignment to desired behavior.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Large Language Models can effectively learn and be aligned directly from verbal feedback, challenging the long-standing necessity of scalar rewards in language-centric systems.
> - Direct verbal conditioning preserves the full nuance and context of feedback, avoiding information loss and ambiguity inherent in scalarization.
> - The framework enables fine-grained control over LLM behavior and stylistic attributes at inference time by leveraging diverse feedback without explicit categorization of positive/negative signals.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper addresses a fundamental challenge in the training and alignment of Large Language Models (LLMs): the ubiquitous reliance on scalar rewards in Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) and related methodologies. Since Richard Sutton's "Reward Hypothesis" over two decades ago, the paradigm of reducing environmental feedback to a cumulative scalar signal has dominated Reinforcement Learning (RL). This approach has been widely adopted in LLM alignment, notably in methods like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), and Reinforcement Finetuning (RFT), where human preferences or AI critiques are distilled into scalar reward models (e.g., Ziegler et al., 2019; Bai et al., 2022; Rafailov et al., 2023; Guo et al., 2025).

The authors argue that while scalar rewards were historically necessary for RL algorithms, the rise of powerful LLMs with inherent linguistic and commonsense priors offers an opportunity to reconsider this paradigm. LLMs already demonstrate an implicit understanding of verbal feedback, as seen in their ability to iteratively refine responses in agentic tasks by integrating natural language critiques (Wang et al., 2025b; Novikov et al., 2025). However, this implicit understanding has not been fully leveraged for direct, explicit model training, often remaining an inefficient, multi-turn trial-and-error process at inference time.

This work directly challenges the necessity of scalarization by proposing a new framework that treats verbal feedback as a first-class conditioning signal, moving beyond the reward maximization paradigm. It draws an insightful analogy to text-to-image generation, where models learn to synthesize novel images from unseen combinations of descriptive captions, suggesting that LLMs can similarly learn to produce high-quality responses by conditioning on diverse verbal feedback, even if mixed or novel. By reframing feedback-driven learning as a conditional generation problem rather than a reward optimization one, the paper offers a principled alternative that promises to better preserve the richness of human or AI feedback, avoid issues of reward scale imbalance, and potentially mitigate reward hacking, thereby pushing the boundaries of LLM alignment techniques.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and validate a novel framework that enables Large Language Models (LLMs) to learn directly from verbal feedback without the need to convert it into scalar rewards. The authors are motivated by several critical limitations inherent in the prevailing scalar-based RL approaches for LLM training:

- **Information Loss**: Verbal feedback, such as "Good start, but the code can be more efficient," is rich with nuanced information. When compressed into a single scalar reward (e.g., 0.8), much of this detail is inevitably lost. Different critiques can map to the same scalar, obscuring distinct patterns of desirable or undesirable behavior. This is particularly problematic as internal thoughts or explanations generated by AI-based reward models are often discarded, with only the final numerical score retained.
- **Ambiguity and Nuance in Feedback**: Real-world feedback, especially from human users, is rarely clear-cut. It can be mixed (containing both positive and negative aspects), emotional ("I’m so happy"), or uncertain ("I’m not sure, maybe try again?"). Mapping such complex, often fuzzy, feedback to a precise scalar is inherently ambiguous and arbitrary, leading to potential misinterpretations during training.
- **Imbalanced Reward Scales Across Tasks**: In multi-task learning environments (e.g., math, coding, general reasoning), maintaining a consistent and fair reward scale is exceedingly difficult. A "good" reward on a simple task might be disproportionately easier to obtain than on a complex one, leading to imbalanced learning signals and biases across domains.

Recognizing that LLMs already possess strong linguistic priors and can implicitly understand verbal feedback, the authors aim to distill this capability into an explicit, direct training signal. Their core motivation is to provide a more expressive, efficient, and robust way for LLMs to leverage the full richness of verbal feedback, thereby improving model performance and alignment without the compromises introduced by scalarization.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The paper proposes the **Feedback-Conditional Policy (FCP)**, a framework that treats verbal feedback as a direct conditioning signal for LLM training, akin to how captions guide text-to-image generation. The methodology unfolds in two main stages: offline training and online bootstrapping.

#### 3.1. Offline Training: Initializing Feedback-Conditional Policy (Algorithm 1)

The first stage involves training an initial FCP model using offline data of response-feedback pairs.
- **Reference Policy and Environment**: The process begins with a `reference policy` ($\pi_{ref}$), which is a base LLM that generates a response `o` given an instruction `x`. An `environment` ($p_{env}$) then provides verbal feedback `c` based on the instruction and the generated response. This feedback can be from human users or generative reward models.
- **Joint Distribution and Conditional Posterior**: The training data consists of triples `(x, o, c)`. The authors define the joint distribution of response-feedback pairs as $P_{off}(o, c|x) = \pi_{ref}(o|x) \cdot p_{env}(c|x, o)$. From this, they derive the feedback-conditional posterior $P_{off}(o|x, c) \propto \pi_{ref}(o|x) \cdot p_{env}(c|x, o)$. This posterior intuitively reweighs the reference policy by how likely a response `o` is to elicit the observed feedback `c`.
- **Optimization Objective**: While approximating $P_{off}(o|x, c^+)$ (conditioning on purely positive feedback) is the ultimate goal, directly sampling from it is intractable. Instead, the authors propose to learn a feedback-conditional policy $\pi_\theta(o|x, c)$ that approximates $P_{off}(o|x, c)$ for *any* feedback `c`. This is achieved by minimizing the expected forward KL divergence, which simplifies to maximizing the likelihood of observed `(o, c)` pairs:
 $ \max_{\pi_\theta} E_{\pi_{ref}(o|x)} [ E_{p_{env}(c|x,o)} [\log \pi_\theta(o|x, c)] ] $
 This objective allows training with diverse feedback, including mixed or uncertain signals, without explicit categorization of positive/negative feedback. The LLM's language priors are expected to implicitly interpret and combine the information.
- **Implementation**: The FCP is implemented by concatenating the verbal feedback `c` as a conditioning signal before the instruction `x` (e.g., using special tokens `<EF>` and `</EF>`). Training uses standard maximum likelihood (cross-entropy loss) on the collected offline dataset.

#### 3.2. Online Training: Bootstrapping by Conditioning on Positive Feedback (Algorithm 2)

After the offline FCP is initialized ($\pi_{\theta_{off}}$), an online bootstrapping stage further refines the policy by explicitly conditioning on desired positive feedback.
- **Iterative Refinement**: The model iteratively updates its parameters ($\theta_t$) by sampling rollouts from the current policy conditioned on positive feedback ($\pi_{\theta_t}(o|x, c^+)$).
- **Fresh Feedback Collection**: For each generated response `o`, fresh feedback `c` is obtained from the environment ($p_{env}(c|x, o)$). Crucially, this `c` can differ from the initial `c^+` (which represents desired feedback), providing a real-world assessment.
- **Optimization Objective**: Similar to the offline stage, the model is updated by maximizing the likelihood of the new `(o, c)` pairs collected online, where `o` was generated under the `c^+` condition:
 $ \max_{\pi_{\theta_{t+1}}} E_{p_{user}(c^+|x)} [ E_{\pi_{\theta_t}(o|x,c^+)} [ E_{p_{env}(c|x,o)} [\log \pi_{\theta_{t+1}}(o|x, c)] ] ] $
 This iterative process allows the model to learn to identify cases where conditioning on `c^+` does not actually lead to positive critiques, while reinforcing behaviors that align with the expected feedback, thus strengthening alignment.
- **Flexibility**: The number of gradient steps `S` per rollout round can be adjusted, allowing interpolation between fully online and semi-online training.
- **Feedback Environment Simulation**: For experiments, GPT-5-nano simulates the feedback environment, providing both low-quality "real-world user-style" and high-quality "professional reviewer-style" feedback, along with a scalar score, ensuring a fair comparison with scalar-based baselines.

This dual-stage approach leverages the expressiveness of verbal feedback in both initial broad learning and targeted online refinement, without ever converting feedback to scalar rewards for the policy optimization itself.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The authors evaluate FCP on mathematical and general reasoning tasks, comparing its performance against strong scalar-based baselines like Rejection Sampling Finetuning (RFT) and Group-Normalized Policy Optimization (GRPO). The base model used is Qwen2.5-7B-base.

#### 4.1. Performance Comparison

- **Offline FCP vs. RFT**: On mathematical reasoning benchmarks (AIME24&25, MATH500, Minerva, Olympiad), offline FCP achieved an average accuracy of 28.8%, outperforming the base model (27.0%) but slightly lagging behind RFT (30.4%). A similar trend was observed for general reasoning (GPQA-Diamond, MMLU-Pro, TheoremQA), where FCP (43.5%) was better than the base (38.7%) but below RFT (44.6%). This is expected, as RFT benefits from meticulous correctness filtering, whereas offline FCP learns from all response-feedback pairs, including noisy ones.
- **FCP+Bootstrap Rivals Scalarized RL**: The online bootstrapping stage significantly improved FCP's performance. FCP+Bootstrap reached 38.7% average accuracy on the math suite, slightly *surpassing* GRPO (38.4%) and performing comparably to RFT+GRPO (37.1%). For out-of-distribution instruction following (IFEval), FCP+Bootstrap achieved 39.0%, matching GRPO (38.5%) and RFT+GRPO (38.8%). In general reasoning, FCP+Bootstrap achieved 47.8%, effectively matching the best scalar-based baseline (RFT+GRPO at 47.5%). These results demonstrate that FCP, especially with online bootstrapping, can achieve competitive or superior performance compared to leading scalar-based RL methods, validating its effectiveness.

#### 4.2. Learning Dynamics and Controllability

- **Controllable Behavior**: FCP demonstrated an ability to learn and respond to diverse feedback conditions. When conditioned on "fully_positive" feedback, FCP on MATH500 achieved 68.5% accuracy, significantly outperforming its base model performance. Conversely, under "fully_negative" conditions, accuracy plummeted to 17.1%, far below the base model's 63.8%. This stark contrast confirms that the model effectively internalizes the control signal embedded in verbal feedback.
- **Stylistic Control**: Beyond correctness, FCP adapted to stylistic conditions. Under a "neutral" condition requesting a correct but verbose solution, accuracy slightly decreased, but response length increased, indicating a learned trade-off. When conditioned with "has_code," the proportion of responses containing code rose to 74.3%, demonstrating its ability to follow stylistic attributes. This shows FCP's capacity to absorb negative samples without compromising performance under positive conditions, enabling broad data utilization.
- **Avoiding Reward Hacking**: Analysis of training dynamics (Figure 2) showed that while both FCP+Bootstrap and GRPO achieved peak accuracy within 30 online steps, FCP's scalar reward scores (as assigned by GPT-5-nano) lagged behind GRPO's. Despite lower scalar scores, FCP maintained high accuracy. This suggests that FCP avoids the reward-hacking behavior often observed in scalar-based methods, indicating that verbal feedback acts as a more robust training signal that directly guides desired behavior without optimizing for an imperfect proxy.
- **Length-Related Conditions and Stability**: The study found that feedback conditions related to output length (e.g., "concise") could destabilize online bootstrapping. Including these conditions caused response length to shrink over time and led to unstable cross-entropy loss. Filtering out length-related conditions, however, resulted in steadily longer responses, aligning with the observation that extended reasoning traces are beneficial for mathematical problem-solving.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies

- **Feedback Style**: Using "real-world user-style" feedback (noisier, colloquial) for FCP led to a 2.5-point drop in math accuracy compared to "professional reviewer-style" feedback, but still yielded a notable +5.8% gain over the base model. This highlights that while high-quality feedback is more effective, even noisy, low-cost user feedback can be surprisingly competitive and scalable.
- **Online Update Strategy**: A "partial online" setup (larger prompt batch size, fewer gradient updates per rollout) improved accuracy by +3.4% over a fully online setup, suggesting that optimizing the balance between data collection and gradient steps can enhance efficiency.
- **Loss Aggregation**: A debiased sequence-level averaging and token-level summing scheme for cross-entropy loss provided a consistent +0.7% gain, addressing length bias issues.
- **Comparison to Critique Finetuning (CFT)**: When applied to the same coarse, lightweight feedback as FCP, CFT performed worse than the base model. This underscores FCP's strength in effectively leveraging readily available, less-structured verbal feedback without requiring costly, fine-grained annotations.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant advancement in the field of Large Language Model (LLM) training and alignment, fundamentally challenging the long-standing "Reward Hypothesis" in Reinforcement Learning (RL). The Feedback-Conditional Policy (FCP) framework offers a compelling alternative to scalar-reward-based RLHF, with several profound implications:

- **Rethinking the Reward Hypothesis**: By demonstrating that LLMs can learn effectively from direct verbal feedback without scalarization, the paper reopens the debate on whether reward maximization is the only or best paradigm for language-centric systems. This work suggests that for LLMs, which inherently operate in the linguistic domain, direct linguistic signals may be more natural and informative than numerical abstractions.
- **Preserving Feedback Richness**: FCP's ability to treat verbal feedback as a first-class conditioning signal directly addresses the problem of information loss associated with scalarization. This allows LLMs to leverage the full nuance, context, and mixed sentiments contained in human or AI critiques, leading to a more expressive and precise learning process.
- **Increased Data Efficiency and Robustness**: Unlike methods like RFT that discard "bad" or "noisy" data, FCP utilizes diverse feedback, including mixed and uncertain forms, for training. This increases data efficiency and enables the model to learn from a broader spectrum of interactions. Moreover, by not directly optimizing against a scalar reward model, FCP appears more robust to reward hacking, a common pitfall in scalar-based RL that can lead to misaligned behaviors.
- **Enhanced Controllability and Customization**: The framework introduces a powerful mechanism for controlling LLM behavior at inference time. Users can specify desired positive feedback conditions, allowing for highly customized and context-aware response generation. This has immense potential for personalization and fine-grained control over LLM outputs, which is critical for complex applications and diverse user needs.
- **Scalability and Accessibility of Feedback**: The finding that FCP can achieve competitive performance even with "real-world user-style" (noisier, lower-cost) feedback is crucial for scalability. This broadens the accessibility of feedback sources, potentially reducing the reliance on expensive, high-quality, rubric-constrained annotations.

**Potential Future Directions**: The authors outline several promising extensions:
- **Combining with Verifiable Rewards**: Integrating FCP with verifiable scalar rewards (e.g., treating absence of feedback as a neutral condition) could blend the strengths of both paradigms.
- **Multi-Turn Interactions**: Extending FCP to multi-turn dialogues, where feedback is incorporated before each subsequent generation, could enable more dynamic and iterative human guidance.
- **Test-Time Adaptation**: Conditioning the model on a few user-provided examples to rapidly adapt to individual feedback styles, similar to personalization in text-to-image models.
- **Multimodal Feedback**: Expanding the feedback condition `c` to include multimodal inputs could unlock new capabilities for interactive and context-rich LLMs.

In conclusion, this paper provides a robust framework for feedback-driven LLM learning that bypasses the limitations of scalar rewards. By establishing verbal feedback as a first-class training signal and FCP as a natural, scalable alternative, it paves the way for more aligned, controllable, and adaptable LLMs, significantly impacting the future of AI alignment research.
