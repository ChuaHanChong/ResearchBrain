# Behavior Retrieval: Few-Shot Imitation Learning by Querying Unlabeled Datasets

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Stanford University introduced Behavior Retrieval, a method for few-shot imitation learning that selectively leverages large, unlabeled offline datasets using a learned state-action similarity metric. This approach consistently improved success rates by over 20% on real robot tasks and more than 30% in simulations compared to standard pre-training and fine-tuning baselines, especially under distribution shifts.

## Problem

- Acquiring new visuomotor skills for robots is costly due to the expense and time required for collecting task-specific human expert data.
- Existing methods either overfit when fine-tuning on limited expert data or suffer from including irrelevant/sub-optimal behaviors from large offline datasets.
- There is a lack of effective mechanisms to intelligently leverage broad, unlabeled offline data to learn low-level control policies given minimal task-specific expert data.

## Method

- A ==Variational Autoencoder (VAE)== is pre-trained on a large, unlabeled prior dataset to learn a robust state-action embedding and a similarity metric.
- This similarity metric is then used to filter the prior dataset, retrieving only transitions highly relevant to a small set of task-specific expert demonstrations, forming the **Behavior Retrieval** pipeline.
- The target policy is trained using standard ==behavior cloning== on the combined small expert dataset and the newly retrieved relevant data, using an ==LSTM== architecture with ==GMM== outputs.

## Results

- Behavior Retrieval consistently outperformed traditional pre-training and fine-tuning methods, achieving over **30%** average improvement in success rate across simulated tasks.
- On real robot manipulation tasks (NutAssembly, Pickle, Soap), the method improved success rates by over **20%** compared to baselines and demonstrated robustness to distribution shifts.
- It nearly matched or even exceeded the performance of a 'Ground Truth' oracle (which filters using privileged task labels), especially with limited expert demonstrations.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A small amount of task-specific expert data can serve as a query to identify and retrieve relevant behaviors from vast, unlabeled offline datasets.
> - Functional similarity between state-action pairs, rather than just state similarity, is crucial for effectively identifying useful prior data segments.
> - Behavior Retrieval can extract useful sub-trajectories from otherwise irrelevant demonstrations, allowing it to leverage shared components across diverse tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics is actively pursuing methods to enable robots to acquire novel visuomotor skills with high data efficiency, particularly in complex real-world environments. A common strategy involves leveraging large, diverse, unlabeled datasets to establish general knowledge, subsequently adapting a policy to a specific task using a limited amount of human supervision, such as demonstrations or interventions.

Prior research has explored various facets of this problem. Approaches include visual pre-training and multi-task imitation to learn from sub-optimal offline data, as well as few-shot learning techniques that focus on efficient learning from expert demonstrations. Another line of work, meta-learning, aims to train policies that can adapt efficiently to new tasks given a few demonstrations, but typically requires large datasets of *labeled* tasks for meta-training.

A significant challenge within this landscape is how to effectively integrate and balance the broad, often sub-optimal, offline data with the precise, but scarce, task-specific expert data during the fine-tuning phase. Many existing methods either fail to utilize offline data during fine-tuning or rely on simple heuristics that may not effectively balance these disparate data sources. Fine-tuning solely on a small expert dataset can lead to instability and overfitting, potentially causing the policy to diverge from beneficial pre-trained knowledge. Conversely, naively combining expert and offline data for joint training can be suboptimal if the offline data contains a substantial amount of irrelevant or detrimental behaviors for the target task. Some prior works focus on learning high-level skills, while others explore selective sampling or weighting for learning from mixed sub-optimal experts for a *single* task.

This work addresses the specific gap of intelligently leveraging broad, unlabeled offline data to learn *low-level control policies* when provided with minimal task-specific expert data. It proposes a mechanism to actively select relevant portions of the offline data during fine-tuning, thereby moving beyond simple pre-training and subsequent fine-tuning or generalized multi-task learning, and aiming for more targeted and efficient data utilization.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable robots to learn novel visuomotor skills data-efficiently by optimally utilizing two distinct data sources: a small amount of task-specific human expert data and a large, broad, unlabeled dataset that may contain diverse, potentially sub-optimal behaviors.

The motivation for this objective stems from several challenges inherent in contemporary robot learning paradigms:
1. **Cost of Human Supervision:** Acquiring substantial amounts of high-quality human expert data (e.g., demonstrations or online interventions) for every new task is expensive and time-consuming. This limits the scalability and widespread deployment of robots that rely heavily on such data.
2. **Ineffectiveness of Naive Data Usage:**
 - **Overfitting to Small Expert Data:** When a policy is fine-tuned solely on a limited set of task-specific expert data, it can quickly overfit, losing valuable general knowledge acquired during prior broad pre-training. This leads to unstable learning and poor generalization.
 - **Harm from Irrelevant Offline Data:** Conversely, if the agent is jointly trained on the expert data and the entire broad offline dataset, a significant portion of the offline data may be irrelevant, sub-optimal, or even adversarial to the target task. Including such data can dilute the learning signal from the expert data, making the learning process less efficient or leading to suboptimal policy performance.
3. **Lack of Targeted Prior Information Utilization:** Existing methods often treat the broad offline dataset as a general knowledge base for pre-training, which is then fine-tuned. The precise role of the small task-specific data during fine-tuning is typically limited to providing additional samples for training the target task.

The key insight driving this work is that the small amount of task-specific human expert data can serve a dual function. Beyond simply providing new data for an agent to train on, it can also *inform* the system about which behaviors within the vast, unlabeled offline dataset are relevant and useful for learning the current task. By actively identifying and retrieving only these relevant behaviors, the agent can form a more focused and effective training set, thereby mitigating the issues of overfitting and detrimental data exposure. This strategy aims to leverage the extensive information present in large offline datasets without being hampered by their heterogeneity and lack of explicit task labels.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, termed "Behavior Retrieval," addresses the problem of few-shot imitation learning by intelligently leveraging unlabeled offline datasets. It operates under a problem setup where access is provided to a small dataset of expert (s, a) tuples for a target task (`D_t`) and a large, unsegmented, and potentially sub-optimal prior dataset (`D_prior`) containing behaviors from various tasks. The method comprises three main stages:

**A. Similarity Metric Pre-training**
The initial step involves learning a robust similarity metric to quantify the functional resemblance between any two state-action (s, a) pairs. This metric is crucial because simple state similarity is insufficient, as different actions in similar states can lead to vastly different outcomes or task relevance.
1. **Embedding Learning:** A low-dimensional (s, a) embedding space is learned from the `D_prior` dataset. The chosen approach is to train a Variational Autoencoder (VAE) jointly on states and actions.
2. **VAE Objective:** The VAE is trained to maximize the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO) on state-action pairs sampled from `D_prior`. The objective function is defined as:
 `E_{q_φ(z_sa|s,a)}[log p_θ(s, a|z_sa)] - βD_KL[q_φ(z_sa|s, a)||p(z_sa)]`
 Here, `q_φ` represents the encoder, `p_θ` the decoder, `z_sa` is the state-action embedding, and `β` is a hyperparameter balancing reconstruction and KL divergence.
3. **Embedding Structure:** States and actions are initially encoded separately. These encodings are then combined through a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) to form the state-action embedding `z_sa`. Additionally, the action is concatenated directly to `z_sa` to form the final embedding `z = [z_sa, a]`, which was empirically found to enhance behavior separability.
4. **Similarity Function:** The similarity function `F(s1, a1, s2, a2)` is defined as the negative L2 distance between the embeddings of the two state-action pairs: `F(s1, a1, s2, a2) = -||z1 - z2||^2`.

**B. Filtering Relevant Data**
Once the similarity metric `F` is pre-trained, it is used to retrieve relevant data from `D_prior` based on its similarity to the task-specific expert data `D_t`.
1. **Relevance Calculation:** For each transition `(s*, a*)` in `D_prior`, the maximum similarity to any transition `(s, a)` in `D_t` is computed: `max_(s,a)∈D_t F(s*, a*, s, a)`.
2. **Normalization:** To account for varying magnitudes of embeddings across tasks, this similarity score is normalized using a min-max scaling: `(max_(s,a)∈D_t F(s*, a*, s, a) - F^-) / (F^+ - F^-)`, where `F^+` is the global maximum similarity and `F^-` is the global minimum similarity across all `D_prior` and `D_t` pairs.
3. **Selection Threshold:** A transition `(s*, a*)` is retrieved if its normalized maximum similarity score exceeds a predefined threshold `δ`. The parameter `δ` effectively controls the strictness of the filtering; a lower `δ` retrieves more data, while a higher `δ` filters more aggressively. Empirically, `δ` values between `0.6` and `0.8` were found to be effective. The retrieved subset of `D_prior` is denoted as `D_ret`.

**C. Training with Retrieved Data**
In the final stage, the target policy `π_ψ` is trained using a combination of the small expert dataset `D_t` and the newly retrieved relevant data `D_ret`.
1. **Policy Objective:** A standard behavior cloning (BC) loss is applied to both datasets:
 `min_ψ E_(s,a)∼D_t [-log π_ψ(a|s)] + E_(s,a)∼D_ret [-log π_ψ(a|s)]`
 This objective minimizes the negative log-likelihood of the actions from both the expert and retrieved datasets under the policy.
2. **Policy Architecture:** The policy employs an LSTM architecture to process multi-modal observations, including third-person and eye-in-hand camera views, end-effector position and orientation, and gripper state. Images are processed via ResNet-18 encoders, which are then concatenated with proprioceptive data before being fed into a two-layer LSTM. The policy outputs parameters for a Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) with 5 modes, enabling the modeling of multi-modal behaviors.
3. **Implementation Details:** For real robot tasks, pre-trained R3M weights were used for the ResNet-18 encoders to enhance adaptation to visually diverse data. The entire process, from embedding learning to policy training, is summarized in Algorithm 1 provided in the paper.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments evaluated Behavior Retrieval across various simulated and real robotic manipulation tasks, focusing on its effectiveness, the nature of data retrieved, and its robustness.

**A. Qualitative Analysis of Retrieved Data:**
- **Relevance:** Visualizations (Figure 7, 8, and Appendix Figure 14) demonstrated that Behavior Retrieval successfully identifies and ranks transitions from `D_prior` that are visibly relevant to the target task in `D_t`. Conversely, it assigns low scores to transitions from different or adversarial tasks (e.g., placing a nut on the wrong peg).
- **Fine-Grained Retrieval:** In tasks like `NutAssembly`, the method was observed to retrieve data from both "correct" and "incorrect" `D_prior` demonstrations during the initial grasping phase, as the grasping behavior itself is relevant to the target task. However, once the object was grasped, the similarity score for task-irrelevant `D_prior` data significantly dropped, indicating that the method effectively discards irrelevant subsequent actions while retaining useful sub-trajectories. This highlights its ability to leverage shared, relevant components from otherwise irrelevant demonstrations.
- **Embedding Space Structure:** T-SNE projections of the embedding space (Appendix Figure 12) illustrated that trajectories formed distinct clusters corresponding to different task types, while also showing overlaps where shared sub-trajectories were present.

**B. Performance Comparison with Pre-training + Fine-tuning Schemes:**
- **Outperformance:** Across three simulated tasks (CanPick, NutAssembly, Office) and both image and state-only observations, Behavior Retrieval consistently outperformed traditional multi-task pre-training with goal-conditioned behavior cloning (GCBC D_prior) and GCBC with additional fine-tuning on `D_t` (GCBC D_prior + FT D_t).
- **Average Gains:** Behavior Retrieval achieved an average improvement of over 30% in success rate compared to these baselines (Table I).
- **Explanation:** The paper suggests that pure offline GCBC may struggle with underfitting or over-specification of goal states. Naive fine-tuning, even with balanced batches, can suffer from the inclusion of irrelevant offline data, while training solely on `D_t` risks overfitting. Behavior Retrieval's selective filtering addresses these issues by providing a focused, relevant training set.

**C. Performance Comparison with Other Retrieval Strategies:**
- **Superiority:** Behavior Retrieval demonstrated significant improvements over other data utilization strategies, including training solely on `D_t`, training on balanced batches of `D_prior` and `D_t`, and even a "Ground Truth" oracle that filters `D_prior` using privileged task labels.
- **Context of `D_t`:** This held true for `D_t` provided as either online interventions or a small set of 10 expert demonstrations (Figure 9). With 10 demos, Behavior Retrieval nearly matched the performance of the Ground Truth oracle.
- **Robustness to `D_t` Format:** The method showed robustness to the form of expert data, yielding around 10% improvement with online interventions and over 20% with few-shot demonstrations.
- **Baselines' Limitations:** Training solely on `D_t` often exhibited instability and overfitting, particularly with limited data. Naive mixing of `D_prior` and `D_t` led to suboptimal performance due to the inclusion of task-irrelevant data.

**D. Performance on a Real Robot:**
- **Real-World Efficacy:** The benefits of Behavior Retrieval extended to physical robot experiments using a WidowX robot on three tasks (NutAssembly, Pickle, Soap).
- **Significant Improvement:** It improved success rates by over 20% compared to baseline methods, including pre-training, fine-tuning, and naive data mixing (Figure 10).
- **Exceeding Oracle Performance:** In some real-world scenarios (e.g., Pickle task), Behavior Retrieval *exceeded* the performance of the Ground Truth filtering baseline. This suggests its ability to identify and leverage useful *transitions* even from `D_prior` trajectories that are not globally labeled for the exact target task, demonstrating a finer granularity of data utility.
- **Context-Specific Observations:** Similar to simulations, `D_t`-only learning was unstable. Fine-tuning after GCBC pre-training showed a more pronounced impact in real-world settings, possibly due to larger distribution shifts between the Bridge Data `D_prior` and the specific target environment.

**E. Robustness Evaluation:**
- **Distribution Shifts:** On the real-world Pickle task, Behavior Retrieval demonstrated robustness to various distribution shifts, including changes in cup color, using a real pickle, visual distractors, and physical perturbations.
- **Maintained Performance:** It consistently maintained strong performance (near 50% success rate) across these shifts, whereas the `D_t`-only model's performance significantly collapsed (around 10% success rate) (Table II).
- **Cross-Robot Generalization:** The method successfully utilized Bridge Data collected with a WidowX250 robot for tasks executed on a WidowX200 robot, further indicating robustness to hardware variations.

**F. Ablation Studies:**
- **Embedding Space:** A VAE-based state-action embedding outperformed an embedding learned through contrastive RL across all simulated tasks. Contrastive RL was found to be less stable and sometimes failed to train effectively (Table III).
- **Filtering Threshold `δ`:** The method showed general robustness to the choice of the selection threshold `δ`. Optimal performance was observed around `δ = 0.75`. Performance degraded gracefully with lower `δ` (including more potentially irrelevant data), but sharply with excessively high `δ` (filtering out too much useful data) (Figure 11).

**G. Additional Experiments (Appendix D):**
- **Task Stitching:** Behavior Retrieval successfully learned sequential tasks by stitching together relevant sub-behaviors from short-horizon trajectories in `D_prior`, outperforming baselines significantly (Table IV).
- **Combination with Other Pre-training:** It was shown to be combinable with other policy pre-training methods like LMP (Latent Plans from Play), leading to performance improvements (Table V).
- **Full Bridge Dataset:** Even when `D_prior` consisted of the full, highly diverse Bridge Dataset (with only a small portion relevant to the target task), Behavior Retrieval provided significant performance benefits (55% success vs. 30-35% for baselines) with an appropriate `δ`.
- **Failure Mode Analysis:** The distribution of failure types for Behavior Retrieval in real robot tasks was similar to that of the Ground Truth oracle, with most failures occurring at critical task bottlenecks (grasping and placing), suggesting effective learning of intermediate behaviors (Table VI).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents "Behavior Retrieval," a method that offers a novel approach to addressing a core challenge in robot learning: efficiently acquiring visuomotor skills with limited expert data by leveraging large, unlabeled, and potentially sub-optimal offline datasets.

The significance and potential impact of this work include:

1. **Enhanced Data Efficiency:** The method demonstrates substantial improvements in success rates (consistently over 20% in many scenarios) with a minimal amount of task-specific expert data (e.g., 10 demonstrations or limited interventions). This directly translates to reduced human supervision effort and cost, a critical factor for scaling robotic deployments.
2. **Effective Utilization of Heterogeneous Data:** By proposing a mechanism to actively query and retrieve relevant behaviors from diverse and unlabeled offline datasets, the work moves beyond the limitations of naive data mixing or reliance on perfectly labeled multi-task datasets. This enhances the utility of existing large "play" or pre-collected datasets, which are often unstructured and noisy.
3. **Improved Policy Robustness:** Behavior Retrieval contributes to policies that are more robust to distribution shifts in real-world scenarios, such as changes in object appearance, distractors, or physical perturbations. This increased resilience is attributed to the broader yet task-relevant experience extracted from the offline data.
4. **Flexible Integration:** The modular nature of Behavior Retrieval, particularly its embedding learning and filtering stages, suggests it can be readily combined with various existing pre-training schemes, policy architectures, and action spaces. Its demonstrated ability to integrate with methods like LMP and benefit from R3M pre-trained visual representations supports this adaptability.
5. **Refined Understanding of Data Value:** The qualitative analysis of retrieved data highlights that not all parts of an expert or even an irrelevant offline demonstration are equally valuable. The method's ability to extract specific, useful sub-trajectories from otherwise non-target behaviors offers a more nuanced approach to data utilization, ensuring that the learning signal is clean and pertinent.
6. **Practical Implications for Robot Deployment:** By making robot learning more data-efficient and robust, Behavior Retrieval could accelerate the deployment of robots in complex, unstructured environments like homes, factories, and service industries, where collecting vast amounts of task-specific expert data for every new skill is impractical. It shifts the focus towards methods that can effectively "bootstrap" new skills from a wealth of general interaction data.

While the current approach relies on learned similarity in a compressed embedding space, which might not capture all task-relevant components in extreme domain shifts, and requires access to the prior dataset during new task learning, it lays a foundation for future research. Potential future directions include developing more semantically rich similarity metrics, optimizing for memory-constrained applications, and systematically exploring its synergistic combination with other advanced imitation learning and large-scale pre-training techniques.
