# AceReason-Nemotron 1.1: Advancing Math and Code Reasoning through SFT and RL Synergy

> [!summary] Summary
> NVIDIA researchers developed AceReason-Nemotron 1.1, a Qwen2.5-7B based model, by systematically investigating the synergy between supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and reinforcement learning (RL) to enhance math and code reasoning. The model achieved new state-of-the-art performance for its size on challenging benchmarks like AIME25 (64.8%) and LiveCodeBench v6 (52.1%).

## Problem

- Prior research lacked a systematic study on the synergy between supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and reinforcement learning (RL) in developing reasoning models.
- Technical reports from frontier models often omitted detailed insights into how SFT and RL post-training techniques interact and influence each other's effectiveness.
- Optimizing training pipelines required a better understanding of SFT data scaling strategies, RL hyperparameter tuning, and stage-wise curriculum design.

## Method

- NVIDIA researchers developed a comprehensive, ==stage-wise training pipeline== combining Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and ==GRPO-based Reinforcement Learning (RL)==, building upon a Qwen2.5-Math-7B base model.
- They systematically investigated ==SFT data scaling== by constructing various datasets to analyze the impact of unique prompts versus responses per prompt on performance.
- Extensive RL ablation studies were conducted on factors such as initial SFT model strength, sampling temperature, strategies for handling overlong responses, and the necessity and duration of an initial ==warm-up RL stage==.

## Results

- AceReason-Nemotron-1.1-7B achieved new state-of-the-art pass@1 performance among Qwen2.5-7B-based models, including **64.8%** on AIME25 and **52.1%** on LiveCodeBench v6.
- The model demonstrated absolute performance gains of **10.6%** on AIME24, **16.4%** on AIME25, **8.4%** on LiveCodeBench v5, and **8.3%** on LiveCodeBench v6 over its strong SFT starting model.
- RL training enabled the model to solve a 'long tail' of harder problems that the initial SFT model struggled with, particularly evident on LiveCodeBench where it solved over **ten** additional problems.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Scaling the number of unique prompts in supervised fine-tuning (SFT) had a more significant impact on reasoning performance than increasing the number of generated responses per prompt, as indicated by higher regression coefficients.
> - Reinforcement learning (RL) consistently improved performance even when starting from strong SFT baselines, and while a stronger SFT initialization led to better final results, RL training effectively narrowed initial performance gaps between different SFT models.
> - An initial 'Math-only Stage-1' in the RL curriculum, despite a temporary performance dip, was crucial for learning concise reasoning and enabling higher gains in subsequent stages, facilitating a smoother transition from SFT to RL.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of math and code reasoning with large language models (LLMs) has been an active area of research for years, with initial efforts focusing on enhancing short chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning through pretraining and supervised fine-tuning (SFT). However, the introduction of models like OpenAI o1 and DeepSeek-R1 marked a paradigm shift, demonstrating the remarkable progress in reasoning capabilities achieved through large-scale reinforcement learning (RL), often leveraging rule-based verifiers for reward signals.

Following this success, much of the subsequent research has focused on distilling the capabilities of these large, frontier models into smaller or mid-sized models. This has primarily been achieved through generating synthetic data and employing SFT-only approaches. Concurrently, several efforts have emerged to replicate the success of large-scale RL directly on smaller base or SFT models (e.g., 7B and 14B parameters), frequently using models like DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen2.5 as initial checkpoints.

Despite these advancements, the authors highlight a crucial gap in the existing literature: a systematic study of the *synergy* between SFT and RL. Technical reports from frontier models often lack detailed insights into how these two post-training techniques interact and influence each other's effectiveness. This paper explicitly addresses this void.

Building upon their previous work with AceReason-Nemotron-1.0, which demonstrated an effective and efficient stage-wise RL approach for math and code, this study takes a step further. It aims to provide a holistic perspective on integrating SFT and RL, systematically probing their training dynamics and the interplay between them. By doing so, the paper contributes to a deeper understanding of how to optimally combine these powerful techniques to develop state-of-the-art reasoning models, moving beyond mere application to detailed analysis and optimization. It also situates itself within the broader context of various RL algorithms (like GRPO and DeepScaleR) and SFT distillation methods from larger models, while offering novel insights, such as a more systematic analysis of the "overlong penalty" in RL training.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to comprehensively investigate the synergy between supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and reinforcement learning (RL) in the development of robust reasoning models for complex math and code tasks. The authors are driven by a recognized gap in the current literature, where systematic studies on the interplay and integration of these two crucial post-training techniques are notably limited, especially in the technical reports of frontier models.

The specific objectives and questions that guide this study can be categorized as follows:

**Regarding SFT Training:**
- To understand how scaling SFT training data, through increasing the number of collected prompts and the number of generated responses per prompt, impacts reasoning performance.
- To determine which of these data scaling factors (prompt diversity vs. response richness) has a larger impact on model performance.
- To analyze how training for more epochs improves performance and to identify an appropriate stopping criterion, investigating if a degree of "overfitting" might be beneficial for long Chain-of-Thought (CoT) generation.

**Regarding RL Training and SFT-RL Synergy:**
- To ascertain whether a stronger SFT model consistently leads to better final performance after large-scale RL training, and if the performance gap between different SFT initializations narrows during RL.
- To explore how the sampling temperature during RL training affects the balance between exploration and exploitation, and to provide a practical rule of thumb for setting this temperature.
- To systematically investigate the optimal strategy for handling "overlong" responses (i.e., outputs exceeding a specified length budget) during RL training—whether to assign a negative reward or mask out the entire sample—at various training stages.
- To evaluate the necessity of the initial "Math-only Stage-1 (8K)" in the stage-wise RL curriculum, given its observed initial performance dip, and to determine its optimal training duration.
- To confirm if RL training still improves upon a strong SFT model's `pass@K` accuracy, even for large values of K.
- To determine if RL improves performance by enabling the model to solve harder problems that the initial SFT model could not address.

Ultimately, the motivation behind these objectives is to gain a holistic understanding of the post-training recipe for building state-of-the-art reasoning models. By addressing these nuanced questions, the authors aim to refine the training pipeline, leveraging a strong SFT foundation and the synergistic interplay with RL to achieve new state-of-the-art performance, specifically targeting Qwen2.5-7B-based reasoning models on challenging math and code benchmarks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research adopts a comprehensive, stage-wise approach to training large language models for math and code reasoning, meticulously combining Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and Reinforcement Learning (RL). The base model for all experiments is Qwen2.5-Math-7B, with its `rope_theta` parameter modified to support a 128K context length. The overall training pipeline progresses from SFT to multiple stages of RL training, alternating between math-only and code-only prompts, and progressively increasing the allowed response length.

**Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT):**
1. **Prompt Collection and Filtering:** High-quality math and code reasoning datasets were curated from various sources, including AceMath, NuminaMath, OpenMathReasoning for math, and TACO, APPs, OpenCoder-Stage2, and OpenCodeReasoning for code. Rigorous data preprocessing involved deduplication and decontamination (filtering samples with 9-gram overlap with test benchmarks).
2. **Response Generation:** DeepSeek-R1 was utilized to generate responses for the collected prompts. To ensure a diverse range of difficulty levels, simpler prompts (responses < 2,000 tokens) were filtered out, and proportions of other difficulty levels were adjusted. This resulted in a final SFT dataset of 247K math prompts and 136K code prompts.
3. **Scaling of SFT Data:** The authors systematically investigated two primary scaling strategies: increasing the number of unique prompts and increasing the number of generated responses per prompt. Seven SFT datasets (v1 through v7) were constructed, ranging from 36K to 2.2M samples, to evaluate these strategies. A multiple linear regression analysis (`z = a * log2(x) + b * log2(y) + c`) was performed to quantify the impact of unique prompts (`x`) versus responses per prompt (`y`) on overall accuracy. The training process was monitored across epochs to observe performance plateaus.

**Reinforcement Learning (RL):**
1. **Algorithm and Objective:** The GRPO algorithm was employed, with a strict adherence to on-policy training. This involved generating 8 or 16 rollouts for each question in a global batch of 128 prompts, followed by a single policy gradient update. This on-policy approach was chosen to stabilize RL and prevent entropy collapse. The loss function was a token-level policy gradient loss, assigning higher rewards to longer, correct samples and harsher penalties to incorrect ones, with the KL divergence term removed. The veRL framework was used for implementation.
2. **Data Curation for RL:** The authors emphasized the critical importance of high-quality RL data, stressing that prompts should be neither too easy nor too difficult to ensure a balanced mix of positive and negative rewards. Ground-truth answers were verified via rule-based systems: structured final answers for math and functional correctness against test cases for code. The quality and coverage of test cases were deemed crucial for code, as incorrect or overly simple tests could lead to misleading reward signals.
3. **Stage-wise Training Process:** A sequential, curriculum-based RL training approach was adopted:
 - **Math-only Stage-1 (8K):** A warm-up phase with an 8K response length budget, using relatively simple questions. This stage was hypothesized to facilitate the transition from imitation learning (SFT) to RL and encourage reasoning compression.
 - **Math-only Stage-2 (16K):** Increased proportion of challenging questions, leading to longer average response lengths and substantial performance improvements.
 - **Math-only Stage-3 (24K):** Focused on approximately 2500 hard math problems, resulting in significant math benchmark gains.
 - **Code-only Stage-I (24K):** Initiated *after* math RL training for greater stability, as code rewards can be noisier. Math RL was observed to generalize and prepare the model for code tasks.
 - **Code-only Stage-II (32K):** Employed an epoch-wise filtering strategy, removing easy problems fully solvable by the previous epoch's checkpoint.
 - **Math-only Stage-4 (32K):** A final stage with challenging math problems, similar to Stage-3.
4. **RL Ablation Studies:** Extensive analyses were conducted within the RL framework:
 - **Initial SFT Models:** RL was initiated from various SFT models (including their own SFT-7B v5 and v7, and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B) to observe the impact of SFT strength on final RL performance.
 - **Sampling Temperature:** The effect of different sampling temperatures on the policy LLM was studied, focusing on exploration-exploitation trade-offs and entropy trajectories.
 - **Overlong Filtering:** The effectiveness of masking truncated outputs versus assigning negative rewards was systematically evaluated across all RL stages and different token limits.
 - **Necessity and Duration of Stage-1:** Experiments were conducted to assess whether skipping Stage-1 was beneficial and, if not, how long Stage-1 training should be sustained.
 - **Cross-Domain Generalization:** The impact of math-only RL stages on code reasoning benchmarks was closely monitored.
 - **Pass@K and Problem Difficulty:** The performance improvements on `pass@K` for various K values and the ability to solve harder problems were analyzed.

For evaluation, standard benchmarks were used: AIME24/25, Math500, HMMT2025, BRUMO2025 for math; and EvalPlus, LiveCodeBench v5/v6 for code. All evaluations used a consistent inference setting (temperature 0.6, top-p 0.95, max sequence length 32,768) and reported `pass@1` averaged over multiple runs (n=64 for AIME/HMMT/BRUMO, n=8 for LiveCodeBench, n=4 for Math500/EvalPlus).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study yielded several key findings concerning the independent and synergistic effects of SFT and RL on reasoning model performance.

**SFT Scaling Insights:**
- Both strategies—increasing the number of unique prompts and increasing the number of generated responses per prompt—led to substantial improvements in reasoning performance. However, scaling the *number of unique prompts* was found to have a more significant impact, as indicated by a higher regression coefficient (4.831 for prompts vs. 2.635 for responses per prompt, with an R² of 0.989).
- Performance consistently improved from the first to the fifth training epoch, plateauing around the fifth or sixth epoch. This suggested that a certain degree of "overfitting" might actually enhance test accuracy for long Chain-of-Thought (CoT) generation, potentially due to exposure bias in autoregressive models.

**AceReason-Nemotron 1.1 Performance:**
- Leveraging these SFT insights and optimized RL, the AceReason-Nemotron-1.1 7B model achieved new state-of-the-art performance among Qwen2.5-7B-based reasoning models.
- It significantly outperformed its predecessor, AceReason-Nemotron-1.0, and other comparable models like Light-R1-7B and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B.
- Absolute performance gains over its strong SFT starting model were impressive: 10.6% on AIME24, 16.4% on AIME25, 8.4% on LiveCodeBench v5, and 8.3% on LiveCodeBench v6.
- The model achieved a record-high 64.8% on AIME25 and 52.1% on LiveCodeBench v6 (Pass@1, avg@64/8 respectively), demonstrating the effectiveness of the post-training recipe.

**SFT-RL Synergy and RL Dynamics:**
- **Stronger SFT Initialization:** Starting RL training from stronger SFT models consistently led to better final performance after large-scale RL. However, the performance gap between initially disparate SFT models narrowed significantly throughout the RL process (e.g., a 6.6% difference on AIME24 between SFT-7B v5 and v7 reduced to 1.6% after RL). This suggests RL can effectively bridge initial performance disparities.
- **Sampling Temperature in RL:** The sampling temperature of the policy LLM during RL training was found to be crucial for balancing exploration and exploitation. A rule of thumb emerged: maintaining the temperature-adjusted entropy around 0.3 typically leads to effective RL training. Low temperatures led to over-exploitation, while high temperatures resulted in excessive, unproductive exploration.
- **Overlong Filtering Strategy:** The optimal strategy for handling overlong responses varied by RL stage and token limit. Overlong filtering (masking the sample) provided clear benefits in early stages with shorter token limits (8K, 16K) by reducing noise from negative rewards on truncated, potentially correct, generations. However, this advantage diminished at 24K and could even degrade performance at 32K. This suggests that at larger token budgets, allowing truncated samples to receive negative rewards can encourage more concise and efficient reasoning. These findings contrast with some previous works, indicating that removing overlong filtering did not hinder generalization to longer inference lengths (e.g., 64K).
- **Importance of Stage-1 (8K):** Despite an initial performance dip, the Math-only Stage-1 (8K) proved essential. Skipping it resulted in slower gains and a lower performance plateau in subsequent stages. The authors hypothesize that this stage compels the model to learn a more concise reasoning process, beneficial when larger response length budgets become available later. While Stage-1 training causes an initial performance drop due to length constraints, it facilitates a smoother transition from SFT to RL and enables more substantial gains in later stages. Prolonging Stage-1 training beyond the initial recovery point did not yield further consistent benefits in subsequent stages.
- **Cross-Domain Generalization:** Reaffirming prior observations, math-only RL training significantly improved performance on code reasoning benchmarks. The majority of this cross-domain improvement stemmed from Stage-2 of math-only RL, and the performance gap in coding also narrowed considerably across different SFT initializations.
- **Pass@K Improvements:** RL training consistently improved `pass@K` accuracy across a wide range of K values (8 to 128) for both math and code benchmarks, even when initialized from a substantially stronger SFT baseline. While math performance gains diminished slightly at higher K (e.g., 1.2% gain at K=128 for AIME25), code gains remained substantial (e.g., 5% at K=128 for LiveCodeBench V6).
- **Solving Hard Problems:** RL training specifically enabled the model to solve a long tail of harder problems that the initial SFT model struggled with (achieving less than 20% accuracy). This was particularly evident on LiveCodeBench, where AceReason-Nemotron-1.1-7B solved over ten additional problems compared to the SFT model.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research marks a significant contribution to the field of large language model development, particularly in the domain of complex reasoning. Its primary significance lies in providing a systematic and in-depth analysis of the interplay between Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and Reinforcement Learning (RL), an area previously lacking comprehensive study in open literature and often omitted from technical reports of frontier models.

The potential impact of this work is multifaceted:

1. **Holistic Understanding of Post-Training:** The study offers a holistic perspective on how SFT and RL interact, demonstrating that they are not isolated stages but rather synergistic components of a robust training pipeline. This deeper understanding is critical for researchers and practitioners aiming to build highly capable LLMs, moving beyond ad-hoc application of techniques to a more principled, optimized approach.
2. **Refined Training Recipes and Practical Guidance:** The specific findings provide invaluable practical guidance for LLM development:
 - **SFT Data Optimization:** Clear evidence for the importance of both prompt diversity and richness (multiple responses per prompt) in SFT, along with insights into optimal training epochs, directly informs data curation and training strategies.
 - **RL Hyperparameter Tuning:** The "rule of thumb" for setting RL sampling temperature based on temperature-adjusted entropy offers a concrete, actionable heuristic for balancing exploration and exploitation, which is often a challenging aspect of RL training.
 - **Strategic RL Curriculum Design:** The demonstration of the crucial role of an initial "warm-up" RL stage (Math-only Stage-1) in fostering "reasoning compression," despite initial performance dips, reshapes our understanding of efficient curriculum design for complex tasks.
 - **Nuanced Handling of Truncation:** The detailed analysis of "overlong filtering" across different RL stages and token limits provides a sophisticated understanding of when to penalize truncated outputs versus masking them, optimizing token efficiency and model performance.
3. **Advancing State-of-the-Art for Smaller Models:** By achieving new state-of-the-art performance for Qwen2.5-7B-based models on challenging math and code benchmarks, AceReason-Nemotron-1.1 demonstrates that optimized post-training can significantly elevate the capabilities of smaller, more deployable LLMs. This is crucial for democratizing access to powerful reasoning AI, reducing the computational burden associated with deploying very large models.
4. **Cross-Domain Generalization:** The reaffirmation that math-only RL training significantly boosts code reasoning capabilities highlights a powerful generalization phenomenon. This insight could lead to more efficient training paradigms, where expertise gained in one domain (e.g., math) can be leveraged to improve performance in another (e.g., code) without extensive specialized training for the second domain.
5. **Enhanced Problem-Solving Capabilities:** The finding that RL enables the model to solve a "long tail" of harder problems suggests that RL is not merely fine-tuning but genuinely expanding the model's problem-solving frontier, particularly in challenging scenarios where SFT models might plateau.
6. **Open Science Contribution:** The release of the model and data on Hugging Face (https://huggingface.co/nvidia/AceReason-Nemotron-1.1-7B) fosters reproducibility, encourages further research, and allows the broader AI community to build upon these insights and compare their work against a high-performing baseline.

In essence, this research goes beyond presenting a new model; it provides fundamental insights into the mechanics of high-performance LLM training. By demystifying the SFT-RL synergy, it paves the way for more efficient, effective, and perhaps even smaller, yet more capable, reasoning models in the future.
