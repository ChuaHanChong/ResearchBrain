# Safe Embodied AI for Long-horizon Tasks: A Cross-layer Analysis of Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Survey classifying safety in long-horizon robotic manipulation along two axes: intervention locus (planning/policy/execution) and evidence boundary (formal/statistical/empirical). Synthesizes fragmented literature across grounding, decomposition, verification, constrained policies, and runtime monitoring, finding safety evidence is layer-local and non-transferable, empirical-heavy, and manipulation-specific benchmarks remain scarce.

## Problem

- Existing safety research is fragmented across safe control, safe RL, and alignment/guardrail literatures, each studied in isolation despite embodied failures emerging from unaddressed interactions between these layers.
- Prior surveys treat safety as an abstract concept or a modular technique, without organizing long-horizon safety by where interventions enter the lifecycle or assessing evidence strength.
- Safety-relevant failures in long-horizon manipulation compound silently across grounding, planning, policy, and contact-rich execution, yet no prior framework distinguishes formal, statistical, and empirical evidence strength.

## Method

- **Cross-layer intervention-locus taxonomy** organizing mechanisms into ==planning-time== (grounding, decomposition, verification), ==policy-time== (constraint injection, alignment), and ==execution-time== (monitoring, shielding, recovery) stages.
- **Evidence-boundary framework** classifying each cited work's strongest supported claim as ==formal guarantee==, ==statistical bound==, or ==empirical observation==, separating these from generic robustness or capability results.
- **Lifecycle intervention map** (Table 4) linking each mechanism family to representative failure modes, typical evidence level, and representative citations across planning, policy, and execution stages.

## Results

- Cross-corpus analysis (literature through April 2026) assigns evidence strength across **three** tiers, finding execution-time contact regulation reaches formal/empirical support while most policy-time constraint work stays purely empirical.
- **Six** representative safety-concern categories (task specification, sequencing, spatial feasibility, policy objective, runtime drift, contact risk) recur across the reviewed corpus, none addressed by a single unified mechanism.
- The analysis identifies **five** cross-layer future-direction pathways and finds **zero** existing benchmarks provide a common trace-level record of hazard onset, mitigation, and recovery across planning, policy, and execution.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Safety evidence is not interchangeable across abstraction layers: a formally verified plan, a calibrated policy cost, and a runtime force limit answer fundamentally different questions.
> - Task success and capability improvement should never be conflated with safety evidence unless a metric is explicitly tied to hazard reduction or recoverability.
> - Scaling capability data does not automatically produce safe behavior; systems also need targeted exposure to failures, near-misses, and recoverable-state examples during training.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

The increasing capabilities of embodied AI systems, particularly their integration with foundation models, vision-language-action (VLA) systems, and broad agent architectures, have expanded their deployment into open, physically consequential environments. This advancement shifts the central challenge from merely enabling task completion to ensuring safe execution in real-world settings. Existing literature on safety in embodied AI is fragmented across various sub-disciplines, including safe control, safe reinforcement learning (RL), multimodal alignment, and industrial safety protocols. These efforts often study aspects of safety in isolation, overlooking the complex interactions between different system layers that can lead to emergent failures.

This survey addresses this fragmentation by focusing on long-horizon robotic manipulation as a concentrated domain for studying safety. Robotic manipulation is particularly relevant because it combines several safety pressures: semantic misgrounding (errors in interpreting commands), subtask-level error propagation (mistakes compounding across sequential actions), execution drift (deviations from intended trajectories), and contact-rich physical risks (hazards during physical interaction). These pressures can accumulate within a single closed-loop system, making long-horizon manipulation a rigorous testbed for cross-layer safety claims.

The work differentiates itself from other surveys by providing a structured review that organizes the literature based on *where* safety interventions occur within the system's lifecycle (planning-time, policy-time, execution-time) and by analyzing the *strength of evidence* supporting safety claims (formal guarantees, statistical support, empirical safety heuristics). Unlike surveys that treat safety as an abstract concept or a modular technique, this paper maps the scattered landscape to clarify the integrated components needed for end-to-end safety analysis in embodied AI, specifically within manipulation. It aims to bridge the gap between general embodied AI surveys that acknowledge safety needs and domain-specific surveys that focus on particular technical facets without a holistic safety framework.

### 2. Key objectives and motivation

The core motivation for this research stems from the increasing deployment of embodied AI systems in physical environments. While these systems are becoming more capable of performing complex, long-horizon tasks, their interactions in the real world carry inherent risks, including harm to humans, damage to objects, and disruption of workplaces. Failures in embodied systems, especially in long-horizon tasks, are often not singular events but emerge in delayed, compounding, and partially observable ways. An initial semantic misinterpretation or planning error can propagate through the system, manifesting as a physical safety failure during a later contact-rich interaction. This necessitates treating safety as a fundamental system property rather than an afterthought.

The paper defines safety broadly to encompass:
- **Physical safety:** Preventing harm to humans, the robot, manipulated objects, or the environment.
- **Procedural safety:** Maintaining task order, preconditions, constraints, and recoverable states across a task rollout.
- **Operational safety:** Avoiding states where autonomous execution becomes unjustifiable or unsafe.
- **Semantic safety:** Addressing hazardous instructions, misgrounded goals, hallucinated affordances, or omitted constraints.

Given this context, the key objectives and contributions of this survey are:
1. **Position long-horizon robotic manipulation as an anchor domain:** To demonstrate how this domain integrates diverse safety challenges, making it ideal for in-depth safety analysis.
2. **Introduce a cross-layer organizational framework:** To categorize safety mechanisms by their intervention locus—planning-time (task formation and validation), policy-time (action generation shaping), and execution-time (runtime monitoring and physical interaction)—emphasizing their interconnectedness.
3. **Propose an evidence boundary framework:** To critically assess the rigor of safety claims, distinguishing formal guarantees, statistical safety evidence, and empirical safety evidence, and separating these from generic capability improvements. This aims to prevent overclaiming of safety properties.
4. **Analyze current evaluation and benchmark practices:** To identify limitations in existing capability-oriented benchmarks for assessing procedural safety and to highlight fragmentation in safety-aware evaluation across different layers.
5. **Synthesize cross-layer research directions:** To outline future avenues for achieving more robust, end-to-end safety assurance, including abstraction-preserving safety representations, sim-to-real safety evidence, embodiment-aware revalidation, calibrated intervention selection, procedural safety observability, and safety-oriented data and evaluation infrastructure.
6. **Diagnose critical pitfalls:** To establish fine-grained safety boundaries for long-horizon tasks and identify essential safety constraints and challenges for future manipulation frameworks.

The central motivation is to provide a structured method for evaluating and comparing safety claims in embodied AI, thereby moving beyond a sole focus on system capability to a comprehensive understanding of real-world safety in long-horizon robotic manipulation.

### 3. Methodology and approach

This paper employs a **framework-guided review strategy** to systematically analyze the literature on safe embodied AI for long-horizon robotic manipulation. The methodology is structured around answering three primary analytical questions for each piece of literature:
1. **Where safety enters the manipulation lifecycle:** This led to the classification into *intervention loci*.
2. **Which failure mode or risk pressure is addressed:** This guided the categorization of specific safety concerns.
3. **What form of evidence supports the corresponding safety claim:** This established the *evidence boundary* framework.

**Corpus Construction and Selection Criteria:**
The literature corpus was built in stages:
- Initial focus on work directly related to long-horizon robotic manipulation, language-conditioned planning, VLA policies, runtime monitoring, intervention, recovery, contact-rich execution, and safety-oriented evaluation.
- Expansion through targeted searches across major academic databases (IEEE Xplore, ACM Digital Library, arXiv, Google Scholar, Semantic Scholar, publisher pages) using a combination of keywords such as "long-horizon robotic manipulation," "robot safety," "vision-language-action safety," "runtime monitoring," "failure recovery," "safe control," "safe reinforcement learning," "contact-rich manipulation," and "robot manipulation benchmark."
- Reference tracing from representative and neighboring surveys in safe control, safe RL, embodied-agent evaluation, robot foundation models, and broader embodied AI safety.
- The corpus was updated through April 2026, acknowledging the fast-moving nature of the field.

**Inclusion Criteria:** A paper was included if it met at least one of four criteria:
1. Addresses a safety-relevant failure source in long-horizon manipulation (e.g., specification error, unsafe action generation, execution drift, contact-rich physical risk).
2. Proposes a mechanism for constraining, verifying, monitoring, correcting, recovering, or evaluating robot behavior under safety-relevant conditions.
3. Provides formal, statistical, or empirical evidence directly supporting a safety claim.
4. Supplies contextual foundation necessary for interpreting manipulation-specific safety evidence within adjacent domains.

**Exclusion Criteria:** Papers primarily focused on general capability improvement, foundation-model scaling, broad embodied AI benchmarking (without direct safety evidence), or general robot cybersecurity were excluded, as these were considered outside the immediate scope of functional safety in manipulation.

**Two Primary Organizing Axes:**
The entire survey's structure and analysis are built upon two main axes:
1. **Intervention Locus:**
 - **Planning-time safety (Section 3):** Addresses safety concerns during task representation formation and validation, including goal grounding, constraint interpretation, plan structuring, and model-based planning support, before any physical action.
 - **Policy-time safety (Section 4):** Focuses on shaping action proposals and policy generation (e.g., during RL/IL training), encompassing policy class definition, constraint-aware generation, objective shaping, and long-horizon extensions.
 - **Execution-time safety (Section 5):** Deals with real-time interaction in the physical world, covering runtime monitoring, anomaly detection, intervention, task restoration, and physical contact safety.

2. **Evidence Boundary:**
 - **Formal guarantees:** Deterministic safety conclusions derived from explicit assumptions, such as theorems or logical verification.
 - **Statistical safety evidence:** Probabilistic safety statements, such as calibrated risk estimates or confidence bounds.
 - **Empirical safety evidence:** Observed safety metrics in simulations, datasets, benchmarks, or case studies, without formal or probabilistic guarantees.
 This framework distinguishes direct safety evidence from generic robustness or capability improvements.

**Representative Safety Concerns:** The review identifies recurring safety concerns across layers, such as task specification risk, sequence risk, spatial feasibility risk, policy generation risk, runtime drift risk, and physical interaction risk. These concerns are distinguished from the mechanisms designed to address them.

By adhering to this framework, the survey aims to provide a structured, critical, and comprehensive understanding of safety in long-horizon robotic manipulation.

### 4. Main findings and results

The survey's analysis of the literature on safe long-horizon robotic manipulation yields several main findings and highlights critical gaps in the current research landscape:

1. **Fragmentation and Lack of Holistic Framework:** The existing safety literature is fragmented, with efforts largely localized to specific components (e.g., planning, control, reinforcement learning, alignment). Most work lacks a holistic framework that addresses how safety concerns propagate and interact across different layers of an embodied AI system. This siloed approach fails to capture the emergent nature of safety in long-horizon tasks.

2. **Long-horizon Manipulation as a Concentrated Safety Challenge:** The survey establishes that long-horizon robotic manipulation serves as a unique anchor domain because it concentrates multiple, interacting safety pressures simultaneously. These include semantic misgrounding in task specification, delayed error propagation across subtasks, execution drift, and contact-rich physical interaction. This domain exposes how small, early errors can compound into significant physical safety failures.

3. **Heterogeneity of Safety Evidence and Overclaiming:** A significant finding is the wide variability in the rigor of safety claims. The proposed "evidence boundary" framework reveals that many claims are empirical, based on observed behavior in specific test scenarios, while formal or statistical guarantees are less common, especially for complex, contact-rich, long-horizon tasks. There is a tendency to equate general capability improvements or robustness to noise with direct safety assurance, which the survey argues can lead to overclaiming.

4. **Specific Gaps Across Intervention Loci:**
 - **Planning-time Safety:** While methods exist for grounding goals, interpreting constraints, structuring plans, and formal verification, these often assume perfect abstraction, predicate completeness, or static causal invariance. A significant gap remains in ensuring that semantically or logically sound plans are also physically feasible and robust to continuous, topological challenges in the real world. The computational cost of exhaustive formal checking often necessitates heuristic or learned verifiers, which come with weaker assurances.
 - **Policy-time Safety:** Approaches for constraint-aware policy generation (e.g., safe RL, certificate-guided learning) and objective shaping (e.g., preference alignment, language-guided rewards) can reduce unsafe actions and align behavior. However, formal guarantees for these methods are often expectation-based or limited by strong structural assumptions, making their application to high-dimensional, language-guided manipulation challenging. The evidence typically remains empirical and distribution-dependent, lacking mathematical bounds on procedural safety.
 - **Execution-time Safety:** This layer is crucial for addressing real-world uncertainties. Advances in runtime monitoring, anomaly detection, failure diagnosis, and shielding provide mechanisms for detecting and mitigating risks during live interaction. However, many methods operate within a predominantly empirical regime, making absolute guarantees difficult. Autonomous recovery mechanisms are improving but often rely on aggregate metrics that do not fully characterize the safety or efficiency of the restoration process, potentially masking unsafe intermediate behaviors.
 - **Contact-rich Physical Interaction Safety:** Despite progress in adaptive compliance, formal constraints, and hierarchical refinement for contact tasks, a critical gap persists in providing direct, formal safety guarantees for high-stakes, unmodeled, or complex contact interactions. Most advancements here result in improved empirical success rates rather than certifiable physical safety.

5. **Inadequacy of Current Evaluation and Benchmarks:** Existing benchmarks are primarily capability-oriented, focusing on task success rates. They often fail to capture critical aspects of safety, such as the temporal onset of hazards, the efficacy of interventions, the quality of recovery, or the specific costs incurred during unsafe events. Layer-local metrics (e.g., plan validity, policy cost, runtime alarms, contact forces) are not interchangeable and do not form a coherent, end-to-end safety argument.

6. **Identified Research Directions (as opportunities from findings):** The analysis synthesizes a roadmap for future research, emphasizing:
 - **Abstraction-preserving safety representations:** Ensuring safety-relevant information is retained across different levels of system abstraction.
 - **Sim-to-real safety evidence:** Systematically addressing how safety claims translate from simulation to real-world hardware.
 - **Embodiment-aware revalidation:** Developing methods to revalidate safety properties when policies or representations are transferred to new robot platforms or modified models.
 - **Calibrated intervention selection:** Establishing principled mappings from heterogeneous risk signals to appropriate intervention types and scopes.
 - **Procedural safety observability:** Designing evaluation protocols that record the full safety history of a rollout, distinguishing safe success from unsafe success or safe failure.
 - **Integration with adjacent safety fields:** Leveraging insights from semantic/multimodal safety, deployment assurance, and incident learning for AI agents.

In summary, the paper's findings underscore that while significant progress has been made in individual safety mechanisms, the challenge of achieving end-to-end, certifiable, and procedurally safe long-horizon robotic manipulation remains largely unsolved due to fragmentation, diverse evidence rigor, and insufficient evaluation methodologies.

### 5. Significance and potential impact

This survey significantly impacts the field of embodied AI and robotic manipulation by providing a structured, critical, and comprehensive analysis of safety in long-horizon tasks. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Framework for Safety Analysis:** The introduction of the "intervention locus" and "evidence boundary" frameworks provides a novel and systematic way to categorize, analyze, and compare diverse safety mechanisms. This framework offers a common language for researchers, allowing for clearer communication of safety claims and a more precise understanding of where specific methods contribute to overall system safety.

2. **Highlighting Critical Gaps and Future Research Directions:** By systematically diagnosing limitations across planning, policy, and execution layers, the survey identifies crucial underserved areas in embodied AI safety. It moves beyond generic calls for "more safety" to pinpoint specific challenges such as the need for stronger formal guarantees in contact-rich manipulation, improved policy-time safety evidence, mature uncertainty-triggered interventions, and manipulation-specific safety benchmarks. The outlined future directions provide a roadmap for developing more robust and verifiable safety solutions.

3. **Promoting Rigorous Evaluation:** The paper's critical assessment of current benchmarks underscores the inadequacy of relying solely on task success metrics for safety evaluation. It advocates for a shift towards "procedural safety observability," emphasizing the need for benchmarks that capture the temporal evolution of risks, the efficacy of interventions, recovery quality, and other safety-relevant events throughout a task rollout. This can lead to the development of more comprehensive and diagnostic evaluation protocols, fostering more trustworthy safety claims.

4. **Enabling Safer Deployment:** By clarifying the distinctions between various forms of safety evidence and identifying the interfaces where safety information can be lost or weakened, the survey contributes to the development of more reliable and deployable embodied AI systems. Addressing the challenges related to abstraction layering, sim-to-real transfer, embodiment revalidation, and calibrated risk interpretation is essential for moving robots from controlled lab environments to open, real-world settings with greater confidence.

5. **Cross-Disciplinary Impact:** The work draws connections between manipulation safety and broader AI safety research, including semantic alignment, AI governance, and assurance engineering. This interdisciplinary perspective enriches the understanding of embodied AI safety and encourages the adoption of best practices from other safety-critical domains, such as structured documentation (e.g., policy cards) and incident learning.

6. **Foundational Contribution to Trustworthy AI:** Ultimately, this survey serves as a foundational reference for advancing trustworthy AI in robotics. By advocating for stronger evidence discipline, clearer cross-layer safety architectures, and principled deployment arguments, it contributes to building greater public and expert confidence in autonomous systems. This is vital for the widespread acceptance and integration of robots into human environments, minimizing risks and maximizing the societal benefits of embodied AI.

In conclusion, this paper's comprehensive analysis and structured framework provide essential guidance for both understanding the current state and directing future research efforts toward truly safe and robust long-horizon robotic manipulation.
