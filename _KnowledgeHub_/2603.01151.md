# D-REX: Differentiable Real-to-Sim-to-Real Engine for Learning Dexterous Grasping

> [!summary] Summary
> D-REX, an engine developed by USC and UC Berkeley researchers, identifies object mass from real-world robot interactions using differentiable physics and Gaussian Splats. This real-to-sim-to-real framework enables training force-aware dexterous grasping policies from human demonstrations, achieving an average 86% success rate on tabletop objects.

## Problem

- Bridging the sim-to-real gap, specifically in accurately modeling physical dynamics (e.g., object mass) within simulations from real-world visual observations and robot interactions.
- Developing robust dexterous manipulation policies from human demonstrations, challenged by the embodiment gap between human and robot hands and the need to account for unobserved forces.
- Lack of scalable methods for creating high-fidelity, physically plausible digital twins of real-world objects for robot learning tasks.

## Method

![[2603.01151-method.png]]
*D-REX method overview with four components: Real-to-Sim Gaussian Splat reconstruction, mass identification via a differentiable physics engine running robot actions in sim and real, learning from human demonstrations, and policy learning.*

- A differentiable real-to-sim-to-real engine that reconstructs high-fidelity visual and geometric models using Gaussian Splats and identifies object mass through differentiable physics by minimizing trajectory discrepancies between real and simulated planar pushing tasks.
- A pipeline to transfer human demonstration videos into simulation-executable robot demonstrations by reconstructing human hand-object poses and retargeting them to a robotic hand.
- Training force-aware dexterous grasping policies conditioned on the identified object mass, employing a hybrid position and force control framework to ensure stable grasps under diverse object dynamics.

## Results

- D-REX achieved accurate object mass identification with percentile errors ranging from 4.8% to 12.0% across diverse objects and under 13 grams deviation for objects with varying internal densities.
- Force-aware grasping policies trained with identified mass achieved an average 86% success rate in tabletop grasping across eight diverse objects, outperforming DexGraspNet 2.0 (76%) and Human2Sim2Robot (55%) baselines.
- Policies conditioned on automatically identified mass performed comparably to, or better than, policies using ground-truth mass values, and generalized more robustly to out-of-distribution mass values than domain randomization baselines.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Accurate physical parameter identification, particularly object mass via differentiable physics, is crucial for closing the sim-to-real gap and enabling robust manipulation transfer.
> - Force-aware grasping policies, explicitly conditioned on object mass, exhibit greater stability and higher success rates compared to policies lacking dynamic awareness, especially for objects with varying weights.
> - The integration of photorealistic 3D reconstruction (Gaussian Splats) with differentiable physics engines provides an effective method for automatically creating physically plausible digital twins directly from real-world interaction data.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The research presented in D-REX addresses several interconnected, fundamental challenges at the forefront of modern robotics, particularly in the realm of dexterous manipulation and sim-to-real transfer.

**Bridging the Sim-to-Real Gap:** A persistent bottleneck in deploying robot learning algorithms in the real world is the "sim-to-real gap." Policies trained purely in simulation often fail when transferred to physical robots due to discrepancies in visual appearance, physical dynamics, and sensor noise. Traditional approaches to mitigate this include domain randomization (varying simulation parameters during training to enhance robustness), system identification (calibrating simulation dynamics with real-world observations), and advancements in simulation fidelity. D-REX positions itself within the system identification and real-to-sim paradigms by aiming to create highly accurate "digital twins" of real-world objects and scenes, ensuring that simulated dynamics faithfully reflect reality.

**Differentiable Physics and Real-to-Sim:** Recent advancements in differentiable physical simulation (e.g., Brax, GradSim) have revolutionized the ability to optimize physical parameters and control policies using gradient-based methods. This is a significant departure from traditional, non-differentiable simulators that required computationally expensive surrogate gradient methods like finite differences. D-REX leverages this by building a differentiable engine that enables end-to-end optimization of object physical parameters (specifically mass) directly from visual observations and robot interaction data. This integration of differentiable physics with photorealistic reconstruction (using Gaussian Splats) for real-to-sim mapping is a key innovation, building upon works that focus on reconstructing digital twins from images but often lack accurate physical realism.

**Learning from Human Demonstrations:** Human demonstration videos offer a scalable and intuitive source of data for robotic manipulation. However, transferring human skills to robots is challenging due to embodiment differences. Prior work has explored visual representations, intermediate cues, and 3D human motion estimation for skill transfer. D-REX contributes to this area by proposing a novel method to transfer human demonstrations into simulation-executable robot demonstrations, which are then used to train force-aware policies. This approach aims to make policy learning more scalable and adaptable, conditioned on the accurately identified object mass.

In essence, D-REX integrates state-of-the-art techniques from computer graphics (Gaussian Splats for reconstruction), physics simulation (differentiable engines like Brax/MJX, GradSim), and robot learning (imitation learning, force-aware control) to create a comprehensive framework for learning dexterous manipulation. It stands out by combining accurate visual and geometric reconstruction with physics-driven parameter identification, directly addressing the physical realism aspect often missing in purely visual real-to-sim approaches.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core motivation behind D-REX stems from the recognized difficulty of bridging the sim-to-real gap, particularly concerning the accurate modeling of physical dynamics and the development of robust manipulation policies. The paper identifies two primary objectives:

1. **Accurate Physical Parameter Identification through a Differentiable Real-to-Sim Engine:** The primary challenge in physical simulations is precisely replicating real-world dynamics, which heavily relies on accurate knowledge of physical parameters like object mass. Existing real-to-sim frameworks often struggle to infer these parameters solely from visual observations due to a lack of observable physical cues. D-REX aims to solve this by introducing a differentiable real-to-sim-to-real engine that can identify object mass from real-world visual observations (videos) and robot control signals during robot-object interactions. This process seeks to automatically construct high-fidelity and physically plausible digital twins, making the simulated environment a true reflection of reality.

2. **Learning Force-Aware Dexterous Grasping Policies from Limited Human Demonstrations:** With an accurately modeled physical environment, the next objective is to train robust manipulation policies. Traditional methods for learning from human demonstrations often fall short in dexterous manipulation due to the embodiment gap between human and robot hands and the inability to account for unobserved forces (like those countering gravity). D-REX proposes a novel approach to train force-aware grasping policies by transferring feasible human demonstrations into simulated robot demonstrations. Critically, these policies are conditioned on the identified object mass and integrate both position and force control, ensuring stability under diverse object dynamics and effectively reducing the sim-to-real gap for grasping tasks.

In summary, D-REX is motivated by the need for robotic systems that can learn complex manipulation skills directly from real-world data without extensive manual calibration or ground-truth knowledge of physical properties. It seeks to achieve this by creating a highly accurate "digital twin" that captures both visual appearance and physical dynamics, and then leveraging this twin for efficient and robust policy learning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

D-REX is structured as a four-component real-to-sim-to-real framework (Figure 2): Real-to-Sim Initialization, Mass Identification, Learning from Human Demonstrations, and Policy Learning.

1. **Visual and Geometric Reconstruction (Real-to-Sim Initialization):**
- The process begins by capturing RGB videos (scene-centric $I_s$ and object-centric $I_o$) to reconstruct high-fidelity visual and geometric models of the environment and manipulated objects.
- It adopts **Gaussian Splat representations** (Kerbl et al., 2023) for photorealistic rendering and high-quality mesh generation. Specifically, 2D Gaussian Splats with surface normal estimation (Ye et al., 2024a) are used for accurate collision geometry (mesh $K$), while 3D Gaussian Splats provide high-fidelity visual appearance (particles $P$).
- This reconstruction process converts real-world video scans into simulation-ready assets, with the reconstructed object meshes and coordinate frames aligned with the simulation environment (MuJoCo).

2. **Physical Parameter Identification from Robot-Object Interactions (Mass Identification):**
- An initial MJCF representation $S$ is generated using a Vision-Language Model (VLM) from images and prompts. However, visually inferred parameters often lack real-world accuracy.
- D-REX focuses on identifying **object mass ($m$)** as a critical dynamic parameter. This is done through a planar pushing task, where identical robot actions are executed in both the real world and simulation.
- The discrepancy between real ($s_{real_t}$) and simulated ($s_{sim_t}(m)$) object trajectories is minimized (Equation 1), where $s$ is the object's 6-DoF pose. Real trajectories are obtained via FoundationPose (Wen et al., 2024).
- **Dynamics Modeling:** Object motion is modeled using rigid-body Newton-Euler equations (Equation 2-3) with a compliant penalty-based contact model. A discrete-time update (Equation 4) is implemented using a semi-implicit Euler integration scheme (Equation 5).
- **Differentiable Physics:** To optimize mass, gradients of the simulated trajectory are computed with respect to $m$ using a discrete adjoint method (Jatavallabhula et al., 2021a). This utilizes the differentiable physics engine Brax/MJX (Freeman et al., 2021) and GradSim (Jatavallabhula et al., 2021a), forming a differentiable computation graph (Equation 6-7). The key is that it uses consistent robotic control signals to model external forces, avoiding manual specification.

3. **Transferring Human Demonstrations to Robot Demonstrations:**
- Human demonstration videos ($I_t$) are captured and processed using methods like HaMeR (Pavlakos et al., 2024) and MCC-HO (Wu et al., 2024a) to reconstruct detailed articulated models of human hands and objects (Equation 8).
- These human hand-object poses are then mapped to the robotic hand (e.g., Leap Hand) using Dex-Retargeting (Qin et al., 2023), producing robot-executable actions ($A_t$) as target joint angles (Equation 9). This provides a scalable data source for policy learning.

4. **Policy Learning with Transferred Robot Demonstrations:**
- A manipulation policy $\pi_\phi$ is trained using the transferred robot demonstrations ($A_t$). The policy takes object-centric observations (collision mesh vertices $K$ encoded with positional encoding) and the *identified mass ($m$)* as input.
- **Grasping Position Learning:** $\pi_\phi$ is a multi-head neural network that predicts dexterous hand joint positions ($\hat{A}$), contact-related rewards ($\hat{r}$), and a mass-related control force ($\hat{f}$) (Equation 10).
- **Force-Aware Optimization Design:** The policy incorporates force-based constraints (Equation 11) to encourage sustained contact and object retention. The key innovation is a hybrid control framework that combines position and force control, with the force prediction module conditioned on the optimized mass $m$. This allows adaptive force control based on object mass, ensuring robustness across variations.

The framework emphasizes end-to-end differentiability from visual observations to physical parameters and policy learning, using a two-stage training procedure (supervised pre-training on human data, followed by simulation-based refinement) for the grasping policy.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments comprehensively evaluate D-REX's performance in mass identification and force-aware grasping.

1. **Mass Identification via Object Pushing:**
- D-REX achieved accurate and robust mass identification across diverse object geometries (Letter U, Letter A, Lego, Domino, Cookie, Ketchup) with percentile errors ranging from 4.8% to 12.0% (Table 1). This demonstrates generalization to various shapes without object-specific tuning.
- It also accurately identified mass for objects with identical geometry but varying internal densities (three replicas with different infill ratios), with deviations under 13 grams (Table 2). This confirms sensitivity to subtle physical differences.
- Qualitative results (Figure 4) showed that simulations using the optimized mass closely matched real-world object dynamics, visually and physically, significantly outperforming simulations with incorrect (lighter) mass estimates.
- An ablation study (Table 5, Appendix A.2.4) compared semi-implicit and explicit Euler schemes for mass identification, showing semi-implicit Euler provided more accurate mass estimates at a modest runtime cost. Mass-loss curves (Figure 11) demonstrated robust convergence even from highly underestimated initial mass values.

2. **Effectiveness of Force-Based Control Through Grasping:**
- Grasping policies trained with object mass-aware control showed a strong dependency on accurate mass conditioning. Policies performed well only when training and evaluation masses matched (e.g., 80% success for medium mass on medium object, dropping to 30-40% on mismatched masses; Table 3, Figure 6). Mismatched masses led to under- or over-applied force, causing slippage or bounce-off.
- Policies conditioned on automatically identified mass performed comparably to, or even better than, those using ground-truth mass values, and substantially outperformed policies using arbitrary mass values (Figure 5). This validates the practical utility of the mass identification module.
- A comparison with Domain Randomization (DR) (Table 9, Appendix A.3.5) showed D-REX's mass-aware policy generalized more robustly to out-of-distribution (OOD) mass values (e.g., lighter objects) than a DR-trained baseline, highlighting the advantage of explicit parameter identification.

3. **Tabletop Object Grasping Experiments:**
- D-REX consistently outperformed two strong baselines, DexGraspNet 2.0 (Zhang et al., 2024c) and Human2Sim2Robot (Lum et al., 2025), across eight diverse objects (lightbulb, cube, A, cookie, spam, nutella, spray, ketchup) with varying geometries and masses (Figure 7).
- D-REX achieved higher success rates (average 86%) with lower variance compared to baselines (76% and 55% respectively), especially on heavier objects where baselines struggled due to lack of force-aware control.
- Qualitative results (Figure 8) confirmed stable and secure grasps across a range of objects, demonstrating the policy's robustness.
- Ablation on scaling performance (Figure 9, Appendix A.3.1) indicated that increasing the number of human demonstrations improved grasping success rates, showing the policy's ability to leverage more data effectively.

4. **Runtime Analysis:**
- Offline reconstruction using Gaussian Splatting (COLMAP+3DGS+2DGS) typically took 30–35 minutes per object for approximately 300–340 RGB images (Table 4).
- Mass identification via differentiable physics converged within 200 epochs, taking 5–20 minutes per object.
- These offline steps do not affect the real-time execution of the learned grasping policy, which has an efficient inference time of approximately 0.5 seconds per object pose.
- Real-to-Sim reconstruction quality (Table 7, Appendix A.3.3) showed D-REX outperformed vanilla 4D Gaussian Splatting in photometric metrics while reducing optimization time.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

D-REX represents a significant advancement in robot manipulation by presenting a tightly integrated real-to-sim-to-real framework that addresses critical bottlenecks in current systems. Its key significance and potential impacts are:

1. **Reduced Sim-to-Real Gap through Accurate Physical Digital Twins:** By creating physically plausible digital twins that accurately capture object mass from real-world robot-object interactions, D-REX substantially reduces the sim-to-real gap. This allows policies trained in simulation to transfer more robustly to physical robots, a long-standing challenge in the field. This capability can accelerate the development and deployment of robotic systems in complex, unstructured environments.

2. **Robust Dexterous Grasping with Force-Aware Control:** The ability to learn force-aware grasping policies conditioned on identified object mass is a crucial innovation. Unlike traditional position-based policies that often fail with varying object weights, D-REX's hybrid position-force control ensures stable grasps across diverse objects, especially heavy or irregularly shaped ones. This enhances the reliability and adaptability of robotic manipulators, moving towards more human-like dexterous skills.

3. **Scalable and Data-Efficient Policy Learning from Human Demonstrations:** D-REX offers a practical pathway for leveraging abundant human demonstration data. By translating human actions into simulation-executable robot demonstrations and combining this with accurate physics, it enables scalable policy learning without requiring extensive robot-collected datasets or laborious hand-engineered rewards. This democratization of data sources can make advanced robot learning more accessible.

4. **Foundation for Generalizable Robotic Policies:** While current policies may still be object-specific, the modular nature of D-REX, particularly the mass identification module, provides a "plug-in" signal for more general architectures. The ability to estimate an object's mass and then condition a task policy on this inferred value is a powerful mechanism for robust transfer across various objects and tasks, even those outside the initial training distribution.

5. **Advancement in Differentiable Robotics:** D-REX pushes the boundaries of differentiable simulation by showing its effectiveness in solving inverse problems (system identification) directly from visual and interaction data, beyond just forward dynamics. This work further validates the utility of differentiable physics for end-to-end learning in robotics, opening new avenues for optimizing complex physical interactions.

In conclusion, D-REX offers a robust and practical solution for learning dexterous manipulation skills, combining photorealistic reconstruction with differentiable physics-based parameter identification and force-aware imitation learning. Its contributions pave the way for more intelligent, adaptive, and deployable robotic systems in real-world scenarios.
