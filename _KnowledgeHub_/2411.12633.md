# Instant Policy: In-Context Imitation Learning via Graph Diffusion

> [!summary] Summary
> Instant Policy is an in-context imitation learning method that performs new manipulation tasks from one or two test-time demonstrations, with no retraining. It puts demonstrations, the current point-cloud observation and future actions into one heterogeneous graph, generates actions with graph diffusion, and trains only on procedurally generated simulated pseudo-demonstrations. It reaches 88.75% real-world success on 16 tasks, versus roughly 34-41% for baselines.

## Problem

- Behavioural cloning methods need hundreds or thousands of demonstrations plus hours of network training per task, so acquiring a new skill is slow and inconvenient.
- Prior in-context imitation approaches like BC-Z, Vid2Robot and GPT-style models lack 3D structural inductive biases, so they struggle with unseen tasks and novel object geometries.
- Robotics lacks the huge, readily available datasets that enabled in-context learning in language and vision, and collecting robot data by hand is expensive and does not scale.

## Method

- **Instant Policy** builds a ==heterogeneous graph representation==: segmented point-cloud nodes and gripper keypoint nodes, linked across demonstration waypoints, the current state and imagined future-action gripper poses through ==sinusoidal relative-position edges==.
- ==Graph diffusion==: a ==heterogeneous graph transformer== denoises only the action nodes. It predicts separate per-node translation and rotation flows, and ==SVD alignment== recovers SE(3) updates during ==DDIM== sampling.
- ==Pseudo-demonstration== pipeline: randomly samples object-centric waypoints on ShapeNet objects in simulation, producing semantically consistent but arbitrary trajectories as an effectively unlimited stream, about 700K trajectories over 2.5M training steps.

## Results

- On 24 RLBench tasks, pseudo-demonstrations alone give **71%** average success versus **34-38%** for baselines. Adding in-domain demonstrations raises this to **97%** on seen and **82%** on unseen tasks.
- Real-world Sawyer evaluation on 16 everyday tasks reaches **88.75%** success from two demonstrations, versus **34.38%** for BC-Z, **40.63%** for Vid2Robot and **36.88%** for GPT2.
- Ablations: removing diffusion costs **29%**, quaternion actions cost **37%** and goal-only demonstrations cost **71%**. Success on novel object geometries rises from **40%** with one demonstration to **90%** with four.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - An in-context policy's weights must encode how to interpret context, not task knowledge, so semantically consistent but physically arbitrary synthetic trajectories can be enough training data.
> - Putting observations and actions in one shared geometric graph space gives a strong inductive bias, beating generic token-sequence transformers given the same encoder and data.
> - Gains from more parameters level off around 117M, which suggests the diversity of synthetic data, not model size, becomes the limit for these generalist imitation models.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is positioned at the forefront of several rapidly evolving areas within artificial intelligence and robotics: Imitation Learning (IL), In-Context Learning (ICL), Diffusion Models, and Graph Neural Networks (GNNs).

Traditionally, **Imitation Learning (IL)**, particularly through **Behavioural Cloning (BC)**, has been a cornerstone for teaching robots complex skills. However, a significant limitation of conventional BC methods is their exorbitant data requirement, often demanding hundreds or thousands of demonstrations for each new task. This process is time-consuming, expensive, and impractical for real-world deployment across a diverse range of tasks.

The paper draws direct inspiration from the recent breakthroughs in **In-Context Learning (ICL)**, a paradigm popularized by large language models like GPT-3. ICL enables models to adapt to novel tasks at test time by simply providing a few examples (the "context"), without requiring any weight updates or retraining. The promise of transferring this "instant adaptation" capability to robotics, termed **In-Context Imitation Learning (ICIL)**, is immense. However, a critical hurdle in robotics is the lack of readily available, massive and diverse datasets comparable to those used in natural language processing or computer vision. This paper directly confronts this challenge, seeking ways to enable ICIL in robotics despite data scarcity.

To address the need for efficient learning with limited data, the authors leverage **Diffusion Models**. These generative models have gained significant traction, initially for high-quality image generation, and more recently for robotics applications such as visuomotor policy learning and generating imagined goals. "Instant Policy" introduces a novel application of diffusion models: **graph generation**. By framing the problem of predicting robot actions as denoising a graph that encodes demonstrations, observations, and actions, the paper pushes the boundaries of how diffusion models can be applied to structured data in robotics.

Furthermore, the work heavily relies on **Graph Neural Networks (GNNs)**. GNNs are powerful tools for processing and learning from structured data, widely used in various domains from chemistry to social networks. In robotics, GNNs have proven effective for tasks involving object relationships and complex state representations. This paper employs a heterogeneous graph representation, allowing for structured reasoning over the diverse information sources (demonstrations, current observations, and future actions), capturing complex relationships that are crucial for successful task execution.

In essence, "Instant Policy" synthesizes these advanced machine learning techniques to tackle the fundamental challenge of data-efficient and flexible robot skill acquisition. It moves beyond task-specific behavioral cloning towards a more generalizable, "instant" learning paradigm for robotics, paving the way for robots that can rapidly adapt to new instructions and environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of "Instant Policy" is to enable **In-Context Imitation Learning (ICIL)** for robotics, specifically allowing robots to learn new manipulation tasks **instantly** (without further training) from just one or two demonstrations. This ambitious goal is driven by a clear motivation to overcome the significant limitations of traditional Imitation Learning (IL) methods, particularly Behavioural Cloning (BC).

The authors highlight two core challenges that prevent the widespread adoption of ICIL in robotics, especially when compared to its success in language and vision:
1. **Data Efficiency and Representation:** Robotics lacks the vast, readily available datasets found in other domains. Therefore, there is a critical need for appropriate inductive biases in observation and action representations for efficient learning in 3D space, maximizing the utility of limited real-world data.
2. **Scalable Data Collection:** Manually collecting diverse and extensive robotics data is inefficient and costly. A scalable and practical means of acquiring training data is essential for developing generalizable robot policies.

To address these challenges, the "Instant Policy" framework sets out to achieve the following:
- **Develop a novel representation:** Create a unified graph representation that seamlessly integrates demonstrations, current point cloud observations, and robot actions. This aims to capture complex spatial and temporal relationships and provide suitable inductive biases for efficient learning.
- **Formulate ICIL as a generative problem:** Cast the problem of predicting robot actions within this graph structure as a diffusion-based graph generation task, leveraging the power of diffusion models to learn complex conditional distributions.
- **Propose a scalable data generation strategy:** Introduce the concept of "pseudo-demonstrations," which are procedurally generated robot trajectories in simulation, offering a virtually infinite and scalable source of training data without the need for real-world collection.
- **Demonstrate practical applicability:** Evaluate the proposed Instant Policy in both simulated and real-world environments across a variety of everyday tasks, demonstrating its superior performance compared to state-of-the-art baselines, its ability to generalize, and its potential for downstream applications like cross-embodiment and zero-shot language transfer.

In essence, the motivation is to transform robot learning from a task-specific, data-intensive process into a generalizable, rapid, and adaptable capability, making robot deployment more flexible and accessible.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Instant Policy achieves In-Context Imitation Learning (ICIL) through a novel combination of a structured graph representation, a learned diffusion process for graph generation, and a scalable pseudo-demonstration training data strategy.

#### 3.1. Overview & Problem Formulation

The core problem is to learn a probability distribution of future robot actions, $p(a_{t:t+T} | o_t, \{(o_{ij}, a_{ij})\}_{i=1, j=1}^{L,N})$, conditioned on the current observation ($o_t$) and a set of demonstrations (context $C$). Robot actions $a$ are defined as end-effector displacements in SE(3) and binary gripper commands. The observations $o_t$ consist of segmented point clouds, the current end-effector pose, and gripper state. The framework predicts a sequence of actions for closed-loop reactive control.

#### 3.2. Graph Representation

The paper proposes a **heterogeneous graph** as the core representation to jointly express context, current observation, and future actions, introducing crucial inductive biases:

- **Local Representation ($G_t^l$):** Represents the robot's state at time $t$. It uses $M$ points sampled from segmented point clouds ($P_t$) and encodes local geometry using Set Abstraction (SA) layers and a pre-trained implicit occupancy network. The gripper's state is represented by rigidly transforming key points on the end-effector and assigning them feature embeddings. Scene and gripper nodes are linked by directional edges with sine/cosine embeddings representing relative Cartesian positions, capturing high-frequency spatial changes.
- **Context Representation ($G_c$):** A sequence of local graphs (from demonstrations) interleaved with actions defines a trajectory. This involves linking gripper nodes across time to represent relative movement (red edges) and connecting all demonstration gripper nodes to the current gripper node (grey edges). This design ensures efficient information flow and linear scaling with context length.
- **Action Representation ($G_a^l$):** Future actions are represented as "imagined" local graphs, assuming the actions were executed. This means actions are fully described by the positions and features of nodes in these local graphs. Edges are added between current and future gripper nodes to represent relative movements between timesteps, propagating information from context and current observation to action nodes. The final graph, $G$, aggregates all this information, enabling reasoning about robot actions in a unified space.

#### 3.3. Learning Robot Action via Graph Diffusion

ICIL is framed as a **graph generation problem**, learning a distribution $p_\theta(G)$ using a diffusion model.

- **Training (Forward Process):** Noise is iteratively added to the ground truth robot actions (end-effector SE(3) transformations, projected to se(3) and gripper binary states), gradually transforming the ground truth graph representation into a noisy graph.
- **Training (Reverse Diffusion Process):** A parameterized model $p_\theta(G_{k-1} | G_k)$ learns to reconstruct the original graph from its noise-altered state. The model predicts a gradient field $\epsilon_\theta$ for gripper nodes representing actions. This gradient field has two components: $\nabla p$ for position updates and $\nabla a_g$ for gripper state. Crucially, the position denoising direction is decoupled into centre-of-mass movement ($\nabla \hat{p}_t$) and rotation around it ($\nabla \hat{p}_r$) to improve precision. The model is trained by minimizing the MSE between predicted and true noise.
- **Network Architecture:** A **heterogeneous graph transformer** is used as the parameterized model. It consists of three separate networks: $\sigma$ for local subgraphs (initial information propagation), $\phi$ for context aggregation, and $\psi$ for action prediction. This structured propagation ensures controlled and meaningful information flow, creating a bottleneck representation for efficient context interpretation.
- **Deployment:** At test time, the model iteratively updates *only* the parts of the graph representing robot actions, starting from Gaussian noise. The DDIM (Denoising Diffusion Implicit Models) sampling method is used for denoising. SE(3) transformations (actions) are extracted by aligning point sets (gripper nodes) using Singular Value Decomposition (SVD). This process is repeated for $K$ steps until the denoised actions are extracted, enabling sampling from the learned conditional action distribution.

#### 3.4. An Infinite Pool of Data: Pseudo-Demonstrations

To address the data scarcity in robotics, the paper introduces **pseudo-demonstrations** as a virtually infinite source of training data.

- **Data Generation:** A simulated environment is populated with diverse objects (ShapeNet). "Pseudo-tasks" are created by randomly sampling object-centric waypoints that the robot needs to reach sequentially. By virtually moving the gripper between these waypoints and mimicking rigid grasps, pseudo-demonstrations (trajectories) are generated. The poses of objects and the gripper are randomized to increase diversity. The key insight is that for ICIL, the model's weights encode a general ability to *interpret* context, not task-specific policies, meaning "arbitrary but consistent" trajectories are sufficient. Sampling is biased towards common skills like grasping and pick-and-place.
- **Data Usage:** During training, $N$ pseudo-demonstrations for a given pseudo-task are sampled, with $N-1$ defining the context and the $N$-th used for action prediction. Data augmentation techniques, such as adding local disturbances and flipping gripper states, are applied to enhance robustness and promote recovery behaviors.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

Instant Policy demonstrates strong performance across various experiments in both simulation and the real world, validating its core hypotheses and design choices.

#### 4.1. Simulated Experiments

- **Effectiveness:** Instant Policy consistently achieves significantly higher success rates than state-of-the-art baselines (BC-Z*, Vid2Robot*, GPT2*) on 24 RLBench tasks. This highlights the importance of the proposed graph representation and diffusion-based approach for interpreting context effectively.
- **Pseudo-Demonstrations Impact:** All methods showed non-zero success rates even with only pseudo-demonstrations, demonstrating their power as a limitless data source for ICIL.
- **Real-World Data Integration:** Incorporating a small amount of additional demonstrations (20 demos for 12 tasks) from the target domain (PD++) significantly boosted performance for all methods, showing the benefit of fine-tuning with relevant real-world data.
- **Generalization to Unseen Tasks/Poses:** Instant Policy showed strong generalization capabilities to tasks and object poses unseen during training.
- **Attention Mechanism Insights:** Visualizations of attention weights revealed the model's ability to track task progress and focus on relevant information within the graph context.

#### 4.2. Instant Policy Design Choices & Scaling Trends

- **Action Mode:** Decoupling translation and rotation in Cartesian space for action prediction (using $(\Delta p_t, \Delta p_r)$) was found to be crucial, outperforming other representations by a significant margin (e.g., -15% for $\Delta p$ and -37% for $(\Delta t, \Delta q)$).
- **Diffusion Mode:** Predicting "flow" (denoising direction) yielded the best results, underscoring the vital role of the diffusion process in capturing complex action distributions. Directly sampling or omitting diffusion led to substantial performance drops.
- **Inference Parameters:**
 - **Number of Diffusion Steps (K):** Good performance could be achieved with as few as two denoising steps, balancing computational cost and accuracy.
 - **Demonstration Length (L):** Critical for task conveyance; shorter lengths (e.g., L=1, just the final goal) led to very poor performance, while extending beyond a certain point offered diminishing returns for RLBench tasks.
 - **Number of Demonstrations (N):** Performance generally improved with more demonstrations, with two demonstrations often being sufficient to disambiguate tasks when generalizing over object poses.
- **Scaling Trends:** The model's ability to capture the underlying data distribution (decreasing validation loss) scaled well with both increased training time (optimization steps) and model complexity (number of trainable parameters). This suggests that more compute and larger models could lead to even better performance, although improvements plateaued for very large models, indicating a potential need for more diverse and representative data.

#### 4.3. Real-World Experiments

- **Everyday Tasks:** Instant Policy successfully completed a wide range of 16 everyday real-world manipulation tasks with high success rates (average 88.75%), significantly outperforming all baselines (average 34-40%) even with imperfect segmentations and noisy point clouds. This demonstrates its practical viability.
- **Generalization to Novel Objects:** With an increasing number of demonstrations provided in the context (N=3 or N=4 demos using different object instances), Instant Policy showed an emergent ability to generalize to completely novel object geometries from the same semantic category. This highlights its capacity to selectively aggregate and interpolate information to disambiguate tasks and relevant object parts.

#### 4.4. Downstream Applications

- **Cross-embodiment Transfer:** The framework allows for policy transfer from human-hand demonstrations to robot policies, leveraging the shared point cloud and end-effector pose representation. Qualitative experiments showed success on simple tasks, though limitations arose from the handcrafted mapping.
- **Modality Change (Zero-shot Language Transfer):** By learning to approximate the model's internal bottleneck representation using language commands and current observations (via a smaller, language-annotated dataset and contrastive objective), Instant Policy demonstrated qualitative zero-shot task completion based solely on language. This opens avenues for more intuitive robot control.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Instant Policy" framework represents a significant step forward in the field of robot learning, offering a compelling alternative to traditional, data-intensive Behavioural Cloning methods. Its main contributions and potential impacts are:

- **Paradigm Shift for Robot Skill Acquisition:** By enabling robots to learn new tasks instantly from just one or two demonstrations, Instant Policy moves robot learning towards a more human-like, adaptable, and efficient paradigm. This drastically reduces the time and effort required for robot deployment in new scenarios, making robotic automation more agile and accessible for a wider range of applications.
- **Addressing Data Scarcity in Robotics:** The novel concept of "pseudo-demonstrations" is a game-changer for robot learning. It provides a virtually unlimited and scalable source of training data from simulation, circumventing the perennial bottleneck of real-world data collection. This approach, combined with the efficient learning capabilities of graph diffusion models, makes large-scale generalizable robot learning more feasible than ever before.
- **Innovative Technical Contributions:** The paper introduces several key technical innovations:
 - **Graph-based Diffusion for ICIL:** This novel formulation leverages the strengths of diffusion models for generative tasks and GNNs for structured data, allowing complex relationships between demonstrations, observations, and actions to be learned and exploited.
 - **Heterogeneous Graph Representation:** The carefully designed graph, which unifies context, current state, and actions, provides crucial inductive biases for robust and effective reasoning in 3D space.
 - **Decoupled Action Prediction:** The method of decoupling translation and rotation predictions within the diffusion process contributes to higher precision in low-level action learning.
- **Enhanced Generalization Capabilities:** The demonstrated emergent ability to generalize to novel object geometries—even when not explicitly trained for it—is highly significant. This suggests that the model learns deeper, task-relevant invariants rather than merely memorizing specific object shapes, a critical step towards truly versatile robots.
- **Enabling Downstream Applications:** The successful qualitative demonstrations of cross-embodiment transfer and zero-shot control via language commands highlight the modularity and latent potential of Instant Policy. These capabilities open doors for more intuitive human-robot interaction and abstract task specification, reducing the need for direct robot programming or teleoperation.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
Despite its impressive achievements, the authors candidly acknowledge several limitations. These include the reliance on segmented point clouds (which may be prone to error), the lack of richer semantic information (like color) in observations, focus on short-horizon tasks (Markovian assumption), sensitivity to demonstration quality, and the current absence of built-in collision avoidance or full configuration space control. Addressing these limitations offers clear avenues for future research, such as improving pseudo-demonstration generation to incorporate collision dynamics and long-horizon tasks, and enhancing the graph representation with multimodal features (e.g., vision foundation models, force feedback) and past observations to achieve even greater robustness and precision.

In conclusion, "Instant Policy" offers a robust and innovative framework that significantly advances the state-of-the-art in robot skill acquisition, laying a strong foundation for future developments in adaptable and intelligent robotic systems.
