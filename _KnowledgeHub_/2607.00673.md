# Path Planning in Physically Viable World Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at The University of Texas at Austin developed physically viable world models (PVWMs) that integrate 3D Gaussian splat scene reconstructions with physics-based simulations to enable long-horizon path planning under future, hypothetical terrain changes. This framework allows robots to evaluate mission feasibility and identify critical failure modes, such as complete loss of reachability, before deployment in dynamic outdoor environments.

## Problem

- Robots in unstructured outdoor environments rely on static map representations, which often become "stale" due to dynamic physical changes like flooding or terrain collapse.
- Traditional path planning struggles with long-horizon decision-making, as unforeseen changes can render committed routes unsafe or impassable, potentially leading to mission failure.
- Existing world models and scene representations primarily assume static scenes, lacking explicit incorporation of detailed terrain mechanics or environment evolution under specific physical interactions.

## Method

- A pipeline combining ==3D Gaussian splat reconstructions==, physics-based ==Material Point Method (MPM)== simulations for environmental interventions (e.g., flooding, landslides), and a ==terrain-aware path planner==.
- Scene Gaussians are classified into ground, obstacle, and floater categories to derive a ==Digital Elevation Model (DEM)== and obstacle mask, which are updated by simulated intervention surfaces.
- A ==B-spline trajectory optimizer==, incorporating ==terrain band constraints== and ==volumetric hazard encoding==, is used with an initial path provided by a ==graph search== over the modified navigation surface.

## Results

- In a Central Texas field site simulation, one route became completely unreachable under severe flooding, a critical mission failure mode unidentifiable from static scene analysis.
- For an Alaskan village scene, a simulated vehicle experienced a loss of momentum and mobility in flooded conditions, demonstrating how physical interactions can render routes infeasible despite unchanged static geometry.
- In a controlled sandbox, the planner successfully generated viable detours around newly formed obstructions caused by simulated landslides, showcasing predictive planning for counterfactual hazards.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating physics-based simulation with 3D scene representations enables "what-if" queries, revealing how future physical changes impact robot navigation and mission feasibility.
> - Long-horizon path failures and losses of reachability, unidentifiable with static scene planning, can be exposed proactively by reasoning over physically modified environments.
> - Route feasibility for ground robots can depend on complex physical interactions (e.g., vehicle-fluid dynamics) captured by dynamic world models but absent in static geometric reconstructions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Traditional path planning algorithms typically operate on static representations of an environment, assuming that the map remains unchanged throughout a mission. This assumption presents a significant limitation for robots deployed in unstructured outdoor environments such as off-road terrain or disaster zones, where dynamic physical changes like flooding, terrain collapse, or debris accumulation can rapidly alter traversability after a map has been generated. When a robot commits to a route in such an environment, encountering an unforeseen change could render the path unsafe or impassable, potentially leading to mission failure or irreversible situations.

Existing research in off-road autonomy has addressed challenges such as traversability estimation, terrain classification, local planning, and adaptive control, often through the use of terrain-aware cost maps or dynamic models. However, these approaches largely treat the overarching environment as fixed, focusing on local variations rather than large-scale, physically driven transformations. Similarly, advancements in neural scene representations, such as NeRF-based methods and Gaussian splatting, have enabled planning directly within learned environment models, but these too primarily operate under the premise of static scenes.

The concept of "world models" has gained traction in embodied AI, providing predictive representations for planning and decision-making. While latent and generative world models improve prediction and policy learning, they frequently do not explicitly incorporate detailed terrain mechanics, material properties, or environment evolution under specific physical interactions. More recently, there has been a push towards physics-informed models and physically grounded world models that combine neural scene representations with explicit simulation, such as Gaussian splatting coupled with continuum mechanics and Material Point Method (MPM) dynamics.

This work contributes to this emerging landscape by specifically addressing the problem of long-horizon path planning under *future, hypothetical physical changes* to an environment. It bridges the gap between static scene representations and dynamic, physics-driven predictions by constructing "physically viable world models" (PVWMs). Unlike methods that seek to improve the current world estimate through additional sensing, this research focuses on evaluating how future physical changes might affect navigation *before* execution. This enables query-driven reasoning over families of physically modified scenes, extending planning capabilities beyond merely finding the best path in an observed environment to assessing mission feasibility under specified physical changes. It builds upon foundational work in Gaussian splat-based trajectory optimization (e.g., FOCI) by integrating explicit physical simulation to generate and plan within dynamic, counterfactual environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the challenges faced by robots operating in dynamic, unstructured outdoor environments. Such robots often rely on scene reconstructions collected prior to deployment, as re-mapping large or remote sites before every mission is impractical. Consequently, robots must make long-horizon planning decisions based on "stale maps" that assume static terrain. This assumption is frequently violated by real-world physical changes—such as flooding, terrain collapse, or debris accumulation—which can render previously feasible routes unsafe or entirely unreachable during mission execution. In constrained environments, such as steep ravines, the consequences of misjudging terrain viability can be severe, potentially trapping a robot without a safe means of retreat or recovery.

To address this critical limitation, the core objective of this work is to introduce and evaluate a "physically viable world model" (PVWM). This model is designed to support "what-if" queries for robot navigation under future terrain changes, thereby enhancing pre-execution route evaluation.

The key objectives and motivations can be detailed as follows:

- **Enabling Future Terrain Change Evaluation:** The central aim is to allow robots and human operators to assess the feasibility of planned routes *before* committing to them, specifically under hypothesized future terrain changes. This capability moves beyond reactive replanning during execution, which may be insufficient when a robot has already entered an unrecoverable situation.
- **Augmenting Static Reconstructions with Physics:** The research seeks to develop a system that augments existing 3D Gaussian splat scene reconstructions with physics-based simulation. This enables the generation of physically modified versions of an environment without the need for recollecting sensor data or rebuilding the map for each hypothetical scenario. This is crucial for environments where real-time re-mapping is not feasible.
- **Developing a Terrain-Aware Planner for Dynamic Environments:** A specific objective is to implement a path planner capable of reasoning over these physically modified environments. This planner must account for simulated physical events, newly introduced obstacles, and terrain deformations, ensuring that generated routes are physically realizable for ground-contact vehicles. This includes enforcing ground-contact constraints, blocking intervention-affected regions based on physical severity, and considering the volumetric extent of hazards.
- **Quantifying Route and Mission Feasibility Degradation:** The work aims to quantitatively measure how route and mission feasibility are affected as terrain conditions deteriorate under physically simulated interventions. This involves assessing metrics such as route persistence, the length of alternative routes, the contraction of reachable areas, and overall mission success.
- **Revealing Hidden Failure Modes:** A key motivation is to expose long-horizon route failures, significant rerouting behaviors, and losses of reachability that would not be apparent when planning solely on the original, static reconstructed environment. Such insights are intended to facilitate proactive decision-making and risk stratification.

In summary, the research is motivated by the necessity for robots to anticipate and plan for dynamic environmental changes, rather than merely reacting to them, particularly in safety-critical and physically challenging outdoor settings. The PVWM is presented as a framework to address this need by integrating physical simulation into the planning process.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology presented in this paper involves a pipeline that combines 3D scene reconstruction, physics-based simulation of environmental interventions, and a terrain-aware path planning algorithm. The overall goal is to enable query-driven reasoning over physically modified scenes.

#### 3.1. Scene Reconstruction and Terrain Model

The process begins with a 3D Gaussian splat reconstruction of a scene, generated from historical observations. This reconstruction provides a set of Gaussian primitives, each defined by a mean position, covariance matrix, color coefficients, and opacity.
- **Digital Elevation Model (DEM) Derivation:** A 2D Digital Elevation Model (DEM) is derived from these Gaussian primitives. The primitives are binned into a grid, and the maximum z-value per cell defines the terrain height `h(x, y)`.
- **Gaussian Classification:** A critical step is the classification of Gaussians into ground, obstacle, and floater categories without relying on semantic labels. This is achieved by exploiting geometric properties:
 - Ground surfaces typically produce flat, horizontally-oriented Gaussians.
 - Obstacles (e.g., rocks, walls, vegetation) produce vertically-oriented or compact Gaussians.
 - Floaters (reconstruction artifacts like haze, canopy, view-dependent lighting) can be vertically elongated or isotropic.
 - Two sequential filters are applied:
 1. Gaussians whose vertical extent is smaller than a threshold fraction of their horizontal extent are retained, effectively selecting for ground-like geometry and rejecting vertically elongated primitives.
 2. Cells where the maximum z-value deviates from the local neighborhood median by more than a threshold `∆h_max` are removed, preventing floating Gaussians and aerial artifacts from corrupting the terrain surface.
- **Obstacle Mask Generation:** An obstacle mask is computed over the same grid. A cell is marked impassable if any Gaussian within its column exceeds the local DEM surface by more than a traversability threshold `τ_obs`, identifying steep terrain, boulders, and above-ground clutter.
- The DEM and obstacle mask are combined to form an initial 2D binary occupancy map `G_0`.

#### 3.2. Intervention Simulation

Environmental interventions, such as flooding or terrain collapse, are simulated using the Material Point Method (MPM). MPM is a continuum-based physics simulation method suitable for large deformations, representing materials as discrete particles (material points) interacting via a background grid.
- **Physics Engine:** The system extends methods like PhysGaussian to support multi-material interactions and long-horizon simulations. Different interventions utilize specific constitutive models (e.g., fluids for flooding, granular materials for landslides, elastic models for obstacles).
- **Scene Modification:** Each simulation modifies the baseline Gaussian splat reconstruction by displacing, depositing, or removing Gaussian primitives, yielding a physically consistent modified scene.
- **Intervention Magnitude:** Simulations are conducted across a range of intervention magnitudes (e.g., varying flood levels).
- **Planner Input Generation:** For each simulated scene, three key quantities are derived and passed to the planner:
 - `h_int(x, y)`: The intervention surface height (e.g., water level for flooding, deposited material height for landslides).
 - `M_i(x, y)`: An impassable mask, flagging cells where the intervention depth (`h_int - h_terrain`) exceeds a wading threshold `τ_wade` as hard no-go regions.
 - `h_nav(x, y)`: The navigation surface, defined as `h_int(x, y)` if `M_i(x, y) = 0` and solid material is deposited, otherwise `h(x, y)`. This means the robot navigates on deposited solid material where traversable, and on the dry terrain surface otherwise (including through shallow fluid).

#### 3.3. Traversability-Aware Planning

The planning pipeline builds upon the FOCI framework, which utilizes Gaussian overlap collision integrals and B-spline trajectory optimization. It is extended to incorporate physical consistency in intervention-affected environments.
- **Trajectory Parameterization:** The robot's trajectory is represented as an unclamped uniform cubic B-spline with control points `Q` over (x, y, z, θ), where θ is yaw. The spline is discretized into `K` samples.
- **Robot Representation:** The robot is modeled as `M` Gaussian primitives, and its position and orientation are derived from a three-point kinematic model.
- **Collision Cost `C(x_k)`:** The collision cost is computed as the analytic overlap integral between the robot's Gaussians and the scene Gaussians (both original and intervention-generated). This computation is GPU-accelerated using NVIDIA Warp.
- **Heading Alignment Cost `J_head`:** A term `J_head` is added to penalize deviations between the robot's yaw and its velocity direction, which is important for ground-contact vehicles.
- **Optimization Formulation:** A nonlinear program is solved using IPOPT. The objective function minimizes a weighted sum of jerk, collision cost, distance to goal, and heading alignment.
 - **Constraints:** Constraints include a starting point tolerance, continuous velocity and acceleration bounds (enforced via MINVO spline basis), and a crucial **terrain band constraint**: `h_nav(x_k, y_k) + c ≤ z_k ≤ h_nav(x_k, y_k) + c + b`. This constraint keeps each discretized state within a navigable vertical band above the navigation surface (`h_nav`), preventing the optimizer from exploiting verticality to shortcut through obstacles and enforcing ground contact.
- **Graph Search with Terrain-Based Cell Masking:** A voxel-based graph search provides an initial path for the B-spline optimization. Unlike standard 3D free-space search, this search operates primarily over the `h_nav` surface, restricting neighbor expansion to the XY plane. `z` is constrained to a local band above `h_nav`, and cells flagged in the impassable mask `M_i` are blocked as hard constraints. This ensures physically plausible initial paths for ground robots.
- **Volumetric Hazard Encoding:** To ensure the spline optimizer respects the impassable regions, a "volumetric hazard encoding" is used. For cells flagged in `M_i`, a vertical column of Gaussian obstacle primitives is constructed, spanning from the dry terrain DEM `h(x, y)` up to the intervention surface height `h_int(x, y)`. These volumetric obstacles are added to the collision cost calculation, making these regions costly to traverse at any height within the intervention volume.
- **Post-Optimization Projection:** After optimization, each trajectory state `z_k` is projected onto the dry terrain surface (`h(x_k, y_k) + c`), ensuring the robot remains on the ground.

This comprehensive approach allows the system to generate and evaluate physically consistent routes in dynamic environments derived from a single static reconstruction.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluated the proposed physically viable world model (PVWM) and its associated planning pipeline across three distinct experimental scenarios: a real outdoor field site in Central Texas, an Alaskan village scene, and a controlled toy sandbox environment. These evaluations aimed to demonstrate how the PVWM exposes different types of navigation challenges and planning insights that are not discernible from static scene representations.

#### 4.1. Central Texas Hill Country Field Site (Flooding Scenario)

- **Environment:** A 500 × 500 ft site with steep limestone terrain, narrow drainage channels, and susceptibility to flash flooding, reconstructed from drone video into a 3D Gaussian splat model.
- **Intervention:** Simulated flooding across multiple severity levels (low, medium, high) by releasing varying volumes of water material points into the drainage channel, generating different traversability conditions from a single drought reconstruction.
- **Evaluation:** For four distinct start-goal pairs, the planner generated routes, and their rerouting cost ratio (`δ_i`) relative to the drought route was measured. The planner blocked cells where flood depth exceeded a wading threshold (`τ_wade`) but allowed traversal through shallower water.
- **Results:**
 - **Pairs 1 and 2:** Remained mission-feasible at all flood levels. Rerouting costs increased progressively with flood severity (e.g., Pair 1 showed an increase from +0.4% at low severity to +20.3% at high severity). This indicated minor detours evolving into substantial rerouting as conditions worsened.
 - **Pair 4:** Exhibited a similar monotonic increase in rerouting cost but with smaller overall growth (+1.2% to +7.2%), suggesting a crossing that gradually narrowed without a distinct hard threshold.
 - **Pair 3:** Incurred modest detours at low and medium flood levels (+5.4% and +5.2% respectively) but became **unreachable** under severe flooding. This represented a critical mission failure mode, unidentifiable from the static drought reconstruction. The PVWM revealed this risk, enabling pre-deployment replanning or mission abortion, which is crucial given the steep ravine preventing safe reversal.
- **Conclusion:** The PVWM effectively differentiated outcome profiles for various routes, demonstrating transitions from persistent paths to topologically distinct detours and, in one critical case, complete loss of reachability, all unseen in static planning.

#### 4.2. Alaska Village Scene (Vehicle Mobility Under Flooding)

- **Environment:** A drone-reconstructed Alaskan village scene with a road intersection.
- **Intervention:** Simulated a ground vehicle (truck splatting model) traversing the intersection under both dry and flooded conditions, using the exact same underlying scene geometry. The only difference was the presence of floodwater and its interaction with the vehicle. Terrain was modeled as granular material, and water as additional MPM material points.
- **Evaluation:** Comparison of truck trajectories and mobility under dry versus flooded conditions.
- **Results:**
 - **Dry Conditions:** The truck successfully traversed the intersection and followed its intended route.
 - **Flooded Conditions:** The vehicle entered the inundated region, experienced a loss of momentum, and a substantial reduction in mobility due to interaction with the water. As flooding intensified, the vehicle reached a critical submerged depth and was unable to complete the crossing, rendering the route infeasible despite the scene's geometry remaining unchanged.
- **Conclusion:** This experiment demonstrated that route feasibility can depend on physical interactions (vehicle-fluid dynamics) that are entirely absent from static geometric reconstructions. The PVWM's physical simulation captured these dynamic interactions, predicting a failure mode a static map could not.

#### 4.3. Toy Sandbox (Counterfactual Hazards: Landslides)

- **Environment:** A controlled sandbox environment featuring a granular valley with a narrow central channel and obstacle cubes.
- **Intervention:** Simulated landslides (granular material collapse) occurring on either the left or right side of the valley, creating hazards not present in the original captured scene.
- **Evaluation:** Planning for a start and goal point on opposite sides of the valley under three conditions: no landslide, left-side collapse, and right-side collapse.
- **Results:**
 - **No Landslide:** The planner routed through the central passage, between the obstacle cubes, to reach the goal.
 - **Landslide Scenarios:** When a landslide occurred (e.g., on the left), the collapsing material filled the central passage. The planner successfully incorporated the resulting runout region into the occupancy map and generated an alternative path that routed around the obstruction (e.g., on the right side). The same behavior was observed when the landslide was simulated on the right, with the planner rerouting to the left.
- **Conclusion:** This experiment illustrated the system's ability to evaluate hypothetical, "counterfactual" hazards. It showed that the planner could effectively react to newly formed obstructions by generating viable detours, demonstrating the predictive planning capability for events not yet observed.

Across all experiments, the main finding is that physically modified world models provide crucial insights into route feasibility, rerouting behavior, and reachability loss that static scene reconstructions are unable to offer.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces a significant advancement in robot path planning, particularly for deployments in dynamic and unstructured outdoor environments. By developing and evaluating physically viable world models (PVWMs), the authors address a fundamental limitation of traditional planning methods that rely on static environmental representations.

The primary significance of this work lies in its ability to facilitate **pre-execution evaluation of route feasibility under future physical changes**. This capability moves beyond reactive planning and allows robots and human operators to proactively assess the safety and viability of missions before commitment, which is critical in situations where retreat or recovery might become impossible.

Potential impacts of this research include:

- **Enhanced Robot Autonomy and Safety:** Robots operating in challenging terrains (e.g., off-road vehicles, disaster response robots, planetary explorers) can make more informed long-horizon planning decisions. By anticipating potential environmental changes like floods or landslides, robots can avoid committing to hazardous routes, thus increasing mission success rates and reducing the risk of immobilization or damage.
- **Improved Human-Robot Teaming and Decision Support:** For human operators, the PVWM provides a powerful tool for risk stratification. Operators can query hypothetical future scenarios and visualize their impact on planned routes, enabling them to make better decisions about mission parameters, alternative routes, or even mission abortion, thereby improving overall operational safety and efficiency.
- **Robustness to "Stale Maps":** The approach reduces the reliance on real-time, up-to-date mapping data, which is often impractical or impossible to collect in large, remote, or rapidly changing environments. By augmenting existing, potentially stale reconstructions with physics-based simulations, the system enables effective planning even with historical sensor data.
- **Advanced "What-If" Planning Capabilities:** This work shifts the paradigm from merely finding an optimal path in a given environment to answering more complex "what-if" questions about mission feasibility under unforeseen physical changes. This capability supports more sophisticated risk assessment and strategic planning.
- **Integration of Perception and Physics for Embodied AI:** The methodology effectively integrates recent advances in 3D scene representation (Gaussian splatting) with high-fidelity physics simulation (MPM). This fusion provides a richer, predictive world model that can capture the complex interplay between robot mobility and dynamic terrain, moving towards more physically intelligent embodied AI systems.

Despite these potential impacts, the authors acknowledge several limitations. The current traversability estimation is primarily geometric, lacking semantic information or material properties, which could limit the accuracy of terrain understanding and automatic selection of appropriate physical models. The 2D occupancy map representation might lead to overly conservative obstacle estimates, particularly around complex 3D structures like trees. Furthermore, evaluating routes over a discrete set of intervention levels could miss critical transitions in feasibility. Finally, the fidelity of assessments is dependent on the accuracy of the underlying physical models, and the hypothetical environments exist only in simulation, posing challenges for direct hardware validation. Addressing these limitations could further broaden the applicability and robustness of physically viable world models.
