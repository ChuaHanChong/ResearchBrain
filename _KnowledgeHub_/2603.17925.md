# Multi-Armed Sequential Hypothesis Testing by Betting

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of California, Berkeley, and École Normale Supérieure & Inria generalized sequential hypothesis testing by betting to a multi-armed setting with partial information by developing the SPRUCE algorithm. This method achieves multi-armed Q-log-optimality, meaning its evidence accumulation rate asymptotically matches that of an oracle with full knowledge, and its expected rejection time asymptotically attains the theoretical lower bound as the type-I error rate approaches zero.

## Problem

- Generalizing sequential hypothesis testing by betting from a single data source to multiple, adaptively chosen data sources (arms).
- Efficiently detecting if at least one arm deviates from a global null hypothesis when only the outcome of the chosen arm is observed.
- Developing an optimal strategy for arm selection and portfolio allocation without prior knowledge of the optimal arm or its specific betting strategy.

## Method

- Introduced **SPRUCE** (Sublinear Portfolio Regret Upper Confidence Estimation), an ==Upper Confidence Bound (UCB)-like algorithm== for ==adaptive arm selection==.
- Constructed ==e-processes== as products of ==convex combinations of P-e-values==, dynamically optimizing ==portfolio allocation== for each arm and across arms.
- Developed novel ==nonasymptotic concentration inequalities== for log-increments to build UCB terms, overcoming limitations of standard sub-Gaussian assumptions.

## Results

- The **SPRUCE** algorithm achieved **multi-armed Q-log-optimality**, demonstrating that its asymptotic growth rate matches the maximum possible expected logarithmic increment across all arms and portfolios (**Theorem 3.7**).
- The expected time to rejection for SPRUCE was shown to asymptotically achieve the **theoretical lower bound** in the high-confidence regime (as type-I error α approaches zero) (**Theorem 4.2**).
- Established novel concentration inequalities for universal portfolios, proving that portfolio-oracle allocation regret for SPRUCE scales **logarithmically**, as **O(Σ_a log(n)/Δ_a,Q)** (**Theorem 5.3**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Multi-armed log-optimality can be achieved when an e-process exhibits both sublinear arm-wise portfolio regret and sublinear portfolio-oracle allocation regret.
> - Log-increments under optimal portfolios are sub-exponential, enabling the derivation of general nonasymptotic concentration inequalities crucial for adaptive testing.
> - Adaptive arm selection strategies, like SPRUCE, can effectively match the asymptotic performance of an omniscient oracle in sequential hypothesis testing, despite partial information.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research integrates concepts from several statistical and machine learning domains, including sequential hypothesis testing, multi-armed bandits, and causal inference. It specifically extends the framework of sequential testing by betting, a program with roots in the work of Abraham Wald and Herbert Robbins, which has seen renewed attention in recent decades.

Traditional sequential testing by betting primarily focuses on single-arm scenarios where data is drawn from a single source. This paper generalizes this framework to a multi-armed setting, where a statistician must choose which of several data sources (arms) to sample at each time step. This multi-armed aspect introduces challenges analogous to those encountered in multi-armed bandit problems, where the objective is often to identify a "best" arm or maximize cumulative reward while balancing exploration and exploitation.

The work also connects to global null hypothesis testing, a common challenge in multiple testing literature where the goal is to determine if at least one among several related hypotheses is false. Here, the global null posits that all arms are "null" in a specific sense, and the alternative is that at least one arm is "non-null." This differs from standard multiple testing by incorporating a sequential data collection protocol and partial information, as the outcomes from unselected arms are not observed.

The authors contrast their approach with concurrent work in active nonparametric two-sample testing (Hsu and Shekhar, 2025) and demonstration experiments (Imbens et al., 2026). They note that their work aims for nonasymptotic sequential testing optimality and considers broader distributional assumptions than some existing approaches, which may rely on sub-Gaussian tails or less refined betting strategies. The paper's contribution lies in developing an optimal strategy for arm selection and portfolio allocation within this complex, partial-information sequential testing setting, providing both growth rate and expected rejection time guarantees that match oracle performance.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop efficient sequential hypothesis tests for a multi-armed setting where data is collected iteratively from one of several available arms. The statistician aims to quickly gather evidence against a "global null" hypothesis (P), which states that all arms conform to a certain null property. The alternative hypothesis (Q) is that at least one arm deviates from this null property.

The motivation for this setup is rooted in practical scenarios where multiple variations of an intervention or treatment exist, and the goal is to identify if *any* of them are effective. A motivating example provided is a pharmaceutical company testing a new treatment with various dosages or administration protocols. Instead of running separate tests for each variation or a pooled test that might dilute evidence, the statistician needs a strategy to dynamically choose which treatment variation to test at each step, accumulating evidence against the global null.

A key optimality desideratum for this work is informally described as achieving a performance level as strong as an oracle. Specifically, even if multiple arms are non-null, the goal is to develop e-processes and sequential tests whose performance (in terms of evidence accumulation or rejection time) approaches that of a hypothetical agent with *a priori* knowledge of which arm generates the most evidence against the null. This implies matching the asymptotic behavior of a test constructed with full information about the optimal arm and its corresponding optimal betting strategy.

Formally, the objectives include:
1. **Generalizing Log-Optimality:** Extending the concept of single-arm asymptotic log-optimality (Definition 2.4) to the multi-armed, partial-information setting (Definition 3.1). This involves ensuring the e-process's growth rate matches the maximum possible growth rate across all arms and portfolios under the alternative.
2. **Developing an Optimal Algorithm:** Designing a sequential arm-selection and portfolio-allocation algorithm that achieves the generalized log-optimality without prior knowledge of the alternative distribution or the optimal arm/portfolio.
3. **Analyzing Expected Rejection Time:** Deriving lower and upper bounds on the expected number of samples required to reject the global null hypothesis (E[τ_α]) and demonstrating that the proposed algorithm achieves these bounds, particularly in the high-confidence regime (as type-I error α approaches zero).
4. **Addressing Partial Information Challenges:** Overcoming the statistical challenges posed by partial information, where only the outcome of the chosen arm is observed, and the optimal portfolios for each arm are unknown. This requires developing specialized concentration inequalities and adapting multi-armed bandit strategies.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research develops a framework for multi-armed sequential hypothesis testing by betting, building on the concept of e-processes and their growth rates.

**A. Multi-Armed Data Collection Protocol:**
The work operates under Algorithm 1, where at each time step `n`, Nature samples a `K`-vector of outcomes `(Y_n(1)..., Y_n(K))`. The statistician, based on past observations `(A_i, Y_i(A_i))`, chooses an arm `A_n ∈ {1..., K}` and observes only `Y_n(A_n)`. This introduces a partial information setting. The theoretical analysis frequently references a "history-oracle filtration" `H_n`, which assumes knowledge of all `K` outcomes up to time `n`, even though the statistician does not have this access.

**B. Global Null Hypothesis Testing:**
The focus is on a composite global null `P = ⋂_{a∈A} P_a` against a composite alternative `Q = ⋃_{a∈A} P_a^c`. `P_a` encodes a property for arm `a`, so `P` states all arms satisfy their respective properties, while `Q` states at least one arm does not. Proposition 2.6 establishes that time-uniform type-I error control under multi-armed data collection remains straightforward, provided that the multiplicative increments `f_n(Y_n(A_n))` are `H_{n-1}`-predictable and their expectation under any `P ∈ P` is less than or equal to 1.

**C. Class of Test Statistics:**
The paper focuses on test supermartingales (and e-processes) constructed as products of convex combinations of `P`-e-values. Specifically, the test statistic `W_n` takes the form `W_n = ∏_{i=1}^n λ_i^T E_i(A_i)`, where `E_i(A_i)` is a `(d+1)`-vector of `P`-e-values from the chosen arm `A_i`, and `λ_i` is a `Δ_d`-valued "portfolio" vector chosen at time `i`. Assumption 1 outlines properties for these e-values, including boundedness and the existence of a portfolio `˜λ` that evaluates to 1 under `Q`. The "oracle-history comparator class" `W` (Definition 2.9) represents processes that could be constructed with full oracle access.

**D. Multi-Armed Log-Optimality:**
The paper generalizes the concept of log-optimality to the multi-armed setting (Definition 3.1). An e-process `W_n` is multi-armed Q-log-optimal if its asymptotic growth rate `(1/n) log(W_n)` is not smaller than that of any other process `f_W_n` in the oracle-history comparator class `W` for any `Q ∈ Q`. This implies matching the growth rate of a process that knows the optimal arm `a_Q` and optimal portfolio `λ_Q(a_Q)` *a priori*.

**E. Portfolio and Allocation Regret:**
To achieve multi-armed log-optimality, the authors introduce two regret notions:
1. **Arm-wise portfolio regret (R_Port_n(a), Definition 3.3):** This measures the suboptimality of chosen portfolios `λ_i(a)` compared to the optimal portfolio `λ_Q(a)` *within a specific arm a*, considering only data from that arm. This is a generalization of Cover and Ordentlich's universal portfolio regret.
2. **Portfolio-oracle allocation regret (R_Alloc_n,Q, Definition 3.4):** This measures the suboptimality of the arm selection strategy `A_i` compared to consistently pulling the globally optimal arm `a_Q`, assuming oracle knowledge of the optimal portfolio `λ_Q(a)` for each arm `a`. This resembles regret in stochastic multi-armed bandits.

Proposition 3.5 establishes that if an e-process `W_n` exhibits sublinear arm-wise portfolio regret and sublinear allocation regret, then it is multi-armed Q-log-optimal.

**F. SPRUCE Algorithm (Sublinear Portfolio Regret Upper Confidence Estimation):**
The core algorithm proposed is SPRUCE (Algorithm 2). SPRUCE is an Upper Confidence Bound (UCB)-like algorithm designed to address the partial information problem. It maintains an `UCB_a(n)` for each arm `a`, which is the sum of the empirical mean of log-increments (achieved by maximizing `λ` over past pulls of arm `a`) and a confidence bonus term. The algorithm selects arm `A_n = argmax_a UCB_a(n)`.
SPRUCE supports two types of e-processes:
- `W_UP_n`: Based on Cover's universal portfolio algorithm (Equation 6) for each arm, run independently. This is a test `P`-supermartingale.
- `W_CO96_n`: An e-process based on an in-hindsight maximum less a regret bound (Orabona and Jun, 2023). This is computationally more efficient.

**G. Technical Proof Ingredients:**
A significant part of the methodology involves developing novel concentration inequalities. Standard UCB analyses often assume known sub-Gaussian rewards, which is not applicable here because `log(λ_Q(a)^T E_n(a))` can take arbitrarily large negative values and `λ_Q(a)` is unknown.
- **Lemma 5.1:** Demonstrates that the log-increments under optimal portfolios `log(λ_Q(a)^T E_n(a))` are sub-exponential (have finite moment generating functions in a neighborhood of zero), crucial for bounding their tails.
- **Proposition 5.2:** Derives nonasymptotic concentration inequalities for the difference between the empirical average of log-increments (maximized over portfolios) and the expected log-increment under the optimal portfolio. These inequalities are central to bounding `UCB_a(n)`.
- **Theorem 5.3:** Proves that SPRUCE achieves logarithmic portfolio-oracle allocation regret, `R_Alloc_n,Q = O(Σ_a log(n)/Δ_a,Q)`, where `Δ_a,Q` is the suboptimality gap of arm `a`. This confirms the sublinear regret required by Proposition 3.5.

**H. Expected Rejection Time Analysis:**
The paper also analyzes the expected time to rejection, `E_Q[τ_α]`, for `τ_α = inf{n: W_n ≥ 1/α}`. Proposition 4.1 provides a lower bound for any test of the form (4), which depends on `(max_(a,λ) E_Q[log(λ^T E_1(a))])^{-1}`.

**I. Application to Treatment Effect Testing:**
Section 6 demonstrates the application of SPRUCE to testing for the existence of a positive average treatment effect in a multi-armed randomized control trial. It uses the potential outcomes framework and the Horvitz-Thompson estimator for individual treatment effects. ### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research delivers several findings regarding optimal multi-armed sequential hypothesis testing.

**A. Type-I Error Control:**
- **Proposition 2.6:** This proposition establishes that adaptive, multi-armed data collection does not inherently complicate time-uniform type-I error control under the global null. If `P`-e-values are `H_{n-1}`-predictable and satisfy the supermartingale property for each `P ∈ P`, then their product, even with adaptively chosen arms, constitutes a valid test `P`-supermartingale. This simplifies the validity aspect, shifting complexity to power under the alternative.

**B. Multi-Armed Log-Optimality:**
- **Theorem 3.7:** This is a central finding, demonstrating that SPRUCE-generated e-processes are multi-armed Q-log-optimal according to Definition 3.1. This implies that:
 - For any `Q ∈ Q`, the empirical growth rate `(1/n) log(W_n)` of SPRUCE is asymptotically equivalent (Q-almost surely) to that of an oracle e-process `W*_{n,Q}` that *always* pulls the optimal arm `a_Q` and uses its optimal portfolio `λ_Q(a_Q)`.
 - The asymptotic growth rate `lim_{n→∞} (1/n) log(W_n)` equals `max_{(a,λ)∈A×Δ_d} E_Q[log(λ^T E_1(a))]` (Q-almost surely). This means SPRUCE achieves the maximum possible expected logarithmic increment over all arms and portfolios without prior knowledge of the optimal choices.
 - The result is unimprovable: no other test `f_W_n` (even with oracle access to the full history) can achieve a strictly larger asymptotic growth rate.

**C. Expected Time to Rejection:**
- **Proposition 4.1:** Provides a lower bound on the expected rejection time `E_Q[τ_α]` for any sequential test of the specified form. This lower bound is `(log(1/α)) / (max_{(a,λ)∈A×Δ_d} E_Q[log(λ^T E_1(a))])`. The denominator is precisely the maximum expected logarithmic increment achieved by SPRUCE.
- **Theorem 4.2:** Shows that SPRUCE achieves this lower bound in the "high-confidence" regime (as `α → 0^+`). Specifically, `lim_{α→0^+} E_Q[τ_SPRUCE_α] / log(1/α)` equals the inverse of the maximum expected logarithmic increment. This demonstrates that SPRUCE is asymptotically optimal in terms of how quickly it rejects the null for low type-I error rates, matching the performance of an oracle with full knowledge of the optimal arm and portfolio.

**D. Novel Concentration Inequalities (Independent Interest):**
- **Lemma 5.1:** Establishes that the log-increments `log(λ_Q(a)^T E_1(a))` under optimal portfolios are sub-exponential, meaning their moment generating functions are finite in a neighborhood of zero. This is a critical step, as it enables the application of sub-exponential concentration inequalities, which are more general than sub-Gaussian assumptions.
- **Proposition 5.2:** Derives nonasymptotic confidence intervals for the difference between the empirical average of log-increments (maximized over portfolios) and the expected log-increment under the optimal portfolio. These bounds are crucial for constructing the `UCB_a(n)` terms in SPRUCE.
- **Theorem 5.3:** Proves that the portfolio-oracle allocation regret `R_Alloc_n,Q` for SPRUCE is logarithmic, scaling as `O(Σ_a log(n)/Δ_a,Q)`. This sublinear regret bound, combined with sublinear arm-wise portfolio regret, is a key technical ingredient for proving multi-armed log-optimality.

**E. Empirical Validation:**
Simulations (Figure 1, Figure 2, Figure 3) support the theoretical findings, illustrating how SPRUCE's empirical growth rates converge to the oracle's performance and how its rejection times are reduced compared to naive arm selection strategies (e.g., Round Robin or Random Selection).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work makes a methodological contribution by extending the framework of sequential hypothesis testing by betting to a multi-armed, partial-information setting. It provides theoretical guarantees for log-optimality and expected rejection times, which are shown to be unimprovable by any oracle-aware strategy in the asymptotic or high-confidence regimes.

**Key Contributions:**
- **Unified Framework:** The paper successfully integrates sequential testing by betting with multi-armed bandit principles, offering a coherent approach to global null hypothesis testing under adaptive data collection.
- **Optimal Algorithm:** The SPRUCE algorithm provides a concrete and theoretically-backed method for designing sequential tests that balance exploration of different arms with exploitation of promising ones, while simultaneously optimizing portfolio allocation within each arm.
- **Robustness:** The approach avoids strong distributional assumptions (like sub-Gaussianity), making it potentially applicable to a wider range of real-world data, including heavy-tailed distributions.
- **Novel Technical Tools:** The derived concentration inequalities for optimal wealth growth rates and the analysis of sub-exponential log-increments are novel and may find applications in other areas requiring precise bounds in dynamic, uncertain environments.

**Potential Impact and Applications:**
The methodology has direct implications for fields requiring efficient sequential decision-making for hypothesis testing:
- **Clinical Trials and A/B Testing:** As highlighted in the motivating example, the framework can be used in pharmaceutical research or online platform A/B testing to efficiently identify effective treatment variations or product features. Instead of running numerous independent experiments or a single pooled study, the proposed methods allow for adaptive resource allocation to quickly detect any beneficial intervention.
- **Large Language Model (LLM) Development:** The authors suggest applications in testing properties of LLMs, where different "arms" could correspond to various prompts or candidate models. This could enable efficient evaluation and comparison of LLMs in an iterative manner.
- **Quantum State Certification:** The paper identifies quantum state certification as another domain where sequential hypothesis testing with partial information naturally arises. The proposed methods could potentially lead to more efficient protocols for validating quantum states.
- **General Scientific Discovery:** In any domain where researchers need to assess if "something works" across multiple variations or experimental conditions, and data acquisition is sequential and costly, this framework offers a principled approach to optimize the experimental process.

The work lays a foundation for future research, including incorporating side information (covariates or features) to further refine arm selection and portfolio construction. Overall, this research provides both theoretical guarantees and practical algorithms for complex sequential testing problems, enhancing the efficiency and power of statistical inference in dynamic, multi-source data environments.
