# Habitat-Web: Learning Embodied Object-Search Strategies from Human Demonstrations at Scale

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Georgia Institute of Technology and Meta AI developed Habitat-Web, a scalable web-based platform for collecting human demonstrations in the Habitat simulator. This platform generated over 92,000 human demonstrations for embodied object-search and manipulation tasks, enabling imitation learning agents to outperform state-of-the-art reinforcement learning baselines on ObjectGoal Navigation and achieve success on complex PICK&PLACE tasks where reinforcement learning agents struggled.

## Problem

- Designing effective reward functions for complex, general-purpose embodied AI tasks in reinforcement learning (RL) is highly challenging and time-consuming.
- Imitation learning (IL) for embodied AI has been limited by the scarcity of large-scale, high-quality human demonstration datasets.
- Previous demonstration datasets were often small and based on shortest-path trajectories, lacking the rich exploratory and strategic behaviors crucial for search tasks.

## Method

- Built **Habitat-Web**, a ==web-based teleoperation system== integrating the photorealistic Habitat simulator with Amazon Mechanical Turk for scalable human demonstration collection.
- Generated large-scale human demonstration datasets, totaling over 92,000 trajectories, for ==ObjectGoal Navigation== and ==PICK&PLACE== tasks.
- Applied ==behavior cloning== to train ==imitation learning== agents on these human demonstrations, comparing their performance against state-of-the-art ==reinforcement learning== baselines.

## Results

- The Habitat-Web infrastructure enabled the collection of over **92,000** human demonstrations (**29.3 million** actions), constituting an order of magnitude more data than prior datasets for embodied tasks.
- Imitation learning agents achieved **35.4%** success/**10.2%** SPL on ObjectGoal Navigation (surpassing SOTA RL agents) and ~**18%** success on the complex PICK&PLACE task in new object-receptacle locations, where the RL baseline achieved zero success.
- Performance curves for imitation learning showed continuous improvement with increased human demonstration data, suggesting further potential gains from larger datasets.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Large-scale human demonstrations allow imitation learning agents to implicitly acquire sophisticated exploration and object-search strategies, such as peeking into rooms and panoramic turns, which are difficult to engineer with explicit rewards.
> - Imitation learning demonstrated higher sample efficiency than reinforcement learning, requiring approximately 7x fewer unique steps of experience to achieve superior performance.
> - Oracle-generated shortest-path demonstrations are ineffective for training agents in tasks requiring exploration, as they lack the necessary strategic elements present in human behavior.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is situated at the forefront of embodied artificial intelligence, specifically addressing the critical challenges of visual exploration and object interaction for autonomous agents operating in complex 3D environments. It tackles the fundamental problem of how to enable general-purpose robots to perform diverse tasks like "finding keys" or "rearranging furniture" in unseen, realistic settings.

Historically, embodied AI has made significant strides using **Reinforcement Learning (RL)**, with agents learning through trial-and-error guided by carefully engineered reward functions. Tasks like ObjectGoal Navigation (OBJECTNAV) and object rearrangement have seen commendable success with RL. However, the paper highlights a major limitation of RL: the immense difficulty and scalability issues associated with **reward engineering**. Designing the "right" reward function to induce sophisticated, human-like behaviors (e.g., peeking into rooms efficiently, checking corners for small objects, panoramic views) is often unintuitive, expensive, and not easily transferable to new tasks or behaviors. For highly complex, multi-stage tasks like PICK&PLACE or those involving natural language instructions, RL from scratch can struggle to even initiate learning.

This paper champions **Imitation Learning (IL)** as a compelling alternative. IL involves learning policies directly from demonstrations provided by expert (often human) agents, implicitly capturing desired behaviors without explicit reward engineering. While IL has theoretical advantages, its practical application in embodied AI has been severely hampered by a **lack of large-scale, high-quality human demonstration datasets**. Previous datasets for embodied tasks were typically small (e00–10k episodes) and often relied on shortest-path trajectories from privileged planners rather than genuine human behavior. Such shortest-path demonstrations, while useful for navigation benchmarks, inherently lack the exploratory and strategic elements crucial for search-based tasks.

"Habitat-Web" directly addresses this data bottleneck by introducing a scalable web-based infrastructure for collecting human demonstrations and then using this unprecedented volume of data to conduct a large-scale comparison between IL and RL. By building upon the widely used **Habitat simulator ecosystem**, the work also contributes to a common platform, ensuring its relevance and accessibility to the broader research community. The paper therefore represents a significant step towards enabling more capable and generalizable embodied agents by leveraging the implicit intelligence embedded in human behavior, thus shifting the focus from explicit reward design to scalable data collection and imitation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations behind the Habitat-Web research are multifaceted, aiming to address critical limitations in the current embodied AI landscape:

- **Develop a Scalable Data Collection Infrastructure for Embodied Tasks:** The foremost objective was to create a robust and user-friendly virtual teleoperation system. This system, dubbed "Habitat-Web," connects the photorealistic Habitat simulator to Amazon Mechanical Turk (AMT), enabling remote humans to teleoperate virtual robots. The goal was to overcome the existing bottleneck of small, insufficient human demonstration datasets for embodied tasks.
- **Generate Large-Scale Human Demonstration Datasets:** Utilizing the newly developed infrastructure, a key objective was to collect an unprecedented volume of human demonstrations for two challenging embodied search and interaction tasks: ObjectGoal Navigation (OBJECTNAV) and PICK&PLACE. This scale of data was intended to facilitate a comprehensive study of imitation learning that was previously impossible.
- **Conduct a Direct, Large-Scale Comparison of Imitation Learning (IL) and Reinforcement Learning (RL):** A central objective was to rigorously compare the performance of IL (trained on the newly collected human demonstrations) against state-of-the-art RL methods (the current status quo) on these complex embodied tasks. This comparison sought to establish the relative strengths and weaknesses of each paradigm when IL is no longer data-starved.
- **Characterize and Quantify Learned Behaviors:** Beyond mere performance metrics, the researchers aimed to understand *what* behaviors IL agents learned from human demonstrations. This involved qualitatively and quantitatively analyzing if IL could implicitly acquire sophisticated, human-like exploration and object-search strategies that are difficult to induce through explicit reward engineering in RL.
- **Investigate Scaling Properties of Imitation Learning:** The study aimed to assess how IL performance scales with increasing amounts of human demonstration data, providing insights into the potential for future advancements by simply collecting more demonstrations.

The motivations driving these objectives stem from several critical observations:

- **Limitations of Reward Engineering in RL:** As discussed, engineering effective reward functions for complex, general-purpose embodied tasks is extremely challenging, time-consuming, and often leads to suboptimal or unnatural agent behaviors.
- **Implicit Wisdom in Human Demonstrations:** Humans inherently possess sophisticated strategies for exploration, navigation, and interaction. The hypothesis is that these implicit strategies, when captured through demonstrations, could serve as a powerful training signal for agents, bypassing the need for explicit reward design.
- **Data Scarcity for IL:** The primary barrier to leveraging IL's potential in embodied AI has been the lack of sufficiently large datasets. Prior work relied on small datasets or oracle-generated shortest paths, which are inadequate for tasks requiring exploration.
- **Need for Robust General-Purpose Agents:** The ultimate goal of embodied AI is to create agents capable of performing a diverse set of tasks in a wide range of environments. This requires agents that can learn intelligent, transferable behaviors efficiently.

By achieving these objectives, the research seeks to provide compelling evidence for investing in large-scale imitation learning as a viable and potentially superior path towards building more intelligent and capable embodied AI agents.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed a systematic approach involving the development of a novel data collection infrastructure, the generation of large-scale datasets, and a comparative study of imitation learning and reinforcement learning paradigms.

#### 3.1. Habitat-WebGL Data Collection Infrastructure

The cornerstone of this work is "Habitat-Web," a virtual teleoperation system designed for large-scale human demonstration collection.
- **Web-based Interface:** The Habitat simulator, specifically Habitat-Sim, was integrated into a web browser using WebGL. This allowed remote users (Amazon Mechanical Turk workers) to control a virtual robot directly from their browser.
- **User Interaction:** AMT users were presented with the robot's first-person RGB view and provided keyboard controls for navigation (MOVE_FORWARD, TURN_LEFT/RIGHT, LOOK_UP/DOWN, STOP) and interaction (GRAB_RELEASE). For PICK&PLACE, 3D bounding boxes highlighted interactive objects, and an "inventory" area displayed grabbed objects, enhancing user experience.
- **Backend System:** PsiTurk was used to manage tasks on AMT, while an NGINX reverse proxy handled web requests, and a MySQL database stored the collected demonstration data (keyboard actions, agent states, object states).
- **Simulation Environment:** All data was collected within photorealistic Matterport3D scenes, utilizing the rigid body dynamics of Habitat 2.0 for realistic object interaction.
- **Data Quality Assurance:** Task-specific validation checks (e.g., stopping within 1m of the goal object for OBJECTNAV, placing an object within 0.7m of a receptacle at the correct height for PICK&PLACE) were implemented to ensure the quality and success of human-submitted demonstrations.

#### 3.2. Tasks and Datasets

Two embodied tasks requiring significant visual search and interaction were selected:
- **ObjectGoal Navigation (OBJECTNAV):** Agents must navigate to an instance of a specified object category (e.g., 'chair') in an unseen environment, relying on RGBD and GPS+Compass sensors.
 - **Human Demonstrations (OBJECTNAV-HD):** 80,000 demonstrations (70k in Matterport3D, 10k in Gibson) were collected, totaling ~19.5 million steps. Humans had only RGB input and no GPS+Compass, implicitly inferring depth and odometry.
 - **Shortest Path Demonstrations:** 114,165 oracle-generated shortest paths were created for comparison, explicitly designed to lack exploration, to match the total number of steps (~8.4M) from a subset of human demonstrations (35k).
- **PICK&PLACE:** Agents must find, pick up, and then place a specified object on a specified receptacle (e.g., ‘Place mug on counter’) in a new environment, using RGBD and GPS+Compass.
 - **Human Demonstrations (PICK&PLACE-HD):** 11,955 demonstrations were collected across 9 Matterport3D scenes, totaling ~11.5 million steps. Object and receptacle locations were randomized, and instructions were given in natural language.
 - **Shortest Path Demonstrations:** 25,747 oracle-generated shortest paths (~8.8M steps) were generated for comparison, using a geodesic path follower and heuristic action planner for picking/placing.

#### 3.3. Imitation Learning (IL) Approach

- **Behavior Cloning:** The core IL method used was behavior cloning, where a neural policy $\pi_\theta(a_t | o_t)$ is trained to directly predict human actions given observations, minimizing the negative log-likelihood of expert actions.
- **Inflection Weighting:** To improve learning efficiency for tasks with long sequences of identical actions (e.g., long stretches of `MOVE_FORWARD`), an inflection weighting scheme was employed, upweighting timesteps where actions change.
- **Policy Architecture:** A standard CNN+RNN architecture was used:
 - **Sensory Input:** ResNet18 for RGB (randomly initialized), ResNet50 pre-trained on PointGoal navigation for depth. For OBJECTNAV, additional semantic features (Semantic Segmentation via a pre-trained RedNet, and "Semantic Goal Exists" scalar) and a 32-d object goal category embedding were included. For PICK&PLACE, language instructions were encoded using a single-layer LSTM.
 - **Recurrent Processing:** All embedded sensory and task-specific features were concatenated and fed into a 2-layer, 512-d GRU to predict a distribution over discrete actions.
- **Training:** Policies were trained for approximately 400M steps for OBJECTNAV (~21 epochs on 70k demos) and 90M steps for PICK&PLACE (~10 epochs on 9.5k demos), with checkpoints evaluated periodically for selection based on validation success.

#### 3.4. Reinforcement Learning (RL) Baselines

- **OBJECTNAV:** The IL agent's performance was directly compared against several state-of-the-art RL approaches from prior work and Habitat Challenges (e.g., ExploreTillSeen, Red Rabbit, EmbCLIP), which relied on various reward structures and auxiliary tasks.
- **PICK&PLACE:** An RL baseline was implemented using a combination of sparse success, slack, exploration (visitation-based), object-seen, grab/release success, and drop penalty rewards.

#### 3.5. Evaluation Metrics and Characterization

- **Standard Metrics:** Success rate and Success Weighted by Path Length (SPL) were used to measure task completion and efficiency.
- **Exploration Metrics:** Occupancy Coverage (OC) and Sight Coverage (SC) quantified the extent of environmental exploration.
- **Behavioral Metrics:** To characterize learned strategies, specific behaviors were manually categorized and quantified for IL, RL, and human agents, including Goal Room Time Spent (GRTS), Peeks (checking rooms and returning), Panoramic Turns (turning in place), Beeline (direct path to goal once seen), and Exhaustive Search (high sight coverage).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study yielded several significant findings, particularly highlighting the power of large-scale human demonstrations for imitation learning in embodied AI.

- **Scalability of Human Demonstration Collection:**
 - The Habitat-Web infrastructure successfully enabled the collection of 92,000+ human demonstrations (80k for OBJECTNAV, 12k for PICK&PLACE), a dataset size "an order of magnitude larger" than prior human demonstration datasets in simulation or on real robots. This equates to 29.3 million actions or 22,600 hours of real-world teleoperation time.
 - This data showed rich and diverse strategies employed by humans, confirming the value of human input. Human performance on OBJECTNAV validation split was 93.7% success and 42.5% SPL, indicating high doability but also significant exploration (low SPL).

- **Imitation Learning (IL) vs. Reinforcement Learning (RL) on OBJECTNAV:**
 - **IL Outperforms RL:** IL, using 70k human demonstrations, achieved 35.4% success and 10.2% SPL, outperforming state-of-the-art RL agents on the MP3D VAL split (e.g., Red Rabbit achieved 34.6% success and 7.9% SPL; ExploreTillSeen + THDA achieved 28.4% success and 11.0% SPL).
 - **Sample Efficiency:** While wall-clock training times were comparable, IL required ~7x fewer *unique steps of experience* to outperform RL. This effectively established an "exchange rate" where one human demonstration appeared to be worth ~4 agent-gathered RL trajectories.
 - **Learned Behaviors:** IL agents learned sophisticated object-search strategies from humans that RL agents did not prominently exhibit. These included "peeking into rooms, checking corners for small objects, [and] turning in place to get a panoramic view." These behaviors are implicitly captured in human data, whereas inducing them in RL would necessitate tedious reward engineering.

- **Imitation Learning (IL) vs. Reinforcement Learning (RL) on PICK&PLACE:**
 - **Starker Comparison:** On the more complex, multi-stage PICK&PLACE task, IL agents achieved ~18% success on episodes with new object-receptacle locations when trained with 9.5k human demonstrations. In contrast, the implemented RL baseline "failed to get beyond 0% success," demonstrating the profound difficulty of reward engineering for such interactive tasks and IL's significant advantage.

- **Limitations of Shortest Path Demonstrations:**
 - Imitating oracle-generated shortest paths proved largely ineffective, yielding only 4.4% success and 2.2% SPL on OBJECTNAV, and similarly low performance on PICK&PLACE. This underscores that for tasks requiring exploration and search, shortest paths (which contain no exploration) are inimitable and unsuitable as demonstrations.

- **Scaling Behavior of IL:**
 - Performance vs. training data size plots (for both OBJECTNAV and PICK&PLACE) showed a consistent improvement in success rates as the number of human demonstrations increased. Critically, the performance curves "have not yet saturated," suggesting that collecting even more human data is likely to further advance the state-of-the-art.

- **Sensor Ablations and Generalization:**
 - For OBJECTNAV, semantic inputs (semantic segmentation, "Semantic Goal Exists") were found to be crucial for performance, with significant drops observed when these were removed.
 - IL agents showed reasonable zero-shot generalization capabilities, with a policy trained on MP3D demonstrations achieving 49.5% success and 16.4% SPL on the Gibson VAL split, performing competitively with or better than some RL baselines trained directly on Gibson.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Habitat-Web" paper makes several significant contributions and has substantial potential impact on the field of embodied AI:

- **Validation of Large-Scale Imitation Learning:** The most profound impact is providing compelling empirical evidence that large-scale imitation learning (IL) from human demonstrations can not only match but often surpass the performance of state-of-the-art reinforcement learning (RL) in complex embodied tasks. This challenges the prevailing reliance on complex reward engineering in RL and strongly advocates for a shift in research investment towards scalable human data collection.
- **Novel Data Collection Infrastructure:** The development of "Habitat-Web" itself is a major contribution. This web-based teleoperation system for the Habitat simulator offers a safe, scalable, and extensible platform for the embodied AI community to collect human demonstrations for a wide range of tasks, including future language-based or more complex interaction tasks. This infrastructure addresses a critical bottleneck that has limited IL research.
- **Creation of Benchmark Datasets:** The release of the 92,000+ human demonstration dataset for OBJECTNAV and PICK&PLACE provides an invaluable resource for the community. These datasets, being orders of magnitude larger than previous offerings, enable researchers to train more capable agents, study the scaling properties of IL, and develop new architectural and algorithmic approaches.
- **Insights into Human Exploration Strategies:** The qualitative and quantitative analysis of learned behaviors reveals that IL agents effectively acquire sophisticated human-like exploration strategies (peeking, panoramic turns, exhaustive search) that are crucial for object search but are difficult to encode explicitly in RL reward functions. This highlights the implicit intelligence embedded in human demonstrations.
- **Pathway to More General-Purpose Agents:** By demonstrating IL's effectiveness for both navigation and interaction tasks (PICK&PLACE), the work suggests a promising pathway toward training general-purpose embodied agents capable of a diverse set of tasks without requiring task-specific reward engineering for each new skill. The observed scaling behavior further reinforces this potential.
- **Advancing Sim-to-Real Transfer:** By training agents in photorealistic simulators like Habitat, the learned policies are more likely to exhibit useful behaviors in real-world robotic deployments, bridging the gap between simulation and reality. If IL can train more robust and human-like exploration strategies in simulation, this could directly translate to more capable real-world robots.
- **Informing Future Research Directions:** The findings encourage the community to explore new research avenues in IL, such as more advanced behavior cloning techniques, inverse reinforcement learning from large human datasets, and methods for active learning or curriculum learning with human supervision. The "exchange rate" between human and agent-gathered data also offers a valuable heuristic for future research design.

In conclusion, "Habitat-Web" represents a landmark study that underscores the transformative potential of large-scale human demonstrations for imitation learning in embodied AI, offering a powerful alternative to the traditional RL paradigm and paving the way for the development of more intelligent and versatile autonomous agents.
