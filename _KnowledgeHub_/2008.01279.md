# Reinforced Grounded Action Transformation for Sim-to-Real Transfer

> [!summary] Summary
> Reinforced Grounded Action Transformation (RGAT) is a sim-to-real grounding method that replaces GAT's composed forward/inverse dynamics models with a single action-transformer policy trained end-to-end by reinforcement learning, rewarded by negative squared error against a learned real-world forward model. It outputs an action delta rather than an absolute action, yielding a lower-variance grounded simulator and successful transfer of deep-network policies across three MuJoCo domains.

## Problem

- Simulators are imperfect models, so policies learned in them are suboptimal or unstable in the real world and can exploit simulator inaccuracies.
- Grounded Action Transformation composes separately learned forward and inverse models, so errors in each compound and produce a noisy, imprecise action transformer.
- Prior grounding successes used policies with only about sixteen parameters; GAT fails to transfer policies represented by deep neural networks with orders of magnitude more parameters.

## Method

- ==Action transformer policy==: a single network trained end-to-end with ==reinforcement learning==, replacing GAT's composition of a real ==forward model== and a simulator ==inverse model==.
- ==Grounding reward==: negative squared L2 norm between the learned forward model's predicted next state and the grounded simulator's actual next state, using a separate discount factor.
- ==Delta-action parameterization==: the transformer outputs the change in action rather than the absolute action, normalizing the output space toward zero when the simulator is already accurate.

## Results

- On sim-to-self InvertedPendulum, the RGAT transformer's outputs cluster tightly on the identity line, while GAT's show a wide, biased distribution, confirming greater grounding precision.
- With a two-hidden-layer **64**-unit policy on InvertedPendulum, GAT fails to reach optimal return whereas RGAT nearly matches a policy trained directly on the target environment.
- On Hopper with a **27%** heavier torso, RGAT matches direct target-domain training within **three** grounding steps while GAT barely improves; HalfCheetah with **15%** heavier torso shows the same ordering, both using **50** real trajectories per step.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Composing two independently learned models multiplies their errors; training the composite mapping end-to-end against a single objective is often the more robust design.
> - Predicting residual corrections rather than absolute outputs normalizes the target range and makes learning easier when the base model is already approximately right.
> - Grounding-precision requirements scale with policy capacity: methods that transfer low-dimensional controllers can silently fail once deep networks exploit residual simulator error.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Introduction

Training robots to perform complex tasks in the real world remains one of the most challenging problems in artificial intelligence and robotics. While reinforcement learning (RL) has achieved remarkable successes in simulation environments, these learned behaviors often fail catastrophically when transferred to physical robots due to the "reality gap" - the mismatch between simulated and real-world dynamics.

The paper "Reinforced Grounded Action Transformation for Sim-to-Real Transfer" by Karnan et al. addresses a critical limitation in existing sim-to-real transfer methods. While previous approaches like Grounded Action Transformation (GAT) showed promise for simple policies, they struggle with the complex neural network policies that have become standard in modern deep reinforcement learning. This work introduces Reinforced Grounded Action Transformation (RGAT), which reformulates the action transformation process as a reinforcement learning problem, enabling successful transfer of sophisticated deep RL policies to real-world systems.

### 2. The Sim-to-Real Problem

The sim-to-real problem arises because simulators, despite their sophistication, are imperfect representations of real-world physics. Even minor discrepancies in dynamics, friction, or sensor noise can cause policies that perform perfectly in simulation to fail completely on physical robots. Direct training on real robots is often impractical due to time constraints, safety concerns, and equipment costs.

Existing approaches to bridge this gap fall into two categories:

**Robustness Methods** aim to create policies that are inherently robust to domain variations. These include:
- Domain randomization: Adding noise to simulation parameters during training
- Action Noise Envelope (ANE): Injecting noise into actions to improve robustness
- Adversarial training: Using adversarial agents to create challenging scenarios

**Grounding Methods** seek to improve the simulator's fidelity to match a specific real-world target domain. These approaches typically require some real-world data to calibrate the simulation. GAT falls into this category, treating the simulator as a black box and learning an "action transformer" that modifies actions before they enter the simulator.

### 3. From GAT to RGAT

Grounded Action Transformation (GAT) showed impressive results for simple policies, including enabling stable bipedal walking on a physical NAO robot. GAT works by learning two separate models from data:

1. A **forward model** ($f_{real}$) that predicts next states given state-action pairs using real-world data
2. An **inverse model** ($f_{sim}^{-1}$) that predicts actions given state transitions using simulation data

The action transformer is then formed by composing these models: $g(s_t, a_t) = f_{sim}^{-1}(s_t, f_{real}(s_t, a_t))$.

However, the authors identified a critical limitation: GAT performs poorly with complex neural network policies. They hypothesized that this failure stems from the composition of two separately learned models, which can compound errors and create a noisy action transformer - particularly problematic for high-dimensional policy representations.

RGAT addresses this by learning the action transformer end-to-end as a reinforcement learning agent, eliminating the need for model composition and reducing accumulated errors.

### 4. Methodology

RGAT follows the Grounded Simulation Learning (GSL) framework, which alternates between two steps:

1. **Grounding Step**: Fix the target policy and improve the simulator using real-world data
2. **Policy Improvement Step**: Fix the grounded simulator and train the policy using standard RL

The key innovation lies in how RGAT performs the grounding step:

#### 4.1. Action Transformer as RL Agent

Instead of composing two supervised learning models, RGAT treats the action transformer as a separate RL agent with policy $g_\varphi$. This agent:

- **Observes**: Current state $s_t$ and action $a_t$ from the target policy
- **Acts**: Outputs a transformed action $\hat{a}_t$ (or action change $\Delta a_t$)
- **Gets Rewarded**: Based on how well the grounded simulator matches real-world dynamics

#### 4.2. Reward Function

The action transformer's reward is designed to minimize the discrepancy between grounded simulation and real-world dynamics:

$$R_{AT}(\hat{s}_{t+1}, s_{t+1}) = -||f_\psi(s_t, a_t) - s_{t+1}||^2$$

where $f_\psi$ is a forward model trained on real-world data that predicts what the next state should be, and $s_{t+1}$ is the actual next state produced by the grounded simulator.

#### 4.3. End-to-End Training

By training the action transformer directly via RL, RGAT eliminates the error accumulation problem of GAT's model composition approach. The action transformer learns to make precise adjustments that align simulation behavior with real-world dynamics.

### 5. Experimental Results

The authors conducted extensive experiments using OpenAI Gym MuJoCo environments (InvertedPendulum, Hopper, HalfCheetah) with modified simulators serving as surrogate "real" environments.

#### 5.1. Action Transformer Precision

When tested in identical simulation and "real" environments (where the ideal action transformer should do nothing), RGAT learned transformations very close to the identity function, while GAT produced highly noisy transformations with significant variance.

#### 5.2. Policy Complexity Impact

The results clearly demonstrate RGAT's advantage with complex policies:

- **Shallow Networks**: Both GAT and RGAT performed similarly well with simple policies (4 neurons, 1 hidden layer)
- **Deep Networks**: With deeper networks (64 neurons, 2 hidden layers), GAT completely failed while RGAT achieved near-optimal performance

#### 5.3. Complex Environment Performance

In more complex environments like Hopper and HalfCheetah, RGAT consistently outperformed GAT, achieving performance levels comparable to policies trained directly on the "real" environment. GAT showed only marginal improvements over simulation-only training in these challenging domains.

### 6. Significance and Impact

RGAT represents a significant advancement in sim-to-real transfer for several reasons:

#### 6.1. Enabling Modern Deep RL

The most important contribution is making deep reinforcement learning compatible with grounded simulation approaches. Previous methods like GAT were limited to simple policies, but RGAT successfully handles the complex neural network policies that are standard in modern RL research.

#### 6.2. Improved Transfer Performance

By learning the action transformer end-to-end, RGAT achieves more precise grounding and significantly better transfer performance. The experimental results show that RGAT can match the performance of policies trained directly on real environments - the gold standard for sim-to-real methods.

#### 6.3. Data Efficiency

Like GAT, RGAT achieves successful transfer with limited real-world data (50 trajectories per grounding step), making it practical for real robot applications where data collection is expensive and time-consuming.

#### 6.4. Broad Applicability

RGAT maintains the "black-box" approach of GAT, meaning it can work with any simulator without requiring access to internal parameters or detailed knowledge of the simulation engine.

### 7. Limitations and Future Work

While RGAT shows significant promise, several limitations and future directions are important to consider:

#### 7.1. Real Robot Validation

The experiments used modified simulators as surrogate real environments. The critical next step is validation on physical robots, where additional challenges like sensor noise, hardware limitations, and unmodeled dynamics may arise.

#### 7.2. Computational Overhead

Training the action transformer as an RL agent adds computational complexity compared to GAT's supervised learning approach. The trade-off between improved performance and increased training time needs further analysis.

#### 7.3. Generalization Beyond Tested Domains

While the results on MuJoCo environments are promising, evaluation on a broader range of robotic tasks (manipulation, navigation, etc.) would strengthen the generalizability claims.

#### 7.4. Hyperparameter Sensitivity

The method requires careful tuning of hyperparameters for the action transformer's RL training, including KL divergence constraints and entropy coefficients. More robust hyperparameter selection strategies could improve practical adoption.

The work establishes a strong foundation for advancing sim-to-real transfer in the era of deep reinforcement learning, with clear pathways for addressing current limitations through future research and real-world validation.
