# Argus: A General-Purpose Agentic Runtime for Long-Horizon Reasoning

> [!summary] Summary
> This research introduces Argus, a general-purpose agentic runtime designed for long-horizon reasoning that enables agents to safely revise operational objectives during tasks. It uses evidence-backed, role-gated verification to manage goal evolution and demonstrates consistent performance across diverse benchmarks, including software engineering, hardware design, and scientific paper production.

## Problem

- Existing long-horizon AI agents often assume a fixed objective, which becomes problematic as real-world research and business tasks frequently require dynamic objective revision based on new evidence.
- Allowing agents to revise their objectives without proper safeguards can lead to "goal drift," where changes might merely rationalize failures rather than representing legitimate, evidence-backed pivots.
- Current memory and agent systems lack a robust mechanism for explicit, evidence-backed objective revision, role-gated admission, and persistent state management, hindering true iterative self-evolution.

## Method

- **Argus** formulates long-horizon research as ==verified pivoting== by introducing a formal report-level contract (user intent, operational objective, constraints, verification criteria) and an explicit `ManagerAdmit` operator for evidence-backed contract refinements.
- It implements a runtime that achieves ==fixed-model self-evolution==, where the persistent state (memories, skills, tools, verifiers, routing policy) evolves through verification-gated, role-owned updates without altering the underlying large language model parameters.
- The system employs a ==hierarchical architecture== with three system planes (Control, Execution, Record) and four model-driven roles (Manager, Planner, Engineer, Reviewer) to govern task lifecycle, execution, and state changes through explicit authority and verification gates.

## Results

- Argus achieved competitive or superior performance across seven diverse benchmarks, including ~**78%** accuracy on SWE-Bench Pro, top ranks in GPU kernel optimization (SOL-ExecBench), and faster than human bests in language model training speed (nanochat, nanoGPT speedrun).
- Longitudinal analysis on SWE-Bench Pro revealed that mature operations utilized **21%** fewer solve input tokens and **15%** less active workflow time per task compared to the startup phase, indicating efficiency gains from accumulated verified state.
- The system successfully completed **six** full-lifecycle scientific paper production campaigns and a detailed mathematical research campaign, demonstrating its capacity for iterative research, objective revision, and producing externally adopted artifacts like an optimized GPU kernel.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Safe and verifiable objective revision, rather than fixed goals, is crucial for long-horizon tasks, as demonstrated by Argus's ability to pivot research directions and retain falsified routes as valuable, verified exclusions.
> - Runtime self-evolution, achieved through accumulating and verifying persistent state (knowledge, skills, verification guidance) without modifying model weights, significantly improves efficiency over time, reducing redundant work.
> - A clear separation of concerns, enforced by distinct model-driven roles and persistent, verification-gated state management, is fundamental for building robust, transparent, and continuously improving autonomous agents.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper positions Argus within the evolving landscape of long-horizon reasoning, agentic runtimes, and autonomous research systems. Prior work in tool-using agents, such as ReAct (Yao et al., 2023), SWE-agent (Yang et al., 2024), and OpenHands (Wang et al., 2024a), established interfaces between language models and external environments for executing tasks. These systems provide foundational execution substrates for extended tasks, but the paper notes that their implicit assumption of a fixed objective becomes problematic as task horizons grow, leading to declining agent success.

A related line of research has focused on full-lifecycle autonomous research systems. Examples include The AI Scientist (Lu et al., 2024), CycleResearcher (Weng et al., 2025), AutoSci (Qian et al., 2026), FARS (Tang et al., 2026), and Arbor (Jin et al., 2026). These systems extend model interaction into iterative research programs, incorporating planning, experimentation, review, and memory. While they differ in search, memory, and review mechanisms, the paper states they largely share the assumption of a given, unchanging objective.

The authors identify a gap in these existing approaches: the challenge of safely revising the operational objective itself during a long-horizon campaign without degenerating into "goal drift." Current systems, by fixing the objective, fail to account for the iterative nature of real-world research where objectives, constraints, and verification criteria often become more precise through discovery rather than being static. Existing memory systems (e.g., Generative Agents, Reflexion, Voyager, MemGPT) address continuity across sessions but do not explicitly manage evidence-backed objective revision with role-gated admission and persistent state.

Argus aims to address this gap by taking an opposite position: "pivoting should be permitted and made safe by verification." This work seeks to provide a general-purpose agentic runtime that integrates verification into the core process of objective revision and runtime self-evolution. It aims to offer a persistent, self-evolving system that manages accumulated knowledge, skills, and rejected routes through explicit state ownership and verification gates, beneath any specific scientific workflow. This differentiates it from systems that optimize execution towards a fixed goal or primarily focus on generating ideas or managing memory without the same emphasis on verifiable, evidence-backed objective modification. The framework also considers the value of retaining full process data (not just final artifacts) for future learning and decision-making.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for Argus arises from the observation that long-horizon research is not a linear progression along a fixed plan. In practical research, the initial user intent may be stable, but the operational objective, constraints, and verification criteria frequently evolve as new evidence emerges. For instance, a software request might be underspecified until an implementation reveals missing details, or a mathematical campaign might yield intermediate bounds or counterexamples that reshape the original theorem.

The authors contend that allowing such revision is difficult because it can be externally indistinguishable from failure; a system that abandons its stated target might have legitimately discovered a misspecification or merely rationalized its inability to achieve the original goal. This ambiguity, if unaddressed, can lead to "goal drift," where objectives degrade towards what the executor can easily accomplish. The conventional response in prior agentic work has been to fix the objective, thereby removing the choice and optimizing execution.

Argus takes an alternative approach, proposing that pivoting (revising the operational objective) should be permitted, but made safe through verification. This means that any material change must be:
1. **Evidence-backed**: Supported by data that the previous route or objective was unreachable or misspecified.
2. **Role-gated**: Admitted through an explicit authority boundary to prevent arbitrary changes.
3. **Recorded**: Documented so that later missions inherit both the change and its justification, including refuted routes.

Therefore, verification is not merely a post-execution quality filter but a fundamental mechanism to differentiate legitimate pivoting from mere failure. The paper identifies three properties lacking in long-running agents that prevent such certification: continuity (evidence for revision is lost), acceptance (executor declares work complete, creating an incentive to accept), and experience (only final artifact survives, losing information on refuted routes).

The key objectives of Argus are articulated through its design and stated contributions:

- **Formulate long-horizon research as verified pivoting (C1)**: Introduce a compact, report-level contract `K_t = (ι, o_t, c_t, v_t)` (standing user intent, current operational objective, known constraints, verification criteria) and explicit user decision state `X_t`. The `ManagerAdmit` operator formalizes the evidence, authority, and provenance requirements for contract refinement.
- **Implement a runtime for contract refinement and fixed-model self-evolution (C2)**: Develop a system where contract revisions occur through typed, logged, role-owned surfaces. This admission discipline extends to generated memories, skills, verifiers, routing decisions, and rejected routes, which become reusable only after evidence checks and authorized commitment. This signifies that self-evolution occurs in the persistent runtime state and control policy, not in the model weights.
- **Measure verification and non-termination (C3)**: Quantify where the runtime dedicates verification efforts and where it declines to complete tasks, rather than forcing success.
- **Demonstrate autonomous paper production (C4)**: Provide a case study of multiple paper campaigns, detailing the missions, revisions, and rollbacks involved in reaching submission completion, illustrating persistence and recovery.
- **Reconstruct a mathematical campaign (C5)**: Show a role-resolved trace of a mathematical campaign, making actions of Manager, Planner, Engineer, and Reviewer visible across missions, including retaining falsified routes and accepted theorem updates.
- **Establish a broad capability floor (C6)**: Demonstrate that the verification machinery does not compromise end-task performance across diverse benchmark arenas, including software repair, GPU optimization, and research tasks.
- **Report external adoption of an artifact (C7)**: Document instances where Argus-produced artifacts (e.g., an optimized kernel) are adopted by external maintainers, indicating practical utility.

In summary, the primary motivation is to enable long-horizon AI agents to adapt their objectives safely and verifiably in dynamic research environments, accumulating validated knowledge and approaches without relying on silent goal changes or unbounded model sessions.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Argus is designed as a general-purpose agentic runtime operating around four model-driven roles and three system planes. The methodology integrates a structured problem formulation with an architectural design that emphasizes persistence, verification-gated state evolution, and explicit role authority.

**Problem Formulation (Section 3):**
The authors define a "dense-intelligence task" as one requiring high-frequency reasoning, tool use, verification, and iteration to produce a measurable result. These tasks exhibit:
- **Invention (T1):** The solution requires discovery through search and feedback, not direct retrieval.
- **Long horizon (T2):** Progress requires many dependent iterations over extended periods.
- **Verifiability (T3):** A task-native evaluator can distinguish genuine improvement from incorrect claims.

A campaign is modeled with an objective (`o`), initial artifact (`y0`), evaluation procedure (`f`), and resource budget (`B`). It is divided into bounded missions, each producing a trajectory (`τ_t`) of states, actions, artifact changes, measurements, and review outcomes. Mission termination is explicit, and continuity is managed by persistent campaign state, not unbounded model sessions.

The core of the problem formulation is the "report-level contract model" `K_t = (ι, o_t, c_t, v_t)`, which represents standing user intent (`ι`), current operational objective (`o_t`), known constraints (`c_t`), and verification criteria (`v_t`). User-visible clarifications are denoted `X_t`. Material refinements to this contract are governed by a normative `ManagerAdmit` operator, which ensures evidence, recorded verdicts, and required authority (`u_t`) for any proposed contract `K'_t` before updating `(X_t+1, K_t+1)`.

Efficiency is quantified by "dense-intelligence density" (`ρ_I(T)`), which considers the instantaneous rate of token-mediated reasoning and action (`˙N_tok(t)`) multiplied by reasoning (`η_r`), action (`η_a`), and verification (`η_v`) efficiency factors. These factors attribute how much activity directly contributes to progress versus coordination or repeated work.

The runtime self-evolution is defined by `H_t+1 = U(H_t, τ_t, E_t, K_t+1)` and `θ_t+1 = θ_t`, indicating that model parameters (`θ`) remain fixed, while the persistent runtime state (`H`) evolves based on mission outcomes (`τ_t`), admitted results (`E_t`), and updated contracts (`K_t+1`). This state (`H`) includes Memory, Skills, Tools, Verifiers, and Routing.

The theoretical framework also includes "process-data dominance" (`D_process ⊋ D_final`), stating that the complete process record contains more information than just the final artifact, potentially reducing future decision risk. "Review as selective error correction" is modeled using Bayes' rule, where review increases the precision of accepted state if sensitivity (`α`) exceeds the false-acceptance rate (`β`).

**System Architecture (Section 4):**
Argus employs a runtime with a hierarchical structure:
- **Three System Planes**:
 - **Control Plane**: Anchors the campaign (persistent identity, objective) and schedules missions.
 - **Execution Plane**: Performs bounded missions using real tools and artifacts.
 - **Record Plane**: Stores immutable event logs and artifacts, separate from completion decisions.
- **Four Model-Driven Roles (Table 1)**:
 - **Manager**: Governs the campaign's objective, lifetime, route, and stage transitions. Its authoritative output is campaign division and stage transition decisions.
 - **Planner**: Decomposes the current research state into bounded tasks and dependencies. Its output includes task specifications, plan status, and stage checklist updates.
 - **Engineer**: Modifies artifacts, invokes tools, and runs experiments for a single round. Produces execution results, artifacts, measurements, and indicates whether self-review is allowed or independent review is required.
 - **Reviewer**: Independently inspects artifacts and execution records when required or requested, issuing "done," "continue," or "blocked" verdicts, along with grounded next actions and retained-state edits. Allowed low-risk tasks may use Engineer self-review, but mandatory or requested review involves an independent Reviewer.

The system operates in a recurrent loop (`M → P → E ⇄ R → M`). Within a stage, the Reviewer can return work to the Engineer (`E ⇄ R`). Accepted or blocked verdicts return control to the Manager, who can hold, advance, or roll back stages (`g_n+1 ∈ {g_n, next(g_n)} ∪ prev(g_n)`).

**Bounded Missions over Durable State:** Campaigns are long-lived, while missions are bounded. Continuity across missions is maintained through a shared `CHECKPOINT.md` file, which stores durable state, evidence references, open questions, and the next step. This design facilitates restart and upgrade, as execution resumes from committed campaign state.

**Verification-Gated Fixed-Model Runtime Self-Evolution:** The core mechanism for continuous improvement involves evolving the persistent state (`H_t`) without altering the underlying model parameters (`θ_t`). This "self-evolution" is verification-gated, meaning updates to reusable components (Memory, Skills, Tools, Verifiers, Routing, Tasks) require evidence checks and commitment by an authorized owner (Table 2). For example, Engineer-produced candidate memories and skills are committed by the Reviewer. Rejected branches, if verified, are retained as exclusions, preventing repeated exploration of failed routes.

**Empirical Methodology (Section 5):**
The evaluation addresses four research questions (RQ1-RQ4) across diverse benchmarks and vertical studies:
- **Benchmark Suite**: Seven arenas (Table 3) are used: SWE-Bench Pro (software repair), SOL-ExecBench (GPU kernel optimization), nanochat B200/H100 (language model training efficiency), nanoGPT speedrun (training speed), AARRI-Bench (research-intern tasks), and Math-Reasoning Data Synthesis (mathematical problem generation). Each is evaluated using its native metric, avoiding a single normalized leaderboard. GPT-5.5 (or GPT-5.5/xhigh through Copilot for SWE-Bench Pro) serves as the backbone model.
- **SWE-Bench Pro Longitudinal Analysis**: Measures solve-time resource demand (tokens, active workflow time) across sequential "Waves" of tasks to observe how efficiency changes as runtime state accumulates. This is an observational analysis, not a controlled learning ablation.
- **SWE-Bench Pro Reviewer Analysis**: Quantifies Reviewer invocation rates, revision requests, and recovery rates (tasks passing the official verifier or completing the strict review loop after Reviewer feedback).
- **Representative Vertical-Trace Protocol**: A detailed case study of an Erd˝os–Gyárfás mathematical campaign traces role actions, objective revisions, retained state, and outcomes across multiple missions, demonstrating process-to-capability mechanisms.
- **Theory-to-Measurement Mapping**: Explicitly links theoretical concepts (e.g., information gain, review correction, reuse value, token conversion) to specific measurements in the experiments (Table 8, Appendix D).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of Argus is structured around its performance across diverse benchmarks and detailed analyses of its internal mechanisms in longitudinal and vertical case studies.

#### 4.1. Capability Floor Across Seven Arenas (RQ1)

Argus demonstrates competitive performance across a suite of heterogeneous tasks, as summarized in Table 4:
- **SWE-Bench Pro**: Achieved approximately 78% accuracy on 731 software-repair tasks, compared to 59% for Direct Copilot, while using 1.41 times the aggregate tokens.
- **SOL-ExecBench**: Attained a Global #6 rank (including 2x #1 and 7 top-3 placements) on B200 GPU kernel optimization, outperforming a reference "Recursive" agent in two head-to-head wins.
- **nanochat B200/H100**: Achieved 0.9636 BPB on B200 and 0.9855 BPB on H100, surpassing human bests of 0.9646 and 0.9879 BPB, respectively, in fixed-time language model training.
- **nanoGPT speedrun**: Reached a target validation loss in 79.77 seconds, faster than the human best of 80.18 seconds, for an eight-H100 training run.
- **AARRI-Bench**: Solved 63 out of 82 (76.8%) research-intern tasks, exceeding the reported paper best of 68.3%.
- **Math-Reasoning Data Synthesis**: Achieved a 28.0 gap in generating AIME-style reasoning problems, higher than Arbor (20.83) and other baselines.
- **External Adoption**: An Argus-optimized TileLang RWKV6 kernel was reviewed by an external collaborator and merged into the upstream Flash Linear Attention repository (fla-org:main), demonstrating practical utility and external validation.
- **MLE-Bench Lite (Partial Result)**: Nine Kaggle competitions were closed with medals (3 gold, 3 silver, 3 bronze), demonstrating capability in machine learning engineering tasks against external graders and leaderboards. This included a verification-gated route change in `nomad2018-predict-transparent-conductors` where a public-state-of-the-art method replaced an initially rejected approach, earning a bronze medal.

#### 4.2. Knowing When to Stop: Verification Routing and Non-Termination (RQ3)

The SWE-Bench Pro run provided insight into the runtime's verification mechanisms:
- **Reviewer Invocation**: Of 731 tasks, 466 (63.7%) invoked an independent Reviewer, while 265 (36.3%) used Engineer self-review.
- **Resource Consumption**: Reviewer-routed tasks consumed 2.75 times more solve input tokens and 1.80 times more active workflow time on average, indicating that the adaptive routing policy assigned independent review to a harder workload.
- **Revision and Recovery**: The Reviewer requested revisions for 43 tasks. Among these, 34 subsequently passed the official verifier, and 22 completed the "strict continue→revision→done" loop, demonstrating the Reviewer's role in error correction.
- **Non-Termination**: A further 35 tasks received a "blocked" verdict, signifying the runtime's refusal to declare unsupported completion for underspecified or intractable objectives.

#### 4.3. Cost of the Mechanism as State Accumulates (RQ2)

Longitudinal analysis of the Argus-only SWE-Bench Pro trajectory showed changes in resource demand as persistent runtime state accumulated:
- **Efficiency Improvement**: Mature operation (Waves W19–22) utilized 21% fewer solve input tokens and 15% less active workflow time per task compared to the startup phase (Waves W1–6). This pattern aligns with the hypothesis that accumulated and verified state (knowledge, skills, verification guidance, routing policy) reduces redundant inspection or failed execution.
- **Non-Monotonicity**: The efficiency trajectory was not strictly monotonic; for example, Waves W13–18 showed the lowest token usage but increased active time, and Waves W23–24 (late difficult tasks) exhibited a rebound in both metrics, indicating the influence of task composition and difficulty.

#### 4.4. Measured Process Quantities (RQ4)

The process theory was instantiated through two complementary traces:
- **Mathematical Campaign**: In the theorem-production window, direct reasoning, verification, and action each accounted for approximately 56% of their respective attributed units, indicating focused effort on frontier-changing work (Table 6).
- **SWE-Bench Pro**: Reviewer revision requests led to a 79.1% official-verifier recovery rate and a 51.2% strict review-loop rescue rate.
- **Research Portfolio**: A public research inventory documented 41 de-duplicated artifacts across six programs, demonstrating the runtime's breadth in executing diverse research.

#### 4.5. Objective Revision in a Mathematical Campaign

A detailed trace of an Erd˝os–Gyárfás mathematical campaign (Figure 5, Table 12) illustrates role-resolved objective revision:
- **Falsified Route**: The campaign retained one falsified route (a 24-vertex witness disproving a proposed reduction) as verified exclusion, preventing further exploration of a dead end.
- **Frontier Updates**: Six proof-backed theorem-frontier updates were accepted, including a stricter bound (27 to 25) and two bridge lemmas.
- **Role Dynamics**: The Manager preserved the objective, the Planner proposed specific theorem tasks, the Engineer performed proof writing and checks, and the Reviewer rejected an overstated route and certified bounded theorems. This demonstrated how role separation directed the scientific trajectory, preserving failure as information and controlling state through review.

#### 4.6. Objective Revision at Campaign Scale

Six paper-production campaigns across various domains (evaluation reliability, vision-language matching, test-time adaptation, GUI agents, multimodal hallucination, model quantization) demonstrated end-to-end lifecycle capabilities (Figure 6, Figure 7):
- **Completion Rate**: All 6 canonical pipelines reached submission completion.
- **Process Load**: These campaigns spanned 640 aggregate campaign-hours, 254 bounded missions, 576 Engineer rounds, 286 Reviewer revision verdicts, 89 session rolls, and 16 Manager rollbacks, illustrating an iterative, non-linear process.
- **Scientific Pivoting**: Several projects produced diagnostic or negative results after initial positive hypotheses failed. For example, the multimodal-hallucination campaign pivoted from a positive method claim to a 4,500-row negative-results audit after early methods were rejected by Reviewer gates. Two late rollbacks repaired submission defects without resetting the research state.
- **Consistency Failure**: One project (compositional-matching) revealed a discrepancy where its final PDF completed, but a stored assurance object remained "BLOCKED," highlighting a potential synchronization issue between derived metadata and authoritative pipeline state.

#### 4.7. A Chip the Runtime Designed: ACE-2

Argus specified, wrote the RTL, built verification environments, and drove the ACE-2 inference accelerator through mapped synthesis and static timing:
- **Functional Closure**: Certified end-to-end execution of a 24-layer Qwen2.5-0.5B W4A8 command integration, with exact reference matching for Layer 0.
- **Physical Closure**: Passed canonical SKY130 HD mapped synthesis and OpenSTA at TT 25 °C / 1.80 V, meeting area and clock period targets without relaxation.
- **Scoped Certification**: The certificate explicitly enumerated its exclusions (e.g., no routed timing, power signoff, GDS, silicon validation), demonstrating the gate's ability to define the precise perimeter of validated claims.

#### 4.8. Simplifying a Published Method: Materials Generation

In MOF generation, Argus targeted MOFFlow-2, a published autoregressive language model:
- **Method Simplification**: The runtime found that the published Feynman–Kac steering method for MOF generation discarded most of its exploration. By keeping K trajectories independent ("best-of-K" selection), Argus demonstrated higher external MOFChecker pass rates at matched compute (Table 7), effectively replacing a more complex steering mechanism with a simpler, more effective one.
- **Confound Removal**: This simplification was admissible only after the runtime identified and removed a confound in the original comparison, which had mixed an integrator change with a scoring change. Isolating these factors showed the scoring change was significant, but the integrator alone was not.
- **Scoped Claim**: The campaign withheld a state-of-the-art claim, enumerating outstanding items (e.g., dataset limitations, unjoined systems, lack of DFT checks), consistent with the principled stopping behavior.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Argus research contributes to the field of AI agents and autonomous research in several significant ways:

**General-Purpose Agentic Runtime:** Argus demonstrates a unified runtime capable of addressing a diverse range of complex, long-horizon tasks across software engineering, hardware design, mathematical research, materials science, and scientific publication. This breadth suggests a foundational infrastructure for developing more capable and versatile AI agents, moving beyond specialized systems.

**Verified Pivoting and Goal Drift Mitigation:** The core contribution of Argus lies in its mechanism for verification-guided persistence and pivoting. By formally separating stable user intent from evolving operational objectives, and by mandating evidence-backed, role-gated, and recorded revisions, Argus offers a principled approach to manage goal drift. This directly addresses a fundamental challenge in long-horizon tasks, where objectives are often ill-defined or evolve as knowledge accumulates. The observed non-termination for blocked tasks and the explicit enumeration of exclusions in the ACE-2 chip design underscore the system's ability to report limitations transparently, fostering trustworthiness.

**Fixed-Model Runtime Self-Evolution:** The approach of evolving persistent runtime state (memories, skills, verifiers, routing) without modifying the underlying large language model parameters provides a practical path for sustained improvement. This mechanism allows the system to accumulate verified experience, avoid repeated failures, and refine its operational policies over time. The observed reduction in token usage and active time in mature SWE-Bench Pro operations suggests that this accumulated state can lead to increased efficiency.

**Value of Process Data for Future Learning:** Argus explicitly retains rich process data, including states, actions, measurements, review feedback, and runtime-state changes, which goes beyond merely storing final artifacts. This structured data stream serves as a valuable corpus for future supervised, preference-based, and reinforcement learning methods, enabling subsequent models to internalize complex planning and verification patterns observed in successful (and unsuccessful) research trajectories.

**Enhanced Reliability and Robustness:** The explicit role separation (Manager, Planner, Engineer, Reviewer) and the verification-gated admission processes enhance the reliability and robustness of the system. The Reviewer's role in requesting revisions and facilitating recovery, as demonstrated in the SWE-Bench Pro tasks, indicates that the system can self-correct and improve initially flawed solutions. The ability to declare tasks "blocked" rather than forcing an unsupported completion also contributes to transparent and reliable reporting of progress.

**Advancement in Autonomous Scientific Discovery:** The success of Argus in executing full-lifecycle paper production campaigns and a detailed mathematical research campaign highlights its potential to automate significant portions of the scientific discovery process. The demonstrated ability to pivot research hypotheses (e.g., from a positive method to a diagnostic negative-results study) and repair evidence packages without resetting the entire research state represents progress towards more sophisticated and resilient autonomous research agents. The materials generation vertical, showing simplification of a published method after confound removal, further illustrates the system's capacity for critical re-evaluation of existing scientific claims.

In conclusion, Argus provides a robust, verifiable framework for long-horizon agentic reasoning, emphasizing adaptability and cumulative knowledge generation. Its mechanisms for managing objective evolution and accumulating verified state, while maintaining fixed underlying model parameters, offer a pathway for building more capable and transparent AI systems for complex, iterative tasks, particularly in scientific research.
