# Intelligence Across Embodiments

> [!summary] Summary
> A position paper arguing that general embodied intelligence requires learning that accumulates across differing robot bodies. It contends engineered correspondences, standardized grippers, end-effector action spaces, contact interfaces, humanoid retargeting, give short-term gains but cap long-run transfer, and instead proposes embodiment diversity as a primary scaling axis paired with broad learned priors, plus evaluation protocols that measure embodiment gaps explicitly.

## Problem

- Engineered correspondences assume a prescribed interface suffices, breaking when tasks need whole-body coordination or specific dynamic responses the interface cannot express.
- Contact-based and retargeting approaches presume the target body can reproduce relevant contacts within its geometric, kinematic and dynamic limits, which often fails.
- Calling a target robot unseen says little about generalization difficulty, since studies rarely specify training-population gaps, control interface, or test-time embodiment information.

## Method

- Proposes ==embodiment diversity== as a primary scaling axis alongside tasks and environments, with broad ==learned priors== as the complementary ingredient replacing human-designed correspondence interfaces.
- Calls for simulation with ==procedurally generated, mechanically plausible embodiments== varying sensing, morphology, dynamics, actuation and control, to synthesize transfer experience at scale.
- Advocates architectures fed explicit robot descriptions or inferring embodiment properties from interaction history via ==in-context learning==, since visual identification confuses dynamically different robots.

## Results

- Notes existing cross-embodiment datasets hold on the order of **10^2** embodiments, while systematic procedural variation could expand that count by **orders of magnitude**.
- Cites locomotion evidence that increasing embodiment diversity produced broader generalization than adding more data on a fixed set of embodiments.
- Cites findings that human demonstrations yield larger gains after broad robot pretraining, and that scaling human-video pretraining improves robot-specific fine-tuned performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The Bitter Lesson likely extends to embodiment: learned representations scaling with data and compute should eventually outperform hand-designed cross-embodiment interfaces.
> - Motion-level transfer, preserving task-space motion, and strategy-level transfer, finding a different feasible approach, are distinct capabilities needing separate evaluation.
> - Treat intelligence as the persistent entity across changing bodies, enabling co-design, lifetime self-reconfiguration, and reuse of experience across hardware generations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

Suppose a robot has learned a task on one body and must now work with a different or changed body. The change might be a new tool, an altered joint range, or a shifted calibration. Robots do not share one common "sensorimotor vocabulary," so the same command or interaction may be unavailable or ineffective on another body.

This position paper argues that a robot's body is more than its shape. Its sensors, geometry, kinematics, dynamics, actuators, and low-level control determine what it can perceive, do, and learn from. These properties differ across robots and change over time. The authors therefore argue that general embodied intelligence should accumulate experience across embodiments rather than assume one fixed sensorimotor interface.

Robot data is scarce. Bodies also change through tool use, payloads, calibration, wear, and redesign. Reusing experience across embodiments could reduce the need to start over when the robot or its physical interface changes. The paper offers a research agenda with no new experiments. Early evidence from locomotion suggests that diversity can matter more than repeating data on a small set of bodies.

### 2. From shared interfaces to learned relations

The prevailing strategy is to engineer correspondences between bodies. Examples include standardizing grippers, using leader arms that reproduce target kinematics, retargeting human motions to humanoids, and sharing image, contact, or end-effector representations. These interfaces can make transfer practical now. They also encode assumptions about which motions, contacts, or human-like structures are suitable. Those assumptions can fail when a task requires whole-body coordination or a different strategy.

The authors propose a different approach: learn representations that relate goals, physical effects, and robot capabilities. Such abstractions might capture task semantics, spatial relations, physical consequences, and control. The authors describe these as possible abstractions, not a required hierarchy. The goal is for accumulated experience to broaden transfer and reduce adaptation costs. The paper does not demonstrate that this goal has been achieved.

### 3. What embodiment diversity adds

The authors describe embodiment diversity as a plausible scaling axis alongside tasks and environments. They treat broad learned priors as a complementary ingredient, since broader pretraining can make later data more useful.

The most decisive cited comparison comes from a locomotion study by [Ai et al., *Towards Embodiment Scaling Laws in Robot Locomotion* (CoRL 2025)](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v305/ai25a.html). Its metric was cumulative episode reward averaged on held-out, unseen embodiments. For generalization to these unseen robots, performance improved as the number of training embodiments increased. Adding more trajectories on a fixed small subset quickly saturated. In the data-only condition, training used 5% of the available training embodiments. Increasing data to four times the initial amount brought performance close to its peak, and gains beyond that were negligible.

The reviewed paper summarizes this comparison only qualitatively, as "broader generalization." The study's scaling setup used simulated, procedurally generated embodiments and a fixed held-out test set. It supports the case for diversity in that locomotion setup. It does not establish that diversity is the sole cause of better transfer, and it does not show that the result extends to manipulation and other tasks. The authors call for systematic tests across tasks and environments.

### 4. Making transfer testable

According to the authors, calling a robot "unseen" is not enough. Evaluations should report how the target differs from the training bodies, what interface and embodiment information is available, and whether test-time interaction makes the setting zero-shot or few-shot. They also distinguish two kinds of transfer. **Motion-level transfer** preserves a motion or contact pattern while adapting joint execution. **Strategy-level transfer** finds another way to reach the same goal.

### 5. Bodies that build on experience

If learned capabilities can carry across candidate bodies, the authors envision that evaluating designs could become less of a from-scratch bottleneck. Policies and embodiments might then improve together. The paper's contribution is a direction and an evaluation agenda. Its supporting evidence is an early result in controlled robot locomotion.
