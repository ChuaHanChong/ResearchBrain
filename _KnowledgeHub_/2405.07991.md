# SPIN: Simultaneous Perception, Interaction and Navigation

> [!summary] Summary
> The SPIN framework enables mobile manipulators to reactively navigate and interact in complex, dynamic environments by integrating perception, interaction, and navigation into a single end-to-end policy. It employs an active visual system and a two-phase training strategy to achieve higher success rates in real-world scenarios compared to classical methods, demonstrating emergent behaviors like dynamic obstacle avoidance and whole-body coordination.

## Problem

- Current mobile manipulation approaches struggle with performing diverse, long-horizon tasks in unstructured and dynamic real-world environments.
- Classical planning methods require precise a priori environment knowledge and computational tractability, failing to adapt to dynamic changes or uncertainties.
- Modular learning solutions for mobile manipulation often lead to compounding errors, decision-making delays, and uncoordinated whole-body actions, with fixed camera views limiting perception.

## Method

![[2405.07991-method.png]]
*The training pipeline transitions from a privileged, scandot-based teacher to a vision-based student policy.*

- Develops **SPIN**, an ==end-to-end reactive framework== that integrates perception, interaction, and navigation through a single policy.
- Employs a ==two-phase training strategy==: an initial ==reinforcement learning== phase using ==scandots== for efficient low-level control learning, followed by ==supervised distillation== into a depth-image-based policy.
- Incorporates an ==active visual system==, enabling the robot to dynamically control its ==camera gaze== to acquire relevant information for navigation and interaction.

## Results

- SPIN demonstrated higher success rates in real-world deployments (**0.8** in static, **0.6** in dynamic environments) compared to a classical planning baseline (**0.6** and **0.0-0.2** respectively).
- The framework produced emergent behaviors such as dynamic obstacle avoidance, adaptive rerouting, and whole-body coordination (e.g., lowering the arm for clearance) in unseen real-world conditions.
- Simulations confirmed the necessity of active vision, with SPIN achieving **90-96%** success in hard navigation tasks where fixed-camera baselines fell below **26%** success.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The tight coupling between perception and motion is essential for effective mobile manipulation, allowing the robot to actively "move to see and see to move."
> - Active vision with an actuated camera significantly outperforms fixed-camera systems, demonstrating its necessity for effective navigation in cluttered environments.
> - A two-phase training strategy, starting with reinforcement learning on simplified "scandot" representations and then distilling to depth images, provides a computationally tractable path for end-to-end visuomotor policy learning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Mobile manipulation, which involves robots performing tasks that combine locomotion and manipulation in complex environments, represents a significant and long-standing challenge in robotics. This field extends beyond the complexities of static manipulation or pure locomotion by requiring systems to execute diverse, long-horizon tasks within unstructured and potentially dynamic environments.

Prior approaches to mobile manipulation have typically followed two main paradigms. The first is classical planning-based control, which relies on *a priori* precise knowledge of obstacle locations and detailed environmental maps. This approach faces inherent limitations in real-world scenarios due to computational intractability for high-degree-of-freedom (DoF) systems, as well as its inability to react to dynamic changes or environmental uncertainties. The second paradigm involves learning-based methods, often utilizing disentangled modular skills for mobility and manipulation that are then trivially combined. This modularity can lead to compounding errors, delays in decision-making, and a lack of coordinated whole-body behavior. Within learning-based navigation, prior end-to-end approaches often use a fixed camera view, where what the robot perceives is solely determined by its position.

This research addresses these limitations by introducing SPIN, an end-to-end, reactive mobile manipulation framework. Unlike classical methods, SPIN does not require pre-built maps or precise *a priori* knowledge of the environment. Unlike modular learning approaches, it integrates perception, interaction, and navigation into a single policy, fostering whole-body coordination. A key distinction from previous end-to-end learning methods is its incorporation of an active visual system, allowing the robot to consciously control its camera to gather necessary information, thus coupling vision with movement in a dynamic feedback loop. This work seeks to bridge the gap between human-like reactive adaptation—where perception informs movement and movement in turn influences what can be perceived—and current robotic capabilities.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a mobile manipulator capable of simultaneously perceiving, interacting with, and navigating cluttered, unstructured, and dynamic environments. This overarching goal is motivated by the inherent challenges of real-world mobile manipulation, which necessitate a more integrated and reactive approach than traditional methods.

Specific motivations and objectives include:

- **Addressing the Tight Coupling of Perception and Motion:** The authors highlight that in mobile manipulation, what a robot knows about its environment (perception) directly influences its movement capabilities, and conversely, how it moves affects what it can perceive. The work aims to develop a system that explicitly exploits this interdependent relationship, enabling the robot to "move in order to see and to see in order to move."
- **Overcoming Limitations of Classical Planning:** Classical approaches, requiring perfect *a priori* maps and precise obstacle information, are often impractical due to environmental dynamism, computational expense for high-DoF systems, and lack of reactive improvisation. A key objective is to demonstrate that a reactive, data-driven approach can effectively manage these complexities without such stringent prerequisites.
- **Mitigating Shortcomings of Modular Learning:** Existing modular systems that separate perception, planning, and control often suffer from error accumulation and delayed decision-making. The research aims to develop an end-to-end policy that avoids these issues by integrating all components into a single decision-making process, facilitating agile whole-body coordination.
- **Enabling Active Vision:** Unlike systems with fixed camera viewpoints, the paper aims to equip the robot with an actuated camera that it actively controls to decide "when" and "what" to perceive. This conscious perception is crucial for navigating unseen, heavily cluttered environments effectively.
- **Achieving Whole-Body Coordination:** For mobile manipulators, avoiding obstacles involves not just the base, but also the arm. An objective is to learn policies that coordinate all actuated joints (base, arm, camera) to prevent collisions and efficiently complete tasks, mirroring human hand-eye and whole-body coordination.
- **Learning Reactive Behaviors from Ego-Vision:** The motivation is to train an agent that can navigate and interact based solely on ego-centric visual input, without constructing explicit environmental maps. This reactive capability is intended to allow the robot to improvise and adapt to changes in its environment, including dynamic obstacles not encountered during training.
- **Developing a Computationally Tractable Training Framework:** Given the computational intensity of training complex policies with high-fidelity sensory inputs like depth images, a practical objective is to devise an efficient training methodology that scales to large-scale data and facilitates sim-to-real transfer.

By addressing these objectives, the authors aim to advance the state of the art in mobile manipulation, providing a robust and adaptive solution for deployment in unstructured real-world settings.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces SPIN, an end-to-end reactive mobile manipulation framework that integrates perception, interaction, and navigation through an active visual system. The methodology involves a two-phase training strategy, primarily leveraging reinforcement learning (RL) in simulation, followed by distillation into a depth-image-based policy.

**Robot Platform:**
Experiments are conducted using the Hello Robot Stretch, a mobile manipulator with 10 actuated joints: two for the camera (pan, tilt), two for base rotation and translation, two for the arm (lift, extend), one for the gripper, and three for the dexterous wrist. An Intel D435i depth camera is mounted on the robot's head. The learned policy operates at 10 Hz, with velocity control for the base and position control for other joints.

**Two-Phase Training Strategy:**
To circumvent the computational bottleneck of rendering depth images during extensive RL training, the training process is divided into two phases:

**Phase 1: Learning Simultaneous Perception, Interaction and Navigation (RL-based)**
This phase focuses on learning low-level controls for the robot body, arm, and active camera gaze.
- **Observations:** The robot receives proprioceptive information ($x_t$), including joint angles, velocities, and base linear/angular velocities. For environmental perception, instead of raw depth images, a computationally cheaper proxy called "scandots" is used. Scandots represent the XYZ coordinates of obstacle bounding boxes. Crucially, only scandots visible within the camera's current field of view ($\tilde{s}_t$) are provided, encouraging the policy to learn active camera control. A PointNet architecture ($P$) compresses these visible scandots into a latent representation ($z_t$). For the pick task, the initial target object location ($o_i$) and its current visible location ($F(o_t, x_t)$) are also provided.
- **Actions:** The policy outputs both robot actions ($a_{robot}$) for base and arm movements, and camera actions ($a_{cam}$) for pan and tilt.
- **Policies:** Two distinct LSTM-based policies are trained: $\pi_{nav}$ for navigation and $\pi_{pick}$ for object picking. The $\pi_{pick}$ policy is activated once the robot is near the target object.
- **Navigation Policy ($\pi_{nav}$) Variants:** Two approaches are explored for learning the navigation policy:
 - **Coupled Visuomotor Optimization (CVO):** This approach trains a single RL policy to predict both robot and camera actions simultaneously. The policy's observations ($x_t$, $z_t$) are dynamically influenced by its camera actions, creating a challenging but potentially optimal joint optimization landscape.
 - **Decoupled Visuomotor Optimization (DVO):** To simplify optimization, this approach separates learning.
 - **Phase 1a (Learning to Move):** A policy ($\pi_{1a}^{nav}$) is trained to navigate assuming *privileged access* to all local scandots ($z_t = P(s_t)$) and the goal ($g_t$). Camera motion is irrelevant here.
 - **Phase 1b (Learning to See):** A second policy ($\pi_{1b}^{nav}$), initialized from $\pi_{1a}^{nav}$, is trained via RL to predict both robot and camera actions using only *visible* scandots ($\hat{z}_t$). The robot actions from this policy ($\hat{a}_{robot}$) are supervised to match those of the privileged $\pi_{1a}^{nav}$, forcing it to learn camera movements that gather necessary information for optimal navigation.
- **Reinforcement Learning:** Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) with backpropagation through time is used for training.
- **Reward Functions:**
 - **Navigation:** Rewards are based on distance to goal and forward progress towards the goal.
 - **Pick:** Rewards are given for gripper-object proximity and successful object lifting (based on contact forces and height).
- **Training Environments:** Procedurally generated 3D environments in IsaacGymEnvs are used, featuring long corridors with varying levels of clutter (0-5 obstacles within a 5m goal range) and different layouts (tight 1m wide corridors or L-shaped corridors). Object picking tasks involve objects spawned on tables of varying dimensions.

**Phase 2: From Scandots to Depth (Supervised Learning)**
This phase distills the behaviors learned in Phase 1 (using scandots) into a policy that operates directly from ego-centric depth images, making it deployable in the real world.
- **Architecture:** A Convolutional Network (CNN) ($C$) processes rendered depth images ($d_t$) into perception latents ($\tilde{z}_t$). This latent, along with proprioception and goal information, is fed into a student policy ($\pi'$, initialized from Phase 1's $\pi$).
- **Supervision:** The student policy's predicted actions ($[\tilde{a}_{robot}, \tilde{a}_{cam}]$) are supervised using an L2 loss against the teacher policy's actions from Phase 1.
- **Algorithm:** DAgger is employed for robust imitation learning, with an asynchronous implementation to accelerate training by simultaneously collecting data and updating the student policy.
- **Depth Image Processing:** To address noise and artifacts from real-world depth cameras, a real-time fast hole-filling algorithm and temporal filtering are applied to the depth images.
- **Object Detection (for Pick Policy):** For the pick task, once the robot is near the goal, YOLO is used to detect target objects in the RGB image. The corresponding depth information is then used to deproject pixel points into 3D coordinates, providing the object's goal position to the manipulation policy.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research demonstrates the effectiveness of the SPIN framework through evaluations in both simulation and real-world environments.

**Real-World Results:**
Experiments were conducted in two real-world scenes: an academic lab and an open study area, both featuring static and dynamic clutter, including human presence.
- **Emergent Behaviors:** The learned policy exhibited several emergent behaviors not explicitly trained for in simulation, attributed to the diversity of procedural environments seen during training and the use of relative observation space:
 - **Dynamic Obstacle Avoidance:** The robot demonstrated robustness to adversarially placed dynamic obstacles (e.g., humans kicking a box). It continuously perceived the environment in multiple directions, reacted to changes, and learned to stop and replan when no feasible path was available.
 - **Whole-Body Coordination:** The robot showed 3D spatial awareness and whole-body coordination. For instance, when an overhanging obstacle was suddenly introduced, it lowered its arm to navigate through instead of re-routing its base, which would be slower.
 - **Adaptive Rerouting:** The agent adapted its straight-line motion in real-time when new obstacles appeared in its path.
- **Comparison to Classical Baseline (Table 2):** SPIN was compared against a classical mapping and planning baseline using 2D RPLidar (gmapping + move_base).
 - **Static Environments:** SPIN achieved success rates of 0.8 in both real-world scenarios, outperforming the classical baseline (0.6 and 0.4). While the classical method might have slightly fewer collisions (e.g., 0.4 vs 1.0 in Scenario 1), its overall success rate was lower due to susceptibility to noise and inability to recover from map inaccuracies.
 - **Dynamic Environments:** SPIN demonstrated significant superiority, achieving success rates of 0.6 in both scenarios, whereas the classical baseline had near-zero success (0.0 and 0.2). This highlights SPIN's robust reactivity to dynamic obstacles, even without explicit training for them, outperforming classical methods reliant on static pre-built maps.

**Simulation Results:**
Evaluations were performed across 6 simulated benchmarks (easy, medium, hard difficulty), with two scenario types (tight corridor and L-shaped corridor).
- **Necessity of Active Vision:** The "FixCam" baseline, where the camera joints were fixed to look forward, showed significantly lower success rates (e.g., 20-26% in hard navigation scenarios) compared to SPIN (90-96%). This indicates that active vision with an actuated camera is crucial for effectively navigating cluttered environments.
- **Impact of PointNet Architecture:** The "NoPointNet" baseline, which concatenated scandots instead of processing them with a permutation-invariant PointNet, achieved lower success rates (e.g., 57-63% in hard navigation) than SPIN (90-96%). This demonstrates the benefit of the PointNet for easier optimization and better generalization.
- **Comparison of CVO vs. DVO:** Both Coupled Visuomotor Optimization (CVO) and Decoupled Visuomotor Optimization (DVO) variants of SPIN achieved comparable high performance, with CVO sometimes showing a slight edge in "hard" scenarios. The paper suggests CVO might discover optimal shortcuts faster due to joint optimization under partial observability.
- **Comparison to Mapping Baseline:** The "Mapping" baseline, which assumed *exteroceptive access* to a map, showed high success rates (often 100% in hard navigation/reach tasks). However, this baseline was considered an "easier version" of the problem, given its privileged information and lack of arm motion considerations, and still suffered from systematic noise and lack of online adaptability in real-world tests.
- **Episode Duration:** SPIN (CVO) generally showed shorter average episode durations in hard scenarios compared to baselines, indicating more efficient task completion.
- **Feasibility of Direct Depth Training:** Direct RL training from depth images proved inefficient, achieving less than 1% success after 22 hours of training. This contrasts sharply with SPIN's 2-phase approach, which reached high performance in 16 hours, validating the necessity of using scandots as an intermediate representation for computational tractability.

**Additional Findings:**
- **Optimal Visibility Range (Table 3):** An analysis of visibility range showed that a local range of $\le 2m$ yielded the highest success rates (0.96), while broader ranges ($\le 5m$) led to degraded performance (0.86 success). This indicates the importance of focused, local perception for reactive behaviors.
- **Learned Camera Movements (Figure 8):** The robot learned to actively control its camera, tilting it downward to maximize the field of view near its base when navigating clutter and facing forward when no immediate obstacles were present.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The SPIN framework represents a significant advancement in the field of mobile manipulation, offering a unified, data-driven, and reactive solution to long-standing challenges.

- **Paradigm Shift in Mobile Manipulation:** By demonstrating an effective end-to-end learning approach for simultaneous perception, interaction, and navigation, SPIN challenges the traditional paradigm of modular, non-reactive planning. It provides evidence that complex whole-body control-perception problems can be effectively cast into a reactive model trained via reinforcement learning, drawing parallels to how humans develop agile coordination through experience.
- **Enhanced Robustness and Adaptability:** The system's ability to operate effectively in unstructured, cluttered, and dynamic environments, including the presence of unforeseen dynamic obstacles, highlights its robustness. Its reactive nature allows it to adapt to real-time changes, a critical capability where classical, map-dependent methods often fail due to noise or environmental dynamism.
- **Efficient Training Methodology:** The proposed two-phase training strategy (scandots-based RL followed by depth-based supervised learning) addresses a key computational bottleneck in high-fidelity visual reinforcement learning. This efficiency makes it more feasible to train complex visuomotor policies, potentially accelerating research and development in this area.
- **Relevance for Advanced Robotic Platforms:** With increasing interest in humanoid robots and other mobile manipulators equipped with actuated cameras, SPIN offers a cost-effective and agile whole-body control solution suitable for platforms with limited sensing and onboard compute. This could facilitate the deployment of more dexterous and adaptive robots in various real-world scenarios.
- **Emergent Generalization Capabilities:** The observation of emergent behaviors such as dynamic obstacle avoidance and efficient whole-body coordination, even without explicit training for these specific scenarios, underscores the potential for learned policies to generalize from diverse simulated experiences to novel real-world situations. This suggests a path toward more intelligent and autonomous robotic systems.
- **Future Research Directions:** The identified limitations, such as reliance on stereo-matched depth leading to issues with transparent or shiny surfaces, provide clear avenues for future research, particularly integrating raw RGB data for richer perceptual understanding.

In summary, SPIN contributes a robust, efficient, and highly adaptive framework that moves mobile manipulation closer to human-like capabilities, with substantial implications for the future development and deployment of intelligent robots.
