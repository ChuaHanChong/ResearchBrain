# Scaling Native Multimodal Pre-Training From Scratch

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at The Chinese University of Hong Kong and Tencent systematically characterize the scaling properties of native multimodal pre-training from scratch, revealing distinct compute-optimal allocation behaviors for language and multimodal objectives. Their findings provide quantitative guidance for efficiently scaling unified multimodal models, showing that a higher multimodal data ratio necessitates a shift towards increased data scaling over parameter expansion.

## Problem

- Large language models, primarily text-centric, lack a deep understanding of the multimodal physical world.
- Current multimodal pre-training often uses late-fusion, coupling separately pre-trained vision and language encoders, leading to optimization asymmetries and hindering deep cross-modal integration.
- The scaling properties of native multimodal pre-training (training a single model from scratch on interleaved multimodal data) and optimal resource allocation strategies for distinct language and multimodal objectives remain largely unexplored.

## Method

- The authors systematically investigate ==compute-optimal scaling laws== for native multimodal pre-training by varying model size, total training tokens, and multimodal data ratios under fixed computational budgets.
- Two independent estimators, ==IsoFLOP Profiles== and ==Training-Curve Envelope==, are used to determine optimal model size and token count exponents for language and multimodal objectives.
- A ==Mixture-of-Experts (MoE)== architecture, a decoder-only ==Transformer==, is trained from scratch on a mixed corpus of 250 billion text tokens and up to 75 billion multimodal tokens.

## Results

- Language objective (LtextL_{text}Ltext) scaling exhibits robust power-law behavior that is mostly invariant to the multimodal data ratio, implying consistent optimal model size allocation.
- Multimodal objective (LmmL_{mm}Lmm) scaling shows a substantial decrease in optimal model size exponent as the multimodal data ratio increases, indicating a 'data-hungry' nature for cross-modal alignment.
- Native multimodal pre-training maintains aggregate text performance across **16** benchmarks, achieves improved performance on text-only spatial reasoning tasks (e.g., SpatialEval) over text-only baselines, and demonstrates emergent multimodal in-context learning that strengthens with increasing model scale and data scaling.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The optimal allocation for the language objective remains largely composition-invariant to the multimodal data ratio, primarily governed by the dedicated text compute budget.
> - The optimal allocation for the multimodal objective is strictly composition-variant, shifting significantly towards data scaling (more tokens) over parameter expansion (larger models) as the multimodal data ratio increases.
> - Native multimodal pre-training preserves core language capabilities and induces positive cross-modal transfer, particularly in pure-text spatial reasoning, which improves with increasing model size.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of large language models (LLMs) has advanced significantly, with models demonstrating capabilities in reasoning and generation primarily through text-only pre-training. However, this text-centric approach inherently limits their understanding of the multimodal physical world. Multimodal pre-training aims to address this limitation by incorporating diverse data modalities, such as images and text, to foster a more grounded and comprehensive understanding.

Currently, the prevailing paradigm for multimodal pre-training is late fusion. This approach typically involves coupling a pre-trained, text-only language model with a separately pre-trained vision encoder. A lightweight projection layer then bridges these distinct encoders, followed by continued training on multimodal data. While this strategy efficiently leverages existing unimodal checkpoints and their robust representations, it introduces a fundamental asymmetry. The vision and language representations are learned independently, from disparate data distributions, and often under different optimization objectives. This separation can hinder deep cross-modal integration, as the modalities may not fully align within a unified representational space.

An alternative, emerging paradigm is native multimodal pre-training, where a single model is trained from scratch on interleaved multimodal data. This approach aims to establish a shared representational capacity and achieve deep modality integration from the outset, thereby mitigating the optimization asymmetries inherent in late-fusion architectures. However, the systematic characterization of scaling properties for native multimodal pre-training has remained largely unexplored. Prior work on scaling laws has primarily focused on unimodal language models, establishing predictable relationships between model performance, size, training data, and computational budget. The transferability of these unimodal scaling principles to the more complex native multimodal setting, particularly concerning the optimal allocation of resources between model size and data for distinct language and multimodal objectives, was an open question. This research specifically investigates these scaling properties for native multimodal pre-training, differentiating itself from prior analyses that might have considered multimodal training with a single aggregate loss function. By analyzing the distinct scaling behaviors of language and multimodal objectives under varying data compositions, this work aims to provide foundational insights for the design and efficient scaling of future multimodal foundation models.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to systematically characterize the scaling properties of native multimodal pre-training from scratch. This involves investigating how optimal resource allocation, particularly concerning model size and the quantity of training tokens, behaves under a fixed computational budget when training a transformer-based vision-language model.

The motivation for this investigation stems from several points:

1. **Addressing a Research Gap:** While large language models have demonstrated impressive capabilities, their text-only pre-training inherently limits their perception of the multimodal physical world. Native multimodal pre-training, which trains models from scratch on multimodal inputs, offers a promising pathway to deep cross-modal integration and the mitigation of optimization asymmetries found in traditional late-fusion architectures. Despite these conceptual advantages, the empirical scaling properties of this paradigm, particularly how models behave as computational resources are scaled, have not been systematically characterized. This lack of understanding makes it difficult to efficiently design and train increasingly capable native multimodal models.

2. **Optimal Resource Allocation:** For unimodal language models, compute-optimal scaling laws guide the distribution of a fixed computational budget between increasing model size and increasing data. However, it is unclear whether native multimodal pre-training adheres to analogous scaling laws. Specifically, the research aims to determine if the optimal allocations for language and multimodal objectives, which are simultaneously optimized within a shared parameter space, align or conflict. Understanding these dynamics is crucial for making informed decisions about how to invest compute resources to maximize model performance.

3. **Impact of Data Composition:** A core question is how the composition of the training data, specifically the ratio of multimodal data to text data, influences the optimal allocation strategy. Native multimodal models process diverse data streams, and it is hypothesized that the efficiency of learning from these streams might be sensitive to their relative proportions. Characterizing this sensitivity is necessary for designing effective multimodal data mixtures.

4. **Deriving Quantitative Guidance:** The ultimate motivation is to establish a principled framework that provides quantitative guidance for scaling native multimodal foundation models. This includes deriving precise configurations of model size, token count, and data mixture that define an "efficiency frontier" for a given computational budget. Such a framework would move the development of these complex models from heuristic approaches to a more predictable and compute-optimal methodology.

5. **Understanding Cross-Modal Transfer and Capabilities:** Beyond scaling dynamics, the research is motivated to evaluate the downstream implications of native multimodal pre-training. This includes assessing whether this paradigm preserves core language capabilities, induces positive cross-modal transfer (e.g., improved text-only spatial reasoning from multimodal training), and enables emergent properties like robust multimodal in-context learning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research investigates compute-optimal scaling laws for native multimodal pre-training by systematically varying model size, token count, and multimodal data ratios under fixed computational budgets. The methodology is structured around defining the problem, employing two distinct estimators for scaling laws, and evaluating the models on a range of downstream tasks.

#### 3.1. Compute-Optimal Allocation Formulation

The problem is formalized as minimizing the final pre-training loss $L(N, D)$ as a function of model size $N$ (number of activated non-embedding parameters) and total training tokens $D$, subject to a fixed computational budget $C$. The standard approximation $C = 6ND$ is used. A key aspect is the introduction of a multimodal data ratio $r$, which parameterizes the data composition.
To analyze the interplay between different objectives, the problem is decoupled into optimizing language ($L_{text}$) and multimodal ($L_{mm}$) objectives independently. Corresponding effective compute budgets are defined as $C_{text} = 6ND_{text}$ and $C_{mm} = 6ND_{mm}$, where $D_{text} = D/(1+r)$ and $D_{mm} = Dr/(1+r)$. The research hypothesizes that compute-optimal allocations follow a power-law relationship: $N_{opt}(C) \propto C^a$ and $D_{opt}(C) \propto C^b$, where $a+b=1$. Smoothed training loss serves as a proxy for test loss, ensuring standardized evaluation.

#### 3.2. Estimators of the Compute-Optimal Frontier

Two independent methodologies are employed to estimate the optimal allocation:

- **IsoFLOP Profiles:** This is the primary estimator. For a predetermined compute budget $C$, the loss of various models is plotted against $\log N$. A parabolic function is fit to this profile, and its minimum identifies the optimal model size $N_{opt}(C)$. The corresponding optimal token count $D_{opt}(C)$ is then derived as $C/(6N_{opt})$. By performing this across multiple compute budgets, the allocation exponents $a$ and $b$ are determined through regression of $N_{opt}$ and $D_{opt}$ on $C$.
- **Training-Curve Envelope:** This method serves as an independent cross-validation. For a fixed model size $N$, training involves increasing the token count $D$, generating a trajectory of loss versus total compute $C$. By pooling these trajectories from all evaluated models and extracting the lower envelope, the minimum achievable loss for any given budget $C$ is identified. Each point on this envelope corresponds to a specific $(N, D)$ configuration, allowing for independent estimation of exponents $a$ and $b$ by regressing $\log N$ and $\log D$ against $\log C$.
- **The Compute Frontier $L(C)$:** Along the lower envelope, the loss is modeled by a power law: $L(C) = E + \left(\frac{C_c}{C}\right)^\beta$, where $E$ is an irreducible floor, $C_c$ is a critical compute scale, and $\beta$ is a decay exponent. These parameters are fit via least squares in logarithmic space to provide a smooth analytical description and reliable extrapolation of loss.

#### 3.3. Experimental Setup

- **Training Data:** The models are trained on a mixed corpus comprising 250 billion text tokens (from web pages, books, academic papers, etc.) and 75 billion multimodal tokens. The multimodal data consists of web-crawled image-text pairs and interleaved image-text documents. Images are converted into continuous patch embeddings.
- **Model Architecture:** A Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architecture based on a decoder-only Transformer is used. Images are directly processed by a single patch embedding layer, circumventing traditional vision encoders. The MoE models are trained using an auxiliary-loss-free approach. Six model sizes, ranging from A71M (71 million parameters) to A3B (3 billion parameters), are explored.
- **Hyperparameters:** Key hyperparameters include the Muon optimizer with a weight decay of 0.1, a gradient clipping threshold of 1.0, a warmup-stable learning rate schedule, a global batch size of 16M sequences, and a maximum sequence length of 4096 tokens. The training loss is computed exclusively on text tokens, with vision tokens masked out.
- **Multimodal Ratio ($r$):** The multimodal data ratio is varied, with values of $r=0$ (text-only), $r=0.1$ (25B multimodal tokens), $r=0.2$ (50B multimodal tokens), and $r=0.3$ (75B multimodal tokens) relative to the 250B text tokens.

#### 3.4. Downstream Implications Evaluation

To assess the impact of native multimodal pre-training, models are evaluated on a comprehensive suite of tasks:

- **Text Capabilities:** 16 benchmarks across aggregate reasoning (e.g., MMLU-Redux, AGIEval), coding (HumanEval+, MBPP+), mathematics (GSM8K, MATH), logic reasoning (BBH, SpatialEval), knowledge (NaturalQuestions, TriviaQA), and commonsense reasoning (Hellaswag, PIQA).
- **Multimodal Capabilities:** 23 benchmarks across aggregate multimodal understanding (MMStar, MMMU, MME), Visual Question Answering (VQAv2, TextVQA), STEM problems (MathVista, ScienceQA), document understanding (HallusionBench, ChartQA), vision knowledge (MMBench$_{cc}$, SimpleVQA), counting (CountBench, CountQA), and spatial reasoning (RealWorldQA, OmniSpatial, SpatialEval).
- **Evaluation Protocol:** Models are evaluated in an in-context learning setting. For multiple-choice tasks, the option yielding the lowest perplexity is selected. Open-ended tasks use exact-match, and coding tasks use Pass@1. Images are processed into 32x32 patches, limited to 1536 tokens per image (512 in few-shot scenarios to fit templates). Few-shot templates are interleaved image-question-answer sequences from development/training splits.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings regarding the scaling of native multimodal pre-training, its impact on model capabilities, and the distinct behaviors of language and multimodal objectives.

#### 4.1. Scaling of the Language Objective

The language objective ($L_{text}$) demonstrated a compute-optimal allocation that is largely composition-invariant to the multimodal data ratio ($r$). IsoFLOP profiles (Figure 1) revealed stable parabolic minima for $L_{text}$ across various values of $r$, indicating a consistent optimal model size for a given compute budget dedicated to text. This observation was corroborated by the training-curve envelope (Figure 2), which showed that the compute frontier for language loss strictly followed a power law. Although the IsoFLOP parametric fits showed a minor decrease in allocation scaling exponents as $r$ increased, this variation was minimal and not consistently supported by the training-curve envelope, suggesting it fell within fitting error margins. These results indicate that the parameter capacity required to minimize $L_{text}$ is primarily governed by the isolated text compute budget ($C_{text}$), exhibiting robustness to the introduction of multimodal tokens.

#### 4.2. Scaling of the Multimodal Objective

In contrast to the language objective, the multimodal objective ($L_{mm}$) exhibited strictly composition-variant scaling behavior. As the multimodal ratio ($r$) increased from 0.1 to 0.3, the optimal model size exponent derived from IsoFLOP profiles (Figure 4) substantially decreased. This downward trajectory was validated by the training-curve envelope (Figure 5), showing a consistent decline in optimal model size allocation. This strong agreement between estimators indicates a robust scaling law: as the data mixture becomes denser with multimodal inputs, the optimal compute allocation shifts significantly towards data scaling (more tokens) rather than parameter expansion (larger models). This suggests a "data-hungry" nature for cross-modal alignment.

#### 4.3. Joint Pareto Frontier

When optimizing the shared parameter count under a unified computational budget ($C_{total}$), a clear Pareto frontier emerged, illustrating the trade-off between language loss ($L_{text}$) and multimodal loss ($L_{mm}$) across varying data ratios $r$ (Figure 7a). The research employed an asymmetric modeling framework, combining the composition-invariant language objective with the composition-variant multimodal objective. This framework revealed that increasing the multimodal ratio (e.g., from $r=0.1$ to $r=0.3$) necessitated a deliberate architectural shift. Specifically, a higher multimodal allocation reduced the optimal parameter scaling proportionality (from $N_{opt} \propto C^{0.69}_{total}$ to $N_{opt} \propto C^{0.66}_{total}$), while simultaneously requiring a more aggressive scaling of the system-wide token allocation (from $D_{opt} \propto C^{0.31}_{total}$ to $D_{opt} \propto C^{0.34}_{total}$) (Figure 7b, 7c). This implies that scaling unified multimodal models efficiently demands constraining theoretical parameter expansion in favor of training on substantially larger token budgets.

#### 4.4. Downstream Implications

- **Preservation of Core Language Capabilities:** Native multimodal pre-training was found to preserve core language capabilities. Despite the introduction of multimodal data, aggregate text performance across 16 benchmarks remained consistent across all multimodal data ratios ($r$) and model scales (Figure 8a, 8b). The average scores deviated by less than one percentage point, indicating that shared parameters successfully optimized both language and multimodal objectives without measurable interference or degradation of text competence.

- **Positive Cross-Modal Transfer in Spatial Reasoning:** A notable finding was the positive cross-modal transfer, particularly in pure-text spatial reasoning tasks (SpatialEval's MazeNav and SpatialMap). Multimodal models (r=0.3) consistently outperformed text-only baselines (r=0) on these tasks, even though the queries were strictly text-based (Figure 9a, 9b). This advantage scaled with model size, with the performance gap widening significantly for larger models. This suggests that spatial and relational structures learned from visual modalities were successfully embedded within the shared parameter space and generalized to enhance unimodal text comprehension.

- **Emergence of Multimodal In-Context Learning:**
 - **Scaling with Model Size:** Multimodal in-context learning emerged and strengthened with increasing model scale (Figure 10a, 10b). For smaller models (e.g., A71M), in-context templates provided negligible benefit, sometimes even degrading performance. However, for larger models (e.g., A3B), 1-shot and 3-shot settings yielded substantial performance gains (up to +2.43 points) over 0-shot baselines, mirroring observations in text-only LLMs.
 - **Strengthening with Data Scaling:** The efficacy of few-shot prompting also increased as models saw more training data. Initially, few-shot curves were indistinguishable from 0-shot, but a clear performance gap emerged and widened during later stages of training for larger models (Figure 11). This indicates that sufficient model scale and substantial training data are both prerequisites for the manifestation of in-context learning.
 - **Concentration on Spatial Reasoning:** Few-shot gains varied across task categories, being most pronounced and consistent in spatial reasoning benchmarks, and modest in aggregate and vision-knowledge suites (Figure 12). Performance on OCR- and recognition-oriented tasks often plateaued or degraded with in-context templates, suggesting that templates primarily aid in resolving spatial and relational structures rather than enhancing fine-grained visual recognition or text extraction. The A3B model also showed an overall upward trend in few-shot gains with increased total training tokens (Figure 13a, 13b).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research establishes foundational empirical groundwork for understanding and predictably scaling native multimodal pre-training from scratch. The findings offer quantitative guidance crucial for the efficient development of multimodal foundation models.

**Key areas of significance and potential impact include:**

1. **Principled Resource Allocation:** The derivation of compute-optimal scaling laws for native multimodal models provides a principled framework for allocating computational resources. By distinguishing between language and multimodal objectives and characterizing their distinct scaling behaviors, the research offers quantitative insights into how to distribute a fixed compute budget optimally between model size, text token count, and multimodal token count. This can lead to more efficient training processes, reducing wasted compute and accelerating the development of capable models.

2. **Informed Architectural Design:** The discovery of composition-invariant language scaling and composition-variant multimodal scaling highlights fundamental differences in how these objectives learn. This mechanistic insight suggests that architectural designs for multimodal models should account for these divergences. For instance, the finding that text-heavy mixtures become compute-efficient only at larger model scales, shifting optimal resource allocation toward greater model capacity, can inform decisions about model family design and training strategies.

3. **Efficiency Frontier for Development:** The established language-multimodal Pareto frontier offers concrete, deployable training configurations. This allows researchers and practitioners to select optimal combinations of model size, token count, and data mixture based on specific compute budgets and desired performance trade-offs between modalities. Such a frontier can serve as a roadmap for scaling, enabling more predictable outcomes in the development cycle.

4. **Enhanced Model Capabilities:** The empirical demonstrations of positive cross-modal transfer and the emergence of robust multimodal in-context learning signify that native multimodal pre-training yields tangible benefits beyond simply combining modalities. The improvement in pure-text spatial reasoning, derived from multimodal training, suggests a deeper, integrated understanding that generalizes across modalities. The scalable nature of multimodal in-context learning indicates that these models can adapt to new tasks with few examples, enhancing their versatility and applicability in real-world scenarios.

5. **Foundation for Future Research:** While the analysis is limited to models up to 3 billion active parameters, a single image-text data family, and loss-based scaling proxies, the proposed framework provides a solid foundation. These findings motivate subsequent validation at larger scales, across diverse modalities (e.g., audio, video), and with varied data compositions. The work opens avenues for investigating how these scaling laws might interact with different architectural choices, optimization techniques, and evaluation metrics beyond training loss. Ultimately, this research contributes to building a more robust theoretical and empirical understanding necessary for the scalable development of truly multimodal artificial intelligence.
