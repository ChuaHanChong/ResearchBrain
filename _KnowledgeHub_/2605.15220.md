# Always Learning, Always Mixing: Efficient and Simple Data Mixing All The Time

> [!summary] Summary
> OP-MIX is an algorithm that efficiently and continually optimizes data mixtures across the entire language model training lifecycle, encompassing pretraining, midtraining, and instruction tuning. It achieves performance comparable to or better than specialized baselines while significantly reducing computational costs, ranging from 14% to 95% less compute in various scenarios.

## Problem

- Existing data mixing methods are computationally expensive, often relying on training numerous "proxy models" for different data mixtures.
- Current algorithms are primarily designed for pretraining with fixed data domains, limiting their use in dynamic continual learning scenarios where new data continuously emerges.
- The absence of a unified, efficient data mixing strategy across the entire language model training lifecycle leads to phase-specific complexities and suboptimal "off-policy" proxy model issues.

## Method

- **OP-MIX** uses ==Low-Rank Adapters (LoRAs)== trained directly on the current model (==on-policy==) to efficiently approximate the effect of training on new or existing data domains.
- It employs ==linear interpolation== of these trained LoRA adapters to simulate the loss surface of various data mixtures without requiring full training runs for each candidate combination.
- A ==log-linear regression== model is fitted to the interpolated evaluations to estimate an optimal, regularized data mixing ratio, which is then used for full training of the base model.

## Results

- OP-MIX improved average perplexity by **6.3%** over ERM in pretraining and matched MergeMix performance with **14% less compute**, outperforming OLMix by **5-6%** across model scales.
- It mitigated catastrophic forgetting in continual midtraining, achieving performance comparable to full retraining with up to **66% less compute** and outperforming other continual learning baselines by an average of **27%**.
- In continual instruction tuning, OP-MIX achieved mean accuracy of **60.0%** (comparable to SDFT's **60.2%**) with a **95% reduction in compute**, and its combination with SDFT yielded the highest overall performance of **61.9%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Low-rank adapters (LoRAs) can serve as effective and computationally inexpensive "on-policy" proxies to estimate the learning dynamics of a large language model for different data sources.
> - Linear interpolation of LoRA adapters allows for efficient simulation of the loss landscape across numerous data mixtures, bypassing the combinatorial cost of training full models for each mix.
> - Data mixing is fundamentally an "online decision making problem" that recurs throughout the LLM lifecycle, justifying a single, adaptive approach rather than separate phase-specific algorithms.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development and training of large language models (LLMs) critically depend on the composition of their training data. Data mixing, the process of determining how to combine different data sources or types, significantly influences model quality during pretraining and governs knowledge retention and acquisition during continual learning and adaptation.

Existing data mixing methods exhibit several limitations. A prevalent approach involves training smaller "proxy models" on various candidate data mixtures and then extrapolating their performance to larger models. This method is computationally intensive, particularly as the number of data domains increases, leading to a combinatorial explosion of training runs. Furthermore, many current data mixing algorithms are designed specifically for the pretraining phase and assume a fixed set of data domains. This design restricts their applicability in dynamic scenarios where new tasks, corpora, or capabilities continuously emerge, necessitating a continual learning paradigm. Such scenarios are common in the post-pretraining lifecycle of language models, including midtraining and instruction tuning.

The absence of universally applicable, efficient data mixing strategies across the entire language model training lifecycle presents a challenge. Specialized phase-specific algorithms introduce complexity and require individual tuning. Moreover, methods relying on separate proxy models become problematic after pretraining, as publicly released models often lack corresponding small proxies, and these proxies may not accurately reflect the learning dynamics of the full-scale target model. This work introduces an algorithm intended to provide a unified solution to data mixing that is effective across various training phases and adapts to evolving data landscapes, addressing the computational and logistical challenges of existing approaches.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a single, unified data mixing algorithm that operates efficiently and effectively across the entire lifecycle of language model training, from initial pretraining to continual midtraining and instruction tuning. The authors identify a fundamental need for such an algorithm, motivated by several key limitations of current methods:

- **Computational Expense:** Existing data mixing strategies, particularly those relying on proxy models, are combinatorially expensive. Training numerous proxy models on different data mixtures to identify an optimal composition becomes impractical as the number of data domains grows. The authors seek to reduce this computational burden.
- **Fixed Data Domains:** Most current data mixing algorithms are tailored for pretraining and assume a static set of data domains. In real-world scenarios, language model training involves continuous evolution of available data, with new datasets and tasks emerging periodically. This necessitates a mechanism for continually adjusting data mixtures.
- **Lack of Unified Approach:** The language model training lifecycle is often viewed as a sequence of distinct phases, each potentially requiring specialized data mixing techniques. The authors propose that data mixing is fundamentally an "online decision making problem" that recurs throughout training, arguing for a single, unified solution rather than phase-specific algorithms. This aims to reduce complexity and improve consistency.
- **"Off-Policy" Proxy Models:** Methods that use separately initialized small proxy models can diverge from the actual learning dynamics of the large model being trained. This can lead to suboptimal mixture estimations for the target model. The motivation is to develop an "on-policy" approach where the proxy is directly tied to the current state of the model being trained.

To achieve these objectives, the research is guided by two core insights:
1. **On-Policy Proxy Estimation:** Candidate data mixtures can be simulated efficiently by using low-rank adapters (LoRAs) trained directly on the current model. This eliminates the need for separate, potentially "off-policy" proxy models and grounds the search for optimal mixtures in the actual learning dynamics of the target model.
2. **Efficient Mixture Simulation:** Linear interpolation between LoRA adapters is proposed as a method to approximate the loss surface of various data mixtures without requiring new training runs for each candidate mix. This addresses the combinatorial explosion issue by allowing the exploration of the mixture space post-hoc using pre-trained adapters.

By integrating these insights, the research aims to enable continuous and efficient data mixing as new data domains become available, thereby supporting a more dynamic and adaptive language model training paradigm.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed algorithm, OP-MIX (On-Policy Mix), addresses the challenges of efficient and continuous data mixing across the language model training lifecycle by integrating Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) with linear interpolation of model weights. The methodology is structured around an iterative process that adapts to new data domains and optimizes mixing ratios with minimal computational overhead.

#### 3.1. OP-MIX Algorithm Overview

OP-MIX operates in a continual setting where new data domains may arrive periodically. The process can be summarized in three main steps, as illustrated in Figure 1 and detailed in Algorithm 1:

1. **LoRA Adapter Training:** When a new set of `K` domains `{D_{m+1}..., D_{m+K}}` arrives, the base model `θ_base` (the current state of the language model) is used to train lightweight LoRA adapters.
 - A single LoRA adapter, `θ_LoRA_old`, is trained on the existing aggregated "old" data mixture (`p_{t-1}`). This adapter approximates continued training on the previously learned distribution.
 - For each new domain `D_{m+k}`, a separate LoRA adapter, `θ_LoRA_Dm+k`, is trained. These adapters provide a computationally inexpensive approximation of how the model would perform if fully finetuned on each new domain individually.
 - The "on-policy" nature of these LoRAs is crucial: they are built directly from the `θ_base` model, ensuring their learning dynamics are reflective of the target model.

2. **Mixture Simulation via Adapter Interpolation:** Instead of training new models for every candidate data mixture, OP-MIX leverages linear interpolation of the trained LoRA adapters.
 - The algorithm defines a mixture expansion function `E(α)` that maps a reduced-simplex weight vector `α = (α_old, α_{m+1}..., α_{m+K})` (representing the proportions of old data and new domains) to the full `m+K-1` simplex.
 - Various interpolation points are sampled from the K-simplex. For each sample `α_p`, a merged adapter `θ_LoRA_αp` is formed by linearly combining `θ_LoRA_old` and the `θ_LoRA_Dm+k` adapters using the weights in `α_p`.
 - The performance (e.g., loss) of these interpolated adapters `θ_LoRA_αp` is evaluated on each domain. This process simulates the effect of different mixing ratios between old and new data without requiring additional training runs for each mixture.

3. **Optimal Mixture Estimation and Full Training:**
 - A log-linear regression model is fit to the evaluations from the interpolated adapters, creating a smooth, estimated loss surface over the mixture proportions.
 - A regularized optimization problem is then solved over this surface to find `α*`, the optimal mixing ratio between old and new data. A KL-divergence regularization term `λ D_KL(E(α) || μ)` is used to keep the mixture `E(α)` close to a prior `μ`.
 - Finally, the base model `θ_base` undergoes full training (finetuning) using the newly computed optimal mixture `p_t = E(α*)`.

#### 3.2. Specifics for Different Training Phases

- **Pretraining:** OP-MIX begins with an initial "warmup" phase where data is sampled uniformly (empirical risk minimization, ERM). After warmup, each initial dataset is reintroduced as a "new domain," and OP-MIX is applied to determine their optimal mixture.
- **Continual Midtraining:** This setting simulates updating a pretrained model with a stream of new reference datasets. For each new dataset, the OP-MIX pipeline is executed to incorporate it while considering the performance on previously learned data.
- **Continual Instruction Tuning:** Here, the model is finetuned on successive instruction-following datasets. OP-MIX is applied to manage the mix of current and historical instruction data. It is tested with both standard cross-entropy loss (SFT) and on-policy self-distillation (SDFT) objectives.

#### 3.3. Theoretical Analysis

The paper provides a theoretical analysis to formalize the sources of error in OP-MIX, defining two main types:
- `ε_merge`: The error between the true loss surface of a model trained on a specific mixture `F(α)` and the loss surface obtained by interpolating full finetuning updates `F_M(α)`. This measures the effectiveness of linear mode connectivity for model merging.
- `ε_LoRA`: The error between `F_M(α)` and the loss surface derived from interpolating LoRA adapters `F̂(α)`. This quantifies how well LoRA adapters approximate full finetuning.
The analysis provides a bound on the suboptimality of OP-MIX's predicted optimal weight (`J(α̂) - J(α*) ≤ 2(ε_merge + ε_LoRA)`), indicating that if these two approximation errors are small, OP-MIX can reliably find near-optimal mixtures.

#### 3.4. Baselines and Evaluation

- **Pretraining Baselines:** Empirical Risk Minimization (ERM), MergeMix (a related method that uses full finetuning for proxies), and OLMix (which uses small, separate proxy models).
- **Continual Learning Baselines:** Continual fine-tuning with Weight-Stable-Decay-Simplified (WSD-S) learning rate schedule, 10% data replay, and Full Retraining (the upper bound performance).
- **Evaluation Metrics:** Perplexity for pretraining, average performance across domains for continual midtraining (assessing forgetting mitigation), and accuracy for continual instruction tuning. Efficiency is measured by total training FLOPs.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of OP-MIX across three distinct phases of language model training demonstrates its effectiveness and computational efficiency.

#### 4.1. Performance Across the Language Model Lifecycle

A single instantiation of OP-MIX was shown to be effective across pretraining, continual midtraining, and continual instruction tuning.

- **Pretraining:**
 - OP-MIX improved upon training without data mixing (ERM) by 6.3% in average perplexity across 150M, 300M, and 530M parameter models.
 - It matched the performance of MergeMix, a baseline that uses full finetuning for proxies, while utilizing 14% less compute.
 - OP-MIX outperformed OLMix, which relies on separate small proxy models, by 5-6% in perplexity across all model scales, supporting the "on-policy" hypothesis.
 - Downstream task performance evaluations (Table 2 in Appendix) indicated that OP-MIX consistently ranked among the best-performing or second-best methods.

- **Continual Midtraining:**
 - OP-MIX mitigated catastrophic forgetting more effectively than other continual learning baselines, such as Continual SFT with WSD-S (27% better on average, 71% better on Reddit).
 - It achieved performance comparable to full retraining, which serves as an upper bound, while using substantially less overall compute (up to 66% less).
 - An ablation showed that merely merging LoRA adapters without subsequent full finetuning (LoRA-Merge) was less effective than the full OP-MIX pipeline, indicating the benefit of using LoRA primarily as a proxy for mixture estimation.

- **Continual Instruction Tuning:**
 - When applied atop standard Supervised Finetuning (SFT), OP-MIX achieved a mean accuracy of 60.0%, which closely matched the performance of Self-Distillation Finetuning (SDFT) at 60.2%. This was achieved with a 95% reduction in compute compared to SDFT, demonstrating that data mixing alone can yield gains comparable to more sophisticated continual learning algorithms.
 - Combining OP-MIX with SDFT resulted in the highest overall performance of 61.9%, suggesting that data mixing and modifications to the training objective (like self-distillation) represent independent axes of improvement that can be synergistically combined.

#### 4.2. Efficiency and Pareto-Dominance

OP-MIX consistently Pareto-dominated the performance-efficiency frontier across all tested scenarios (Figure 2). This indicates that no baseline achieved better performance with lower computational cost.
- Its efficiency stems from the use of LoRA for cheap proxy training and linear interpolation for simulating mixtures, which avoids the combinatorial cost of training full models for each candidate mix.
- In continual midtraining, OP-MIX's ability to reuse mixtures efficiently was particularly beneficial, as the cost of naive retraining would otherwise increase significantly with each new domain.

#### 4.3. Reliability of Optimal Mixture Estimation

Empirical analysis confirmed that OP-MIX reliably estimated optimal data mixtures:
- The loss surface estimated by merging LoRAs closely tracked the true data mixing loss surface, which was obtained by running full finetuning for various mixture proportions (Figure 7).
- In the continual midtraining setting, OP-MIX exhibited an average increase in loss of 0.9% compared to the optimal proportions (estimated by grid sweeping), in contrast to a 2.9% increase for a fixed 10% data replay baseline. This suggests the approximation gap is small and does not significantly increase with more datasets (Figure 8).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents OP-MIX as a notable advancement in the field of language model training, with several implications for current practices and future directions.

#### 5.1. Unified and Adaptive Data Mixing

OP-MIX represents the first data mixing algorithm designed to operate universally across the entire language model training lifecycle—pretraining, continual midtraining, and continual instruction tuning. This unified approach mitigates the need for phase-specific data mixing strategies, reducing complexity and potential inconsistencies in training pipelines. Its ability to continually adapt and incorporate new data domains, without relying on static datasets or separate proxy models, is critical for supporting the dynamic nature of real-world language model development.

#### 5.2. Substantial Computational Efficiency

The algorithm significantly reduces the computational overhead associated with optimizing data mixtures. By leveraging LoRA adapters for on-policy proxy training and linear interpolation for mixture simulation, OP-MIX achieves performance comparable to or superior to baselines while using a fraction of the compute (e.g., 14% less in pretraining, 66% less in continual midtraining, and 95% less in continual instruction tuning compared to certain baselines). This efficiency can accelerate research and development cycles, making adaptive data mixing more accessible for resource-constrained environments or for iterating rapidly on data compositions.

#### 5.3. Effective Catastrophic Forgetting Mitigation

In continual learning scenarios, OP-MIX demonstrated effectiveness in mitigating catastrophic forgetting. By intelligently mixing new data with a representation of old data, it allows models to acquire new knowledge without significantly degrading performance on previously learned tasks. This is a central challenge in continual learning and positions OP-MIX as a relevant method for maintaining model capabilities over time.

#### 5.4. Conceptual Reorientation of Training

The success of OP-MIX across disparate training phases suggests a re-evaluation of language model training as a continuous process rather than a sequence of discrete, independently optimized stages. This conceptual shift could lead to more integrated and streamlined approaches to other training decisions, such as learning rate scheduling or objective function design, fostering more holistic optimization strategies.

#### 5.5. Future Research Directions

The work opens avenues for further research, including:
- **Scalability:** Investigating OP-MIX's behavior and performance at frontier scales (e.g., 70B+ parameters) and with a much larger number of data domains (10-100).
- **Reward-Based Objectives:** Extending OP-MIX to optimize data mixtures for reward-based objectives, such as Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), where LoRA has shown promise.
- **Generalization of Unified Approaches:** Exploring whether other critical training decisions (e.g., learning rate schedules, training objectives) can also benefit from similar lifecycle-unified formulations, potentially leading to further integration and optimization of the entire training pipeline.

In summary, OP-MIX offers a computationally efficient and adaptable solution to data mixing that is applicable throughout the language model training lifecycle, with the potential to influence how language models are developed and continually updated.
