# Semantic-aware Wasserstein Policy Regularization for Large Language Model Alignment

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from KAIST and UNIST developed Wasserstein Policy Regularization (WPR), a new framework for aligning large language models that integrates semantic similarity into policy optimization by leveraging entropy-regularized Wasserstein distance between token distributions. This method consistently achieves higher win rates and MT-Bench scores compared to KL- and f-divergence baselines across various tasks, leading to more semantically coherent LLM outputs.

## Problem

- Current Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) techniques for LLM alignment use Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence or f-divergence for policy regularization, which are semantically unaware.
- These traditional methods only compare token probabilities at identical indices, failing to account for semantic similarity between different tokens, leading to suboptimal alignment and reduced output diversity.
- The semantic blindness can cause models to be excessively penalized for semantically close but lexically different responses, hindering nuanced alignment with human preferences.

## Method

- Proposed ==Wasserstein== Policy Regularization (**WPR**), which replaces f-divergence with entropy-regularized Wasserstein (Sinkhorn) distance in the RLHF objective to incorporate semantic awareness.
- Leveraged a tractable dual formulation of the Sinkhorn distance, converting the complex regularization term into a token-wise penalty compatible with standard RL algorithms like PPO.
- Defined the cost matrix for the Wasserstein distance using Euclidean distances between fixed token embeddings, and employed nearest-k1 and top-k2 truncations to ensure computational tractability for large vocabularies.

## Results

- WPR consistently achieved superior win rates against SFT-only models (e.g., **0.924** on TL;DR summarization) and RKL-regularized models (e.g., **0.608** on TL;DR), outperforming all f-divergence baselines.
- The method attained the highest MT-Bench score of 4.272 and improved pass@1 metrics in code generation across different difficulty levels, demonstrating its general applicability and improved broader conversational abilities.
- Analysis revealed WPR provides a smoother and less punitive regularization (Pearson correlation **0.917** with KL, but slope **0.579**), correlates more strongly with semantic similarity metrics like BERTScore, and produces more semantically coherent next-token candidates.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating the geometry of the token space through Wasserstein distance directly into policy regularization addresses the semantic blindness of traditional divergence methods.
> - The dual formulation of the entropy-regularized Wasserstein distance allows its complex computation to be reduced to an efficient token-wise penalty, making it compatible with existing RL algorithms like PPO with a modest 2.5% increase in training time.
> - Semantic-aware regularization leads to more stable training and robust performance compared to f-divergences, which often suffer from instability due to exploding probability ratios.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Executive Summary

The research addresses a critical limitation in current Large Language Model (LLM) alignment techniques, particularly Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), which predominantly rely on Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence or its f-divergence variants for policy regularization. These traditional methods compare token probabilities only at identical indices, failing to account for the semantic similarity between different tokens. The authors propose Wasserstein Policy Regularization (WPR), a novel framework that leverages the entropy-regularized Wasserstein (Sinkhorn) distance to incorporate the geometry of the token space, thereby enabling semantic-aware policy regularization. Through a tractable dual formulation, WPR integrates a semantic-aware penalty into standard RL algorithms like PPO. Empirical evaluations demonstrate that WPR consistently outperforms KL- and f-divergence-based baselines across summarization, dialogue generation, and code generation tasks, yielding improved win rates and MT-Bench scores. This work marks a significant step towards more stable, robust, and semantically coherent LLM alignment.

### 2. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The rapid advancements in Large Language Models (LLMs) have led to their widespread adoption across diverse applications, from conversational AI to content generation. A paramount challenge in deploying these powerful models is "alignment"—ensuring their behavior adheres to human preferences, such as helpfulness, harmlessness, and factual accuracy.

The dominant paradigm for achieving this alignment is **Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)**. Introduced by Christiano et al. (2017) and popularized by models like InstructGPT (Ouyang etal., 2022) and ChatGPT (Achiam et al., 2023), RLHF involves three key steps:
1. **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT):** Initial training on human-curated data to provide a baseline policy.
2. **Reward Model Training:** A separate model is trained on human preference data (e.g., pairwise comparisons of LLM outputs) to learn a scalar reward function that reflects human preferences.
3. **Policy Optimization:** The LLM's policy is then fine-tuned using reinforcement learning (typically Proximal Policy Optimization, PPO, Schulman et al., 2017) to maximize the reward from the reward model.

A crucial component of this policy optimization step is **regularization**, specifically using the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence. The KL divergence term, usually $D_{KL}(\pi_{\theta} || \pi_{ref})$, penalizes deviations of the learned policy ($\pi_{\theta}$) from the initial SFT-trained reference policy ($\pi_{ref}$). This regularization is vital for preventing policy collapse, maintaining diversity, and ensuring that the model does not "over-optimize" for the reward model at the expense of general language capabilities or safety.

However, the paper highlights a significant limitation of KL divergence and its variants, such as those used in f-DPO (Wang et al., 2024) and $\chi$PO (Huang et al., 2025). These "f-divergences" are **semantically unaware**. They quantify the discrepancy between two probability distributions by comparing probabilities at *identical token indices*. For instance, if a reference policy prefers "cat" and a learned policy prefers "kitten," KL divergence would assign a large penalty, treating these two semantically close tokens as entirely distinct. This token-level, index-based comparison fails to capture the rich semantic relationships inherent in natural language. This limitation can lead to suboptimal alignment, reduced output diversity, and potentially hinder the model's ability to explore semantically relevant but lexically different responses.

The **Wasserstein distance** (also known as Earth Mover's Distance) offers a promising alternative. Unlike f-divergences, Wasserstein distance incorporates a "cost" of moving probability mass between elements of the support, inherently considering the "geometry" of the underlying space. While Wasserstein distance has seen successful applications in various machine learning domains—such as generative adversarial networks (WGANs, Arjovsky et al., 2017), robust optimization (Sinha et al., 2018), and even other areas of reinforcement learning (Moskovitz et al., 2021; Song et al., 2023)—its application as a *semantic-aware policy regularization* directly within the RLHF framework for LLM alignment has been underexplored.

This work directly addresses this gap, positioning itself as a novel approach that moves beyond the semantic limitations of traditional regularization techniques in RLHF, aiming to achieve more sophisticated and human-like alignment by integrating semantic understanding directly into the policy optimization process.

### 3. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core objectives and motivations behind this research are clearly articulated:

**Motivation:**
1. **Semantic Blindness of Current Regularization:** The primary motivation stems from the inherent flaw of KL divergence and its f-divergence variants in RLHF. These methods compare token probabilities based solely on their indices within the vocabulary, ignoring any semantic relationships between tokens. As illustrated by the "cat, kitten, dog, table" example (Figure 1), traditional divergences treat "kitten" (semantically close to "cat") and "table" (semantically distant) as equally distinct from "cat" simply because their token indices differ. This semantic blindness prevents LLMs from being adequately penalized for generating semantically distant tokens or, conversely, from being rewarded for generating semantically similar alternatives.
2. **Suboptimal Alignment and Reduced Diversity:** By not capturing semantic similarity, existing regularization can lead to suboptimal alignment with human preferences, especially in scenarios where humans implicitly accept semantically related but lexically different responses. It can also restrict output diversity by overly penalizing any deviation from the reference policy, even if those deviations are semantically coherent and desirable.
3. **Computational Tractability Challenge:** While Wasserstein distance is theoretically appealing for its semantic awareness, its direct computation for high-dimensional discrete distributions (like LLM vocabularies) can be computationally intensive, posing a practical barrier to its integration into RLHF.

### 4. Key Objectives

1. **Develop a Semantic-Aware Policy Regularization Framework:** The central objective is to propose a novel regularization framework, named Wasserstein Policy Regularization (WPR), that explicitly incorporates semantic similarity between tokens using the Wasserstein distance.
2. **Ensure Computational Tractability:** To overcome the computational challenges, the authors aim to leverage the entropy-regularized Wasserstein distance (Sinkhorn distance) and reformulate its dual problem such that the regularization penalty is tractable and compatible with standard RL algorithms like PPO.
3. **Demonstrate Empirical Superiority:** The research aims to empirically validate that WPR outperforms existing KL- and f-divergence-based regularization methods in various LLM alignment tasks (summarization, dialogue generation, code generation) based on metrics that reflect human preference.
4. **Analyze the Impact of Semantic Awareness:** To provide deeper insights, the authors seek to analyze how the Wasserstein penalty behaves, whether it correlates with semantic similarity measures, and how it influences the semantic coherence of generated outputs.

In essence, the authors aim to introduce a more sophisticated and "intelligent" regularization mechanism for RLHF that aligns LLMs not just at the lexical level, but at the semantic level, leading to more human-preferred and contextually appropriate generations.

### 5. Methodology and Approach

The proposed **Wasserstein Policy Regularization (WPR)** method fundamentally modifies the policy optimization objective in RLHF by replacing traditional f-divergence-based regularization with a semantic-aware Wasserstein distance.

#### 5.1. RLHF Objective with Wasserstein Policy Regularization

The standard RLHF objective (Eq. 5) is:
$max_{\pi_{\theta}} J(\pi_{\theta}; \pi_{ref}) := E_{x \sim D} [E_{y \sim \pi_{\theta}(y|x)} [r(x, y)] - \beta D(\pi_{\theta}(y|x)||\pi_{ref}(y|x))]$
where $D$ is a policy divergence, and $\beta$ is a regularization hyperparameter.

The core of WPR is to replace the term $D(\pi_{\theta}(y|x)||\pi_{ref}(y|x))$ with an entropy-regularized Wasserstein distance, specifically the Sinkhorn distance. The objective becomes (Eq. 10):
$max_{\pi_{\theta}} J_{\tilde{W}}(\pi_{\theta}; \pi_{ref}) := E_{x \sim D} [ \sum_{n=1}^{N} E_{y_n \sim \pi_{\theta}(y_n|x,y_{1:n-1})} [R(x, y_{1:n})] - \beta \sum_{n=1}^{N} D_{\lambda}^{\tilde{W}} (\pi_{\theta}(y_n|x, y_{1:n-1})||\pi_{ref}(y_n|x, y_{1:n-1})) ]$
where $D_{\lambda}^{\tilde{W}}$ denotes the entropy-regularized Wasserstein (Sinkhorn) distance between the next-token distributions of the learned policy $\pi_{\theta}$ and the reference policy $\pi_{ref}$, conditioned on the prompt $x$ and previous tokens $y_{1:n-1}$. The parameter $\lambda$ controls the strength of entropy regularization.

The Sinkhorn distance itself is defined as a minimization problem (Eq. 11):
$D_{\lambda}^{\tilde{W}}(\pi||\pi') := min_{P \in U(\pi,\pi')} [ \langle P, C \rangle - \frac{1}{\lambda} H(P) ]$
where $P$ is a coupling matrix representing the transport plan between distributions $\pi$ and $\pi'$, $C$ is a cost matrix, and $H(P)$ is an entropy regularization term.

The crucial insight lies in reformulating this primal optimization problem into its dual form. As proven in Theorem 2, the entropic Wasserstein-regularized RLHF objective can be equivalently expressed as:
$J_{\tilde{W}}(\pi_{\theta}; \pi_{ref}) = E_{x \sim D} [ \sum_{n=1}^{N} E_{y_n \sim \pi_{\theta}(y_n|x,y_{1:n-1})} [R(x, y_{1:n}) - \beta \phi^{*}_{y_n}(x, y_{1:n-1})] ] + C'$
where $\phi^{*}_{y_n}$ are the optimal dual variables (Lagrange multipliers) associated with the row marginal constraints of the optimal transport problem, and $C'$ is a constant with respect to $\pi_{\theta}$. This elegant reformulation means that the complex Wasserstein distance term effectively becomes a *token-wise penalty* applied to the reward, analogous to how KL regularization is handled in PPO by adding $\beta \log(\pi_{\theta}/\pi_{ref})$ to the reward. This makes WPR directly compatible with standard RL algorithms like PPO.

#### 5.2. Computation of Wasserstein Penalty

The key to implementing WPR is efficiently computing the optimal dual variables $\phi^*$. According to Proposition 1, these dual variables are related to scaling vectors $u$ and $v$ that characterize the optimal transport plan. These vectors can be efficiently obtained using the **Sinkhorn-Knopp algorithm** (Algorithm 1). The Sinkhorn-Knopp algorithm iteratively normalizes the rows and columns of an exponentiated cost matrix ($K = \exp(-\lambda C)$) to match the target marginal distributions ($\pi_{\theta}$ and $\pi_{ref}$). Once $u$ is computed, $\phi^*$ is directly obtained as $\phi^* = - \frac{1}{\lambda} \log(u)$.

The **cost matrix C** is central to semantic awareness. It defines the "cost" of transforming one token into another. In this work, $C_{ij}$ is defined as the Euclidean distance between the fixed token embeddings of token $i$ and token $j$ from the SFT model. This allows WPR to naturally encode semantic similarity: tokens that are semantically closer in the embedding space will have lower transport costs.

#### 5.3. Practical Considerations (Truncations)

To mitigate the computational overhead of the Sinkhorn-Knopp algorithm, which typically scales as $O(d^2)$ with vocabulary size $d$, two forms of truncation are applied:
1. **Nearest-$k_1$ Truncation for Cost Matrix:** During pre-computation of the cost matrix $C$, only distances to the $k_1$ nearest neighbors are computed for each token. All other entries in $C$ are effectively set to infinity (or zero in the exponentiated kernel $K = \exp(-\lambda C)$), yielding a sparse kernel matrix and enabling efficient sparse matrix multiplications. This reduces memory and computation.
2. **Top-$k_2$ Truncation for Token Distributions:** During each Sinkhorn iteration, the probability distributions $\pi_{\theta}$ and $\pi_{ref}$ are truncated to their top-$k_2$ most probable indices, along with the actually sampled token index. The remaining probability mass is aggregated into a dummy index. This effectively reduces the support size from $d$ to at most $2k_2+2$, reducing the computational complexity from $O(d^2)$ to $O(k_2^2)$.

These truncations significantly reduce the computational cost, allowing the Sinkhorn-Knopp algorithm to run with only a modest 2.5% increase in training time per step compared to standard KL regularization. The full RLHF training algorithm with WPR is provided in Algorithm 2 in the appendix.

### 6. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrate that Wasserstein Policy Regularization (WPR) consistently outperforms traditional regularization methods across various LLM tasks and model architectures.

#### 6.1. Quantitative Performance

- **Overall Superiority:** As shown in Table 1 and Figure 2, WPR achieves the best win rates against both SFT-only models and RKL-regularized models on both the TL;DR summarization dataset and the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless (HH-RLHF) dialogue generation dataset. For example, on TL;DR, WPR obtains a win rate of 0.924 vs. SFT and 0.608 vs. RKL, significantly higher than all f-divergence baselines. Similar trends are observed for HH-RLHF (0.852 vs. SFT, 0.616 vs. RKL).
- **Stability of WPR vs. f-divergences:** The paper highlights that f-divergence-based methods often exhibit unstable training due to exploding probability ratios, leading to poor performance (e.g., FKL and TV on TL;DR). WPR, by contrast, remains well-defined even with support mismatch, ensuring stable training and robust performance.
- **MT-Bench Score:** WPR also achieves the highest MT-Bench score (4.272) among all baselines (Table 4), indicating improved broader conversational and instruction-following abilities, validating its effectiveness beyond specific task metrics.
- **Code Generation:** In a different domain, code generation on the APPS dataset (Table 5), WPR consistently improves both reward and pass@1 metrics across different difficulty levels (Introductory, Interview, Competition), further demonstrating its general applicability.

#### 6.2. Robustness and Generalization

- **Larger Models:** WPR scales effectively to larger models. On Gemma-7B for TL;DR (Table 2), WPR continues to outperform RKL-regularized PPO.
- **Different Architectures:** WPR's effectiveness generalizes to different LLM architectures, as demonstrated by its improved performance over RKL on Qwen-1.5-1.8B-Chat for HH-RLHF (Table 3).
- **Embedding Space Sensitivity:** An analysis (Table 12 in Appendix D.1) shows that while WPR benefits from higher-quality embeddings (e.g., Gemma-7B embeddings for a Gemma-7B policy), using embeddings from a larger model for a smaller policy does not necessarily yield better results than using the policy's own SFT embeddings. This suggests the importance of using embeddings aligned with the policy's internal representation.

#### 6.3. Analysis of Wasserstein Policy Regularization

- **Ablation Study (Table 6):**
 - **Cost Function:** Changing the cost function from Euclidean to cosine distance yields slightly better results, suggesting WPR is robust to the choice of metric, as long as it captures semantic similarity.
 - **Truncation Parameters ($k_1, k_2$):** Decreasing $k_1$ (nearest neighbors for cost matrix) or $k_2$ (top-k truncation for distributions) slightly reduces performance, but WPR still outperforms RKL. This indicates a trade-off between computational efficiency and approximation accuracy. $k_2=128$ was found to be optimal (Table 13).
 - **Entropy Regularization Strength ($\lambda$):** Decreasing $\lambda$ leads to a performance drop, as smaller $\lambda$ values produce overly soft transport plans, diminishing the semantic influence. Conversely, excessively large values can cause numerical instability. $\lambda=100$ was found to be a good balance (Table 14).
 - **Sinkhorn Iterations:** Insufficient iterations (e.g., 5) severely degrade performance, while excessive iterations (e.g., 30) offer no additional benefit over 10 iterations, demonstrating a sweet spot for convergence.
- **Sensitivity to $\beta$ (Figure 4):** WPR shows stable performance across a broader range of the policy regularization hyperparameter $\beta$ compared to f-divergence methods, indicating greater robustness. However, it still requires appropriate tuning of $\beta$.
- **Wasserstein Penalty Behavior (Figure 5, Table 7, Figure 6, Table 8):**
 - **Correlation with KL Penalty:** The Wasserstein penalty exhibits a strong positive correlation (Pearson coefficient of 0.917) with the KL penalty, showing that both increase with deviation from the reference policy. However, the Wasserstein penalty tends to be more "lenient" (slope of 0.579), suggesting it provides a smoother, less punitive regularization.
 - **Semantic Alignment:** The Wasserstein penalty shows a stronger positive correlation with BERTScore (Table 7), providing quantitative evidence that it better reflects semantic similarity.
 - **Qualitative Analysis (Figure 6):** Case studies reveal that WPR assigns minimal penalty for semantically similar token substitutions ("fairs" vs. "fair") where KL assigns a large penalty due to exact index mismatch. Conversely, WPR correctly assigns a large penalty when probability mass shifts towards semantically unrelated tokens, indicating its ability to prevent semantic drift.
 - **Semantic Coherence:** WPR consistently produces more semantically coherent sets of top-10 next-token candidates (smaller mean pairwise embedding distance) compared to RKL (Table 8), further validating its semantic awareness.

In summary, the results convincingly demonstrate that WPR effectively addresses the semantic blindness of previous RLHF regularization methods, leading to superior and more semantically coherent LLM outputs.

### 7. Significance and Potential Impact

This work makes a significant contribution to the field of Large Language Model alignment, with several important implications and potential impacts:

1. **Advancing LLM Alignment Beyond Lexical Similarity:** The most significant contribution of WPR is its novel integration of semantic awareness into the RLHF policy regularization framework. By moving beyond mere token-level probability comparisons, WPR enables LLMs to be aligned with human preferences in a more nuanced and "intelligent" way, respecting the underlying semantic structure of language. This represents a substantial leap from traditional KL-divergence-based methods, which are inherently semantically blind.

2. **More Robust and Stable Training:** The empirical results consistently show WPR outperforming f-divergence methods, which often suffer from training instability due to exploding probability ratios. WPR's well-defined nature, even under support mismatch, contributes to more stable and robust fine-tuning, a critical factor for deploying reliable LLMs.

3. **Improved Output Quality and Coherence:** The enhanced semantic awareness leads to LLMs that generate responses more aligned with human intent. The improved win rates on summarization and dialogue tasks, higher MT-Bench scores, and better performance in code generation all point towards a general improvement in the quality, helpfulness, and coherence of LLM outputs. The analysis showing that WPR produces more semantically coherent token candidates further reinforces this.

4. **Practical Tractability of Optimal Transport:** The paper successfully tackles the computational challenge of applying Wasserstein distance to high-dimensional LLM vocabularies. By leveraging the entropy-regularized Sinkhorn distance and its dual formulation, combined with efficient truncation strategies, the authors demonstrate that semantic-aware regularization can be incorporated with only a modest increase in computational overhead, making it practical for real-world LLM training.

5. **New Avenues for Policy Regularization:** This work opens up new research directions for exploring other Integral Probability Metrics (IPMs) and cost functions within the RLHF framework. The flexibility in defining the cost matrix `C` allows for customization based on specific semantic needs or domain knowledge, potentially leading to even more fine-grained control over LLM behavior.

6. **Broader Impact on Generative AI:** Beyond LLM alignment, the principles demonstrated by WPR could inspire similar semantic-aware regularization techniques in other generative AI models (e.g., image generation, multimodal models) where capturing the "distance" or "cost" in a rich feature space is crucial for improving generation quality and alignment with desired properties.

While the paper acknowledges the remaining challenge of selecting the policy regularization hyperparameter $\beta$, the overall impact of WPR is profound. It provides a robust, efficient, and semantically sophisticated mechanism for aligning LLMs, pushing the boundaries of what is achievable in controlling and refining the behavior of these powerful AI systems.
