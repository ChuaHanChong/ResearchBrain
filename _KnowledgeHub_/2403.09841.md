# MultiGripperGrasp: A Dataset for Robotic Grasping from Parallel Jaw Grippers to Dexterous Hands

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the University of Texas at Dallas developed MultiGripperGrasp, a dataset with 30.4 million physically-verified and ranked grasps for 11 diverse robotic grippers across 345 objects. This resource enables generalized grasp planning and demonstrates effective grasp transfer, providing a continuous measure of grasp quality through object fall-off time.

## Problem

- Most existing robotic grasping datasets are specific to a single gripper, which limits the transferability of learned grasping models.
- Current multi-gripper datasets offer limited diversity in gripper morphologies, restricting the study of generalized grasping principles across different robot hands.
- Many datasets provide only binary success/failure labels for grasps, lacking a nuanced, continuous measure of grasp quality or robustness.

## Method

- Constructed **MultiGripperGrasp**, a large-scale dataset featuring 30.4 million grasps using 345 3D objects and 11 diverse robotic grippers, ranging from 2-finger parallel-jaw to 5-finger dexterous hands.
- Generated initial grasp proposals using **GraspIt!** and then verified and ranked their stability using **NVIDIA Isaac Sim**, recording ==object fall-off time== as a continuous quality metric.
- Implemented a ==fixed palm pose convention== for all 11 grippers to enable standardized grasp pose alignment and facilitate ==grasp transfer== between different morphologies.

## Results

- The MultiGripperGrasp dataset contains **30.4 million** grasps: **43.3%** were highly successful (object held > 3s), **49.2%** failed quickly (0-1s), and **5.2%** failed to establish contact.
- The Robotiq 3-finger gripper demonstrated the highest success rate, with **76.09%** of its grasps lasting over 3 seconds, while highly dexterous hands like the Shadow Hand (**4.37%**) and Human Hand (**13.56%**) showed lower success rates due to controller limitations.
- Grasp transfer experiments effectively augmented successful grasps for target grippers, such as increasing the H5 Hand's successful grasps from **24K** to an additional **598K** through transfers from other grippers.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Grasp transfer across diverse gripper morphologies is feasible and effective for augmenting datasets, particularly for grippers that struggle with initial automated grasp generation.
> - A simple position controller proves sub-optimal for highly articulated dexterous hands, indicating a need for more sophisticated control strategies to fully utilize their capabilities.
> - Providing continuous grasp quality metrics (object fall-off time) offers richer information for learning robust grasp evaluators compared to binary success/failure labels.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of generalized and robust grasping methods is a fundamental challenge in robotics, crucial for autonomous object manipulation. The research landscape for grasp planning has evolved significantly, shifting from purely analytical approaches to increasingly data-driven, learning-based methodologies. This paper positions itself squarely within this evolving landscape, specifically addressing critical data scarcity and diversity issues that impede the progress of learning-based methods.

Traditionally, grasp planning relied on analytical techniques that calculated grasp quality based on principles like task wrench space analysis or force closure. While theoretically sound, these methods typically require full 3D geometric information of the target object, making them impractical for real-world scenarios where only partial observations (e.g., from cameras or depth sensors) are available.

The advent of machine learning, especially deep neural networks, has revolutionized grasp synthesis. Learning-based approaches can predict suitable gripper configurations directly from sensory inputs, allowing robots to generalize to novel objects. However, the success of these methods is heavily contingent on the availability of large-scale, high-quality datasets for training.

The existing landscape of grasping datasets, as summarized in Table I of the paper, reveals several limitations that MultiGripperGrasp aims to overcome:
- **Gripper Specificity:** Most prominent datasets, such as ACRONYM for parallel-jaw grippers or DexGraspNet for the Shadow hand, are constructed for a *single specific gripper*. This severely limits the transferability of learned models, meaning a model trained on one dataset cannot be easily applied to a different robot hand. This creates a significant hurdle for developing truly generalized grasping capabilities.
- **Limited Gripper Diversity:** While some datasets like UniGrasp, Fast-Grasp’D, and GenDexGrasp have ventured into multi-gripper territory, they typically offer a constrained range (e.g., UniGrasp has 12 grippers but none with more than 3 fingers; others have only 3 to 5 grippers). This lack of broad diversity, particularly across the spectrum from simple two-finger grippers to complex dexterous hands, restricts the study of how grasping principles generalize across different morphologies.
- **Binary Grasp Labels:** Many existing datasets only provide binary labels—either a grasp is successful or it fails. This provides limited information about the *quality* or *robustness* of a successful grasp. Learning models that can differentiate between a marginally stable grasp and a highly robust one requires more nuanced quality metrics.

MultiGripperGrasp directly addresses these gaps by introducing a dataset with an unprecedented diversity of 11 grippers, ranging from simple parallel-jaw types to highly dexterous five-finger hands, including a human hand model. Furthermore, it goes beyond binary success/failure by providing a *ranked* grasp quality metric (object fall-off time) and incorporates explicit support for *grasp transfer* through aligned gripper poses. By tackling these fundamental limitations, this work significantly pushes the frontier toward developing generalized, robust, and transferable grasping skills for a wide array of robotic manipulators.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for introducing the MultiGripperGrasp dataset stems directly from the recognized shortcomings in the existing robotic grasping datasets, as highlighted in the broader research landscape. The authors identify a critical need for a dataset that supports the development of more generalized and robust learning-based grasping methods, especially those capable of operating across diverse robotic gripper morphologies.

The key objectives of this research, as explicitly stated and implied throughout the introduction and contributions section, are as follows:

1. **To Construct a Large-Scale, Diverse Gripper Grasping Dataset:** The overarching goal is to create "MultiGripperGrasp," a substantial dataset comprising 30.4 million grasps. This dataset is designed to feature an extensive and unprecedented diversity of 11 robotic grippers, encompassing the entire spectrum from simple two-finger parallel jaw grippers to complex five-finger dexterous hands, notably including a human hand model. This diversity is crucial for moving beyond gripper-specific solutions. The dataset also includes a considerable set of 345 different 3D objects, aiming for varied interaction scenarios.
2. **To Provide Ranked Grasp Quality Metrics Beyond Binary Labels:** A significant objective is to enhance the utility of grasp data by providing a more granular measure of grasp quality. Instead of merely labeling grasps as "successful" or "failed," the authors aim to rank grasps based on their stability. This is achieved by recording the "object fall-off time" in a physics simulator (Isaac Sim) for each grasp. This ranked quality measurement is intended to enable learning models that can not only predict grasp success but also quantify and optimize for grasp robustness.
3. **To Facilitate Grasp Transfer Across Different Grippers:** A core objective is to enable the study and implementation of "grasp transfer," which involves transferring grasping strategies or poses learned for one gripper to another. To achieve this, the authors aim to align all 11 grippers within the dataset according to a consistent "palm pose convention." This standardized alignment is designed to be a crucial enabler for skill sharing, allowing for the augmentation of successful grasps for new grippers and fostering research into cross-functional skill learning between diverse robotic hands.

In essence, MultiGripperGrasp is motivated by the desire to provide a foundational dataset that supports advanced research in generalized grasping, grasp quality prediction, and inter-gripper grasp transfer, thereby accelerating the development of more versatile and capable autonomous robotic manipulation systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed for creating the MultiGripperGrasp dataset involves a systematic pipeline that combines an initial grasp generation tool with a high-fidelity physics simulator for verification and quality ranking. The process is outlined in Fig. 2 and detailed in Section III of the paper.

**A. Dataset Generation Pipeline Overview**
The core pipeline consists of three main stages:
1. **Object and Gripper Setup:** Defining the collection of 3D objects and robotic grippers to be used.
2. **Initial Grasp Generation:** Using a specialized software (GraspIt!) to generate a large pool of candidate grasps for each object-gripper pair.
3. **Grasp Labeling, Filtering, and Ranking:** Employing a physics simulator (Isaac Sim) to physically verify each candidate grasp, filtering out unsuccessful ones, and ranking the successful ones based on their stability.

**B. Objects and Grippers**
- **Object Set:** The dataset leverages a combination of 329 objects from the GoogleScannedObjects dataset, chosen from the FewSOL dataset for their relevance to robotic settings and everyday items. Additionally, 16 popular objects from the YCB object set were included, totaling 345 unique 3D objects. For simulation purposes, a fixed density of 100 kg/m^3 and friction coefficients of 0.5 were assigned to all objects due to the unavailability of ground truth physical properties.
- **Gripper Set:** A diverse collection of 11 robotic grippers was chosen, meticulously covering a wide range of dexterity:
 - **2-finger grippers:** Fetch, Franka Panda, WSG50, Sawyer, H5 Hand (5 grippers)
 - **3-finger grippers:** Barrett, Robotiq-3F, Jaco Robot (3 grippers)
 - **4-finger grippers:** Allegro (1 gripper)
 - **5-finger grippers:** Shadow, Human Hand (2 grippers)
 For each gripper, contact regions on the fingers and palm were manually specified, which is necessary for the initial grasp generation phase.
- **Gripper Alignment:** A crucial aspect for grasp transfer, all grippers were aligned using a fixed grasp pose convention for their palms. This involved defining a canonical translation (palm center) and orientation (fixed palm direction), allowing for the standardized transformation of grasp poses between different gripper types. For collision checking in Isaac Sim, convex decompositions of the gripper meshes were utilized.

**C. Grasp Generation**
- **Tool:** GraspIt!, a widely used grasp simulator, was selected for its ease of integrating new grippers and its memory efficiency.
- **Process:** For each object-gripper pair, GraspIt! was used to generate approximately 8,000 candidate grasps. This involved sampling numerous 6-DOF gripper poses around the object and optimizing a contact-based energy function using a simulated annealing algorithm. The goal was to generate a diverse set of initial grasp proposals.

**D. Grasp Labeling, Filtering, and Ranking with Isaac Sim**
This stage is central to the dataset's unique contributions, moving beyond simple geometric analysis to physics-based verification and nuanced quality assessment.
- **Simulator:** NVIDIA Isaac Sim, a high-fidelity physics simulator, was employed to verify the physical stability of each candidate grasp.
- **Procedure:**
 1. A grasp is initialized in Isaac Sim by placing the gripper with its palm normal aligned with the direction of gravity, and the object positioned relative to the gripper according to the generated grasp pose. This setup ensures gravity acts as an adversarial force.
 2. A custom controller (described below) gradually closes the gripper fingers.
 3. Upon establishing contact between the gripper and the object, gravity is fully applied, and a timer begins.
 4. **Stability Measure (Object Fall-off Time):** The timer records the duration until the object falls. A grasp is deemed stable if the object remains held for the maximum test time (3 seconds). An object is considered to have fallen if it loses contact with the gripper fingers or if its current pose deviates by more than 0.5 meters from its initial position.
 5. **Ranking:** Grasps are ranked according to their recorded fall-off time, with longer times indicating higher stability and quality. Grasps where the gripper could not establish contact with the object were assigned a negative fall-off time and classified as failed. This approach allows for the creation of "hard negative examples" (grasps that geometrically seemed plausible but fail quickly), which are valuable for training robust learning models.

**E. Controller Design**
Given the large number and diversity of grippers, a simple yet effective position controller was designed.
- **Grasp Ranking Control:** For GraspIt!-generated grasps, the controller moves the root joints of the gripper fingers to pre-specified closing positions, attempting to establish a stable grip.
- **Grasp Transfer Control:** For transferred grasps, the target gripper is first moved to an open configuration. It then gradually closes all fingers until contact with the object is made. Subsequently, only the root joints of the fingers are moved to their closing positions. This simplified control strategy was implemented to be broadly applicable across all 11 grippers, acknowledging that a universal, optimal controller for highly articulated hands remains a significant challenge.

This meticulous methodology ensures that the MultiGripperGrasp dataset provides a comprehensive, physically verified, and ranked collection of grasps, specifically designed to advance research in generalized and transferable robotic grasping.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted to build the MultiGripperGrasp dataset yielded extensive data on grasp stability and demonstrably validated the concept of grasp transfer.

**A. Grasp Ranking Results**
The core result of the dataset generation is a massive collection of 30.4 million grasps, each with a recorded object fall-off time, distributed across 3,795 unique object-gripper pairs.
- **Overall Grasp Distribution:**
 - **Highly Successful Grasps (Fall-off > 3s):** 13.16 million (approximately 43.3% of all grasps). These are robust grasps that withstood gravity for the maximum test duration.
 - **Moderately Stable Grasps (2s to 3s):** 0.17 million.
 - **Less Stable Grasps (1s to 2s):** 0.53 million.
 - **Quickly Failing Grasps (0s to 1s):** 14.97 million (approximately 49.2%). These are grasps where the object fell shortly after contact, representing valuable "hard negative examples" for machine learning.
 - **Failed Grasps (No Contact):** 1.59 million (approximately 5.2%). These grasps were unable to even establish contact with the object.

- **Gripper-Specific Performance (Table II):**
 - **Top Performer:** The Robotiq 3-finger gripper demonstrated the highest success rate, with 76.09% of its grasps lasting over 3 seconds, and an exceptionally low failure rate of only 0.06%. This highlights the robust design and effective grasping capabilities of this commercial gripper type.
 - **Two-Finger Grippers:** Generally performed well, with Fetch (69.42%), Franka (70.17%), and WSG50 (61.29%) showing high success rates. Sawyer had a respectable 48.15%, while the H5 Hand performed poorly at 1.18%.
 - **Dexterous Hands (Low Success Rates):** The H5 Hand (2-finger, but likely more complex joint structure), Shadow Hand (5-finger, 4.37%), and the Human Hand (5-finger, 13.56%) exhibited significantly lower success rates for grasps generated by GraspIt! and evaluated by the simple position controller.
 - **Reasons for Lower Performance:** The authors attribute the H5 Hand's low performance to GraspIt!'s difficulties with its "mimic joints," leading to sub-optimal initial grasp proposals. For the Shadow and Human hands, the sub-optimal performance is largely blamed on the simplicity of the position controller, which struggles with highly articulated grippers. Complex joint configurations, particularly of the thumbs, were observed to sometimes create stable grasps while at other times generating adversarial forces. This finding underscores the challenge of designing universal controllers for highly dexterous manipulators.

**B. Grasp Transfer Results**
The grasp transfer experiments demonstrated the practical utility of gripper alignment and the potential to augment datasets for less capable grippers.
- **Process:** 500 successful grasps (fall-off > 3s) were sampled from each source object-gripper pair. These grasps were then transformed to a target gripper using the established pose alignment and re-evaluated in Isaac Sim using the target gripper's position controller.
- **Effectiveness (Table III):**
 - **Significant Augmentation:** Grasp transfer proved to be highly effective in generating new successful grasps for target grippers, especially those that performed poorly with original GraspIt! grasps. For instance, the H5 Hand, which only had 24K original successful grasps, gained an additional 598K successful grasps through transfers from other grippers. This highlights the power of shared grasping knowledge.
 - **Cross-Gripper Transferability:** Many successful grasps from one gripper translated into successful grasps for others. For example, the Franka Panda gripper received 561K successful transferred grasps. This validates the premise that grasping principles can, to a degree, generalize across different morphologies when properly aligned.
 - **Morphological Nuances:** While generally successful, the confusion matrix in Table III also revealed that not all transfers, even between seemingly similar grippers, resulted in success. The authors explain this by noting that factors like gripper reach, specific finger shapes, and sizes (morphology) are not explicitly accounted for in the simple pose alignment, leading to some transferred grasps being ill-suited for the target gripper. This points to avenues for future refinement in grasp transfer mechanisms.

In summary, the MultiGripperGrasp dataset successfully provides a large-scale, ranked, and diverse collection of grasps. The findings demonstrate the varied performance of different grippers under a standardized evaluation, highlighting both the strengths of robust commercial grippers and the challenges in controlling highly dexterous hands. Crucially, the experiments validate the effectiveness of grasp transfer as a method for enriching datasets and sharing skills across heterogeneous robotic platforms.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The MultiGripperGrasp dataset represents a significant contribution to the field of robotic manipulation, with far-reaching potential impacts on research and the development of autonomous systems. Its significance stems from directly addressing critical limitations in existing datasets and enabling novel avenues of research.

**A. Enabling Generalized Grasp Planning**
- **Cross-Morphology Learning:** By offering an unprecedented diversity of 11 grippers, MultiGripperGrasp is a crucial resource for developing and evaluating generalized grasp planning algorithms. Researchers can now train models that are not tethered to a single robot hand but can predict effective grasps for a broad spectrum of manipulators, from simple parallel jaw grippers to complex dexterous hands. This is a foundational step towards universal robotic grasping.
- **Reduced Development Time:** Generalized models can reduce the need to re-train or re-engineer grasp planners for every new gripper, significantly accelerating the deployment of robots in diverse applications.

**B. Advancing Grasp Quality Prediction**
- **Nuanced Understanding of Grasp Stability:** Moving beyond binary success/failure labels, the ranked object fall-off time provides a rich, continuous measure of grasp quality. This allows machine learning models to learn not just *if* a grasp is successful, but *how good* it is, enabling systems to prioritize more robust and stable grasps.
- **Hard Negative Mining:** The inclusion of numerous grasps that quickly fail (but were initially considered geometrically plausible) serves as excellent "hard negative examples." Training with these examples can make grasp evaluators more robust and discerning, improving their ability to identify truly stable grasps in complex scenarios.

**C. Facilitating Grasp Transfer and Skill Sharing**
- **Inter-Robot Skill Learning:** The carefully aligned gripper poses within the dataset are a direct enabler for research into grasp transfer. This allows for the investigation of how grasping skills and knowledge can be effectively shared between robots with different physical embodiments. This is paramount for creating more adaptable robots and for transferring skills learned in simulation or from human demonstrations to various real-world platforms.
- **Dataset Augmentation for Less Capable Grippers:** The experiments already demonstrate how grasp transfer can dramatically increase the number of successful grasps for grippers that initially struggled with automated generation. This offers a practical pathway to quickly populate datasets for new or less common grippers, reducing the expensive and time-consuming process of generating data from scratch.

**D. Catalyzing Further Research Directions**
- **Dexterous Hand Control:** The observed limitations of the simple position controller for highly articulated hands like the Shadow and Human Hand highlight a significant open research problem. This dataset can serve as a benchmark for developing more sophisticated, adaptive, or learning-based controllers that can exploit the full dexterity of multi-finger grippers.
- **Affordance-Driven Grasping:** While the current dataset focuses on geometry and physics, the authors acknowledge the future potential for "affordance-driven grasps." The diverse object set provides a good starting point for extending the dataset to incorporate semantic information, leading to more intelligent and task-oriented grasping.
- **Benchmarking and Reproducibility:** By making the data, code, and videos publicly available, the authors promote transparency, reproducibility, and collaborative research within the robotics community. This dataset can become a standard benchmark for comparing new grasp planning and transfer algorithms.

In conclusion, MultiGripperGrasp is more than just a large collection of data; it is a strategically designed resource that directly addresses key bottlenecks in generalized robotic manipulation. By providing diverse, ranked, and transferable grasp information, it is poised to significantly accelerate the development of more intelligent, versatile, and autonomous robotic systems capable of interacting with the physical world in increasingly sophisticated ways.
