# AutoTool: Automatic Scaling of Tool-Use Capabilities in RL via Decoupled Entropy Constraints

> [!summary] Summary
> AutoTool, a two-stage reinforcement learning framework from Harbin Institute of Technology and Huawei Technologies, enables large language models to automatically scale their tool-use capabilities, addressing challenges of reasoning collapse and overthinking. The method achieved an 11.95 percentage point accuracy improvement on the BFCL benchmark and reduced inference token costs by approximately 81% compared to distilled models.

## Problem

- Reinforcement learning (RL) models often exhibit 'reasoning collapse,' failing to extend their thinking length sufficiently for complex tool-use problems.
- Scaled-up models frequently engage in 'overthinking' on simpler problems, leading to significant token inefficiency and increased computational overhead.
- Current RL-based scaling approaches for agentic tool use lack adaptive mechanisms to dynamically adjust reasoning length based on problem difficulty.

## Method

- A two-stage training paradigm, **AutoTool**, was developed, starting with supervised fine-tuning (SFT) for basic difficulty perception, followed by reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR).
- Implemented ==decoupled== adaptive entropy constraints within the RL policy loss (GRPO), using separate fixed and adaptive entropy coefficients for short and long reasoning trajectories respectively, to regulate exploration.
- Designed an auto-thinking reward module that uses a rule-based system combining format rewards for structured reasoning modes and asymmetric answer rewards to balance efficiency and accuracy.

## Results

- AutoTool-**7B** improved overall accuracy by 11.95 percentage points on the BFCL benchmark compared to PubTool-SFT and by **16.43%** relative to the base Qwen2.5-**7B**-Instruct model.
- The method achieved an approximately **81%** reduction in inference token cost compared to a distilled model, demonstrating substantial computational efficiency.
- AutoTool successfully adapted its reasoning scale, with a **45%** thinking rate for complex multi-turn scenarios and **0%** for simple non-live scenarios, extending reasoning trajectories by approximately 5 times for complex questions.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Low information entropy in the actor model is strongly correlated with insufficient reasoning length and 'reasoning collapse,' highlighting the importance of entropy regulation for scaling capabilities.
> - Decoupling entropy constraints for different reasoning complexities (short vs. long trajectories) is crucial to prevent objective interference and enable stable adaptive scaling.
> - A carefully balanced and refined dataset, specifically targeting a diverse range of difficulties and high-signal instances, significantly improves the stability and effectiveness of RL training for adaptive tool use.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The integration of agentic large language models (LLMs) with external tools has been identified as a significant area of advancement, enhancing LLM capabilities in addressing complex tasks across various fields. This paradigm facilitates the automation of reasoning and supports diverse agent applications, positioning agentic tool use as a critical pathway toward artificial general intelligence. In this context, LLMs are designed to respond to queries by dynamically selecting and invoking relevant tools from an available pool.

A prevalent approach to improving LLM performance, known as Test-Time Scaling (TTS), involves utilizing additional computational resources at inference time to enhance reasoning capabilities. Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) has emerged as an effective method for achieving TTS by scaling up a model's explicit reasoning length. Unlike Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), which primarily imitates reasoning patterns from labeled examples, RLVR is reported to foster more intrinsic reasoning, demonstrating robustness in domains such as mathematics and coding. This shift from SFT to RL is recognized as a significant paradigm change in LLM training, underscoring the importance of developing suitable scaling strategies for agentic tool use.

However, the application of RLVR for scaling tool-use capabilities faces specific challenges. Prior work has indicated that direct RL training can lead to "reasoning collapse," where models fail to extend their thinking length sufficiently for complex problems. Conversely, scaled-up models often exhibit "overthinking" on simpler problems, resulting in inefficient resource consumption. While some recent research has explored automatic scaling to enable models to adaptively select optimal reasoning modes based on problem difficulty in other domains (e.g., mathematics), this specific approach has been largely unexplored within the context of agentic tool use. This work aims to address these identified gaps by proposing a novel method for automatic and efficient scaling of tool-use capabilities in RL.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a training paradigm that enables LLMs to automatically scale their tool-use capabilities in a computationally efficient manner, specifically addressing two identified challenges within current RL-based scaling approaches for agentic tool use:

1. **Insufficient Reasoning Length for Complex Problems:** Direct reinforcement learning (RL) training often struggles to scale up the explicit thinking length of models to adequately solve complex problems. This phenomenon, termed "reasoning collapse," limits the model's ability to perform robustly on intricate multi-turn or long-context tool-use scenarios. The researchers observed that in contrast to mathematical tasks where response length typically scales with accuracy, tool-use tasks trained with direct RL exhibited a decrease in response length as performance improved, indicating a failure to sufficiently extend reasoning trajectories.
2. **Token Inefficiency Due to Overthinking Simpler Problems:** Scaled-up models, including those trained via distillation from reasoning LLMs, tend to generate excessively long reasoning trajectories for problems that could be solved with shorter, more direct responses. This "overthinking" leads to substantial token inefficiency and increased computational overhead, consuming unnecessary resources. For example, distilled models showed minimal accuracy gains over base SFT models but incurred more than a tenfold increase in output token costs.

To address these challenges, the study is motivated by the need for an adaptive model that can dynamically integrate both short and long reasoning trajectories. Preliminary analyses highlighted that low information entropy within the actor model, which quantifies response certainty and exploration capability, correlates strongly with insufficient reasoning length and reasoning collapse. This suggested that maintaining appropriate entropy levels could be crucial for unlocking scaling capabilities.

Therefore, the key objectives are to:
- Develop a training paradigm that first helps models distinguish between simple and complex problems.
- Enable models to automatically determine appropriate reasoning trajectories, extending reasoning for complex tasks while maintaining conciseness for simple ones.
- Introduce an optimization objective, specifically entropy-based, to maintain model diversity and facilitate the scaling of thinking length.
- Implement a long-short reasoning fusion RL strategy, guided by entropy constraints, to achieve adaptive and efficient tool use.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, AutoTool, employs a novel two-stage training paradigm: an initial warm-up phase using supervised fine-tuning (SFT) to establish a basic understanding of problem difficulty, followed by reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) incorporating decoupled adaptive entropy constraints.

#### 3.1. Data Preparation and Warm-up

To facilitate robust and general-purpose tool use, a mixed dataset named `PubTool` was constructed from public sources: ToolACE, xLAM, and Hermes Function-Calling.
- **Data Collection and Downsampling:** Raw data from these sources were downsampled to balance sample sizes.
- **Difficulty Balancing:** The dataset's difficulty distribution was analyzed (e.g., using `pass@8` inference with a base model). The original data showed concentrations of overly simple (47%) and excessively difficult (31.8%) samples. To create a more balanced distribution, half of the simple and difficult samples were randomly removed, leading to a `PubTool` dataset where hard samples constituted 44.6% and easy samples 15.4%. This re-balancing aimed to provide more moderately difficult samples beneficial for learning test-time scaling.
- **Warm-up Training:** SFT was performed on a base model using a mixed dataset of long and short reasoning data. This mixed data was constructed by using a "no-thinking" model (Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct) for correct simple responses and a "thinking" model (Qwen3-32B) for correct responses requiring explicit long reasoning. An auto-thinking template was designed to guide the model in selecting reasoning modes based on perceived difficulty. This warm-up phase aimed to provide the model with an initial perception of data difficulty.
- **RL Data Refinement:** For the subsequent RL training, a refined subset of the data was used. Samples were prioritized based on their alignment with model learning trajectories, quantified by the variance of reward scores from the mean (lower variance indicating higher alignment). This process involved multi-epoch GRPO training on the full training data, calculating reward score changes, and then filtering out the bottom 55% of samples with higher variance. This refinement reduced the RL dataset from 21k to 7k samples, aiming to retain high-signal instances and improve training stability.

#### 3.2. Decoupled Adaptive Entropy Constraints

The core of the RLVR training involves a decoupled adaptive entropy constraint strategy integrated into the policy loss function, building upon GRPO. This strategy aims to regulate exploration differently for short and long reasoning trajectories and adaptively adjust the exploration strength for long trajectories.
- **Decoupled Entropy Constraints:** The policy loss `L_p` incorporates an entropy term `β_i * H_i`, where `H_i` is the entropy at step `i`. A crucial aspect is the introduction of an indicator variable `m_i`, which is 1 for short trajectories and 0 for long trajectories. This allows for separate entropy coefficients: `β_s` (fixed for short paths) and `β_l` (adaptive for long paths). The `β_i` term is only activated if the entropy `H_i` falls below a target entropy `H_s` (for short paths) or `H_l` (for long paths). This mechanism prevents excessive exploration in simple cases while ensuring sufficient exploration for complex ones.
- **Adaptive Entropy Coefficient Loss:** To address the sensitivity of pre-selected entropy coefficients, an adaptive loss `L_Lβ` is introduced for `β_l`. This loss dynamically adjusts `β_l` based on the deviation of the actual entropy `H_i` from the target entropy `H_l` for long trajectories. If `H_i < H_l`, `β_l` increases to encourage exploration; if `H_i > H_l`, it decreases to suppress excessive randomness. `β_s` remains fixed. This dynamic adjustment is designed to maintain exploration capacity for complex problems without requiring manual tuning.

#### 3.3. Auto Thinking Reward Module

A rule-based reward module evaluates the model's output to compute the estimated advantage for the policy loss. This module combines format and answer rewards.
- **Format Reward (`R_format`):** This binary reward (0 or 1) checks whether the model's output adheres to specific structural templates for two reasoning modes: `think` and `no-think`.
 - `[mode]think[/mode][think]reasoning process here[/think]answer` for complex problems.
 - `[mode]no_think[/mode][no_think]\n[/no_think]answer` for simple, direct generation.
 This design encourages explicit reasoning for complex tasks and direct responses for simple ones. Controllable reasoning modes during inference are enabled by prepending special tokens.
- **Answer Reward (`R_answer`):** An asymmetric reward structure is used to balance reasoning efficiency and accuracy.
 - `+1.0` if `o_i = y*` (correct) in `no-think` mode.
 - `+0.5` if `o_i = y*` (correct) in `think` mode.
 - `-0.5` if `o_i ≠ y*` (incorrect) in `think` mode.
 - `-1.0` if `o_i ≠ y*` (incorrect) in `no-think` mode.
 This asymmetry incentivizes short, correct responses and encourages longer reasoning when mistakes occur, promoting more careful processing in uncertain scenarios.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were conducted using the Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct model as a base and evaluated on three benchmarks: BFCL, API-Bank, and ACEBench.

#### 4.1. Overall Performance

- **Accuracy Improvement:** The AutoTool-7B model demonstrated substantial performance gains. Compared to the PubTool-SFT model, it achieved an 11.95 percentage point improvement in overall accuracy on the BFCL benchmark. Relative to the base Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct model, AutoTool-7B showed a 16.43% boost in overall accuracy (70.12% vs 53.69%).
- **Robustness in Complex Scenarios:** The model exhibited enhanced robustness in challenging scenarios, such as the Multi-Turn sub-metric of BFCL, where it achieved a 28.5% improvement compared to PubTool-SFT.
- **Comparison to Baselines:** AutoTool-7B outperformed most SFT-trained and RLVR-trained models on BFCL and showed comparable performance with frontier models. On API-Bank and ACEBench, it consistently demonstrated advantageous performance compared to baselines, achieving a 6.5 improvement over GRPO and 5.9 over Distilled on ACEBench.
- **Controllable Inference:** In controllable inference modes, forcing the model to "think" resulted in performance on par with automatic thinking. Forcing "no-think" significantly improved performance on Multi-turn tasks compared to models explicitly trained without thinking (e.g., PubTool-SFT).

#### 4.2. Ablation Study

An ablation study on the BFCL benchmark confirmed the criticality of each proposed component:
- **Data Refinement:** Removing data quality refinement led to the largest performance drop (6.43% overall reduction), highlighting its foundational role.
- **Adaptive Entropy Coefficient:** Removing the adaptive coefficient (using a fixed one) resulted in a 10.53% decline in Multi-turn performance, indicating its importance for stable multi-round interactions.
- **Decoupled Loss:** Removing the decoupling mechanism (using a unified loss) caused a 2.34% overall drop, suggesting that decoupling successfully avoids objective interference between reasoning modes.

#### 4.3. Inference Efficiency Analysis

- **Token Cost Reduction:** AutoTool achieved a significant reduction in inference token cost, cutting it by approximately 81% compared to the distilled model (requiring ~183 tokens versus ~966 tokens).
- **Accuracy per Computation Unit (ACU):** Using the ACU metric, which balances accuracy against parameters and tokens, AutoTool demonstrated excellent inference efficiency. In the forced "no-think" mode, it achieved the optimal ACU score of 0.97. Even in "think" mode, it delivered high accuracy while halving the token cost relative to the distilled model.
- **Adaptive Thinking Rate:** The model's adaptive nature was demonstrated by its thinking rates: 45% in complex Multi-Turn scenarios, but 0% in simple Non-Live scenarios. Visualizations confirmed that the model extended reasoning trajectories by approximately 5 times for complex questions while maintaining concise responses for simple ones, indicating automatic adjustment of test-time scale based on problem difficulty.

#### 4.4. Generalizability

The method's generalizability was tested across different model architectures (Llama 3.2-inst, Qwen 2.5-inst series) and sizes (1.5B to 32B parameters). AutoTool consistently improved performance across all tested configurations, with Qwen models showing substantial gains (over 12 percentage points for 3B-32B models, e.g., Qwen2.5-7B from 53.69% to 70.12%). Even compact 1.5B models showed significant lifts (approx. 8.5 points).

#### 4.5. Hyperparameter Sensitivity

Analysis showed that model performance was sensitive to target entropy settings, with optimal performance around H_s = 0.1 and H_l = 0.2. The model exhibited considerable stability with respect to the adaptive coefficient `β_l`, while lower values of the fixed coefficient `β_s` generally yielded better performance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work makes a contribution to the field of agentic LLMs by addressing critical challenges in scaling tool-use capabilities through reinforcement learning. The identified problems of reasoning collapse and token inefficiency in existing RL-based approaches represent significant hurdles for developing robust and economically viable AI agents.

The proposed decoupled adaptive entropy constraint strategy offers a practical solution that balances performance and computational efficiency. By enabling models to automatically adjust their reasoning scale based on problem difficulty, the research moves towards more intelligent and resource-aware agent behavior. The significant improvements in accuracy (e.g., +9.8% over distilled models) coupled with substantial reductions in computational overhead (approximately 81% token cost reduction) demonstrate the practical utility of the method. This could lead to:
- **More Efficient AI Agents:** Agents that can intelligently decide when to engage in complex reasoning versus providing direct answers, leading to lower inference costs and faster response times.
- **Enhanced Robustness:** Improved performance in complex, multi-turn tool-use scenarios, where previous methods often struggled with reasoning collapse.
- **Broader Adoption of RL for Tool Use:** The findings suggest that with appropriate training paradigms, RL can be effectively leveraged to scale tool-use capabilities, potentially opening avenues for further research into advanced agentic functions.

While the study presents promising results, the authors acknowledge limitations, including the need for further verification of scalability across an even broader range of model architectures and scales, evaluation of generalizability beyond tool-use tasks to other complex reasoning domains, and testing compatibility with additional RL algorithms beyond GRPO. Addressing these aspects in future work could further solidify the impact and applicability of this auto-scaling approach.
