# Yell At Your Robot: Improving On-the-Fly from Language Corrections

> [!summary] Summary
> This research introduces a hierarchical robot learning framework, Yell At Your Robot (YAY Robot), that enables real-time adaptation and continuous policy improvement from natural language corrections. The system demonstrated up to 50% increases in task success rates during human intervention and subsequent autonomous performance improvements of up to 45% across complex bimanual manipulation tasks.

## Problem

- Achieving high success rates for complex, long-horizon robotic tasks is challenging due to brittleness and compounding errors over many steps.
- Scalably acquiring high-quality training data for robust high-level policies, which are often costly or not sufficiently grounded for real robot behaviors.
- Enabling intuitive and accessible human intervention to correct robot failures and facilitate learning without requiring specialized interfaces or programming.

## Method

- A hierarchical learning architecture with a low-level language-conditioned behavior cloning (LCBC) policy for executing actions and a high-level policy for generating language instructions.
- An on-the-fly mechanism allowing human verbal commands to temporarily override the high-level policy's output, feeding corrections directly to the low-level policy for immediate adaptation.
- An iterative fine-tuning process, analogous to DAgger, where the high-level policy is retrained on a dataset augmented with human language corrections, leading to continuous autonomous improvement.

## Results

- On-the-fly language corrections significantly improved success rates by 25-**50%** in Bag Packing, 30-**45%** in Trail Mix Preparation, and 15-**25%** in Plate Cleaning tasks on real robots.
- Iterative fine-tuning with collected language corrections enhanced the autonomous policy's success rate by 20-**45%** in Bag Packing, 15-**20%** in Trail Mix, and 15-**25%** in Plate Cleaning.
- The hierarchical YAY Robot framework consistently outperformed flat imitation learning policies (ACT) on long-horizon tasks, demonstrating superior management of compounding errors.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Leveraging natural language as a real-time corrective feedback mechanism allows for scalable human-robot interaction and on-the-fly adaptation.
> - Training a learned high-level policy on human language corrections enables continuous improvement of autonomous robot behavior, outperforming pre-scripted or ungrounded VLM approaches.
> - Hierarchical policies with language-conditioned low-level skills effectively manage compounding errors in complex, long-horizon tasks, providing robustness over flat policy architectures.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research sits at the intersection of several rapidly evolving areas in robotics and artificial intelligence: hierarchical control, language-conditioned robot learning, imitation learning, human-robot interaction, and the deployment of large language models (LLMs) and vision-language models (VLMs) in embodied AI.

**Hierarchical Control for Long-Horizon Tasks:** The paper acknowledges the traditional challenge of performing complex, multi-stage robotic tasks. Hierarchical policies, where a high-level component plans abstract steps and a low-level component executes fine-grained actions, are a common paradigm. This work contributes to this area by proposing a novel method for *training* and *improving* the high-level policy in a scalable manner, specifically addressing the compounding error problem inherent in long-horizon tasks.

**Language as a Robotic Interface:** Natural language has emerged as a powerful and intuitive interface for instructing robots, enabling multi-task learning and generalization. The paper builds on this by employing language not only for initial task specification but, crucially, for *real-time corrective feedback*. This extends prior work on mapping language instructions to actions by demonstrating how fine-grained language can be used for on-the-fly adjustments and continuous learning.

**Imitation Learning and Continuous Improvement:** Behavior Cloning (BC) is a foundational imitation learning technique, often susceptible to distribution shift. The paper draws a direct parallel to DAgger (Dataset Aggregation) and its human-gated variant (HG-DAgger). While DAgger typically involves human intervention at the low-level action space (teleoperation), "Yell At Your Robot" innovatively applies this concept to the *high-level language instruction space*. This represents a significant advancement in interactive robot learning, moving beyond costly, low-level teleoperation to more natural and scalable language-based human supervision. It contrasts with works that focus on one-time corrections or post-hoc action relabeling by emphasizing *continuous improvement* through iterative fine-tuning.

**Addressing Brittle LLM/VLM Planning:** Recent advancements in LLMs and VLMs have enabled impressive high-level planning for robots. However, as the authors note, these models often lack grounding in robotic affordances and real-world physics, leading to brittle performance. This paper offers a complementary approach: instead of solely relying on pre-trained models for perfect planning, it proposes a mechanism for a robot to refine its high-level decision-making *through direct human feedback* on its actual performance. The ablation studies vividly demonstrate the limitations of ungrounded VLMs like GPT-4V for direct high-level policy control in complex manipulation, reinforcing the need for grounded learning from interaction.

**Human-Robot Interaction (HRI):** The core innovation lies in making human feedback intuitive and accessible. By leveraging natural language, the system bypasses the need for specialized interfaces or robotics expertise. This aligns with a broader trend in HRI research to enable non-expert users to effectively interact with and teach robots, paving the way for wider adoption of robotic systems in everyday environments. The paper's focus on "yelling" (or rather, speaking naturally) at the robot directly addresses a common frustration point in HRI: when a robot fails, how can a human quickly and easily provide a meaningful correction?

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind "Yell At Your Robot" stems from the persistent challenges in achieving high success rates for complex, long-horizon robotic tasks, particularly those involving dexterous manipulation in unstructured environments. While hierarchical policies and language conditioning offer promising frameworks, two critical bottlenecks remain:

1. **Brittleness and Compounding Errors:** As tasks become longer and more intricate, the probability of failure increases exponentially. Even minor errors at early stages can cascade, leading to complete task failure. Current high-level controllers, whether derived from large datasets or pre-trained models, often lack the robustness to dynamically compensate for low-level execution failures or make necessary adjustments on the fly.
2. **Scalability of High-Quality Training Data:** Training truly robust high-level policies that are aware of the physical intricacies and limitations of low-level skills typically requires expensive, time-consuming "gold standard" demonstrations of entire multi-stage tasks. While knowledge transfer from LLMs/VLMs offers scalability, this knowledge is often not sufficiently grounded in real robotic behaviors, leading to brittle performance. Similarly, other indirect supervision methods (language-only, human videos) also suffer from indirectness. The fundamental challenge is obtaining scalable and high-quality training data for robust high-level policies.

Given these challenges, the paper posits a crucial observation: if high-level policies can index into sufficiently rich and expressive low-level language-conditioned skills, they can be readily supervised with human feedback in the form of natural language corrections. This insight directly leads to the key objectives of the "YAY Robot" framework:

- **Objective 1: Enable On-the-Fly Adaptation from Language Corrections.** To allow robots to rapidly adjust their behavior in real-time based on immediate verbal feedback from a human observer. This includes incorporating even fine-grained corrections like "move a bit to the left." The motivation here is to provide an immediate recovery mechanism for failures during deployment.
- **Objective 2: Facilitate Continuous Improvement of Autonomous Policy from Language Feedback.** Beyond real-time adaptation, the goal is for the robot to *learn* from these human interventions. By incorporating the collected language correction data into an iterative training scheme, the high-level policy should continuously improve its ability to self-correct errors, both in low-level execution and its own high-level decision-making, purely from verbal feedback, reducing the need for perpetual human oversight.
- **Overarching Goal:** To develop a system that allows humans to teach robots complex, long-horizon tasks through natural, intuitive language interaction, thereby reducing the reliance on costly, expert-driven teleoperation and moving towards everyday robotic applications for non-expert users.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The "Yell At Your Robot" (YAY Robot) framework employs a hierarchical learning architecture designed for both real-time adaptation and continuous improvement from natural language feedback.

**A. Hierarchical Policy Structure:**
The system is built upon two core policies:
1. **Low-Level Language-Conditioned Behavior Cloning (LCBC) Policy ($\pi_L$):** This policy is responsible for executing precise, continuous robot actions based on visual observations and a given language instruction. It is implemented as a deep neural network, specifically using Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT) as its base architecture, known for its efficacy in dexterous manipulation. The visual backbone uses EfficientNet b3, and language inputs are processed via DistilBERT embeddings fused with visual features using FiLM layers. $\pi_L$ is pre-trained using standard Behavior Cloning on a diverse dataset covering a wide range of task-centric and corrective skills (e.g., "pour into the bag" as well as "move the left arm towards me"). During the post-training phase for continuous improvement, this low-level policy is kept frozen.
2. **High-Level Language Policy ($\pi_H$):** This policy's role is to autonomously generate sequential language instructions for the low-level $\pi_L$ to execute, thereby orchestrating long-horizon tasks. It takes a history of observations (RGB images and proprioceptive information) as input. The vision backbone is a Vision Transformer (ViT) initialized with pre-trained CLIP weights (which are frozen during training). The ViT output is then processed through Transformer and MLP layers to produce language embeddings. The policy is trained with a cross-entropy loss to predict the next appropriate language instruction, conditioned on a temporal context of up to four images spaced one second apart. Cosine similarity is used for logits against a vocabulary of known language commands.

**B. Policy Integration for Real-Time Adaptation (On-the-Fly):**
During robot deployment, the high-level policy ($\pi_H$) continuously generates language instructions, which are then passed to the low-level policy ($\pi_L$) for execution. The crucial innovation lies in the human intervention mechanism:
- A microphone allows a human user to provide verbal commands.
- If a human intervenes with a corrective language command (e.g., "stop", "move a bit to the left"), this command *temporarily overrides* the output of the high-level policy.
- The human's `l_user` command is directly fed into the low-level policy ($\pi_L$), allowing for immediate, on-the-fly behavioral adaptation. This dynamic override mechanism is key for rapid error recovery.

**C. Continuous Improvement from Human Feedback (Post-Training):**
The system is designed to learn from these real-time human interventions:
- When a human provides a verbal correction, the corresponding observation context (including data from 2 seconds prior to intervention for richer context) and the `l_user` command are recorded.
- This data forms a "correction dataset" (`D_corr`).
- The high-level policy ($\pi_H$) is then fine-tuned iteratively on a combination of the original base dataset (`D`) and the accumulated correction dataset (`D_corr`). This process is analogous to DAgger, but the "actions" being corrected and learned are high-level language commands rather than low-level motor actions.
- The fine-tuning updates $\pi_H$ to better predict appropriate instructions and corrections in future autonomous operations, aiming to minimize the need for subsequent human interventions.

**D. Practical Implementation Details:**
- **Data Collection:** A novel "live narration" method is used for pre-training data. Operators narrate skills into a microphone while teleoperating, with foot pedals used to differentiate between main "instructions" and "corrections." This streamlines language annotation and allows for filtering of suboptimal segments. Specific efforts were made to collect "correction skills" by identifying common robot failure modes.
- **Hardware:** The ALOHA bimanual teleoperation setup is used, with a 14-dimensional action space (bimanual target joint positions) and observations from four RGB cameras (two wrist, two stationary).
- **Optimization:** The high-level policy's output is optimized using a cross-entropy loss with a learned temperature parameter, predicting upcoming instructions by offsetting the target prediction.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted on three complex, long-horizon bi-manual manipulation tasks (Bag Packing, Trail Mix Preparation, and Plate Cleaning) on real hardware yielded several significant findings:

- **Significant On-the-Fly Improvement via Language Corrections:** The ability to incorporate real-time language corrections dramatically improved success rates. For instance, in Bag Packing, success rates increased by 25-50%, in Trail Mix Preparation by 30-45%, and in Plate Cleaning by 15-25%. This highlights the immediate utility of natural language as an intuitive feedback mechanism for error recovery and adaptation to out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios (e.g., precise grasping adjustments, preventing spills).
- **Continuous Improvement of Autonomous Policy Performance:** Fine-tuning the high-level policy with the collected language corrections led to substantial improvements in the *autonomous* success rate. Compared to the base policy, YAY Robot after fine-tuning showed increases of 20-45% in Bag Packing, 15-20% in Trail Mix Preparation, and 15-25% in Plate Cleaning. This demonstrates that the high-level policy successfully learns to generate corrective behaviors autonomously after being trained on human feedback, progressively reducing the need for intervention.
- **Hierarchical Policies Outperform Flat Policies on Long-Horizon Tasks:** The hierarchical approach of YAY Robot consistently achieved higher success rates than a state-of-the-art flat imitation learning method, ACT (Flat-BC). This performance gap was particularly noticeable in later stages of the long-horizon tasks, indicating the hierarchical policy's superior ability to manage and recover from compounding errors.
- **Ablation Studies Confirm Design Choices:**
 - **Learned High-Level Policy is Essential:** Replacing the learned high-level policy with a *scripted* sequence of instructions resulted in a significant performance decrease (up to 30% worse than the Base Policy). This underscores that dynamic, reactive high-level planning is crucial for handling real-world uncertainties and low-level policy imperfections.
 - **Pre-trained VLMs (GPT-4V) Lack Grounding:** Using GPT-4V as a high-level policy yielded poor performance. While it could generate plausible reasoning, it consistently failed in spatial reasoning and understanding the robot's physical capabilities (e.g., instructing the robot to lower its gripper when already on the table). This highlights the brittleness of ungrounded VLMs for direct control in complex manipulation tasks.
 - **Language Embeddings are Superior to One-Hot Encodings:** Substituting language embeddings with one-hot skill encodings resulted in inferior performance. This supports the hypothesis that leveraging the semantic similarities inherent in language is vital for effective learning, especially with a large and diverse set of commands.
 - **Data Quality Over Quantity:** Training on filtered data (excluding suboptimal behaviors before corrections) led to better performance than training on a larger dataset of mixed quality. This emphasizes the importance of curating high-quality demonstrations for robust policy learning.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Yell At Your Robot" framework presents a significant advancement in robot learning and human-robot interaction, with profound implications for the future of robotic autonomy.

**Increased Robot Autonomy and Robustness:** By enabling robots to continuously learn from natural language corrections, the system addresses a critical bottleneck in deploying robots for complex, long-horizon tasks. The ability to autonomously generate and execute corrective behaviors drastically improves task success rates and robustness, reducing the likelihood of catastrophic failures and the need for constant human supervision. This is particularly impactful for dexterous manipulation where small errors can quickly derail an entire sequence.

**Democratizing Robot Teaching and Interaction:** Perhaps the most impactful aspect is the shift towards intuitive, accessible human feedback. By allowing non-expert users to teach and guide robots using everyday language, the framework moves robotics out of specialized labs and into broader applications. This lowers the barrier to entry for customizing robot behavior, making robots more adaptable to user preferences and dynamic environments without requiring programming skills or complex teleoperation. This fosters a more natural and collaborative human-robot relationship.

**Scalable Data Collection for Complex Tasks:** Collecting large, high-quality demonstration datasets for long-horizon tasks is notoriously expensive and time-consuming. "Yell At Your Robot" offers a more scalable approach by leveraging natural language corrections, which can be gathered naturally during robot operation. This "human-in-the-loop" learning paradigm promises a more efficient pathway to acquiring the diverse data needed for robust policy training, particularly for error recovery and adaptation.

**Complementary to Foundation Models:** While the paper demonstrates the current limitations of off-the-shelf VLMs for direct high-level control, it also implicitly suggests a powerful synergy. The proposed fine-tuning mechanism for grounded language understanding could be applied to adapt and specialize pre-trained VLMs for robotic interaction. This could lead to more robust and generalized robotic systems that combine the broad knowledge of foundation models with the grounded, interaction-derived understanding of real-world robotic capabilities.

**Future Research Directions:** The work also clearly articulates its limitations, such as the critical reliance on a highly performant low-level policy and the current inability to handle non-verbal communication (e.g., pointing, gestures). These limitations naturally open up avenues for future research, including improving the flexibility of low-level policies, exploring multimodal human feedback, and more deeply integrating and grounding large language and vision models within interactive robot learning loops. Ultimately, "Yell At Your Robot" represents a crucial step towards equipping robots with the ability to learn and adapt continually from natural human supervision, bringing us closer to truly intelligent and widely deployable robotic assistants.
