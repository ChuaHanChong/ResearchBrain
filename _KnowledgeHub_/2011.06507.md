# Reinforcement Learning with Videos: Combining Offline Observations with Interaction

> [!summary] Summary
> RLV (Reinforcement Learning with Videos) trains robot policies from action-free, reward-free human or robot videos alongside online interaction. It adds videos to the replay buffer, imputing missing actions with an inverse model over adversarially domain-confused features and missing rewards with a terminal-state bonus. On vision-based manipulation it solves tasks with roughly half to a third the samples of soft actor-critic trained from scratch.

## Problem

- Reinforcement learning needs large online trial-and-error data collection, limiting the behavioral diversity robots can experience and hence how broadly learned policies generalize.
- Imitation-from-observation methods match demonstrated transitions but are capped by demonstrator quality, so they cannot exploit suboptimal or cross-morphology human video.
- Prior action-free approaches rely on accurate learned dynamics models or ignore domain shift in morphology, viewpoint, and background between human demonstrator and robot.

## Method

- Dual replay pools: an action-conditioned interaction pool plus an action-free observation pool, both sampled into every off-policy ==soft actor-critic== gradient batch after annotation.
- ==Inverse model== trained by supervised regression on robot transitions predicts robot-space actions for video state pairs from encoded feature tuples, exploiting an assumed MDP isomorphism.
- ==Adversarial domain confusion== trains an encoder against a domain discriminator for invariant features; a joint objective weights the reinforcement learning, inverse-model, and domain losses.

## Results

- On Acrobot with medium-quality observations, **RLV** exceeds behavioral cloning from observation, imitating latent policies, and standard soft actor-critic in final reward and sample efficiency.
- RLV solves the state pusher with **30%** of the samples soft actor-critic needs and needs **3x** fewer samples on visual pusher and visual door opening.
- With real human videos, RLV reaches soft actor-critic performance in **50%** of the samples on pushing and drawer opening, beating random-network-distillation exploration bonuses.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Action-free video is usable as reinforcement learning data, not just imitation targets, so the agent can surpass suboptimal demonstrators rather than merely copy them.
> - Crude annotations suffice: even zero actions or zero rewards on the video pool beat standard reinforcement learning, since videos mainly accelerate exploration.
> - Feature-level adversarial invariance handles embodiment, viewpoint, and background shift without any paired data or image-to-image translation between human and robot.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The acquisition of robotic skills through reinforcement learning (RL) typically necessitates substantial online data collection via trial-and-error interactions. This requirement often leads to data inefficiency and limits the diversity of experiences, which in turn hinders a robot's ability to generalize broadly to novel situations. Simultaneously, large and diverse real-world datasets have been shown to be critical for achieving broad generalization and high performance in other areas of deep learning.

This paper addresses the challenge of data inefficiency and limited generalization in robotic RL by proposing to leverage readily available observational data, specifically videos of humans. The broader research landscape encompasses several related areas:

- **Learning from Demonstration (LfD):** Traditional LfD approaches, including imitation learning, aim to transfer skills from an expert demonstrator to an agent. Early works often assumed access to high-quality demonstrations with corresponding actions.
- **Imitation Learning without Actions:** More recent advancements have focused on scenarios where only observations are available, without explicit action labels. These methods often involve matching demonstrated state transitions or learning a reward function from observations. However, a common limitation of these approaches is that the learned policy is bounded by the performance of the demonstrator, potentially struggling to improve upon sub-optimal demonstrations or to generalize beyond the specific demonstrated behaviors.
- **Reinforcement Learning with Demonstrations:** Other works combine demonstrations with RL to accelerate learning and potentially surpass expert performance. These methods typically assume that demonstration data includes both states and actions. While they allow for improvement over expert behavior, they do not address the common scenario where actions are not available in observational data.
- **Domain Adaptation:** Learning from human videos introduces a significant domain shift between the human demonstrator and the robot agent. This shift can manifest in differences in morphology, action space, viewpoint, and environment. Existing domain adaptation techniques include unpaired image-to-image translation and domain confusion methods, which aim to learn domain-invariant features.

This work, Reinforcement Learning with Videos (RLV), is positioned as a method that integrates action-free, observation-only data, particularly human videos, into a reinforcement learning framework. It differentiates itself from prior imitation learning from observation methods by explicitly allowing the agent to improve upon the observed behaviors through RL. Unlike existing RL with demonstrations, RLV does not assume access to actions in the observational data and directly tackles the challenge of substantial domain shift between human and robot embodiments and environments. It aims to bridge the gap between vast, cheap, and diverse human video data and the data-hungry nature of robot reinforcement learning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a framework that enables robots to learn challenging vision-based skills more efficiently by combining online interaction experience with large datasets of offline observational videos, especially those recorded from human activities.

The motivation for this objective stems from several key challenges in current robotic reinforcement learning:

- **High Data Requirements for Online RL:** Reinforcement learning algorithms often require a substantial volume of online trial-and-error data collection. This process can be time-consuming, expensive, and impractical for real-world robotic systems, particularly when tasks are complex or require extensive exploration.
- **Limited Generalization from Scarce Online Data:** The online data collected by a robot is often limited in diversity due to practical constraints. This lack of diversity restricts the ability of learned policies to generalize broadly to new situations, environments, or task variations.
- **Abundance of Diverse Offline Observational Data:** In contrast to the scarcity of robot interaction data, videos of humans performing a wide array of tasks are readily available. These videos offer a potentially rich source of diverse experiences, insights into object interactions, and environmental physics. Leveraging this data could significantly enhance the generalization capabilities of robotic agents.
- **Challenges of Using Observational Data Directly in RL:** Integrating human videos into an RL framework presents several significant technical difficulties:
 - **Absence of Action Annotations:** Human videos typically do not contain explicit labels for the actions taken by the demonstrator. For an RL agent to learn, it needs to associate states with actions.
 - **Absence of Reward Annotations:** Similarly, human videos lack explicit reward signals, which are fundamental for reinforcement learning to guide policy optimization towards desired outcomes.
 - **Significant Visual Domain Shift:** The visual appearance, kinematics, and action spaces of humans differ substantially from those of robots. This "domain shift" makes it challenging for a robot to interpret human observations and transfer skills directly. Differences can include morphology, viewpoint, environment background, and lighting conditions.
 - **Sub-optimality of Observed Behaviors:** Videos of humans may depict behaviors that are sub-optimal, or even irrelevant, for a robot's specific morphology or task objective. Imitation learning methods struggle with sub-optimal data, as they are often limited to replicating the demonstrated performance.

To address these challenges, the authors propose the RLV framework with the goal of creating a generalizable approach that can effectively utilize diverse, action-free, reward-free, and domain-shifted observational data alongside online robot interaction to learn robust and data-efficient robotic policies.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Reinforcement Learning with Videos (RLV) framework combines offline observational data with online robot interaction data to train a robot policy. The core idea is to process the action-free and reward-free observational data to make it compatible with a standard off-policy reinforcement learning algorithm, while simultaneously addressing the domain shift between observations and robot experiences.

### 4. Problem Formulation

The problem is formulated within the Markov Decision Process (MDP) framework. The robot operates in an MDP defined by $(S_{int}, A_{int}, P, R)$, while observational data comes from another agent (e.g., a human) in an MDP defined by $(S_{obs}, A_{obs}, P, R)$. A key assumption is that the environment's dynamics ($P$) and reward function ($R$) are isomorphic between the two MDPs, implying that unknown invertible functions ($g_o$ for states and $g_a$ for actions) can transform one state and action space into the other while preserving dynamics and rewards. The agent aims to learn a policy that maximizes expected return in its own environment, leveraging initial offline observations and supplemental online interaction.

### 5. Overview of RLV Framework

The RLV framework operates by maintaining two replay pools:
- **$D_{int}$:** Stores action-conditioned interaction data collected by the robot ($s_{int}, a_{int}, s'_{int}, r_{int}$). This pool is continuously updated during training.
- **$D_{obs}$:** Contains the initial set of action-free observation data ($s_{obs}, s'_{obs}$). This pool is static.

The process involves three main steps to integrate $D_{obs}$ into the RL training:
1. **Domain Adaptation:** Observations from both domains are mapped to a shared, domain-invariant feature space.
2. **Action Prediction:** An inverse model, trained on robot interaction data in the invariant feature space, predicts actions for the observation data.
3. **Reward Generation:** A simple heuristic is used to assign rewards to the observation data.

These processed observational tuples, along with robot interaction tuples, are then combined into a single batch and used to train an off-policy reinforcement learning algorithm, specifically Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) in this work.

### 6. Key Components

#### 6.1. Action Prediction

To estimate actions for the observational data, an inverse model, $f_{inv}$ (parameterized by $\theta$), is trained. This model takes a pair of invariant feature encodings ($h, h'$) and predicts the corresponding action in the robot's action space ($a_{int}$). The model is trained using supervised learning on the robot's own interaction data, minimizing the mean squared error between predicted and true actions:
$L_a(a_{int}, h_{int}, h'_{int}, \theta) = \|a_{int} - f_{inv}(h_{int}, h'_{int}; \theta)\|^2$
Once trained, this inverse model is applied to the invariant features extracted from the observation data ($h_{obs}, h'_{obs}$) to generate predicted actions, $\hat{a}_{int}$.

#### 6.2. Reward Generation

Since observational data lacks explicit rewards, a simple scheme is employed to assign them, drawing inspiration from prior work. For each observed trajectory, the final timestep is assigned a large constant reward ($c_{large}$), while all other timesteps receive a small constant reward ($c_{small}$). This approach implicitly assumes that the observed trajectories are generally goal-achieving, encouraging the agent to reach similar terminal states. The authors note its robustness even with sub-optimal demonstrations.

#### 6.3. Domain Adaptation

To bridge the visual and morphological gap between the human (or other agent) observations and the robot's own experiences, a feature extractor, $f_{enc}$ (parameterized by $\psi$), is trained. This encoder maps raw observations ($s$) to a domain-invariant feature vector ($h$). Domain invariance is achieved through an adversarial training setup:
- A discriminator, $f_{discr}$ (parameterized by $\phi$), is trained to distinguish whether an encoded feature ($h$) originated from the interaction data ($h_{int}$) or the observation data ($h_{obs}$).
- The feature extractor ($f_{enc}$) is simultaneously trained to minimize the discriminator's ability to correctly classify the domain, effectively pushing the features from both domains closer in the latent space. The loss function for domain adaptation is:
 $L_{dom}(s_{int}, s_{obs}, \psi, \phi) = \log(f_{discr}(f_{enc}(s_{int}; \psi); \phi)) + \log(1 - f_{discr}(f_{enc}(s_{obs}; \psi); \phi))$
The encoder learns to produce representations that contain task-relevant information while being agnostic to the source domain. The paper states that using the learned encoding for the inverse model while allowing the RL to use raw observations yields the best performance.

#### 6.4. Joint Optimization

The entire system is optimized jointly by combining three loss terms:
1. The optimization objective of the underlying reinforcement learning algorithm ($L_{RL}$), which is SAC in this case.
2. The inverse model loss ($L_a$) for action prediction.
3. The adversarial domain confusion loss ($L_{dom}$).

The overall objective function is:
$\min_{\psi,\theta,\phi} \sum_{\{s_{obs},s'_{obs}\} \in D_{obs}, \{s_{int},a_{int},s'_{int}\} \in D_{int}} c_2 L_{RL} + c_1 L_a(a_{int}, f_{enc}(s_{int}; \psi), f_{enc}(s'_{int}; \psi), \theta) + c_3 \max_{\phi} L_{dom}(s_{int}, s_{obs}, \psi, \phi)$
where $c_1, c_2, c_3$ are hyperparameters balancing the contributions of each loss term. All networks (encoder, inverse model, discriminator, and SAC's policy/Q-functions) are instantiated as deep neural networks.

### 7. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted aimed to evaluate RLV's ability to facilitate more data-efficient reinforcement learning by incorporating action-free video data. The Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) algorithm was used as the base RL method for all comparisons.

### 8. Learning from Observations of Sub-optimal Policies (Acrobot-v1)

- RLV was tested in the Acrobot-v1 environment using observational data generated from SAC policies at different training stages (25K, 225K, 975K steps), thus representing varying levels of sub-optimality.
- **Finding:** RLV consistently achieved equal or higher final rewards compared to Imitating Latent Policies from Observation (ILPO), Behavioral Cloning from Observation (BCO), and standard SAC, while requiring fewer samples.
- **Finding:** The performance improvements were most notable with medium-quality observational data (from a SAC policy trained for 225K steps), suggesting robustness to non-expert demonstrations. ILPO, while data-efficient, was unable to surpass the demonstrator's performance, and standard RL was less data-efficient.

### 9. Robotic Tasks (Simulated)

RLV was evaluated on more complex robotic manipulation tasks without domain shift, using simulated observational data.

- **State Pusher Environment (State-based observations):**
 - **Finding:** RLV required approximately 30% fewer samples to solve the task compared to standard SAC.
 - **Finding:** BCO and ILPO struggled and failed to reliably solve the task, particularly due to the continuous and higher-dimensional action space of this environment.

- **Visual Pusher and Visual Door Opening Environments (Image-based observations):**
 - **Finding:** RLV achieved the tasks with 3 times fewer samples than standard SAC in both environments.
 - **Finding:** In the Visual Door Opening task, RLV demonstrated greater robustness, with all five training seeds converging to the optimal policy, whereas only three out of five SAC seeds converged.
 - **Finding:** BCO and ILPO were unable to achieve non-zero rewards on these image-based tasks.

### 10. Learning from Real-World Human Videos (Simulated Robot)

This set of experiments addressed the significant challenge of learning from real-world human videos, which involve substantial domain shift to a simulated robot environment. Tasks included pushing an object and opening a drawer.

- **Finding:** RLV successfully leveraged human observational videos to accelerate training, requiring only 50% of the samples compared to standard SAC to achieve comparable performance in both pushing and drawer opening tasks.
- **Finding:** RLV's performance surpassed a version of SAC augmented with Random Network Distillation (RND), a task-agnostic exploration technique. While RND improved learning speed over default RL, it did not match RLV's efficiency or final performance.
- **Finding:** The framework successfully managed considerable domain shift in color, lighting conditions, object positions, and embodiment between real human videos and simulated robot observations.

### 11. Ablations over Action and Reward Prediction (Visual Pusher)

Ablation studies were conducted to assess the contribution of estimated actions and rewards.

- **Action Prediction:**
 - **Finding:** Using the inverse model to predict actions for observational data performed comparably to using ground truth actions.
 - **Finding:** Even when assigning "zero actions" to the observational data, RLV initially learned faster than standard RL, but converged to a sub-optimal final score. This suggests that even imperfect action estimates can accelerate exploration, but accurate action prediction is necessary for optimal policy learning.

- **Reward Generation:**
 - **Finding:** The proposed simple reward generation scheme (sparse, with large reward at terminal state) performed comparably to using ground truth rewards.
 - **Finding:** Assigning "zero rewards" to observational data also allowed the agent to converge to an optimal solution, albeit with slightly reduced performance. This indicates a degree of robustness in RLV to the specific reward labeling, possibly because the observational data primarily aids exploration, and the ground truth rewards for the robot's own experiences guide the final policy.

In summary, the main findings indicate that RLV is a robust and data-efficient framework capable of leveraging diverse, action-free, and domain-shifted observational data (including human videos) to significantly accelerate reinforcement learning for robotic manipulation tasks, often outperforming existing imitation learning and standard RL approaches.

### 12. Significance and Potential Impact

The Reinforcement Learning with Videos (RLV) framework addresses several fundamental limitations in current robotic skill acquisition and has significant potential impact:

- **Enhanced Data Efficiency in Robotic RL:** A primary challenge in deploying reinforcement learning to real-world robots is the extensive amount of online interaction data required. RLV demonstrates a substantial reduction in the number of interaction samples needed to learn complex tasks (e.g., 30% to 50% fewer samples in various experiments). This improvement in data efficiency makes the application of RL to complex robotic manipulation tasks more practical and less resource-intensive.
- **Leveraging Abundant Unstructured Data:** The ability of RLV to learn from action-free and reward-free observational videos, particularly from humans, unlocks a vast and readily available resource for robot training. This contrasts with previous methods that often require meticulously curated, action-annotated demonstrations or complex instrumentation. Tapping into open-world human video data could provide robots with a diverse range of experiences that would be impractical to collect through direct robot interaction.
- **Overcoming Domain Shift:** RLV successfully addresses the significant domain shift between human observations and robot execution, encompassing differences in morphology, viewpoint, and environment. This capability is crucial for transferring skills from human demonstrations, which often visually differ greatly from a robot's perspective and embodiment. This represents a step towards robots learning from "real-world" human actions rather than constrained, lab-specific demonstrations.
- **Improved Generalization:** By incorporating diverse observational data, RLV has the potential to lead to more generalizable robotic policies. Exposure to varied environments and behaviors, even if sub-optimal, can enrich the robot's understanding of task-relevant features and object interactions, leading to more robust skills.
- **Robustness to Sub-optimality and Imperfect Labels:** The framework's demonstrated robustness to sub-optimal observation data and simple reward generation schemes is critical. Real-world human videos are unlikely to be perfectly optimal or consistently goal-directed. RLV's ability to still learn effectively under these conditions increases its practical applicability.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** This work lays a foundation for future advancements in learning from observation. It suggests avenues for generalizing to more visually complex scenes, handling even more natural and diverse human demonstrations, and enabling the learning of increasingly complex behaviors. The integration of offline passive observation with active online learning offers a promising hybrid approach for robot learning.

In conclusion, RLV represents a step toward enabling robots to autonomously acquire a broader range of skills with less explicit human supervision and data collection effort, by effectively integrating the rich, diverse, and naturally occurring observational data from human activities into the reinforcement learning paradigm.
