# EdgeBench: Unveiling Scaling Laws of Learning from Real-World Environments

> [!summary] Summary
> A precise log-sigmoid scaling law governing the performance improvement of large language model agents from interaction in real-world environments was identified, exhibiting a mean R² of 0.998 on the new EdgeBench benchmark and revealing that frontier agent learning speed doubled every three months.

## Problem

- The dynamics of how large language models learn and improve from interaction with real-world environments post-deployment were not well understood, unlike pretraining scaling laws.
- Existing AI benchmarks lacked the ultra-long-horizon, diverse, and interactive real-world tasks necessary to study continuous agent improvement with rich, multi-level feedback.
- There was no established framework to systematically measure and predict the performance trajectory of AI agents over extended interaction time in complex, iterative scenarios.

## Method

- A new benchmark called **EdgeBench** was created, featuring 134 ultra-long-horizon, diverse real-world tasks spanning six capability families, each requiring at least 12 hours of continuous agent operation.
- EdgeBench tasks incorporate a ==dual-loop feedback structure==, providing fast local interaction and authoritative judge-mediated evaluation with calibrated scores, mirroring human expert workflows.
- Five frontier large language models were evaluated on EdgeBench for approximately 38,000 hours of interaction, with performance data collected and analyzed to identify learning patterns.

## Results

- A ==log-sigmoid scaling law== for agent environment learning was empirically observed with high precision (mean **R² = 0.998**) across **134** diverse real-world tasks in EdgeBench.
- Frontier agent learning speed demonstrated a rapid increase, approximately **doubling every three months** between September 2025 and April 2026.
- Accumulated experience during continuous **12-hour** runs consistently outperformed repeated **2-hour** sampling, achieving an average gain of **+6.9 performance points**.
- Utilizing a **1M** context window offered a stable performance advantage of **+4.4 to +5.8 points** over a **200k** context window across **12 hours** of interaction.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Environment learning in agents follows a precise log-sigmoid scaling law, which can be theoretically derived by modeling learning as a frontier expansion process on latent task graphs.
> - Agent learning speed, measured as performance gain over a fixed interaction budget, is rapidly increasing across frontier model generations.
> - Accumulated experience, retained over continuous interaction, is crucial for performance gains and significantly outperforms repeated short-duration sampling.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research contributes to the expanding field of artificial intelligence, specifically focusing on the capabilities of large language models (LLMs) when deployed as autonomous agents in interactive, real-world environments. The paper positions itself in the context of established "scaling laws" observed in the pretraining phase of LLMs, where model performance predictably correlates with data and computational resources. While pretraining scaling laws have guided significant advancements in LLM development, the dynamics of how these models learn and improve from interaction with real-world environments post-deployment have remained less explored and understood.

Existing benchmarks for AI systems often concentrate on evaluating static task completion, final answer accuracy, or end-state artifact quality within relatively short interaction windows. While some benchmarks address aspects of iterative improvement, sequential learning, or long-horizon tasks, they typically do so within narrower domains, with controlled task sequences, or without explicitly measuring performance trajectories against continuous interaction time. This paper addresses a gap by introducing a benchmark designed for ultra-long-horizon, diverse, and interactive real-world tasks that provide rich, multi-level feedback, enabling the systematic study of within-run agent improvement. By proposing a scaling law for environment learning, the work aims to extend the predictability observed in pretraining to the post-deployment phase, thus offering a framework for understanding and potentially accelerating the development of more capable and adaptive AI agents.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The research outlines several key objectives and motivations:

- **To investigate the existence of scaling laws for agent learning from real-world environments:** A primary motivation is to determine if the predictable scaling relationships observed in LLM pretraining also manifest when agents learn through interaction in real-world environments. The authors highlight the lack of understanding in this domain and aim to identify if a consistent mathematical relationship governs performance improvement over interaction time.
- **To establish a comprehensive, long-horizon benchmark for agent environment learning:** The authors identify a need for robust evaluation environments that accurately simulate real-world use cases, provide informative feedback, and allow agents sufficient time to exhibit complex learning behaviors such as exploration, strategy revision, and experience accumulation. Existing benchmarks are noted for often lacking these characteristics, frequently limiting agents to short interaction periods or insufficient feedback channels.
- **To develop EdgeBench as a diverse suite of real-world tasks:** Motivated by the need for a suitable evaluation platform, the authors undertook substantial expert effort to create EdgeBench. This benchmark comprises 134 tasks spanning six distinct capability families: scientific problems & machine learning, systems & software engineering, combinatorial optimization, professional knowledge work, formal mathematics & theorem proving, and interactive games & simulators. Each task is designed to sustain at least 12 hours of continuous agent operation and provide rich, multilevel feedback, mirroring actual human expert workflows.
- **To analyze the dynamics of environment learning in frontier agents:** Beyond merely identifying a scaling law, the research seeks to understand how current state-of-the-art AI agents improve over extended interaction periods. This includes examining how agent performance progresses, the efficiency of their learning (e.g., how often submissions lead to improvements), the impact of accumulating experience versus repeated sampling, and the role of context length in retaining and utilizing learned information.
- **To quantify the pace of improvement in agent learning speed across model generations:** The paper aims to measure if newer generations of AI models are not only more capable but also learn more rapidly from their environments. This involves analyzing performance gains over fixed interaction budgets across models released over time.
- **To publicly release part of the benchmark and evaluation framework:** A further objective is to accelerate research in agent learning by making 51 tasks and the full evaluation framework publicly available. This aims to foster broader scientific inquiry into how agents acquire and reuse experience in real-world settings.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed a multi-faceted methodology encompassing benchmark construction, agent evaluation, and data analysis.

#### 3.1. EdgeBench Benchmark Design and Construction

EdgeBench was developed to meet two primary design goals: ultra-long-horizon, diverse tasks, and realistic, multi-level feedback.
- **Task Taxonomy:** The benchmark comprises 134 tasks categorized into six capability families:
 - Scientific Problems & ML (39 tasks): Real-world research data, iterative hypothesis formulation, model validation against noisy data.
 - Systems & Software Engineering (36 tasks): Production-grade codebases requiring large-scale changes, reasoning about cross-module coupling, correctness, and performance.
 - Combinatorial Optimization (19 tasks): Open-ended NP-hard problems requiring heuristic search and iteration.
 - Professional Knowledge Work (19 tasks): White-collar deliverables graded by client-style rubrics and multi-round feedback.
 - Formal Math & Theorem Proving (13 tasks): Machine-checked proofs in Lean 4 & Coq, designed for iterative progress.
 - Interactive Games & Simulators (8 tasks): Human-designed games requiring sequential decision-making and strategy refinement in out-of-distribution worlds.
- **Task Characteristics:** Each task is designed for at least 12 hours of continuous agent operation. Tasks required significant human expert effort, averaging 57.2 hours per task, to ensure realism and sufficient depth for continuous learning. Tasks whose primary difficulty relied on visual understanding or GUI operation were excluded.
- **Feedback Loop and Evaluation Protocol:** A dual-loop feedback structure was implemented:
 - **Inner Loop (Local):** Agents interact with an executable workspace (e.g., compilers, linters, simulators, documentation) to perform fast, self-driven exploration, debugging, and refinement.
 - **Outer Loop (Judge-mediated):** Agents submit artifacts to a separate judge environment that evaluates against hidden test cases or private grading criteria, returning calibrated scores, verdicts, or diagnostics. This loop is slower and authoritative, designed to prevent overfitting to local checks.
- **Evaluation Harness (SForge):** An isolated work–judge container architecture was used. The agent operates in a work container, while a judge container performs hidden evaluations. A host-side judge server mediates submissions, queues, cooldowns, and asynchronous grading. Host-side auto-evaluation at fixed intervals records performance snapshots for analysis without exposing results to the agent. Safeguards against evaluation hacking (e.g., oracle reconstruction, stochastic optimization, overfitting seeds) were implemented.

#### 3.2. Agent Evaluation

- **Models:** Five frontier large language models were evaluated: Claude Opus 4.8, GPT-5.5, GPT-5.4, GLM-5.1, and DeepSeek-V4-Pro (preview).
- **Interaction:** Approximately 38,000 hours of agent environment interaction were analyzed. For each task-model pair, three independent 12-hour trials were conducted, with full submission trajectories recorded. GPT models used Codex; GLM-5.1 and DeepSeek-V4-Pro used Claude Code. Context window sizes varied (e.g., 256k for GPT, 200k/1M for Claude Opus 4.8).

#### 3.3. Data Analysis

- **Scaling Law Identification:** The primary analysis involved fitting the benchmark-averaged performance curves with a three-parameter log-sigmoid model: $S(t) = S_{max} / (1 + (t_{mid}/t)^\beta)$, where $S(t)$ is best-so-far performance and $t$ is elapsed interaction time. The coefficient of determination ($R^2$) was used to quantify fit precision. Comparisons were made against other common S-curves (log-probit, log-Gompertz, Weibull CDF, log-linear baseline).
- **Theoretical Derivation:** A theoretical model was proposed, framing environment learning as a frontier expansion process on latent task graphs. This model posits that the expected score-growth rate is proportional to a weighted frontier cut from unlocked to locked score units, and that a log-time coordinate emerges from self-similar graph structure.
- **Agent Learning Speed:** To measure learning speed across model generations, an 18-task slice with comparable initial model performance was selected. Learning speed was defined as the average performance gain over a fixed two-hour budget. A log-linear fit was applied to the top-performing models over time. Submission efficiency (fraction of submissions improving best-so-far score) was also tracked.
- **Environment Learning Dynamics:**
 - **Accumulated Experience vs. Repeated Sampling:** For 17 tasks, Claude Opus 4.8 was run in two modes over a 12-hour budget: (1) continuous run with accumulated state, and (2) six independent 2-hour runs with state discarded, taking the best result.
 - **Context Length Ablation:** Performance of Claude Opus 4.8 was compared with 200k-context versus 1M-context on a 42-task subset.
 - **Case Study:** The gravitational-wave reconstruction task was analyzed in detail to illustrate iterative improvement patterns of a GPT-5.5 agent.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The analysis of agent interaction with EdgeBench yielded several key findings:

- **Precise Log-Sigmoid Scaling Law for Environment Learning:**
 - Overall performance across all 134 tasks follows a log-sigmoid scaling law as a function of environment interaction time with high precision (mean $R^2 = 0.998$).
 - This log-sigmoid form is robust, consistently observed when averaged across the full benchmark, within each of the six heterogeneous task families, and under substantially longer interaction horizons (up to 72 hours with $R^2 \geq 0.993$).
 - The log-sigmoid law demonstrates predictive power, accurately forecasting 12-hour performance using only the first 6.5 hours of interaction ($R^2 \geq 0.997$, RMSE < 1.0 performance point).
 - Among common S-shaped growth curves, the log-sigmoid provided the lowest RMSE, and the precision of the fit improved monotonically as more tasks were included in the average, suggesting an emergent, population-level regularity.
- **Theoretical Derivation of the Log-Sigmoid Law:**
 - A theoretical model was proposed, describing environment learning as a frontier expansion process on latent task graphs. In this model, the score units are nodes, and progress occurs when the frontier between unlocked and locked units advances.
 - The model suggests that, under conditions of weighted cut mixing and sufficiently many small score units, the normalized score's growth rate approximates a logistic function, proportional to the product of unlocked and locked score mass ($x(1-x)$).
 - The effective log-time coordinate is derived from a hypothesis of self-similar task-graph structure, where additive increases in task difficulty require multiplicative increases in raw interaction time.
- **Rapid Increase in Agent Learning Speed:**
 - Analysis of frontier models released between September 2025 and April 2026 (including GPT-5-Codex to GPT-5.5) on an 18-task slice indicated a rapid increase in learning speed.
 - The learning speed, defined as performance gain over a two-hour budget, approximately doubled every three months.
 - This improvement was correlated with a higher fraction of submissions leading to best-so-far performance improvements, rather than merely an increased submission frequency, suggesting more effective learning from interaction.
- **Accumulated Experience Outperforms Repeated Sampling:**
 - A comparison of continuous 12-hour runs with accumulated experience against multiple independent 2-hour restarts (totaling 12 hours) on Claude Opus 4.8 showed that accumulated experience led to significantly higher performance. For example, at 12 hours, the continuous run achieved a gain of +6.9 performance points over the best of independent restarts. This indicates that progress is driven by stateful learning, not solely by increased opportunities from repeated attempts.
- **Longer Context Windows Provide Stable Advantage:**
 - An ablation study comparing 200k-context and 1M-context Claude Opus 4.8 revealed that the longer context provided a consistent multi-point performance gain across the entire 12-hour interaction window. The performance gap ranged from +5.8 at 2 hours to +4.4 at 12 hours, demonstrating that extended context remains beneficial for long-horizon interaction even when external state channels are available.
- **Illustrative Case Study:**
 - A detailed analysis of the gravitational-wave reconstruction task demonstrated that an agent's improvement trajectory is often characterized by sparse but structured diagnose-edit-evaluate loops. The agent iteratively refined its solution by first making the problem measurable, then decomposing errors into subproblems, identifying and addressing main bottlenecks, and finally refining a stable core solution. This process generated uneven jumps in performance rather than a smooth climb, reflecting cumulative breakthroughs.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The findings of this research have several significant implications for the field of artificial intelligence:

- **Systematic Study of Post-Deployment Learning:** The discovery of a precise log-sigmoid scaling law for environment learning elevates post-deployment agent improvement from a collection of idiosyncratic task outcomes to a measurable and predictable scaling object. This provides a quantitative framework for the systematic study and engineering of how AI agents acquire and leverage experience in real-world settings, akin to how pretraining scaling laws have guided LLM development.
- **Advancement of Agent Development:** The observed rapid increase in agent learning speed across model generations indicates a trajectory of continuous improvement in models' ability to adapt and learn from dynamic environments. This suggests a pathway toward more robust and autonomous AI systems that can continuously enhance their capabilities after deployment, potentially reducing the need for constant human supervision or retraining.
- **Impact on Benchmark Design:** EdgeBench, with its ultra-long horizons, diverse real-world tasks, and emphasis on continuous feedback, serves as a significant contribution to the AI benchmarking landscape. Its public release is intended to foster further research into the mechanisms of agent learning, enabling rigorous evaluation of exploration strategies, experience retention, and adaptive behaviors over extended periods. The benchmark’s design, incorporating dual feedback loops and robust evaluation protocols, offers a model for creating more realistic and informative assessment tools for advanced AI agents.
- **Understanding Learning Dynamics:** The findings regarding the value of accumulated experience over repeated sampling and the persistent advantage of longer context windows provide practical insights for designing and deploying effective AI agents. This suggests that agent architectures and deployment strategies should prioritize mechanisms for coherent state retention and extensive context processing to maximize learning from interaction.
- **Theoretical Foundations for Agent Intelligence:** The proposed theoretical derivation of the log-sigmoid law, based on frontier expansion on task graphs and self-similar search geometry, offers a mechanistic explanation for the observed empirical regularity. This contributes to the theoretical understanding of intelligence and learning beyond empirical observation, potentially guiding the design of more intrinsically adaptive agents.
- **Societal and Economic Implications:** As AI agents move from static problem-solving to continuous operation in complex real-world domains (e.g., scientific discovery, software engineering, professional knowledge work), their ability to learn and improve from experience becomes crucial for widespread, safe, and effective deployment. The insights from this research can inform the development of AI systems capable of handling unforeseen challenges, adapting to changing circumstances, and continuously enhancing their utility in economically valuable tasks.
