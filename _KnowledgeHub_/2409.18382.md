# CurricuLLM: Automatic Task Curricula Design for Learning Complex Robot Skills using Large Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> CurricuLLM is an LLM-driven task-curriculum generator for robot RL. An LLM decomposes a target task into a short sequence of subtask descriptions grounded in state variables, a second LLM translates each into executable reward and goal-distribution code, and a third LLM picks the best policy from trajectory rollouts. Policies are fine-tuned sequentially, matching hand-tuned rewards on Berkeley Humanoid and transferring to hardware.

## Problem

- Manual curriculum design demands expert intervention and predefined task sets, limiting transfer across robots, environments, and task families.
- Automatic curriculum learning stays confined to initial-state, goal-state, or environment curricula because ranking subtask relevance and difficulty autonomously is unresolved.
- LLM reward-design methods need explicit evaluation metrics for their feedback loops and use sample-inefficient evolutionary search, contradicting curriculum learning's efficiency goal.

## Method

- ==Curriculum design LLM== turns robot, environment, and target-task descriptions into at most five ordered subtask descriptions expressed only in available state variables, ending at the target task.
- ==Task code generation LLM== samples K reward-plus-goal-distribution code candidates per subtask, conditioned on a ==chain-of-thought==-style history of prior subtask descriptions and their selected reward code.
- ==Evaluation LLM== reads trajectory rollout statistics from the K fine-tuned candidate policies, selects the best-aligned healthy policy, and archives it as the pretrained initialization for the next subtask.

## Results

- On Gymnasium-Robotics Fetch-Slide, Fetch-Push, and AntMaze-UMaze with SAC over 5 seeds, **CurricuLLM** matched or beat SAC, HER, and LLM-zeroshot, winning clearly on AntMaze.
- Berkeley Humanoid simulation: **0.46 m/s** tracking error, **8.79%** failure, versus human reward **0.41** and **7.31%**, and LLM-zeroshot **1.13** and **28.57%**.
- Fewer than **5** code samples per subtask sufficed; the learned humanoid policy tracked commands on real hardware within **±1 m/s** linear velocity.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Grounding subtask descriptions in normalized state variables lets an LLM pick reward weights directly, removing the metric-driven evolutionary search other reward-generation methods depend on.
> - Passing prior subtasks and their reward code forward acts like chain-of-thought, building complex rewards from simple ones while mitigating catastrophic forgetting.
> - Reward shaping is brittle even for experts: adding an alive bonus to a hand-designed humanoid reward collapsed the policy into standing and ignoring commands.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper addresses a significant challenge in deep reinforcement learning (RL) for robotics: the efficient learning of complex policies. RL often suffers from sample inefficiency, especially when tasks are complex or require intricate coordination, making it difficult for agents to learn from scratch. Curriculum learning (CL) offers a solution by structuring the learning process from simpler to more complex tasks, mimicking human learning. However, the manual design of effective curricula is notoriously difficult, requiring extensive domain knowledge and human effort, thus limiting its applicability.

The research landscape around curriculum learning has seen various attempts at automation. Initial state curricula, goal state curricula, and environment curricula have been explored. These approaches often define task difficulty based on metrics like distance to a goal or environmental parameters. However, they struggle to generate *task-level* curricula that involve decomposing complex behaviors into meaningful subtasks, defining appropriate reward functions, and sequencing them. The paper argues that autonomously evaluating the relevance and difficulty of these diverse subtasks remains an open challenge for traditional Automatic Curriculum Learning (ACL) methods.

The emergence of Large Language Models (LLMs) has introduced a new paradigm. LLMs, trained on vast textual datasets, encapsulate extensive world knowledge and exhibit remarkable capabilities in high-level planning, task decomposition, and translating natural language into executable code. This confluence of CL's need for structured task design and LLMs' planning and coding prowess forms the intellectual backdrop for CurricuLLM.

CurricuLLM differentiates itself from prior LLM-robotics work in several ways:
- **Task Planning:** While LLMs have been used for task planning, these are often limited to predefined skill sets or discrete actions. CurricuLLM focuses on generating curricula for *continuous control problems*, where skills themselves need to be learned through customized reward functions and goal distributions.
- **Reward Design:** Previous LLM-based reward design efforts either translate motion descriptions to cost parameters for hand-coded functions, use LLMs/VLMs directly as reward functions (which is computationally expensive during training), or employ evolutionary search to identify effective reward functions. The latter, while powerful, is sample-inefficient and requires predefined evaluation metrics. CurricuLLM, in contrast, uses a "Chain-of-Thought" reasoning with historical awareness to *sequentially evolve* reward functions across a curriculum, without relying on iterative search or external metrics for subtask evaluation.
- **Curriculum Scope:** Importantly, CurricuLLM explicitly distinguishes itself from works like DrEureka and Eurekaverse, which use LLMs to generate *environment curricula* (e.g., varying gravity, terrain height) for generalization. CurricuLLM focuses on *task curricula*, breaking down a single complex task into a sequence of simpler subtasks.

By synergizing the strengths of LLMs with the principles of curriculum learning, CurricuLLM aims to overcome the "curriculum bottleneck" in RL, offering a generalizable and automated approach to designing sophisticated learning trajectories for robots.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to **autonomously design effective task curricula for learning complex robot control policies using Large Language Models, thereby reducing the need for extensive human intervention and domain knowledge.**

This overarching goal is driven by several key motivations:
1. **Addressing RL's Sample Inefficiency for Complex Tasks:** Deep reinforcement learning, despite its successes in robotics (manipulation, navigation, locomotion), often requires an enormous number of samples to learn complex behaviors. Starting from a random policy is highly inefficient, leading to prolonged training times and practical limitations.
2. **Mimicking Human Learning Strategies:** Humans typically learn complex skills by starting with simpler tasks and progressively increasing difficulty. Curriculum learning is inspired by this natural progression, but its successful application in RL is hampered by the difficulty of manual curriculum design.
3. **Overcoming Limitations of Manual Curriculum Design:** Manually crafting curricula demands significant human expertise, time, and trial-and-error. Such hand-designed curricula are often task-specific and do not generalize well, severely limiting their broader applicability across diverse robotics domains.
4. **Leveraging LLMs' Emerging Capabilities:** Recent advancements have shown that LLMs possess extensive world knowledge, strong high-level planning abilities, and proficiency in translating natural language into executable code. These capabilities present a significant opportunity to automate the traditionally human-intensive process of curriculum design. Specifically, LLMs can be used to:
 - Break down complex target tasks into a sequence of simpler, more manageable subtasks.
 - Decompose skills required for these subtasks.
 - Generate appropriate reward functions and goal distributions in the form of executable code.
5. **Achieving Task-Level Curriculum Automation:** While some existing automatic curriculum learning methods focus on state-space or environment variations, a robust solution for *task-level* curriculum design – which involves defining distinct subtask objectives and their associated learning mechanisms – remains a significant challenge. CurricuLLM aims to fill this gap.

Formally, the problem is defined as generating an ordered list of tasks $C = [m_1, m_2, \ldots, m_N]$ (where $m_T$ is the target task) that helps in learning a policy $\pi$ maximizing the cumulative reward on the target task $m_T$. Specifically, the objective is to find a curriculum $C$ (represented by a sequence of reward functions and goal distributions) that maximizes the performance of the trained policy on the target task: $\arg \max_C V_{m_T}(\pi_C)$.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

CurricuLLM is designed as a three-step iterative framework that leverages LLMs for sequential curriculum design, task code generation, and policy evaluation (illustrated in Figure 1). The process iteratively refines a robot's policy by guiding it through progressively more complex subtasks.

**Step 1: Curriculum Design (Generating Sequence of Language Description)**
- **Input:** The process begins by providing a "curriculum generation LLM" with natural language descriptions of the robot, the environment, and the ultimate target task ($l_T$ and $l_E$).
- **Output:** The LLM generates a sequence of *natural language descriptions* of subtasks, $C_l = [l_1, l_2, \ldots, l_N]$, which form the curriculum.
- **Constraints & Guidance:**
 - The target task ($m_T$) is explicitly designated as the final task ($m_N$) in the curriculum.
 - The LLM is instructed to describe subtasks using *available state variables* (e.g., base linear velocity, angular velocity, joint angles). This "grounding" in environmental variables is crucial for later code generation and reduces ambiguity.
 - Prompts include instructions for conciseness, limiting the number of subtasks (e.g., max 5), and prioritizing stability in early stages while shifting focus to command tracking later.
- **Example:** For a humanoid learning to run, the LLM might generate subtasks like "Basic Stability Learning," "Learn to Walk," and "Increase Speed," with each having specific velocity and heading angle command ranges.

**Step 2: Task Code Sampling (Translating Natural Language into Executable Task Code)**
- **Input:** For each subtask $l_n$ in the generated curriculum, a "task code generation LLM" receives its language description ($l_n$), environment description ($l_E$), and a history ($h$) of previously learned tasks and their corresponding task codes.
- **Process:** The LLM samples $K$ different *task code candidates* for the current subtask, each comprising a reward function ($r_n^k$) and a goal distribution ($\rho_{g,n}^k$), both expressed as executable code.
- **Policy Training:** These $K$ task codes are then used to fine-tune $K$ candidate policies ($\pi_n^k$), starting from the optimal policy trained for the *previous* subtask ($\pi_{n-1}^*$). This sequential fine-tuning accelerates learning.
- **Strategies for High-Quality Task Code:**
 - **Grounding:** The state-variable-grounded descriptions from Step 1 minimize ambiguity.
 - **Normalized Variables:** Providing normalized state variables helps the LLM find appropriate weighting parameters for reward functions without extensive trial-and-error.
 - **Historical Awareness:** The history $h$ (analogous to Chain-of-Thought prompting) guides the LLM to build upon prior knowledge, generating more complex reward functions for later stages and preventing "forgetting" of previously learned skills by including elements of past rewards.
 - **Weight Adjustment:** The LLM is instructed to emphasize current task rewards while retaining relevance to previous tasks.
- **Efficiency:** The paper notes that typically no more than 5 samples ($K=5$) are needed to capture feasible task codes and handle variations.

**Step 3: Optimal Policy Selection (Evaluating Trained Policies)**
- **Input:** After training $K$ candidate policies, an "evaluation LLM" receives the language description of the current subtask ($l_n$) and *trajectory rollout statistics* ($\tau_n^k$) from each of the $K$ trained policies. It also receives the task history ($h$).
- **Process:** The evaluation LLM analyzes the trajectory statistics to determine which policy ($\pi_n^*$) best aligns with the current subtask's description and objectives, while also considering performance on previous subtasks (to avoid forgetting).
- **Key Feature:** Crucially, this evaluation does *not* rely on human feedback or predefined quantitative metrics specific to the subtask. Instead, it leverages the LLM's understanding of language descriptions and its ability to interpret abstract trajectory statistics.
- **Output:** The selected optimal policy $\pi_n^*$ is then stored and used as the pre-trained starting point for the *next* subtask. The task history $h$ is updated with the details of the selected task code.
- **RL Algorithms:** The framework is compatible with various RL algorithms. In the experiments, Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) is used for Gymnasium environments, and Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) for the Berkeley Humanoid. For the Humanoid, pre-defined safety reward functions and termination conditions are also integrated to ensure desirable real-world motion.

By structuring the curriculum design, code generation, and policy evaluation within this LLM-driven feedback loop, CurricuLLM aims to autonomously navigate the complexities of teaching robots new skills.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

CurricuLLM's effectiveness was evaluated across a diverse range of robotics tasks, including manipulation, navigation, and locomotion, demonstrating its ability to facilitate the learning of complex control policies.

**A. Gymnasium Environments (Manipulation and Navigation)**
- **Tasks:** Fetch-Slide, Fetch-Push (7DOF Fetch robot manipulating blocks), and AntMaze-UMaze (Ant robot navigating a U-shaped maze).
- **Baselines:** Soft Actor-Critic (SAC), Hindsight Experience Replay (HER), and LLM-zeroshot (LLM generating a single reward function for the target task directly).
- **Results (Figure 5):**
 - CurricuLLM achieved comparable or superior success rates compared to all baselines across all tasks.
 - **AntMaze-UMaze:** CurricuLLM significantly outperformed all baselines, highlighting its advantage in more challenging, complex navigation scenarios where a structured curriculum is crucial. In this environment, CurricuLLM was able to dynamically adjust the goal distribution, enhancing learning.
 - **Fetch Environments:** While HER is specialized for goal-conditioned sparse reward problems, CurricuLLM achieved comparable results by primarily modifying reward functions, demonstrating its versatility.
 - **LLM-zeroshot:** This baseline generally performed poorly, underscoring that a direct, one-shot reward generation from an LLM for complex tasks is often insufficient.
 - **Variance:** CurricuLLM exhibited higher variance in success rates, indicating that the quality of the generated curriculum can influence performance.

- **Quality of Reward Code (Figure 6):**
 - **CurricuLLM:** The reward code generated by CurricuLLM for AntMaze demonstrated a sophisticated understanding, incorporating terms that encourage diverse behaviors beneficial for the target task, such as fast movement for exploration and angular velocity regulation for stability.
 - **LLM-zeroshot:** In contrast, the zero-shot generated reward was sparse and focused almost exclusively on the final goal, lacking the intermediate motivators essential for effective learning.
 - **Conclusion:** This comparison validates CurricuLLM's two-step approach (curriculum generation followed by evolving task code), showing it effectively generates informative and progressive reward functions.

**B. Berkeley Humanoid (High-Dimensional Locomotion)**
- **Task:** Controlling the Berkeley Humanoid (45 state dimensions, 12 action dimensions) to run by following random velocity and heading angle commands within a wide range.
- **Baselines:** Two human-designed reward functions (one from the original paper, one with an additional "alive bonus") and LLM-zeroshot. HER was excluded due to its limited applicability. PPO was used as the RL algorithm. Pre-defined safety rewards and termination conditions were added for real-world considerations.
- **Simulation Results (Table I):**
 - **CurricuLLM:** Achieved comparable tracking error and failure rate to the complex human-designed reward functions. This is a significant finding, as human-designed rewards often require extensive, tedious trial-and-error by robot designers.
 - **LLM-zeroshot:** Policies trained with zero-shot LLM rewards were often unstable (high failure rates) or suboptimal (sacrificing tracking for stability), reflected in high standard deviations.
 - **Human Reward with Alive Bonus:** Interestingly, this baseline converged to a standing behavior, ignoring commands. This highlights the extreme sensitivity of reward tuning, even for human experts, where a seemingly minor addition can drastically alter the learned policy.
 - **Conclusion:** CurricuLLM demonstrated its ability to create effective curricula and reward functions for high-dimensional, complex locomotion tasks without prior human knowledge, matching or even outperforming expert-tuned baselines.

- **Hardware Validation (Figure 8):**
 - The policy learned through CurricuLLM was successfully deployed on the physical Berkeley Humanoid robot.
 - Despite hardware-specific restrictions on command velocity ranges during testing, the robot's base velocity accurately tracked the given commands.
 - **Conclusion:** This real-world validation confirms the practical applicability and effectiveness of CurricuLLM, demonstrating that its generated policies can transfer robustly from simulation to hardware.

Overall, the findings strongly support CurricuLLM's ability to automate the design of task curricula, generate appropriate reward functions, and learn complex robot skills across diverse environments, ultimately leading to robust and deployable control policies.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

CurricuLLM represents a significant advancement in the intersection of large language models, reinforcement learning, and robotics. Its primary significance lies in providing an **automated, LLM-driven framework for designing task-level curricula**, a process traditionally plagued by the need for extensive human expertise and intervention.

The potential impacts of this work are manifold:

1. **Reduced Human Effort and Domain Expertise:** By automating the design of subtask sequences, reward functions, and goal distributions, CurricuLLM drastically lowers the barrier to entry for developing complex robot skills. Robot engineers and researchers can spend less time on tedious reward shaping and curriculum crafting, allowing them to focus on higher-level problem definitions. This democratizes access to sophisticated RL techniques.
2. **Enhanced Efficiency and Learnability:** The progressive nature of curriculum learning, effectively designed by CurricuLLM, addresses the notorious sample inefficiency of RL for complex tasks. By guiding agents through a structured learning path, the system can achieve target task performance more reliably and potentially faster, as demonstrated in the AntMaze and Humanoid experiments.
3. **Generalization Across Diverse Robotics Tasks:** The successful evaluation across manipulation (Fetch-Slide, Fetch-Push), navigation (AntMaze-UMaze), and locomotion (Berkeley Humanoid) tasks showcases the versatility and generalizability of the CurricuLLM framework. This suggests its applicability beyond the tested domains to a wide array of robotic challenges.
4. **Robust Real-World Deployment:** The successful hardware validation on the Berkeley Humanoid is a critical achievement. It demonstrates that policies learned through LLM-designed curricula are not just effective in simulation but also robust enough to transfer to the physical world, a major step towards practical embodied AI.
5. **Advancing LLM Capabilities in Embodied AI:** CurricuLLM pushes the boundaries of how LLMs are utilized in robotics. Beyond simple task planning or code generation, it leverages LLMs for sophisticated reasoning about task progression, dynamic reward engineering, and policy evaluation based on trajectory semantics. This highlights LLMs' potential as intelligent "teachers" for robotic agents.
6. **Towards Adaptive Learning Systems:** The proposed feedback loop, where LLMs generate, evaluate, and refine task codes and policies, lays the groundwork for more adaptive and autonomous robot learning systems. The ability to integrate training progress (e.g., learning curves) into this feedback loop, as mentioned in future work, could lead to self-improving curricula and even more efficient learning.

In conclusion, CurricuLLM not only provides a powerful tool for developing advanced robot skills but also opens new avenues for research into intelligent curriculum design and the deeper integration of large language models with robotic control. Its ability to generate effective, human-competitive curricula and demonstrate real-world utility marks a significant stride towards truly autonomous and adaptive robotic learning.
