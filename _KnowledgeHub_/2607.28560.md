# X-NavDP: Generalizing Navigation Diffusion Policy to Novel Behavior and Embodiments with Group Q-score Reweighted Matching

> [!summary] Summary
> The X-NavDP framework enhances pretrained diffusion navigation policies through reinforcement learning post-training, improving generalization to diverse robotic embodiments and complex, novel behaviors. The method achieved an average success rate of 84.28% and SPL of 77.19% in simulation across varied robots and scenes, and showed substantial gains in real-world deployment without fine-tuning, improving success in hard cases from 10% to 65%.

## Problem

- Existing imitation learning approaches for diffusion navigation policies, supervised by oracle planners, suffer from a domain gap when robots operate with partial local observations, limiting failure recovery and diverse behavior generation.
- Policies trained on 'embodiment-blind' simulation data often exhibit inconsistent performance when deployed on different robot platforms due to unaddressed robot-specific dynamics and physical constraints.
- Reinforcement learning fine-tuning of diffusion policies is challenging, with policy gradient methods facing instability due to complex likelihood calculations, and latent space methods restricting necessary behavioral exploration.

## Method

- **X-NavDP** employs a data-efficient ==RL post-training== pipeline with massive parallelization in IsaacLab, optimizing a modified NavDP backbone for 500+ heterogeneous robots across diverse scenes.
- A ==self-bootstrapped perturbation== module leverages the policy's internal goal-agnostic branch to generate diverse trajectories (e.g., lateral, backward-recovery, detours) for robust exploration without unstructured noise.
- ==Group Q-score Reweighted Matching (GQRM)== refines score matching by normalizing Q-scores within same-state candidate groups, providing stable and efficient actor updates even for sparse and hard navigation states.

## Results

- X-NavDP improved the overall average Success Rate (SR) from **61.20% (NavDP) to 84.28%** and Success Weighted by Path Length (SPL) from **58.95% to 77.19%** across all evaluated embodiments and scene types in simulation.
- Simulation-trained X-NavDP policies achieved substantially higher real-world success rates in complex layouts without fine-tuning, improving from **10% to 65%** in hard cases compared to baselines.
- Ablation studies confirmed GQRM's contribution, raising SR/SPL from **71.57/67.32** (DPMD) to **80.48/74.78**, and demonstrated the criticality of the self-bootstrapped perturbation for diverse behavior generation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Normalizing Q-scores within same-state candidate groups, rather than globally, provides more effective learning signals for RL fine-tuning of diffusion policies, particularly for challenging, low-return states.
> - Structured exploration, facilitated by self-bootstrapped perturbation from a policy's own goal-agnostic capabilities, is crucial for acquiring novel behaviors like failure recovery and complex detours beyond initial imitation.
> - Embodiment-modulated architecture, incorporating learned robot embeddings, enables a single navigation policy to generalize robustly across diverse robot morphologies (wheeled, quadruped, humanoid).

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of general visual navigation policies using large-scale simulation datasets has gained prominence in robotics. Many current approaches leverage imitation learning to train diffusion-based navigation policies, such as Diffusion Policy, NoMaD, and NavDP. These policies are typically supervised by expert trajectories generated by an oracle global planner. This paradigm offers advantages in data scalability and zero-shot generalization across various scenes and robotic embodiments.

However, several limitations are inherent in this imitation learning pipeline. First, real-world robots operate with partial local visual observations, unlike the fully-informed oracle planners that generate training data. This discrepancy can lead to decision ambiguity and limit a policy's ability to explore alternative behaviors or recover from failures in challenging scenarios like escaping dead ends or detouring long obstacles. Second, the data generation process often overlooks robot-specific dynamics and physical motion constraints, which can result in inconsistent navigation performance when policies are transferred across different robot platforms.

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has been identified as a potential method to address these limitations by enabling policies to learn through interactive trial-and-error, adapting to local observations and specific robot dynamics. Despite this potential, fine-tuning pretrained diffusion navigation policies with online RL presents its own challenges. Policy gradient-based methods, such as DDPO and DPPO, often suffer from training instability due to the complex and chained likelihood calculations involved in the diffusion denoising process. Alternatively, latent space-based methods can limit policy exploration by freezing core diffusion parameters, hindering the generation of diverse behaviors necessary for complex, long-horizon navigation. Score-based reweighted methods, like DPMD, offer a more stable alternative by preserving pretrained priors and avoiding issues such as catastrophic forgetting; however, their application to high-dimensional visual inputs and long-horizon navigation has remained an area requiring further investigation. Previous work, such as NavDP-RL, applied online RL to fine-tune NavDP but utilized only the inherent stochasticity of diffusion sampling for exploration, without explicit mechanisms for generating behaviorally diverse trajectories.

This work contributes to the field by proposing a novel framework designed to address these combined challenges, offering a data-efficient and stable method for RL post-training of navigation diffusion policies. It aims to bridge the gap between simulation-trained policies and their real-world deployment by enhancing generalization to diverse embodiments and enabling complex, reactive behaviors.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust, data-efficient, and stable reinforcement learning (RL) post-training framework that can significantly improve the generalization capabilities of pretrained navigation diffusion policies. This framework, named X-NavDP, aims to enable policies to adapt to diverse robotic embodiments and master novel, complex navigation behaviors that are not adequately captured by traditional imitation learning from oracle demonstrations.

The motivation for pursuing this objective stems from several identified limitations of existing approaches:

1. **Limited Generalization to Diverse Embodiments**: Current large-scale simulation datasets for visual navigation often generate expert demonstrations without considering the unique dynamics, kinematics, and physical constraints of different robot platforms (e.g., wheeled, quadruped, humanoid). Policies trained on such "embodiment-blind" data exhibit unstable and inconsistent performance when deployed on varied hardware. The motivation is to introduce mechanisms that allow a single policy to adapt effectively to robot-specific characteristics, thereby strengthening cross-embodiment navigation.
2. **Inadequate Performance in Challenging Scenarios**: Policies trained through imitation learning, even those using diffusion models, tend to mimic globally optimal trajectories derived from fully-informed planners. In real-world scenarios, robots operate with partial local observations, leading to a critical domain gap. This often results in a lack of autonomous failure recovery capabilities and difficulties in tough scenarios such as escaping dead ends, navigating narrow passages, or performing long detours around obstacles. The motivation is to enable policies to learn diverse local reactive behaviors through interactive experience, allowing them to handle unforeseen situations and demonstrate robust problem-solving skills.
3. **Technical Bottlenecks in RL Fine-Tuning of Diffusion Policies**: While RL offers a principled way to refine policies through self-collected experience, applying it to pretrained diffusion models is challenging. Policy gradient methods for diffusion policies (e.g., DDPO, DPPO) often suffer from severe training instability due to the intricate and computationally intensive likelihood calculations across the diffusion chain. Conversely, methods that operate in the latent space by freezing core diffusion parameters (e.g., DSRL, some latent-space methods) can unduly restrict policy exploration, preventing the emergence of new, complex behaviors beyond the initial policy distribution. The motivation is to develop an RL fine-tuning mechanism that is both stable and efficient, capable of preserving the pretrained diffusion prior while also facilitating substantial behavioral improvement and exploration. Specifically, to overcome the limitations of globally normalized Q-scores in reweighted score matching methods (like DPMD) that often overlook valuable learning signals from low-return, hard-to-solve states.

By addressing these motivations, the research aims to produce more capable, adaptable, and robust visual navigation policies that can perform reliably across a broader spectrum of environments and robotic platforms.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The X-NavDP framework enhances pretrained diffusion navigation policies through an RL post-training pipeline, focusing on data efficiency, stability, and generalization across embodiments. The overall pipeline integrates a modified NavDP backbone with several complementary modules.

#### 3.1. RL Post-Training Framework

The system utilizes massive parallelization for data-efficient RL training, deploying on IsaacLab. This setup supports over 500 heterogeneous robots (wheeled, quadruped, humanoid) interacting simultaneously across more than 50 scenes. All collected rollouts are aggregated into a shared replay buffer, facilitating joint cross-embodiment policy optimization.

A hierarchical control stack is employed: the diffusion policy outputs short-horizon waypoint chunks (H-step future waypoints), a unified Model Predictive Control (MPC) converts these into base velocity commands, and an embodiment-specific locomotion policy executes these commands. The MPC and locomotion controllers operate at 25 Hz, while the navigation policy is invoked chunk-wise, with each predicted trajectory tracked for 3 seconds. The cumulative reward over this 3-second interval is recorded as a single macro-step transition, which reduces inference overhead and aligns value learning with trajectory-level actions.

The reward structure incorporates six components: a living regularizer, obstacle-aware path progress, goal arrival, collision penalty, immobility penalty, and humanoid stability penalty. The path progress reward is calculated using A\* search on an occupancy map, favoring paths with greater clearance and rewarding forward progress while accounting for turns.

#### 3.2. Self-Bootstrapped Perturbation

To address the limited exploration capabilities of pretrained goal-conditioned policies, which tend to generate conservative, forward-moving trajectories, the framework introduces a self-bootstrapped perturbation module. This module leverages the pretrained policy's internal goal-agnostic branch to diversify navigation behaviors without resorting to unstructured noise.

For each observation, the policy generates two types of trajectories: a goal-conditioned trajectory ($\tilde{\tau}_{pointgoal}$) and a goal-agnostic trajectory ($\tilde{\tau}_{nogoal}$). These are combined to form a mixed trajectory: $\tau_{mixed} = s \odot (\tilde{\tau}_{pointgoal} + \lambda \tilde{\tau}_{nogoal})$. Here, $\lambda$ is a signed mixing coefficient, and $s$ is a sign vector that independently flips the x and y coordinates of the entire trajectory chunk with a Bernoulli probability $\epsilon$. This mechanism enables the generation of lateral, backward-recovery, and detour trajectories, which are crucial for navigating complex environments, while preserving the inherent smoothness and dynamic feasibility of the pretrained waypoints. This perturbed set is consistently used for both data collection and actor updates, maintaining alignment between exploration and policy improvement.

#### 3.3. GQRM: Group Q-score Reweighted Matching

The GQRM framework refines the DPMD (Diffusion Policy Mirror Descent) reweighted score matching approach to ensure stable and efficient actor updates, particularly for sparse and rarely visited hard navigation states. Unlike DPMD, which normalizes Q-scores globally across a minibatch, GQRM performs normalization *within each same-state candidate group*.

For a given state $s$, a group of denoised action candidates $a_0 \sim \pi_{old}(\cdot|s)$ is generated. From these, the group mean ($\bar{Q}_G(s)$) and standard deviation ($\sigma_G(s)$) of their Q-values are computed. The group-normalized value ($e_{Q_G}(s, a_0)$) is then calculated as a clipped and scaled advantage, allowing for effective discrimination even when all absolute Q-values are low.

The GQRM objective is formulated as:
$L_{GQRM}(\theta; s, t) = E_{a_0 \sim \pi_{old}(\cdot|s), a_t \sim q_{t|0}(\cdot|a_0)} \left[ \exp\left( \frac{e_{Q_G}(s, a_0)}{\lambda} \right) \left\| s_\theta(a_t; s, t) - \nabla_{a_t} \log q_{t|0}(a_t|a_0) \right\|^2 \right]$.
Here, $\lambda$ is a temperature parameter controlling the peakedness of the actor weights. This approach ensures that the actor learns to shift probability mass towards locally superior candidates within a specific state's context, providing meaningful gradients for hard states. In practice, only a subset of top-k candidates with positive advantage are retained to reduce noise and computational cost.

#### 3.4. Cross-Embodiment Training and Closed-Loop Execution

To enable a single policy to generalize across different robot morphologies, the NavDP model is architecturally modified with an embodiment-modulated module. A learned robot embedding ($z_e$) is injected at two points within the diffusion decoder:
1. **Action Token Modulation**: The robot embedding shifts the action-token features before they enter the decoder ($u_e = u + f_{\Delta}(z_e)$).
2. **Trajectory Feature Modulation**: The same robot embedding generates FiLM (Feature-wise Linear Modulation) parameters to modulate the hidden trajectory features after the decoder ($h_e = (1 + \Delta\gamma_e) \odot h + \Delta\beta_e$).
This dual modulation allows the shared noise predictor to generate embodiment-specific trajectories without requiring separate policy weights for each robot.

For improved temporal consistency during deployment, a lightweight **Closed-Loop Temporal Guidance** (inspired by Real-Time Chunking, RTC) is implemented. This guidance term reduces discontinuities between consecutive receding-horizon predictions. It applies a guidance gradient during DDPM sampling that encourages the current prediction to align with the previously committed trajectory, with decaying weights that prioritize near-term consistency while allowing replanning for the distant future.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were designed to evaluate X-NavDP's performance against strong baselines across various embodiments and scenes, its ability to induce new behaviors, and the contribution of its individual components.

#### 4.1. Experimental Setup

- **Simulation Environment**: IsaacLab, utilizing scenes from GRScenes-100.
 - **Training Set**: 56 scenes (47 home, 9 commercial) for RL post-training.
 - **Evaluation Set**: 40 held-out, unseen scenes (20 home, 20 commercial).
 - **Embodiments**: Dingo (differential-drive wheeled robot), Unitree Go2 (quadruped), and Unitree G1 (humanoid).
- **Real-World Deployment**: Lab, hall, and office environments.
 - **Robots**: Turtlebot, Unitree Go2, and Unitree G1, equipped with onboard RGB-D cameras.
 - **Evaluation**: 10 trials per real-world setting, using simulation-trained policies without sim-to-real fine-tuning.
- **Metrics**:
 - **Success Rate (SR)**: Percentage of episodes reaching within 0.5 m of the goal.
 - **Success Weighted by Path Length (SPL)**: A metric reflecting both success and path efficiency.
- **Baselines**: iPlanner, ViPlanner, NavDP, NavOL, SIDP, and NavDP-RL.
- **Ablation Studies**: Conducted on a smaller 24-scene subset (18 home, 6 commercial) for efficiency.

#### 4.2. Main Results

**Performance in Simulation (Table 1)**:
X-NavDP demonstrated consistent superiority over the NavDP base policy and other baselines across all evaluated embodiments and scene types.
- The overall average Success Rate (SR) improved from 61.20% (NavDP) to 84.28% (X-NavDP).
- The overall average SPL increased from 58.95% (NavDP) to 77.19% (X-NavDP).
- The improvements were particularly notable for humanoid robots (Unitree G1), where X-NavDP raised SR from 64.35% to 84.25% in commercial scenes and from 50.70% to 84.50% in home scenes. These consistent gains across diverse embodiments and scenes indicate strong cross-embodiment generalization.

**Performance in Real-World Deployment (Table 2)**:
X-NavDP policies, trained solely in simulation, achieved substantially higher success rates in real-world complex layouts compared to baselines, without any specific real-world fine-tuning.
- For instance, in a lab setting, X-NavDP achieved 60% SR for wheeled robots, where NavDP and other baselines recorded 0%.
- In a hall environment, X-NavDP improved quadruped SR from 30% (NavDP) to 60%.
- The abstract reports an overall improvement in real-world hard cases from 10% to 65%.

**Qualitative Observations (Figure 3)**:
Qualitative analysis revealed that the fine-tuned X-NavDP policy acquired new navigation capabilities not present in the original NavDP policy. These included:
- **Failure Recovery**: Successfully backing out of dead ends or confined spaces.
- **Detouring**: Effectively navigating around long or complex obstacles.
- **Safer Path Selection**: Choosing less direct but safer routes when multiple options were available.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies

**RL Fine-Tuning Method Comparison (Table 3)**:
- **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)** on collected embodiment-specific data yielded 53.50/50.84 SR/SPL, showing limited improvement.
- **Policy-gradient methods (DPPO)** exhibited instability, leading to degraded performance (33.98/33.28 SR/SPL).
- **DSRL** nearly collapsed (13.65/12.26 SR/SPL), suggesting that optimizing only initial noise is insufficient.
- **DPMD-original** (reweighted score matching without group normalization) provided a stronger baseline (71.57/67.32 SR/SPL).
- **X-NavDP** (incorporating group Q-score reweighted matching) further improved performance to 80.48/74.78 SR/SPL, highlighting the importance of same-state Q-score normalization for efficient learning, especially in difficult states.

**Exploration Policy Ablation (Figure 4, Table 6)**:
- Removing both **goal-agnostic perturbation** and **trajectory reverse** components led to a collapse in training performance (6.23/4.75 SR/SPL), indicating their criticality for diverse exploration.
- Employing either goal-agnostic perturbation (without reverse) or reverse exploration alone recovered substantial performance (65.51/62.87 and 77.89/72.18 SR/SPL, respectively).
- The **full exploration policy** achieved the best overall performance (80.48/74.78 SR/SPL), demonstrating that structured, diverse exploration is crucial.
- A variant using fixed-weight mixing ("w/o extrapolation") underperformed the full policy, suggesting that extrapolative perturbation is important for generating behaviors outside the pretrained distribution.

**Cross-Embodiment Modulation Ablation (Table 5)**:
- Both **Soft-Prompt Concat** and **Embodiment FiLM Modulation** strategies benefited from scaling up training scenes (from 24 to 56).
- **Embodiment FiLM Modulation** scaled more effectively, achieving an overall SR/SPL of 84.28/77.19 on 56 scenes, compared to Soft-Prompt Concat's 82.84/75.99.
- FiLM modulation demonstrated particular benefits for humanoid robots, suggesting it provides a more robust mechanism for adapting to diverse robot morphologies.

**Closed-Loop RTC Guidance Ablation (Table 7)**:
- The **RTC guidance** (used during deployment for temporal consistency) provided a consistent but moderate improvement.
- Full X-NavDP (with RTC) achieved an overall SR/SPL of 84.28/77.19, compared to 82.17/75.81 without RTC, indicating its role in reducing discontinuities in consecutive predictions.

#### 4.4. Failure Case Analysis

The paper identifies several persistent failure modes:
- **Embodiment Constraints**: Humanoid robots (Unitree G1) struggled in narrow passages due to their bulk and turning radius, sometimes contacting obstacles.
- **Limited Long-Horizon Memory**: During long detours, the policy occasionally lost trajectory consistency and reverted to its original path.
- **Imperfect Obstacle Perception**: Transparent and hollow obstacles (e.g., glass partitions, perforated panels) were challenging to perceive via RGB and resulted in unreliable depth measurements.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of X-NavDP represents a notable advancement in the field of generalizable visual navigation for robotics, primarily through its effective integration of reinforcement learning with diffusion policies.

One key significance is the **substantial improvement in generalization capabilities** demonstrated by X-NavDP. The framework successfully enables pretrained navigation diffusion policies to adapt to diverse robotic embodiments—including wheeled, quadruped, and humanoid platforms—and acquire novel, complex navigation behaviors. This includes crucial skills like autonomously recovering from dead ends, performing long detours, and selecting safer paths, which were often absent in policies trained solely through imitation learning. This enhancement allows for more robust robot operation in varied and unconstrained environments.

Furthermore, the research addresses critical **technical challenges in applying RL to diffusion models**. The proposed Group Q-score Reweighted Matching (GQRM) framework offers a data-efficient and stable method for fine-tuning diffusion policies, circumventing the instability issues associated with policy gradient methods and the restricted exploration of latent space approaches. The innovation of within-group Q-score normalization is particularly impactful, as it enables effective learning even in states with uniformly low Q-values, thereby allowing the policy to discriminate and improve upon locally better actions in challenging scenarios. This contributes a valuable methodological tool for future research in RL for generative models.

The strong **sim-to-real transfer performance** of X-NavDP, where simulation-trained policies achieve high success rates in real-world settings without additional fine-tuning, highlights its practical applicability. This capability reduces the dependency on extensive and costly real-world data collection and training, accelerating the deployment of autonomous navigation systems.

The identified **limitations and failure modes** also provide clear directions for future research. Addressing issues such as limited long-horizon memory, improving semantic obstacle understanding, and refining RGB perception for challenging obstacles (e.g., transparent surfaces) can further enhance the system's capabilities.

The work lays a foundation for future advancements by suggesting pathways for **scaling the system** to an even wider array of scenarios and robot morphologies. The potential for investigating tighter coordination between locomotion and navigation policies, as well as transferring this post-training framework to broader embodied AI tasks like vision-language navigation, indicates a sustained impact on the development of more intelligent and adaptable robotic systems.
