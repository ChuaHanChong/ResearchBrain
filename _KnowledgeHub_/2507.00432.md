# Does Math Reasoning Improve General LLM Capabilities? Understanding Transferability of LLM Reasoning

> [!summary] Summary
> This research investigates how fine-tuning methods for math reasoning affect the broader capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs), revealing that Reinforcement Learning (RL) approaches foster superior generalization across diverse reasoning and non-reasoning tasks compared to Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT). RL-tuned models successfully transfer math gains and preserve performance on general tasks, while SFT models often exhibit narrow specialization and degradation in other domains.

## Problem

- It is unclear whether impressive gains in specialized math reasoning translate into broader, general problem-solving abilities in LLMs or merely reflect narrow overfitting.
- Current post-training recipes, particularly those relying on Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) with distilled data, might lead to catastrophic forgetting or reduced versatility in other tasks.
- A lack of mechanistic understanding exists regarding why certain fine-tuning paradigms succeed or fail in generalizing LLM knowledge across diverse domains.

## Method

- An initial audit of over 20 open-weight LLMs was conducted across math, other reasoning, and non-reasoning benchmarks, utilizing a novel ==Transferability Index (TI)== to quantify generalization.
- Controlled experiments on Qwen3-14B compared Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and Reinforcement Learning (RL) using identical math-only data to isolate the effects of each tuning method.
- Mechanistic analyses, including ==latent-space PCA== and token-space distribution analysis (KL-divergence, rank shifts), were performed to understand internal model changes induced by different fine-tuning approaches.

## Results

- RL-tuned models exhibited superior generalization, showing a **+17.1%** improvement on tasks like LiveCodeBench2 over SFT variants, and recovered or exceeded base performance on non-reasoning tasks where SFT models often declined.
- PCA analysis revealed RL caused significantly lower latent-space shifts across all task categories, indicating better preservation of the model's internal representation structure than SFT, which induced substantial, scattered shifts.
- KL-divergence analysis demonstrated that RL models had significantly lower token distribution shifts from the backbone and selectively adjusted a small set of task-relevant tokens, unlike SFT which perturbed a much larger number of irrelevant tokens.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) for math reasoning often leads to narrow specialization, failing to transfer gains to other domains and frequently causing performance degradation on non-reasoning tasks.
> - Reinforcement Learning (RL) consistently promotes broad generalization, improving math reasoning while positively transferring gains to other reasoning tasks and maintaining or improving performance on non-reasoning tasks.
> - RL-based fine-tuning induces significantly smaller latent-space shifts and less perturbation to output token distributions compared to SFT, better preserving the model's original feature geometry and overall competence.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of Large Language Models (LLMs) has witnessed an astonishing pace of advancement, particularly in specialized reasoning tasks. Mathematical reasoning, in particular, has emerged as a "poster child" for demonstrating progress, with models rapidly achieving and even surpassing human-level performance on benchmarks such as MATH and AIME. This success is often attributed to structured problem-solving, unambiguous solutions, and straightforward evaluation metrics, making math an ideal proxy for measuring LLM reasoning capabilities. Consequently, there's been a fervent race to develop increasingly sophisticated fine-tuning techniques (e.g., Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting, various supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and reinforcement learning (RL) methods) to optimize models for these benchmarks.

However, a critical question arises from this specialized success: Do these impressive gains in narrow mathematical reasoning translate into broader, general problem-solving abilities, or do they merely reflect narrow overfitting? Real-world applications of LLMs, from scientific discovery to everyday instruction following, demand a far wider range of linguistic and commonsense competence that math-centric benchmarks alone do not fully encompass. Previous works, such as discussions around OpenAI's o1 model, have hinted at potential trade-offs, where models excelling in STEM benchmarks might exhibit reduced versatility in other tasks.

This paper directly addresses this crucial gap by rigorously investigating the transferability of mathematical reasoning gains to a diverse suite of other reasoning and non-reasoning domains. It critically evaluates whether the current "post-training recipes," especially those relying on SFT-distilled data for reasoning, are optimal for developing truly general-purpose LLMs. By providing a deep, mechanistic analysis—beyond mere performance metrics—into *why* certain fine-tuning paradigms succeed or fail in generalization, the work seeks to refine the fundamental understanding of how LLMs learn and transfer knowledge. It contributes to the ongoing discourse on catastrophic forgetting and the development of robust, versatile AI systems capable of advanced reasoning across numerous contexts.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation of this research stems from a critical observation in the current LLM landscape: the rapid advancements in math reasoning capabilities raise questions about their broader utility. While LLMs are achieving unprecedented performance on math benchmarks, it is unclear whether these specialized gains translate to a general improvement in problem-solving or merely represent narrow specialization.

Against this backdrop, the **key objectives** of the paper are:

1. **Evaluate Transferability:** To quantitatively assess whether improved math reasoning abilities in LLMs transfer to a broad spectrum of other reasoning tasks (e.g., scientific QA, coding, agent planning) and non-reasoning tasks (e.g., conversational QA, instruction following).
2. **Identify Driving Factors:** To rigorously investigate which fine-tuning paradigms (specifically Supervised Fine-Tuning vs. Reinforcement Learning) are responsible for observed divergences in transferability.
3. **Provide Mechanistic Insights:** To understand *why* certain fine-tuning methods lead to better or worse generalization by analyzing changes in the model's internal representations (latent space) and output token distributions.
4. **Inform Post-Training Strategies:** To offer data-driven recommendations for designing more effective and generalizable post-training recipes for LLMs.

The **motivation** is multifaceted:
- **Addressing the "Poster Child" Dilemma:** Math reasoning has become a significant indicator of LLM progress. The authors are motivated to probe whether this progress reflects true general intelligence or merely benchmark-specific optimization.
- **Bridging the Gap to Real-World Applications:** Most practical LLM applications require broad linguistic and commonsense capabilities, not just specialized math skills. Understanding transferability is crucial for building models that are genuinely useful across diverse user-facing scenarios.
- **Optimizing LLM Development:** By pinpointing the efficacy of different fine-tuning approaches and their underlying mechanisms, the research aims to guide the LLM community toward developing more robust and versatile models, potentially reducing issues like catastrophic forgetting.
- **Advancing the Vision of AGI:** Enabling machines to reason precisely over math is foundational for automated scientific discovery. However, for a broader vision of Artificial General Intelligence (AGI), these reasoning skills must be seamlessly integrated with and transferred to other cognitive domains.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a multi-pronged approach, combining large-scale empirical evaluation with controlled experiments and mechanistic analysis:

#### 3.1. Initial Large-Scale Audit (Phenomena Observation)

- **Models:** Over 20 diverse, open-weight reasoning-tuned LLMs were selected for evaluation, representing a wide range of architectures, sizes, and training data distributions.
- **Benchmarks:** Models were tested across a comprehensive suite of tasks categorized into three groups:
 - **Math Reasoning:** MATH500, AIME24, AIME25, OlympiadBench (pure mathematical problems).
 - **Other Reasoning:** LiveCodeBench (code generation), GPQA-Diamond (scientific QA), ACPBench (agent planning), HeadQA (medical reasoning) (general reasoning beyond pure math).
 - **Non-Reasoning:** CoQA (conversational QA), IFEval (instruction following), HaluEval (hallucination detection), MC-TACO (temporal commonsense QA) (tasks requiring broader linguistic and commonsense competence).
- **Evaluation Metric:** A novel **Transferability Index (TI)** was proposed. This metric normalizes performance gains across benchmarks, applies a robust gain score (using signed square-root and difficulty weighting), and aggregates these into a group-level Domain Index. Finally, TI quantifies transfer by ratioing improvement in 'other reasoning' or 'non-reasoning' groups to that in the 'math' group. Positive TI indicates successful transfer, negative TI indicates degradation.

#### 3.2. Controlled Study (Identifying Causal Factors)

- **Base Model:** Qwen3-14B-Base was chosen as a consistent starting point to isolate the effects of different fine-tuning methods.
- **Training Data:** A high-quality, math-only dataset comprising 47,000 problems from DeepScaler and SimpleRL (levels 3-5) was curated.
 - For **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)**, detailed Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning traces were generated by a powerful teacher model (Qwen3-32B-Instruct) using rejection sampling to ensure correctness. These CoT traces served as explicit training targets.
 - For **Reinforcement Learning (RL)**, a standard GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) setup was used, where the original answer labels provided the scalar reward signal, aligning with answer correctness.
- **Fine-tuning Implementations:** SFT utilized the LLaMA-Factory framework, while RL was implemented using the Verl framework (Sheng et al., 2025). Hyperparameters were carefully chosen to ensure a fair comparison.
- **Evaluation:** The fine-tuned Qwen3-14B models were evaluated on the same three categories of benchmarks as the initial audit.

#### 3.3. Mechanistic Analysis (Understanding "Why")

To understand the internal changes induced by different fine-tuning methods, two diagnostic tools were employed:
- **Latent-Space Principal Component Analysis (PCA):** Hidden states were extracted from various layers of the models before and after fine-tuning across different query distributions. PCA was applied to these hidden states to visualize and quantify the "PCA shift" – the Euclidean distance between representation centroids, which reflects how much the model's internal feature geometry changes.
- **Token-Space Distribution Analysis:**
 - **KL-Divergence:** Measured the shift in output token distributions from the original backbone model to the fine-tuned model. Higher KL-divergence indicates a greater distributional change.
 - **Token Rank Shift Analysis:** Evaluated how the ranking of tokens in the model's output vocabulary changes after fine-tuning, reflecting shifts in token probabilities.
 - **Qualitative Case Studies:** Examined specific examples to identify *which types* of tokens (e.g., logical structure words vs. content-specific words) were most affected by fine-tuning.

#### 3.4. Ablation Study (Pinpointing RL Components)

- **Framework:** A unified surrogate loss function was developed to explicitly compare SFT and RL objectives along four key levers: (i) **sampling distribution** (off-policy vs. on-policy), (ii) **credit assignment** (uniform vs. advantage-weighted), (iii) **KL regularization** (presence/absence), and (iv) **negative gradient learning** (inherent in RL's advantage function).
- **Settings:** Five different training configurations were tested on a Qwen3-8B-Base model to isolate the impact of these components.
- **Metrics:** Performance across task categories, as well as analysis of gradient norms (to understand training stability) and response length (to infer exploration behavior).

This comprehensive methodological approach allows the researchers to move beyond correlational observations to establish causal relationships between fine-tuning methods and LLM transferability, providing both empirical evidence and mechanistic explanations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several significant findings, providing compelling evidence for the differential impact of SFT and RL on LLM reasoning transferability:

#### 4.1. Observed Performance Discrepancies (Initial Audit & Controlled Study)

- **SFT’s Limited Transfer:** A surprising observation from the initial audit of over 20 open-weight models was that most models excelling in math reasoning (often SFT-tuned) failed to transfer these gains to other reasoning domains and frequently exhibited performance degradation (catastrophic forgetting) on non-reasoning tasks. The Transferability Index (TI) for SFT models on non-reasoning tasks was often negative.
- **RL’s Broad Generalization:** In stark contrast, models fine-tuned with Reinforcement Learning (RL) consistently demonstrated superior generalization. They not only improved math reasoning but also transferred these gains positively to other reasoning tasks and, crucially, maintained or even improved performance on non-reasoning tasks, resulting in positive TI scores across the board (Figure 1, Figure 2, Table 1).
- **Controlled Study Validation:** The controlled experiments on Qwen3-14B, using identical math-only data but different tuning methods (SFT vs. RL), robustly confirmed these patterns. The RL-tuned UniReason model outperformed SFT variants on math, showed significant lifts in "other reasoning" tasks (e.g., +17.1% on LiveCodeBench2 over SFT), and remarkably recovered/exceeded base performance on non-reasoning tasks where SFT models often declined.

#### 4.2. Latent Representation Shifts (PCA Analysis)

- **RL Preserves Structure:** PCA analysis of hidden states revealed that RL-based fine-tuning induces significantly *lower* latent-space shifts across math, other-reasoning, and non-reasoning tasks (Figure 3, Table 2). This indicates that RL better preserves the original geometry of internal features, maintaining a more stable and generalizable representation space.
- **SFT Induces Drift:** SFT, particularly on non-reasoning inputs, caused substantial and scattered shifts in the latent space. This "drift" makes the model's internal representations less aligned with general-domain tasks, contributing to performance degradation.
- **Impact of Model Size & Family:** Larger models (32B vs. 7B) generally showed reduced PCA shifts, indicating greater inherent stability. Llama models, when RL-tuned, exhibited slightly greater resilience to drift in general domains compared to Qwen models, suggesting architectural or pre-training differences influence representational stability.

#### 4.3. Token Distribution Shifts (KL Divergence & Rank Analyses)

- **RL's Minimal Distribution Shift:** KL-divergence analysis consistently showed that RL models exhibited *significantly lower* KL-divergence from the backbone model's token distributions across all task categories compared to SFT models (Figure 4). This implies that RL training causes less perturbation to the overall token generation probabilities.
- **RL’s Reduced Token Rank Shifts:** Correspondingly, RL models demonstrated markedly smaller average token rank shifts, indicating that the relative probabilities of tokens changed less drastically.
- **Selective Token Shifting:** Qualitative analysis further revealed that RL models selectively adjusted a *small set of task-relevant or logical structure tokens* (e.g., "define," "add," "but," "so"). In contrast, SFT models perturbed a *much larger number of irrelevant tokens*, often injecting reasoning-specific vocabulary into non-reasoning prompts, leading to unnecessary "overthinking" and degrading performance (Figure 5, Table 8 in Appendix).

#### 4.4. Ablation Study: Drivers of RL Generalization

- **Sampling Distribution is Critical:** On-policy training (where the model learns from data generated by its current, evolving policy) consistently outperformed off-policy methods (learning from a fixed dataset) in transferability for both SFT and RL. On-policy methods exhibited steadier, more conservative gradient updates, suggesting reduced distribution mismatch and better generalization (Figure 6).
- **Credit Assignment and Negative Examples Matter:** Mechanisms like advantage-weighted credit assignment (focusing updates on salient tokens) and learning from negative examples (inherent in RL) were found to significantly improve transferability. These mechanisms encouraged broader policy exploration, leading to longer and more varied responses (Figure 14 in Appendix).
- **KL Regularization's Subtle Role:** While KL regularization is commonly used in RL, its specific impact on *transferability* in on-policy RL was found to be subtle and limited, suggesting that the on-policy nature itself already provides sufficient constraint against drastic policy shifts.

In summary, the research unequivocally demonstrates that the fine-tuning paradigm is a decisive factor in LLM transferability, with RL-based approaches proving superior in fostering generalized reasoning capabilities while preserving overall model competence.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research carries significant implications for the future development and fine-tuning strategies of large language models, particularly those aspiring to broad, general-purpose intelligence:

1. **Rethinking Post-Training Paradigms:** The most profound impact lies in challenging the conventional reliance on Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) using distilled "teacher" data for enhancing LLM reasoning. While SFT can achieve high performance on specific benchmarks, this work provides strong evidence that it often leads to narrow specialization and catastrophic forgetting of general capabilities. This calls for a fundamental re-evaluation of current post-training recipes and suggests a strong shift towards Reinforcement Learning (RL)-based methodologies.

2. **Guiding Model Development with Mechanistic Insights:** Beyond mere empirical performance, the paper offers crucial mechanistic insights into *why* RL generalizes better than SFT. The findings from latent-space PCA and token-space distribution analysis provide a deeper understanding of how internal representations and output probabilities are altered by different training regimes. This mechanistic understanding is invaluable for guiding future LLM architecture design, regularization techniques, and data curation efforts, enabling developers to build models that inherently resist forgetting and promote robust generalization.

3. **Mitigating Catastrophic Forgetting:** One of the persistent challenges in fine-tuning LLMs is balancing specialized skill acquisition with the retention of broad knowledge. This study demonstrates that RL can effectively improve advanced reasoning capabilities (like math) *without* sacrificing performance on general-domain tasks. This capability to achieve specialized mastery while preserving general competence is critical for creating truly versatile and robust AI systems.

4. **Advancing Towards General AI:** The long-term vision of Artificial General Intelligence (AGI) and automated scientific discovery hinges on LLMs that can not only reason precisely but also fluidly transfer knowledge across diverse domains. By elucidating the factors that drive or hinder transferability, this research contributes directly to the foundational understanding required for building more generally intelligent agents. It provides a roadmap for developing models that are not just "math specialists" but "general problem-solvers."

5. **New Research Avenues:** The study opens several promising directions for future research. This includes exploring hybrid fine-tuning recipes that combine the strengths of both SFT and RL, developing adaptive regularization strategies tailored to different backbone architectures and sizes to further enhance transferability, and creating more comprehensive evaluation tools for multi-modal, long-context, and interactive generation settings, which are increasingly relevant for real-world applications.

In conclusion, this paper delivers a timely and impactful message: while LLMs have made impressive strides in specialized reasoning, particularly in mathematics, the *method* of achieving these gains profoundly dictates their broader utility. By championing RL as a superior paradigm for fostering generalized reasoning, and providing both empirical and mechanistic justifications, it sets a new direction for the responsible and effective development of future LLM capabilities.
