# Towards Safe Robot Foundation Models

> [!summary] Summary
> This research develops a modular safety layer for pre-trained robot foundation models (RFMs), enabling them to operate safely by enforcing explicit, domain-specific constraints without requiring retraining or fine-tuning of the base policy. The integrated safety module demonstrates complete adherence to safety constraints while maintaining and improving task performance in a robotic air hockey task.

## Problem

- Robot Foundation Models (RFMs) offer broad generalization but lack formal safety guarantees, relying implicitly on expert demonstration data.
- Current RFMs can lead to catastrophic failures and physical damage when encountering observations outside the training data distribution.
- The absence of explicit safety assurances limits the reliable deployment of RFMs in real-world, safety-critical applications.

## Method

- A modular safety layer is introduced as a final processing step for actions generated by pre-trained generalist policies (e.g., ==Vision-Language-Action== models).
- The module extends the ATACOM algorithm to project proposed actions onto a constraint manifold, ensuring compliance with predefined, continuously differentiable safety constraints.
- Implementation requires access to the robot's system state and a control-affine dynamics model, common in robotic manipulators.

## Results

- The safety-augmented policy consistently operated without any violations of defined safety constraints throughout all tests on the physical robot.
- The unsafe baseline (unmodified policy) exhibited frequent and increasing safety constraint violations with prolonged training.
- The safety module maintained and improved task success rates as fine-tuning progressed, while the unsafe baseline's performance degraded due to safety breaches.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating domain-specific knowledge through a model-based safety layer effectively addresses safety shortcomings of purely data-driven RFMs.
> - A post-hoc, modular safety mechanism allows pre-trained generalist policies to operate safely without requiring extensive retraining or safety fine-tuning.
> - Safety can be rigorously guaranteed for RFMs without compromising their ability to learn and achieve complex task objectives.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper positions itself within the evolving field of robot foundation models (RFMs), which aim to develop generalist policies capable of operating across diverse environments and tasks, ranging from industrial applications to household duties. Current research efforts in RFMs, exemplified by models such as OCTO and OpenVLA, largely focus on enhancing the generalization capabilities of these policies. These models are typically trained using behavior cloning (BC) on large-scale datasets of expert demonstrations.

However, a critical aspect that remains largely unaddressed by contemporary RFMs is safety. While expert data generally contains safe trajectories, leading to an implicit notion of safety in BC-trained policies, this approach does not provide formal safety guarantees. The paper highlights that such policies can still lead to catastrophic failures, including physical damage to the robot, when encountering observations that deviate from the training data distribution. This limitation prevents their reliable deployment in real-world, safety-critical applications.

The control community has a well-established history of developing methods to ensure safety in robotic systems. These include approaches based on control barrier functions, reachability analysis, and shielding. These methods commonly rely on incorporating domain knowledge and system dynamics to construct guaranteed safety filters. The paper argues that integrating such analytical models and inductive biases is crucial for addressing the shortcomings of purely data-driven approaches, leading to sound inference, data-efficient learning, and, critically, safety.

This research endeavors to bridge the gap between the generalization capabilities of RFMs and the safety guarantees provided by control-theoretic methods. It proposes a modular safety layer that can be integrated with pre-trained generalist policies, thereby allowing them to operate safely in environments by adhering to explicit, domain-specific safety constraints. By extending an existing safe reinforcement learning algorithm, ATACOM, the work aims to enable RFMs to ensure safe state transitions without requiring modification or specific safety fine-tuning of the underlying foundation model. This approach represents an effort to combine the benefits of large-scale, data-driven generalization with rigorous, model-based safety assurance in robotics.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and demonstrate a safety module that can be universally applied as a final layer to any pre-trained Robot Foundation Model (RFM) or generalist policy. This module aims to enable the RFM to operate safely within an environment by enforcing adherence to domain-specific safety constraints, without necessitating modifications or safety fine-tuning of the original policy.

The motivation for pursuing this objective stems from several critical limitations and requirements in the current landscape of robot learning and deployment:

1. **Addressing the Safety Gap in Robot Foundation Models:** While RFMs offer promising generalization capabilities across diverse tasks and environments, current research predominantly focuses on performance and adaptability, often neglecting explicit safety considerations. Relying on implicit safety encoded in expert demonstration data via behavior cloning (BC) is deemed insufficient because it does not provide formal guarantees. Policies trained this way can exhibit unsafe behaviors or catastrophic failures when exposed to novel or out-of-distribution observations in real-world deployments.
2. **Enabling Real-World Deployment:** Safety is a fundamental prerequisite for the widespread deployment of autonomous agents, particularly sophisticated RFMs, in practical applications, whether in industrial settings or domestic environments. Without robust safety guarantees, their practical utility and public acceptance are severely limited.
3. **Leveraging Domain Expertise for Reliable Safety:** The authors argue that purely data-driven methods, while powerful for generalization, are insufficient for ensuring reliable safety. They advocate for the integration of domain expertise, such as detailed knowledge of system dynamics and explicit safety constraints, as an essential component for achieving trustworthy safety. This contrasts with the data-centric paradigm of many RFMs by re-emphasizing model-based control principles.
4. **Modular and Post-Hoc Safety Integration:** A key motivation is to design the safety mechanism as an independent, "add-on" module. This design principle allows the safety layer to be incorporated with pre-trained RFMs without requiring their extensive retraining or fine-tuning for safety purposes. This modularity reduces computational burden and makes the safety solution readily applicable to existing generalist policies.
5. **Guaranteeing Safe State Transitions:** The explicit goal is to ensure that any action proposed by the RFM, even if potentially unsafe according to the constraints, is transformed by the safety module into a demonstrably safe action. This process guarantees compliance with safety constraints and prevents the robot from transitioning into unsafe states during execution. The research seeks to demonstrate that this can be achieved effectively while maintaining the policy's task performance.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology introduces a safety module designed to act as a final layer for pre-trained generalist robot policies, specifically Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, which are a class of RFMs. The core idea is to process the actions output by the VLA policy to ensure they comply with predefined safety constraints, leveraging domain-specific knowledge about the robot's dynamics and the environment.

**Core Algorithm: Extension of ATACOM**
The safety module is built upon an extension of **ATACOM (Acting on the TAngent space of the COnstraint Manifold)**. ATACOM is a safe reinforcement learning algorithm that constructs a constraint manifold representing the set of safe robot configurations. It then maps proposed actions into the tangent space of this manifold, ensuring that all resulting actions maintain the system within safe boundaries. In this work, ATACOM is adapted to operate with generalist policies.

**System Requirements for the Safety Module**
The application of this safety module requires two fundamental prerequisites from the robotic system:

1. **Access to System State and Control-Affine Dynamics:** The system must provide access to its current state `s` and be describable by a control-affine system model of the form `ṡ = f(s) + G(s)a`. This means the rate of change of the state (`ṡ`) can be expressed as a function of the current state (`f(s)`) plus a term (`G(s)a`) that linearly depends on the control action `a`. The paper states that most robotic manipulators adhere to rigid-body assumptions, which typically satisfy this requirement.
2. **Continuously Differentiable Safety Constraints:** Safety conditions must be formulable as continuously differentiable constraint functions, `0 ≥ g(x) ∈ C¹`. This implies that practitioners possess sufficient prior knowledge to accurately define the robot's safety requirements, such as joint limits, collision avoidance boundaries, or operational envelopes.

**Mechanism of Action Modification**
1. A pre-trained VLA policy, denoted `π_VLA(· | x)`, receives multimodal observations `x` (e.g., images, language instructions, proprioceptive data) and outputs a desired action `a`.
2. The proposed safety module, `a_safe = ψ_G,f(a, s, g)`, takes this raw action `a` from the VLA policy, the robot's current state `s`, and the set of pre-defined safety constraints `g` as inputs.
3. The module then computes a modified action `a_safe`. This computation involves projecting the original action `a` onto the tangent space of the constraint manifold defined by `g` and the system dynamics. This projection ensures that `a_safe` will result in a state transition that complies with all safety constraints, even if the original action `a` would have led to a violation.

**Experimental Setup**
To empirically evaluate the safety module, the researchers conducted experiments on a robotic air hockey task:
- **Task Objective:** The robot's goal was to hit a puck into a goal while rigorously adhering to several safety constraints. These constraints included keeping the end-effector on the table surface, preventing collisions between the robotic arm and the table, and respecting joint position limits.
- **Policy under Test:** A pre-trained OCTO policy was used as the generalist policy. This policy was fine-tuned using behavior cloning (BC) both in a simulated MUJOCO environment and on a physical robotic system. Importantly, the fine-tuning data was generated by an expert policy that did *not* utilize ATACOM, ensuring that any observed safety improvements were attributable to the introduced safety module.
- **Observations and Actions:** The policy's observations included language instructions, a goal image, and proprioceptive data (joint positions, velocities, puck position, and velocity). The policy generated desired end-effector velocities in the x-y plane of the table. These velocities were then converted into joint velocities using inverse kinematics.
- **Safety Layer Integration:** The calculated joint velocities were then passed through the ATACOM safety layer. This layer transformed them into "safe" joint velocities before they were executed by a joint-space controller.
- **Baseline Comparison:** The performance and safety of the safety-augmented approach were compared against an "unsafe baseline." In this baseline, the joint-space controller directly executed the unfiltered joint velocities output by the OCTO policy, bypassing the safety module.
- **Evaluation:** The effectiveness of the safety module was assessed across different fine-tuning checkpoints of the OCTO policy on the physical system, focusing on both safety compliance and task success rates.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of the safety module in the robot air hockey task yielded several findings regarding safety compliance and task performance:

1. **Complete Safety Constraint Adherence:** When the proposed safety module, leveraging ATACOM, was integrated with the OCTO policy, the system consistently operated without violating any of the defined safety constraints. This was observed across all tested fine-tuning checkpoints and during inference on the physical robot. The maximum constraint violation recorded for the safety-augmented policy remained at zero.
2. **Significant Safety Violations by Baseline:** In contrast, the unmodified OCTO policy, operating without the safety layer (the unsafe baseline), exhibited substantial violations of safety constraints. These violations occurred frequently, even though the policy had been fine-tuned using expert demonstrations that were inherently safe. This finding supports the argument that implicit safety from expert data is insufficient to guarantee safety in deployment, especially when encountering potential distribution shifts.
3. **Maintained and Improved Task Performance:** Despite enforcing strict safety, the policy equipped with the safety module demonstrated a steady improvement in its task success rate (hitting the puck into the goal) as fine-tuning progressed. This indicates that the safety module did not introduce overly conservative actions that would impede the policy's ability to achieve its primary objective. The safety layer enabled the robot to learn the task effectively while remaining safe.
4. **Degradation of Unsafe Baseline Performance:** Interestingly, the constraint violations of the unsafe OCTO baseline were observed to increase with prolonged training (more training iterations). This rise in violations correlated with a negative impact on the policy's overall task performance, resulting in a lower success rate compared to the safety-augmented policy. This suggests that without explicit safety guarantees, the policy might learn aggressive or unsafe behaviors that, while potentially improving initial performance in some metrics, ultimately lead to failures and decreased success rates due to safety breaches.
5. **Empirical Evidence (Figure 2):** The provided graph visually supports these findings. It clearly illustrates that the "atacom + octo" approach maintained a maximum constraint violation of 0 throughout the training steps, while its success rate steadily climbed. Conversely, the "octo" baseline showed increasing maximum constraint violations over training time, accompanied by a lower and eventually declining success rate, highlighting the detrimental effect of unaddressed safety issues on task completion.

In summary, the results demonstrate that the proposed safety module effectively prevents safety violations in a generalist robot policy operating in a dynamic environment, without sacrificing task performance. This is achieved by transforming potentially unsafe actions into safe ones, thereby enabling the reliable deployment of foundation models in safety-critical applications.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of robotics by addressing a critical challenge in the deployment of Robot Foundation Models (RFMs): ensuring safety. The work's significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Enabling Safe Real-World Deployment of RFMs:** The primary significance lies in providing a practical solution for deploying RFMs in safety-critical real-world environments. Current RFMs, while capable of broad generalization, lack formal safety guarantees. By introducing a modular safety layer, this work facilitates the transition of powerful generalist policies from research settings to practical applications in industrial, service, or household robotics, where safety is paramount.
2. **Modular and Computationally Efficient Safety Integration:** The design of the safety module as an independent, add-on layer is a key advantage. It enables the application of safety guarantees to *already trained* RFMs without requiring any specific safety fine-tuning or modification of the foundation model itself. This approach significantly reduces the computational burden and development time associated with making large-scale generalist policies safe, as it avoids the need for expensive retraining cycles.
3. **Reaffirming the Role of Domain Expertise:** The research highlights that while data-driven approaches excel in generalization, reasoning with system dynamics and incorporating domain-specific knowledge remains essential for ensuring reliable safety in robotics. The proposed hybrid approach, combining data-driven policy learning with model-based safety control, suggests a more robust pathway for developing capable and trustworthy robotic systems.
4. **Maintaining Performance While Ensuring Safety:** The experimental results demonstrate that the safety module, based on the ATACOM algorithm, effectively prevents safety violations without forcing overly conservative robot behaviors. The ability to maintain and even improve task success rates while ensuring strict adherence to safety constraints is crucial for the practical utility of such systems.
5. **Foundational Step for Future Research in Automated Safety:** The paper identifies important directions for future research. One significant challenge is the manual formulation of detailed, scene-specific safety constraints. The authors suggest exploring methods to automate this process, potentially by leveraging the inherent knowledge of vision-language models (VLMs) to integrate semantic safety constraints (e.g., "keep the cup upright") into an existing set of constraints. This could lead to more adaptive and less labor-intensive safety engineering.
6. **Towards Generalizable Safety Concepts:** The work also opens up a broader research question regarding the development of a more generalizable concept of safety. This involves investigating how safety principles can be formulated and applied consistently across diverse robot embodiments, operational environments, and task requirements, moving beyond platform-specific safety definitions. This future direction could lead to truly universal safety frameworks for autonomous systems.

In conclusion, this research presents a tangible step towards making advanced robot foundation models suitable for real-world deployment by systematically addressing the critical challenge of safety through a modular, domain-knowledge-informed approach.
