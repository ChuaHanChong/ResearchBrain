# Betting for Sim-to-Real Performance Evaluation

> [!summary] Summary
> A sequential betting framework is introduced for sim-to-real robot performance evaluation, leveraging abundant simulator data to efficiently estimate real-world outcomes. This bet-weighted estimation method, which statistically benefits from "wrong but informative" simulators, empirically achieved superior performance compared to Monte Carlo estimators, demonstrating win rates (where its error was lower than Monte Carlo) ranging from 70% to 100% in synthetic benchmarks.

## Problem

- Real-world robot performance evaluation is time-consuming, expensive, and constrained by safety and hardware limitations.
- Existing sim-to-real methods for performance evaluation often assume high simulator fidelity is paramount, not fully exploiting the scalability or structured variability of simulators.
- There is a need for accurate and efficient real-world performance estimates, using abundant, low-cost simulated data with theoretical guarantees.

## Method

- A ==sequential betting framework== is proposed where simulators inform strategic "wagers" on real-world outcomes, driving a ==bet-weighted estimator==.
- A practical approximation, inspired by ==Cover's universal portfolio==, adaptively combines predictions from a bank of diverse simulators to compute ==Kelly-style bet sizes==.
- The framework employs a ==double betting mechanism== to align betting with performance estimation goals, explicitly tolerating simulator bias when an informative predictive edge is present.

## Results

- Ideal Kelly betting consistently outperformed Monte Carlo estimators, achieving win rates (lower error than Monte Carlo) ranging from **70% to 100%** across synthetic examples.
- The approximated betting method successfully leveraged diverse simulator banks to estimate real-world robot pick-and-place accuracy, even using generic synthetic distributions, outperforming Monte Carlo.
- In sim-to-sim locomotion tasks, a bank of **domain-randomized simulators** converged rapidly and significantly outperformed synthetic variants in later rounds, demonstrating robust estimation performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A bet-weighted estimator achieves lower expected squared error than Monte Carlo if the variance reduction due to differential weighting outweighs any induced bias penalty.
> - Kelly-style betting, which maximizes asymptotic wealth growth in sequential betting, theoretically induces optimal inverse-variance weighting for improved statistical efficiency.
> - Sustained growth in the algorithm's conceptual "wealth" serves as a practical, real-time diagnostic, empirically indicating that the betting strategy is effectively exploiting predictive signals.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a fundamental challenge in robotics: efficiently obtaining accurate estimates of real-world robot performance. This problem is critical for various applications, including algorithm benchmarking, controller validation, design comparison, and regulatory compliance, yet real-world physical robot testing is often resource-intensive, time-consuming, and subject to safety limitations.

Current approaches to mitigating the scarcity of real-world trials in sim-to-real methodologies generally fall into two categories:
1. **Variance Reduction:** Methods such as importance sampling (IS) reweight samples from an auxiliary distribution to reduce the variance of an estimator. In robotics, simulators typically guide the construction of these auxiliary distributions. This approach generally implies that effective simulators closely match the real-world distribution (P) to achieve optimal variance reduction.
2. **Bias Correction:** These methods explicitly or implicitly model and correct discrepancies between a simulator's distribution (Qθ) and the real-world distribution (P). Examples include prediction-powered inference (PPI) and learned control variates. Similar to variance reduction, these approaches suggest that simulator effectiveness is contingent on its fidelity to the real distribution, as closer alignment simplifies bias correction.

The authors identify a gap in these existing paradigms: they do not fully exploit the scalability of modern robotic simulation, where abundant, inexpensive simulated data often does not translate proportionally into reliable real-world performance estimates due to the underlying assumption that a "good" simulator is one that accurately mimics reality. This perspective contrasts with a successful paradigm in sim-to-real policy transfer, where techniques like domain randomization deliberately embrace distributional mismatch and structured variability to promote generalization, rather than striving for perfect fidelity.

This paper introduces a novel "betting" perspective that aims to bridge this gap. It proposes that for performance evaluation, predictive advantage is paramount, rather than perfect simulator fidelity. A simulator that is "wrong but informative"—one that generates structured uncertainty or directional predictive signals—could be more beneficial than one that merely attempts to mimic reality without providing exploitable variance. This re-framing positions the work as complementary to, yet distinct from, established variance reduction and bias correction techniques, potentially enabling a unified viewpoint where tools from policy transfer can also be applied to performance evaluation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a method for obtaining accurate and efficient estimates of real-world robot performance under severe constraints on physical experimentation, utilizing the abundance of data available from simulators.

The motivation for this objective stems from several practical challenges in robot development and deployment:
- **Cost and Time:** Real-world robot trials are expensive and time-consuming, limiting the number of experiments that can be performed.
- **Safety Concerns:** Testing certain robot behaviors in the real world can pose safety risks.
- **Hardware Limitations:** Physical robots are subject to wear and tear, and environmental variability can complicate repeatable measurements.
- **Critical Need for Evaluation:** Accurate performance evaluation is essential for benchmarking algorithms, comparing design alternatives, validating control systems, and supporting certification or regulatory decision-making processes.

Against this background, simulators offer an attractive solution by providing low-cost, abundant, and controlled observations. The central question motivating this research is whether the configurable and scalable nature of simulators can be leveraged to achieve accurate and efficient real-world performance inference with theoretical guarantees.

Specifically, the paper aims to:
- **Formalize a Betting Mechanism:** Introduce a sequential betting framework for sim-to-real performance inference, where simulator-informed bets drive a bet-weighted estimator.
- **Establish Theoretical Guarantees:** Determine the conditions under which this betting mechanism can yield performance estimates that are provably more accurate and efficient than standard Monte Carlo estimators.
- **Characterize Optimal Betting Strategies:** Define how ideal bets should be constructed to achieve these theoretical benefits, drawing insights from classical information theory and finance (e.g., the Kelly criterion).
- **Develop Practical Implementations:** Translate theoretical optimal betting strategies into practically executable algorithms suitable for sim-to-real testing, inspired by principles such as Cover's universal portfolio.
- **Provide Diagnostic Tools:** Furnish concrete decision rules or signals to diagnose when the approximate betting strategies are functioning as intended, thereby offering performance guarantees in real-world settings.
- **Demonstrate Effectiveness:** Empirically validate the proposed methods across synthetic examples and various computational simulators, including scenarios that involve unconventional sim-to-real transfers (e.g., synthetic distributions informing real robotic manipulation tasks).

Ultimately, the work is motivated by a desire to fundamentally leverage the scalability of modern robotics simulation and to offer a new theoretical perspective on the utility of domain randomization and simulation variance for improving real-world performance evaluation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces a "betting" framework for robot performance evaluation, conceptualized as a sequential decision-making process where simulators inform strategic "wagers" on real-world outcomes. The methodology progresses from an abstract theoretical framework to a practically implementable algorithm, supported by theoretical guarantees and diagnostic tools.

**A. Abstract Sequential Betting Algorithm (Algorithm 1)**
The core of the methodology is an abstract algorithm that simulates a gambler making sequential bets.
- **Betting Process:** At each round *t*, before observing a real-world sample *x<sub>t</sub>*, a bet *b<sub>t</sub>* is chosen. This bet represents a fraction of the potential payoff and reflects the algorithm's confidence in an upcoming prediction. The decision for *b<sub>t</sub>* is informed by both accumulated simulator samples (*S<sub>t</sub>*) and previously observed real-world data (*D*).
- **Double Betting Mechanism:** The algorithm incorporates a "double betting" mechanism. It computes separate bets *b<sub>t</sub><sup>↑</sup>* (for *ψ(x<sub>t</sub>) > τ<sub>t-1</sub>*) and *b<sub>t</sub><sup>↓</sup>* (for *ψ(x<sub>t</sub>) < τ<sub>t-1</sub>*), where *τ<sub>t-1</sub>* is the current bet-weighted mean of observed real-world performance. The final bet *b<sub>t</sub>* is the maximum of these two directional bets. This mechanism is designed to align the betting process with the goal of performance estimation.
- **Payoff and Wealth Update:** If *b<sub>t</sub> > 0*, a real sample *x<sub>t</sub>* is drawn from the real-world distribution *P*. A payoff *Y<sub>t</sub>* is then calculated: *|ψ(x<sub>t</sub>) - τ<sub>t-1</sub>|* if the bet's direction was correct, and * -|ψ(x<sub>t</sub>) - τ<sub>t-1</sub>|* otherwise. The gambler's wealth *W* is updated multiplicatively based on *b<sub>t</sub>* and *Y<sub>t</sub>* (i.e., *W ← W(1 + b<sub>t</sub>Y<sub>t</sub>)*).
- **Bet-Weighted Estimator:** The algorithm outputs *ˆμ<sub>BW</sub> = τ<sub>t</sub>*, a bet-weighted estimate of the target performance mean, rather than a simple equally-weighted Monte Carlo average.

**B. Theoretical Conditions for Betting Effectiveness (Theorem 1)**
The paper establishes a universal performance guarantee, characterizing when the bet-weighted estimator *ˆμ<sub>BW</sub>* outperforms the standard Monte Carlo estimator (*ˆμ<sub>MC</sub>*). It proves that *ˆμ<sub>BW</sub>* achieves lower expected squared error if and only if the "variance reduction" term (related to *σ<sup>2</sup>/n - Σ w<sub>t</sub><sup>2</sup> σ<sub>t</sub><sup>2</sup>*) exceeds the "bias penalty" term (*(Σ w<sub>t</sub> β(w<sub>t</sub>))<sup>2</sup>*). This theorem separates the question of *when* betting helps (a statistical property of the bets) from *how* to generate good bets (an algorithmic design question).

**C. Theoretical Construction of Optimal Bets (Theorem 2 - Kelly Principle)**
To address how to generate good bets, the methodology draws upon the classical Kelly criterion, which maximizes asymptotic wealth growth in sequential betting.
- **Kelly Rule:** The Kelly rule suggests that optimal bet size is proportional to the perceived advantage divided by the uncertainty (variance).
- **Connection to Variance Reduction:** Theorem 2 demonstrates that if bet sizes *b<sub>t</sub>* are chosen according to a Taylor-expanded Kelly rule (*b<sub>t</sub> ∝ E[Y<sub>t</sub> | F<sub>t-1</sub>] / Var(Y<sub>t</sub> | F<sub>t-1</sub>)*), the induced normalized weights *w<sub>t</sub>* are proportional to *κ<sub>t-1</sub> / σ<sub>t</sub><sup>2</sup>* (where *κ<sub>t-1</sub>* is the predictive "edge" and *σ<sub>t</sub><sup>2</sup>* is the conditional outcome variance). Furthermore, if the predictive edge is approximately constant, these weights reduce to inverse-variance weighting (*w<sub>t</sub> ∝ 1/σ<sub>t</sub><sup>2</sup>*), which approximately maximizes the variance-reduction component identified in Theorem 1. This establishes a theoretical link between successful wealth growth and improved statistical efficiency.
- **Practical Challenge:** The "ideal" Kelly rule requires prior knowledge of the true predictive advantage and conditional variances, which are generally unknown in practice, creating a circular dependency.

**D. Practical Approximation of Optimal Bets (Algorithm 2 - Universal Portfolio Principle)**
To overcome the practical limitations of the ideal Kelly rule, the paper proposes an approximation inspired by Cover's universal portfolio principle.
- **Simulator Bank:** A bank of *K* diverse simulator instances (*{Q<sub>θ</sub><sup>(k)</sup>}*) is maintained. Each simulator *k* acts as an "expert," providing its own predictive mean *μ<sup>(k)</sup>* and variance *(σ<sup>(k)</sup>)<sup>2</sup>* for the performance measure. These experts can be domain-randomized models or other plausible hypotheses.
- **Score-Weighted Mixture:** The algorithm adaptively reweights these experts. It computes a combined mean *m<sub>t</sub>* and variance *v<sub>t</sub>* as weighted sums of individual expert predictions, where weights *π<sub>t</sub><sup>(k)</sup>* are assigned to each expert.
- **Kelly-Style Bet:** A Kelly-style bet size *b<sub>t</sub>* is then computed using these aggregated moments: *b<sub>t</sub> = λ|m<sub>t</sub> - τ<sub>t-1</sub>| / v<sub>t</sub>*, where *λ* is the Kelly fraction. The betting direction *B<sub>t</sub>* is determined by the sign of *(m<sub>t</sub> - τ<sub>t-1</sub>)*.
- **Expert Weight Update:** After observing the real-world outcome *y<sub>t</sub> = ψ(x<sub>t</sub>)*, each expert's cumulative log-score *s<sub>t</sub><sup>(k)</sup>* is updated based on a Gaussian log-score (negative log-likelihood) of *y<sub>t</sub>* under the expert's predictive distribution, using a learning rate *η*. The normalized trust weights *π<sub>t</sub><sup>(k)</sup>* are then derived via softmax normalization of these log-scores. This allows the system to prioritize simulators that are more informative for predicting real-world outcomes, even if they are fundamentally "wrong."

**E. Diagnostic for Approximate Betting Effectiveness (Theorem 3 - Wealth Growth)**
The methodology includes a simple yet powerful diagnostic for assessing the practical effectiveness of the approximate betting strategy: wealth growth.
- **No-Advantage Hypothesis (H<sub>0</sub>):** Theorem 3 states that if there is no systematic positive expected gain from betting (i.e., *E[Y<sub>t</sub> | F<sub>t-1</sub>] ≤ 0* for all *t*), then the probability of the wealth *W<sub>T</sub>* growing beyond a certain threshold is small.
- **Interpretation:** Sustained growth in the algorithm's wealth *W* empirically indicates that the betting strategy is successfully extracting predictive signals from the data, implying operation in a regime where bet-weighted estimation is expected to outperform plain Monte Carlo.

The proposed method involves two interpretable hyperparameters: the learning rate *η* (for score updates) and the Kelly fraction *λ* (for risk exposure). The authors note that the framework explicitly tolerates simulator bias, trading it against variance when an informative predictive edge is present.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents both theoretical findings and empirical results across synthetic and robotic case studies, supporting the proposed betting framework for sim-to-real performance evaluation.

**A. Theoretical Findings:**
- **Theorem 1 (Bet-weighted inference V.S. Monte Carlo efficiency):** This theorem establishes a fundamental condition for the bet-weighted estimator to outperform the Monte Carlo estimator. It demonstrates that a lower expected squared error is achieved if and only if the reduction in variance due to differential weighting outweighs any penalty incurred from induced bias. This provides a clear statistical criterion for the utility of the proposed betting approach.
- **Theorem 2 (Kelly-style betting induces optimal bet-weighted inference):** This finding links the classical Kelly criterion for maximizing wealth growth in sequential betting to the problem of optimal statistical estimation. It shows that adopting a Kelly-style betting strategy, where bet sizes are proportional to the ratio of predictive edge to conditional variance, approximately leads to inverse-variance weighting. This weighting, in turn, is proven to approximately maximize the variance-reduction component identified in Theorem 1, thus connecting wealth growth to improved estimation accuracy and efficiency.
- **Theorem 3 (Growing wealth implies effective estimate, statistically):** This theorem provides a practical diagnostic for the effectiveness of the betting strategy. It proves that under a "no-advantage" null hypothesis (i.e., no systematic positive expected gain from betting), the probability of observing substantial wealth growth is low. Consequently, sustained wealth growth during experimentation serves as empirical evidence that the betting strategy is successfully exploiting predictive signals, indicating that the bet-weighted estimator is likely outperforming the Monte Carlo baseline.

**B. Empirical Results (Synthetic Examples - Section III-A):**
- **Outperformance of Ideal Kelly:** Across various synthetic real-world distributions (Real_6, Real_20, Real_40), the ideal Kelly betting strategy consistently demonstrated superior performance compared to the Monte Carlo estimator, achieving win rates (where its error was lower than Monte Carlo) ranging from 70% to 100%. Performance improved with an increasing number of rounds, aligning with the asymptotic nature of the theoretical analysis.
- **Effectiveness of Approximated Kelly:** The practical, approximated Kelly method (Algorithm 2) also performed well, particularly with larger and more diverse simulator banks. The Sim_172 bank, for instance, generally achieved the best performance. However, merely increasing the number of experts did not guarantee better results (e.g., Sim_35 sometimes outperformed Sim_94), suggesting that the quality and diversity of information within the simulator bank are more critical than sheer quantity. The learning rate *η* was observed to influence performance, though its role requires further systematic characterization.
- **Empirical Validation of Wealth as a Diagnostic:** The wealth analysis (Fig. 2b) provided empirical support for Theorem 3, showing a correlation between growing wealth and improved estimation performance, thus confirming its utility as a diagnostic signal.

**C. Empirical Results (Synthetic Simulators to Real-World Pick-and-Place - Section III-B):**
- This study demonstrated an "unconventional" sim-to-real transfer, using synthetic distributions to infer the real-world pick-and-place accuracy of a robotic manipulator.
- **Consistent Outperformance:** Both ideal Kelly (full and half-Kelly) and most approximated Kelly variants using synthetic simulator banks outperformed the Monte Carlo baseline in estimating the manipulator's positioning error. Half-Kelly exhibited greater stability in early rounds.
- **Impact of Mismatch:** The Sim_17_biased bank, designed with a pronounced mismatch to the task distribution, remained ineffective, illustrating that while perfect fidelity is not required, completely uninformative or highly mismatched simulators hinder performance.
- **Distributional Signal over Task Alignment:** The success of generic synthetic distributions in this task highlighted that the bet-weighted estimator's effectiveness hinges on the presence of an exploitable distributional signal, rather than strict task-specific or application-level alignment between simulators and reality.

**D. Empirical Results (Sim-to-Sim Locomotion Tracking Performance - Section III-C):**
- In this sim-to-sim scenario (evaluating a humanoid robot's velocity-tracking in MuJoCo), both a bank of domain-randomized MuJoCo simulators and synthetic Sim variants were used for the approximated Kelly method.
- **Ideal Kelly Dominance:** Ideal Kelly betting again remained the overall dominant method.
- **Comparative Performance of Approximated Variants:** Synthetic Sim distributions performed well in early rounds, possibly due to "lucky" initial guesses. However, the bank of domain-randomized MuJoCo-based simulators, while starting less favorably, converged rapidly and significantly outperformed synthetic variants in later rounds. This suggests the benefit of application-specific, even if randomized, simulators for robust long-term estimation.

**E. Comparisons with Other Methods (Supplementary Material):**
- **Comparison with SureSim (PPI variant):** Under fair allocation of simulator resources, the approximated betting method outperformed SureSim in terms of point estimate accuracy. SureSim's performance improved with significantly more simulator data, highlighting its data requirements. The authors also noted that PPI's primary strength lies in providing confidence intervals with guaranteed coverage, which is a different objective from point estimation.
- **Comparison with Optimal Importance Sampling (IS):** Even with oracle access to the true mean and target distribution, optimal IS achieved only a ~50-55% win rate against Monte Carlo in synthetic examples. The proposed Kelly betting approach achieved 60-100% win rates. This discrepancy was attributed to the bias of self-normalized IS estimators at finite sample sizes and the sequential, adaptive nature of Kelly betting.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces a novel theoretical and practical framework that has several significant implications for robot performance evaluation and the broader field of sim-to-real transfer:

- **Novel Paradigm for Performance Evaluation:** By framing robot performance evaluation as a sequential betting problem, the paper offers a new perspective that moves beyond traditional variance reduction and bias correction techniques. This allows for a different understanding of how simulators contribute to real-world inference.
- **Theoretical Grounding for Sim-to-Real Mismatch:** The work provides a theoretical basis for understanding why "wrong but informative" simulators, which embrace distributional mismatch or structured variability (akin to domain randomization), can be beneficial for performance evaluation. This addresses a long-standing gap in the theoretical understanding of successful sim-to-real practices in policy transfer.
- **Direct Exploitation of Simulation Scalability:** The proposed framework fundamentally leverages the abundance and low cost of simulated data. Unlike methods bottlenecked by scarce real-world data, it is designed to provably benefit from a diverse bank of simulators, even if they are individually imperfect or mismatched with reality. This enables the use of large, diverse simulator collections to improve real-world performance estimates.
- **Improved Efficiency and Accuracy:** The theoretical results demonstrate that the bet-weighted estimator, guided by Kelly-style betting, can provably achieve lower expected squared error compared to the standard Monte Carlo estimator under specified conditions. Empirical results across varied tasks confirm these theoretical benefits, showcasing significant improvements in estimation accuracy and efficiency.
- **Practical Implementability with Diagnostics:** The development of Algorithm 2, based on Cover's universal portfolio, provides a concrete, executable method for approximating optimal betting strategies. Crucially, Theorem 3 offers an inherent diagnostic tool—wealth growth—to assess the real-time effectiveness and reliability of the betting strategy, providing confidence in its application without needing ground truth.
- **Broad Applicability in Robotics:** Accurate and efficient performance evaluation is critical across numerous robotics subfields for tasks such as benchmarking learning algorithms, validating safety-critical behaviors, comparing system designs, and supporting regulatory certification. The proposed method has the potential to enhance these processes by making real-world evaluation more tractable.
- **Potential for Integration:** The framework is presented as complementary to existing methods like importance sampling and prediction-powered inference, suggesting future research directions for hybrid approaches that could combine the strengths of various techniques to further improve sim-to-real performance evaluation.
- **Expanding Sim-to-Real Scope:** The demonstration that generic synthetic distributions can inform real-world robotic manipulation tasks highlights the broad applicability of the betting framework, potentially opening doors for sim-to-real transfers in scenarios where high-fidelity, task-specific simulators are unavailable or prohibitively expensive to develop.
