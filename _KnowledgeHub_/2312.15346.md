# Learning Multi-Step Manipulation Tasks from A Single Human Demonstration

> [!summary] Summary
> A system learns multi-step manipulation from one RGBD human demonstration by segmenting actions into three contact-based robot primitives (make, maintain, break contact). Eight depth cameras plus Grounded Segment Anything and ICP recover object poses; an alternative-pose proposal with RRT-Connect planning adapts human motion to robot kinematics. On dishwashing it reached 50-100% per step and up to 40% whole-task success in an unseen home kitchen.

## Problem

- Robot learning relies on massive interaction datasets that, unlike web-scale vision and language corpora, are prohibitively expensive to collect for diverse real manipulation.
- Prior demonstration methods use teleoperation or kinesthetic teaching, which are laborious, or retarget hand poses directly, ignoring human-robot differences in kinematics and collision geometry.
- Object pose estimators and video action-segmentation methods need task-specific training, generalize poorly outside training data, and action detection rarely exceeds 60% accuracy.

## Method

- Vision module fuses eight calibrated RealSense streams into registered point clouds, segments objects via ==Grounded Segment Anything== text prompts, builds Poisson/V-HACD meshes, and estimates pose with ==ICP==.
- Learning module detects hand-object and object-object contact using dual hysteresis distance thresholds tested forward and backward, then segments demonstrations into ==make/maintain/break-contact primitives== with learned contact locations and key poses.
- Manipulation module converts primitives into a timed object trajectory, and an **Alternative Object Pose Proposal** rotates objects about symmetric axes so ==TRAC-IK== plus ==RRT-Connect== can find collision-free paths.

## Results

- In the demonstration lab kitchen with seen objects and locations, per-step success was at least **80%**, but error accumulation dropped whole-task success to **40%**.
- Moving to unseen object locations cut per-step rates to **40-80%** and whole-task success to **20%**; the unseen home kitchen still achieved **20-40%** overall.
- Failure analysis attributes **50%** of failures to pose-estimation inaccuracy and **26%** to motion-planning infeasibility; faucet-lever pose error halved switching success to **50%** in the home kitchen.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Encoding manipulation intent as contact-state transitions, rather than joint or hand trajectories, yields abstractions transferable across objects, environments, and robot embodiments.
> - Object-centric timed trajectories plus pose-alternative proposals let a robot honor demonstrated intent without copying human motion that its kinematics cannot reproduce.
> - Chaining primitives compounds per-step error multiplicatively, so open-loop multi-step systems need feedback and recovery; perception accuracy dominates the remaining failure budget.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Current robot learning research extensively focuses on collecting large and diverse datasets, assuming that sufficiently broad training data enables autonomous action extrapolation. This approach has yielded significant advancements in fields like computer vision and natural language processing. However, applying this paradigm to robotics presents distinct challenges, primarily due to the difficulty of acquiring comparable volumes of diverse interaction data in real-world robot settings. Existing robot learning methods often exhibit data inefficiency, as they primarily rely on neural networks to infer all necessary information from data without incorporating prior knowledge.

In contrast, human learning from demonstration is characterized by high data efficiency, leveraging an inherent understanding of task abstractions. For instance, a human observing a dishwashing task readily understands concepts like "picking up," "rinsing," and "placing down" an object. However, robots cannot directly execute these abstract human actions due to fundamental differences in kinematics, collision geometry, and perception.

This research aims to bridge this gap by enabling robots to learn multi-step manipulation tasks efficiently from a single human demonstration. It departs from data-intensive approaches by proposing a system that interprets human actions into generalizable and executable robot primitives, focusing on the underlying contact dynamics between the human hand and objects, as well as between objects themselves.

The work builds upon and differentiates itself from existing literature in several areas:

- **Learning from Demonstration (LfD):** Traditional LfD methods, such as teleoperation or kinesthetic teaching, are often laborious. More recent approaches involving hand-pose retargeting or direct policy training from human videos attempt to replicate human actions directly but often overlook crucial human-robot kinematic and geometric differences or do not scale to complex, multi-step, contact-rich tasks. This paper specifically addresses these limitations by focusing on contact dynamics to enhance generalizability and data efficiency for complex manipulation.
- **Object Pose Estimation:** Accurate object pose estimation is critical for robotic manipulation. While various methodologies exist (e.g., Point Pair Features, Template Matching, Learning-based methods, Transformers, Neural Radiance Fields), many require extensive training or struggle with generalization to novel objects and environments outside their specific training datasets. This work employs Grounded Segment Anything for robust object detection and segmentation, combined with Iterative Closest Point (ICP) for pose estimation, which offers generalizability without additional training and modularity for future upgrades.
- **Temporal Video Segmentation:** Segmenting human demonstrations into executable robot primitives is a core challenge. Existing video datasets and segmentation techniques often focus on bounding boxes or lack the fine-grained action labels and accuracy required for precise robot control, with accuracy rates typically below 60%. This research overcomes these limitations by leveraging RGBD videos to reconstruct object point clouds and calculate precise contact relationships, thus providing accurate segmentation points for robot primitives independently of specific datasets.

By focusing on contact relationships as a fundamental abstraction and integrating generalizable vision models with adapted motion planning, this work positions itself as a data-efficient approach towards robust and adaptable robot manipulation in unstructured real-world scenarios.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robot system capable of learning and executing complex multi-step manipulation tasks from a *single human demonstration*. This goal is motivated by the recognized inefficiency of current robot learning methods, which often require vast amounts of data, contrasting sharply with the human ability to quickly grasp and generalize new tasks from minimal examples.

The core motivation stems from the observation that while humans intuitively understand the abstract steps of a task (e.g., "pick up," "rinse," "place down"), robots lack this inherent understanding and cannot directly mimic observed human actions due to fundamental anatomical and mechanical differences. Therefore, the central challenge is to interpret a human demonstration into a set of generalizable and executable abstractions that a robot can perform reliably in varied environments and with different objects.

Specifically, the system aims to achieve the following:

- **Data Efficiency:** Learn proficiency in a new task from a single human demonstration, significantly reducing the data collection burden typically associated with robot learning.
- **Interpretation of Human Actions:** Process RGBD videos of human demonstrations to extract meaningful robot primitives. This involves identifying key task-relevant object poses and the contact dynamics between hands and objects, as well as between objects themselves. The paper proposes segmenting manipulation tasks based on three fundamental hand-object contact states: "making contact," "maintaining contact," and "breaking contact."
- **Generalizability:** Enable the robot to replicate the demonstrated task with different objects and in environments that vary from the demonstration setting in geometry and appearance.
- **Adaptation to Human-Robot Differences:** Develop mechanisms to address the kinematic and collision geometry discrepancies between humans and robots, allowing the robot to achieve the task's intent without necessarily replicating the exact human motions. This involves generating alternative robot motions that achieve the same task goal.
- **Real-world Applicability:** Demonstrate the system's effectiveness in a challenging, multi-step manipulation task in a real-world setting. The manual dishwashing task was selected for its complexity, encompassing manipulation of articulated objects (faucet), non-prehensile manipulation (rinsing), and pick-and-place actions, as well as involving liquids and various object types. Practical considerations such as machine-unsuitable items and the common practice of pre-rinsing dishes further highlight the relevance of robotic assistance in this domain.

Formally, the problem is formulated as developing a function `g` that maps each frame `f_i` in a human demonstration video `V` to a robot primitive `p` from a predefined set `P`, considering the set of task-relevant objects `O`. The set `P` is designed to encompass all possible observable human actions and ensures that primitives are clearly demarcated and non-overlapping.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed system is structured into three main modules: Vision, Learning, and Manipulation, which collectively enable the robot to learn and execute multi-step manipulation tasks from a single human demonstration.

**A. Vision Module**
This module is responsible for processing raw visual input from RGBD cameras into actionable spatial data.

- **(a) Multi-Camera System:** Eight Intel RealSense D435 depth cameras are used. They are calibrated using the Multical tool with a customized April Tag board. During demonstrations, all cameras capture 640x480 resolution videos at 30 frames per second synchronously.
- **(b) Point Cloud Registration:** Color and depth information from the calibrated cameras are used to construct composite point clouds, amalgamating data from all eight cameras into a comprehensive spatial representation. A fixed camera's coordinate frame serves as the origin for consistent referencing.
- **(c) Object Segmentation:** Image segmentation is performed on captured color images to generate masks for objects of interest. The off-the-shelf Grounded Segment Anything system (a combination of Grounding DINO and Segment Anything) is employed, which uses text prompts to detect objects and produce detailed masks.
- **(d) Model Construction:**
 - An environment point cloud is generated from initial frames where no objects are present.
 - Both environment and object point clouds are refined by removing outliers.
 - These refined point clouds serve as models for pose estimation.
 - The point clouds are transformed into triangle meshes using Poisson surface reconstruction.
 - These meshes are then decomposed into convex components using the V-HACD technique for efficient collision detection.
- **(e) Object Pose Estimation:** For each video frame, the relative pose between a segmented object point cloud and its corresponding model point cloud is computed using the Iterative Closest Point (ICP) algorithm. This process is also applied during robot execution to determine real-time object poses, aligning the execution environment with the learned demonstration.

**B. Learning Module**
This module translates human actions into robot primitives by focusing on contact relationships.

- **(f) Hand-Object and Object-Object Contact Detection:** Contact relationships are determined by assessing distances between point clouds. To mitigate noise, a sequential test with two distinct thresholds is used, where the threshold for breaking contact is higher than for making contact. The test is also performed in reverse, and the outcome leading to the fewest contact changes is selected. Additionally, contact locations between the human hand and the object it holds are recorded by clustering points close to the hand in the object's reference frame. For objects requiring more contact points than a robot's gripper (e.g., a cup), applicable contact locations can be manually defined.
- **(g) Primitive Segmentation and Classification:** Human actions are categorized into three generalizable robot primitives based on changes in hand-object contact relationships:
 - **Make Contact:** Initiates interaction (e.g., picking up an object).
 - **Maintain Contact:** Sustains interaction while manipulating (e.g., rinsing a bowl).
 - **Break Contact:** Terminates interaction (e.g., placing down an object).
 The system assumes rigid body manipulation.
- **(h) Policy Generation:** A robot policy is generated as a sequence of these primitives, mirroring the human demonstration. Each primitive includes learned parameters from the demonstration and execution parameters for the robot.
 - For 'Make Contact', learned parameters include hand-object contact location(s).
 - For 'Break Contact', the learned parameter is the object's final pose when contact is broken, which can be overridden if a specific placement is desired.
 - For 'Maintain Contact', learned parameters capture object poses and contact relationships whenever changes occur (e.g., object-object contact, object appearance/disappearance), aiming to replicate relative object poses with preserved contact relationships.

**C. Manipulation Module**
This module executes the generated robot policy, adapting it to robot kinematics and avoiding collisions.

- **(i) Policy Execution:** The robot policy, a sequence of primitives, takes learned and execution parameters to generate a "timed object trajectory." This trajectory consists of a series of timestamped object poses, relative to each other, translating the learned contact relationships and temporal evolution from the demonstration into the execution scene.
- **(j) Alternative Object Pose Proposal:** Recognizing human-robot kinematic and geometric differences (e.g., a robot's larger wrist vs. a human's wrist), a function is implemented to propose alternative desired object poses. This function rotates an object around its symmetric axis (full circle) and non-symmetric axes (small angles), while maintaining necessary contact relationships. This increases the probability of finding a feasible motion solution for the robot.
- **(k) Inverse Kinematics and Motion Planning with Collision Avoidance:** Object and environment meshes (from the Vision Module) are imported into Pybullet for collision checking. The RRT-Connect algorithm is used to compute collision-free paths between Inverse Kinematics (IK) poses, which are solved using TRAC-IK. If no path is found after iterating through all proposed alternative poses, a motion planning failure is registered.
- **(l) Robot Controller:** The robot's path duration is aligned with the timed object trajectory. A time-optimal trajectory generation method is applied, considering robot velocity and acceleration limits. The path's duration is adjusted to match the object trajectory's specified time. A Proportional-Derivative (PD) joint position controller, as implemented in Polymetis, is used. Joint angle commands are upsampled to 1000 Hz and transmitted to the robot via a real-time kernel. The robot used is a Franka Research 3, which was waterproofed and fitted with custom-designed longer fingers to facilitate dishwashing tasks without blocking water flow.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluated the system's effectiveness and adaptability across various experimental conditions, including seen and unseen objects, varying initial object locations, and different environments.

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Robot Hardware:** A Franka Research 3 robot arm was used, customized with longer, 3D-printed fingers at a 135-degree angle to prevent the robot's wrist from blocking water flow during operations (unlike the original short fingers). The robot arm was waterproofed with poly tubing, and the gripper was covered with a latex glove.
- **Demonstration Environment:** Human demonstrations were recorded in a mockup kitchen in the lab.
- **Testing Environments:**
 - The same lab kitchen (referred to as the "seen environment").
 - A home kitchen (referred to as the "unseen environment"), presenting different geometries and appearances, and inherently different initial object locations.
- **Objects:** Experiments involved bowls (blue, pink, red), cups, and forks. The task involved washing ketchup or mustard off these objects.
- **Visual Aids:** Faucet levers were covered with blue tape, and water outlets with green tape to aid the vision module in distinguishing these components. Water flow was modeled as a rigid cylinder at the water outlet.

**Results Summary (Table I):**
The system's performance was measured by success rates over five trials for individual steps (Faucet On, Faucet Off, Pick, Place, Rinse) and for completing the entire multi-step task ("All").

- **Seen Environment (Lab Kitchen):**
 - **Seen Objects, Seen Locations (S S S):** Achieved 80-100% success for each individual step. However, cumulative errors across steps resulted in an overall task success rate of 40%.
 - **Unseen Objects, Seen Locations (U S S):** Individual step success rates ranged from 80-100% for pick, place, and rinse, but were not tested for faucet operations. The overall task success rate was 20%.
 - **Seen Objects, Unseen Locations (S U S):** Modifying object initial locations led to a decline in individual step success rates, ranging from 50-80%. The overall task success rate was 20%.
 - **Unseen Objects, Unseen Locations (U U S):** Individual step success rates ranged from 40-80% for pick, place, and rinse, not tested for faucet operations. The overall task success rate was 20%.

- **Unseen Environment (Home Kitchen):**
 - **Seen Objects, Unseen Locations (S U U):** The vision module faced difficulties with faucet lever pose estimation, resulting in a 50% success rate for switching the faucet on and off. Pick, place, and rinse steps maintained 80% success. The overall task success rate was 40%.
 - **Unseen Objects, Unseen Locations (U U U):** Similar to the previous unseen environment scenario, faucet operations achieved 50% success. Pick and rinse steps were at 60%, and place at 100%. The overall task success rate for the entire task was 20%.

**Failure Analysis:**
An analysis of failure cases identified key areas for improvement:
- **Pose Estimation Inaccuracies:** This was the most significant factor, accounting for 50% of all failures.
- **Motion Planning Errors:** The robot failed to find a feasible collision-free path for a primitive in 26% of failures.
- **Other Failure Types (excluded from reported success rates as they did not reflect system design intent):**
 - Inadequate rinsing due to external factors like different object materials: 10% of failures.
 - Robot dropping the bowl because it became too heavy with water, indicating robot strength limitations: 8% of failures.
 - Low water flow from the faucet despite being opened: 3% of failures.
 - Unclassified or unrepeatable issues (e.g., external disturbances): 3% of failures.
These latter categories suggest that while the robot successfully replicated the intended motions, external factors or limitations beyond the core learning and manipulation modules contributed to task failure.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a system capable of learning and executing multi-step manipulation tasks from a single human demonstration, addressing a key challenge in robot learning regarding data efficiency and generalizability. The core insight—that the identification and replication of hand-object and object-object contact relationships are crucial—demonstrates a viable approach to abstracting complex human actions into robot-executable primitives.

The system's significance and potential impact lie in several aspects:

- **Data Efficiency:** By enabling learning from a single demonstration, the work substantially reduces the data requirements for deploying robots in new manipulation tasks. This is a critical step towards more practical and scalable robot deployment in diverse, unstructured environments where collecting large datasets is impractical.
- **Enhanced Generalizability:** The use of a large generalizable vision model (Grounded Segment Anything) for object identification, combined with an object-centric approach to policy generation, allows the robot to adapt to different objects and environments. The experiments in both a lab mockup kitchen and an unseen home kitchen with various objects validate this generalizability, albeit with varying success rates.
- **Robustness to Human-Robot Differences:** The explicit consideration of human-robot kinematic and collision geometry differences, particularly through the "Alternative Object Pose Proposal" and sophisticated motion planning, enables the robot to achieve the task's intent without needing to precisely mimic human motions. This adaptability is essential for practical robotic systems that operate with different physical constraints than humans.
- **Practical Application in Complex Tasks:** The successful, albeit limited, demonstration in the manual dishwashing task highlights the system's potential for handling real-world, multi-step manipulation problems that involve articulated objects, non-prehensile actions, and liquids—domains typically challenging for robots. This opens avenues for robotic assistance in domestic or industrial settings where such tasks are common.

While the system is presented as a prototype with acknowledged limitations, it lays foundational groundwork for future advancements. The identified areas for improvement, such as enhancing vision module accuracy (e.g., better depth cameras or calibration-free 3D reconstruction like NeRF), expanding to non-rigid objects, integrating grasping planners, and generalizing the approach to a broader range of tasks and robots, outline clear directions for future research.

In summary, this work contributes to the field by offering a data-efficient and generalizable framework for teaching robots complex manipulation skills from minimal human input, moving towards more autonomous and adaptable robotic systems for real-world applications.
