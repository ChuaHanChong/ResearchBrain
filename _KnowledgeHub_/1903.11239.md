# TossingBot: Learning to Throw Arbitrary Objects with Residual Physics

> [!summary] Summary
> A robotic system learns to grasp and throw diverse rigid objects into target bins, leveraging a "Residual Physics" approach that blends analytical ballistic models with learned neural network corrections. The system demonstrates a high throughput of 514 picks per hour and 84.7% throwing accuracy, while generalizing to novel objects and target locations.

## Problem

- Traditional robotic pick-and-place operations are constrained by a manipulator's kinematic reach and speed, limiting overall efficiency and workspace footprint.
- Precisely throwing arbitrary objects is inherently difficult due to varying object properties (mass distribution, shape, friction), grasp-dependent release dynamics, and complex unmodeled aerodynamics.
- Prior robotic throwing research often relied on simplified assumptions, such as pre-fixtured or uniform objects, making them unsuitable for unstructured, real-world environments.

## Method

- An end-to-end deep learning framework learns to infer parameters for both grasping and throwing motion primitives directly from RGB-D visual observations of objects in a bin.
- The system employs a **Residual Physics** approach, which uses an analytical ==ballistic model== to estimate an initial release velocity, and a deep neural network to predict a ==residual correction== to this estimate.
- Self-supervised training through extensive trial and error optimizes these parameters, with automated object reset mechanisms to minimize human intervention.

## Results

- TossingBot achieved **514** mean picks per hour (MPPH) with **84.7%** throwing accuracy in real-world scenarios, significantly surpassing traditional pick-and-place rates and human performance for unseen objects.
- The Residual-physics method consistently outperformed physics-only and purely data-driven baselines, achieving **82.3%** throwing accuracy for unseen objects in real-world tests, compared to **58.5%** for the physics-only approach.
- The system demonstrated strong generalization to novel objects and target locations, and implicitly learned object-level semantic features relevant for physical interaction through task-driven training.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Throwing significantly boosts robotic pick-and-place efficiency, achieving higher throughput and extending the functional workspace beyond the robot's direct kinematic reach.
> - Jointly optimizing grasps based on downstream throwing accuracy enables the robot to learn stable grasps that are more conducive to successful and predictable throws.
> - The "Residual Physics" hybrid approach effectively combines the generalizability of physics models with data-driven corrections for unmodeled dynamics, leading to robust performance and strong generalization.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation often focuses on pick-and-place operations. Traditional pick-and-place methods involve grasping an object and transporting it to a designated location, which can be limited by the robot's kinematic reach and operational speed. This work introduces throwing as a mechanism to extend a robot manipulator's capabilities, leveraging dynamics to achieve what the authors term "dynamic extrinsic dexterity." Throwing allows objects to be placed rapidly into containers situated beyond the robot's maximum kinematic range, thereby potentially reducing the physical footprint of the robot's workspace and increasing its picking efficiency by allowing immediate transfer of objects without a full transportation sequence.

However, precisely throwing arbitrary objects in unstructured environments presents several challenges. These include acquiring objects in grasps suitable for reliable throwing, accommodating diverse object-centric properties such as mass distribution, friction, and shape, and managing complex aerodynamic effects during flight. Prior research in robotic throwing has frequently relied on simplified assumptions, such as homogeneous pre-throw conditions (e.g., objects fixtured in a gripper) or predetermined, uniform objects (e.g., balls or darts). Such assumptions typically do not hold in real-world, unstructured settings where a robot must independently acquire objects and adapt its throws to varying properties and dynamics.

This research addresses these limitations by proposing an end-to-end learning formulation. It integrates grasping and throwing control, allowing the robot to learn from visual observations through trial and error. The approach leverages a hybrid control strategy termed "Residual Physics," which combines analytical physics models with data-driven deep learning. This positions the work within the broader context of hybrid controllers that aim to exploit the generalization capabilities of analytical models while using learned residuals to compensate for unmodeled dynamics or difficult-to-measure properties. This contrasts with purely analytical models, which struggle with real-world complexities, and purely data-driven methods, which often have limited generalization or require extensive, costly training data under diverse conditions. The work seeks to advance robotic manipulation by demonstrating the practical benefits of throwing in increasing efficiency and reach, especially for arbitrary objects in dynamic, uncertain environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate whether a robot arm can learn to pick and throw arbitrary rigid objects into selected target boxes with speed and accuracy. This overarching goal is driven by several motivations and broken down into specific objectives:

- **Enhancing Robot Capabilities:** The fundamental motivation for exploring throwing is to augment the capabilities of robotic manipulators. Throwing offers the potential to overcome physical reach limitations and significantly increase the speed of pick-and-place operations, thereby improving overall system efficiency in tasks like sorting or bin picking. By allowing objects to be immediately "passed to Newton" rather than meticulously transported, the total cycle time for each pick can be reduced.
- **Addressing Challenges of Unstructured Environments:** Precisely throwing arbitrary objects is inherently difficult due to numerous interacting factors:
 - **Pre-throw conditions:** The initial grasp of an object significantly influences its trajectory. For example, grasping a screwdriver at different points can alter its release velocity due to centripetal accelerations.
 - **Varying object properties:** Objects possess diverse mass distributions, frictional characteristics, and shapes, all of which affect their flight dynamics.
 - **Complex dynamics:** Aerodynamics, which can drastically alter the trajectory of lightweight objects like ping-pong balls, are difficult to model analytically.
 - These factors are often challenging to model or measure precisely, making purely analytical solutions impractical for real-world scenarios involving arbitrary objects.
- **Developing an End-to-End Learning Formulation:** The core objective is to create an end-to-end system that jointly learns to infer control parameters for both grasping and throwing motion primitives directly from visual observations (RGB-D images of objects in a bin). This formulation aims to eliminate the need for explicit object models or pre-defined grasp strategies.
- **Investigating Grasping-Throwing Synergies:** A specific objective is to explore how learning can discover synergistic relationships between grasping and throwing. The system is designed to learn grasps that inherently enable more predictable and accurate throws, rather than simply achieving a successful grasp independently of the subsequent throwing action.
- **Integrating Physics with Deep Learning (Residual Physics):** A key objective is to investigate a hybrid approach that combines a physics simulator with deep learning. The motivation is to leverage the generalizability of physics-based models (e.g., for ballistic trajectories) while using deep networks to predict "residuals"—corrections on top of these physics-based predictions. These residuals are intended to compensate for unmodeled dynamics, measurement noise, and object-specific properties that are difficult for a purely analytical model to capture. This aims to achieve both robustness and generalization.
- **Generalization to Novel Objects and Locations:** A practical objective is for the resulting system to generalize its learned policies to new objects and target locations not explicitly seen during training, crucial for deployment in dynamic environments.
- **Minimizing Human Intervention:** The system is designed for self-supervised training through trial and error, including automated reset mechanisms, to minimize human involvement during the learning process.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

TossingBot employs an end-to-end formulation where a deep neural network, $f(I, p)$, takes an RGB-D visual observation ($I$) of objects in a bin and the 3D position of a target landing location ($p$) as input. It then outputs parameters ($\phi_g$ and $\phi_t$) for grasping and throwing motion primitives, respectively. The system is trained via self-supervision through trial and error, aiming to optimize these parameters such that the grasped object lands accurately on the target.

The network architecture comprises three main modules:

- **Perception Module: Learning Visual Representations**
 - **Input Representation:** Visual input $I$ is an RGB-D heightmap image of the workspace. This is generated by capturing RGB-D images from a fixed camera, projecting them into a 3D point cloud, and then orthographically back-projecting upwards to construct a heightmap with color (RGB) and height-from-bottom (D) channels. Channels are normalized.
 - **Spatial Feature Extraction:** The heightmap (180x140 pixels, 5x5mm resolution per pixel) is fed into a 7-layer fully convolutional residual network (interleaved with 2x2 max-pooling layers). This module outputs a spatial feature representation $\mu$ of size 45x35x512, which serves as a shared input for the subsequent grasping and throwing modules.

- **Grasping Module: Learning Parallel-jaw Grasps**
 - **Grasping Primitive:** A top-down parallel-jaw grasp centered at a 3D location $x = (x_x, x_y, x_z)$ with orientation $\theta$ around the gravity direction. The gripper approaches $x$, closes, and lifts 10cm. Motion planning uses an IK solver.
 - **Grasping Network:** A 7-layer fully convolutional residual network (with spatial bilinear 2x upsampling layers) takes the visual feature representation $\mu$ as input. It outputs a pixel-wise probability map $Q_g$ (same resolution as input heightmap), where each pixel value represents the predicted probability of grasping success for a horizontal grasp at that 3D location.
 - **Orientation Handling:** To account for different grasping angles, the input heightmap is rotated by 16 orientations (multiples of 22.5°) before being fed into the network. The pixel with the highest probability across all 16 maps determines the grasp parameters $\phi_g = (x, \theta)$.

- **Throwing Module: Learning Throwing Velocities (Residual Physics)**
 - **Throwing Primitive:** The primitive takes parameters $\phi_t = (r, v)$, executing an end-effector trajectory to reach a desired release position $r = (r_x, r_y, r_z)$ and velocity $v = (v_x, v_y, v_z)$, at which point the gripper opens. The robot arm curls inward, then uncurls outward at high speed. The gripper axis is orthogonal to the intended aerial trajectory plane.
 - **Release Position Planning:** The release position $r$ is constrained based on the target $p$ by assuming a linear trajectory on the xy-plane and fixing release distance ($c_d$) from the robot base and height ($c_h$).
 - **Release Velocity Planning:** To resolve ambiguity, the direction of $v$ is constrained to be angled 45° upwards towards $p$, making the magnitude $\Vert v_{x,y} \Vert$ the only unknown variable. Ballistic equations are used to derive $\Vert v \Vert$ for a given target $p$, release position $r$, and release height $r_z$.
 - **Residual Physics-Based Controller:** This is a core innovation.
 - **Physics-based controller:** A standard ballistic model (assuming a point particle, gravity only) analytically estimates an initial release velocity $\hat{v}$ given $p$ and $r$. This $\hat{v}$ is also provided as a concatenated input to both grasping and throwing networks to condition their predictions.
 - **Throwing Network:** A 7-layer fully convolutional residual network processes $\mu$ and the physics-based velocity $\hat{v}$. It outputs an image $Q_t$ (same resolution as $I$), where each pixel $i$ holds a prediction of a residual value $\delta_i$. The final release velocity $v_i$ for a grasp at pixel $i$ is calculated as $\Vert v_{x,y} \Vert = \Vert \hat{v}_{x,y} \Vert + \delta_i$. This enables the data-driven network to correct for unmodeled dynamics (e.g., aerodynamics, grasp-dependent velocity changes).

- **Joint Learning and Training:**
 - **Loss Function:** The overall loss is $L = L_g + y_i L_t$.
 - $L_g$ is binary cross-entropy for grasping success classification.
 - $L_t$ is Huber loss for regressing the residual throwing velocity $\delta_i$.
 - **Supervision:** Ground truth labels ($y_i$ for grasp success, $\bar{\delta}_i$ for residual) are obtained through trial and error. Grasp success is determined either by checking gripper fingertip distance or, critically, by the accuracy of the subsequent throw (object landing in the target box). The latter encourages learning grasps that lead to successful throws.
 - **Training Process:** Stochastic gradient descent with momentum, prioritized experience replay, and $\epsilon$-greedy exploration are used. Training and inference occur in parallel to robot actions.
 - **Real-world Setup:** A UR5 arm with an RG2 gripper, Intel RealSense D415 cameras for perception and landing zone tracking, and an automated object reset system minimize human intervention.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of TossingBot was conducted in both simulated and real-world environments, assessing overall performance, generalization capabilities, the synergy between grasping and throwing, and the effectiveness of the Residual Physics approach.

- **Overall Performance and Efficiency:**
 - TossingBot (using Residual-physics) demonstrated a high level of performance, achieving **514 mean picks per hour (MPPH)**, with **608 grasps per hour** and an **84.7% throwing accuracy** in real-world settings.
 - This MPPH rate surpassed several state-of-the-art pick-and-place systems and a variant of TossingBot that only places objects (432 MPPH), indicating that throwing significantly enhances picking efficiency for certain applications.
 - High efficiency is also attributed to fast algorithmic inference (220ms), real-time TSDF fusion of RGB-D data, and parallel online training.

- **Baseline Comparisons for Throwing Accuracy:**
 - **Residual-physics consistently outperformed all baseline methods** in throwing accuracy across both simulation and real-world experiments, and for both seen and unseen object sets.
 - In simulation, Residual-physics achieved 99.6% for balls, 86.3% for cubes, 86.4% for rods, and 81.2% for hammers, leading to 88.6% for seen objects and 66.5% for unseen objects.
 - In real-world experiments, Residual-physics achieved 84.7% accuracy for seen objects and 82.3% for unseen objects.
 - **Physics-only** performed competitively in simulation but showed a notable drop in real-world performance (61.3% seen, 58.5% unseen). This highlights the limitations of purely ballistic models in complex real environments with unmodeled aerodynamics and contact dynamics, which Residual-physics could compensate for via learned residuals.
 - **Regression-PoP (Regression pre-trained on Physics)** consistently outperformed **Regression (purely data-driven)**, suggesting that bootstrapping with physics knowledge benefits even fully data-driven approaches.
 - **Comparison to Human Performance:** TossingBot's throwing accuracy (80.1% for unseen objects) slightly exceeded that of untrained human participants (80.1% ± 10.8% for unseen objects), noting that humans exhibited performance degradation due to fatigue and varied strategies.

- **Grasping Performance:**
 - Grasping success rates were generally high across all methods in simulation (96.0% for seen objects with Residual-physics) and real-world (86.9% seen, 73.2% unseen with Residual-physics).
 - While grasping success rates were similar, the *quality* of grasps learned differed significantly based on the supervisory signal.

- **Learning Stable Grasps for Throwing:**
 - A key finding was the impact of the supervisory signal for grasping. When the grasping network was supervised by **throw accuracy** (i.e., grasp success = object landed on target), both grasping and throwing performance significantly improved compared to supervision by a simple gripper width heuristic (grasp success = object in gripper).
 - This indicates that the grasping policy learns to favor a subset of grasps that lead to more predictable throws, demonstrating a learned synergy.
 - Analysis of grasp histograms on hammer objects showed that supervision by throw accuracy resulted in a more restricted, stable, and homogeneous set of grasps, slightly further from the object's center of mass (CoM) to avoid finger-object collisions and avoid less predictable throws.
 - Furthermore, the ability to learn throwing residuals (Residual-physics) allowed for a broader range of successful grasps compared to Physics-only, where the grasping policy was forced to optimize for grasps closer to the CoM due to the lack of dynamic compensation.

- **Generalizing to New Target Locations:**
 - Residual-physics demonstrated strong generalization to target locations unseen during training, significantly outperforming the Regression-PoP baseline (87.2% vs. 26.5% in simulation; 83.9% vs. 32.7% in real-world). This directly illustrates the advantage of the physics-based controller in providing a foundational generalization capability.

- **Deep Object Semantics Emerging from Task Training:**
 - Analyzing the pixel-wise deep features ($\mu$) learned by TossingBot revealed an implicit understanding of object categories. Similarity maps generated from query pixels on specific objects (e.g., a ping-pong ball or a marker pen) correctly localized other objects of the same category in the scene, even if they had different visual textures or colors.
 - This suggests that the network learned features biased towards geometric and physical attributes relevant for grasping and throwing, rather than solely visual textures. These features were more informative for the task than those from an ImageNet-pretrained ResNet.
 - This finding implies that robots can implicitly learn object-level semantics relevant to physical interaction without explicit human-defined supervision, contributing to broader discussions on machine vision and how robots learn about their environment.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The TossingBot project presents a significant contribution to the field of robotic manipulation, with several areas of potential impact:

- **Advancement in Robotic Manipulation Capabilities:** The work demonstrates a practical and effective method for robots to pick and throw arbitrary objects into target locations outside their direct kinematic reach. This extends the functional workspace of robots without requiring larger, more expensive manipulators, offering a novel solution for tasks that traditionally rely on slower pick-and-place operations.
- **Enhanced Robotic Efficiency and Throughput:** By enabling rapid object transfer through throwing, TossingBot achieves a high pick-and-place rate (500+ MPPH). This speed improvement has direct implications for industrial automation, logistics, and order fulfillment, where maximizing throughput is critical. The ability to generalize to new objects and target locations further enhances its utility in dynamic, real-world environments.
- **Validation of the "Residual Physics" Paradigm:** The research provides strong evidence for the effectiveness of "Residual Physics" as a hybrid control strategy. This approach, which combines the generalization power of analytical physics models with the adaptability of deep learning to capture unmodeled dynamics and system nonlinearities, offers a robust framework for controlling complex robotic interactions. It suggests a promising direction for developing more capable and reliable robotic systems that operate in uncertain physical environments.
- **Understanding Learned Synergies and Implicit Knowledge:** The findings on joint learning of grasping and throwing demonstrate that optimizing for a downstream task (throwing accuracy) can lead to the discovery of more stable and task-appropriate pre-conditions (grasps). Furthermore, the emergence of object-level semantic representations from task-driven physical interaction, without explicit supervision, offers insights into how robots might develop their understanding of the world. This contributes to the broader discussion on representation learning in robotics and machine intelligence.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** TossingBot, while a significant step, also highlights areas for future exploration. This includes extending the framework to handle fragile, articulated, or deformable objects, integrating additional sensing modalities (e.g., force-torque, tactile) for richer object property inference, achieving more fine-grained control over object pose and orientation in flight, and investigating the applicability of Residual Physics to a wider array of complex robotic manipulation tasks beyond throwing. Such advancements could lead to even more versatile and intelligent robotic systems.
