# Automating the Refinement of Reinforcement Learning Specifications

> [!summary] Summary
> AUTOSPEC is a framework developed to automatically refine coarse logical specifications for reinforcement learning tasks, enabling existing specification-guided RL algorithms to learn policies for previously unlearnable tasks. It improved overall task satisfaction probability from near 0% to approximately 60% in complex grid environments and also demonstrated effectiveness in continuous 3D robotic manipulation.

## Problem

- Users often provide coarse or under-specified logical task specifications, which translate into sparse or misleading reward functions for RL agents.
- Designing effective reward functions (reward engineering) is challenging, and manual refinement of logical specifications is labor-intensive and error-prone.
- RL algorithms frequently fail to learn useful policies from insufficient or poorly defined specifications due to a lack of precise feedback.

## Method

- AUTOSPEC operates as a wrapper around existing ==SpectRL==-compatible RL algorithms, monitoring policy learning for failures to achieve a specified satisfaction probability.
- When an edge (reach-avoid subtask) in the specification's abstract graph fails, AUTOSPEC employs exploration-guided strategies to gather trajectory data.
- It iteratively applies four refinement procedures (SeqRefine, AddRefine, PastRefine, OrRefine) to modify the abstract graph, generating a more detailed specification, and then prompts re-learning for the updated policies.

## Results

- Integrated with DIRL, AUTOSPEC improved overall satisfaction probability from near **0%** to approximately **60%** in a 100-rooms environment by rectifying unreachable targets, problematic initial states, and enabling alternative paths.
- In randomized gridworlds, AUTOSPEC achieved a terminal success probability of about **60%**, significantly outperforming a DIRL baseline that stagnated at **20%**, particularly by introducing waypoints (AddRefine) and tightening target regions (ReachRefine).
- Validated individual refinement procedures, showing improvements in satisfaction probability (e.g., from **15%** to **85%** with SeqRefine, **20%** to **90%** with AddRefine), and demonstrated applicability in continuous 3D robotic manipulation tasks like PandaGym.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Coarse logical specifications can be automatically refined by identifying specific failure modes in policy learning and applying targeted geometric and structural modifications.
> - The framework's effectiveness in refinement is significantly dependent on the underlying RL algorithm's exploration strategy and its ability to generate some successful trajectories.
> - Refined specifications maintain soundness, guaranteeing that any policy satisfying the refined task also satisfies the original, higher-level intent.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) algorithms have demonstrated capabilities in solving complex tasks. A common challenge in applying RL is the design of appropriate reward functions, often referred to as reward engineering. Traditional scalar Markovian reward functions can be insufficient for complex or long-horizon tasks, leading to an increased interest in non-Markovian rewards, frequently represented through logical specifications. This area of research is known as specification-guided reinforcement learning.

Existing approaches in specification-guided RL focus on translating logical specifications, such as those expressed in Linear Temporal Logic (LTL) or its fragments like SpectRL, into reward functions to guide policy learning. While these methods allow for task decomposition and denser rewards, they face two primary challenges:
1. **Granularity of specifications:** Users often provide coarse or under-specified logical formulas that, while logically correct, lack the necessary detail to effectively guide RL agents.
2. **Accuracy of labeling functions:** The mapping of environment states to specification predicates can be imprecise, leading to ambiguous or unhelpful regions for learning.

Current research on specification-guided RL primarily assumes fixed, well-defined specifications. Works by Icarte et al. (2018), Li et al. (2017b), and Hasanbeig et al. (2019, 2022) focus on translating various LTL fragments into reward functions. Jothimurugan et al. (2019, 2021) introduced SpectRL and compositional methods for deriving reward functions from its abstract graph representation. Other advancements include goal-conditioned RL for LTL tasks (Qiu et al., 2023) and counterexample-guided abstraction and refinement for policy improvement (Jin et al., 2022). However, these methods typically do not address the problem of refining the *specification itself* when it proves inadequate for learning.

This paper positions itself to address this gap by introducing a framework for *automatically refining* coarse logical specifications. It aims to improve the quality of reward functions derived from user-provided specifications, thereby enhancing the learnability of tasks without requiring manual re-engineering of the specification. The work is complementary to existing specification-guided RL algorithms, as it seeks to preprocess or iteratively modify the specification to make it more amenable to these algorithms.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an automated framework, named AUTOSPEC, for refining coarse-grained logical specifications used in reinforcement learning. This framework aims to enable existing specification-guided RL algorithms to solve tasks that would otherwise be difficult or impossible to learn due to insufficient or poorly defined specifications.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges observed in the practical application of specification-guided RL:

1. **Prevalence of Coarse Specifications:** Users or domain experts frequently define logical specifications at a high level of abstraction. While these specifications capture the desired task semantics, they may not contain enough granular detail to translate into effective reward functions for an RL agent. For example, a "reach region X" specification might be logically sound but fail to consider complex environmental geometry, leading to agents trying to reach unreachable parts of X.
2. **Insufficient Guidance for RL Algorithms:** When logical specifications are coarse, the reward functions derived from them can be sparse or misleading. This often results in RL algorithms failing to learn useful policies, even with methods designed for non-Markovian rewards. The agent might get stuck in local optima, explore inefficiently, or fail to converge to a satisfactory policy due to a lack of precise feedback.
3. **Difficulty of Manual Refinement:** Manually refining logical specifications or their associated labeling functions is a labor-intensive and error-prone process. It requires domain expertise, an understanding of RL learning dynamics, and iterative trial-and-error, which is impractical for complex tasks or environments.
4. **Addressing Specific Failure Modes:** The authors identify common ways in which specifications can be "coarse" or "under-specified":
 - **Overly broad target regions:** A goal region might include sections that are physically inaccessible or lead to traps, making the subtask unlearnable in practice.
 - **Insufficient safety constraints:** An agent might find a "short path" that is dangerously close to obstacles or leads to failure, even if not explicitly forbidden by the initial specification.
 - **Missing waypoints:** A complex, long-horizon task might be too difficult to learn as a single reach-avoid subtask, benefiting from decomposition into smaller, sequential steps.
 - **Heterogeneous source regions:** The starting region for a subtask might contain states from which successful completion is impossible, making the policy learning process inefficient or prone to failure.
 - **Lack of alternative paths:** The primary logical path specified might become blocked or infeasible, necessitating the discovery of alternative routes.

By addressing these issues through automated refinement, AUTOSPEC aims to make specification-guided RL more robust and accessible, reducing the burden on human engineers and expanding the applicability of these methods to real-world problems with inherently imperfect initial specifications. The core idea is to leverage empirical trajectory data to identify specific failures and then systematically adjust the specification to provide better guidance to the learning agent while ensuring the refined specification remains consistent with the original intent.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

AUTOSPEC is a framework designed to automatically refine coarse SpectRL specifications by observing policy learning failures. It operates as a wrapper around existing SpectRL-compatible reinforcement learning algorithms. The framework identifies problematic components of an initial specification and applies targeted refinement procedures to generate a more detailed specification, which then guides further policy learning. A critical property of AUTOSPEC is that any trajectory satisfying the refined specification is guaranteed to also satisfy the original specification (soundness).

**Overview of AUTOSPEC Framework:**
1. **Input:** An MDP, an initial SpectRL specification ϕ, a satisfaction probability threshold *p*, and a specification-guided RL algorithm *A*.
2. **Initial Learning:** The initial specification ϕ is translated into an abstract graph G. Algorithm *A* is then used to learn policies for the reach-avoid tasks associated with each edge in G.
3. **Failure Detection:** AUTOSPEC monitors the learning process. If a policy π learned for an edge *e* in G fails to achieve a satisfaction probability of at least *p* for its corresponding reach-avoid task, that edge is identified as problematic.
4. **Exploration-Guided Refinement:** For each problematic edge, AUTOSPEC employs an exploration-guided strategy. It samples trajectories using the current learned policy to gather empirical data about successes and failures. This data informs the refinement procedures.
5. **Iterative Refinement Procedures:** Four distinct refinement procedures are applied sequentially to the problematic edge. These procedures address different types of specification inadequacies, ordered by increasing structural modification to the abstract graph. The first refinement that successfully improves the edge’s satisfaction probability above *p* is applied.
6. **Graph Update and Re-learning:** If a refinement succeeds, the abstract graph G is updated to G_r, reflecting the refined specification. Algorithm *A* is then invoked again to learn new policies for the modified edges within the updated graph.
7. **Iteration:** This process repeats until a satisfactory policy for the overall specification is learned, or no further refinements yield improvement.

**SpectRL and Abstract Graphs:**
AUTOSPEC leverages the compositional nature of SpectRL specifications. SpectRL, a fragment of LTL, allows for boolean and sequential combinations of reach-avoid tasks. Any SpectRL specification can be translated into an equivalent directed acyclic graph (DAG) called an *abstract graph*. In this graph, vertices represent sets of MDP states (predicates), and edges represent reach-avoid tasks, where the agent must reach the target vertex's states from the source vertex's states while staying within the edge's safe states. AUTOSPEC’s refinements primarily modify these abstract graphs.

**Specification Refinement Subprocedures:**
AUTOSPEC employs four main subprocedures, each designed to address a particular type of specification inadequacy:

1. **SeqRefine (Refining Predicates):** This procedure refines the target and safety predicates associated with a problematic edge *e* = *u* → *u'*.
 - **ReachRefine:** Identifies states in the target region β(*u'*) that were successfully reached by sampled trajectories. It calculates a refined goal region β_r(*u'*) = β(*u'*) ∩ ConvexHull(successful_reached_states), effectively removing unreachable parts of the original target.
 - **AvoidRefine:** Identifies states where sampled trajectories entered unsafe regions (complement of β(*e*)). It computes a refined safe region β_r(*e*) = β(*e*) \ ConvexHull(last_k_unsafe_trajectory_states), excluding empirically identified unsafe areas.
 - *Effect:* Provides more precise guidance by narrowing down overly broad reach or avoid regions.

2. **AddRefine (Introducing Waypoints):** If direct navigation between *u* and *u'* is challenging, AddRefine decomposes the task.
 - It collects "midpoint" states from successful trajectories traversing *e*.
 - It defines a new intermediate vertex *u''* with its predicate β(*u''*) = β(*e*) ∩ ConvexHull(midpoints).
 - The original edge *e* = *u* → *u'* is replaced by two sequential edges: *e''* = *u* → *u''* and *e'* = *u''* → *u'*, both inheriting the original safety predicate β(*e*).
 - *Effect:* Breaks down a long-horizon task into easier-to-learn subtasks.

3. **PastRefine (Partitioning Source Regions):** This procedure addresses situations where some initial states in the source region *u* consistently lead to success, while others lead to failure.
 - It classifies initial states from sampled trajectories into successful and failing sets.
 - It learns a hyperplane that best separates these successful and failing initial states.
 - A new vertex *u*** is introduced with predicate β(*u***) = {*s* ∈ β(*u*) : hyperplane(*s*) ≥ 0}, representing the "successful" portion of the original source region.
 - The problematic edge *e* = *u* → *u'* is conceptually replaced or refined to *e*** = *u*** → *u'*, focusing learning on viable initial conditions.
 - *Effect:* Isolates promising initial conditions, making the subtask more learnable.

4. **OrRefine (Exploiting Alternative Paths):** When the direct path *e* = *u* → *u'* proves infeasible or consistently fails, OrRefine seeks alternative routes by leveraging existing graph structure.
 - It identifies other parents *u_i* of *u'* (i.e., vertices with existing edges *e_i* = *u_i* → *u'*).
 - For each viable *u_i*, it adds a new edge *e_new* = *u* → *u_i* with safety predicate β(*e_new*) = β(*e*). It also refines the safety predicate of *e_i* to β(*e_i*) = β(*e*) ∩ β(*e_i*).
 - *Effect:* Creates alternative disjunctive paths to the target, allowing the agent to succeed even if the original direct path is blocked.

**Correctness Guarantees (Soundness):**
Theorem 1 states that AUTOSPEC computes a refined specification ϕ_r whose satisfaction implies satisfaction of the original specification ϕ. This ensures that any policy learned for the refined task will also achieve the original task. The proof for each refinement procedure relies on the fact that the refined predicates are subsets of the original ones (SeqRefine, PastRefine) or that new paths only combine existing, valid sub-specifications and respect original constraints (AddRefine, OrRefine).
The paper acknowledges that the specification refinement problem is undecidable in general, meaning AUTOSPEC cannot provide completeness guarantees (it might fail to find a refinement even if one exists). It prioritizes soundness to maintain the user's original intent.

**Integration with RL Algorithms:**
AUTOSPEC is designed to be algorithm-agnostic in terms of the underlying RL technique. It works with any SpectRL-compatible algorithm that learns policies for abstract graph edges. The experiments utilize DIRL (Jothimurugan et al., 2021) and LSTS (Shukla et al., 2024), both of which employ PPO (Schulman et al., 2017) for policy learning. The framework estimates satisfaction probabilities through policy rollouts.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of AUTOSPEC was conducted across grid-based (n-Rooms) and continuous 3D robotic manipulation (PandaGym) environments. The experiments aimed to assess AUTOSPEC's ability to identify and rectify specification failures, enable learning from previously unlearnable specifications, and evaluate the requirements and limitations of the refinement process.

#### 4.1. Algorithm-Dependent Effectiveness: DIRL vs. LSTS

- **Context:** A 100-rooms environment with a complex specification involving multiple disjunctive branches and sequential compositions was used.
- **DIRL Integration (Successful Refinement):** When integrated with DIRL, which employs systematic exploration, AUTOSPEC successfully refined the specification.
 - ReachRefine was applied to the ϕm1 edge, removing unreachable target regions.
 - PastRefine was applied to the ϕm3 edge, identifying successful starting regions.
 - OrRefine was used when direct paths failed, leading to alternative routes through ϕm2.
 - **Result:** The overall satisfaction probability improved from near 0% to approximately 60%, demonstrating that DIRL's deep exploration provides sufficient data for AUTOSPEC to perform meaningful refinements.
- **LSTS Integration (Refinement Failure):** LSTS, which uses bandit-based exploration spreading effort across all edges, failed on the same specification.
 - Edges to M4, M5, and Goal achieved 0% satisfaction, providing no successful trajectory data for refinement.
 - **Result:** AUTOSPEC reported an inability to refine due to a lack of samples, indicating that the quality of refinement is fundamentally dependent on the base algorithm's exploration strategy and its ability to generate some successful trajectories, even if infrequent.

#### 4.2. Evaluation on Randomized 100-rooms and Predicate Placement

- **Context:** A procedurally generated 4-Way Gridworld with randomized wall connectivity and predicate placement was used to test generalization.
- **Result:** AUTOSPEC significantly outperformed the DIRL baseline. The baseline stagnated at approximately 20% success probability, while AUTOSPEC achieved a terminal success probability of around 60%.
 - **"Bridge" Bottleneck:** In 80% of random seeds, a key failure point was the transition from a central convergence point (MG → MG1) due to narrow or non-linear passages. AUTOSPEC applied AddRefine (introducing waypoints) or ReachRefine (tightening target constraints), consistently raising the local success rate for this bottleneck edge to 50–70%.
 - **Autonomous Corridor Switching:** When one path (e.g., through MG1) remained blocked or extremely difficult, AUTOSPEC effectively pruned that branch, allowing the search strategy to shift to an alternative branch (e.g., MG → MG2) and optimize that route, achieving >78% local success on the alternative. This demonstrated structural exploration.

#### 4.3. Evaluation of Individual Refinements

Targeted experiments were designed to validate each refinement procedure:
- **SeqRefine (Trap State Elimination):**
 - **Setup:** 9-rooms environment with a goal region containing a blocked (trap) room.
 - **Mechanism:** ReachRefine identified that successful trajectories only reached the accessible part of the goal.
 - **Result:** Satisfaction probability improved from 15% to 85% by excluding the trap region from the refined goal.
- **SeqRefine (Safety Constraint Discovery):**
 - **Setup:** 9-rooms with a narrow, dangerous passage below the goal.
 - **Mechanism:** AvoidRefine identified failure states near the passage.
 - **Result:** Satisfaction improved from 30% to 75% as the agent learned to use a wider, safer path by expanding the avoid region.
- **AddRefine (Waypoint Introduction):**
 - **Setup:** Long-horizon navigation across multiple rooms.
 - **Mechanism:** AddRefine introduced intermediate waypoints from successful trajectory midpoints.
 - **Result:** Satisfaction improved from 20% to 90% by decomposing the complex task into manageable subtasks.
- **PastRefine (Initial State Partitioning):**
 - **Setup:** Starting region included states from which the goal was unreachable.
 - **Mechanism:** PastRefine learned a hyperplane separating successful from failing initial states.
 - **Result:** Satisfaction improved from 40% to 80% by focusing learning on viable initial states.
- **OrRefine (Alternative Path Discovery):**
 - **Setup:** Specification with two paths, one blocked (ϕ_MID1_; ϕ_GOAL_ or ϕ_MID2_; ϕ_GOAL_).
 - **Mechanism:** OrRefine added a new edge (ϕ_MID1_ → ϕ_MID2_), creating an alternative route.
 - **Result:** Enabled satisfaction through the alternate path when the direct path had 0% success, demonstrating the necessity of disjunctive refinement when sequential modifications are insufficient.

#### 4.4. High-Dimensional Validation: PandaGym

- **Context:** A continuous 3D robotic manipulation task requiring navigation around an invisible wall, specified as (reach red-region avoid wall); (reach green-region avoid wall).
- **Result:** AUTOSPEC, integrated with DIRL, successfully applied refinements:
 - ReachRefine on the first edge identified and excluded unreachable portions of the red region behind the wall.
 - PastRefine on the second edge partitioned the intermediate state space, revealing that only certain approach angles from the red region led to successful reach of the green region.
 - This validated that AUTOSPEC's geometric refinements (convex hulls, hyperplanes) remain effective in high-dimensional continuous spaces without explicit knowledge of obstacle geometry.

#### 4.5. Computational Overhead

- AUTOSPEC avoids full retraining by only updating policies for refined edges. The total cost is T_total = T_base + Σ T_e (for e in refined edges R).
- **Result:** In the 100-room experiments, the baseline required ~240s for 8 fixed edges, while AUTOSPEC averaged 390 ± 42s. This overhead, corresponding to training 4–7 additional refinement edges, scaled linearly with detected bottlenecks, representing a favorable trade-off for the substantial improvement in success probability.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents AUTOSPEC, a framework that addresses a critical challenge in specification-guided reinforcement learning: the automatic refinement of coarse or under-specified logical task descriptions. The significance of this work lies in several areas:

1. **Enhancing Practicality of Specification-Guided RL:** By automating the refinement process, AUTOSPEC reduces the need for extensive manual reward engineering or meticulous specification design, which are often significant bottlenecks in applying RL to complex, real-world problems. This makes specification-guided RL more accessible and robust.
2. **Increased Task Learnability:** The framework enables existing specification-guided RL algorithms to solve tasks that were previously unlearnable due to overly broad target regions, insufficient safety constraints, complex long-horizon paths, or problematic initial conditions. This expands the range and complexity of problems that can be tackled with formal specifications.
3. **Formal Guarantees (Soundness):** The proven soundness of AUTOSPEC ensures that any policy learned for a refined specification will also satisfy the original, coarser specification. This is crucial for maintaining the user's intended task semantics and for applications requiring verifiable behavior.
4. **Modular and Algorithm-Agnostic Design:** AUTOSPEC acts as a wrapper, integrating with existing SpectRL-compatible RL algorithms. This modularity means its benefits can be readily incorporated into various current and future specification-guided RL frameworks.
5. **Addressing Key Failure Modes Systematically:** The four distinct refinement procedures (SeqRefine, AddRefine, PastRefine, OrRefine) systematically target different common inadequacies in logical specifications, offering a comprehensive approach to improvement. The experimental validation of each procedure demonstrates their utility in diagnosing and rectifying specific learning failures.
6. **Validation in Diverse Environments:** Successful application in both grid-based navigation and high-dimensional continuous robotic manipulation (PandaGym) suggests the generalizability of the geometric refinement techniques (e.g., convex hulls, hyperplanes) beyond simple environments.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Reduced Development Effort:** Engineers and domain experts can focus on defining high-level task objectives without being burdened by the need for low-level, granular details, accelerating the development of RL systems.
- **Improved Robustness:** By refining specifications based on empirical failures, agents can learn more robust policies that account for unstated environmental constraints or hazards.
- **Enhanced Autonomy:** The ability of an RL system to diagnose and fix issues with its own task definition represents a step towards more autonomous and adaptive intelligent agents.
- **Ethical Considerations:** As with any advanced RL system, applications in sensitive domains (e.g., military, surveillance, critical infrastructure) would require careful consideration of societal impacts, aligning with responsible AI development. The framework's ability to refine human specifications might lead to systems performing tasks in ways not fully anticipated but still logically consistent with the original intent.

**Limitations Acknowledged by the Authors:**
The paper acknowledges several limitations:
- **Dependence on Exploration Data:** AUTOSPEC requires sufficient exploration data and at least some successful trajectories from the base RL algorithm to compute meaningful refinements. If the base algorithm fails to generate any successful traces (e.g., LSTS on complex tasks), refinement becomes impossible.
- **Focus on Finitary LTL (SpectRL):** The current framework relies on finite witnesses to specification satisfaction, limiting its direct applicability to finite-horizon tasks or finitary fragments of LTL like SpectRL. Extending it to infinite-horizon ω-regular specifications would require further research.
- **Incompleteness:** Due to the undecidability of the specification refinement problem, AUTOSPEC is not complete; it may fail to find an existing refinement, especially when initial satisfaction probability is extremely low.
- **Sub-optimality of Refinements:** There is no guarantee that the candidate refinement generated is "optimal" in terms of providing the tightest possible bounds on predicates.

Despite these limitations, AUTOSPEC represents a systematic approach to automating a complex and previously manual process, making a significant contribution towards making specification-guided RL more practical and widely applicable.
