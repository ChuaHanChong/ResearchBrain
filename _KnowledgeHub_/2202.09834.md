# Real-time Model Predictive Control and System Identification Using Differentiable Physics Simulation

> [!summary] Summary
> A dual-threaded framework leverages differentiable physics for real-time model predictive control and adaptive system identification, allowing robots to continuously refine their internal models and control policies post-deployment. This approach achieves superior control performance and rapid parameter identification across various simulated dynamic tasks and on a real robot arm.

## Problem

- Robotic systems struggle to continuously adapt their control policies and internal models to dynamic environmental changes or evolving robot parameters after initial deployment.
- Traditional online system identification methods often yield unstable or inaccurate parameter estimations due to noisy, outdated, or uninformative observations.
- Maintaining real-time optimal control while simultaneously and efficiently updating system parameters poses significant computational and scheduling challenges.

## Method

![[2202.09834-method.png]]
*The dual-threaded framework utilizes a differentiable physics engine to simultaneously perform online system identification (modeling thread) and model predictive control (planning thread).*

- The framework utilizes a ==dual-threaded architecture== with an asynchronous "planning thread" for ==Model Predictive Control (MPC)== and a "modeling thread" for ==real-time system identification (SysID)==.
- ==Differentiable physics simulation== is employed to efficiently compute gradients for both the SysID optimization (minimizing state discrepancy) and the MPC (using ==iLQR==).
- An ==adaptive online SysID== method computes a ==confidence score== for parameter excitation, uses ==weighted parameter averaging==, and triggers ==active exploration== (planning for better identification) when observations are uninformative or parameters change significantly.

## Results

- The proposed method consistently demonstrated superior performance in terms of control effectiveness and system identification accuracy across various simulated dynamic motor control tasks compared to five baselines.
- On a real 7-DOF robot arm, the method swiftly identified new payload masses, maintaining precise control with a **2 cm** tracking error compared to **7 cm** for an offline, non-adaptive method.
- Actively controlled system identification reduced the average task completion time by **2.072 seconds** in an elastic rod manipulation task by rapidly correcting initial parameter underestimation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Differentiable physics can enable the simultaneous and efficient execution of real-time online system identification and optimal control within a dual-threaded architecture.
> - An adaptive online system identification approach, incorporating a confidence score for parametric excitation and weighted averaging, improves robustness against noisy or uninformative data in dynamic environments.
> - Integrating actively controlled system identification into the MPC framework allows robots to strategically explore behaviors that maximize parametric excitation, leading to faster and more accurate model adaptation when passive observations are insufficient.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses the "sim-to-real gap" in robotics, a recognized challenge where control policies or mechanical designs developed in simulation often fail when deployed on physical systems. Existing research typically approaches this problem through two main avenues: improving the accuracy and fidelity of robot models and simulators, or designing control policies that are more robust and adaptive to noisy or changing system parameters.

Within the first avenue, System Identification (SysID) has been explored for decades, with methods ranging from classical offline techniques (e.g., frequency/impulse response, regression for linear/nonlinear systems) to more recent approaches utilizing differentiable physics engines or learned neural networks. However, many of these methods are offline or struggle with parameter changes during online control. Online SysID methods, such as recursive least squares or stochastic optimization, exist but often face challenges with noisy or uninformative observations and computational efficiency. Learning-based approaches have also been developed to model time-varying parameters or learn transition functions directly from data.

In the second avenue, robust control aims to account for inaccurate dynamics and noisy measurements, often sacrificing task performance for generality. Adaptive control, on the other hand, explicitly relies on an online SysID module to continuously update the controller with current system parameters. This category includes approaches using stochastic optimization, or trained neural networks to infer parameters for universal policies.

More recently, differentiable physics simulators have emerged as a powerful tool for various robotic tasks, including control design, parameter identification, and design optimization. They provide gradient information that can be exploited for these problems. This paper integrates concepts from adaptive control and differentiable physics, specifically focusing on continuous improvement of modeling and control *after* deployment in dynamically changing real-world environments. It distinguishes itself by simultaneously leveraging differentiable physics for both online system identification and optimal control within a dual-threaded framework, aiming for sample-efficient adaptation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable robotic systems to continuously improve their modeling and control capabilities in real-world, dynamically changing environments *after* initial deployment. This addresses a critical limitation of traditional sim-to-real transfer methods that often assume static or pre-defined environmental conditions.

The key motivations driving this work are:

- **Continuous Adaptation Post-Deployment:** Robotic systems frequently operate in environments where their own parameters (e.g., wear and tear, changing payloads) or environmental parameters (e.g., varying terrain friction, object properties) can change over time. The paper aims to provide a mechanism for robots to adapt to these changes without requiring re-training or extensive offline recalibration. Examples provided include a quadruped adapting to different terrains or a manipulator re-estimating object weights.
- **Addressing Challenges in Online System Identification:** Traditional online SysID methods often encounter issues with noisy, outdated, or uninformative (not "parametrically exciting") observations. These issues can lead to unstable and erroneous parameter estimations. The research aims to develop a robust online SysID method that can assess the confidence of estimated parameters and respond appropriately to different observation scenarios.
- **Ensuring Real-time Optimal Control:** For continuous adaptation, the control policy must be re-planned rapidly enough to keep up with the robot's operation while also incorporating the most recent model updates. This presents a scheduling challenge to balance computational efficiency with the freshness of the control plan.
- **Leveraging Differentiable Physics:** The authors hypothesize that differentiable physics simulation can efficiently provide the necessary gradient information for both system identification and control optimization in real-time, thereby enabling the desired continuous adaptation in a sample-efficient manner.
- **Active System Identification:** When the system's current trajectory does not provide sufficient information to identify certain parameters, the robot needs a mechanism to actively explore behaviors that would yield better data. The paper aims to integrate this capability into the control framework.

In essence, the work seeks to develop a real-time, dual-purpose framework that simultaneously refines the robot's model of itself and its environment, and generates optimal control actions based on that continuously improving model.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method utilizes a differentiable physics simulation framework to perform online system identification (SysID) and optimal control concurrently, processing streaming observations from the target environment in real-time. The framework operates with two parallel, asynchronous threads: a **planning thread** for optimal control and a **modeling thread** for system identification.

**A. Dual-Threaded Framework Overview:**
- **Modeling Thread:** This thread processes a history buffer (`H`) containing recent observed states from the robot's environment. It optimizes system parameters ($\mu$) to minimize the discrepancy between simulated and observed states using gradients from the differentiable physics engine. Upon completion, it updates the controller with the refined $\mu$. This process repeats with new batches of states.
- **Planning Thread:** This thread continuously solves for a future sequence of actions over a finite horizon. It formulates an optimal control problem as an iterative Linear Quadratic Regulator (iLQR) problem within a Model Predictive Control (MPC) paradigm, utilizing the most recent $\mu$ provided by the modeling thread. The resulting action trajectory is stored in a plan buffer (`P`) for the robot to execute. The next optimization starts immediately after the current one, extending the planning horizon into the future.

**B. Real-time System Identification:**
The modeling thread aims to find optimal system parameters $\mu^*$ by minimizing the difference between forward simulation results and observed states: $\mu^* = \text{argmin}_{\mu} ||\text{sim}(q_0, \dot{q}_0, u_{0:H}; \mu, H) - q_{0:H}||$. Differentiable physics is used to compute gradients for this optimization.

To address challenges of noisy, outdated, or uninformative observations in an online setting, an **adaptive online SysID** method is developed:
- **Confidence Score (`w`):** For each SysID optimization, a confidence score `w` is computed, which quantifies how "parametrically exciting" the observed state trajectory is for the parameters being identified. This score is inspired by persistent excitation analysis.
- **Weighted Parameter Averaging:** The currently estimated system parameter $\bar{\mu}$ is maintained as a weighted sum of past solutions: $\bar{\mu} = \frac{\sum w_t \mu_t}{\sum w_t}$.
- **Adaptive Response:**
 - If `w*` (new confidence) is high and `||μ* - μ̄||` (new solution difference) is small, $\mu^*$ is accepted and $\bar{\mu}$ is updated using the weighted sum.
 - If `w*` and `||μ* - μ̄||` are both large, it indicates a significant environmental change. $\bar{\mu}$ is reset to $\mu^*$, and previous weights are discarded.
 - If `w*` is small, $\mu^*$ is discarded, and $\bar{\mu}$ is not updated.
- **Confidence Score Computation (Parameter-Specific):**
 - **Masses:** The system's equations of motion are linearized with respect to mass. The confidence score is based on the magnitude of the generalized forces components relating to each mass, summed over the history buffer. A small magnitude indicates sensitivity to noise or lack of identifiability.
 - **Moment of Inertia:** Similar to masses, the equations are rearranged to factor out principal inertia terms. The confidence score is based on the magnitudes of terms related to angular acceleration, summed over the history.
 - **Center of Mass (COM):** The effect of COM on the Jacobian matrix is analyzed. The confidence score relies on terms related to both linear and angular accelerations that affect the COM, summed over the history. Quadratic terms in $r_k$ are dropped for simplification.
 - **Joint Stiffness and Damping:** For joint stiffness, the score is based on the magnitude of joint displacement from the rest position. For damping, it's based on the magnitude of joint velocities. Both are summed over the history buffer.

**C. Real-time Optimal Control:**
- **iLQR for MPC:** The planning thread uses iLQR, known for its predictable runtime, to solve the optimal control problem in an MPC fashion. Warm-starting with previous solutions (linear control laws, state/action trajectories) is employed to improve computational performance.
- **Adaptive Starting Time (`_t`):** To ensure the action buffer (`P`) is replenished faster than real-time while keeping the plan fresh, the starting time `_t` of the optimization horizon `T` is adaptively chosen. It is set to `t + t_e * N_iter`, where `t` is the current time, `t_e` is the profiled duration of one iLQR iteration, and `N_iter` is the number of iterations from the *previous* iLQR solve. This aims to predict when the current optimization will finish and plan from that point.

**D. Actively Controlled System Identification:**
If the confidence score `w` remains low for a predefined number of consecutive SysID optimizations (`N_his`), the planning thread switches its objective function from the primary `f_task` to an `f_exp` objective. `f_exp` is designed to actively explore system behaviors that maximize parametric excitation, specifically, `f_exp = -w(x_{0:H})`. This means the controller plans a trajectory to increase the confidence score for the parameters that are currently difficult to identify.

The method employs an off-the-shelf differentiable physics engine, NimblePhysics, and introduces simulated sensing and actuating errors via Gaussian noise to approximate real-world conditions.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The method was evaluated on four dynamic motor control tasks in simulation and one on a real robot, comparing its performance against five baseline methods: Naive, Smooth, Weighted, UP-OSI, and DuST.

**A. General Performance Across Tasks:**
- **Superior Performance:** The proposed method consistently demonstrated superior performance across multiple experiments in terms of both system identification accuracy and control effectiveness (measured by time to reach control objective).
- **Baseline Comparison:**
 - **Naive, Smooth, Weighted:** The proposed method outperformed these baselines, which are simpler variations of online SysID. The "Weighted" baseline (using the confidence score for averaging without adaptive responses) also performed better than Naive and Smooth, indicating the effectiveness of the confidence score itself.
 - **UP-OSI (MLP-based SysID + RL Policy):** This method failed in the Cartpole task and took longer in the Inverted Double Pendulum task. The paper suggests this is due to its brittleness when parameter changes result in out-of-distribution state trajectories.
 - **DuST (Stochastic Optimization):** This method failed in both Cartpole and Inverted Double Pendulum tasks. The paper notes that its controller was significantly slower (80x) than the proposed method's MPC-iLQR, highlighting the computational advantages of gradient-based optimization with differentiable physics.

**B. Specific Task Results:**

- **Swing-Up Cartpole with Unknown Masses:**
 - The proposed method accurately and rapidly identified changing cart and pole masses.
 - It achieved the control objective (swing-up and balance) in an average of 6.31 seconds (SD 0.42), outperforming all baselines. UP-OSI and DuST failed to complete the task within 1000 steps.
- **Inverted Double Pendulum (InvDP) with Unknown Damping:**
 - The method identified damping coefficients more accurately than baselines.
 - It completed the task in an average of 3.53 seconds (SD 0.22), outperforming baselines. UP-OSI took 6.23 seconds, and DuST failed.
- **Robot Arm with Unknown End-Effector Mass Distribution:**
 - The adaptive SysID component effectively tracked changes in the center of mass (COM) and moment of inertia (MOI) of the end-effector-object assembly.
 - This accurate estimation led to faster control convergence, completing the task in 2.10 seconds (SD 0.20), again outperforming baselines.
- **Moving an Elastic Rod with Unknown Elasticity:**
 - This task demonstrated the benefit of **actively controlled system identification**. Initially, conservative actions due to underestimated joint stiffness prevented effective SysID. The confidence score correctly identified this issue, triggering the active SysID mode (`f_exp`).
 - Active SysID quickly corrected the joint stiffness estimate, allowing the controller to switch back to the main task objective and resulting in a faster control sequence. Active SysID reduced average task completion time by 2.072 seconds.
 - The method accurately identified rod elasticity and adapted quickly to changes.
- **System ID with Contact (Robot Hopper):**
 - The SysID method was demonstrated in a contact-rich environment (a hopper robot).
 - It accurately tracked changes in leg damping coefficients, which was crucial for maintaining energy and successfully navigating stairs. The NAIVE baseline failed to re-estimate damping, leading to robot failure.
- **Adaptive Starting Time for Planning Thread:**
 - On the cart-pole example, the adaptive starting time method for the planning thread resulted in a mean absolute error (MAE) of 3.04ms between actual and estimated start times.
 - Compared to a fixed starting time (averaged from profiling), the adaptive method completed the cartpole task in 6.31 seconds without failure, while the fixed method took 8.20 seconds with two failures.
- **Hyper-parameter Selection Study:**
 - The model demonstrated relative robustness to variations in the SysID horizon (`H`), the threshold for detecting parameter changes ($\epsilon_1$), and the confidence threshold ($\epsilon_2$).
 - It showed more sensitivity to the MPC control horizon (`T`), a common observation in practice.

**C. Real Robot Experiment:**
- A 7-DOF Rokae Xmate3 Pro robot arm was used to validate online SysID. The task involved moving the end-effector vertically along a sinusoidal trajectory, with changing end-effector payload mass and mass attached to a body link.
- The method swiftly identified new masses after payload changes, maintaining precise control with a 2 cm tracking error.
- An offline SysID method (with initially correct mass but no adaptation) showed 7 cm tracking error after payload changes, demonstrating the importance of online adaptation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presents a framework for continuous improvement of robot modeling and control capabilities in dynamically changing real-world environments. This has several significant implications and potential impacts:

- **Enhanced Robot Autonomy and Robustness:** By enabling robots to continuously adapt their internal models and control policies based on real-time observations, the framework could lead to more robust and autonomous robotic systems that can operate effectively in unpredictable or long-term deployments without human intervention for recalibration.
- **Reduced Sim-to-Real Gap:** The approach directly addresses the sim-to-real gap by continuously refining the simulation model with real-world data post-deployment. This contrasts with methods that primarily focus on making policies robust in simulation pre-deployment.
- **Sample-Efficient Adaptation:** The use of differentiable physics allows for efficient gradient-based optimization for both system identification and control, potentially leading to more sample-efficient adaptation compared to purely learning-based or stochastic optimization methods that might require vast amounts of real-world data.
- **Improved Control Performance:** Accurate and up-to-date models are critical for precision control. The demonstrated ability to swiftly identify changing parameters (e.g., mass, inertia, damping, stiffness) directly translates to improved control performance and task success rates, as evidenced by the experimental results.
- **Active Exploration for Better Identification:** The integration of active system identification is a notable contribution, allowing the robot to strategically generate "parametrically exciting" trajectories when observational data is insufficient. This enhances the reliability of online parameter estimation.
- **Practical Application Potential:** The demonstrated applications on tasks like manipulator payload changes, unstable locomotion, and elastic object manipulation highlight the method's potential for various real-world robotics domains where environmental or robot properties are subject to change.

**Limitations:**
The authors acknowledge that optimizing complex trajectories involving contacts remains challenging for the method. While extensible in principle, the convergence of the iLQR planner can be inconsistent in computation time and solution quality due to "ambiguous contact gradient and rough loss landscape." This suggests that further research into more robust controllers for contact-rich scenarios is a necessary next step.
