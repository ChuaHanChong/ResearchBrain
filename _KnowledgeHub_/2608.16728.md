# Design Optimization for Large High-Force Soft Robot Manipulators Under Gravitational Loads

> [!summary] Summary
> A closed-form design-optimization method for large soft robot limbs that maximizes end-effector blocking force under an anti-buckling gravitational constraint, using Greenhill's beam theory plus a monotonicity theorem. Three PneuNet arm prototypes validate that the analytically predicted optimal geometry withstands its own weight and produces the highest measured force.

## Problem

- No systematic framework exists to determine a-priori whether a fully-soft manipulator design is viable at human scale under its own gravitational load.
- Prior soft-robot geometry optimization relies on iterative finite-element analysis tied to specific tasks and robots, limiting generalizable early-stage design conclusions.
- Existing rod-model optimization methods target locomotion objectives or lack analytical optimality proofs, leaving force-maximization under self-weight unaddressed.

## Method

- Formulates a constrained optimization over limb geometry parameters maximizing blocking force subject to a volume budget and an anti-buckling constraint from ==Greenhill's maximum stable height formula==.
- Proves an ==Active Constraint theorem== showing that under convexity and componentwise monotonicity assumptions the optimum lies analytically on the constraint boundary, avoiding iterative search.
- Introduces a heuristic under symmetric-periodic geometry assumptions that under-approximates maximum stable length for nonuniform cross-sections using minimum inertia and average linear density.

## Results

- Across three PneuNet limb prototypes, the heuristic buckling-length approximation matched empirical measurements within a maximum error of **3.5 cm**, about **6%** of the **0.49 m** robot height.
- The theoretically optimal design (chamber width **0.045 m**) produced the highest blocking force among all three limbs for pressures above a critical threshold of about **20 hPa**.
- The under-constrained design (chamber width **0.030 m**) failed to satisfy the anti-buckling requirement and produced zero measurable blocking force across the tested **0-290 hPa** pressure range.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Blocking force can serve as a tractable analytical surrogate objective for physical-interaction force when full workspace force models are unavailable or complex.
> - Componentwise monotonicity plus convex feasible regions let designers skip iterative FEA entirely and solve geometry optimization in closed form for an entire design class.
> - Self-buckling under gravity, not payload or actuation limits, can be the binding constraint separating viable from non-viable large-scale soft manipulator designs.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of soft robotics has seen considerable interest in developing manipulators for human-scale tasks, such as healthcare assistance, due to their inherent compliance and safety features. However, a significant challenge in this domain is the design of large soft robots that are capable of generating substantial forces while simultaneously supporting their own weight under gravitational loads.

Prior work in large soft robots has typically focused on specific proof-of-concept prototypes, often incorporating rigid or stiff materials, limiting softness to the end-effector, or operating under conditions where gravitational self-loading is not a primary concern (e.g., underwater or in environments with external supports). These existing large soft manipulators often exhibit limitations such as bulkiness or slow actuation speeds. Furthermore, current design optimization methods for soft robots frequently rely on computationally intensive techniques like finite element analysis (FEA), which iteratively adjust design parameters to reach local optima. These methods are often highly task-specific and robot-specific, making it difficult to draw generalizable conclusions early in the design process. Other approaches using rod models for soft robot optimization primarily focus on locomotion objectives rather than manipulation and force generation, and often lack analytical proofs of optimality. To date, a systematic framework for analytically determining the suitability of a soft robot design paradigm for a desired task, particularly under gravitational loads without iterative computation, has been largely absent.

This research addresses this gap by proposing a method for optimizing the geometry of a soft robot limb to maximize its blocking force, subject to anti-buckling constraints under its own gravitational loading. The novelty lies in providing an explicit, analytical solution to this optimization problem under specific assumptions, enabling a "one-shot determination" of design suitability. This contrasts with iterative, simulation-based approaches, offering a more efficient way to evaluate if a fully-soft manipulator can meet engineering goals at human scales for physical interaction.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from the persistent question of "How large can soft robots be?" while remaining functional and robust. Specifically, the authors seek to address the challenge of designing soft robot manipulators that can generate high interaction forces at human scales while simultaneously supporting their own weight. This is critical for applications like healthcare assistance, where safe and strong physical human-robot interaction is desired, but current large soft robots often fall short due to stability or force limitations.

The research is driven by the lack of a formal methodology to systematically determine the appropriateness of fully-soft manipulators for larger sizes, particularly under gravitational loads. Current approaches are often ad-hoc or computationally expensive, hindering early-stage design decisions. The authors propose that if a robust analytical design framework existed, designers could determine *a priori* if an intended class of soft manipulators is a suitable choice for a given physical interaction task at larger scales.

To address this, the paper establishes three key objectives:
1. **Develop a methodology for optimizing the geometric design of soft robotic limbs under gravity-induced loading:** This involves formulating a constrained optimization problem that balances maximizing end-effector blocking force with ensuring structural stability against buckling due to self-weight.
2. **Propose a heuristic approach to approximate maximum stable heights of beams with complex geometries:** Recognizing that classical Euler-Bernoulli beam theory typically applies to uniform beams, this objective aims to extend its applicability to more complex, non-uniform soft robot geometries through suitable approximations.
3. **Validate the proposed approach through hardware experiments:** This objective involves manufacturing and testing physical prototypes based on the optimized designs to confirm the theoretical predictions regarding buckling behavior and force generation.

By achieving these objectives, the research aims to provide a practical tool for designers to efficiently assess the viability of soft robot concepts for real-world, human-scale manipulation tasks where both strength and stability under gravity are paramount.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on formulating the design of a soft robotic limb as an optimization problem. This problem is designed to identify geometric parameters that maximize the robot's blocking force while ensuring structural stability under gravitational loads.

**A. Problem Setup**
The soft robotic limb is considered to be made of a homogeneous material, with its geometry defined by a vector of design parameters, $\theta = [\theta_1..., \theta_n]^\top$. The total length of the limb, $L$, is considered fixed but not directly optimized in the primary formulation.

Two primary scenarios are modeled:
1. **Blocking Force ($F$):** This represents the force exerted by the limb when it is actuated by an input $u$ (e.g., pneumatic pressure) and brought into contact with an object in a static equilibrium condition. It is assumed that in this blocking position, the limb's deflection $\delta(z)$ along its central axis is approximately zero for all $z$, thus simplifying the moment calculations. The blocking force $F$ is modeled as a function of the geometry $\theta$ and the actuation input $u$, denoted as $F(\theta, u)$. For optimization, $u$ is fixed at $\bar{u}$, making $F(\theta)$ the objective function.
2. **Self-Buckling:** This scenario investigates the limb's structural stability when standing passively under its own weight. It is modeled as a uniform, linearly elastic beam with fixed and free ends. Key properties are a constant cross-sectional area $A$, a constant second moment of inertia $I$, material density $\rho$, and Young's modulus $E$. Drawing from Euler-Bernoulli beam theory and Greenhill's solution, the maximum stable height $L_{max}$ (below which buckling does not occur) is given by:
 $L_{max}(\theta) = (7.8373 \frac{EI(\theta)}{\rho g A(\theta)})^{1/3}$
 where $I$ and $A$ are functions of the geometric parameters $\theta$.

**B. Optimization Approach**
The design optimization problem is formulated as follows:
$\theta^* = \text{argmax}_{\theta} F(\theta)$
subject to:
- $V(\theta) \le \bar{V}$ (Volume/mass constraint, where $\bar{V}$ is the maximum allowed volume of material)
- $L_{max}(\theta) \ge \bar{L}$ (Anti-buckling constraint, where $\bar{L}$ is the desired operational length of the limb)

To enable an analytical solution, two key assumptions are introduced:
- **Assumption 1 (Convex $\Omega$):** The set of feasible designs $\Omega$, defined by the constraints, is convex.
- **Assumption 2 (Componentwise monotonicity):** The objective function $F(\theta)$ is smooth, differentiable, and strictly monotonic (either strictly increasing or strictly decreasing) with respect to each design parameter $\theta_k$ within the feasible set $\Omega$.

Under these assumptions, **Theorem 1 (Active Constraint)** is proven. This theorem states that the global maximizer $\theta^*$ is unique (under specific conditions) and must lie on the boundary of the feasible set $\Omega$, meaning at least one of the inequality constraints ($V(\theta) \le \bar{V}$ or $L_{max}(\theta) \ge \bar{L}$) must be active. This significantly simplifies the search for the optimal solution.

**C. Heuristic Approximation for Nonuniform Geometries**
A practical challenge arises because Greenhill's solution (Eq. 1) assumes a cross-sectionally uniform beam. To extend this to non-uniform soft robot limbs, a heuristic approach is proposed:
- **Assumption 3:** The limb's cross-sectional area $A(\theta, z)$ and second moment of inertia $I(\theta, z)$ are symmetrical and periodic along its central axis $z$.
- **Assumption 4:** If Assumption 3 holds, $L_{max}(\theta)$ can be under-approximated using the minimum second moment of inertia ($I_{min} = \text{min}_z I(\theta, z)$) and an average linear density ($\lambda(\theta) = \rho V(\theta)/L$). This leads to a modified $L_{max}$ formula:
 $L_{max}(\theta) = (7.8373 \frac{EI_{min}(\theta)}{g \lambda(\theta)})^{1/3}$
 This approximation allows the buckling constraint to be applied to more complex, segmented soft robot designs.

**D. Application to Pneumatic Network Actuators (PneuNets)**
The methodology is applied to a specific architecture: a soft limb composed of three individual PneuNet actuators.
- **Design Parameters:** Initially, six parameters ($\theta := [h, w, t, m, \ell, L]^\top$) are defined. For the proof-of-concept, manufacturing limits and simplicity reduce the optimization to a single variable: chamber width, $w$. Other parameters like chamber height ($h$), inextensible layer thickness ($t$), chamber length ($\ell$), number of chambers ($m$), and total length ($L=\bar{L}$) are fixed.
- **Blocking Force Model:** A simplified blocking force model adapted from prior work for 1D PneuNets is used, yielding $F(w) \propto w(h/2 + t + w/2 \tan(\pi/6))$. This function is shown to be a positive quadratic, implying strict monotonicity under certain conditions.
- **Analytical Solution:** Using specified design requirements ($\bar{L}=0.49m$, $\bar{V}=8.387e^{-4}m^3$) and material properties (Smooth-On Smooth-Sil 945), the feasible set $\Omega$ is analytically determined for $w$. Both $V(w)$ and $L_{max}(w)$ are shown to be strictly increasing functions of $w$. This establishes that $\Omega$ is convex (Assumption 1) and $F(w)$ is strictly increasing on $\Omega$ (Assumption 2). Applying Theorem 1, the optimal chamber width $w^*$ is found to be the upper bound of $\Omega$, constrained by the maximum volume $\bar{V}$. The calculated optimal width is $w^* = 0.0452m$, rounded to $0.045m$.

**E. Hardware Experimentation**
Three prototypes are fabricated with different chamber widths:
- $w_{45} = 0.045m$ (optimal)
- $w_{40} = 0.040m$ (sub-optimal)
- $w_{30} = 0.030m$ (predicted to violate anti-buckling constraint)

- **Fabrication:** A multi-segment silicone mold casting process is used to create limbs longer than the target $\bar{L}$, overcoming 3D printer bed size limitations. Connecting areas are designed to have higher $I/A$ ratios to ensure Assumption 3 holds.
- **Maximum Stable Length ($L_{max}$) Determination:** Limbs are manufactured longer than their predicted $L_{max}$ (approx. 0.6m). Varying effective lengths $L$ are created using chamber clamps. Tip deflection $\delta(L)$ is measured using an OptiTrack motion capture system. An empirical buckling length, $\hat{L}_{max}$, is defined as the length where the rate of change of deflection with respect to length ($d\delta/dL$) is maximal.
- **Optimal Design Validation (Blocking Force):** Each limb is clamped at the desired length $\bar{L}=0.49m$. A pneumatic system with closed-loop pressure control is used to actuate one side of the limb, and blocking force is measured using a strain gauge sensor over a range of gauge pressures ($u \in$ hPa). The objective is to verify that $w_{45}$ produces the highest blocking force among the designs.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental results validate the proposed optimization method, demonstrating its ability to predict buckling behavior and identify the optimal design for maximizing blocking force.

**A. Buckling Length**
Experimental measurements of tip deflection ($\delta$) at varying effective limb lengths ($L$) were conducted for the three prototypes. The results indicated that buckling instability manifests as a gradual increase in deflection rather than an abrupt point, consistent with behavior of elastic columns with imperfections.
To quantify the empirical buckling length, $\hat{L}_{max}$, it was defined as the length at which the rate of change in deflection along the change of length is maximal. This was determined by observing the steepest slope in the $\delta$ vs. $L$ plots.

The comparison between the predicted $L_{max}$ from Equation (6) and the empirically measured $\hat{L}_{max}$ is as follows:
- **$w_{30}$ limb:**
 - Predicted $L_{max}$: 0.438 m
 - Empirical $\hat{L}_{max}$: 0.447 $\pm$ 0.007 m
 - Discrepancy ($\Delta L_{max}$): 0.009 m
- **$w_{40}$ limb:**
 - Predicted $L_{max}$: 0.515 m
 - Empirical $\hat{L}_{max}$: 0.517 $\pm$ 0.007 m
 - Discrepancy ($\Delta L_{max}$): 0.002 m
- **$w_{45}$ limb:**
 - Predicted $L_{max}$: 0.551 m
 - Empirical $\hat{L}_{max}$: 0.517 $\pm$ 0.007 m
 - Discrepancy ($\Delta L_{max}$): 0.035 m

The results indicate that the heuristic approximation (Assumption 4 leading to Eq. 6) provides a reasonable prediction of the maximum stable height. The maximum observed error was 3.5 cm for the $w_{45}$ prototype, which represents approximately 6% of the robot's intended height ($\bar{L}=0.49m$). The $w_{30}$ limb, predicted to violate the anti-buckling constraint ($L_{max} = 0.438m < \bar{L} = 0.49m$), consistently exhibited buckling at the target length $\bar{L}$ and longer lengths.

**B. Blocking Force**
Blocking force measurements were performed for each limb at the desired length $\bar{L} = 0.49m$ across a range of normalized pneumatic pressures ($\Delta u$).
- The $w_{30}$ limb, which failed the anti-buckling constraint, exerted no measurable blocking force across the tested pressure range, indicating its inability to support itself and apply force.
- For the $w_{40}$ and $w_{45}$ limbs, blocking force increased with pressure.
- A critical pressure difference, $\Delta u_{crit} = 20.22$ hPa, was identified as the point where the relationship between forces became consistent.
- For all pressures $\Delta u > \Delta u_{crit}$, the $w_{45}$ limb consistently produced the highest blocking force, followed by the $w_{40}$ limb, and then the $w_{30}$ limb (which produced zero force). Specifically, $F_{45}(\Delta u) > F_{40}(\Delta u) > F_{30}(\Delta u)$.

These results confirm the theoretical prediction that $w_{45}$ is the optimal design for maximizing blocking force within the given constraints and assumptions. The initial low-pressure behavior where the force relationship was not perfectly consistent was attributed to noise in the experimental setup, with the higher pressure range demonstrating clear optimality.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a notable advancement in the design of large, high-force soft robot manipulators by offering a systematic and analytical framework.

**A. Significance**
The primary significance of this work lies in providing the first known analytical method for determining the force-optimal geometry of a large soft robot arm that is explicitly designed to operate under gravitational loads without buckling. This addresses a fundamental question in soft robotics: whether fully soft manipulators can simultaneously be large and strong enough for practical applications.

The proposed methodology offers several key advantages:
- **Analytical Solution:** By framing the design problem as a constrained optimization problem and leveraging assumptions of convexity and monotonicity, the approach yields a closed-form solution. This eliminates the need for computationally intensive iterative methods (such as FEA) that are common in soft robot design, thereby streamlining the design process.
- **Early-Stage Design Assessment:** The analytical nature of the solution allows designers to make a "one-shot determination" early in the design phase. This enables quick assessment of whether a given class of soft manipulator is appropriate for human-scale physical interaction tasks, saving significant time and resources.
- **Generalizability of Core Concept:** While demonstrated with PneuNet actuators, the underlying problem formulation is independent of the specific actuation mechanism. This suggests that the core principles of maximizing interaction force under buckling constraints could be extended to other types of soft manipulators, provided analytical models for force and structural properties are available or can be adequately approximated.
- **Validation of Heuristic for Non-uniform Geometries:** The introduction and experimental validation of Assumption 4 for approximating the maximum stable height of non-uniform beams is crucial for applying classical beam theory to the complex, segmented geometries often found in soft robots. The observed error of approximately 6% for half-meter prototypes suggests its practical utility for first-pass design.

**B. Potential Impact**
The potential impact of this research is substantial, particularly for the development and application of human-scale soft robots:
- **Advancing Human-Robot Interaction (HRI):** By enabling the design of large, strong, and stable soft manipulators, this work can accelerate the development of safer and more effective robots for direct physical interaction with humans. This is especially relevant for fields such as healthcare assistance (e.g., patient handling, rehabilitation), where gentle yet powerful manipulation is required.
- **Improved Design Efficiency:** The ability to analytically derive optimal geometries will significantly reduce the time and computational resources typically consumed by iterative design and simulation cycles. This can lead to faster prototyping and deployment of new soft robot designs.
- **Expanding the Scope of Soft Robotics:** By addressing the fundamental challenges of self-weight support and high-force generation at larger scales, this methodology contributes to expanding the range of applications for which soft robots are considered viable, moving beyond small-scale or purely compliant tasks.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The identified limitations, such as the reliance on an analytical force model and the use of blocking force as a surrogate for full workspace interaction, pave the way for future work. This includes developing more sophisticated analytical models, exploring multi-variable optimization, and integrating dynamic and deflection considerations, ultimately leading to even more capable soft robotic systems.
