# Training Tactile Sensors to Learn Force Sensing from Each Other

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at King's College London developed GenForce, a framework for transferable force sensing that allows diverse tactile sensors to learn force prediction from each other. This approach unifies tactile signals and translates deformation patterns, reducing the need for extensive, sensor-specific calibration and enabling accurate force prediction and robust robot manipulation across heterogeneous sensor types.

## Problem

- Diverse tactile sensor designs create significant "domain gaps" hindering the transfer of learned experiences and requiring laborious, costly data collection for each new sensor.
- Soft tactile sensor elastomers are prone to degradation, necessitating frequent replacement and recalibration which currently demands repeated data collection and model retraining.
- Existing transfer learning and representation learning methods often require new labeled data or fall short in generalizing across fundamentally different sensing principles and material properties.

## Method

- A unified marker representation converts diverse raw tactile signals (e.g., images, electrical signals) into standardized marker-based images.
- An image-conditioned diffusion model performs Marker-to-Marker (M2M) translation, transforming deformed marker images from a source sensor into the visual style of a target sensor while preserving deformation.
- A spatiotemporal force prediction model, trained on transferred marker images, predicts three-axis forces, complemented by a material hardness compensation method that adjusts force labels based on material priors.

## Results

- M2M translation achieved approximately 100-fold decreases in FID and KID metrics in simulation and over 98% reduction in real-world image similarity for homogeneous sensors.
- GenForce, with material compensation, reduced the average Mean Absolute Error (MAE) across heterogeneous sensor transfers to less than 0.92 N for normal force and less than 0.3 N for shear forces.
- The framework enabled a robot to successfully perform damage-free grasping of nine diverse, unseen objects and achieve robust slip detection and avoidance in manipulation tasks using transferred force models.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Tactile signals from highly diverse sensors can be unified into a common marker representation, enabling a single transfer framework across various sensing principles and designs.
> - Diffusion models are effective for translating tactile deformation information between different sensor 'styles' (marker patterns, illuminations) without requiring new ground truth force data for the target sensor.
> - Material hardness differences significantly impact force-depth relationships, necessitating a compensation mechanism to achieve robust and accurate force transfer across sensors with varying elastomer properties.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robots capable of dexterous manipulation and safe human-robot interaction are becoming increasingly vital across various industries. Tactile sensing is indispensable for these capabilities, allowing robots to perceive and interact with their environment in a nuanced way, akin to human touch. However, replicating human-like touch in robotics presents a fundamental challenge.

The current landscape of tactile robotics is characterized by the development of diverse bio-inspired tactile sensors, employing various sensing principles (piezoresistive, capacitive, magnetic, optical) and structural designs (e.g., GelSight, uSkin, TacTip). While this diversity offers unique advantages for different tasks (e.g., high-resolution imagery for object recognition, multi-axis force sensitivity for manipulation), it also creates significant "domain gaps." These gaps hinder the transfer of learned tactile experiences between different sensors, necessitating laborious and costly data collection and model retraining for each new sensor deployment. This issue is exacerbated in multi-fingered robot hands, where each finger might have multiple sensors, and by the inevitable degradation of soft sensor elastomers, requiring frequent replacement and recalibration.

Previous attempts to address this challenge have included:
- **Traditional transfer learning:** Requires labeled data from new sensors for fine-tuning.
- **Representation learning:** Primarily focuses on vision-based sensors, aligning feature spaces, but often still needs large-scale datasets and task-specific decoders.
- **Direct signal transformation:** Falls short on one-to-one translation and generalizability, often neglecting fundamental material differences.

This paper directly addresses these limitations by drawing inspiration from the human somatosensory cortex and its unified tactile memory system. Humans can flexibly reuse and transfer touch information across different skin regions. GenForce aims to mimic this biological capability, positioning itself as a foundational advancement that enables "transferable force sensing" across heterogeneous tactile sensors. By offering a solution that significantly reduces the need for repetitive data collection and recalibration, GenForce presents a scalable paradigm for cross-sensor robotic tactile learning, pushing the field closer to adaptable, tactile memory-driven manipulation in unstructured environments. The work distinguishes itself by being the "first framework" to achieve this generalizability across *diverse* tactile sensors, including those with different sensing principles and material properties, operating at the tactile-signal level rather than just feature spaces.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a general framework that enables transferable force sensing across diverse tactile sensors in robotic systems, fundamentally addressing the challenge of data collection and model retraining for each new sensor.

The key motivations driving this research are:
- **Replicating Human Tactile Intelligence:** Humans achieve stable and dexterous object manipulation by coordinating grasp forces across multiple fingers and palms, facilitated by a unified tactile memory system. This biological capability, which allows encoding and storing tactile experiences for flexible reuse and transfer, serves as a core inspiration.
- **Overcoming Domain Gaps:** The inherent diversity in sensing principles, structural designs, and material properties among existing tactile sensors creates significant "domain gaps." These gaps necessitate exhaustive and repetitive force data collection for each sensor, leading to high costs, time consumption, and practical barriers, especially for large-scale deployments on multi-fingered robot hands.
- **Addressing Sensor Degradation:** Soft elastomers in tactile sensors are prone to wear and tear, requiring frequent replacement and recalibration. Current practices demand repeated data collection and retraining, which is unsustainable.
- **Enabling Scalable Deployment:** The current limitations hinder the scalable deployment of tactile sensing in real-world robotic applications. A framework that allows sensors to learn from each other and reuse tactile experience would greatly facilitate widespread adoption.
- **Enhancing Dexterity and Adaptability:** Transferable tactile sensing is essential for robots to perform complex in-hand object manipulation tasks in unstructured environments, improving their dexterity and adaptability.

In essence, the authors aim to shift the paradigm from sensor-specific, costly calibration to a cross-sensor learning approach, making advanced tactile sensing more accessible and practical for real-world robotic applications.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The GenForce framework addresses the challenge of transferable force sensing through a three-step approach inspired by human tactile memory, along with a crucial material compensation method.

#### 3.1. Unified Marker Representation

- **Concept:** Mimicking how human mechanoreceptors translate diverse stimuli into a unified sensory encoding, GenForce unifies tactile signals from various sensors into a "marker representation."
- **Implementation:** This involves converting raw tactile signals (e.g., high-resolution images from vision-based sensors like GelSight/TacTip or multichannel electrical signals from sensor arrays like uSkin) into standardized marker-based images. For vision-based sensors, marker segmentation extracts marker deformation. For electronic sensor arrays, a novel signal-to-marker pipeline converts multichannel raw signals into marker displacement and diameter changes, creating a visual representation. This ensures a consistent input representation regardless of the sensor's underlying sensing principle or physical configuration.

#### 3.2. Marker-to-Marker Translation (M2M)

- **Concept:** Once tactile signals are unified into marker representations, the core challenge becomes translating these representations between different sensor "styles" (e.g., different marker patterns, densities, or illuminations).
- **Implementation:** An image-conditioned diffusion model is employed for M2M translation. This model takes a deformed marker image from a "source" (calibrated) sensor as input and a *reference image* (non-contact state) from a "target" (uncalibrated) sensor as a conditional input. It then generates a deformed marker image that preserves the deformation information from the source but adopts the visual style (marker pattern, density, illumination) of the target sensor. This allows force prediction models trained on one sensor's data to be effectively transferred to another. The model uses a VAE architecture for marker encoding/decoding and a UNet-based conditional diffusion model with a DDPM Scheduler, optimized with LoRA for efficient fine-tuning.

#### 3.3. Spatiotemporal Force Prediction

- **Concept:** With generated, transferable marker images, force prediction models can be trained without direct calibration of the target sensor. To enhance accuracy, the models leverage not just static images but sequential contact information.
- **Implementation:** A spatiotemporal force prediction model is used, incorporating:
 - A **marker feature encoder** (from RAFT) to extract spatial representations from sequential tactile images.
 - A **ConvGRU** module to capture temporal dependencies while preserving spatial information.
 - A **ResNet** module for hierarchical feature processing.
 - A **multilayer perceptron regression head** for final three-axis force predictions.
 - The model is trained in two stages: pre-training with transferred data, followed by fine-tuning.

#### 3.4. Material Hardness Compensation

- **Motivation:** Recognizing that differences in elastomer hardness significantly impact force-depth relationships and introduce errors during transfer, a compensation method is integrated.
- **Implementation:** The method uses "material priors"—fitted elastic modulus profiles (force-normalized depth curves) for different skin hardnesses. These priors are used to correct force labels *before* training the force prediction model, intuitively increasing force labels when transferring from soft to hard skin and decreasing them in the opposite direction. This mitigates errors caused by varying material properties.

#### 3.5. Experimental Setup and Evaluation

- **Simulation:** A simulation pipeline (Tacchi) was used to generate extensive deformed marker images for 12 distinct marker patterns and 18 indenters, creating 132 sensor combinations to pre-train and test M2M generalizability. Quantitative metrics like Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) and Kernel Inception Distance (KID), along with t-SNE visualizations, assessed image similarity and feature space alignment.
- **Real-world Data Collection:**
 - **Homogeneous Sensors:** Five custom-fabricated GelSight sensors with varying illuminations and marker patterns were used. ~180,000 force-image pairs per sensor were collected using a UR5e robot arm, an ATI Nano17 F/T sensor, and 18 indenters.
 - **Material Compensation:** Seven GelSight sensors with elastomers of varying hardness (base-to-activator ratios from 6 to 18) were fabricated and tested.
 - **Heterogeneous Sensors:** Three distinct sensor types—GelSight (vision-based), uSkin (magnetic, 3-axis, and z-axis only), and TacTip (vision-based, curved surface)—were evaluated for cross-modality transfer.
- **Robot Manipulation Tasks:** Demonstrated applicability in real-world scenarios:
 - **Daily Object Grasping:** GelSight and uSkin sensors on a Franka FR3 robot arm grasping nine unseen objects with force control.
 - **Slip Detection and Avoidance:** TacTip and uSkin sensors on a Franka FR3 robot arm, detecting and avoiding slip by adjusting gripper force.
- **Metrics:** Mean Absolute Error (MAE) and coefficient of determination (R²) were used to quantify force prediction accuracy.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several significant findings, demonstrating the efficacy and generalizability of the GenForce framework across various scenarios.

#### 4.1. Arbitrary Marker-to-Marker Translation (M2M)

- **Simulation Success:** In simulations, M2M translation achieved remarkable results. Before M2M, FID values were over 400 and KID values over 0.75 across 132 sensor combinations, indicating vast visual differences. After M2M, FID dropped to ~4 and KID to ~0.01, representing approximately 100-fold decreases and visually indistinguishable generated images from target images. t-SNE visualizations confirmed strong alignment in feature space, proving the model's ability to transfer marker patterns and deformations effectively.

#### 4.2. Learning Across Homogeneous Tactile Sensors

- **Image Similarity:** For real-world homogeneous GelSight sensors with varying marker patterns and illuminations, M2M translation similarly improved image similarity, with FID and KID values dropping by over 98%, confirming generalizability to real-world variations.
- **Force Prediction Improvement:** The "source-only" method (direct application of a model trained on one sensor to another) showed large errors (max normal force error > 4.8 N, shear force errors avg > 0.28 N) and poor R² values. GenForce significantly reduced these errors. Maximum normal force error decreased to < 1 N (e.g., from 4.8 N to 0.96 N for C-II_D-I), and minimum shear force errors dropped to < 0.06 N. R² values consistently improved to average above 0.8 across all combinations, demonstrating high accuracy in real-time force prediction.

#### 4.3. Skin Hardness Effect and Compensation

- **Hardness Impact:** Experiments with seven silicone elastomers of varying hardness (ratio-6 to ratio-18) revealed that material hardness differences significantly impact force prediction accuracy. Normal-force errors generally increased with larger hardness gaps (average 1.41 N for hard-to-soft, 1.03 N for soft-to-hard). Shear errors were more stable but still present.
- **Compensation Effectiveness:** The proposed material compensation method, which uses material priors to adjust force labels, substantially reduced these errors. Average normal-force error dropped by 30% (to 0.99 N) in the hard-to-soft group and 16% (to 0.87 N) in the soft-to-hard group. Shear errors also reduced significantly (e.g., 44% reduction on x-axis in hard-to-soft). The method improved R² values across axes and was effective in 95% of hard-to-soft and 57% of soft-to-hard combinations, showcasing its importance for real-world robustness.

#### 4.4. Learning Across Heterogeneous Tactile Sensors

- **Unified Representation:** The signal-to-marker pipeline successfully unified diverse tactile signals from GelSight, uSkin (3-axis and z-axis), and TacTip into a common marker representation.
- **M2M Generalization:** M2M translation generalized well to heterogeneous sensors, with improved FID and KID values and aligned t-SNE feature spaces, demonstrating the ability to translate between vastly different sensing modalities and form factors.
- **Robust Force Prediction:** Before compensation, transferring between heterogeneous sensors with large hardness and marker density gaps (e.g., uSkin_TacTip) resulted in negative R² values and MAEs up to 7.76 N. After applying GenForce *with material compensation*, all R² values became positive and significantly improved. Average MAE over all six combinations decreased to < 0.92 N for normal force and < 0.3 N for shear forces. Notably, uSkin_TacTip saw a 93% improvement in normal force MAE (from 7.76 N to 0.52 N). Real-time tests with dynamic contact events and daily objects showed fast, accurate responses comparable to a commercial F/T sensor.

#### 4.5. Transferable Force Sensing and Control in Robot Manipulation

- **Grasping with Force Control:** A Franka FR3 robot arm equipped with a GelSight and a uSkin sensor successfully grasped nine diverse, unseen daily objects (including fragile items like potato chips and grapes) using force control models transferred from a third GelSight sensor. This demonstrated accurate, damage-free grasping.
- **Slip Detection and Avoidance:** With a TacTip and a uSkin sensor, the robot successfully performed grasping, lifting, slip detection (via shear force changes), and slip avoidance (by narrowing gripper width) for challenging objects. This confirmed the practical applicability and robustness of GenForce in complex manipulation tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The GenForce framework represents a significant advancement in tactile robotics, addressing a long-standing bottleneck in the deployment and scalability of tactile sensing systems. Its potential impact is multifaceted and far-reaching:

- **Cost-Efficiency and Accessibility:** By alleviating the need for expensive force/torque sensors and extensive, sensor-specific data collection, GenForce dramatically reduces the cost and time associated with equipping new robots or replacing worn-out sensors. This makes advanced tactile sensing more accessible to researchers and industries, democratizing its use.
- **Scalability for Robotic Systems:** The "foundational and scalable" nature of GenForce, where M2M translation requires minimal "location-paired" data and can operate with a single trained model across numerous sensor combinations, facilitates the deployment of large-scale tactile sensor networks on complex robotic hands. As sensor networks grow, the model's efficiency becomes even more pronounced.
- **Generalizability Across Sensor Diversity:** The ability to transfer force sensing across both homogeneous sensors (with varying configurations) and highly heterogeneous sensors (with distinct sensing principles, structural designs, and material properties) is a groundbreaking achievement. This level of generalization was previously unmet and allows for unprecedented flexibility in designing and integrating tactile perception into robots.
- **Robustness and Reliability:** Despite relying on unsupervised learning for translation, GenForce achieves high accuracy and reliability in force prediction, with errors tightly centered around zero within practical operating ranges. The successful integration of material compensation further enhances its robustness against real-world variations. Its validation against a commercial F/T sensor in dynamic scenarios underscores its practical utility.
- **Enhanced Robot Dexterity and Autonomy:** The successful demonstration of GenForce in real-world robot grasping and slip avoidance tasks highlights its immediate applicability for improving robot dexterity, stability, and adaptability in unstructured environments. This paves the way for robots capable of more human-like, nuanced manipulation.
- **Foundation for Tactile AI:** By establishing a unified framework for transferable sensing, GenForce lays the groundwork for future advancements in embodied AI. It encourages the creation of foundational tactile databases, analogous to ImageNet for vision, where rich, reusable tactile experiences can be shared globally, further accelerating research and development.
- **Broader Applications:** Beyond force sensing, the core contributions of GenForce—the unified marker representation, M2M translation, and spatiotemporal regression—are highly transferable. The authors suggest that by replacing the output layer, the framework could be extended to other critical tactile tasks such as in-hand pose estimation, 3D reconstruction, and multimodal perception, unlocking new capabilities for dexterous manipulation.

While the model has some minor limitations (flicker effect with certain GelSight configurations, sensitivity to marker size in heterogeneous translation, initial zero-shift, and magnetic interference with uSkin), these are acknowledged and discussed, often with proposed mitigation strategies. The current requirement for at least one fully calibrated sensor with material priors is also a point for future work, with the ultimate goal being a foundational tactile database requiring no new data collection.

In conclusion, GenForce presents a transformative paradigm for robotic tactile sensing, addressing critical limitations in cost, scalability, and generalizability. Its impact will likely accelerate the development of more intelligent, adaptable, and autonomous robots capable of complex interactions with the physical world.
