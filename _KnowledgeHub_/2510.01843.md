# Like Playing a Video Game: Spatial-Temporal Optimization of Foot Trajectories for Controlled Football Kicking in Bipedal Robots

> [!summary] Summary
> An integrated system developed by The University of Hong Kong's Adaptive Robotic Controls Lab enables bipedal robots to execute controlled football kicks. Employing a Spatial-Temporal Optimization of Foot Trajectory (STOFT) Planner, this system achieved nearly 100% success rates across various kicking angles in both simulation and hardware, demonstrating precise ball trajectory control and robot stability superior to baseline approaches.

## Problem

- Humanoid robots struggle with precise ball trajectory control during aggressive kicks while maintaining dynamic stability, a long-standing challenge in robot soccer.
- Existing methods like predefined motions lack adaptability, and reinforcement learning approaches often suffer from generalization issues and 'black box' interpretability.
- Model Predictive Control (MPC) in legged robots typically oversimplifies swing leg trajectory planning, limiting dynamic interaction crucial for complex tasks like kicking.

## Method

- An integrated control framework featuring a novel Spatial-Temporal Optimization of Foot Trajectory (STOFT) Planner generates dynamically feasible and collision-free foot trajectories.
- The STOFT Planner uses a MINCO polynomial trajectory framework to simultaneously optimize both the shape and timing of the foot's movement, incorporating dynamic, kinematic, and self-collision constraints.
- The system combines vision-based aiming, an adaptive gait schedule with a double support phase, and ==Model Predictive Control== for balance, enabling the robot to autonomously plan and execute target-based kicks.

## Results

- The STOFT planner achieved a nearly **100%** success rate in simulated kicking tasks across a wide range of angles (-90° to 90°), consistently outperforming a traditional 'straightforward forward kick' baseline, particularly for off-center shots.
- Hardware experiments on a PEARL bipedal robot validated the system's ability to closely follow desired foot trajectories, maintain balance through adaptive gait extensions during kicks, and restore stability after ball impact.
- The spatial-temporal trajectory planner demonstrated high computational efficiency, completing optimization within 1 millisecond, making it suitable for real-time operation in dynamic robotic applications.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Simultaneously optimizing both the spatial path and temporal timing of the swing foot's trajectory (spatial-temporal planning) is critical for achieving precise and powerful kicking motions, mimicking human-like backswing.
> - An integrated control architecture, combining advanced trajectory planning with MPC for balance and an adaptive gait schedule, is essential for robustly executing dynamic and precise interactions like football kicks on bipedal robots.
> - Explicitly modeling and optimizing for dynamic feasibility, kinematic constraints, and self-collision avoidance within the trajectory planner enables efficient (sub-1ms) and reliable generation of complex, safe motions for real-time applications.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of humanoid robot soccer presents a complex challenge, requiring robots to maintain dynamic stability while executing aggressive maneuvers and achieving precise control over ball trajectories. This research operates within the context of advancing humanoid robot capabilities, specifically addressing the long-standing difficulties in precise passing and shooting in robot football.

Previous approaches in robot soccer, particularly in competitions like RoboCup, frequently relied on predefined motions or simple trajectory interpolations for kicking. These methods primarily focused on the execution of a kick rather than fine-grained control over the ball's final placement. While effective for basic kicking tasks, they lacked the adaptability and precision required for strategic gameplay.

In recent years, reinforcement learning (RL) has emerged as an alternative, demonstrating capabilities such as single-leg passing in quadruped robots and autonomous dribbling and shooting in small-scale humanoids. However, RL-based methods often exhibit limitations in generalization, as policies are typically tailored to specific robot designs and environmental conditions. Furthermore, the "black box" nature of RL policies can impede interpretability and direct control over decision-making processes, which is a significant drawback for tasks requiring high precision and safety.

Model Predictive Control (MPC) has gained prominence in legged robot locomotion, achieving success in quadrupedal and bipedal systems by optimizing ground reaction forces and torques over a finite horizon. Despite these advancements, existing MPC applications in legged robots have often simplified the swing leg trajectory planning, commonly using basic Bézier curves or treating foot placement as a terminal constraint. This simplification limits the swing leg's ability to interact dynamically with the environment, particularly for complex tasks like kicking where the foot's entire trajectory, velocity, and acceleration at impact are critical.

Inspired by human kicking mechanics, which involve a backswing to generate force and precision, the authors explore the application of spatial-temporal trajectory planning. This approach has previously demonstrated success in drone applications, enabling coordinated flight, obstacle avoidance, and high-speed navigation by simultaneously optimizing both the path shape and its timing. By adapting this concept to bipedal robots, this research aims to bridge the gap in humanoid robot systems that can autonomously plan and execute precise shots based on human-specified targets, moving beyond the limitations of pre-defined motions, robot-specific RL policies, and simplified MPC swing leg control. The work seeks to introduce a method that enables robust, precise, and dynamically feasible kicking motions, directly contributing to more capable and adaptable humanoid robots for complex physical interactions.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a comprehensive system that enables bipedal humanoid robots to perform controlled and precise football kicking, specifically by autonomously planning and executing shots based on human-specified targets. This overarching goal is driven by several key motivations and specific objectives:

1. **Address Limitations of Existing Kicking Methods:** Current robotic football approaches, whether based on predefined motions or reinforcement learning, face significant limitations in achieving precise ball trajectory control while maintaining system stability during aggressive kicking. Predefined motions lack adaptability for varying target conditions, while RL methods often struggle with generalization to different robots or real-world scenarios, and their "black box" nature hinders interpretability. The research aims to overcome these limitations by proposing a more flexible and transparent planning approach.

2. **Enhance Swing Leg Control in MPC:** While Model Predictive Control (MPC) has proven effective for overall legged locomotion, its application to swing leg control in prior studies has often been oversimplified. Existing MPC implementations frequently rely on basic trajectory interpolation methods (e.g., Bézier curves) for the swing leg, primarily ensuring foot placement at a touchdown time rather than precisely tracking the entire trajectory. This simplification restricts the foot's ability to engage in complex environmental interactions like ball kicking, where the full spatial and temporal characteristics of the foot's movement are crucial. A core motivation is to develop a sophisticated swing leg control strategy that allows for detailed trajectory tracking.

3. **Mimic Human Kicking Mechanics:** Human kicking behavior involves natural adjustments based on target distance, often featuring a "backswing" motion for longer or more powerful shots. This backswing extends the acceleration distance and duration, allowing the foot to achieve a higher velocity at impact and precisely control the ball's motion. The research is motivated by replicating this sophisticated human-like movement, recognizing that simultaneously optimizing trajectory shape and timing (spatial-temporal planning) is fundamental to achieving such dynamics.

4. **Integrate a Comprehensive System Architecture:** Achieving precise shooting requires the coordination of multiple robotic functionalities. The research aims to integrate various components, including vision-based ball detection and localization, visual aiming, robot state estimation, MPC-based balance control, adaptive gait generation, and the novel foot trajectory planning, into a cohesive and functional system. This integration is crucial for transitioning from theoretical planning to practical, real-world execution.

5. **Develop an Efficient and Reliable Trajectory Planner:** A critical objective is to design a foot trajectory planner that can generate dynamically feasible and collision-free trajectories quickly. The requirement for real-time operation in dynamic environments like robot soccer necessitates a planner that can complete computations in a very short timeframe (e.g., under 1 ms). This efficiency, coupled with reliability in task completion, is essential for competitive performance.

6. **Demonstrate Practical Viability on Hardware:** Ultimately, the motivation extends to validating the proposed methods on a physical humanoid robot. This involves conducting hardware experiments to confirm the system's ability to perform precise shooting, maintain stability, and generate the planned human-like motions in a real-world setting.

By addressing these objectives, the research seeks to significantly advance the capabilities of humanoid robots in complex, dynamic interaction tasks, particularly within the domain of sports robotics.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research presents an integrated control framework designed for dynamic and precise football kicking in bipedal robots, centered around a novel Spatial-Temporal Optimization of Foot Trajectory (STOFT) Planner. The overall system operates in a closed loop, taking operator commands and current robot states to generate optimal control inputs.

**A. Overall System Framework:**
The control framework (Figure 2) operates at each sampling interval.
1. **Operator Commands:** The system receives desired robot velocity (v_des, ω_des) and kicking foot target (p_des_kick, v_des_kick) from an operator.
2. **State Estimation:** A state estimator calculates the current robot's base states (position, velocity, orientation, angular velocity) and foot states (position, velocity).
3. **Gait Generator:** Based on commands and states, an adaptive gait schedule is produced.
4. **Model Predictive Control (MPC):** The MPC module solves an Optimal Control Problem (OCP) using current states, commands, and gait schedule to compute optimal Ground Reaction Forces (GRFs) and Ground Reaction Torques (GRTs) for maintaining balance.
5. **Foot Planner (STOFT):** Simultaneously, the STOFT planner generates a reference trajectory for either kicking or regular walking.
6. **Joint Torque Calculation:** Finally, desired joint torques are computed based on the desired reference trajectory, GRFs, and GRTs for precise actuation.

**B. Model Predictive Control (MPC):**
The robot's body is modeled as a Single Rigid Body (SRB), neglecting leg masses due to their relative lightness. Assuming small roll and pitch angles, the SRB dynamics are expressed for CoM position, velocity, and angular dynamics (Eq. 1). The net GRFs and GRTs are sum of individual contact forces and torques (Eq. 2). The system state vector includes Euler angles, CoM position, angular velocity, and CoM velocity (Eq. 3). The system input comprises GRFs and GRTs from both feet (Eq. 4). Constraints include friction cone, force limits, and torque bounds for each contact point (Eq. 5). The MPC formulates an OCP (Eq. 6) to minimize a cost function (tracking reference states and minimizing control effort) over a finite horizon, subject to discretized SRB dynamics and inequality constraints.

**C. Soccer Dynamics and Motion Prediction:**
The soccer ball is approximated as a point mass, neglecting rotational effects. When airborne, its motion follows free-fall dynamics. For foot-ball collision dynamics, an impulse-based model is used, with the normal direction governed by a classical coefficient of restitution model (Eq. 7) to determine the desired foot velocity at impact.

**D. Swing Foot Trajectory Planning:**
The swing foot trajectory planning aims for smooth movement from an initial position (Point A) to an intermediate point (Point B) and then to a target foothold (Point C). The foot state (position, velocity, acceleration) is defined (Eq. 8). For kicking, the intermediate point (s_kick) is set by an RViz-based aiming tool; for walking, it's a symmetric midpoint (s_walk). The target foothold (s_hold) at Point C is determined by a heuristic foot placement strategy (Eq. 9). The STOFT planner, detailed below, generates the feasible foot trajectory (ϕ(t)) by solving a spatio-temporal optimization problem (Eq. 10), adaptively optimizing the swing phase duration (T_swing). Foot orientation is interpolated using rotation matrices (Eq. 11).

**E. Adaptive Gait Schedule:**
To enhance stability, especially during dynamic actions, a double support (stance-hold) phase is introduced between stance and swing. A Finite State Machine (FSM) is designed for each leg with states SWING, STANCE, and STANCE_HOLD, and defined transition rules (Algorithm 1). This allows for a regular gait timetable during normal walking and an adaptive schedule for extended swing phases required during kicking (Figure 4).

**F. Torque Calculation:**
For the swing leg, a Cartesian PD controller computes the desired force and torque (Eq. 12). For the stance leg, the desired wrench from MPC (Eq. 6) is mapped to joint torques using the Jacobian matrix (Eq. 13).

**G. Spatial-Temporal Trajectory Optimization (STOFT Planner):**
The STOFT Planner utilizes the MINCO (MINimum COntrol) polynomial trajectory framework for spatial-temporal decoupled optimization, ensuring dynamic feasibility and avoiding self-collision.
1. **Trajectory Definition:** A 2-segment, 3-dimensional 5th-order polynomial defines the trajectory (Eq. 14), parameterized by segment durations (T) and intermediate waypoints (q) using a space-time deformation (Eq. 15).
2. **Problem Formulation:** The optimization problem (Eq. 16) minimizes a cost function that includes jerk (ϕ^(3)) and a time penalty (ρT_l), subject to several constraints:
 - **Duration Constraints:** Each segment duration T_l > 0 (Eq. 17a).
 - **Boundary Conditions:** Initial, intermediate, and final position and velocity constraints for the foot (Eq. 17b-g).
 - **Generalized Time Integral Penalty (I\*):** This term (Eq. 18, 20) incorporates various soft constraints:
 - **Time Penalty:** A cubic penalty function discourages segment durations from exceeding a maximum threshold (T_max) (Eq. 21).
 - **Dynamical Feasibility Constraints:** Penalizes if foot velocity or acceleration exceeds defined maximum limits (v_max, a_max) (Eq. 22).
 - **Self-Collision Avoidance:** Approximates the foot as a sphere and uses an Euclidean Signed Distance Field (ESDF) to represent distances to obstacles (other robot parts). A penalty is applied if the foot's distance to an obstacle falls below its radius (Eq. 23, 24).
 - **Bipedal Robot Kinematic Constraints:** Ensures the foot position remains within a spherical workspace centered at the robot's hip, respecting leg length limits (Eq. 25).
3. **Optimization:** The complete cost function and its gradients (Eq. 19) are used to solve the optimization problem with the L-BFGS algorithm, implemented via an open-source MINCO library.

**H. Experimental Setup:**
Experiments were conducted in both simulation (MuJoCo physics engine) and on a physical PEARL bipedal robot. Dynamic constraints were set (v_max=5 m/s, a_max=15 m/s², T_max=1.2s), and a restitution coefficient (α=0.65) was calibrated. A 0.9m x 0.6m x 0.6m soccer goal and regulation football were used for accuracy tests.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental validation of the STOFT planner and integrated system was conducted through both simulation and hardware experiments, assessing toe kicks, inside foot kicks, shooting accuracy, and gait schedule.

**A. Toe Kick Experiments:**
In a simulated toe kick, the robot successfully struck a ball positioned 0.3 meters ahead with a target velocity of 3.5 m/s. The data showed that upon impact, the robot's foot began to decelerate, although it continued a slight forward motion. The kick induced a transient disturbance in the robot's body velocity, but the control system quickly restored stability (Figure 5, left side, Part b).

**B. Inside Foot Kick Experiments:**
For scenarios requiring a larger kicking angle, the robot executed an inside foot kick in simulation, targeting a ball positioned to its front-left at 2.4 m/s. The experimental data indicated that at the moment of ball impact, a noticeable yaw deviation occurred in the foot's orientation. However, the foot rapidly recovered and resumed tracking its desired trajectory (Figure 5, right side, Part b). This approach highlighted that minimal foot orientation adjustment was required for effective side kicks, primarily relying on leg swing.

**C. Accuracy Test for STOFT:**
A comprehensive accuracy test involved kicking tasks with varying angles from -90° to 90°, with the ball placed 3 meters from the robot and aimed at a soccer goal. Each scenario was tested ten times, and the success rate (SR) was recorded.
The STOFT planner's performance was compared to a baseline strategy, which was described as a "straightforward forward kick" typically employed by competitive RoboCup teams. The baseline method was effective when the ball was directly in front of the robot but showed a significant drop in success rate when the ball was off-center.
In contrast, the proposed STOFT planner demonstrated a higher and more consistent scoring success rate (Figure 6).
- For straight-ahead shots (angles between -30° and 30°), the "toe kick" method resulted in a nearly 100% success rate, indicating precise control over foot position and impact speed.
- For side shots (angles from 60° to 90°, and from -90° to -60° using the left foot), the "inside foot kick" strategy also achieved a 100% success rate. This was attributed to the ability to accurately score by simply swinging the leg sideways with minimal foot orientation adjustment.
Overall, the STOFT planner consistently outperformed the baseline method across the tested angular range, achieving perfect success rates for a wide range of angles and significantly higher rates for challenging off-center kicks.

**D. Foot Trajectory and Gait Schedule (Hardware Experiments):**
Hardware experiments performed on the PEARL biped robot further validated the system.
- **Foot Trajectory:** Figure 7(a) illustrates the foot-end trajectory during a kicking motion designed to achieve 3 m/s velocity at the ball impact point (0.2 m ahead of the robot). The actual trajectory closely followed the target, and the ball collision induced only limited disturbance to the foot-end trajectory.
- **Gait Schedule:** Figure 7(b) displays the corresponding gait schedule. During regular walking, the robot maintained a periodic gait pattern. Crucially, during a left leg kicking motion (initiated at t = 51.2 s, lasting 0.59 s), the right stance phase was adaptively extended. This adaptive gait accommodation enabled the robot to maintain balance and stability during the dynamic demands of the kick.
- **Computational Efficiency:** The planner completed a single computation for trajectory optimization in under 1 ms, demonstrating its efficiency for real-time applications.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents an advancement in the field of bipedal robot control, particularly in complex dynamic interactions such as football kicking. The significance and potential impact of this work can be understood through several dimensions:

1. **Advancement in Humanoid Robot Soccer:** The work directly addresses a long-standing challenge in humanoid robot soccer: achieving precise ball trajectory control while maintaining robot stability during aggressive kicking motions. By enabling robust and accurate target-based shooting across a wide range of angles, the system moves beyond previous limitations of predefined motions or context-specific reinforcement learning approaches. This could significantly enhance the strategic capabilities and overall performance of humanoid robots in competitive environments like RoboCup.

2. **Novel Application of Spatial-Temporal Trajectory Planning:** A key contribution is the innovative adaptation of spatial-temporal trajectory planning, previously successful in drone applications, to the complex dynamics of bipedal robotic systems. This approach allows for the simultaneous optimization of both the shape and timing of the foot's trajectory, moving beyond simplified swing leg control methods common in existing Model Predictive Control (MPC) frameworks for legged robots. This methodology could be transferable to other bipedal manipulation or interaction tasks requiring high precision and dynamic coordination.

3. **Mimicking Human Biomechanics:** The proposed STOFT Planner is capable of generating "human-like backswing motions." This biomimetic quality is significant because the backswing in human kicking naturally extends acceleration distance and duration, enabling higher impact velocities and greater control over ball trajectory. Replicating such nuanced human-like movements in robots contributes to more versatile and effective robot behaviors.

4. **Robust and Efficient Real-time Control:** The integrated system demonstrates reliability with nearly 100% task completion accuracy within the tested range of kicking angles. Furthermore, the STOFT planner achieves rapid trajectory planning times (under 1 ms), which is critical for real-time responsiveness in dynamic environments. The adaptive gait schedule also contributes to maintaining robot stability during the aggressive demands of kicking, handling transient disturbances effectively.

5. **Addressing Generalization and Interpretability:** Unlike some reinforcement learning approaches that can be robot-specific and operate as "black boxes," the model-based optimization approach of the STOFT Planner offers greater interpretability and potential for generalization. The explicit formulation of dynamic, kinematic, and collision constraints enhances transparency and facilitates debugging or adaptation to new scenarios.

6. **Broader Impact on Bipedal Robot Manipulation:** The principles and methodologies developed for controlled football kicking could extend beyond sports. Any bipedal robot application requiring precise end-effector control, dynamic interaction with objects, or navigation in cluttered environments (e.g., in manufacturing, assistive robotics, or hazardous environments) could benefit from the advanced spatial-temporal trajectory planning and integrated control framework presented. This research contributes foundational elements for developing more dexterous and capable humanoid robots for a variety of complex real-world tasks.
