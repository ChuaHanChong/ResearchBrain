# Learning Process Rewards via Success Visitation Matching for Efficient RL

> [!summary] Summary
> UC Berkeley researchers developed Success Visitation Matching (SVM) process rewards, an automated method that converts sparse outcome rewards into dense, step-level feedback for reinforcement learning. This approach utilizes a discriminator to differentiate between successful and unsuccessful state-action visitations, accelerating learning in robotic control tasks while provably maintaining the optimal policy. Experiments showed SVM enabled over 80-90% success rates in simulations and real-world robot tasks, often requiring approximately 2x fewer environment steps for convergence compared to sparse reward baselines.

## Problem

- Reinforcement learning often faces sparse reward functions, where agents receive feedback only upon task completion, leading to a difficult credit assignment problem.
- The sparsity of rewards makes learning inefficient, slow, or ineffective, as agents struggle to identify which intermediate actions contribute to eventual success.
- Existing dense reward shaping methods typically rely on extensive domain-specific knowledge, human engineering, or additional supervision, limiting their generalizability and risking alteration of the original task's optimal policy.

## Method

- The paper introduces Success Visitation Matching (SVM) process rewards, which automatically generate dense, step-level feedback from sparse outcome rewards.
- This is achieved by training a discriminator to distinguish between state-action pairs observed in successful episodes (D+D^+D+) and unsuccessful episodes (D−D^-D−) collected online.
- The SVM reward function integrates the original outcome reward with a clipped log ratio of these visitation probabilities, encouraging actions more likely to lead to success and theoretically preserving the optimal policy in deterministic settings.

## Results

- SVM process rewards consistently led to significantly faster and more stable reinforcement learning, achieving over 80-**90%** success rates across various simulated robotic manipulation tasks (LIBERO, RoboCasa) and often requiring **2x** fewer samples for convergence than baselines.
- The method successfully scaled to real-world robotic control, enabling online improvement for tasks like 'Pick and Place' and 'Open Drawer' on a WidowX arm, where training with only sparse outcome rewards failed to learn.
- SVM also improved the efficiency of finetuning large Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models (e.g., π0), reducing the required environment steps by approximately **2x** to reach a **90%** success rate without compromising final performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Dense, informative rewards can be learned dynamically and automatically from sparse outcome signals by matching the visitation patterns of successful trajectories, rather than requiring static expert demonstrations or human-engineered features.
> - Framing the reward as a log ratio of successful to unsuccessful state-action visitation probabilities allows for principled reward shaping that guides learning while ensuring the optimal policy for the original sparse task is preserved.
> - Continuously updating the positive example set (D+D^+D+) with newly observed successful episodes, similar to an online form of inverse reinforcement learning, is critical for enabling efficient and stable policy improvement in sparse reward environments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has demonstrated capabilities across various domains, including robotics and large language model (LLM) reasoning. A common challenge in these applications is the prevalence of sparse reward functions, where an agent receives a significant reward only upon successful task completion, with zero reward otherwise. This sparsity creates a difficult credit assignment problem, as the agent struggles to identify which intermediate actions contribute to the eventual success, leading to slow or ineffective learning.

The broader research landscape for addressing sparse rewards includes several approaches:
- **Reward Shaping:** A historical area of research focused on designing dense reward functions that provide feedback on progress towards a goal. Traditional reward shaping methods often require domain-specific knowledge, human engineering, or additional supervision (e.g., human feedback, distance-to-goal functions, expert demonstrations). This dependency limits their applicability, particularly in complex robotic tasks where such information is expensive or unavailable.
- **Exploration-based Methods:** Techniques like Random Network Distillation (RND) aim to encourage exploration by providing intrinsic rewards based on novelty or prediction error, indirectly mitigating the sparse reward problem.
- **Imitation Learning/Inverse RL:** Methods such as Generative Adversarial Imitation Learning (GAIL) learn a reward function from expert demonstrations, implicitly providing dense feedback by encouraging the agent to mimic expert behavior. However, these typically assume a fixed set of expert demonstrations, which may not always be available or dynamically updated.
- **Vision-Language Model (VLM) based Rewards:** More recent approaches leverage pretrained VLMs to provide dense rewards or success detection, but these rely on additional sources of supervision like large-scale visual data or internet pretraining.

This work addresses the limitations of existing reward shaping methods by proposing a fully automated approach to derive a dense "process reward" directly from a sparse "outcome reward," without requiring human engineering, side knowledge, or additional supervision. It seeks to bridge the gap between the theoretical benefits of dense rewards and the practical challenges of their acquisition, particularly in real-world robotic control settings. By transforming the sparse outcome reward into a dense signal that guides learning while provably preserving the optimal policy, this research offers an alternative to methods that rely on external information or modify the underlying optimal policy. Its connection to adversarial inverse RL, but with dynamic positive examples, positions it as an evolution of imitation learning techniques adapted for sparse reward environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to develop a method for transforming sparse outcome rewards into dense process rewards for reinforcement learning. This transformation aims to achieve significantly faster and more stable RL improvement, particularly in challenging domains like robotic control, without altering the optimal policy of the original task.

The motivation for this objective stems from several key problems associated with sparse outcome rewards:
- **Credit Assignment Problem:** When rewards are received only at the very end of an episode (e.g., +1 for success, 0 otherwise), an agent faces a difficult credit assignment problem. It must discern which sequence of actions, potentially taken much earlier in the episode, contributed to the eventual success. This makes learning inefficient and slow.
- **Slow or Ineffective RL Improvement:** The difficulty in credit assignment frequently leads to slow convergence or outright failure of RL algorithms to learn effective policies, especially in environments where successful outcomes are rare or require precise sequences of actions.
- **Lack of Intermediate Feedback:** Sparse rewards provide no intermediate feedback on whether the agent is making progress towards the task goal. This lack of guidance means the agent must rely heavily on exploration to stumble upon successful states, which is computationally expensive and often impractical in high-dimensional or real-world settings.
- **Limitations of Existing Process Reward Methods:** Prior attempts to create dense process rewards often suffer from significant drawbacks:
 - **Reliance on Side Knowledge:** Many methods require task-specific side information (e.g., human-engineered features, distance functions, or explicit human supervision), making them difficult to generalize and costly to implement across diverse tasks.
 - **Potential for Suboptimal Policies:** Some reward shaping techniques can inadvertently change the optimal policy, leading the agent to optimize for the shaped reward rather than the true task objective.
 - **Fixed Expert Demonstrations:** Imitation learning approaches, while providing dense feedback, typically require a fixed set of expert demonstrations, which may not be available or may become outdated as the agent learns new behaviors.

Therefore, the key motivations are to:
- **Automate Reward Generation:** Develop a fully automated method for generating dense process rewards from sparse outcome rewards, reducing the need for manual engineering or additional data sources.
- **Preserve Optimal Policy:** Ensure that the policy optimized using the derived dense process reward remains optimal for the original sparse outcome reward.
- **Provide Dense, Step-Level Feedback:** Offer continuous guidance throughout an episode, rewarding progress towards success and penalizing actions leading to failure, thereby accelerating learning.
- **Enhance Sample Efficiency:** Improve the sample efficiency and stability of RL algorithms, making them more practical for real-world applications where data collection is expensive.

By addressing these motivations, the research aims to make RL more applicable and efficient in domains characterized by sparse rewards, particularly in robotics where task complexity and real-world constraints often amplify these challenges.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology, termed Success Visitation Matching (SVM) process rewards, transforms a sparse outcome reward into a dense process reward by leveraging a discriminator trained on past successful and unsuccessful episodes. The approach consists of a principled reward formulation, a theoretical guarantee, and a practical implementation for large state spaces, integrated into an iterative RL training loop.

#### 3.1. Core Principle: Visitation Matching

The foundational idea is to reward an agent for visiting state-action pairs that have historically led to success and penalize it for visiting state-action pairs associated with failure. This principle is formalized by comparing the visitation distributions of successful episodes ($D^+$) and unsuccessful episodes ($D^-$) collected from the environment.

#### 3.2. SVM Process Reward Formulation

The SVM process reward, $r_{svm,h}(s, a)$, at step $h$ for state $s$ and action $a$, is defined as:
$r_{svm,h}(s, a) := r_{out}(s) + \lambda \cdot \text{clip}_{\beta} \left( \log \frac{bw_{h}^+(s, a)}{bw_{h}^-(s, a)} \right)$
where:
- $r_{out}(s)$ is the original sparse outcome reward (0 or 1).
- $\lambda$ is a positive scaling factor.
- $\text{clip}_{\beta}(\cdot)$ is a clipping function that limits the magnitude of the log ratio to $\pm \beta$.
- $bw_{h}^+(s, a)$ and $bw_{h}^-(s, a)$ are estimates of the state-action visitation distribution at step $h$ for successful and unsuccessful episodes, respectively. A log ratio of $0/0$ is defined as $-\infty$.

This reward has two components: the original outcome reward and a term that encourages visiting state-actions more likely in successful episodes than unsuccessful ones.

#### 3.3. Theoretical Guarantee

The paper provides Theorem 4.1, which states that in deterministic environments with countable state and action spaces, any policy maximizing the SVM process reward also maximizes the original sparse outcome reward, given count-based visitation estimates. This theorem is critical as it ensures that the proposed dense reward does not alter the optimal policy, maintaining alignment with the true task objective. The proof relies on showing that if a state is "success reachable," an SVM-maximizing policy will take actions that lead to success.

#### 3.4. Connection to KL-Regularized RL

Without the clipping function (i.e., $\beta \to \infty$), the expected SVM reward can be decomposed into the original outcome reward and a sum of KL divergence terms:
$E_{\pi}[\sum_{h=1}^H r_{svm,h}(s_h, a_h)] = J(\pi) + \lambda \sum_{h=1}^H [KL(w_{\pi,h} \parallel bw_{h}^-) - KL(w_{\pi,h} \parallel bw_{h}^+)]$
where $w_{\pi,h}(s, a)$ is the state-action visitation for policy $\pi$. This formulation indicates that SVM encourages policy visitations to have low KL divergence with successful episode visitations (matching them) and high KL divergence with unsuccessful episode visitations (avoiding them). This provides a dense, step-level signal for policy optimization.

#### 3.5. Practical Implementation: Estimating Visitation Ratios with a Discriminator

For large, continuous state spaces, direct count-based visitation estimation is impractical. The methodology addresses this by employing a discriminator (a binary classifier) to estimate the ratio of visitation densities. Given distributions $P$ and $Q$, a discriminator $f^\star$ trained to distinguish between samples from $P$ and $Q$ will satisfy $f^\star(x) = P(x) / (P(x) + Q(x))$, leading to the ratio $f^\star(x) / (1 - f^\star(x)) = P(x) / Q(x)$.
Therefore, the approach trains a discriminator $\hat{f}_h$ at each step $h$ to distinguish between state-action pairs from $D_h^+$ (successful episodes at step $h$) and $D_h^-$ (unsuccessful episodes at step $h$). The log ratio $\log \frac{\hat{f}_h(s,a)}{1 - \hat{f}_h(s,a)}$ is then used in the $r_{svm}$ formula. In practice, a single discriminator can be trained for all timesteps.

#### 3.6. Reinforcement Learning with SVM Process Rewards (Algorithm 1)

The overall RL training procedure alternates between policy improvement and discriminator refinement:
1. **Initial Rollouts (Optional):** If a pretrained policy ($\pi_{pre}$) is available, initial episodes are collected to populate $D^+$ and $D^-$.
2. **Discriminator Initialization:** A discriminator $\hat{f}$ is initialized and trained on the initial successful ($D^+$) and unsuccessful ($D^-$) episodes.
3. **Iterative Loop:**
 - **Episode Collection:** The current policy $\pi_t$ is run for one episode.
 - **Data Update:** The collected episode is added to $D^+$ if successful, otherwise to $D^-$.
 - **Discriminator Update:** The discriminator $\hat{f}$ is updated using the augmented datasets $D^+$ and $D^-$.
 - **Policy Update:** The policy $\pi_t$ is updated to $\pi_{t+1}$ by maximizing the SVM reward $r_{svm,h}(s, a)$ using any standard RL algorithm.

#### 3.7. Connection to Adversarial Inverse RL (AIRL)

SVM shares similarities with AIRL, which learns a reward function from expert demonstrations. Both use a discriminator of the form $\log \frac{\hat{f}(s,a)}{1 - \hat{f}(s,a)}$. The key distinction is that AIRL uses a fixed set of expert demonstrations as positive examples, while SVM dynamically updates $D^+$ with newly observed successful episodes, leveraging the online outcome reward signal. This continuous update of positive examples is identified as crucial for performance.

#### 3.8. Experimental Setup

- **RL Algorithms:** Evaluated with DSRL (Diffusion-based State-space Reinforcement Learning) and Residual RL for finetuning pretrained policies, and RLPD (Reinforcement Learning from Pretrained Policies via Data) for learning from scratch with demonstrations.
- **Environments:** Simulated (LIBERO-90, RoboCasa, Robomimic) and real-world (WidowX 250 6-DoF robot arm) manipulation tasks.
- **Baselines:** Compared against other reward shaping methods (SORS, SASR, RND, GAIL-REWARD, GVL) and direct outcome reward maximization.
- **Discriminator Architecture:** A three-layer MLP taking image representations (from ResNet encoder) and actions as input. In some π0 experiments, a CNN-based discriminator was used.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrate that Success Visitation Matching (SVM) process rewards consistently lead to faster and more stable reinforcement learning (RL) performance across various simulated and real-world robotic control tasks, compared to existing reward shaping methods and direct sparse outcome reward maximization.

#### 4.1. Efficient RL Finetuning in Simulation (LIBERO and RoboCasa)

- **LIBERO-90 (DSRL and RESIDUAL RL):** SVM rewards consistently improved both the final converged success rate and sample efficiency. With DSRL, SVM achieved a higher final success rate and faster convergence. With RESIDUAL RL, SVM required approximately 2x fewer samples to converge or achieved a significantly higher success rate. For both algorithms, SVM policies often reached over 80% success, indicating that the dense reward did not compromise final performance.
- **RoboCasa (RESIDUAL RL):** SVM substantially improved RL finetuning, quickly reaching approximately 90% success on tasks where other methods, including sparse outcome rewards, failed to enable any RL improvement.

#### 4.2. Efficient Real-World RL for Robotic Control Policies (WidowX Robot Arm)

- **Scalability to Real-World:** SVM rewards effectively scaled to real-world robotic tasks (Pick and Place, Open Drawer, Cover Knife with Cloth) using a multi-task diffusion policy pretrained on BridgeData V2.
- **Significant Improvement:** SVM substantially improved finetuning performance, allowing policies to achieve over 80% success with significantly fewer environment steps. On "Pick and Place" and "Open Drawer," SVM enabled online improvement, whereas training solely with outcome rewards failed to learn.

#### 4.3. Finetuning Vision-Language-Action (VLA) Models (π0)

- **Efficiency Gains:** SVM improved the efficiency of finetuning the 3.3B-parameter π0 VLA model. It required approximately 2x fewer environment steps to reach 90% success compared to RL without reward shaping.

#### 4.4. RL from Demonstrations (Robomimic)

- **Comparison with GAIL:** When learning from scratch with a given set of successful demonstrations (Robomimic Can and Square tasks using RLPD), SVM provided substantial gains over sparse outcome reward, enabling the agent to solve the task. Its performance was comparable to GAIL, which directly leverages expert demonstrations. This suggests that in settings with a fixed, successful demonstration set, SVM's dynamic positive examples might converge to a similar reward signal as GAIL.

#### 4.5. Understanding SVM Process Rewards (Ablation Studies)

- **Policy Extraction Approach:** Ablations showed that training an RL policy to maximize the SVM process reward was more effective than alternative policy extraction methods.
 - "$\hat{f}$ maximization" (directly maximizing discriminator score) and "$\hat{f}$ sampling" (filtering actions by discriminator score) performed worse than using SVM as a reward for standard RL.
 - Adding a behavioral cloning term over successful trajectories ("DSRL-BC") to the actor loss also performed less effectively than SVM.
- **Functional Form of Reward:** Testing various monotonic transformations of the discriminator probability $\hat{f}$ (e.g., $\log \hat{f}$, $\log(1/(1-\hat{f}))$, $\hat{f}/(1-\hat{f})$, $\hat{f}$) revealed that $\log(\hat{f}/(1-\hat{f}))$ generally yielded the most consistently effective performance, although all tested forms improved over sparse outcome rewards.
- **Impact of Negative Feedback:** The presence of negative feedback (penalizing visitations in unsuccessful trajectories) was found to be significant. When the proportion of successful trajectories in the negative example set ($D^-$) was increased (parameter $\alpha$), performance decreased. Explicitly penalizing unsuccessful visitations was important for faster improvement.
- **Timestep Conditioning:** Timestep conditioning of the discriminator did not, on average, improve performance.
- **Symmetric Sampling:** Symmetric sampling (balancing positive and negative examples during discriminator training) led to slightly higher aggregate performance, and was found to be essential for effective RL improvement on some tasks, particularly with RESIDUAL RL.
- **Visualizations:** State visitation heatmaps showed that SVM-induced trajectories rapidly concentrated around regions corresponding to successful behaviors, while other approaches led to less focused or unsuccessful trajectories. The learned SVM reward function assigned high values to state-action pairs leading to correct task progress and penalized irrelevant behaviors.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces Success Visitation Matching (SVM) process rewards, a method that addresses the pervasive challenge of sparse rewards in reinforcement learning by automatically generating dense feedback without external supervision or human intervention.

The primary significance of this work lies in its ability to:
- **Accelerate RL Convergence:** By transforming sparse outcome rewards into dense process rewards, SVM significantly speeds up the learning process, requiring fewer environment steps for policies to converge to high success rates. This is a critical factor for the practical deployment of RL, especially in domains like robotics where data collection is costly and time-consuming.
- **Enhance Learning Stability:** The dense feedback provided by SVM contributes to more stable RL training, preventing agents from getting stuck in local optima or failing to learn due to insufficient reward signals.
- **Maintain Policy Optimality:** A key theoretical contribution is the proof that, under certain conditions, a policy optimizing the SVM process reward also optimizes the original sparse outcome reward. This ensures that the accelerated learning does not lead to suboptimal task performance, maintaining alignment with the true objective.
- **Generalizability and Automation:** Unlike many reward shaping methods that require task-specific knowledge, human engineering, or additional supervision, SVM is fully automated and learns from the online interaction data and the sparse outcome signal itself. This increases its generalizability across diverse tasks and reduces the engineering burden.
- **Applicability to Real-World Robotics:** The demonstration of SVM's effectiveness in real-world robotic manipulation tasks highlights its practical utility. By enabling faster and more stable learning on physical robots, this method contributes to closing the sim-to-real gap and advancing autonomous robotic capabilities.
- **Integration with Existing RL Paradigms:** SVM can be integrated with various RL algorithms and finetuning strategies (e.g., DSRL, Residual RL, RLPD, and finetuning of large pre-trained models like VLAs), making it a versatile tool for enhancing existing RL pipelines.

The potential impact of SVM extends to various domains where sparse rewards are common:
- **Robotic Control:** Enabling faster and more reliable training for complex manipulation, locomotion, and assembly tasks on physical robots.
- **LLM Reasoning:** While the current focus is robotics, the paper explicitly mentions the relevance to "RL with verifiable rewards" in LLMs, suggesting its potential to provide dense process rewards for multi-step reasoning tasks, which could improve efficiency and performance in that domain.
- **General RL Applications:** Any RL problem characterized by sparse rewards could benefit from this automated reward shaping technique, potentially broadening the applicability of RL to more complex and real-world scenarios.

**Limitations:**
A primary limitation acknowledged by the authors is that the theoretical guarantee of optimal policy preservation currently assumes a deterministic environment. While empirical results in non-deterministic real-world settings show significant improvements, extending this theoretical understanding to stochastic environments would be a valuable future direction.

In summary, SVM process rewards represent a step towards more efficient and autonomous reinforcement learning by providing a principled and empirically effective method for converting sparse outcome rewards into dense, guiding signals, without sacrificing the optimality of the learned policy.
