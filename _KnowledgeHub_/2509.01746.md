# Fail2Progress: Learning from Real-World Robot Failures with Stein Variational Inference

> [!summary] Summary
> Fail2Progress enables robots to learn from real-world manipulation failures by employing Stein Variational Inference to generate targeted, diverse simulation data. This approach effectively refines skill effect models, substantially increasing success rates across various mobile manipulation tasks in both simulation and physical environments.

## Problem

- Learned skill effect models struggle with "out-of-distribution" failures when deployed in complex, real-world manipulation tasks.
- Collecting sufficient real-world failure data for model refinement is unsafe, inefficient, and costly.
- Existing recovery strategies or general Real2Sim methods often don't provide targeted data to directly address model prediction errors.

## Method

- Introduces **Fail2Progress**, a framework that detects and classifies real-world robot failures, specifically focusing on "incorrect symbolic predictions."
- Formulates the problem of generating new, informative simulation data (D+) for model refinement as a constrained ==variational inference== task.
- Employs ==Stein Variational Inference (SVI)== to efficiently generate diverse and targeted simulation states and actions in parallel, conditioned on observed failures, for subsequent model fine-tuning.

## Results

- Fail2Progress significantly improved task success rates, for example, increasing performance from **11%** to **86%** in the Hierarchical Tabletop Organization task with Points2Plans.
- The approach using Stein Variational Inference consistently outperformed baselines like "Gradient" (**45%** success) and "Sampling" (**53%** success) while maintaining computational efficiency.
- Fine-tuned models demonstrated robust generalization to novel scenarios, including varying object counts and viewpoints, in mobile manipulation tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Small, targeted simulation datasets generated from observed failures are significantly more effective for model refinement than large, non-targeted datasets.
> - Stein Variational Inference (SVI) excels at generating diverse and informative simulated failure scenarios by efficiently exploring high-dimensional state-action spaces.
> - Learning from incorrect symbolic predictions in simulation enhances the skill effect models' ability to generalize to new object configurations and tasks in the real world.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research addresses the challenges of deploying learned skill effect models for long-horizon robot manipulation tasks in real-world, unstructured, and uncertain environments. While skill effect models have demonstrated capabilities in solving complex tasks by sequencing skills, they are susceptible to failures when encountering conditions not represented in their training data distributions. Existing research in robotics has explored failure case reasoning, primarily focusing on detecting failures and implementing recovery strategies. Some approaches attempt to improve symbolic abstractions from observed failures or enable robots to explore real-world environments to refine their models. However, direct real-world exploration for generating failure scenarios can pose safety risks and efficiency challenges in open settings.

A parallel line of research involves Real-to-Sim (Real2Sim) methods, which aim to create simulation environments informed by real-world observations to safely and efficiently generate large-scale datasets for policy learning or parameter optimization. Current Real2Sim techniques often prioritize high-fidelity simulations, which can be computationally intensive and require extensive fine-tuning. This paper diverges by advocating for the generation of *low-fidelity* simulation environments that are specifically targeted to observed real-world failures.

Furthermore, Stein Variational Inference (SVI) has been applied in robotics to approximate high-dimensional, multimodal posterior distributions, particularly for generating diverse and robust plans under uncertainty. This work distinguishes itself by applying SVI not for planning, but for the novel purpose of efficiently generating diverse simulated datasets that are conditioned on observed failures, thereby enabling the continuous improvement of learned skill effect models. The paper positions itself at the intersection of failure reasoning, efficient data generation for model refinement, and the application of advanced probabilistic inference techniques. The specific gap addressed is the lack of an efficient and safe method for robots to learn from real-world failures by generating targeted simulation data to minimize future occurrences of incorrect symbolic predictions in skill effect models.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the inherent limitations of learned skill effect models when deployed in real-world scenarios. Despite being trained on large, diverse simulation datasets, these models are prone to "out-of-distribution" failures—situations that differ significantly from their training experiences. To enable robust and adaptive robotic systems, it is necessary for robots to not only detect and recover from failures but also to actively learn from these failures to minimize their recurrence.

The specific objectives of this work are:
- **To address the problem of efficient data generation for failure learning:** Recognizing that single real-world failure instances are insufficient for effectively refining large neural network models, and that explicit real-world failure generation is unsafe and inefficient, the research aims to develop a method for efficiently generating targeted simulation data.
- **To formulate failure learning as a variational inference problem:** The authors propose casting the problem of generating new, informative datasets (D+) that are similar to observed failures as a probabilistic inference task. This allows for the systematic search for simulator states and actions that can effectively improve the robot's skill effect model.
- **To leverage Stein Variational Inference (SVI) for parallel data generation:** To overcome the computational challenges associated with searching for optimal datasets (such as the intractability of maximizing information gain and the difficulty of solving inverse problems for scene reconstruction), SVI is proposed as an efficient solver capable of generating multiple simulation environments in parallel. This exploits the capabilities of modern GPU-accelerated simulators.
- **To enable fine-tuning of skill effect models:** The generated targeted datasets are intended to be used for fine-tuning pre-trained skill effect models, thereby improving their ability to handle scenarios similar to the observed failures.
- **To classify real-world failures:** The work also defines a two-category failure classification scheme, distinguishing between failures due to "incorrect symbolic predictions" (model errors) and those due to a "Sim2Real gap" (discrepancies between simulation and reality in perception, control, or physics). This paper specifically focuses on learning from the former category.
- **To demonstrate applicability in mobile manipulation tasks:** The approach aims to be applicable to challenging mobile manipulation tasks, including multi-object transport, constrained packing, and tabletop organization, demonstrating its effectiveness in both simulation and real-world settings.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed approach, termed Fail2Progress, integrates real-world failure detection and classification with an efficient simulation data generation pipeline powered by Stein Variational Inference (SVI) for model fine-tuning.

**Skill Effect Models:**
The foundation of Fail2Progress relies on pre-trained skill effect models. These models operate on high-dimensional, partial-view point clouds, requiring a perception pipeline that provides object semantics and segmentation. They define a set of manipulation skill primitives (e.g., push, pick and place) each parameterized by continuous values (a_k). The models predict changes in world state (e.g., object poses or symbolic relations) when a skill is executed. A skill effect model, denoted as Γ, predicts the probability of achieving specific relations given an initial observation, a skill sequence, and its parameters. Planning involves finding a skill sequence and parameters that maximize the probability of achieving goal relations.

**Detecting and Classifying Failures Autonomously:**
When a robot executes a planned skill sequence, it continuously monitors the environment. A failure is detected if the observed symbolic state (ˆR_k) after a skill execution does not match the model's predicted symbolic effect state (R'_k). Upon detecting a failure, the robot records a "failure event" (F), which includes the pre-failure observation (O_F), relations (R_F), the executed skill (ϕ_F), and the actual post-execution relations (ˆR_k).

Failures are classified into two categories:
1. **Incorrect Symbolic Predictions:** The robot reconstructs the pre-failure simulation scene from O_F and executes the same skill in simulation, predicting relational effects (R''_k). If R''_k matches ˆR_k (the real-world observed outcome), the failure is attributed to incorrect symbolic predictions, indicating a flaw in the model's understanding of skill effects.
2. **Sim2Real Gap:** If R''_k does not match ˆR_k, the discrepancy is classified as a Sim2Real gap, potentially due to real-world perception noise, controller mismatch, or inaccurate physical modeling. This work specifically focuses on learning from failures categorized as "incorrect symbolic predictions."

**Generating Targeted Datasets to Learn from Failure:**
To address the inadequacy of single failure instances for model refinement, Fail2Progress aims to generate a new dataset (D+) for fine-tuning. This dataset generation is formulated as a constrained optimization problem. The objective is to maximize the expected information gain (quantified as the KL-divergence between the predictive distributions of the updated model Γ+ and the original model Γ) with respect to the detected failure. The constraint ensures that the generated samples in D+ correspond to states that would yield the same relations and observations as those observed by the robot prior to the failure.
A single sample in D+ consists of an initial simulator state (s+_i), its observation (O+_i), the failed skill (ϕ_F), an action parameter (a+_i), and the subsequent state (s++_i) and observation (O++_i) after skill execution. The skill ϕ_F is fixed, while s+_i and a+_i are the variables to be optimized.

**Approximate Constrained Expected Information Gain:**
To render the problem tractable, two approximations are introduced:
1. The expected information gain objective (KL-divergence) is replaced with maximizing the entropy of the epistemic uncertainty of the current model. This avoids computationally expensive retraining of the skill effect model at each iteration of dataset optimization.
2. The constraint (P(R_F,O_F|S)) is simplified. Instead of an exact inverse problem for matching point clouds to simulator states, the method searches over object poses (S) by segment-wise transforming the original failure observation (O_F), ensuring these transformed point clouds (ξ(S)O_F) still satisfy the failure relations (r_F) when evaluated by the model. This bypasses the need for a full physics simulator or renderer during the optimization phase.

**Generating Datasets via Stein Variational Inference (SVI):**
The approximated constrained optimization is solved in two stages using SVI:
1. **Generating State Samples (S+):** SVI is used to approximate a posterior distribution over simulation states (S+) that match the failure relations (r_F) given the transformed point clouds (ξ(S)O_F) and a uniform prior over feasible states. This forms a variational inference problem where SVI particles are guided by gradients to minimize the KL-divergence.
2. **Generating Action Samples (A+):** With the state samples S+ fixed, generalized Bayesian inference (GBI) is employed to find continuous action parameters (A+) that maximize the entropy (i.e., information gain) of the skill effect model's predictions. SVI is again used to optimize these action particles.

SVGD updates particles using a Stein variational gradient, which combines an attractive force (based on the gradient of the log-posterior) and a repulsive term (based on a kernel function) to prevent particle collapse and ensure diversity. RBF kernels with median heuristic bandwidths and Adam optimizer for step size are employed.

**Real-to-Sim Object Generation:**
After SVI yields optimized state-action pairs ({s+_i, a+_i}), a full simulation dataset (D+) is generated. For each (s+_i), a simulation scene is constructed by fitting object shape priors (e.g., cuboids) to the segmented point clouds from the original failure observation (O_F), using bounding boxes for dimensions and s+_i for object poses. Pre-defined physical parameters are used for object classes. The skill (ϕ_F, a+_i) is then executed in this scene to obtain pre/post-manipulation data for D+. This bounding-box-based Real2Sim approach is chosen for its efficiency, though the framework is complementary to higher-fidelity Real2Sim methods.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper presents experimental results from both simulation and real-world environments to evaluate Fail2Progress against several baselines. The evaluation addresses key questions regarding the effectiveness, efficiency, and generalization capabilities of the approach.

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Pre-training:** Skill effect models (Points2Plans, Stow-GNN, Binary-Pred) were pre-trained on a large simulation dataset of 40,000 skill executions using IsaacGym.
- **Fine-tuning:** Upon detecting a failure, Fail2Progress generates 20 targeted simulation data points using SVI to fine-tune the skill effect model.
- **Tasks:** Multi-object Transport, Hierarchical Tabletop Organization (for quantitative evaluation), and Constrained Packing (for qualitative evaluation).
- **Baselines:**
 - **Original:** Pre-trained skill effect models without any fine-tuning.
 - **Small:** Pre-trained models fine-tuned with 20 randomly sampled data points.
 - **Large:** Pre-trained models fine-tuned with 2000 randomly sampled data points.
 - **Gradient:** Uses stochastic gradient descent for particle updates, generating samples sequentially.
 - **Sampling:** Iteratively generates samples using a sampling-based method.
 - **Replanning:** Attempts to recover from failures by replanning without updating the model.
 - **Domain Randomization (Appx A.16):** An additional baseline where object states and environments are randomized for failure-targeted data generation while actions are held constant.

**Key Findings:**

- **Q1: Improvement from Learning from Failures:** Fail2Progress significantly increased success rates compared to the "Original" baseline. For instance, in the Hierarchical Tabletop Organization task, Points2Plans improved from 11% (Original) to 86% (Fail2Progress) (Table 1). This indicates the importance of learning from out-of-distribution failures.

- **Q2: Sufficiency of More Data:** Fail2Progress (using 20 *targeted* data points) substantially outperformed "Small" (20 *non-targeted* data points) and "Large" (2000 *non-targeted* data points) baselines (e.g., Points2Plans: 86% vs. 13% for Small and 16% for Large). This suggests that the quantity of data alone is insufficient; the quality and targeted nature of the data generated by Fail2Progress are crucial.

- **Q3: Effectiveness of Replanning Alone:** The "Replanning" baseline showed only a marginal improvement over "Original" (e.g., Points2Plans: 24% vs. 11%), falling significantly short of Fail2Progress. This indicates that pure replanning is inadequate for recovering from failures caused by substantial prediction errors in the underlying dynamics model.

- **Q5: SVI Performance and Efficiency:**
 - **Performance:** Fail2Progress consistently outperformed the "Gradient" and "Sampling" baselines in both simulation (Table 1) and real-world experiments (Figure 3a). For Points2Plans, Fail2Progress achieved 86% success, compared to 45% for Gradient and 53% for Sampling. This superior performance is attributed to SVI's capacity to approximate high-dimensional, multi-modal posterior distributions effectively, whereas Gradient suffered from mode collapse and Sampling struggled with inefficiency and poor exploration in high-dimensional spaces.
 - **Efficiency:** Fail2Progress demonstrated comparable efficiency to the "Gradient" baseline and significantly greater efficiency than "Sampling" (Figure 6, Appendix A.3). This efficiency gain is due to SVI's ability to leverage parallel computation on GPUs. The study also determined that 20 particles achieved a favorable balance between performance and optimization time (Figure 7, Appendix A.5).

- **Q4 & Q7: Performance Under Noisy Input and Real2Sim Accuracy:** In real-world experiments (Figure 3b), Fail2Progress achieved high success rates without artificially added noise, suggesting a small Sim2Real gap and sufficient accuracy of the Real2Sim component for failure classification. However, performance degraded with increasing Gaussian noise in point clouds, leading to a larger Sim2Real gap. This indicates that while Fail2Progress effectively addresses incorrect symbolic predictions, its performance is influenced by the quality of real-world perception and the magnitude of the Sim2Real gap.

- **Q6: Generalization to Novel Scenarios:** Fail2Progress demonstrated robust generalization in the Multi-object Transport task (Table 2, Figure 8, Appendix A.14). It maintained strong performance when tested on unseen numbers of objects (e.g., 5 and 7 objects after training on 3) and unseen viewpoints, outperforming Gradient and Sampling. This suggests that the framework does not overfit and can generalize object interactions effectively.

- **Additional Baseline Comparison:** A comparison against a "Domain Randomization" baseline (Appendix A.16) showed that Fail2Progress achieved an average success rate of 86% versus 48% for domain randomization, further highlighting the benefit of targeted data generation.

In summary, Fail2Progress consistently outperformed baselines across diverse tasks and evaluation metrics, demonstrating the efficacy of using SVI for generating targeted, diverse simulation data to learn from real-world robot failures.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of robot learning by addressing a critical challenge: enabling robots to learn from their failures in complex, long-horizon manipulation tasks within real-world environments.

**Significance:**
- **Novel Problem Formulation:** The work introduces a novel formulation of learning from failures as a variational inference problem. This allows for the systematic generation of highly targeted and informative simulation datasets for refining skill effect models, moving beyond reactive recovery to proactive model improvement.
- **Efficient Data Generation via SVI:** By leveraging Stein Variational Inference, Fail2Progress offers an efficient method for generating diverse simulation environments in parallel. This significantly reduces the computational burden and safety risks associated with collecting failure data directly in the real world or relying on computationally expensive high-fidelity Real2Sim approaches.
- **Enhanced Robustness and Adaptability:** The demonstrated ability of Fail2Progress to improve skill effect models and achieve higher success rates across various mobile manipulation tasks, including generalization to unseen scenarios, suggests a pathway towards more robust and continuously adaptable robotic systems. This is crucial for deployment in dynamic and unstructured real-world settings.
- **Bridging Learning Paradigms:** The approach effectively combines elements of active learning, probabilistic inference, and simulation-to-real transfer to create a cohesive framework for lifelong robot learning.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Improved Robot Autonomy:** Robots equipped with Fail2Progress could become more autonomous, capable of learning from their own mistakes post-deployment, thereby reducing the need for constant human intervention or extensive pre-deployment training for every possible scenario.
- **Safer Robot Operation:** By generating failure-like scenarios in simulation, the approach allows robots to learn from potentially dangerous situations without physical risk to themselves or their environment.
- **Reduced Development Costs:** The efficiency of targeted data generation can potentially streamline the development and deployment cycles of robotic manipulation systems, as models can be refined with minimal, high-impact data.
- **Foundation for Lifelong Learning:** Fail2Progress lays groundwork for lifelong learning systems where robots continually update their understanding of the world and their own capabilities as they accumulate experience, making them more proficient over extended operational periods.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The paper also acknowledges several limitations that suggest avenues for future research:
- **Reliability Gap:** The approach, while significantly improving performance, does not achieve 100% reliability (e.g., ~80% in real-world tasks). This indicates that some scenarios remain out-of-distribution even after fine-tuning. Future work involves continuous deployment as a lifelong learning system, allowing for multiple rounds of refinement.
- **Deployment Safety in Open Environments:** Safely deploying such a continuous learning framework in open, dynamic real-world environments remains an open research question.
- **Limited Task and Object Diversity:** Current evaluations are limited to specific mobile manipulation tasks and object types. Extending the framework to more complex and dexterous manipulation, including deformable objects and liquids, is necessary.
- **Untackled Sim2Real Gap Failures:** The current work focuses on "incorrect symbolic predictions." Addressing failures explicitly caused by the Sim2Real gap (e.g., perception noise, control mismatch) is a complementary and important next step, potentially integrating with existing Sim2Real mitigation techniques.
- **Real2Sim Accuracy:** The bounding-box-based Real2Sim scene generation, while efficient, may not perfectly capture complex object geometries or deformable object properties, impacting failure classification and data quality.
- **Restricted Failure Classification:** The two-category classification does not account for external environmental disturbances (e.g., human interference) or hardware degradation, which are relevant in long-term deployment.
- **Limited Simulation State:** The simulation state currently only considers object poses. Incorporating other physical parameters like friction and center of mass could enhance model fidelity.
- **Fixed Relation Set:** The reliance on a pre-defined set of relations limits open-world capabilities; discovering new relations could be beneficial.
- **Scalability to Large Spaces:** Extending the mobile manipulation capabilities to building-wide spaces would require integration with scene graph construction and online updating.

These limitations highlight that while Fail2Progress represents a notable advancement, continuous research is needed to develop truly robust, general-purpose, and adaptable robotic systems.
