# AVID: Learning Multi-Stage Tasks via Pixel-Level Translation of Human Videos

> [!summary] Summary
> AVID learns multi-stage robot tasks from raw human demonstration videos. CycleGAN translates human frames pixel-wise into robot images; selected translated key frames become stage instruction images, and per-stage success classifiers over a learned latent state supply rewards for latent-space model-predictive control with automatic stage resets. On a Sawyer arm it learns coffee making from 20 minutes of human video plus 180 minutes of robot interaction.

## Problem

- Robotic RL needs hand-engineered reward functions requiring motion capture, object tracking, or special sensors, which is impractical outside heavily instrumented lab setups.
- Imitation learning usually needs demonstrations on the robot itself via kinesthetic teaching or teleoperation, demanding special hardware, operator expertise, and physical effort.
- Prior human-video imitation resolves the human-robot appearance gap through hand-specified key points, pose detectors, or paired data, and rarely handles long multi-stage tasks.

## Method

- ==CycleGAN== unpaired image translation maps human demonstration frames into robot images, trained on human demos plus random-motion robot data covering varied object placements, removing manual correspondence.
- Translated key frames at user-picked time steps become per-stage instruction images; per-stage success classifiers on latent states from a ==variational sequential latent-variable model== give the reward.
- Stage-wise latent-space ==MPC== with ==cross-entropy== planning, plus a forward-reset loop that replans against the previous stage's classifier when the threshold fails, giving autonomous retries with sparse human key presses.

## Results

- AVID reaches **80%** success on three-stage coffee making and **70%** on five-stage cup retrieval over 10 trials, best among all human-demonstration methods.
- Ablations collapse on later stages: full-video BCO gets **0%** on coffee stage 3 and **0%** throughout cup retrieval; pixel-space DVF planning gets **0%** on both final stages.
- AVID beats behavioral cloning from teleoperated robot demos on cup retrieval (**70%** vs **40%**), using 30 human demos, ~180 minutes robot time, and under **150** key presses per task.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Pixel-level unpaired translation can substitute for hand-designed embodiment correspondence, and the translation module is task-agnostic, so its data cost amortizes across tasks in one scene.
> - Imitating a few goal key frames beats imitating full trajectories when translations carry artifacts; decoupling goals from trajectories avoids compounding error on long horizons.
> - Learned per-stage resets convert human effort from constant manual resets to occasional binary success signals, but stage boundaries still need manual specification.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of intelligent robots capable of performing complex tasks in unstructured environments is a grand challenge in AI and robotics. Robotic learning, particularly through Reinforcement Learning (RL) and Imitation Learning (IL), offers promising avenues. This paper addresses several critical limitations within these fields, positioning itself at the forefront of efforts to make robotic skill acquisition more practical and scalable.

Traditionally, **Reinforcement Learning (RL)** promises autonomous skill acquisition through experience, but its application to real-world robots is hampered by two major issues:
1. **Reward Function Engineering:** Defining a precise and reliable reward function for complex tasks can be extremely challenging, often requiring expensive instrumentation (e.g., motion capture, object trackers) or creative, task-specific setups. This significantly increases human burden.
2. **Data Inefficiency:** Model-free RL methods, while powerful, typically demand vast amounts of interaction data, which is slow and costly to collect in real-world robotic setups.
3. **Long-Horizon Tasks:** Complex tasks often involve multiple sequential stages, making exploration difficult and compounding errors over long time horizons.

**Imitation Learning (IL)** offers an alternative by learning from expert demonstrations, thereby sidestepping the need for explicit reward functions. However, conventional IL methods face their own set of challenges:
1. **Demonstration Collection Burden:** Most robotic IL relies on demonstrations provided directly on the robot, often through kinesthetic teaching (physically guiding the robot) or teleoperation (remote control). Both methods are laborious, require specialized hardware and expertise, and can sometimes introduce visual obstructions or distractions for image-based learning.
2. **The Correspondence Problem / Embodiment Gap:** A more natural approach, akin to how humans learn, is to learn from observing other humans. However, this introduces a significant challenge: the visual and physical differences (appearance, morphology, action space) between a human demonstrator and a robot. Bridging this "embodiment gap" or "correspondence problem" without explicit manual matching (e.g., key points, pose estimation) or paired data is a major hurdle. Prior work in this area has often required instrumented setups, multiple camera views, or direct robot demonstrations for context.

This paper, AVID, ingeniously integrates solutions to these challenges. It aims to empower robots to learn **multi-stage, long-horizon tasks** by observing **uninstrumented human video demonstrations**, overcoming the **appearance mismatch** between humans and robots, and significantly **reducing human burden** throughout the entire learning process, from task specification to skill acquisition. By leveraging unsupervised image-to-image translation (CycleGAN) to bridge the appearance gap, combining it with data-efficient model-based RL in a learned latent space, and introducing automated stage-wise learning with learned resets, AVID provides a comprehensive framework that pushes the boundaries of autonomous robot learning from diverse, accessible data sources. It directly competes with and aims to surpass methods requiring "oracle" robot demonstrations, demonstrating the potential for truly "learning by watching."

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of the AVID framework is to enable robots to **learn complex, multi-stage tasks autonomously from raw image observations, with task specification provided simply by videos of a human demonstrator.** This overarching goal is driven by several key motivations:

1. **Minimize Human Burden in Robotic Learning:** The authors explicitly state their aim to alleviate the substantial human effort currently required in robotic learning. This includes reducing the burden associated with:
 - **Task Definition:** Moving away from laborious reward function engineering towards intuitive task specification via human demonstrations.
 - **Demonstration Provision:** Shifting from physically demanding or specialized robot-specific demonstrations to easily recordable human videos.
 - **Learning Process Scaffolding:** Automating environment resets and minimizing interactive human feedback during the robot's learning phase.

2. **Leverage the Naturalness and Abundance of Human Demonstrations:** Humans intuitively learn by observing others. The paper motivates equipping robots with this same ability. Videos of human demonstrations are far easier for users to provide, require minimal setup or technical expertise, and represent an enormous, largely untapped source of supervision for robots.

3. **Bridge the Appearance Gap Between Humans and Robots Automatically:** A critical technical barrier to learning from human videos is the significant difference in visual appearance between a human performing a task and a robot attempting the same task. The authors are motivated to find an automated, pixel-level solution to this "correspondence problem" that does not require manual correspondence definitions or paired data.

4. **Tackle Long-Horizon, Multi-Stage Tasks Effectively:** Many real-world tasks are complex and can be naturally decomposed into simpler stages. The paper seeks to develop a framework that can leverage this stage-wise decomposition for more efficient and robust learning, without requiring separate reward functions or manual resets for each stage.

5. **Achieve Data Efficiency in Real-World Robotic Learning:** Given the high cost and slowness of real-world robot interaction, the authors are motivated to employ data-efficient learning techniques (e.g., model-based RL, representation learning) to enable tasks to be learned in a practical amount of robot interaction time.

In essence, the motivation is to make robotic learning more accessible, scalable, and human-friendly by mimicking human learning principles and automating away current manual bottlenecks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

AVID (Automated Visual Instruction-following with Demonstrations) is a multi-stage robotic learning framework that integrates several advanced techniques to achieve its objectives: pixel-level image translation, structured latent representation learning, classifier-based reward functions, and stage-wise model-based reinforcement learning with automated resets.

#### 3.1. Unsupervised Image-to-Image Translation (Goal Concept Acquisition)

The core challenge of learning from human videos is the appearance mismatch between the human demonstrator and the robot. AVID addresses this by employing **CycleGAN** for unsupervised pixel-level image translation.
- **Domains:** The "source domain" consists of images of humans performing tasks, and the "target domain" comprises images of the robot.
- **Training Data:** CycleGAN is trained on *unpaired* images from both domains. To ensure robustness and diversity, human data includes both task demonstrations and "random" movements, while robot data consists of the robot executing random actions in varied environmental settings (e.g., objects shuffled, drawer opened/closed manually). This manual intervention for data collection is minimal and does not require specialized expertise.
- **Translation:** Once trained, the CycleGAN model `G: X → Y` translates human demonstration video frames into visually plausible robot-centric images of the task. This effectively creates "translated robot demonstrations."
- **Instruction Images:** Instead of using entire translated videos, the human user manually selects a few key frames from the translated videos that correspond to the completion of natural stages within the task. These "instruction images" serve as concise visual goals for each stage.

#### 3.2. Structured Representation Learning

To enhance data efficiency and enable planning in a lower-dimensional space, AVID utilizes a **probabilistic temporally-structured latent variable model**, similar to prior work.
- **Generative Model:** This model assumes an unobserved latent state `s_t` that evolves over time according to a learned neural network dynamics model `p(s_t+1 | s_t, a_t)`. Robot images `o_t` are considered observations of this state, decoded by a convolutional neural network `p(o_t | s_t)`.
- **Variational Approximation:** A variational distribution `q(s_t | o_t)` (another convolutional network acting as an encoder) approximates the true posterior distribution `p(s_t | o_t, a_t)`.
- **Training:** Both the generative model and the variational encoder are jointly learned by maximizing the variational lower bound (ELBO) over collected robot interaction data.
- **Purpose:** This provides a compact, robust **latent state representation** of robot observations and a learned **dynamics model** for planning, which are crucial for the subsequent RL phase.

#### 3.3. Reward Function Generation via Success Classifiers

For each stage `i` of the task, a binary **success classifier `C_i`** is trained to determine if the robot has successfully reached the goal state for that stage.
- **Input:** The classifiers take the latent state `s_t` (encoded by `q(s_t | o_t)`) as input, leveraging the learned representation.
- **Training Data:** The translated instruction image for stage `i` (after being encoded into the latent space) serves as a positive example for `C_i`. All other robot images (encoded) are considered negative examples.
- **Reward Signal:** The log probabilities output by `C_i` are used directly as the reward signal for the RL algorithm, eliminating the need for manual reward engineering. This approach also allows for online refinement of the classifiers through human feedback.

#### 3.4. Stage-Wise Model-Based Reinforcement Learning

AVID employs a **latent space Model-Predictive Control (MPC)** method using the Cross-Entropy Method (CEM) for planning and control. The learning procedure (Algorithm 1) is designed to be largely autonomous and stage-wise:

- **Stage Progression:** The robot attempts to complete the task one stage at a time (from `stage = 1` to `S`).
- **Planning for Goal Achievement:** For the current `stage`, the MPC-CEM algorithm plans a sequence of actions in the learned latent space, using the log probability of `C_stage` as the reward function, aiming to exceed a predefined classifier threshold `α`.
- **Automated Resets (Forward-Reset Behavior):** If the robot fails to meet the `C_stage` threshold (or after a fixed number of attempts `K`), it automatically switches its goal to `C_stage-1`, attempting to reset itself to the beginning of the current stage. This "learned reset" mechanism significantly reduces the need for human manual intervention to reset the environment after failures.
- **Human Feedback for Success/Failure:** If the robot's planning leads to a state where `C_stage` signals success (exceeds `α`), the system queries the human user for confirmation via a key press.
 - **Human Success:** The robot moves to the next stage. The current observation is added as a positive example for `C_stage` to refine its understanding.
 - **Human Failure:** This indicates a false positive by `C_stage`. The robot then enters an `EXPLORE()` subroutine (random actions from the current state) to gather more diverse data, and the current observation is added as a negative example for `C_stage`. The models `M` and `C_i` are retrained online with this new data to improve classifier accuracy and combat artifacts from translated images.
- **Full Task Automation:** After completing the last stage, the entire Algorithm 1 is run in reverse to teach the robot how to return to the initial state. This allows for continuous practice of the entire task without manual resets, further enhancing autonomy.

By combining these components, AVID creates a robust, data-efficient, and minimally-supervised framework for robots to learn complex visual manipulation skills.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted aimed to answer three key questions: AVID's capability on long-horizon tasks, the benefits of instruction images and latent space planning, and the costs of learning from human (vs. robot) demonstrations.

#### 4.1. Tasks Evaluated

AVID was evaluated on two complex, temporally extended manipulation tasks using a 7-DoF Sawyer robotic manipulator, receiving only 64x64x3 RGB image observations:
1. **Coffee Making (3 stages):** Pick up cup, place cup in machine, press button.
2. **Cup Retrieval (5 stages):** Grasp drawer handle, open drawer, move arm out of way, pick up cup, place cup on top of drawer.

#### 4.2. Performance Comparison

AVID's performance was compared against several baselines (see Table II):
- **Ablations of AVID:**
 - **Full-video ablation:** Learns from entire translated human demonstrations (using BCO).
 - **Pixel-space ablation:** Uses translated instruction images and stage-wise learning but plans directly in pixel space (like DVF).
- **Prior Methods:**
 - **Time-Contrastive Networks (TCN):** A method for learning embeddings from video, adapted to single-view human demonstrations.
- **"Oracle" Methods (using teleoperated robot demonstrations):**
 - **Behavioral Cloning from Observation (BCO):** Learns actions from robot observations.
 - **Behavioral Cloning:** Directly applies supervised learning on robot observation-action pairs.

**Key Results (Success Rates over 10 trials):**

- **AVID Outperforms Human-Demo Baselines:**
 - For **Coffee Making**, AVID achieved 100% success on Stage 1 (pick up cup) and 80% on Stages 2 and 3 (place cup, press button). In contrast, the full-video ablation managed 70% only on Stage 1 and 0% on Stage 3, the pixel-space ablation 60% on Stage 1 and 0% on Stage 3, and TCN performed poorly (10% on Stage 1, 0% on Stage 3).
 - For **Cup Retrieval**, AVID showed excellent performance: 100% success for Stages 1-3, 80% for Stage 4, and 70% for Stage 5 (placing cup). All other human-demonstration baselines performed very poorly or entirely failed on this more complex task.
- **AVID Competes with or Exceeds Robot-Demo Baselines:**
 - AVID (learning from human videos) significantly outperformed BCO (learning from teleoperated robot demonstrations) on the later stages of both tasks. For example, BCO achieved 0% on the final stages of both tasks, while AVID achieved 80% and 70%.
 - On **Coffee Making**, standard Behavioral Cloning (BC) from teleoperated robot demonstrations slightly surpassed AVID (90% vs. 80% on Stage 3). However, on the more complex **Cup Retrieval** task, AVID (70% on Stage 5) outperformed BC (40% on Stage 5), demonstrating the benefits of stage-wise training for longer-horizon tasks.

#### 4.3. Insights from Ablation Studies

- **Benefits of Instruction Images and Stage-Wise Learning:** The poor performance of the "full-video ablation" highlights the difficulty of learning from entire translated demonstrations. This is attributed to artifacts in the translated videos and the compounding errors of trying to learn a long-horizon task without explicit stage decomposition. Using selected instruction images and stage-wise training is crucial.
- **Benefits of Latent Space Planning:** The "pixel-space ablation" performed worse than AVID, likely because planning directly in high-dimensional pixel space is computationally expensive, preventing online training and replanning during execution. This underscores the data efficiency and robustness gained from AVID's learned latent state representation and model-based planning.

#### 4.4. Human Supervision and Data Efficiency

- **Human Demonstration Time:** Only 20 minutes were required to collect human demonstration videos (30 for coffee, 20 for cup retrieval), along with some "random" human data.
- **Robot Interaction Time:** The robot interacted with the environment for approximately 180 minutes (3 hours) for coffee making and similar for cup retrieval, collecting online training data.
- **Human Feedback Burden:** During the robot's learning phase, AVID required a minimal number of human key presses for success/failure feedback: 131 for coffee making and 126 for cup retrieval. This "one hour per task" with such low key press count is presented as a practical and low-burden way to supervise learning.
- **Automated Resets:** The framework's ability to automatically reset to the beginning of a stage significantly reduced the need for manual human resets, further lowering the supervisor's effort.

In summary, AVID successfully demonstrates that complex, multi-stage robotic tasks can be learned efficiently and robustly from easily provided human video demonstrations, outperforming most alternative approaches, including some that rely on more costly robot-specific demonstrations.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

AVID represents a significant step forward in the field of robotic learning, particularly at the intersection of imitation learning, reinforcement learning, and computer vision. Its contributions and potential impact are multifaceted:

1. **Bridging the Human-Robot Embodiment Gap:** The core innovation of using CycleGAN for pixel-level, unsupervised image translation between human and robot domains is a powerful and automated solution to the long-standing "correspondence problem." This capability unlocks the vast potential of learning from readily available human videos, rather than labor-intensive robot-specific demonstrations.

2. **Radically Reducing Human Burden in Robotic Learning:** AVID's most impactful contribution is its comprehensive strategy to minimize human effort. By allowing task specification through simple human videos, generating reward functions automatically via classifiers, and implementing learned, automated stage resets with minimal human feedback (~150 key presses per task), AVID drastically lowers the barrier to entry for teaching robots new skills. This moves robotic learning closer to a practical reality for non-expert users.

3. **Enabling Robust Learning of Long-Horizon Multi-Stage Tasks:** The framework's stage-wise approach, coupled with data-efficient latent space model-based RL, effectively tackles the challenge of learning complex, temporally extended tasks. The ability to decompose tasks into manageable stages, learn resets, and iteratively refine behavior makes the learning process more stable and successful for challenging real-world scenarios. The superior performance of AVID compared to non-stage-aware methods (even those using "oracle" robot data) on complex tasks like cup retrieval underscores this significance.

4. **Promoting Data Efficiency in Real-World Robotics:** By leveraging structured representation learning and model-based RL, AVID achieves impressive data efficiency, learning complex tasks within a few hours of robot interaction. This is crucial for real-world deployment, where robot operation is costly and time-consuming.

5. **A Step Towards Truly Autonomous Skill Acquisition:** AVID embodies a vision where robots learn new skills by "watching and practicing," much like humans do. This paradigm shift, moving away from explicit programming or heavily instrumented learning, is fundamental for achieving adaptable and intelligent robots capable of operating in diverse, unstructured human environments.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Increased Accessibility:** The reduced human burden and reliance on easily recorded human videos could make advanced robotic capabilities accessible to a wider range of users, beyond specialized AI researchers.
- **Faster Skill Deployment:** New robotic skills could be taught and deployed much faster, accelerating progress in areas like service robotics, manufacturing, and assistance technologies.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** AVID's modular design (translation decoupled from RL) provides a strong foundation for future advancements, such as exploring more sophisticated video segmentation for automatic stage discovery or improving the generalization of the translation module.

**Future Work (as outlined by authors):**
The authors themselves highlight an exciting direction for future work: **amortizing the cost of data collection and CycleGAN training across multiple tasks.** Instead of training a new CycleGAN for each task, a single, general CycleGAN could be trained on a large, diverse dataset (e.g., various human and robot behaviors in a kitchen). This would enable robots to learn *any* new task within that environment from just a handful of human demonstrations, with virtually no additional upfront cost. This "zero-shot" or "few-shot" learning capability across tasks would truly unlock the potential of learning by watching humans in complex, open-ended environments, representing the next frontier for this line of research.
