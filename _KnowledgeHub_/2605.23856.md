# Point Tracking Improves World Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> This research introduces JOPAT, a unified world-action model that jointly denoises visual latents, 2D point tracks with visibility, and robot actions within a diffusion transformer to improve robotic manipulation. JOPAT achieved a 97.8% average success rate on the LIBERO benchmark and an average of 57.5% on real-world LeRobot tasks, outperforming pixel-based baselines and demonstrating enhanced robustness to partial observability and distribution shifts.

## Problem

- Existing Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models often lack an inherent understanding of environmental dynamics, making them less robust to partial observability and long-horizon tasks.
- Current World Action Models (WAMs) using pixel-level future state prediction entangle true dynamics with nuisance factors like lighting and texture, making representations susceptible to task-irrelevant visual variations.
- It is challenging to effectively translate general video dynamics into generalizable policies for robotic tasks, as pixel-level pre-training on action-free videos does not consistently improve success rates.

## Method

- **JOPAT**, a ==Joint Pixel-And-Track World-Action Model==, is proposed, which integrates visual latents, 2D point tracks with explicit visibility, and robot actions into a unified ==denoising diffusion transformer==.
- Off-the-shelf ==point trackers== (CoTrackerV3) are used to generate 2D point trajectories and their visibility over a future horizon, which are then encoded into track tokens.
- The model is trained with a ==dual objective==, allowing it to learn visual-track dynamics from abundant action-free videos and ground these priors in executable robot actions using limited demonstrations.

## Results

- JOPAT achieved an average success rate of **97.8%** across **40** LIBERO manipulation tasks, outperforming pixel-based baselines, especially on long-horizon tasks, and reached **57.5%** on real-world tasks (e.g., **100%** on Push-Tomato).
- Ablation studies confirmed that joint pixel-and-track modeling significantly improved success rates (**96.4%** vs. **77.4%** for Latent-only and **26.2%** for Track-only on LIBERO-Long) in both simulation and real-world.
- Action-free video pretraining consistently improved performance across various demonstration budgets, boosting success from **11.9%** to **64.2%** with **10** demonstrations, demonstrating effective prior transfer from both robot-domain and generic videos.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - An explicit future state that exposes how robot-controllable scene elements move and their visibility status, rather than just appearance, is more beneficial for control.
> - Jointly predicting visual latents (for semantic context) and 2D point tracks with visibility (for correspondence-level motion) provides a more robust and complete representation for robotic policy learning.
> - Supervising the explicit pixel-track future state using action-free videos effectively extracts and transfers scalable motion priors, significantly improving performance, especially in low-data regimes.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation is progressing towards developing policies that exhibit generalization across diverse objects, scenes, and viewpoints, while maintaining reliability under partial observability, occlusion, and long-horizon temporal dependencies. Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, which leverage large pre-trained Vision-Language Models (VLMs) to map language instructions and visual observations directly to robot actions, represent a significant advancement in this direction. However, VLA models, primarily trained on image-language data, often lack an inherent understanding of environmental dynamics, a crucial aspect for effective robotic policy learning.

In parallel, World Action Models (WAMs) have emerged as a paradigm for integrating video priors into robot control systems. These models aim to acquire motion priors and physical grounding from diverse visual dynamics through large-scale visual pre-training. A recurring challenge in this area is how to effectively translate general video dynamics into policies that are generalizable for robotic tasks. Prior research has indicated that pixel-level pre-training on action-free videos does not consistently lead to improved policy success rates in manipulation tasks.

Current instantiations of WAMs, often referred to as pixel-latent WAMs, typically define the "future state" interface using pixels, latent images, or visual tokens. A limitation of this approach is that the representation of the future state is predominantly dictated by appearance. Consequently, motion variables that are directly relevant to actions—such as which object moved, its direction, and whether it became occluded—must be inferred implicitly from these appearance-dominated tokens. This entanglement of dynamics with nuisance factors like lighting and texture can make learned representations vulnerable to task-irrelevant visual variations.

The present work addresses a specific gap by posing the question of "what future state should serve as the interface between video prediction and control." It posits that for control, a useful future state should explicitly expose how robot-controllable scene elements move and their visibility status, while still retaining sufficient visual semantics for object identification and affordance recognition. This distinguishes it from approaches focused solely on photorealistic reconstruction.

Related work includes various intermediate motion representations, such as pixel motion fields, optical flow, point trajectories, and 3D point tracks. While these approaches have demonstrated that motion and spatial abstractions can be more control-relevant than raw pixels, they typically use motion as an input, an auxiliary prediction target, or a separate planning representation. This paper proposes a different integration by treating 2D tracks with visibility as an integral part of the generative future state itself, sampled jointly with visual latents and actions. This design aims to preserve semantic grounding through visual latents while providing a scalable, correspondence-level interface for motion. Furthermore, by using action-free videos to supervise this explicit pixel-track future state, rather than directly supervising actions, the approach seeks to harness the scalability of video data more effectively for learning motion priors.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind this research stems from the observation that while robot policy learning benefits significantly from world-action models that capture environment dynamics, existing pixel-level prediction methods often present a fundamental challenge. Pixel-based representations tend to entangle the true dynamics with nuisance factors such as lighting, texture, and background elements. This entanglement renders learned representations susceptible to task-irrelevant visual variations, making them less robust and generalizable for real-world robotic manipulation.

The central question guiding this work is an interrogation of the "future state" interface in world models: specifically, "what future state should serve as the interface between video prediction and control?" The authors argue that a future state optimized for control is distinct from one aimed at photorealistic reconstruction. For manipulation tasks, the most beneficial state should explicitly reveal how robot-controllable scene elements move, whether their evidence remains visible, and still retain sufficient visual semantics to identify objects and their affordances. This contrasts with pixel-only WAMs, where action-relevant motion variables are recovered implicitly from appearance-dominated tokens.

Based on this insight, the key objectives of the research are:

1. **To Propose JOPAT, a Joint Pixel-And-Track World-Action Model:** The core objective is to introduce a novel architecture, JOPAT, that jointly denoises visual latents, 2D point tracks with visibility, and executable robot actions within a single denoising diffusion transformer. This unified generative framework is designed to make correspondence-level motion an explicit interface between perception and control.
2. **To Leverage 2D Point Tracks with Visibility as a Motion Representation:** A specific objective is to utilize 2D point tracks with visibility to encode persistent scene correspondences over time. Tracks are intended to capture long-horizon dynamics and maintain robustness under occlusion or partial out-of-frame motion, providing greater utility than modeling pixel appearance alone. Visibility information is explicitly included to indicate when these correspondences become occluded or leave the camera view.
3. **To Enhance Robustness and Generalization:** The work aims to demonstrate that this joint pixel-and-track future state, by preserving object identity, affordance, and appearance cues through visual latents, while explicitly representing object displacement, contact-induced motion, occlusion, and out-of-frame transitions through tracks, leads to improved robustness. This robustness is expected under visual out-of-distribution variations, self-induced occlusions, and long-horizon dependencies.
4. **To Improve Policy Learning Efficiency from Limited Demonstrations:** A further objective is to show that integrating pixel-based visual representations with explicit point-track dynamics can yield more reliable control from limited robot demonstrations. This is particularly achieved by enabling effective policy learning from action-free videos, which are used to supervise the pixel-track future state, thereby transferring scalable motion priors.

In essence, the motivation is to move beyond appearance-centric world models by explicitly incorporating motion and visibility cues, thereby providing a more direct and robust interface for robot control, especially in complex and dynamic environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, JOPAT (JOint Pixel-And-Track World-Action Model), integrates visual latents, 2D point tracks with visibility, and robot actions into a unified generative framework based on a denoising diffusion transformer (DiT).

#### 3.1. Unified Pixel-Track-Action Architecture

The JOPAT architecture processes an input observation window ($o_{t-1:t}$), consisting of two RGB frames, through an observation encoder ($E_c$) to produce a global conditioning feature ($c_t$). This feature, along with modality-specific diffusion timesteps ($\tau_a, \tau_o, \tau_p$), is injected into each DiT block using AdaLN.

JOPAT performs joint denoising of three types of tokens within a shared sequence:
- **Action tokens ($\tilde{z}_a$):** Representing future robot actions ($a_{t:t+K-1}$).
- **Future visual-latent tokens ($\tilde{z}_o$):** Derived from a future observation window ($o_{t+H:t+H+1}$).
- **Track tokens ($\tilde{z}_p$):** Encoding 2D point tracks with visibility information ($p_{t:t+H_p-1}$).
- Additionally, learnable register tokens ($r$) are included in the sequence.

A DiT-style transformer applies bidirectional full attention over this concatenated sequence, $Z = [\tilde{z}_a, \tilde{z}_o, \tilde{z}_p, r]$, and predicts modality-specific noise ($\hat{\epsilon}_a, \hat{\epsilon}_o, \hat{\epsilon}_p$). Separate decoding heads are then used to reconstruct the action noise, visual-latent noise, track-coordinate noise, and visibility logits from these predicted noise components. This shared denoising mechanism allows direct interaction between action generation and imagined future pixels and point tracks, rather than relying on separate decoders.

For implementation, JOPAT conditions on two RGB frames, predicts a two-frame future observation window with an offset $H = 16$, and uses $H_p = K = 19$ for both track and action prediction horizons.

#### 3.2. Track Construction and Modeling

The track construction and encoding pipeline involves the following steps:
1. **Reference Frame and Query Points:** The last frame of the current observation window ($o_t$) serves as the reference frame. A grid of $N$ query points (specifically, a 25x25 grid, resulting in $N=625$ points) is initialized on $o_t$.
2. **Point Tracking:** An off-the-shelf point tracker (CoTrackerV3) is used to track these query points over a future horizon, from $t+1$ to $t+H_p$. This process yields two outputs:
 - Point trajectories: $P^{(t)} \in \mathbb{R}^{H_p \times N \times 2}$, representing the 2D coordinates of each point over time.
 - Visibility: $V^{(t)} \in \{0, 1\}^{H_p \times N}$, indicating whether each point is visible at each timestep.
3. **Track-as-Video Representation:** To compactly encode the tracks, the point dimension is reshaped back into its spatial grid configuration. The point trajectories $P^{(t)}$ are transformed into a spatiotemporal grid $G_p \in \mathbb{R}^{2 \times H_p \times H_g \times W_g}$, where $H_g \times W_g = N$.
4. **Track Encoder:** A 3D convolutional patchifier ($E_p$) processes $G_p$ to produce the track tokens $z_p \in \mathbb{R}^{L_p \times d}$.
5. **Track Decoding:** After the unified denoising by the DiT, the track tokens are decoded into predicted coordinate noise ($\hat{P}^{(t)}$) and visibility logits ($\hat{V}^{(t)}$) using separate decoders ($D_p, D_v$). Notably, ground-truth visibility is not provided to the track encoder during denoising to prevent information leakage, but is predicted as an output.

#### 3.3. Training and Inference

**Training:**
JOPAT is trained using a unified denoising objective across all modalities (action, visual-latent, and track-coordinate tokens). For each modality $m \in \{a, o, p\}$, a diffusion timestep $\tau_m$ is independently sampled, and the noisy input $x_{m, \tau_m}$ is constructed. The primary loss for each modality is a mean squared error between the predicted noise ($\hat{\epsilon}_m$) and the true Gaussian noise ($\epsilon_m$), i.e., $L_m = \lVert \hat{\epsilon}_m - \epsilon_m \rVert_2^2$. The track diffusion loss ($L_p$) is applied solely to the 2D coordinates. Visibility prediction is supervised separately using binary cross-entropy (BCE) over all timesteps and points ($L_{vis}$).

The overall training objectives depend on the data source:
- **Action-labeled demonstrations (D):** The full loss is used, $L_D = L_a + \lambda_o L_o + \lambda_p L_p + \lambda_{vis} L_{vis}$.
- **Action-free videos (V):** The action branch is masked, and only the visual-latent, track-coordinate, and visibility losses are applied, $L_V = \lambda_o L_o + \lambda_p L_p + \lambda_{vis} L_{vis}$.
This dual objective allows the architecture to learn visual-track dynamics from action-free videos and ground these priors in executable robot actions using limited demonstrations.

**Inference:**
At inference time, JOPAT employs receding-horizon action generation. Given the current observation context ($c_t$), action, visual, and track tokens are initialized from Gaussian noise and iteratively denoised using a DDIM sampler. While all token types interact through self-attention during sampling, only the action branch is decoded for execution. The first few actions (e.g., 8 actions) from the predicted chunk are executed before replanning. The visual and track tokens, although not directly executed, serve as implicit latent future-state variables that influence the generated actions.

**Implementation Details:**
The model uses a DiT-style Transformer with 12 layers, a hidden dimension of 768, and 12 attention heads. It incorporates 8 learnable register tokens. The visual target encoder utilizes a frozen SDXL VAE. Training is performed with an AdamW optimizer, a learning rate of $1 \times 10^{-4}$, and a DDIM scheduler.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates JOPAT across various axes, including main performance, representation design, robustness, and scalable pretraining, in both simulated and real-world environments.

#### 4.1. Simulation Results (LIBERO Benchmark)

- **Main Performance (Q1):** JOPAT achieved an average success rate of 97.8% across 40 LIBERO manipulation tasks, establishing a new baseline. It demonstrated notable improvements over pixel-based baselines, particularly on the "Long" suite, which involves the most temporally extended tasks. This outcome suggests that joint pixel-and-track prediction is beneficial for policy learning requiring long-horizon state maintenance.

#### 4.2. Real-world Experiments (LeRobot SO-101)

- JOPAT was evaluated on four real-world tasks (Cook-Soup, Insert-Peg, Push-Tomato, Pick-Grocery) using the LeRobot SO-101 platform.
- It achieved an average success rate of 57.5%, outperforming baselines such as ACT (40%) and UWM (32.5%).
- The largest gain was observed on "Push-Tomato," where JOPAT reached 100% success rate, indicating the utility of explicit track prediction for contact-rich object displacement.
- Improvements were also noted on "Cook-Soup," aligning with its simulated robustness to occlusion and long-horizon state drift. "Insert-Peg" remained challenging for all methods, suggesting that millimeter-level insertion might require additional geometric or contact-aware sensing.

#### 4.3. Ablation on Joint Pixel-Track Modeling (Q2)

Ablation studies were conducted to assess the contribution of joint pixel-and-track modeling by comparing JOPAT with variants: "Latent-only" (removes track prediction) and "Track-only" (removes visual-latent prediction).

- **Simulation (LIBERO-Long):** The joint model achieved an average success rate of 96.4%, significantly outperforming Latent-only (77.4%) and Track-only (26.2%). This supports the hypothesis that track tokens provide correspondence-level motion constraints, while visual latents offer semantic context. Failures in Latent-only were attributed to a lack of precise long-horizon motion, and in Track-only to insufficient visual grounding for object identification.
- **Real-robot:** Similar trends were observed, with the joint model achieving 57.5% average success rate, compared to 35.0% for Latent-only and 15.0% for Track-only. Latent-only struggled with implicit contact-relevant motion, while Track-only lacked visual grounding.

#### 4.4. Ablation on Track Visibility Prediction

- Removing the visibility prediction head reduced the real-robot average success rate from 57.5% to 47.5%. The most significant impact was on "Cook-Soup." This indicates that visibility supervision acts as a crucial missing-evidence constraint, enabling the model to account for unobservable correspondences rather than incorrectly inferring stationary points from hidden ones. This is particularly valuable for tasks involving self-occlusion or transient out-of-view motion.

#### 4.5. Does Action-Free Video Pretraining Help? (Q4)

- Experiments on LIBERO-Long demonstrated that action-free video pretraining consistently improved performance over training from scratch across different demonstration budgets.
- In the low-data regime (10 demonstrations), DROID (robot-domain) pretraining significantly improved success rate from 11.9% to 64.2%, and even generic OpenVid-1M pretraining achieved 48.5%. This indicates that JOPAT's pixel-track future-state objective effectively extracts useful motion priors from both robot-specific and general videos.
- At 50 demonstrations, the performance gap between DROID and OpenVid-1M pretraining narrowed considerably (96.4% vs. 95.1%), suggesting that generic video priors become equally effective once sufficient action-labeled demonstrations are available.

#### 4.6. Robustness on OOD Tasks (Q3)

- JOPAT was evaluated on a modified LIBERO-Long setting designed to introduce stronger partial observability and distribution shifts (expanded object range, unseen background objects, unseen distractors).
- JOPAT achieved the highest average success rate of 66%, surpassing Diffusion Policy (32%), UWM (34%), and fine-tuned $\pi_{0.5}$ (53%).
- It showed substantial gains on tasks requiring object state maintenance through extended interactions and resistance to distractor interference (e.g., Mug-Mug, Moka-Moka, Book-Candy). This suggests that explicit correspondence and visibility prediction provide a robust future-state interface under long-horizon distribution shifts.
- While some baselines performed better on individual OOD tasks, JOPAT demonstrated the strongest average performance, particularly where distractor-aware object tracking was critical.

#### 4.7. Prediction-Horizon Sensitivity

- On real-robot tasks, moderate future-observation offsets (e.g., H=16) were found to capture the necessary dynamics and match the performance of longer offsets on average. Longer offsets could benefit multi-stage tasks but might introduce increased uncertainty, potentially hindering precision-sensitive tasks.

#### 4.8. Qualitative Behavior and Failure Modes

- Qualitative analysis indicated that predicted tracks maintained temporal coherence even when the robot arm occluded the target or objects moved near image boundaries. This highlights the model's ability to maintain a belief about hidden object motion (object permanence), which is useful for long-horizon tasks with temporary perceptual dropout.
- Remaining task-level failures were attributed to specific challenges: specular reflections on the pot lid for "Cook-Soup," millimeter-level 3D alignment for "Insert-Peg," small localization errors compounding for "Push-Tomato," and limited spatial generalization in clutter for "Pick-Grocery." These suggest that while JOPAT improves robustness to visual dropout, fine-grained contact geometry may require additional sensing.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces JOPAT, a unified world-action model that makes a notable advancement by explicitly integrating 2D point tracks and visual latents as joint predictive modalities for action generation. This approach represents a shift from purely appearance-dominated future state prediction towards a more motion-centric and correspondence-aware interface for robotic control.

The **significance** of this work is multifaceted:
- **Enhanced Robustness:** By making motion correspondences explicit alongside pixel-level visual latents, JOPAT improves the robustness of manipulation policies against common real-world challenges such as occlusion, partial observability, long-horizon temporal dependencies, and visual distribution shifts. The explicit prediction of visibility, in particular, allows the model to maintain a coherent understanding of object state even when objects are temporarily hidden or move out of view, a crucial aspect for continuous interaction in dynamic environments.
- **Improved Data Efficiency and Transfer Learning:** JOPAT effectively leverages scalable motion priors derived from action-free videos, which are significantly easier to acquire than action-labeled robot demonstrations. This capability to transfer knowledge from diverse video sources (both robot-domain and generic) to downstream control tasks, especially in low-data regimes, is critical for accelerating the development of capable robotic systems.
- **Advancement in World-Action Models (WAMs):** The proposed pixel-track future state addresses a fundamental limitation of prior WAMs that rely solely on pixel or latent-image predictions. By explicitly exposing control-relevant motion variables, JOPAT provides a more direct and actionable interface for policy learning, bridging the gap between perception and control more effectively.
- **State-of-the-Art Performance:** Achieving state-of-the-art results on the challenging LIBERO benchmark and demonstrating improved performance in real-world LeRobot tasks validates the practical efficacy of the JOPAT framework. The consistent gains across various task categories, particularly those involving long-horizon and contact-rich interactions, underscore its capabilities.

The **potential impact** of JOPAT extends to several areas within robotics and AI:
- **Generalist Robot Policies:** By providing a robust mechanism for learning from abundant passive video data, JOPAT contributes to the development of more generalist robot policies that can adapt to new tasks and environments with limited task-specific demonstrations.
- **Foundation Models for Robotics:** The approach's ability to integrate diverse data sources and learn rich dynamic representations positions it as a step towards more capable foundation models for embodied AI, where a deep understanding of environmental dynamics is paramount.
- **Applications in Complex Manipulation:** Tasks requiring interaction with occluded objects, long-sequence actions, or handling of visually variable objects could see significant performance improvements due to JOPAT's explicit motion and visibility modeling.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
Despite its contributions, the paper acknowledges several limitations, which also suggest avenues for future research:
- **Sparsity of Point Tracks:** The reliance on grid-based 2D point tracks might be insufficient for capturing fine-grained deformations necessary for sub-centimeter dexterity. Future work could explore denser or more adaptive point representations, or integrate 3D point tracking.
- **Dependency on Off-the-Shelf Trackers:** The performance is constrained by the capabilities of the underlying point tracker (CoTrackerV3). Developing end-to-end learning methods that can jointly learn tracking and dynamics might overcome this limitation.
- **Inference Speed:** The current inference speed of approximately 10 Hz, while efficient for a unified transformer, could be a bottleneck for applications requiring high-frequency control (>20 Hz). Optimizations in model architecture or sampling strategies could address this.
- **Static Camera Assumption:** The architecture is optimized for static camera setups, limiting its direct applicability to mobile manipulation scenarios where ego-motion complicates tracking and perception. Adapting the model for dynamic camera movements or incorporating egocentric visual cues would be a valuable extension.
- **Fine-grained Contact Geometry:** JOPAT, while robust to visual dropout, still faces challenges in tasks requiring millimeter-level 3D alignment and precise contact-aware execution. Integrating explicit 3D sensing (e.g., depth cameras, LiDAR), tactile feedback, or incorporating contact-aware objectives into the model's learning could further enhance its capabilities for such delicate manipulation tasks.

Overall, JOPAT presents a compelling argument for the utility of explicit motion representation in world-action models, charting a course for more robust and data-efficient robot policy learning in complex environments.
