# HumanEgo: Zero-Shot Robot Learning from Minutes of Human Egocentric Videos

> [!summary] Summary
> HumanEgo introduces a framework enabling robots to learn bimanual manipulation skills directly from minutes of human egocentric video, achieving zero-shot transfer to diverse robot platforms without any robot-specific data. The system demonstrates an average 92.5% success rate across four complex manipulation tasks, outperforming existing baselines and matched-time robot teleoperation.

## Problem

- High cost and labor associated with collecting extensive robot demonstrations (hundreds to thousands per task) for robot manipulation learning.
- Existing approaches using human egocentric video for robot learning either still require substantial robot-specific data or rely on computationally intensive internet-scale pretraining followed by robot-specific fine-tuning.
- The inherent visual and kinematic differences between human hands/arms and robot grippers/manipulators, known as the 'embodiment gap,' makes direct transfer challenging, especially when learning from minimal human video data.

## Method

- A four-stage pipeline processes human egocentric video by segmenting and inpainting human hands, then rendering virtual grippers and tracked object keypoints into the frames to create embodiment-agnostic visual observations.
- Explicit spatial observations are generated using Interaction-Centric Tokens (ICTs), which encode relative 3D poses and grasp states between tracked hands and objects, providing a viewpoint-invariant representation.
- A ==flow matching== policy, trained with a 6-layer ==transformer== decoder, generates bimanual action trajectories and is augmented with three dense auxiliary objectives (object motion, 2D trace, latent consistency) to maximize learning efficiency from limited human demonstrations.

## Results

- HumanEgo achieved an average **92.5%** success rate across four real-world bimanual manipulation tasks (Serve Bread, Downstack Cups, Water Flowers, Adjust Table) with just 30 minutes of human egocentric data, outperforming other zero-shot baselines which ranged from **1.9%** to **45.0%**.
- The framework demonstrated superior data efficiency, achieving a **75.0%** success rate with only 15 minutes of human video, which surpassed a robot teleoperation baseline (ACT) trained on 30 minutes of robot-specific data (**51.2%**).
- HumanEgo exhibited robust zero-shot generalization, maintaining high success rates (**85%**-**91.25%**) across nine out-of-distribution conditions, including novel backgrounds, lighting, viewpoints, distractors, object instances, and different robot embodiments/camera setups without retraining.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Interaction-Centric Tokens (ICTs) that capture 3D spatial relationships are more crucial for bridging the human-robot embodiment gap and enabling effective skill transfer than solely relying on visual domain adaptation.
> - Dense multi-task supervision derived from auxiliary objectives (object motion, 2D trace, latent consistency) significantly boosts data efficiency, amplifying the learning signal from scarce human demonstrations.
> - High-quality 3D hand tracking, particularly from stereo-based systems like Meta Aria MPS, is critical for accurate spatial representation and ultimately for successful human-to-robot policy transfer in low-data regimes.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot manipulation learning has advanced significantly, yet a persistent challenge remains the high cost and labor associated with collecting large datasets of robot demonstrations. Traditional methods often necessitate hundreds to thousands of task-specific robot demonstrations, which are time-consuming and inconvenient to acquire. Human egocentric video has emerged as a potentially more accessible and cost-effective alternative, allowing humans to record manipulation tasks using head-mounted cameras in diverse environments.

Existing approaches to leveraging human egocentric video for robot learning generally fall into two categories, both with limitations that HumanEgo aims to address. The first paradigm involves co-training methods, which supplement robot data with human video. While these methods can reduce the total robot data requirement, they do not eliminate it, meaning substantial robot demonstrations are still needed for each new task. The second paradigm utilizes large-scale pretraining on massive egocentric corpora. These approaches often require significant computational resources and still typically necessitate robot-specific post-training to produce policies deployable on physical robots.

HumanEgo positions itself as a direct response to these limitations by pursuing the goal of learning deployable manipulation policies exclusively from minutes of human egocentric demonstrations, without requiring any robot-specific data or internet-scale pretraining. This places it in the sub-area of zero-shot human-to-robot transfer, where the objective is to achieve transfer without any domain adaptation or fine-tuning on robot hardware. The paper specifically targets overcoming the "embodiment gap" between humans and robots, which manifests in both visual appearance and kinematics, and addressing the challenges of learning from minimal, potentially sparse human video data. It contributes to the development of data-efficient and hardware-agnostic robot learning systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a framework, HumanEgo, that enables robots to learn complex manipulation policies directly from a minimal amount of human egocentric video data. This learning should be entirely robot-data-free, hardware-agnostic, and facilitate zero-shot transfer to various robot embodiments, camera setups, and environments.

The motivation behind this objective stems from two fundamental challenges inherent in learning from human egocentric videos for robot control:

1. **The Representation Challenge: Bridging the Embodiment Gap.**
 - **Visual Discrepancy:** Human hands and robot grippers possess distinct visual appearances. Existing retargeting methods that synthesize robot-like imagery from human video can be brittle to morphological and viewpoint differences. Point-tracking approaches extract sparse geometric features but may discard rich visual context.
 - **Kinematic Discrepancy:** The kinematics of human arms and robot manipulators differ. Hierarchical methods often separate high-level plans from low-level execution, but the low-level controllers still typically require robot-specific data. Object-centric approaches, which track only the manipulated object, risk losing critical information about the hand's approach, grasp, and release mechanics. The authors argue that manipulation skills are defined by the interaction between the hand and the object, rather than by either entity in isolation.

2. **The Learning Challenge: Learning from Minimal Data.**
 - While raw human video is abundant, obtaining clean clips with precise action labels for specific tasks can be challenging. This necessitates data-efficient learning, especially when only minutes of per-task videos are available.
 - **Multi-modality:** The same task can be accomplished through various valid strategies. Diffusion-based methods can capture this distribution but are often slow during inference, while faster alternatives may lack expressiveness.
 - **Signal Sparsity:** A single human demonstration trajectory contains rich information beyond just the hand action, including object motion, visual traces, and the overall hand-object state. Prior work has often utilized only a fraction of this signal, such as single auxiliary targets or relying on upstream pretraining corpora. The authors propose that a fast generative policy, combined with multi-type dense supervision, is critical for effective data-efficient learning from limited human egocentric videos.

HumanEgo is therefore motivated to provide specific solutions to each of these challenges, aiming to unlock human egocentric video as a superior and more accessible data source for robot learning compared to traditional methods.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

HumanEgo integrates a four-stage pipeline to convert human egocentric video into a deployable bimanual robot policy.

#### 3.1. Egocentric Data Collection

Human demonstrators wear Meta Aria Gen1 glasses to record task demonstrations. These glasses provide high-quality 6-DoF SLAM tracking, calibrated 3D hand pose estimation, and synchronized egocentric RGB streams. Demonstrations are collected in varied environments without specialized setups or calibration, with approximately 30 minutes of human demonstrations per task recorded at 30 Hz. A short scene sweep is performed at the start of each demonstration to aid 3D object triangulation. Automatic phase detection is applied to segment recordings, retaining only active manipulation frames for training.

#### 3.2. Visual Observation Preprocessing

To address the visual embodiment gap, raw egocentric RGB frames are transformed into embodiment-agnostic observations:
1. The human hand and arm are segmented using SAM2 and subsequently removed via LaMa inpainting.
2. A virtual gripper and tracked object keypoints, derived from the spatial observation, are rendered into the inpainted image. This implicitly encodes 6D pose information as visual cues without expensive domain adaptation.

#### 3.3. Spatial Observation Preprocessing: Interaction-Centric Tokens (ICT)

This stage creates an explicit entity-level spatial observation by tracking hands and objects, then encoding their relative poses into Interaction-Centric Tokens (ICTs).
1. **Hand Tracking and Motion Optimization:** 3D hand keypoints from Aria MPS are lifted to the world frame via SLAM and smoothed (Savitzky–Golay for positions, Exponential Moving Average for rotations). A virtual parallel-jaw gripper's SE(3) end-effector pose ($T_{ee}$) and a scalar grasp ($g$) are derived. The fingertip midpoint defines $p_{ee}$. The orientation $R_{ee}$ is constructed from the thumb and index metacarpophalangeal (MCP) joints to ensure stability during pinch grasps. The grasp scalar $g$ normalizes the thumb-index fingertip distance.
2. **Object Tracking and Pose Estimation:** Objects are detected with Grounding DINO, segmented with SAM2, and contour keypoints are sampled. These 2D keypoints are tracked across video frames using CoTracker3 and lifted to 3D via triangulation, using camera intrinsics and Aria SLAM poses. The centroid of tracked points forms the object position ($p_{obj}$), and orientation ($R_{obj}$) is estimated with Orient-Anything V2. During hand occlusion, kinematic latching ties the object pose to the hand.
3. **Entity Spatial Encoding via Interaction-Centric Tokens (ICT):** For each entity (object or hand), a 29-dimensional ICT is constructed. Each token includes:
 - An entity type (hand or object).
 - The entity's 6-DoF pose in a shared static camera reference frame ($^{REF}T_E$).
 - The left-hand and right-hand poses expressed in the entity's local frame ($^{E}T_{LH}$, $^{E}T_{RH}$).
 - The grasp state (binary finger distance for hands, a sentinel for objects).
 Each SE(3) transform is flattened to a 9D vector. This representation is designed to be interaction-centric, focusing on evolving relative relationships between entities, and invariant to viewpoint, enabling direct human-to-robot transfer.

#### 3.4. Flow Matching Policy with Dense Auxiliary Objectives

The policy takes the scene state (ICT tokens and RGB image) and generates a bimanual action trajectory over a K-step horizon.
1. **Flow Matching Action Generation:** Action generation is formulated as a conditional flow matching problem. A velocity field $v_\theta$, parameterized by a transformer decoder conditioned on the scene state $s_t$, is trained to transport a Gaussian prior sample to the target action. The primary training loss ($L_{FM}$) is an MSE over the predicted velocity, with weights for position, rotation, and grasp. Inference involves integrating the learned ODE with a fixed-step Euler solver.
2. **Dense Auxiliary Objectives:** To enhance learning from minimal data, three auxiliary objectives share the context encoder with the flow matching head:
 - **Object Motion ($L_{OM}$):** Predicts the manipulated object’s future 6-DoF trajectory, compelling the encoder to model object dynamics.
 - **2D Trace ($L_{2D}$):** Regresses future 2D projections of entity trajectories, grounding the representation in visual observations.
 - **Latent Consistency ($L_{LC}$):** Predicts the ICT state K steps ahead, encouraging the encoder to capture scene dynamics.
 These objectives are automatically derived from the perception pipeline, providing dense multi-task supervision. The overall loss combines $L_{FM}$ with weighted auxiliary losses.
3. **Training Details:** The velocity field is a 6-layer transformer decoder. Context is derived from RGB (patch embedding, sinusoidal time embedding) and ICT tokens. Training incorporates data augmentation (image, action-target, temporal), region attention to bias visual focus, and state-noise injection to improve robustness.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of HumanEgo addressed its capacity to bridge the embodiment gap, its data efficiency, its robustness to distribution shifts, and the contribution of its individual components.

#### 4.1. Embodiment Gap Bridging Efficiency

HumanEgo was evaluated on four real-world manipulation tasks: Serve Bread (pick-and-place), Downstack Cups (long-horizon multi-step), Water Flowers (contact-rich bimanual), and Adjust Table (sustained rotational control). It was compared against five zero-shot human-video baselines (EgoZero, Point Policy, ZeroMimic, Track2Act, SPOT) and a robot teleoperation baseline (ACT), all trained with 30 minutes of data.
- **Superior Performance:** HumanEgo achieved a 92.5% average success rate across all four tasks with 30 minutes of human data, outperforming baselines which ranged from 1.9% to 45.0%.
- **Data Efficiency against Robot Data:** With only 15 minutes of human data, HumanEgo reached a 75.0% success rate, surpassing ACT trained on 30 minutes of robot teleoperation data (51.2%). This indicates that human videos can be a potent data source for robot learning.
- **Performance on Complex Tasks:** HumanEgo maintained high performance on tasks requiring precise coordination and spatial reasoning (e.g., 87.5% on Downstack Cups, 95% on Water Flowers), where baselines performed significantly worse.

#### 4.2. Efficiency of Human Demonstrations

A data efficiency study on the Serve Bread task compared HumanEgo trained on human videos against ACT and HumanEgo trained on robot teleoperation data.
- **Effective Learning from Minimal Data:** HumanEgo reached 50% success with approximately 7 minutes of human demonstrations and 95% at 30 minutes, showing a steep and monotonic scaling curve.
- **Auxiliary Objectives Impact:** Auxiliary objectives significantly improved performance in the low-data regime (e.g., 57.5% vs. 37.5% at 8 minutes), indicating they amplify supervision when demonstrations are scarce. Their benefit diminished as data became abundant.
- **Human Data Quality:** Human video was observed to be a more efficient data source, with HumanEgo trained on 8 minutes of human video (57.5%) outperforming ACT trained on 30 minutes of robot teleoperation (52.5%), representing a 3.75x reduction in collection effort. Offline metrics indicated higher signal-to-noise ratio, smoother motion, less idle time, and greater spatial and trajectory diversity in human demonstrations.

#### 4.3. Zero-Shot Cross-Condition Generalization

HumanEgo's robustness was tested on Serve Bread and Downstack Cups across nine out-of-distribution conditions without retraining.
- **Visual Robustness:** The policy exhibited success rates between 85% and 91.25% when encountering novel backgrounds, lighting, viewpoints, distractors, and even novel object instances not present in the training set.
- **Object Placement Invariance:** The policy robustly handled arbitrary object placements and novel heights, attributed to the object-centric nature of the interaction-centric representation.
- **Hardware Agnostic Transfer:** Despite training on data collected with Aria glasses, the policy transferred successfully to different robot embodiments (Trossen, Franka, UR10) and camera setups (RealSense, ZED), sharing no hardware in common between training and deployment.

#### 4.4. Component Contribution Analysis

Ablation studies on the Water Flowers task investigated the contribution of the spatial representation (ICT) and auxiliary objectives.
- **Spatial Representation Criticality:** Explicit spatial representation, specifically ICT, was found to be crucial. Progressively reducing the visual embodiment gap alone (from raw human RGB to robot RGB) yielded modest gains (up to 32.5% success). However, adding ICT to raw human RGB dramatically increased success from 7.5% to 85%, emphasizing that 3D spatial relationships are more impactful than visual fidelity for manipulation.
- **Complementary Gains from Auxiliary Objectives:** Each auxiliary objective individually improved performance (object motion: +17.5 pp; latent consistency: +12.5 pp; 2D trace: +5 pp at 15 minutes of data). Combined, they yielded a cumulative +25 pp improvement, indicating they provide complementary insights into scene dynamics.

#### 4.5. Additional Analyses (Appendix)

- **Hand Tracking Method Study:** The quality of the upstream hand tracker significantly impacted performance. Stereo-based Aria-MPS (95% success) substantially outperformed monocular RGB trackers (WiLoR: 45%, HaMeR: 32.5%, MediaPipe: 0% success). This was attributed to stereo's ability to provide metric 3D depth, leading to smoother and more accurate trajectories, crucial for ICT.
- **Human-Robot Co-Training Study:** In a co-training setting with a fixed total data budget (30 min), increasing the proportion of human data monotonically improved performance. Pure human data (95%) outperformed any mix and pure robot data (65%), suggesting that human data's higher information density makes it more valuable per minute of collection.
- **Reference Frame Study:** In the low-data regime, an anchor frame (relative to the first grasped object) outperformed a camera frame by leveraging a strong inductive bias for grasping geometry. In the large-data regime, the camera frame achieved comparable or slightly better performance due to less noise contamination from upstream object pose estimation. However, the anchor frame maintains deployment-time invariance to camera placement.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

HumanEgo presents a framework that addresses several long-standing challenges in robot learning from human demonstrations. The work demonstrates that it is feasible to train robust robot manipulation policies from minutes of human egocentric video, eliminating the need for robot-specific data collection and large-scale pretraining.

The development of the Interaction-Centric Token (ICT) representation is significant as it provides an embodiment-agnostic and viewpoint-invariant encoding of hand-object interactions, directly bridging the visual and kinematic gaps between human and robot morphology. This representation, combined with a flow matching policy and dense auxiliary objectives, facilitates efficient learning from limited, high-quality human demonstrations.

The empirical results indicate that HumanEgo achieves high success rates across diverse real-world tasks, consistently outperforms existing human-video baselines, and shows a substantial performance advantage over matched-time robot teleoperation. Its robust zero-shot generalization capabilities across novel robot embodiments, camera setups, and environments suggest that the learned policies can be deployed in varied real-world scenarios without additional retraining or fine-tuning.

This research has the potential to significantly lower the barrier to entry for robot skill acquisition, making advanced manipulation capabilities more accessible to researchers and practitioners who lack extensive robot hardware or data collection infrastructure. By leveraging readily available human video data, HumanEgo could accelerate the development and deployment of robots in various applications, particularly those requiring dexterous manipulation in unstructured environments.

However, the authors identify several limitations, including the reliance on high-quality stereo hand tracking (e.g., Aria's MPS) and the sequential chaining of off-the-shelf perception modules, which can lead to cascading failures. Future work could focus on developing more robust monocular hand pose estimators, real-time object trackers for dynamic scenarios and in-hand manipulation, and jointly-trained perception frontends to enhance overall system robustness. The current precision limitations at the millimeter level suggest a potential need for integration with reinforcement learning or other fine-tuning approaches for tasks requiring higher accuracy.
