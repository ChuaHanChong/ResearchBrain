# Non-Euclidean Motion Planning with Graphs of Geodesically-Convex Sets

> [!summary] Summary
> This research introduces the Graphs of Geodesically-Convex Sets (GGCS) framework, extending the Graph of Convex Sets (GCS) to Riemannian manifolds, enabling optimal, collision-free motion planning for robots with non-Euclidean configuration spaces. The method transforms the manifold problem into a solvable Euclidean GCS, demonstrating its effectiveness on high-dimensional systems like a 15-DoF mobile manipulator with competitive planning times.

## Problem

- Existing GCS-Planner is restricted to Euclidean configuration spaces, limiting its application to robots with non-Euclidean components like mobile bases (SE(2)) or continuous revolute joints (T¹).
- Sampling-based planners struggle with the "curse of dimensionality" in high-DoF systems, often producing suboptimal paths or requiring excessive computation.
- Trajectory optimizers frequently converge to local minima due to the highly nonconvex nature of collision avoidance, failing to find optimal or even feasible solutions.

## Method

- Developed the Graphs of Geodesically-Convex Sets (**GGCS**) framework, defining ==motion planning== on Riemannian manifolds using strongly geodesically-convex sets and associated g-convex cost functions.
- Transformed the **GGCS** problem into an equivalent Euclidean GCS problem by assuming the robot's configuration space is a flat Riemannian manifold and constructing an atlas of local isometries.
- Utilized an extended IRIS algorithm to generate g-convex regions and formulated affine transition maps between overlapping Euclidean charts, allowing the problem to be solved via mixed-integer convex programming.

## Results

- Successfully computed collision-free, near-optimal trajectories for diverse robotic systems, including a 5-DoF planar arm and a 15-DoF PR2 bimanual mobile manipulator in obstacle-rich, non-Euclidean environments.
- Achieved planning times ranging from approximately 25 to 66 seconds for complex PR2 tasks, which are competitive with or faster than traditional trajectory optimizers while offering stronger theoretical guarantees.
- The GGCS-Planner produced trajectories that were consistently shorter or competitive compared to sampling-based methods and trajectory optimizers, effectively navigating scenarios where other approaches struggled with local minima or artificial joint limits.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - For flat Riemannian manifolds, optimal collision-free paths are piecewise geodesics, with each segment contained within a geodesically-convex set.
> - A crucial theoretical equivalence exists: an optimal solution in the transformed Euclidean GCS problem directly corresponds to an optimal piecewise geodesic path in the original Riemannian manifold.
> - The presence of positive sectional curvature in a Riemannian manifold inherently induces nonconvexity in the distance function, validating the flatness assumption for the proposed method's guarantees.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic motion planning aims to compute optimal, collision-free trajectories for robots through environments with obstacles. This is a complex problem, particularly for high-dimensional systems. Existing approaches primarily fall into two categories: sampling-based planners and trajectory optimizers.

**Sampling-based planners** (e.g., Probabilistic Roadmaps (PRMs) and Rapidly-Exploring Random Trees (RRTs)) are probabilistically complete, meaning they can find a path if one exists given sufficient samples. However, they often produce locally suboptimal paths and can be limited by the "curse of dimensionality," where the number of samples required to cover the configuration space increases exponentially with dimension. This limitation makes them less effective for high-degree-of-freedom (DoF) robots like bimanual manipulators.

**Trajectory optimizers** (e.g., CHOMP, STOMP, KOMO) formulate motion planning as an optimization problem. While suitable for complex systems and not inherently limited by dimensionality, the optimization landscape for collision avoidance is highly nonconvex. This can cause these methods to converge to local minima, resulting in suboptimal trajectories or failure to find a valid path entirely.

A more recent development involves **mixed-integer programming (MIP)**, which has shown promise for finding globally optimal trajectories by encapsulating nonconvexities. Specifically, the "Graph of Convex Sets (GCS)" framework by Marcucci et al. decomposes the collision-free configuration space into convex sets, transforming motion planning into a shortest-path problem in this graph. This approach benefits from tight convex relaxations and efficient approximation strategies, which have reduced runtimes and demonstrated effectiveness in challenging, high-dimensional problems in Euclidean configuration spaces.

However, a significant limitation of the original GCS-Planner is its restriction to **Euclidean configuration spaces**. Many robotic systems, such as those with mobile bases (e.g., planar rotation, SE(2)) or continuous revolute joints (e.g., 360° rotation, S1 or T1), possess configuration spaces that are inherently non-Euclidean. These spaces are often "locally Euclidean" and can be described as differentiable manifolds.

Simultaneously, there has been an increasing trend towards using **Riemannian geometry** to model and solve problems in robotics, for instance, in Riemannian Motion Policies (RMPs) or for generating motion synergies. Riemannian metrics allow for the measurement of distance and generalization of convexity concepts in nonlinear spaces, offering a mathematical framework for optimization on manifolds with rigorous guarantees.

This paper addresses the gap left by existing GCS-Planner by extending the framework to Riemannian manifolds. It aims to integrate the advantages of GCS (global optimality, collision-freeness) with the ability to model non-Euclidean configuration spaces, thereby expanding the applicability of these optimization-based motion planning techniques to a wider range of robotic platforms.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to generalize the Graph of Convex Sets (GCS) framework to handle motion planning in Riemannian manifolds, specifically for robotic systems with non-Euclidean configuration spaces. This generalization, termed "Graphs of Geodesically-Convex Sets (GGCS)," aims to enable the computation of optimal, collision-free trajectories with theoretical guarantees that are analogous to those provided by the Euclidean GCS-Planner.

The motivation for this work arises from several limitations in current motion planning methodologies:

- **Inability of Existing GCS to Handle Non-Euclidean Spaces:** The GCS-Planner, while effective for Euclidean configuration spaces, is inherently limited when dealing with robots that have mobile bases (e.g., driving robots) or continuous revolute joints. The configuration spaces of such robots (e.g., SE(2) for a mobile base, T1 for a continuous revolute joint) are non-Euclidean. Without a framework that accommodates these geometries, the benefits of GCS cannot be fully realized for a broad class of robots.
- **Shortcomings of Conventional Motion Planning Techniques:**
 - **Sampling-based planners** frequently struggle with the "curse of dimensionality" when applied to high-dimensional systems like bimanual mobile manipulators (e.g., the PR2 robot used in experiments). They may require an unmanageable number of samples, leading to long planning times or an inability to find paths, and often yield suboptimal, jerky trajectories.
 - **Trajectory optimizers** face challenges with the nonconvex nature of collision avoidance constraints. This often leads to convergence to local minima, producing suboptimal paths or failing to find a feasible solution altogether, even when one exists.
- **Need for Global Optimality and Collision-Free Guarantees:** The GCS framework offers the potential for globally optimal solutions and guarantees of collision avoidance, properties that are highly desirable for robust and safe robot operation. Extending these guarantees to non-Euclidean spaces is a key motivation to enhance the reliability of motion planning for complex robotic systems.
- **Addressing Suboptimal Workarounds:** Prior attempts to adapt Euclidean GCS to non-Euclidean spaces, such as introducing artificial joint limits or generating multiple copies of convex sets across angle wraps, often introduce their own problems. Artificial limits can render optimal paths infeasible if placed incorrectly (e.g., an optimal path might traverse a joint more than 360°). Multiple copies increase the problem's size and computational burden, and both workarounds may require *a priori* knowledge of the solution structure to function effectively.
- **Focus on Flat Manifolds:** The work is particularly motivated by the observation that many robot configuration spaces, including those formed by open kinematic chains with continuous revolute joints and mobile bases, can be naturally modeled as flat Riemannian manifolds (i.e., locally isometric to Euclidean space). This property is leveraged to facilitate the reduction to a convex optimization problem.

In summary, the paper aims to develop a theoretically sound and practically effective motion planning framework that combines the global optimality and collision-free guarantees of GCS with the ability to model the non-Euclidean configuration spaces prevalent in advanced robotic systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology developed in this paper extends the Graph of Convex Sets (GCS) framework to Riemannian manifolds, creating a "Graph of Geodesically-Convex Sets (GGCS)." The core approach involves defining the problem on a Riemannian manifold and then reducing it to an equivalent Euclidean GCS problem that can be solved using mixed-integer convex optimization techniques.

**A. Theoretical Framework: Graph of Geodesically-Convex Sets (GGCS)**
The GGCS framework is defined as a directed graph G = (V, E):
- **Vertices:** Each vertex `v ∈ V` is associated with a strongly geodesically-convex (g-convex) subset `Y_v` of a Riemannian manifold `(M_v, g_v)`. G-convexity ensures that for any two points in `Y_v`, there is a unique length-minimizing geodesic between them, and this geodesic is entirely contained within `Y_v`.
- **Edges:** Each edge `e = (u, v) ∈ E` is associated with a g-convex cost function `l_Ye: Y_u × Y_v → R≥0 ∪ {∞}`.
The problem is to find the shortest path between a source vertex `p` and a target vertex `q`, where the path length is the sum of edge costs along a sequence of points `y_π = (y_0..., y_K)` such that each `y_k ∈ Y_{v_k}`.

**B. Problem Transformation to Euclidean GCS**
The key step is to transform the GGCS problem, defined on a Riemannian manifold, into an ordinary GCS problem, which operates in Euclidean space and can be solved using existing mixed-integer convex programming solvers. This transformation relies on specific assumptions about the configuration space:
- **Assumptions:**
 - **Configuration Space `Q` is Flat:** The most critical assumption is that the robot's configuration space `Q` is a flat Riemannian manifold (i.e., its sectional curvature is everywhere zero). This implies that `Q` is locally isometric to Euclidean space. Many robot configuration spaces, such as products of 1-dimensional manifolds (continuous revolute joints) and SE(2) (mobile bases), satisfy this condition. However, manifolds with positive curvature, such as SO(3) (ball joints), are explicitly stated to induce nonconvexity and cannot be handled by this method.
 - **Atlas of Local Isometries:** An atlas `A = {(Y_v, ψ_v)}` is provided or constructed for `M` (the collision-free subset of `Q`). Each `Y_v` is a g-convex subset of `Q`, and `ψ_v` is a local isometry mapping `Y_v` to a convex subset `X_v = ψ_v(Y_v)` in Euclidean space. The union of the `Y_v` is assumed to cover `M`.
- **Consequences of Assumptions:**
 - If `Q` is flat and `ψ_v` is a local isometry, then `X_v = ψ_v(Y_v)` is convex in Euclidean space.
 - The Riemannian distance `d(y_0, y_1)` between two points `y_0, y_1 ∈ Y_v` is equal to the Euclidean distance `||ψ_v(y_0) - ψ_v(y_1)||_2` between their images in Euclidean space.
 - Transition maps `τ_u,v = ψ_v ◦ ψ_u⁻¹` between overlapping charts are Euclidean isometries, and thus affine transformations.
- **GCS Formulation:**
 - **Decision Variables:** For each vertex `v`, decision variables `x_v = (x_{v,0}, x_{v,1})` are introduced, representing the start and end points of a line segment within the Euclidean convex set `X_v`.
 - **Edge Cost:** The edge cost `l_e(x_u, x_v)` is defined as the Euclidean length of the segment within `X_u`: `||x_{u,0} - x_{u,1}||_2`. This directly corresponds to the geodesic length in `Y_u` due to the local isometry property.
 - **Equality Constraints:** To ensure continuity, the end point of a segment in `X_u` must correspond to the start point of the next segment in `X_v` after applying the transition map: `τ_u,v(x_{u,1}) = x_{v,0}`. Since `τ_u,v` is affine, this constraint remains convex.
 This transformation results in a standard mixed-integer convex program that can be solved to find optimal paths. The solution from the Euclidean GCS problem can then be lifted back to a piecewise geodesic path on the Riemannian manifold.

**C. Construction of the Atlas**
- **IRIS Extension:** The paper employs an extension of the IRIS (Iterative Region Inflation by Semidefinite Programming) algorithm to generate the g-convex sets `Y_v`. This algorithm produces inner approximations of the collision-free space. Given a seed point, IRIS grows a region around it in a local coordinate system, implicitly mapping to the manifold.
- **Ensuring G-Convexity:** To guarantee `Y_v` is g-convex when lifted to `Q`, the generated regions are bounded by the convexity radius on a per-joint basis. For a flat manifold, bounding the diameter of the projection of `Y` onto each component `Q_i` by `2r_i` (where `r_i` is the convexity radius of `Q_i`) ensures `Y` is g-convex.
- **Coverage:** The method typically generates an inner approximation of the collision-free space, meaning planning occurs within "certified" collision-free regions.

**D. Generalization to B´ezier Curves and Differentiability**
The framework can be extended to parametrize trajectories as piecewise B´ezier curves on Riemannian manifolds, which allows for handling more complex costs (duration, energy, velocity bounds) and differentiability constraints. Since the method is restricted to flat manifolds, the local geometry is Euclidean. This simplifies the application of B´ezier curves, and differentiability constraints across segments (comparing tangent vectors via pushforwards of transition maps) remain convex because transition maps are Euclidean isometries, and derivatives of B´ezier curves are linear expressions of control points.

**E. Theoretical Guarantees**
The paper provides several theorems underpinning the methodology:
- **Existence of Optimal Trajectories:** Proves that for any two points in the closure of the collision-free space `M`, a continuous length-minimizing curve exists.
- **Piecewise Geodesic Optimal Paths:** Demonstrates that if `Y_v` sets satisfy the atlas assumptions, an optimal path is a piecewise geodesic, with each segment contained in a g-convex `Y_v`, and passing through each `Y_v` at most once.
- **Problem Equivalence:** Formally proves that an optimal solution to the transformed Euclidean GCS problem corresponds to an optimal piecewise geodesic path in the original Riemannian manifold.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides both theoretical foundations and empirical demonstrations of the GGCS-Planner's capabilities.

**A. Theoretical Findings**

- **Existence and Structure of Optimal Paths:**
 - **Theorem 1 (Existence of Optimal Trajectories):** The paper proves that for any start and goal configurations `p, q` within the closure of the collision-free space `M`, a continuous curve exists that minimizes arc length.
 - **Theorem 2 (Piecewise Geodesic Optimal Paths):** It is shown that given a finite atlas of g-convex sets covering `M`, the length-minimizing path between `p` and `q` is a piecewise geodesic of the configuration space `Q`. Each geodesic segment is contained within one of the g-convex sets `Y_v`, and the path passes through each such set at most once. This result supports the graph-based decomposition.
- **Equivalence of GGCS and GCS Problems:**
 - **Theorem 3 (Proof of Problem Equivalence):** The paper formally establishes that if the robot's configuration space `Q` is flat and local isometries are used for coordinate charts, an optimal solution to the transformed Euclidean GCS problem (with costs based on Euclidean distances and affine transition maps) corresponds to an optimal piecewise geodesic path in the original Riemannian manifold. This is a crucial finding, enabling the use of established GCS solvers.
- **Conditions for G-Convexity:**
 - **Theorem 4:** Provides a practical condition for ensuring that regions generated by algorithms like IRIS are g-convex when lifted to a flat manifold `Q`. Specifically, if `Q` is a product of manifolds `Q_1 ×... × Q_m` (each with convexity radius `r_i`), and a coordinate chart `(Y, ψ)` has `ψ` as a local isometry and `ψ(Y)` convex in Euclidean space, then `Y` is g-convex if the diameter of the projection of `Y` onto each `Q_i` is less than `2r_i`.
- **Limitations Due to Positive Curvature:**
 - **Theorem 5 (Positive Curvature Induces Nonconvexity):** The paper demonstrates a fundamental limitation: if a Riemannian manifold `M` has any point with positive sectional curvature, the Riemannian distance function `d: M² → R` is nonconvex, even in arbitrarily small neighborhoods of that point. This theoretical result explains why the flatness assumption for `Q` is essential for the proposed method's validity and why configuration spaces involving ball joints (subsets of SO(3), which has positive curvature) cannot be directly handled.

**B. Experimental Results and Demonstrations**

The GGCS-Planner was evaluated on various robotic platforms and scenarios, with interactive recordings provided online.

- **Point Robot in Toroidal World (T²):**
 - Successfully planned a collision-free path that "wraps around" a toroidal configuration space. This demonstration illustrates the handling of continuous angle dimensions beyond a single Euclidean projection.
 - Planning time: 0.79 seconds.
- **5-DoF Planar Arm (T⁵):**
 - Generated collision-free trajectories for an arm with five continuous revolute joints in a planar workspace with obstacles.
 - One demonstrated trajectory involved a joint traversing more than 360°, showcasing the ability to find paths that would be infeasible with artificial joint limits.
 - Planning times: 5.36 and 4.63 seconds.
- **Modified KUKA iiwa Arm (T¹ × I⁶):**
 - Demonstrated a mug reorganization task, where the first joint of the 7-DoF arm was modified to be continuous revolute.
 - 14 individual motions were planned. The robot utilized the full 360° range of the base joint, always choosing the shortest path without unwinding rotations. Region refinement was used to account for dynamic object placement.
 - Average planning time per segment: 25.75 seconds (range: 4.63 to 50.30 seconds).
- **PR2 Bimanual Mobile Manipulator (SE(2) × T² × I¹⁰ ≈ T³ × I¹²):**
 - This represents a high-dimensional (15-DoF) and challenging scenario with a mobile base and two arms operating in an obstacle-dense environment (shelves).
 - **Trajectory Lengths (Table I):** GGCS-Planner produced trajectories that were generally significantly shorter than those from a sampling-based PRM. When compared to kinematic trajectory optimization (Drake-Trajopt), GGCS-Planner's paths were often competitive or shorter, particularly in complex scenarios where Drake-Trajopt struggled to find collision-free paths without manual waypoints. PRM + Drake-Trajopt sometimes yielded shorter paths than GGCS-Planner, but sometimes at the cost of minor collisions (Fig. 10).
 - **Planning Times (Table II):** GGCS-Planner's online planning times ranged from approximately 25 to 66 seconds for the PR2 experiments, which are longer than standalone PRM but competitive with or faster than Drake-Trajopt, especially considering the guarantees.
 - **Comparison to GCS Workarounds:** The paper compared GGCS to two workarounds for non-Euclidean spaces using standard GCS:
 1. Adding artificial joint limits.
 2. Treating angles as unbounded real numbers with multiple copies of convex sets.
 Both workarounds required *a priori* knowledge to guarantee finding the optimal path. The best-case scenarios for these workarounds could sometimes yield slightly shorter paths than GGCS if their regions were not bounded by the convexity radius (allowing larger regions), but their worst-case performance was significantly worse or infeasible. When restricted to the same region definitions as GGCS, their performance was indistinguishable.

In summary, the empirical results demonstrate the GGCS-Planner's ability to generate collision-free, near-optimal trajectories for complex robots in non-Euclidean configuration spaces. It addresses scenarios that pose difficulties for both sampling-based and trajectory optimization methods, providing a robust alternative with theoretical guarantees.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of robotic motion planning by extending a rigorous, optimization-based framework to a broader and more representative class of robot configuration spaces. The significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Enabling Global Optimality in Non-Euclidean Spaces:** The primary significance is the generalization of the Graph of Convex Sets (GCS) framework to Riemannian manifolds, creating the Graph of Geodesically-Convex Sets (GGCS). This allows for the computation of globally optimal, collision-free trajectories for robots whose configuration spaces are non-Euclidean but flat, such as those with mobile bases or continuous revolute joints. This addresses a critical limitation of the original GCS-Planner, which was restricted to Euclidean spaces.
- **Overcoming Limitations of Conventional Methods:** The GGCS-Planner offers a viable alternative to existing motion planning techniques:
 - It bypasses the "curse of dimensionality" that often hinders sampling-based planners in high-DoF systems.
 - It mitigates the issue of local minima that commonly affects trajectory optimizers, providing a method with stronger guarantees of finding optimal solutions.
- **Rigorous Guarantees for Robustness and Safety:** By formulating the problem within a mixed-integer convex optimization framework, the method provides theoretical guarantees for both global optimality (in the context of the defined graph) and collision-freeness for the planned trajectories. Even with approximate solving techniques, collision-freeness is maintained, which is crucial for safety-critical robotic applications. This level of guarantee is not typically offered by trajectory optimization methods that rely on discrete collision checking.
- **Practical Applicability to Complex Robots:** The demonstrations on high-degree-of-freedom systems, including a 5-DoF planar arm, a modified KUKA iiwa manipulator, and a PR2 bimanual mobile manipulator, illustrate the practical utility of the GGCS-Planner. The ability to plan complex maneuvers (e.g., reaching into shelves with both arms, traversing more than 360 degrees with a joint) in obstacle-dense environments showcases its relevance for real-world robotics.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The paper lays theoretical groundwork for further advancements in motion planning. The authors explicitly mention intentions to generalize the techniques for use in bimanual manipulation with implicit kinematic constraints and planning through contact. These extensions are vital for enabling more sophisticated and interactive robotic tasks.
- **Improved Autonomy for Mobile Manipulators:** By effectively handling non-Euclidean configuration spaces like SE(2) for mobile bases and T¹ for continuous joints, the research directly enhances the capabilities of mobile manipulators. This class of robots is increasingly important for tasks in human environments, and improved motion planning allows them to operate more effectively and reliably.
- **Computational Efficiency via Convex Relaxation:** The reduction of the GGCS problem to a GCS problem, which can be solved with tight convex relaxations, contributes to the computational efficiency. This makes the approach feasible for complex problems within reasonable planning times, as demonstrated by the experimental results.

In conclusion, this work represents an advancement in motion planning by extending a theoretically robust and empirically effective framework to a broader set of robotic systems, enhancing their autonomy, safety, and operational capabilities in challenging, non-Euclidean environments.
